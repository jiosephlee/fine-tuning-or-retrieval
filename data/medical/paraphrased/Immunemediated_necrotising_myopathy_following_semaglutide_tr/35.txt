Title: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

Brian Lam, Eleni Tiniakou, Xinhai Robert Zhang, Shervin Assassi

Abstract:

Summary: This case report details a woman in her 60s who developed immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) following administration of semaglutide. Over a four-month period, she experienced worsening dysphagia, dysphonia, proximal muscle weakness, and elevated creatine kinase, all of which began after a single dose of semaglutide. Diagnostic evaluation revealed findings compatible with necrotising myopathy with inflammatory infiltration on muscle biopsy. She was treated with corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), resulting in gradual recovery. Ultimately, she resumed her pre-illness functional level, remained off immunosuppressive therapy for several months, and continued to avoid glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists. This is presented as the first documented occurrence of IMNM likely linked to GLP-1 receptor agonist exposure, showing a favorable response to brief immunosuppression and IVIG.

Background:

Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have become widely prescribed in recent years for metabolic conditions such as type 2 diabetes and obesity. A recognized side effect among these agents is the reduction in skeletal muscle mass. Recently, a case report detailed rhabdomyolysis with necrotising characteristics linked to tirzepatide, a frequently utilized GLP-1 receptor agonist. In the reported scenario, the patient experienced a marked increase in creatine kinase (CK) levels, reaching several thousand units, while undergoing therapy; following cessation of the medication, CK values began to decline within a few days, suggesting a drug-induced rhabdomyolysis.

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) represents a unique form of myopathy, marked by rapidly progressive weakness in proximal muscles, necrosis of muscle fibers, and minimal lymphocytic infiltration observed in tissue samples. The presence of anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (SRP) antibodies is strongly linked to IMNM. Statin therapy, frequently employed for metabolic disorders, has a particularly strong association with positivity for HMGCR autoantibodies. Prior to the recognition of this link, numerous individuals taking statins developed necrotising myopathy and were previously misclassified as having toxic myopathy or polymyositis. Notably, many affected patients continued to exhibit ongoing muscle symptoms and elevated CK levels even after discontinuing statins. Subsequent response to immunosuppressive treatment further indicated an immune-mediated etiology and facilitated the establishment of HMGCR autoantibody as a defining feature of IMNM. Of note, approximately one-third of IMNM patients are seronegative. The absence of detectable autoantibodies in this group adds complexity to diagnosis and implies the existence of additional, yet unidentified, autoantibodies and possible inciting factors.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 60s, whose past medical history includes obesity, fatty liver, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and a remote episode of deep venous thrombosis and pulmonary embolism, presented with gradually worsening proximal muscle weakness, as well as difficulty swallowing and speaking.

Prior to her decline, she enjoyed good health and was fully capable of managing daily activities independently. Three years before her current symptoms, she was evaluated in the outpatient setting for elevated transaminases. At that time, laboratory tests revealed slight increases in aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT)—each around two times the upper limit of normal, with ALT higher than AST. Results for alkaline phosphatase, albumin, bilirubin, and platelet count were normal. She reported no alcohol consumption and was not using any hepatotoxic drugs. Abdominal ultrasound demonstrated an enlarged liver with diffusely increased echogenicity, most compatible with hepatic steatosis. Serologic tests for viral hepatitis, along with anti-nuclear, anti-mitochondrial, anti-smooth muscle, and liver-kidney microsomal antibodies, all returned negative. A liver biopsy identified steatohepatitis consistent with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD)/NAFLD Activity Score grade 4/8, stage 0. She was instructed to adhere to a low-calorie diet and maintain regular physical activity.

Because she continued to have fatty liver and obesity 2.5 years following her NAFLD diagnosis, semaglutide therapy was begun. Shortly after administration of her initial semaglutide dose (0.25 mg, subcutaneous), she developed nausea and vomiting. The symptoms did not abate and, two days later, she was admitted to the hospital with ongoing nausea, vomiting, decreased oral intake, dyspnea, acute renal failure, and severe metabolic acidosis. Management included intravenous fluids and bicarbonate, in addition to antiemetic agents. She required brief admission to the intensive care unit for non-invasive ventilatory support. Following hydration, her renal function recovered and dialysis or haemofiltration were not needed. On admission, her creatine kinase was elevated to 337 U/L, but she exhibited no additional findings such as rash, myalgia, or dark urine. During the hospital stay, she experienced episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, but serum troponin remained within normal limits. She was released after five days. By discharge, her gastrointestinal complaints had resolved, although she was still deconditioned. In the subsequent 4 months, she experienced worsening weakness. She reported no prior statin therapy, nor did she consume notable amounts of other supplements linked to IMNM, including mushrooms or red yeast rice, or other nutritional supplements. She also denied relevant family history of myopathy, excessive or prolonged physical exertion, alcohol use, or illicit drug use.

Four months following her first and only administration of semaglutide, her condition progressively worsened. She became unable to stand or sit upright without help, and for several days had experienced difficulties swallowing both solids and liquids. As a result, she sought care at an outside hospital, where laboratory evaluation revealed a creatine kinase (CK) level of 3992 U/L (reference range: 12–191 U/L) upon admission. Management included daily administration of 1000 mg intravenous methylprednisolone for 5 days, guided by neurology consultation through telemedicine. Subsequently, she was transferred to a tertiary facility for further rheumatologic assessment. Upon arrival, she was afebrile, had normal blood pressure, and appeared comfortable without signs of distress. Examination highlighted marked weakness, especially in proximal muscle groups (refer to table 1). There were no observed rashes, skin changes, crackles, synovitis, edema, sicca symptoms, oral ulcers, or additional neurologic deficits.

After receiving pulse methylprednisolone, she was started on oral prednisone at a dose of 1 mg/kg per day. Because of ongoing weakness and pronounced clinical manifestations, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at 2 g/kg divided over 4 days. Over a 2-week period, her CK showed significant reduction (see figure 1). She was released from the hospital following an inpatient stay of almost 4 weeks. Twelve days post-discharge, she was readmitted for 10 days because of septic shock resulting from a pseudomonal urinary tract infection, during which her CK remained within normal limits.

She attended multiple outpatient follow-up visits, demonstrating ongoing gains in her functional abilities and general well-being, with CK values remaining within normal limits. As a result, prednisone was tapered off quickly and discontinued. Owing to financial considerations and her sustained progress without semaglutide, she received no further IVIG after the initial infusion administered during her hospitalization.

At her most recent outpatient appointment, 10 months following her original assessment and start of treatment, she had returned to her pre-morbid level of function and had been without immunosuppressive therapy for 8 months.

Investigations:

Her laboratory investigations revealed a CK of 1237 U/L at the time of assessment, with a previous maximum value of 3992 U/L recorded one week earlier at the referring facility. Thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH) was within normal parameters. Aspartate aminotransferase (AST) measured 191 U/L and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) was 165 U/L, consistent with raised CK levels and a documented history of fatty liver disease. Autoantibody testing showed negative results for anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-Sjogren’s syndrome antibodies type A (anti-SSA), anti-Smith antibodies, anti-U1 ribonucleoprotein antibodies (anti-U1 RNP), anti-double-stranded DNA, ANCA, and anti-mitochondrial antibodies. Complement concentrations were normal, creatinine was within standard limits, and there were no signs of cytopenia. The myositis antibody panel was negative for SRP, HMGCR, Jo-1, Pl-7, Pl-12, EJ, OJ, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-y, NXP-2, and cN-1A antibodies. She did exhibit a low-titer positive SS-B autoantibody result at 2.4 (reference range: <1.0). Her erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was raised at 22 mm/hour and C-reactive protein measured 22.8 mg/L. Additionally, high-sensitivity troponin was elevated at 62 pg/mL (reference range ≤53 pg/mL) and pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (proBNP) was found to be 4358 pg/mL (reference range: 0–125 pg/mL).

Key imaging studies included MRI of both femurs, echocardiography, cardiac MRI, liver ultrasound with Doppler, high-resolution chest CT, and abdominal MRI. MRI scans of the femurs identified mild, symmetric muscular edema, most pronounced in the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps muscles, as well as subcutaneous edema on the lateral sides of both thighs (figure 2). Echocardiography showed a left ventricular ejection fraction estimated at 65%–70% and a right ventricular systolic pressure measured at 46 mm Hg. Cardiac MRI revealed mild hypokinesis in the mid-anteroseptal and anterior regions with associated myocardial edema and absence of definite late gadolinium enhancement; findings possibly suggest myocardial inflammation or early myocarditis, or alternatively focal stress-induced cardiomyopathy. No evidence of significant myocardial scar tissue, infarction, or notable valvular disease was present. Liver ultrasound with Doppler detected hepatosteatosis. High-resolution CT of the chest did not reveal interstitial lung disease or lymphadenopathy. Abdominal MRI showed steatotic enlargement of the liver, but was otherwise unremarkable with no evidence of lymphadenopathy.

Additional diagnostic workup included a repeated liver biopsy as well as a biopsy of the thigh muscle. The liver biopsy, conducted during her hospitalization, revealed multiple specimens of liver tissue exhibiting both small and large droplet macrovesicular steatosis involving roughly 70% of the sampled hepatic parenchyma. There was no notable portal inflammation or interface hepatitis present. These results aligned with her previous diagnosis of hepatosteatosis, showing mild advancement of her chronic liver disease, reaching an NAFLD Activity Score of 5 out of 8 and stage 1. Examination of the right vastus lateralis muscle biopsy indicated scattered necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers (figure 3A,B). Immunohistochemical analysis showed positivity for acid phosphatase (figure 3D) and for cluster of Differentiation-68 (CD68), which is indicative of macrophage activity, with evidence of myophagocytosis (figure 4B). Additionally, there was positive staining for the membrane attack complex in both necrotic and non-necrotic muscle fibers (figure 4C), reflecting an inflammatory process. Occasional cells expressing CD3, a marker for T-cells (figure 4A), along with perivascular inflammatory cells (figure 3C), were observed. The muscle biopsy findings were indicative of an IMNM.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient demonstrated a pronounced and rapid adverse response following her first and only administration of semaglutide, indicating intolerance. She initially presented with gastrointestinal complaints and acute renal failure, which was presumed to be due to hypovolaemia. Although her creatine kinase (CK) was only mildly raised and not indicative of rhabdomyolysis during the initial assessment, her ongoing deterioration despite discontinuation of semaglutide, the pattern of muscle weakness, and a twelvefold increase in CK over four months after exposure point towards immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) likely related to semaglutide. Additionally, biopsy findings typical of drug-induced IMNM, absence of other clinical signs of connective tissue disease, and persistent clinical improvement after stopping semaglutide and starting immunosuppressive therapy lend further credence to this suspected relationship.

Of note, the patient exhibited elevated transaminases before initiating semaglutide, a phenomenon observed in certain myopathies. Nevertheless, a liver biopsy conducted three years prior to starting semaglutide confirmed hepatosteatosis, and her mildly increased AST/ALT remained after normalization of CK. Therefore, her chronic transaminase elevations most likely stem from hepatic etiology rather than muscle injury. Additionally, her cardiac evaluation did not definitively indicate ongoing myocarditis. In this clinical context, stress-induced myopathy could also be considered, particularly given her swift improvement.

Her positive SS-B antibody in conjunction with negative ANA and SS-A was a surprising finding. To date, the literature does not document cases in which IMNM is associated with isolated SS-B positivity, absent ANA and SS-A. Regrettably, we were unable to obtain a CK level from before the commencement of semaglutide, making it impossible to conclusively rule out pre-existing, subclinical autoimmune IMNM prior to semaglutide initiation. Nevertheless, the marked 12-fold increase in CK and the onset of progressive proximal muscle weakness following the introduction of semaglutide make a medication-induced IMNM episode more probable. Moreover, her significant IMNM responded well to a brief course of corticosteroids and IVIG and by discontinuing semaglutide. This pattern of improvement is more characteristic of drug-induced IMNM, which typically resolves with short-term immunosuppression and withdrawal of the causative agent, as opposed to idiopathic IMNM, which often necessitates extended and aggressive immunosuppressive treatment.

Treatment:

She received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for 5 days, then transitioned to daily oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg. Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at 2 g/kg, divided over 4 days in light of her ongoing weakness and severe clinical presentation. Multiple outpatient follow-up visits demonstrated ongoing recovery in her physical function and general well-being, with creatine kinase (CK) levels remaining within normal limits. As a result, she was able to discontinue prednisone quickly over a 2-month period. Owing to both financial factors and her continued improvement after stopping semaglutide, she did not require further IVIG treatment beyond the initial hospitalization.

Outcome and follow-up:

At her most recent outpatient follow-up, occurring 10 months after starting therapy, she had returned to her usual functional level and had discontinued immunosuppressive medications for the preceding 7 months. Her creatine kinase concentrations stayed within the normal limits after hospitalization and immunosuppressive therapy. Repeat assessments with echocardiogram and cardiac MRI demonstrated resolution of previous cardiac findings. Furthermore, paroxysmal atrial fibrillation had not recurred.

Discussion:

GLP-1 receptor agonists, including semaglutide, are increasingly utilized and have shown efficacy in managing metabolic conditions such as obesity and type 2 diabetes. While gastrointestinal symptoms are the most frequently observed adverse reactions, these agents may also be linked to reduced muscle mass and, in some instances, rhabdomyolysis. In the current case, the patient’s presentation aligns more closely with IMNM rather than rhabdomyolysis. The patient experienced a 12-fold rise in CK levels over a period of four months after a single dose of semaglutide; this contrasts with the anticipated normalization of CK within a few weeks if a direct toxic effect of semaglutide were responsible, as documented in other drug-induced rhabdomyolysis scenarios. Of note, a prior rhabdomyolysis case associated with tirzepatide followed the expected clinical pattern: the patient exhibited a substantial acute increase in CK (CK=57 014 at initial assessment) and demonstrated rapid improvement after discontinuation of the drug and initiation of hydration, within a few days, without requiring immunosuppressive therapy.

According to the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) Causality Categories and Criteria, semaglutide is likely linked to the development of IMNM in our patient, as the timing was appropriate, alternative causes including other diseases and medications were improbable, and there was a satisfactory clinical improvement upon discontinuation of the drug. Reintroducing semaglutide would not be ethical due to the severity of the adverse reaction. Nonetheless, we recognize that definitive causality cannot be established in this instance. Given the increasing usage of semaglutide, we present this case to alert clinicians to this possible adverse event and to support ongoing pharmacovigilance efforts. Additional research is necessary to determine if the observed association is causal or merely coincidental.

The underlying factors that may have made our patient susceptible to this pronounced response to semaglutide remain uncertain. With GLP-1 receptor agonists becoming increasingly widely used, investigations into risk factors will be essential should further reports with similar temporal correlation to semaglutide exposure emerge.
