Title: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

Brian Lam, Eleni Tiniakou, Xinhai Robert Zhang, Shervin Assassi

Abstract:

Summary: This report presents a case involving a woman in her 60s who developed immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) following exposure to semaglutide. The patient exhibited increasing dysphagia, dysphonia, proximal muscle weakness, and elevated creatine kinase levels over a period of four months. These symptoms began after administration of a single dose of semaglutide. Diagnostic evaluation indicated necrotising myopathy, confirmed by biopsy showing inflammatory infiltration. The patient showed clinical improvement when treated with corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG). Over time, she recovered and returned to her prior level of function, remaining off immunosuppressive therapy for several months and avoiding further glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists. This is the first documented instance of IMNM likely related to GLP-1 receptor agonist exposure, demonstrating apparent benefit from brief immunosuppression and IVIG therapy.

Background:

In recent times, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have become widely utilized in the management of metabolic conditions such as type 2 diabetes and obesity. Reports have indicated that this drug class may lead to certain adverse reactions, including a decrease in skeletal muscle mass. A recent case report detailed the occurrence of rhabdomyolysis with necrotizing characteristics following the administration of tirzepatide, a commonly prescribed GLP-1 receptor agonist. The patient involved exhibited a significant rise in creatine kinase (CK) levels, reaching into the thousands during ongoing therapy, with CK concentrations beginning to decline within several days after the cessation of the medication, aligning with a profile typical of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis.

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) represents a specific form of myopathy characterized by rapid onset weakness in proximal muscles, presence of necrotic muscle fibers, and minimal lymphocytic infiltration upon histological examination. The autoantibodies anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (SRP) are strongly linked to IMNM. Statins, which are frequently prescribed for metabolic conditions, have a significant correlation with HMGCR autoantibody positivity. Historically, patients who developed necrotising myopathy after statin exposure were often labeled as having toxic myopathy or polymyositis before the relationship between HMGCR autoantibodies and IMNM became clear. In many such cases, affected individuals exhibited ongoing muscle weakness and elevated creatine kinase (CK) levels even after discontinuation of the statin. These symptoms, together with response to immunosuppressive therapy, pointed towards an autoimmune etiology and ultimately contributed to the recognition of HMGCR autoantibody as a defining feature of IMNM. Notably, approximately one-third of individuals with IMNM do not display identifiable autoantibodies. This seronegativity introduces further complexity in diagnosis and indicates the existence of additional autoantibodies and unidentified triggers yet to be elucidated.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 60s with a past medical history including obesity, fatty liver disease, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and a remote episode of deep vein thrombosis with pulmonary embolism presented with gradually worsening weakness of the proximal muscles, difficulty swallowing, and voice changes.

She had previously been in good health and was completely independent in her daily activities. Three years before her decline, she was evaluated in the outpatient setting for elevated liver enzymes. During this assessment, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels were mildly increased to about twice the upper limit of normal, with ALT higher than AST. Alkaline phosphatase, albumin, bilirubin, and platelet levels were all within normal ranges. She reported no alcohol consumption and was not using any medications known to be harmful to the liver. An abdominal ultrasound demonstrated an enlarged liver with diffusely increased echogenicity, most consistent with hepatic steatosis. Laboratory investigations, including viral hepatitis serologies and autoantibodies (anti-nuclear, anti-mitochondrial, anti-smooth muscle, and liver-kidney microsomal antibodies), were all negative. Liver biopsy findings were in keeping with steatohepatitis associated with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) and an NAFLD Activity Score of 4/8, stage 0. Recommendations included maintaining a low-calorie diet and engaging in regular physical activity.

Given continued steatosis and obesity 2.5 years post-NAFLD diagnosis, semaglutide therapy was initiated. A few hours after her initial subcutaneous injection of semaglutide (0.25 mg), she began to experience nausea and vomiting. These symptoms persisted, leading to hospital admission two days later with complaints of nausea, vomiting, poor oral intake, dyspnea, acute renal failure, and profound metabolic acidosis. Management consisted of intravenous fluids and bicarbonate, alongside anti-emetic medications. She was temporarily transferred to the intensive care unit for non-invasive ventilation support. Renal function subsequently improved following hydration and did not necessitate hemofiltration or dialysis. Creatine kinase measured on admission was elevated at 337 U/L, although she exhibited no additional symptoms such as rash, muscle aches, or dark-colored urine. While hospitalized, she developed intermittent atrial fibrillation; however, her serum troponin levels remained within normal limits. She was discharged after five days; at the time of discharge, her gastrointestinal symptoms had resolved although she was physically deconditioned. Over the ensuing four months, she experienced worsening weakness. She denied previous statin use, significant intake of supplements linked to IMNM including mushrooms or red yeast rice, or other nutritional supplements. She also reported no significant family history of myopathy, recent vigorous or prolonged exercise, nor use of alcohol or illicit drugs.

Four months following her first and only semaglutide dose, her condition progressively worsened. She was unable to stand or sit upright without assistance and experienced difficulty swallowing both liquids and solids for several days. This led her to seek care at another hospital, where her admission labs showed a markedly elevated CK of 3992 U/L (reference range: 12–191 U/L). Management included 1000 mg intravenous methylprednisolone administered daily for 5 days, guided remotely by neurology via telemedicine. Subsequently, she was transferred to a tertiary care center for rheumatology assessment. Upon arrival, she was afebrile, had normal blood pressure, and appeared comfortable otherwise. Examination demonstrated profound weakness, predominantly affecting proximal muscles (see table 1), without evidence of rash, cutaneous changes, pulmonary crackles, synovitis, edema, sicca symptoms, oral ulcers, or other neurological abnormalities.

After receiving pulse methylprednisolone, the patient was commenced on daily oral prednisone at a dose of 1 mg/kg. Ongoing significant weakness and pronounced clinical findings led to administration of intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) at 2 g/kg, divided across 4 days. Within 2 weeks, her creatine kinase (CK) showed substantial reduction (see figure 1). She was discharged following an inpatient stay of nearly 4 weeks. Approximately 12 days post-discharge, she was readmitted for 10 days with septic shock secondary to a pseudomonal urinary tract infection, during which her CK remained within normal limits.

She attended multiple outpatient follow-up visits, during which she demonstrated ongoing enhancement in both her functional capabilities and general health, with CK values remaining within the normal range. This allowed for the expedited discontinuation of prednisone. Additionally, because of financial constraints and her marked progress after stopping semaglutide, no further IVIG was administered apart from her initial inpatient course.

At her most recent outpatient appointment, 10 months following her initial assessment and commencement of therapy, she had returned to her previous level of functioning and had been without immunosuppressive treatment for 8 months.

Investigations:

Her laboratory evaluation revealed a creatine kinase (CK) level of 1237 U/L at presentation, with a maximum value of 3992 U/L documented one week earlier at the referring facility. Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) was within reference range. Aspartate aminotransferase (AST) was measured at 191 U/L and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) at 165 U/L, findings attributed to increased serum CK and known nonalcoholic fatty liver disease. The autoantibody panel demonstrated negative results for anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-Sjogren’s syndrome antibodies type A (anti-SSA), anti-Smith, anti-U1 ribonucleoprotein (anti-U1 RNP), anti-double-stranded DNA, anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), and anti-mitochondrial antibodies. Complement fractions and creatinine were within normal parameters, and there was no evidence of cytopenias. Myositis-specific antibodies, including SRP, HMGCR, Jo-1, Pl-7, Pl-12, EJ, OJ, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-y, NXP-2, and cN-1A, were all negative. However, a low-titer positive SS-B autoantibody was detected at 2.4 (normal: <1.0). The erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was elevated at 22 mm/hour, and C-reactive protein was also raised at 22.8 mg/L. Additionally, high-sensitivity troponin (HS-Troponin) was increased at 62 pg/mL (reference range: ≤53 pg/mL), and pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (proBNP) was markedly elevated at 4358 pg/mL (reference range: 0–125 pg/mL).

Key imaging studies included MRI of both femurs, echocardiography, cardiac MRI, liver ultrasound with Doppler evaluation, high-resolution chest CT, and abdominal MRI. MRI of the bilateral femurs identified mild, symmetric muscle edema, most pronounced in the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps muscles, as well as subcutaneous edema over the lateral thighs (see figure 2). Echocardiography demonstrated a left ventricular ejection fraction estimated at 65%–70%, with right ventricular systolic pressure measured at 46 mm Hg. Cardiac MRI findings included mild hypokinesis involving the mid-anteroseptal and anterior wall in conjunction with myocardial edema, without discrete late gadolinium enhancement, findings suggestive of myocardial inflammation/early myocarditis or a possible focal stress-induced cardiomyopathy. There was an absence of substantial myocardial scarring, infarction, or notable valvular abnormalities. The liver ultrasound with Doppler indicated the presence of hepatic steatosis. High-resolution CT scan of the chest revealed no evidence of interstitial lung disease or lymphadenopathy. Abdominal MRI revealed steatotic hepatomegaly but was otherwise unremarkable, with no lymphadenopathy detected.

Additional diagnostic evaluations included a repeat biopsy of the liver and a muscle biopsy from the thigh. During her hospitalization, the liver biopsy yielded several tissue cores showing macrovesicular steatosis with both small and large fat droplets involving about 70% of the examined hepatic parenchyma, without evidence of significant portal inflammation or interface hepatitis. These results were in alignment with her previously established diagnosis of hepatosteatosis, and demonstrated a mild advancement of her liver pathology to a NAFLD Activity Score of 5 out of 8, at stage 1. The muscle biopsy from the right vastus lateralis showed scattered necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers (see figure 3A,B). Immunohistochemical staining highlighted acid phosphatase activity (figure 3D) as well as positivity for cluster of Differentiation-68 (CD68)—a macrophage marker—revealing the presence of myophagocytosis (figure 4B). Additionally, membrane attack complex staining was present on both necrotic and intact muscle fibers (figure 4C), indicative of an inflammatory reaction. A small number of CD3-positive T cells (figure 4A), and perivascular inflammatory infiltrates (figure 3C) were also observed. The overall muscle biopsy findings supported a diagnosis of immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM).

Differential diagnosis:

The patient exhibited a pronounced and immediate adverse reaction following her first and only administration of semaglutide, indicative of intolerance. She initially presented with gastrointestinal symptoms and acute renal failure, thought to be due to hypovolemia. At presentation, her creatine kinase (CK) was only mildly elevated, not supporting a diagnosis of rhabdomyolysis. However, her clinical deterioration after discontinuing semaglutide, the specific pattern of muscle weakness, and a twelvefold rise in CK levels over four months post-exposure point toward an immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) likely related to semaglutide. Additionally, muscle biopsy findings compatible with drug-induced IMNM, absence of other signs or symptoms of connective tissue disease, and a sustained response to initial immunosuppression with discontinuation of semaglutide further reinforce this suspected link.

It is significant that elevated transaminases were present before she started semaglutide, as this abnormality can occur in myopathies. Nevertheless, a liver biopsy conducted three years prior to semaglutide initiation confirmed hepatosteatosis, and her mild increases in AST and ALT remained even after her CK normalized. Therefore, her persistent transaminase elevations are most likely attributable to chronic liver disease rather than muscle injury. Additionally, her cardiac evaluation did not yield definitive evidence of ongoing myocarditis. Given the context and her swift improvement, stress-related myopathy remains a plausible consideration.

The detection of SS-B antibody alone, with negative ANA and SS-A antibodies, was unanticipated. To date, isolated SS-B positivity in the absence of ANA and SS-A has not been documented as a cause of IMNM in the literature. A baseline CK level before starting semaglutide was not available, making it impossible to entirely rule out an indolent autoimmune IMNM prior to the drug’s introduction. Nevertheless, the observed twelvefold increase in CK and the onset of worsening proximal muscle weakness following initiation of semaglutide suggest that this case of IMNM was more likely drug-induced. Additionally, the patient’s marked IMNM responded rapidly to a brief regimen of corticosteroids and IVIG along with discontinuation of semaglutide. This pattern is more characteristic of drug-induced IMNM, which tends to resolve with short-term immunosuppression and withdrawal of the causative medication, as opposed to idiopathic IMNM, which typically requires ongoing, intensive immunosuppressive treatment.

Treatment:

She received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for 5 days, then transitioned to oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg per day. IVIG was administered at a total dose of 2 g/kg, divided over 4 days, given her ongoing weakness and pronounced clinical manifestations. During multiple outpatient follow-up visits, she demonstrated continued improvement in both her functional abilities and general health status, with her CK levels remaining consistently within the normal range. As a result, her prednisone could be tapered off successfully and relatively quickly over a period of 2 months. Taking into account both financial factors and her ongoing clinical improvement after discontinuing semaglutide, she did not require further IVIG treatments following her initial inpatient course.

Outcome and follow-up:

Ten months after her first assessment and starting therapy, she was seen again in clinic, having returned to her baseline functional level after being off immunosuppressive agents for seven months. Her CK concentrations stayed within the normal limits after hospital admission and immunosuppressive treatment. Follow-up echocardiogram and cardiac MRI demonstrated resolution of the previous abnormalities. Additionally, she did not have any further episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation.

Discussion:

GLP-1 receptor agonists, including semaglutide, are increasingly used and have shown efficacy in treating metabolic conditions such as obesity and type 2 diabetes. While gastrointestinal side effects are frequently observed, these agents may also be linked to reductions in muscle mass and, in some cases, rhabdomyolysis. In this particular case, the patient’s progression appears to align more closely with IMNM rather than rhabdomyolysis. After a single dose of semaglutide, the CK concentration rose twelve times its baseline over a four-month period; this contrasts with the expected normalization of CK levels within weeks in cases where the direct myotoxic action of semaglutide is implicated, as documented in other episodes of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis. Of interest, a previously described instance of rhabdomyolysis related to tirzepatide displayed the classic pattern: the patient developed a pronounced acute spike in CK (CK=57 014 at initial assessment) that improved quickly following discontinuation of the medication and administration of fluids, all within days, and without the requirement for immunosuppressive therapy.

Based on the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) Causality Categories and Criteria, semaglutide administration in this patient appears to have a probable causal relationship with the onset of IMNM, as the temporal association is reasonable, alternative etiologies including other drugs and diseases are unlikely, and withdrawal of the drug produced an appropriate clinical improvement. Reintroducing semaglutide would not be ethical due to the severity of the reaction observed. Nonetheless, certainty regarding causality cannot be established in this instance. With the growing use of semaglutide, this report is intended to alert clinicians to this possible adverse event and underscore the importance of pharmacovigilance. Further research is needed to determine whether this link is causal or merely coincidental.

The factors contributing to our patient’s heightened susceptibility to this severe reaction to semaglutide remain undetermined. As GLP-1 receptor agonists become increasingly widespread, identifying predisposing factors through dedicated studies will be critical if more cases with similar temporal associations to semaglutide administration are identified in the future.
