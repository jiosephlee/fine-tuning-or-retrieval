Title: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

Brian Lam, Eleni Tiniakou, Xinhai Robert Zhang, Shervin Assassi

Abstract:

Summary: This case involves a woman in her 60s who developed immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) after receiving semaglutide. Over four months, she experienced worsening dysphagia, dysphonia, proximal muscle weakness, and elevated creatine kinase. These manifestations began following a single administration of semaglutide. Diagnostic evaluation identified findings compatible with necrotising myopathy and inflammatory cell infiltrates on muscle biopsy. Treatment with corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) led to improvement in her symptoms. She was able to return to her previous functional level, remaining off immunosuppressive therapy for several months, and continued to avoid glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists. This report presents the first known instance of IMNM likely related to GLP-1 receptor agonist exposure, showing improvement after brief immunosuppressive therapy and IVIG.

Background:

Over the past few years, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have been widely employed in the management of metabolic conditions such as type 2 diabetes and obesity. One documented adverse effect linked to this medication class is reduction in skeletal muscle mass. Recently, a case report detailed the occurrence of rhabdomyolysis with necrotizing features in a patient receiving tirzepatide, another frequently prescribed GLP-1 receptor agonist. The patient exhibited creatine kinase (CK) levels reaching several thousands during therapy, and these elevated CK values began to decrease within days after the drug was stopped, supporting a diagnosis of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis.

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) represents a separate form of myopathy, noted for rapidly progressive proximal muscle weakness, histological findings of necrotic muscle fibers, and minimal lymphocytic infiltration. IMNM is strongly linked with two autoantibodies: anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (SRP). Notably, statin medications, commonly prescribed for metabolic disorders, have a significant association with the presence of HMGCR autoantibodies. Prior to the identification of this association, multiple individuals exposed to statins developed necrotising myopathy features, which were frequently categorized as toxic myopathy or polymyositis. Many such patients continued to experience elevated CK levels and myopathy even after discontinuing statin therapy. These presentations, coupled with subsequent responsiveness to immunosuppressive treatment, indicated an underlying autoimmune myopathy, which ultimately contributed to recognizing HMGCR autoantibody as a defining feature of the condition. Importantly, approximately one-third of IMNM patients do not express detectable autoantibodies. This seronegative group presents further diagnostic difficulties and implies the existence of other, as yet unidentified, autoantibodies and possible precipitating factors.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 60s, whose medical background included obesity, fatty liver disease, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and a distant history of deep venous thrombosis/pulmonary embolism, presented with gradually worsening proximal muscle weakness as well as difficulties with swallowing and speech.

Previously, she was healthy and managed all daily tasks independently. Three years before her current symptoms began, she was evaluated as an outpatient for elevated transaminase levels. At that time, laboratory tests revealed mildly increased aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT), both approximately two times the upper reference range, with ALT higher than AST. Other blood values including alkaline phosphatase, albumin, bilirubin, and platelets were normal. The patient reported no alcohol consumption and was not using medications known to cause liver toxicity. An abdominal ultrasound demonstrated an enlarged liver with diffusely increased echogenicity, most likely indicating hepatic steatosis. Screening for viral hepatitis, and testing for anti-nuclear, anti-mitochondrial, anti-smooth muscle, and liver-kidney microsomal antibodies yielded negative results. Liver biopsy confirmed steatohepatitis, consistent with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), with a NAFLD Activity Score of 4/8 and stage 0 fibrosis. Recommendations included adhering to a low-calorie diet and participating in regular physical activity.

Because of ongoing hepatic steatosis and obesity 2.5 years after being diagnosed with NAFLD, semaglutide therapy was started. A few hours after the initial subcutaneous administration of semaglutide (0.25 mg), she developed nausea and vomiting. These symptoms continued, leading to hospital admission two days later for persistent nausea, vomiting, inadequate oral intake, dyspnoea, acute renal impairment, and profound metabolic acidosis. Management included intravenous fluids, bicarbonate therapy, and anti-emetics. She required brief transfer to the intensive care unit for non-invasive ventilatory support. Her renal function subsequently recovered with fluid resuscitation, and no renal replacement therapy such as haemofiltration or haemodialysis was necessary. Her creatine kinase was assessed at admission and noted to be elevated at 337 U/L. She did not display other associated complaints, such as rash, myalgia, or dark-colored urine. During the same hospital stay, she experienced an episode of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation; however, her serum troponin concentrations were within normal limits. She was discharged after five days. By discharge, her gastrointestinal complaints had abated, although she remained physically weakened. Over the ensuing four months, she reported worsening weakness. She stated there was no previous statin use, intake of other supplements linked to IMNM such as mushrooms or red yeast rice, or any additional nutritional supplements. She also denied a significant family history of myopathies, recent intense or extended physical exertion, and alcohol or illicit substance use.

Four months following her first and only administration of semaglutide, her condition further deteriorated. She was unable to stand or sit without support and experienced dysphagia for both liquids and solids for several days. This led her to seek care at an external hospital, where laboratory analysis revealed a creatine kinase level of 3992 U/L (reference range: 12–191 U/L) at the time of admission. Neurology, consulted through telemedicine, initiated treatment with intravenous methylprednisolone at 1000 mg daily for 5 days. Subsequently, she was transferred to a tertiary care center for rheumatological assessment. Upon arrival, vital signs showed she was afebrile and normotensive, with no evidence of distress. Examination demonstrated notable muscle weakness, predominantly affecting the proximal muscle groups (see table 1). There were no skin rashes, dermatological changes, pulmonary crackles, synovitis, edema, sicca symptoms, oral ulcers, or any additional neurological findings.

After receiving pulse methylprednisolone therapy, she commenced oral prednisone at a daily dose of 1 mg/kg. Because of ongoing muscle weakness and pronounced symptoms, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at 2 g/kg, divided across 4 days. Her CK levels demonstrated significant improvement within a 2-week period (figure 1). She was released from the hospital following an admission that lasted close to 4 weeks. Twelve days post-discharge, she required rehospitalization for 10 days after developing septic shock due to a pseudomonal urinary tract infection; during this second hospitalization, her CK levels remained within the normal range.

She underwent multiple outpatient follow-up visits, during which her functional abilities and general health consistently improved and her CK levels remained within the normal range. Consequently, she was able to taper off prednisone quickly and successfully. Additionally, because of both the expense and her notable recovery following cessation of semaglutide, she did not receive further IVIG beyond the initial course administered during her hospital stay.

At her most recent outpatient visit, which occurred 10 months after she first presented and began treatment, she had returned to her baseline functional level and had been free from immunosuppressive therapy for 8 months.

Investigations:

Her laboratory evaluation revealed a CK of 1237 U/L at the time of assessment, having reached a maximum of 3992 U/L one week earlier at the referring facility. TSH was within the reference range. Liver enzymes were elevated, with AST at 191 U/L and ALT at 165 U/L, occurring alongside increased serum CK and a documented history of hepatic steatosis. Autoantibody testing showed negative results for anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-Sjogren’s syndrome antibodies type A (anti-SSA), anti-Smith antibodies, anti-U1 ribonucleoprotein antibodies (anti-U1 RNP), anti-double-stranded DNA, ANCA, and anti-mitochondrial antibodies. Complement levels, creatinine, and blood cell counts were normal, with no evidence of cytopenias. The myositis panel did not detect SRP or HMGCR antibodies, nor Jo-1, Pl-7, Pl-12, EJ, OJ, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-y, NXP-2, or cN-1A autoantibodies. A low titre positive SS-B autoantibody was observed at 2.4 (reference: <1.0). Inflammatory markers were raised, with ESR at 22 mm/hour and C-reactive protein at 22.8 mg/L. Of note, there was a HS-Troponin elevation to 62 pg/mL (normal: ≤53 pg/mL) and pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (proBNP) measured at 4358 pg/mL (reference range: 0–125 pg/mL).

Key imaging studies included MRI scans of both femurs, echocardiography, cardiac MRI, liver ultrasound with Doppler, high-resolution chest CT, and abdominal MRI. MRI of the femurs demonstrated mild, symmetrical muscle edema bilaterally, most pronounced in the proximal portions of the vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps, along with subcutaneous edema on the lateral sides of both thighs (figure 2). Echocardiogram findings included a left ventricular ejection fraction estimated at 65%–70% and a right ventricular systolic pressure of 46 mm Hg. Cardiac MRI revealed mild hypokinesis of the mid-anteroseptal and anterior walls in conjunction with myocardial edema, without clear evidence of late gadolinium enhancement. These findings were interpreted as suggestive of myocardial inflammation/early myocarditis or possibly focal stress-related cardiomyopathy. There was no indication of significant myocardial scarring or infarction, nor were any notable valvular abnormalities seen. Liver ultrasound with Doppler identified hepatosteatosis. High-resolution CT scan of the chest showed no evidence of interstitial lung disease or lymphadenopathy. Abdominal MRI revealed steatotic enlargement of the liver, but otherwise normal findings with absence of lymphadenopathy.

Further advanced diagnostic evaluations included a repeat liver biopsy and biopsy of the thigh muscle. During the patient's hospitalization, a liver biopsy was performed, revealing several cores of hepatic tissue with both small and large droplet macrovesicular steatosis occupying roughly 70% of the sampled hepatic parenchyma, without notable portal inflammation or evidence of interface hepatitis. These results aligned with her prior diagnosis of hepatosteatosis and indicated mild advancement of her chronic liver pathology, with an NAFLD Activity Score of grade 5/8 and stage 1 disease. Biopsy of the right vastus lateralis muscle showed scattered necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers (figure 3A,B). Immunohistochemical staining demonstrated acid phosphatase positivity (figure 3D) and CD68 staining, a macrophage marker, as well as evidence of myophagocytosis (figure 4B). Additionally, staining for membrane attack complex was positive on both necrotic and non-necrotic myofibers (figure 4C), suggesting an ongoing inflammatory reaction. Occasional CD3-positive cells, marking T lymphocytes (figure 4A), and perivascular inflammatory cells were also observed (figure 3C). Collectively, these muscle biopsy findings supported a diagnosis of IMNM.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient displayed a marked and prompt reaction following her first and only administration of semaglutide, indicating possible intolerance. She initially developed gastrointestinal symptoms along with renal failure presumed secondary to hypovolemia. At first assessment, creatine kinase (CK) levels were only slightly raised and not consistent with rhabdomyolysis, yet her progressive deterioration off semaglutide, the specific pattern of weakness, and a subsequent twelvefold rise in CK over four months post-semaglutide point toward an immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) likely related to semaglutide. In addition, histopathologic evidence compatible with drug-induced IMNM, absence of other clinical signs of an underlying connective tissue disorder, and persistent improvement following discontinuation of semaglutide and initial immunosuppressive therapy further support this relationship.

It is worth highlighting that she exhibited elevated transaminase levels before initiating semaglutide, a finding sometimes associated with myopathies. Nonetheless, a liver biopsy conducted three years prior to starting semaglutide confirmed hepatosteatosis, and her mildly increased AST and ALT continued even after her CK returned to normal. Therefore, it appears probable that her chronic transaminase elevations stem from hepatic causes rather than muscle injury. Additionally, her cardiac assessments did not definitively indicate ongoing myocarditis. Considering her clinical context and swift improvement, stress-induced myopathy also remains a plausible explanation.

The presence of a positive SS-B antibody alongside negative ANA and SS-A results was unusual. To date, the literature does not document cases of isolated SS-B antibody positivity with negative ANA and SS-A as precursors to IMNM. Unfortunately, we lacked a CK level prior to the initiation of semaglutide, preventing us from ruling out a pre-existing, indolent autoimmune IMNM. Nevertheless, the twelve-fold elevation in CK and emergence of worsening proximal muscle weakness following the commencement of semaglutide suggest that the episode of IMNM was likely medication-induced. Moreover, the patient’s severe IMNM responded to a brief course of corticosteroids and IVIG, coupled with discontinuation of semaglutide. This trajectory is more consistent with drug-induced IMNM, which often resolves after short-term immunosuppressive treatment and stopping the offending agent, as opposed to idiopathic IMNM, which typically demands extended, intensive immunosuppression.

Treatment:

She received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for 5 days, after which she was started on oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg each day. Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at 2 g/kg, divided over 4 days, owing to her ongoing weakness and pronounced clinical manifestations. The patient was seen several times in the outpatient clinic, showing continued progress in both her functional abilities and overall health, with consistently normal creatine kinase levels. As a result, prednisone was successfully tapered and discontinued over a 2-month period. Given her ongoing clinical stability, cost concerns, and sustained improvement following cessation of semaglutide, she did not undergo any further IVIG therapy beyond the initial treatment course provided during hospitalization.

Outcome and follow-up:

At her most recent outpatient follow-up, which occurred 10 months after her first assessment and commencement of therapy, she had returned to her baseline level of functioning and had been off immunosuppressive medication for 7 months. Her creatine kinase concentrations remained normal subsequent to hospitalization and immunosuppressive treatment. Follow-up echocardiography and cardiac MRI showed resolution of previous abnormalities. In addition, she did not have any further episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation.

Discussion:

GLP-1 receptor agonists, such as semaglutide, are increasingly utilized and have shown efficacy in managing metabolic conditions, including obesity and type 2 diabetes. These agents frequently produce gastrointestinal adverse effects, but there is also evidence suggesting an association with reduced muscle mass and, potentially, rhabdomyolysis. In the current case, the patient’s progression aligns more closely with IMNM than with rhabdomyolysis. Creatine kinase (CK) levels rose twelvefold over a period of four months after a single semaglutide dose, in contrast to the expected resolution of CK within several weeks if semaglutide’s direct toxicity had been responsible, as observed in other instances of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis. Of note, a previously documented rhabdomyolysis case involving tirzepatide exhibited the classic course: the patient presented with a pronounced acute CK elevation (CK=57,014 at presentation) and experienced swift improvement following drug cessation and hydration within days, without the necessity for immunosuppressive therapy.

According to the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) Causality Categories and Criteria, the association between semaglutide administration and IMNM in this patient is considered probable. This determination is based on an appropriate temporal association, the improbability of IMNM being attributed to alternative diseases or medications, and a clinically plausible improvement following discontinuation of semaglutide. A rechallenge would not be ethical in this scenario due to the significant adverse event linked to the drug. Nonetheless, we recognize that definite causality cannot be established in this case. Given the rapidly increasing use of semaglutide, this report intends to inform healthcare professionals of this possible adverse reaction and support efforts in pharmacovigilance. Future research will be necessary to discern whether this relationship is causal or merely coincidental.

Furthermore, the factors that may have rendered our patient susceptible to this notable adverse response to semaglutide remain uncertain. As GLP-1 receptor agonists become increasingly common, investigations into potential predisposing elements will be critical, particularly if additional instances with a similar temporal connection to semaglutide use are observed.
