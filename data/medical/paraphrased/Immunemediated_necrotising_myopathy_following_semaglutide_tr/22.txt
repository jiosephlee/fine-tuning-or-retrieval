Title: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

Brian Lam, Eleni Tiniakou, Xinhai Robert Zhang, Shervin Assassi

Abstract:

Summary: This case details a woman in her sixties who developed immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) subsequent to administration of semaglutide. Over a four-month period, she experienced worsening dysphagia, dysphonia, proximal muscle weakness, and increased creatine kinase levels. These symptoms began shortly after a single exposure to semaglutide. Diagnostic evaluation identified necrotising myopathy characterized by inflammatory infiltration on muscle biopsy. Treatment with corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) resulted in clinical improvement. Over several months off immunosuppressive therapy and avoidance of glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists, she restored her prior level of function. This account represents the first documented instance of IMNM likely associated with GLP-1 receptor agonist use, and demonstrates resolution following brief immunosuppression and IVIG therapy.

Background:

GLP-1 receptor agonists have become widely utilized in the management of metabolic disorders such as type 2 diabetes and obesity in recent years. One of the reported adverse reactions linked to these agents is a decrease in skeletal muscle mass. A recent case report documented a presentation of rhabdomyolysis with necrotizing characteristics associated with tirzepatide, a frequently prescribed GLP-1 receptor agonist. The patient in this report exhibited creatine kinase (CK) levels elevated into the thousands during ongoing therapy, with CK values beginning to decline only days after cessation of the drug, supporting a diagnosis of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis.

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) represents a unique form of myopathy, distinguished by rapid onset proximal muscle weakness, histopathological evidence of necrotic myofibers, and minimal lymphocytic infiltration. IMNM is strongly linked to autoantibodies against hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and signal recognition particle (SRP). Notably, statin therapy, frequently prescribed for metabolic disorders, has a significant association with HMGCR autoantibody positivity. Before the identification of HMGCR autoantibody as a disease marker, many individuals treated with statins who exhibited signs of necrotising myopathy were often diagnosed with toxic myopathy or polymyositis. In these instances, myopathic symptoms and elevated creatine kinase levels frequently persisted even after discontinuation of statins. The subsequent improvement with immunosuppressive therapy indicated an underlying autoimmune myopathy, ultimately facilitating the recognition of the HMGCR autoantibody as a defining feature. Nevertheless, it is important to acknowledge that approximately one-third of IMNM cases lack detectable autoantibodies. This seronegativity poses further diagnostic difficulties and implies that additional autoantibodies and precipitating factors remain unidentified.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 60s with obesity, hepatic steatosis, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and a remote history of deep venous thrombosis and pulmonary embolism presented with gradually worsening proximal muscle weakness as well as dysphagia and dysphonia.

She had previously enjoyed good health and maintained complete independence in daily activities. Three years prior to the onset of these symptoms, she had been evaluated as an outpatient for elevated transaminases. During this assessment, her aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels were mildly increased, reaching about twice the upper limit of normal, with ALT higher than AST. Alkaline phosphatase, albumin, bilirubin, and platelet numbers were within reference ranges. She reported no alcohol consumption and was not taking any medications known to cause hepatotoxicity. Abdominal ultrasound detected hepatomegaly along with diffusely increased liver echotexture, most consistent with hepatic steatosis. Laboratory testing for viral hepatitis, anti-nuclear, anti-mitochondrial, anti-smooth muscle, and liver-kidney microsomal antibodies yielded negative results. A liver biopsy demonstrated steatohepatitis, matching the criteria for non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) with a NAFLD Activity Score of 4/8 and stage 0. She received recommendations to maintain a low-calorie diet and engage in regular physical activity.

Owing to ongoing hepatic steatosis and obesity 2.5 years following her NAFLD diagnosis, semaglutide therapy was commenced. Shortly after administering her initial subcutaneous dose of semaglutide (0.25 mg), she developed nausea and vomiting. These symptoms continued, resulting in hospital admission 2 days later for persistent nausea, vomiting, inadequate oral intake, dyspnoea, acute renal failure, and pronounced metabolic acidosis. Management included intravenous fluids, bicarbonate administration, and anti-emetic agents. She was temporarily moved to the intensive care unit to receive non-invasive ventilatory support. Renal function subsequently improved with hydration alone, obviating the necessity for haemofiltration or haemodialysis. Creatine kinase (CK) measured at admission was elevated at 337 U/L. She did not exhibit other symptoms such as rash, myalgia, or dark urine suggestive of rhabdomyolysis. During this hospital stay, paroxysmal atrial fibrillation occurred, though her troponin levels remained within normal limits. After 5 days, she was discharged. At discharge, gastrointestinal complaints had resolved, although she was noted to be physically deconditioned. Over the following 4 months, she experienced progressive muscle weakness. She reported no prior statin exposure, no significant intake of IMNM-associated supplements—including mushrooms, red yeast rice, or other nutritional supplements—nor a relevant family history of myopathy, recent intense or sustained physical exertion, or use of alcohol or illicit drugs.

Four months following her initial and sole administration of semaglutide, her condition worsened. She was unable to sit or stand upright independently, and had developed difficulty swallowing both liquids and solids over several days. This led her to seek care at another hospital, where laboratory evaluation showed a creatine kinase (CK) level of 3992 U/L (reference: 12–191 U/L) upon admission. Management included daily intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg for five days, coordinated by neurology specialists via telemedicine. Subsequently, she was transferred to a tertiary care center for assessment by rheumatology. On arrival, she was afebrile and normotensive, with no apparent distress. Examination identified marked weakness, particularly involving proximal muscle groups (see table 1). No rashes, skin alterations, crackles, synovitis, edema, sicca symptoms, oral ulcers, or other neurological abnormalities were observed.

After completion of pulse methylprednisolone therapy, the patient received daily oral prednisone at a dose of 1 mg/kg. Due to ongoing muscle weakness and pronounced clinical manifestations, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) at a total dose of 2 g/kg was administered across 4 days. Over the following 2 weeks, there was significant reduction in her CK levels (see figure 1). She was discharged after an inpatient stay of almost 4 weeks. However, 12 days post-discharge, she required rehospitalization for 10 days with septic shock secondary to a Pseudomonas urinary tract infection, during which time her CK was noted to be within normal limits.

She attended multiple outpatient visits, during which her functional abilities and general well-being continued to improve, and her CK levels remained consistently within normal limits. As a result, prednisone tapering was achieved quickly and without difficulty. Because of her clinical progress after discontinuing semaglutide and financial considerations, she received no further IVIG apart from the initial inpatient course.

At her most recent outpatient appointment, conducted 10 months after starting treatment, she had returned to her baseline level of function and had remained off immunosuppressive therapy for 8 months.

Investigations:

Her laboratory investigations revealed a CK level of 1237 U/L during assessment, with a highest value of 3992 U/L recorded one week earlier at the referring facility. Thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH) was within reference range. Aminotransferase levels were elevated with AST at 191 U/L and ALT at 165 U/L, in the context of increased CK and a prior diagnosis of fatty liver. Autoimmune serology showed negative results for antinuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-SSA, anti-Smith, anti-U1 RNP, anti-double-stranded DNA, ANCA, and anti-mitochondrial antibodies. Complement levels and creatinine were both normal, and no cytopenias were identified. A comprehensive myositis antibody panel was negative for SRP, HMGCR, Jo-1, Pl-7, Pl-12, EJ, OJ, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-y, NXP-2, and cN-1A autoantibodies. However, she demonstrated a low titre positive SS-B antibody at 2.4 (with the upper limit of normal defined as <1.0). Inflammatory markers included an ESR of 22 mm/hour and CRP of 22.8 mg/L, both above normal. Additionally, high-sensitivity troponin was found to be 62 pg/mL (reference ≤53 pg/mL) and proBNP was 4358 pg/mL (reference: 0–125 pg/mL).

Significant imaging assessments included MRI of both femurs, echocardiography, cardiac MRI, liver ultrasound with Doppler, high-resolution chest CT, and abdominal MRI. The MRI of the femurs showed mild, symmetric edema in the proximal vastus lateralis muscles and distal quadriceps bilaterally, in addition to subcutaneous edema along the outer thighs (see figure 2). Echocardiogram findings indicated a left ventricular ejection fraction of 65%–70% and a right ventricular systolic pressure of 46 mm Hg. Cardiac MRI identified mild hypokinesis of the mid-anteroseptal and anterior walls accompanied by myocardial edema, but without definite late gadolinium enhancement—findings that may suggest myocardial inflammation or early myocarditis, or possibly localized stress-induced cardiomyopathy. No substantial myocardial scarring, infarction, or notable valvular dysfunction was observed. Doppler ultrasound of the liver revealed hepatic steatosis. High-resolution CT of the chest did not show evidence of interstitial lung disease or lymphadenopathy. Abdominal MRI demonstrated hepatomegaly due to steatosis but was otherwise unremarkable and did not identify any lymphadenopathy.

Further diagnostic evaluation included repeating the liver biopsy and obtaining a biopsy of the thigh muscle. During her hospitalization, a liver biopsy was conducted and revealed several fragments of hepatic tissue showing both small and large droplet macrovesicular steatosis involving about 70% of the examined liver parenchyma, without notable portal inflammation or interface hepatitis. These histopathological findings aligned with her prior diagnosis of hepatosteatosis and indicated mild advancement of her underlying liver condition to a NAFLD Activity Score of 5 out of 8, stage 1. In addition, a biopsy taken from the right vastus lateralis muscle showed scattered necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers (figure 3A,B). Immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated positive acid phosphatase (figure 3D) and cluster of Differentiation-68 (CD68), which is a macrophage marker, alongside evidence of myophagocytosis (figure 4B). Staining for membrane attack complex revealed positivity on both necrotizing and non-necrotizing muscle fibers (figure 4C), suggestive of an inflammatory reaction. Occasional CD3-positive cells, indicative of T-cell presence (figure 4A), and perivascular inflammatory cells (figure 3C) were also noted. The histopathology from the muscle biopsy supported a diagnosis of IMNM.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient exhibited a rapid and pronounced response to her first and only administration of semaglutide, indicating intolerance. She initially developed gastrointestinal symptoms along with renal failure believed to be due to hypovolemia. Although her creatine kinase (CK) was only mildly elevated and did not meet criteria for rhabdomyolysis during initial assessment, her ongoing deterioration after discontinuing semaglutide, the pattern of muscle weakness, and a 12-fold elevation in CK over four months post-exposure point toward an immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) likely related to semaglutide. Additionally, the muscle biopsy findings were consistent with drug-induced IMNM, absence of other features indicating connective tissue disease, and ongoing improvement following immunosuppressive therapy and withdrawal of semaglutide, all further reinforce this link.

It is important to note that elevated transaminase levels were present before she began semaglutide therapy, a phenomenon sometimes observed in myopathies. Nevertheless, she had undergone liver biopsy three years before starting semaglutide, which confirmed hepatosteatosis, and her mildly increased AST and ALT remained after normalization of creatine kinase. This suggests that her chronic transaminase elevation likely stems from hepatic pathology rather than muscle injury. Additionally, her cardiac evaluation did not demonstrate definitive evidence of active myocarditis. In the context of her clinical scenario and swift improvement, a stress-related myopathy would also be a plausible consideration.

Her isolated SS-B antibody positivity, with negative ANA and SS-A antibodies, was unexpected. There are no documented cases in the literature of IMNM occurring in the context of isolated SS-B positivity with negative ANA and SS-A. We were not able to obtain a baseline CK value before semaglutide initiation, so a pre-existing, low-grade autoimmune IMNM cannot be definitively ruled out. Nevertheless, the 12-fold elevation in CK and the worsening proximal muscle weakness that occurred following the commencement of semaglutide suggest that this presentation of IMNM may be related to the medication. Furthermore, the patient experienced resolution of severe IMNM after a brief course of corticosteroids and IVIG, coupled with discontinuation of semaglutide. This pattern is more consistent with drug-induced IMNM, which often improves with short-term immunosuppression upon stopping the causative medication, in contrast to idiopathic IMNM, which typically necessitates extended and intensive immunosuppressive treatment.

Treatment:

She received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for 5 days, after which she began oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg per day. Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at 2 g/kg, divided across 4 days owing to her ongoing weakness and pronounced clinical manifestations. Subsequent outpatient evaluations demonstrated continual recovery in both her functional capacity and general health, with consistently normal creatine kinase (CK) levels. As a result, prednisone was tapered quickly and discontinued within a 2-month period. Considering both financial factors and her ongoing clinical stability after discontinuation of semaglutide, no additional IVIG was provided apart from the initial treatment during her hospital stay.

Outcome and follow-up:

At her most recent outpatient visit, which occurred 10 months after starting therapy and assessment, she had recovered her pre-illness functional capacity and had been without immunosuppressive therapy for 7 months. Throughout this period, her CK values stayed normal after hospitalization and immunosuppressive treatment. Subsequent echocardiogram and cardiac MRI showed resolution of previous cardiac abnormalities. Additionally, she did not have further episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation.

Discussion:

GLP-1 receptor agonists like semaglutide are increasingly utilized and have shown efficacy in managing metabolic conditions such as type 2 diabetes and obesity. Although gastrointestinal side effects are frequently observed, these agents may also lead to reduced muscle mass and, in some instances, rhabdomyolysis. In this particular case, the patient’s presentation aligns more closely with IMNM than with rhabdomyolysis. The CK elevation, reaching a twelvefold increase over four months after a single semaglutide dose, differs from what would typically be expected if a direct toxic mechanism from semaglutide was responsible—as CK levels would usually return to normal within weeks, similar to other cases of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis. Of note, a previously documented rhabdomyolysis case related to tirzepatide exhibited the conventional clinical pattern: the patient had an acute, pronounced CK rise (CK=57,014 at presentation) and showed rapid improvement over several days after discontinuing the drug and receiving hydration, without requiring immunosuppressive therapy.

According to the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) Causality Categories and Criteria, our case demonstrates a probable association between semaglutide administration and IMNM, based on an appropriate temporal relationship, the improbability of IMNM resulting from other conditions or medications, and a clinically justified improvement after discontinuing semaglutide. Moreover, considering the severity of the adverse event, repeating exposure would be unethical. Nevertheless, we recognize that definitive causality cannot be established in this instance. Given the rapidly increasing utilization of semaglutide, this report seeks to alert clinicians to this possible adverse reaction and to strengthen pharmacovigilance efforts. Additional research is required to determine whether the observed association is indeed causal or merely coincidental.

Furthermore, the factors that might have predisposed this patient to develop such a notable response to semaglutide remain uncertain. As GLP-1 receptor agonists gain popularity, investigations into potential risk factors will be crucial, especially if further cases with a temporal association to semaglutide are reported.
