Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy associated with semaglutide administration: a potential role?

Brian Lam, Eleni Tiniakou, Xinhai Robert Zhang, Shervin Assassi

Abstract:

Summary: This case report details a woman in her 60s who developed immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) following the administration of semaglutide. Over a four-month period, she exhibited gradually worsening dysphagia, dysphonia, proximal muscle weakness, and elevated creatine kinase. Notably, her symptoms commenced after a single semaglutide dose. Diagnostic evaluation demonstrated necrotising myopathy with an inflammatory cell infiltrate on muscle biopsy, and she experienced clinical improvement following immunosuppressive therapy with corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG). She subsequently achieved a full return to her baseline level of functioning, remaining off immunosuppressive treatment for several months and abstaining from glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists. This report presents the first known instance of IMNM likely linked to GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy, showing marked improvement after a brief regimen of immunosuppression and IVIG.

Background:

Over the past few years, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have become widely prescribed for managing metabolic disorders such as type 2 diabetes and obesity. Among the potential side effects linked to this medication class is the reduction of skeletal muscle mass. A recent case report detailed an incidence of rhabdomyolysis with necrotising characteristics in a patient receiving tirzepatide, another frequently used GLP-1 receptor agonist. In this case, the patient exhibited a significant increase in creatine kinase (CK) levels—reaching into the thousands—while undergoing treatment, with CK concentrations beginning to normalize within a few days after the cessation of the medication, aligning with a pattern typical of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis.

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) constitutes a unique form of myopathy marked by pronounced proximal muscle weakness and necrosis of muscle fibers, with minimal lymphocytic infiltration evident upon histopathological examination. The autoantibodies anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (SRP) display a strong association with IMNM. Notably, statin therapy—which is frequently prescribed for metabolic diseases—shows a well-established correlation with HMGCR autoantibody positivity. Before the relationship between HMGCR autoantibodies and IMNM was established, several individuals treated with statins manifested symptoms consistent with necrotising myopathy; these cases were often misattributed to toxic myopathy or polymyositis. In such scenarios, discontinuation of statin therapy failed to resolve the muscle symptoms and elevated CK levels in many patients. The subsequent therapeutic response to immunosuppressants highlighted the autoimmune nature of the condition, ultimately leading to the recognition of HMGCR autoantibody as a diagnostic marker. Nevertheless, it is important to recognize that approximately one-third of IMNM patients test negative for known autoantibodies. The absence of detectable autoantibodies in this group introduces further diagnostic complexity and points to the possibility of unidentified antibodies or alternative disease triggers.

Case presentation:

A woman in her sixth decade, whose past medical history includes obesity, hepatosteatosis, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and a distant episode of deep venous thrombosis with pulmonary embolism, came to medical attention due to gradually worsening proximal muscle weakness, as well as the development of dysphagia and dysphonia.

The patient had previously enjoyed good health and maintained complete independence in her daily activities. Three years before her clinical decline, she was assessed as an outpatient for elevated transaminases. At that evaluation, laboratory results revealed mildly increased aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels, both reaching up to twice the upper limit of normal, with ALT exceeding AST. Tests showed that alkaline phosphatase, albumin, bilirubin, and platelet levels remained within reference ranges. She reported no history of alcohol consumption and was not using any hepatotoxic agents. Abdominal ultrasound demonstrated hepatomegaly along with a generalized increase in hepatic echotexture, findings most consistent with hepatic steatosis. Serological screening for viral hepatitis as well as autoantibodies—including anti-nuclear, anti-mitochondrial, anti-smooth muscle, and liver-kidney microsomal antibodies—were all negative. Histopathological examination of a liver biopsy revealed steatohepatitis compatible with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), specifically a NAFLD Activity Score of grade 4/8, stage 0. She received recommendations to adopt a calorie-restricted diet and to engage in regular physical activity.

Following ongoing fatty liver disease and obesity 2.5 years post-NAFLD diagnosis, semaglutide therapy was initiated. Shortly after the initial dose (0.25 mg administered subcutaneously), the patient developed nausea and vomiting, which persisted. Two days later, she was admitted to the hospital due to persistent nausea, vomiting, decreased oral intake, dyspnoea, acute renal failure, and pronounced metabolic acidosis. Management included intravenous fluids, bicarbonate administration, and anti-emetics. Due to respiratory compromise, she required a brief period of non-invasive ventilation in the intensive care unit. Renal function subsequently improved with rehydration alone, avoiding the necessity for haemofiltration or dialysis. Upon admission, creatine kinase (CK) was elevated at 337 U/L, though she reported no muscle pain, skin rash, or dark urine. During her hospital stay, she also developed intermittent episodes of atrial fibrillation, though troponin measurements remained within the normal range. She was discharged on day 5, by which point gastrointestinal complaints had abated, although she remained physically deconditioned. Over the ensuing 4 months, she noticed gradually worsening weakness. She specifically denied any prior statin exposure, consumption of supplements implicated in IMNM such as mushrooms or red yeast rice, or any additional nutritional supplements. There was also no family history of myopathy, nor recent intense or sustained exercise, or alcohol or illicit drug intake.

Four months following her initial and sole administration of semaglutide, her condition continued to deteriorate. She lost the ability to stand or maintain an upright sitting position unaided, and developed dysphagia to both liquids and solids that persisted for several days. These symptoms led her to seek care at an external hospital, where laboratory evaluation demonstrated a creatine kinase (CK) level of 3992 U/L (normal range: 12–191 U/L) upon admission. Management included administration of intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for a total of 5 days, overseen by neurology via telemedicine. Subsequently, she was transferred to a tertiary care facility for rheumatological assessment, during which she was found to be afebrile, with normal blood pressure and no signs of acute distress. On physical examination, she exhibited pronounced muscular weakness, particularly affecting the proximal muscle groups (table 1). No cutaneous findings, rashes, crackles, synovitis, edema, sicca symptoms, oral ulcers, or additional neurological abnormalities were identified.

After receiving pulse methylprednisolone, she commenced daily oral prednisone at a dose of 1 mg/kg. Due to ongoing muscle weakness and pronounced symptoms, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at 2 g/kg, divided over 4 days. Over the next two weeks, there was a significant reduction in her CK levels (see figure 1). She was discharged following a hospitalization period of nearly 4 weeks. However, 12 days post-discharge, she required readmission for 10 days as a result of septic shock secondary to a pseudomonal urinary tract infection, during which her CK levels remained within the normal range.

She attended multiple outpatient follow-up appointments, during which her functional abilities and general health consistently improved, and her CK levels remained within the normal range. As a result, prednisone was able to be tapered and discontinued quickly. Owing both to financial considerations and her marked recovery after discontinuing semaglutide, no further IVIG was administered beyond the initial course she received during her hospitalization.

At her most recent follow-up in the outpatient clinic, conducted 10 months following her first assessment and the start of therapy, she had returned to her prior level of functioning and had remained off immunosuppressive medication for 8 months.

Investigations:

Her laboratory investigations revealed a creatine kinase (CK) level of 1237 U/L at the time of assessment, with the highest value documented as 3992 U/L one week earlier at the referring institution. Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) remained within the reference range. Aspartate aminotransferase (AST) measured 191 U/L and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) was 165 U/L, both in the context of increased serum CK and a prior diagnosis of fatty liver disease. Autoantibody testing showed negative results for anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-Sjogren’s syndrome antibodies type A (anti-SSA), anti-Smith, anti-U1 ribonucleoprotein (anti-U1 RNP), anti-double-stranded DNA, antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), and anti-mitochondrial antibodies. Complement and creatinine concentrations were within normal limits, and she did not display any evidence of cytopenia. The myositis antibody panel was negative for SRP and HMGCR, as well as for Jo-1, Pl-7, Pl-12, EJ, OJ, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-y, NXP-2, and cN-1A autoantibodies. The only autoantibody identified was a low-titer SS-B positivity at 2.4 (reference: <1.0). Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was mildly increased at 22 mm/hour, with a C-reactive protein (CRP) of 22.8 mg/L. She also had an elevated high-sensitivity troponin (HS-Troponin) at 62 pg/mL (reference ≤53 pg/mL) and a markedly increased pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (proBNP) of 4358 pg/mL (reference: 0–125 pg/mL).

Significant imaging assessments included MRI scans of both femurs, echocardiography, cardiac MRI, liver ultrasound with Doppler studies, high-resolution CT of the chest, and abdominal MRI. The MRI of the bilateral femurs demonstrated mild, symmetric muscle oedema, most pronounced in the proximal regions of the vastus lateralis and the distal quadriceps muscles, in addition to subcutaneous oedema localized along the lateral sides of both thighs (figure 2). The echocardiogram revealed a left ventricular ejection fraction estimated at 65%–70% and a right ventricular systolic pressure measuring 46 mm Hg. Cardiac MRI identified mild hypokinesis in the mid-anteroseptal and anterior wall, accompanied by myocardial oedema, without definite late gadolinium enhancement—findings that may indicate myocardial inflammation, early myocarditis, or possibly a localized stress-induced cardiomyopathy. There was no evidence of substantial myocardial scar formation or infarction, nor was any significant valvular dysfunction observed. Liver ultrasound with Doppler demonstrated hepatosteatosis. The high-resolution CT of the chest did not show features of interstitial lung disease or lymphadenopathy. MRI of the abdomen revealed an enlarged, steatotic liver but was otherwise unremarkable and did not demonstrate lymphadenopathy.

Further diagnostic evaluations included conducting an additional liver biopsy as well as a biopsy of the thigh muscle. The liver biopsy, undertaken during her hospital stay, revealed several fragments of hepatic tissue exhibiting both small and large droplet macrovesicular steatosis, involving roughly 70% of the evaluated hepatic parenchyma. Importantly, there was an absence of significant portal inflammation or interface hepatitis. These pathological features aligned with her previously known hepatosteatosis, showing a mild advancement of her liver pathology, classified as NAFLD Activity Score grade 5/8, stage 1. 

The biopsy of the right vastus lateralis muscle identified scattered necrotic and regenerating myofibres (figure 3A,B). Immunohistochemical analyses showed positive staining for acid phosphatase (figure 3D) and cluster of Differentiation-68 (CD68), a marker for macrophages, indicative of myophagocytosis (figure 4B). Additionally, deposition of membrane attack complex was detected on both necrotic and viable myofibres (figure 4C), reflective of an active inflammatory process. A few CD3-positive T-cells (figure 4A) and perivascular inflammatory infiltrates (figure 3C) were also observed. Collectively, these muscle biopsy findings were diagnostic of IMNM.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient exhibited a pronounced and immediate adverse response following her first and only administration of semaglutide, indicating a probable intolerance to the medication. She initially presented with gastrointestinal disturbances and acute renal failure, the latter likely attributable to hypovolaemia. At the time of her first assessment, her creatine kinase (CK) levels were only mildly increased, not meeting criteria for rhabdomyolysis; however, her clinical condition continued to deteriorate despite discontinuation of semaglutide. The distribution of muscle weakness and a twelvefold escalation in CK over the ensuing four months post-semaglutide point towards an IMNM likely triggered by the drug. Additionally, the presence of histopathological findings typical of drug-induced IMNM, the absence of signs suggestive of an underlying connective tissue disorder, and her sustained recovery following both withdrawal of semaglutide and initiation of immunosuppressive therapy provide further evidence supporting this linkage.

It is worth highlighting that transaminaemia was present before she began semaglutide therapy, a finding that may occur in cases of myopathy. Nevertheless, a liver biopsy conducted three years earlier had already established a diagnosis of hepatosteatosis, and her mildly elevated AST/ALT levels persisted even after normalization of CK concentrations. This suggests that the chronic elevation of her transaminases is most plausibly attributed to liver pathology rather than muscle injury. Furthermore, cardiac evaluation did not yield definitive evidence of ongoing myocarditis. Considering the clinical context and her swift improvement, stress-induced myopathy would also be a plausible explanation.

The detection of a positive SS-B antibody alongside negative ANA and SS-A results was unanticipated in this case. There is currently no literature documenting cases of IMNM arising from isolated SS-B seropositivity in the absence of ANA and SS-A antibodies. It should be noted that a CK level prior to initiating semaglutide therapy was not available, thus a pre-existing, subclinical autoimmune IMNM cannot be entirely ruled out. Nevertheless, the observation of a 12-fold increase in CK as well as the emergence of progressive proximal muscle weakness following the commencement of semaglutide supports the hypothesis that the IMNM episode was drug-related. Furthermore, the patient’s severe IMNM demonstrated significant improvement with a brief regimen of corticosteroids and IVIG, coupled with discontinuation of semaglutide. This response is more characteristic of drug-induced IMNM, where improvement often follows temporary immunosuppression and withdrawal of the causative medication, whereas idiopathic IMNM typically necessitates sustained, intensive immunosuppressive intervention.

Treatment:

The patient received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dosage of 1000 mg daily for 5 days, after which she transitioned to oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg per day. IVIG was administered at a total dose of 2 g/kg, distributed over 4 days, in response to ongoing muscle weakness and pronounced clinical manifestations. Multiple follow-up evaluations in the outpatient setting demonstrated progressive enhancement in her physical abilities and overall health, with creatine kinase (CK) levels consistently within normal limits. Owing to this sustained recovery, prednisone was tapered off completely within a 2-month period. Considering both the financial burden and her maintained remission after cessation of semaglutide, no additional IVIG infusions were given after her initial hospitalization.

Outcome and follow-up:

Ten months following her initial assessment and commencement of therapy, she was reviewed in the outpatient clinic and had returned to her pre-illness level of function, having discontinued immunosuppressive therapy for seven months. Her CK levels persisted within normal limits after her hospital admission and subsequent immunosuppressive treatment. Follow-up echocardiography and cardiac MRI demonstrated resolution of previously observed abnormalities. Additionally, there was no recurrence of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation.

Discussion:

Agonists of the GLP-1 receptor, such as semaglutide, are increasingly prescribed and have shown efficacy in treating metabolic conditions like obesity and type 2 diabetes. While gastrointestinal symptoms are the most frequent side effects, there is evidence that these drugs can be linked to reductions in muscle mass and, in rare instances, rhabdomyolysis. In the current case, the patient's progression aligns more closely with IMNM rather than rhabdomyolysis. Following a single administration of semaglutide, the patient’s CK levels rose twelvefold over a period of four months, contrasting with the rapid normalization of CK that typically occurs within weeks when rhabdomyolysis is triggered by direct drug toxicity, as documented in other cases. For instance, in the previously documented episode of rhabdomyolysis associated with tirzepatide, the patient displayed a sudden, substantial increase in CK (CK=57 014 at admission), followed by swift clinical improvement and normalization of CK within days after discontinuation of the medication and supportive hydration, all without resorting to immunosuppressive therapy.

In accordance with the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) Causality Categories and Criteria, our case demonstrates a probable association between semaglutide administration and the development of IMNM. This is based on the presence of an appropriate temporal relationship, the improbability of alternative diseases or medications being responsible, and notable clinical improvement following discontinuation of semaglutide. Importantly, due to the severity of the adverse reaction observed, re-exposure to semaglutide would not be ethically permissible. Nonetheless, we recognize that definitive causality cannot be established from this single case. Given the rapidly increasing use of semaglutide, our report seeks to alert clinicians to this possible adverse event and to promote ongoing pharmacovigilance. Additional research will be necessary to determine if this relationship is indeed causal or simply coincidental.

The underlying factors that may have made our patient susceptible to such a pronounced response to semaglutide are still not well understood. As the use of GLP-1 receptor agonists becomes increasingly widespread, identifying risk factors through further research will be essential, particularly if additional incidents closely linked in time to semaglutide administration are documented.
