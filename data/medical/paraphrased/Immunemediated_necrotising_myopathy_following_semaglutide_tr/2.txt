Semaglutide therapy and the onset of immune-mediated necrotising myopathy: potential involvement?

Brian Lam, Eleni Tiniakou, Xinhai Robert Zhang, Shervin Assassi

Abstract:

Summary: This report presents the case of a woman in her 60s who developed immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) subsequent to receiving semaglutide. The patient experienced a four-month progression of symptoms, including worsening dysphagia, dysphonia, proximal muscle weakness, and elevated creatine kinase. These symptoms began following a single administration of semaglutide. Diagnostic investigations demonstrated necrotising myopathy with an inflammatory infiltrate observed on muscle biopsy, and she exhibited clinical improvement upon treatment with corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG). Over time, she returned to her pre-illness level of function and maintained remission off immunosuppressive therapy for several months, while continuing to avoid glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists. This case represents the first documented occurrence of IMNM likely related to GLP-1 receptor agonist use, with notable clinical response to a brief period of immunosuppression and IVIG.

Background:

Over the past few years, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have become widely utilized in the management of metabolic conditions such as type 2 diabetes and obesity. One noted side effect of this medication class is a reduction in skeletal muscle mass. A recent case report documented an instance of rhabdomyolysis with necrotising characteristics associated with tirzepatide, a frequently prescribed GLP-1 receptor agonist. In this particular case, the individual experienced a substantial rise in creatine kinase (CK) levels, reaching several thousand units, during active therapy. Remarkably, CK concentrations began to decline within days after discontinuation of the drug, a timeline that supports the diagnosis of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis.

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) constitutes a specific subtype of myopathy, marked by rapidly progressive weakness in proximal muscles and the presence of necrotic myofibres on histological examination, typically with minimal lymphocytic infiltration. Two autoantibodies—anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (SRP)—demonstrate strong associations with IMNM. Notably, the administration of statins, frequently prescribed for metabolic conditions, exhibits a robust correlation with positivity for HMGCR autoantibodies. Historically, patients exposed to statins who developed necrotising myopathy were frequently misclassified as having toxic myopathy or polymyositis, prior to the recognition of the link with HMGCR autoantibodies. Importantly, these individuals often continued to experience myopathy and sustained elevated creatine kinase (CK) levels after discontinuing statin therapy. Subsequent improvements following immunosuppressive treatment indicated an autoimmune mechanism, which eventually facilitated the identification of HMGCR autoantibodies as a defining feature of IMNM. It should be emphasized, nevertheless, that approximately one-third of IMNM patients do not display detectable autoantibodies. The seronegative status of this group presents not only heightened diagnostic difficulties, but also points to the existence of as-yet-unidentified autoantibodies and potential disease triggers.

Case presentation:

A woman in her sixth decade of life, whose past medical history includes obesity, hepatic steatosis, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and a distant episode of deep venous thrombosis/pulmonary embolism, was admitted due to gradually worsening weakness of the proximal muscles, as well as the onset of dysphagia and dysphonia.

The patient had previously enjoyed robust health and managed all daily activities independently. Approximately three years before her health began to decline, she was evaluated as an outpatient for elevated transaminase levels. At that point, mild increases in aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) were observed, both measuring about twice the upper limit of normal, with ALT levels surpassing those of AST. Other laboratory parameters, including alkaline phosphatase, albumin, bilirubin, and platelet count, remained within normal ranges. She reported no alcohol consumption and was not using any medications known to be hepatotoxic. Imaging with abdominal ultrasound identified hepatomegaly and diffusely increased echotexture, findings most compatible with hepatic steatosis. Laboratory investigations for viral hepatitis and autoantibodies—including anti-nuclear, anti-mitochondrial, anti-smooth muscle, and liver-kidney microsomal antibodies—were negative. Histological assessment from a liver biopsy revealed steatohepatitis consistent with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), showing an NAFLD Activity Score of grade 4/8 and stage 0. Recommendations were made for her to adhere to a calorie-restricted diet and engage in consistent physical activity.

Ongoing hepatic steatosis and obesity persisted 2.5 years after she was diagnosed with NAFLD, prompting the initiation of semaglutide therapy. Shortly after her first subcutaneous dose of semaglutide (0.25 mg), she developed nausea and vomiting that did not abate. Two days later, due to sustained nausea, vomiting, poor oral intake, dyspnoea, acute renal insufficiency, and severe metabolic acidosis, she was admitted to the hospital. Treatment included intravenous fluids, bicarbonate supplementation, and anti-emetic medications. Owing to her respiratory status, she required a brief transfer to the intensive care unit for non-invasive ventilation. Her renal function subsequently improved with aggressive hydration, without the need for haemofiltration or haemodialysis. On admission, her CK was elevated at 337 U/L, though she exhibited no other symptoms typically associated with muscle injury, such as rash, myalgia, or dark-coloured urine. During her hospital stay, she experienced episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, but her troponin levels remained within the normal range. She was discharged after a 5-day admission. By discharge, her gastrointestinal complaints had subsided, although she remained physically deconditioned. Over the next 4 months, she reported increasing muscle weakness. She stated she had never taken statins, had not ingested supplements linked to IMNM (e.g., mushrooms or red yeast rice), nor any other nutritional supplements. She also denied any notable family history of myopathy, recent intensive or extended physical activity, or use of alcohol or illicit substances.

Four months following her initial and sole administration of semaglutide, the patient's condition progressively deteriorated. She became unable to maintain an upright posture independently, either standing or sitting, and developed new-onset dysphagia affecting both liquids and solids over several days. Due to these worsening symptoms, she sought care at an external hospital, where laboratory tests revealed a creatine kinase (CK) level elevated to 3992 U/L (reference range: 12–191 U/L) at the time of admission. Management involved administration of 1000 mg intravenous methylprednisolone daily over a five-day period, coordinated by neurology via telemedicine. Subsequently, she was referred to a tertiary care center for rheumatologic assessment, at which point she was afebrile, maintained normal blood pressure, and showed no acute distress. On examination, there was pronounced muscle weakness, most notably affecting the proximal muscles (see table 1). There were no signs of rash, dermatologic abnormalities, crackles, joint inflammation, oedema, sicca symptoms, oral ulcers, or any additional neurological deficits.

After completing pulse methylprednisolone therapy, the patient began daily oral prednisone at a dosage of 1 mg/kg. Owing to ongoing significant weakness and pronounced symptoms, she also received intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) at a dose of 2 g/kg administered over four days. Over the following two weeks, there was a significant reduction in her CK levels (figure 1). Her hospital stay lasted nearly four weeks before she was discharged. Twelve days post-discharge, she was readmitted for 10 days with septic shock secondary to a pseudomonal urinary tract infection; during this subsequent admission, her CK levels remained within the normal range.

She attended multiple outpatient follow-up appointments, during which she demonstrated sustained enhancements in both her functional capacity and general well-being, alongside consistently normal CK measurements. Consequently, her prednisone tapering proceeded swiftly and without complication. Owing to financial considerations and the marked improvement she experienced after discontinuing semaglutide, she did not undergo further IVIG treatments beyond the initial regimen administered during her hospitalization.

At her most recent follow-up in the outpatient clinic, which occurred 10 months following her initial assessment and commencement of therapy, she had returned to her prior level of functioning and had been without immunosuppressive medication for the past 8 months.

Investigations:

Her laboratory investigations revealed a creatine kinase (CK) level of 1237 U/L at the time of assessment, with a maximum of 3992 U/L recorded one week prior at the referring institution. Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) was within the reference range. Liver enzymes were also raised, with aspartate aminotransferase (AST) at 191 U/L and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) at 165 U/L, in the context of increased serum CK and a known history of hepatic steatosis. Immunological testing demonstrated a negative profile for anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-Sjogren’s syndrome antibodies type A (anti-SSA), anti-Smith antibodies, anti-U1 ribonucleoprotein (anti-U1 RNP), anti-double-stranded DNA, antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), and anti-mitochondrial antibodies. Complement levels and creatinine were within normal limits, and there was no evidence of cytopenias. Myositis-specific antibody testing was negative for SRP, HMGCR, Jo-1, Pl-7, Pl-12, EJ, OJ, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-y, NXP-2, and cN-1A antibodies. However, SS-B autoantibody was detected at a low titre of 2.4 (reference value: <1.0). The erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was increased at 22 mm/hour, and C-reactive protein (CRP) measured 22.8 mg/L. Of additional note, high-sensitivity troponin (HS-Troponin) was elevated at 62 pg/mL (reference: ≤53 pg/mL) and pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (proBNP) was also raised at 4358 pg/mL (normal: 0–125 pg/mL).

Significant imaging studies performed included MRI scans of both femurs, echocardiography, cardiac MRI, liver ultrasound with Doppler assessment, high-resolution CT of the chest, and abdominal MRI. The bilateral femur MRI demonstrated mild and symmetrical muscular edema, particularly notable within the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps muscles, along with subcutaneous edema located on the lateral sides of both thighs (see figure 2). Echocardiography revealed an estimated left ventricular ejection fraction between 65% and 70% and a right ventricular systolic pressure measured at 46 mm Hg. Cardiac MRI findings indicated mild hypokinesis affecting the mid-anteroseptal and anterior myocardial walls in association with myocardial edema, with an absence of definite late gadolinium enhancement. These results suggest possible myocardial inflammation or early myocarditis, or a localized stress-induced cardiomyopathy. Importantly, there was no evidence of substantial myocardial scarring, infarction, or significant valvular abnormalities. Liver ultrasound with Doppler identified the presence of hepatosteatosis. High-resolution CT imaging of the chest excluded interstitial lung disease and lymphadenopathy. Abdominal MRI demonstrated hepatomegaly consistent with steatosis, but was otherwise normal and did not reveal lymphadenopathy.

Comprehensive diagnostic evaluation comprised a second liver biopsy as well as a biopsy of thigh muscle tissue. The liver biopsy, conducted during her hospitalization, revealed several cores of liver tissue characterized by both small and large droplet macrovesicular steatosis involving roughly 70% of the analyzed hepatic parenchyma, without notable portal inflammation or interface hepatitis. These histopathological results aligned with her established diagnosis of hepatosteatosis and showed mild advancement to NAFLD Activity Score grade 5/8, stage 1. Examination of a right vastus lateralis muscle specimen identified scattered necrotic and regenerating myofibres (figure 3A,B); immunohistochemical staining highlighted areas positive for acid phosphatase (figure 3D) and cluster of Differentiation-68 (CD68)—an established marker of macrophages—with evidence of myophagocytosis (figure 4B). Additionally, staining demonstrated the presence of membrane attack complex in both necrotizing and non-necrotizing muscle fibres (figure 4C), indicative of an ongoing inflammatory process. Occasional CD3-positive T-cells (figure 4A) were also detected, alongside perivascular inflammatory cell infiltrates (figure 3C). Collectively, the muscle biopsy findings supported a diagnosis of IMNM.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient displayed an acute and pronounced reaction following her first and sole administration of semaglutide, indicative of drug intolerance. Initial manifestations included gastrointestinal disturbances and renal failure, the latter likely due to hypovolemia. Although her creatine kinase (CK) was only mildly elevated—arguing against rhabdomyolysis—at presentation, her ongoing deterioration despite discontinuation of semaglutide, the specific distribution of muscle weakness, and a twelvefold increase in CK over the subsequent four months following semaglutide exposure, collectively point towards an IMNM potentially triggered by the medication. Additionally, the muscle biopsy findings were compatible with drug-induced IMNM, the absence of clinical signs of an underlying connective tissue disease, and her persistent clinical improvement after both stopping semaglutide and beginning immunosuppressive therapy, all lend further support to a causal relationship.

It is important to observe that the patient exhibited transaminaemia before starting semaglutide, a feature that may occur in myopathic conditions. Nevertheless, a liver biopsy conducted three years prior to semaglutide initiation had confirmed hepatosteatosis, and her marginally elevated AST/ALT levels remained even after her CK concentrations returned to baseline. Therefore, the chronic transaminase elevation is most plausibly attributed to hepatic etiology, rather than to muscle degradation. Additionally, the results of her cardiac assessment did not provide clear evidence of active myocarditis. In the context of this clinical picture and her swift recuperation, stress-induced myopathy would also constitute a plausible diagnosis.

The detection of a positive SS-B antibody in the context of negative ANA and SS-A results was an unanticipated finding. Currently, the literature does not document cases where isolated SS-B positivity, alongside negative ANA and SS-A, is associated with the onset of IMNM. Regrettably, there was no available baseline CK value prior to initiating semaglutide, precluding the ability to fully rule out the possibility of a preexisting, subclinical autoimmune IMNM. Nevertheless, the 12-fold increase in CK and emergence of progressive proximal muscle weakness following commencement of semaglutide strongly suggest a drug-induced etiology for this episode of IMNM. Furthermore, the patient’s severe IMNM rapidly remitted following a brief course of corticosteroids and IVIG, coupled with discontinuation of semaglutide. This pattern of resolution is characteristic of drug-induced IMNM, which often responds well to short-term immunosuppressive treatment after cessation of the inciting agent, as opposed to idiopathic IMNM, which typically demands sustained and intensive immunosuppression.

Treatment:

The patient received a regimen of intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for 5 days, after which she transitioned to oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg per day. Given her ongoing weakness and severity of symptoms, IVIG was administered at 2 g/kg, divided across 4 days. Subsequent outpatient evaluations showed consistent progress in her functional abilities and general health, with creatine kinase (CK) levels remaining within normal limits throughout. As a result of her sustained clinical improvement, prednisone was successfully tapered over a 2-month period. Owing to financial considerations and her maintained response off semaglutide, no further IVIG infusions were provided following her initial hospitalization course.

Outcome and follow-up:

At her most recent outpatient follow-up, conducted 10 months after her first assessment and the commencement of therapy, she had returned to her pre-illness level of functioning, having discontinued immunosuppressive therapy for the preceding 7 months. Throughout the period following her hospital admission and immunosuppressive treatment, her CK levels consistently stayed within normal limits. Subsequent echocardiogram and cardiac MRI demonstrated resolution of the abnormalities previously observed. Additionally, she remained free from further episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation.

Discussion:

GLP-1 receptor agonists—including semaglutide—are increasingly utilized and have shown efficacy in managing metabolic conditions such as type 2 diabetes and obesity. While gastrointestinal disturbances are frequent adverse events, there is also evidence suggesting these agents might contribute to reductions in muscle mass and, on occasion, precipitate rhabdomyolysis. In the present report, the progression of the patient's symptoms aligns more closely with IMNM rather than rhabdomyolysis. Following a single dose of semaglutide, the patient’s CK levels rose twelvefold over four months, whereas, in scenarios where rhabdomyolysis is directly induced by a medication, CK values typically return to baseline within weeks—contrasting with this prolonged course. For comparison, in the previously documented case of rhabdomyolysis attributed to tirzepatide, the patient experienced a pronounced acute spike in CK (CK=57 014 at the time of presentation), followed by a rapid normalization of CK levels upon cessation of the drug and administration of hydration therapy within a matter of days, with no immunosuppressive intervention required.

According to the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) Causality Categories and Criteria, the relationship between semaglutide administration and IMNM in this case can be classified as probable. This assessment is based on the appropriate temporal association, the low likelihood that alternative diseases or medications were responsible for IMNM, and the clear clinical improvement upon discontinuation of semaglutide. Re-exposure to semaglutide is not justifiable due to the significant adverse event previously observed. Nevertheless, we recognize that definitive causality cannot be confirmed in this instance. Given the rapidly increasing use of semaglutide, this case report is intended to inform clinicians about this potential risk and to support enhanced pharmacovigilance efforts. Further research is necessary to determine whether this association reflects a true causal link or is merely coincidental.

The factors that may have made our patient susceptible to this pronounced response to semaglutide are still uncertain. As the use of GLP-1 receptor agonists becomes increasingly widespread, identifying potential predisposing factors through future research will be crucial, particularly if more cases with a similar temporal association to semaglutide administration emerge.
