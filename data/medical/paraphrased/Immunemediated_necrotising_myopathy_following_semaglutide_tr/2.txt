Title: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

Brian Lam, Eleni Tiniakou, Xinhai Robert Zhang, Shervin Assassi

Abstract:

Summary: This case report presents a woman in her 60s who developed immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) following exposure to semaglutide. The patient experienced progressive symptoms of dysphagia, dysphonia, proximal muscle weakness, and elevated creatine kinase over a period of four months. The onset of her condition followed administration of a single semaglutide dose. Diagnostic evaluation demonstrated findings indicative of necrotising myopathy with inflammatory cell infiltration on muscle biopsy. Initiation of immunosuppressive therapy with corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) led to clinical improvement. Ultimately, she made a full recovery and returned to her usual functional abilities, maintaining remission for several months after discontinuation of immunosuppression and avoidance of further glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists. This represents the first documented instance of IMNM potentially linked to GLP-1 receptor agonist treatment, demonstrating clinical improvement with brief immunosuppressive and IVIG therapy.

Background:

Recently, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have become frequently utilized in the management of metabolic disorders such as type 2 diabetes and obesity. Loss of skeletal muscle mass has been identified as one of the documented adverse effects related to this medication class. A recent report highlighted a case of rhabdomyolysis with necrotizing features connected to tirzepatide, a widely prescribed GLP-1 receptor agonist. The patient involved experienced a creatine kinase (CK) increase reaching the thousands during ongoing therapy, with CK levels beginning to decrease just days after discontinuing the drug, which is indicative of rhabdomyolysis caused by the medication.

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) represents a unique form of myopathy, marked by severe weakness in the proximal muscles, histological evidence of necrotising muscle fibers, and minimal infiltration by lymphocytes. IMNM is frequently linked to the presence of anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (SRP) autoantibodies. Notably, HMGCR autoantibody positivity is strongly correlated with prior statin usage, a medication widely prescribed for metabolic disorders. Previously, multiple individuals on statins developed symptoms consistent with necrotising myopathy, but were often diagnosed with toxic myopathy or polymyositis before the association with HMGCR autoantibodies became known. In such cases, many continued to experience myopathy and elevated creatine kinase (CK) levels even after stopping statins. These persistent findings, followed by a favorable response to immunosuppressive therapy, indicated an underlying autoimmune process, ultimately contributing to the recognition of HMGCR autoantibodies as a defining feature of the condition. However, it should be noted that about one-third of IMNM patients do not exhibit detectable autoantibodies. This seronegative group presents further diagnostic difficulties and implies the existence of other, yet unidentified, autoantibodies and possible causative factors.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 60s with a history of obesity, fatty liver, high blood pressure, hypothyroidism, and past episodes of deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism presented with worsening weakness affecting the proximal muscles, as well as swallowing and speech difficulties.

She had previously been healthy and completely independent in daily tasks. Three years before her decline, she underwent outpatient assessment for elevated liver enzymes. At that point, she had mild increases in aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT), both approximately two times the normal upper limit (with ALT higher than AST). Levels of alkaline phosphatase, albumin, bilirubin, and platelets were normal. She reported no alcohol intake and was not using any medications known to cause liver toxicity. Abdominal ultrasound revealed an enlarged liver with a uniformly increased echotexture, suggestive of steatosis. Testing for viral hepatitis, as well as anti-nuclear, anti-mitochondrial, anti-smooth muscle, and liver-kidney microsomal antibodies, was all negative. Liver biopsy indicated steatohepatitis corresponding to non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) with a NAFLD Activity Score of 4 out of 8, and fibrosis stage 0. The patient was recommended to adhere to a reduced-calorie diet and engage in regular physical activity.

Given ongoing hepatic steatosis and obesity 2.5 years post-NAFLD diagnosis, semaglutide therapy was started. Shortly after her initial administration of semaglutide (0.25 mg subcutaneous), she developed nausea and vomiting. These symptoms did not abate, resulting in hospital admission two days later for persistent nausea, vomiting, diminished oral intake, shortness of breath, acute renal impairment, and marked metabolic acidosis. Management included intravenous fluids and bicarbonate, in addition to anti-emetic medications. She was briefly moved to the intensive care unit to receive non-invasive ventilation. Hydration led to improvement in renal function without requiring haemofiltration or dialysis. Creatine kinase was measured at admission, showing an elevation to 337 U/L, although she exhibited no other complaints such as rash, myalgia, or dark urine. During this hospital stay, paroxysmal atrial fibrillation occurred, but her serum troponin levels remained within normal limits. She was discharged after five days, at which point gastrointestinal symptoms had resolved though she remained physically deconditioned. In the subsequent 4 months, she experienced progressive muscle weakness. She reported no history of statin use, consumption of supplements associated with IMNM—including mushrooms or red yeast rice—or any other nutritional additives. Additionally, she had no significant family history of myopathy, recent prolonged or intense physical activity, nor alcohol or illicit drug intake.

Four months after her initial and sole administration of semaglutide, her condition continued to worsen. She became unable to either stand or remain seated upright without help, and developed dysphagia to both liquids and solids over the preceding several days. Due to these new symptoms, she sought care at an outside hospital, where laboratory evaluation revealed a creatine kinase (CK) level of 3992 U/L (reference range: 12–191 U/L) upon admission. Neurology was consulted via telemedicine, and she received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dosage of 1000 mg daily for five days. Subsequently, she was transferred to a tertiary care facility for rheumatology assessment. At presentation, she was afebrile and normotensive, exhibiting no signs of distress. On physical examination, marked muscle weakness was noted, predominantly affecting the proximal muscle groups (refer to table 1). She did not display any rashes, skin abnormalities, crackles, synovitis, edema, sicca symptoms, oral ulcers, or other neurological deficits.

After receiving pulse methylprednisolone therapy, she was started on oral prednisone at a dose of 1 mg/kg per day. Due to ongoing muscular weakness and pronounced symptoms, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at a total dose of 2 g/kg, divided across 4 days. Over the next two weeks, a significant reduction in creatine kinase (CK) levels was seen (figure 1). She was discharged following nearly 4 weeks of hospitalization. Twelve days post-discharge, she was re-admitted for 10 days with septic shock resulting from a pseudomonal urinary tract infection, during which her CK remained within normal limits.

She had multiple outpatient follow-up appointments, during which her physical abilities and general health continued to improve, and her CK values remained within normal limits. These factors allowed for a swift tapering and discontinuation of prednisone. Owing to both financial considerations and her stable condition after discontinuing semaglutide, she did not undergo any further IVIG treatments beyond the original inpatient course.

At her most recent outpatient visit, conducted 10 months after starting treatment, she had returned to her pre-illness level of function and had remained free from immunosuppressive therapy for 8 months.

Investigations:

Her laboratory assessment revealed an elevated creatine kinase (CK) level of 1237 U/L at the time of evaluation, with a previous maximum value of 3992 U/L documented at the transferring institution one week earlier. Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) was found to be within the normal reference range. Aspartate aminotransferase (AST) measured 191 U/L and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) was 165 U/L, both elevated in the context of increased serum CK and a history of hepatic steatosis. Autoantibody testing showed negative results for anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-Sjogren’s syndrome antibodies type A (anti-SSA), anti-Smith antibodies, anti-U1 ribonucleoprotein (anti-U1 RNP), anti-double-stranded DNA antibodies, anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), and anti-mitochondrial antibodies. Complement levels were within normal limits; renal function (creatinine) was normal, and there were no findings of cytopenia. Myositis-specific antibody panel yielded negative results for signal recognition particle (SRP) and 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR), as well as for Jo-1, Pl-7, Pl-12, EJ, OJ, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-y, NXP-2, and cN-1A autoantibodies. There was a low titer positive SS-B autoantibody of 2.4 (reference range: <1.0). The erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was increased at 22 mm/hour and C-reactive protein was 22.8 mg/L. Of note, she exhibited an elevated high-sensitivity troponin (HS-Troponin) at 62 pg/mL (reference range ≤53 pg/mL) and a raised pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (proBNP) at 4358 pg/mL (reference range: 0–125 pg/mL).

Key imaging studies comprised MRI scans of both femurs, echocardiogram, cardiac MRI, liver ultrasound with Doppler, high-resolution chest CT, and abdominal MRI. MRI of the femurs demonstrated mild, symmetric muscular edema affecting both sides, most pronounced in the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps muscles, along with subcutaneous edema on the lateral regions of the thighs (see figure 2). The echocardiogram reported an estimated left ventricular ejection fraction of 65%–70% and a right ventricular systolic pressure of 46 mm Hg. Cardiac MRI identified mild hypokinesis in the mid-anteroseptal and anterior walls accompanied by myocardial edema, without definitive late gadolinium enhancement, findings which may be attributable to myocardial inflammation or early myocarditis versus focal stress-induced cardiomyopathy. There was no evidence of significant myocardial scar, infarct, or notable valvular disease. Liver ultrasound with Doppler showed hepatosteatosis. High-resolution CT of the chest did not reveal interstitial lung disease or lymphadenopathy. Abdominal MRI indicated steatotic hepatomegaly, but was otherwise unremarkable and did not show lymphadenopathy.

Further advanced diagnostic assessments included both a second liver biopsy and a biopsy of the thigh muscle. The liver biopsy, carried out during her hospital stay, showed several sections of hepatic tissue displaying macrovesicular steatosis—comprising both small and large fat droplets—affecting roughly 70% of the submitted liver parenchyma. No noteworthy portal inflammation or interface hepatitis was observed. These results aligned with her prior diagnosis of hepatosteatosis and indicated mild worsening of her liver condition, now graded as NAFLD Activity Score 5/8, stage 1. The biopsy from the right vastus lateralis muscle revealed scattered necrotic as well as regenerating muscle fibers (figure 3A,B), and immunohistochemistry highlighted acid phosphatase (figure 3D) and cluster of Differentiation-68 (CD68) positivity—a marker of macrophages—with evidence of myophagocytosis (figure 4B). Additionally, staining for membrane attack complex was noted in both necrotic and non-necrotic myofibers (figure 4C), indicating an ongoing inflammatory process. The sample also showed occasional CD3-positive cells, a T-cell marker (figure 4A), and perivascular inflammatory infiltrates (figure 3C). Overall, the muscle biopsy findings supported a diagnosis of IMNM.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient exhibited a pronounced and acute response to her first and only administration of semaglutide, indicating intolerance. She developed gastrointestinal adverse effects along with renal failure, which was presumed to result from hypovolemia. At presentation, her creatine kinase (CK) was only mildly increased, not reaching levels typical of rhabdomyolysis. However, the patient's condition worsened despite discontinuing semaglutide, with her pattern of muscle weakness and a 12-fold rise in CK observed over four months following semaglutide exposure, indicating immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) likely related to the medication. In addition, histopathological findings typical of drug-induced IMNM, absence of signs of concomitant connective tissue disease, and lasting clinical improvement following the initial course of immunosuppressive therapy and withdrawal of semaglutide further reinforce this possible link.

It is worth highlighting that elevated transaminases were present before she began taking semaglutide, a finding commonly observed in myopathic conditions. Nonetheless, a liver biopsy conducted three years prior to semaglutide initiation confirmed hepatosteatosis, and her marginally raised AST and ALT values persisted even after normalization of CK levels. Therefore, it appears her chronically increased transaminases likely stem from hepatic causes rather than muscle injury. Her cardiac assessment did not provide definitive evidence of ongoing myocarditis. Considering the clinical circumstances and her swift improvement, stress-related myopathy would also be a plausible diagnosis.

The finding of SS-B antibody positivity alongside negative ANA and SS-A was unanticipated. To date, the literature does not report cases of isolated SS-B antibody positivity with concurrent negative ANA and SS-A resulting in IMNM. We were unable to obtain a CK value predating semaglutide therapy, making it impossible to completely rule out an underlying, subclinical autoimmune IMNM prior to starting semaglutide. Nevertheless, the observed twelvefold elevation in CK and the onset of progressive proximal muscle weakness after initiating semaglutide support the likelihood that this IMNM episode was related to medication use. Moreover, the patient's severe IMNM improved following a brief course of corticosteroids and IVIG, as well as discontinuation of semaglutide. This clinical trajectory is consistent with drug-induced IMNM, which typically responds to short-term immunosuppression once the causative drug is withdrawn, rather than with idiopathic IMNM, which frequently necessitates prolonged, aggressive immunosuppressive treatment.

Treatment:

She received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for 5 days, which was then transitioned to oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg per day. Ongoing significant weakness and pronounced symptoms prompted administration of IVIG at 2 g/kg, divided over 4 days. Subsequent outpatient follow-up visits revealed ongoing enhancements in her functionality and general well-being, with creatine kinase (CK) values remaining consistently within normal limits. As a result, her prednisone was able to be tapered quickly and discontinued within 2 months. Owing to both financial constraints and her stable improvement after discontinuation of semaglutide, she did not require any further IVIG beyond the initial regimen administered during her inpatient stay.

Outcome and follow-up:

At her most recent follow-up in the outpatient clinic, which occurred 10 months after the start of her evaluation and therapy, she had returned to her usual level of function and had not been on immunosuppressive agents for the preceding 7 months. Since her hospital admission and immunosuppressive treatment, her CK values had stayed within normal limits. Subsequent echocardiogram and cardiac MRI demonstrated resolution of earlier abnormalities. She also had no further episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation.

Discussion:

GLP-1 receptor agonists, including semaglutide, are increasingly utilized and have shown efficacy in treating metabolic conditions such as obesity and type 2 diabetes. Gastrointestinal side effects are frequently observed, but these agents might also contribute to reduced muscle mass and, in some instances, rhabdomyolysis. In this report, the patient’s progression aligns more closely with IMNM than with rhabdomyolysis. After a single administration of semaglutide, the patient’s CK rose twelve-fold over a period of four months; this is inconsistent with the rapid CK normalization generally expected if semaglutide were causing a direct toxic effect, as observed in drug-induced rhabdomyolysis. For example, a previous case of rhabdomyolysis attributed to tirzepatide exhibited the typical trajectory: the patient presented with a significant acute rise in CK (CK=57 014), and CK levels swiftly improved following drug discontinuation and hydration within days, eliminating the necessity for immunosuppressive therapy.

Based on the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) Causality Categories and Criteria, semaglutide is likely associated with the development of IMNM in our patient, owing to an appropriate temporal association, the unlikelihood that other diseases or medications contributed to IMNM, and a clinically justified improvement following discontinuation. Rechallenge was considered inappropriate because of the severity of the adverse reaction attributed to semaglutide. Nonetheless, we recognize that definitive causality has not been established in this instance. Given the rapidly increasing use of semaglutide, this report seeks to alert clinicians to this possible side effect and promote improved pharmacovigilance. Further investigation will determine if this relationship is genuine or merely coincidental.

Additionally, the underlying factors that might have predisposed our patient to this pronounced response to semaglutide remain uncertain. As GLP-1 receptor agonists continue to gain popularity, research into potential predisposing factors will be essential, especially if additional cases with a close temporal association to semaglutide are observed in the future.
