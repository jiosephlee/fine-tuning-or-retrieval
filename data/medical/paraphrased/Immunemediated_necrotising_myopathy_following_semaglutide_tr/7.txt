Title: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy after semaglutide therapy: potential involvement?

Brian Lam, Eleni Tiniakou, Xinhai Robert Zhang, Shervin Assassi

Abstract:

Summary: This case report details a woman in her 60s who developed immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) following exposure to semaglutide. The patient experienced worsening dysphagia, dysphonia, proximal muscle weakness, and elevated creatine kinase over a period of four months, with symptom onset occurring after administration of a single semaglutide dose. Diagnostic evaluation identified biopsy findings of necrotising myopathy accompanied by inflammatory infiltrates. The patient exhibited clinical improvement after receiving immunosuppressive therapy, specifically corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG). Over time, she was able to regain her previous level of function and remained stable for several months after discontinuing immunosuppression, as long as she continued to avoid glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists. This report presents the first documented instance of IMNM likely linked to GLP-1 receptor agonist administration, demonstrating resolution with brief immunosuppression and IVIG treatment.

Background:

Over the past several years, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have been widely prescribed for managing metabolic disorders such as type 2 diabetes and obesity. Notably, a reduction in skeletal muscle mass has been reported as a side effect linked to this medication group. Additionally, there has been a recent case report documenting rhabdomyolysis with necrotising characteristics in connection with tirzepatide, another widely utilized GLP-1 receptor agonist. In that instance, the patient experienced a marked elevation of creatine kinase (CK) into the thousands during therapy, and this elevation began to resolve within a few days following the cessation of the drug—findings that align with drug-induced rhabdomyolysis.

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) represents a unique form of myopathy, typically presenting with rapidly progressive proximal muscle weakness, histopathologically characterised by extensive myofibre necrosis and minimal lymphocytic infiltration. The autoantibodies anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (SRP) exhibit strong associations with IMNM. Statins, widely prescribed for metabolic conditions, show a particularly robust correlation with the presence of HMGCR autoantibodies. Before the connection between HMGCR autoantibodies and IMNM was established, patients manifesting statin-induced necrotising myopathy were frequently misdiagnosed as having toxic myopathy or polymyositis. Importantly, many such patients continued to experience elevated creatine kinase levels and ongoing muscle weakness even after discontinuing statin therapy. The subsequent clinical response to immunosuppressive treatment further indicated an underlying autoimmune mechanism, ultimately contributing to the recognition of HMGCR autoantibodies as a defining feature of this disease entity. Nevertheless, it should be highlighted that approximately one-third of individuals with IMNM lack detectable autoantibodies (i.e., are seronegative). The absence of known autoantibodies in these cases poses diagnostic difficulties and implies the existence of as yet unidentified autoantibodies and additional etiological factors.

Case presentation:

A woman in her sixth decade of life, whose past medical history includes obesity, hepatosteatosis, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and a prior episode of deep venous thrombosis and pulmonary embolism, was admitted with worsening proximal muscle weakness accompanied by dysphagia and dysphonia.

The patient had enjoyed prior robust health and was entirely self-sufficient in managing her daily tasks. Three years before her condition worsened, she was being evaluated on an outpatient basis due to elevated transaminases. At that juncture, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels were modestly raised—both at roughly double the upper limit of normal, with ALT exceeding AST. Laboratory assessments revealed that alkaline phosphatase, albumin, bilirubin, and platelet values fell within reference intervals. She reported no history of alcohol consumption and was not using medications known to induce liver toxicity. Imaging with abdominal ultrasound identified hepatomegaly with globally increased echogenicity, findings that favored a diagnosis of hepatic steatosis. Diagnostic workup for viral hepatitis as well as testing for anti-nuclear, anti-mitochondrial, anti-smooth muscle, and liver-kidney microsomal antibodies yielded negative results. Histopathological analysis of a liver biopsy indicated steatohepatitis compatible with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), with a NAFLD Activity Score of 4/8 and no evidence of fibrosis (stage 0). She was counseled to adopt a hypocaloric diet in conjunction with regular physical activity.

Following a sustained course of fatty liver and obesity for 2.5 years after being diagnosed with NAFLD, the patient commenced semaglutide therapy. Shortly after her initial subcutaneous injection of semaglutide (0.25 mg), she developed pronounced nausea and vomiting. These symptoms did not resolve, resulting in hospital admission two days later for ongoing nausea, vomiting, inadequate oral intake, shortness of breath, acute renal failure, and pronounced metabolic acidosis. Management included intravenous fluids, bicarbonate administration, and anti-emetic medications. A temporary transfer to the intensive care unit was required to provide non-invasive ventilatory support. Her renal impairment improved with fluid resuscitation, and there was no requirement for haemofiltration or haemodialysis. Laboratory evaluation at admission revealed an elevated CK level of 337 U/L, though she did not exhibit any additional symptoms such as rash, myalgia, or dark (cola-coloured) urine. During the hospitalisation, she experienced an episode of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation; however, her troponin concentrations remained within normal limits. After a five-day stay, she was discharged with complete resolution of her gastrointestinal complaints, though she remained physically deconditioned. In the months that followed, she reported progressive muscle weakness. She reported no prior use of statins, did not ingest mushrooms, red yeast rice, or other supplements linked with IMNM, nor did she use any nutritional supplements. She also denied a significant family history of muscle disease, excessive or unusually prolonged exercise, and the use of alcohol or illicit substances.

Four months following administration of her first and only semaglutide dose, the patient's condition progressively worsened. She became unable to either stand or sit upright without external support and developed dysphagia to both solids and liquids over several days. As a result, she sought care at an external hospital, where laboratory evaluation revealed a creatine kinase (CK) level of 3992 U/L (reference interval: 12–191 U/L) upon admission. Neurology provided telemedicine consultation, and she received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dosage of 1000 mg daily for a period of five days. Subsequently, she was transferred to a tertiary care facility for further rheumatologic assessment. On arrival, her vital signs were within normal limits—she was afebrile, normotensive, and displayed no acute distress. Physical examination identified marked proximal muscle weakness (see table 1). No dermatologic abnormalities, pulmonary crackles, signs of synovitis, edema, sicca symptoms, oral ulcers, or additional neurological impairments were observed.

After receiving pulse methylprednisolone, the patient commenced a regimen of oral prednisone at a daily dose of 1 mg/kg. Owing to ongoing muscle weakness and pronounced symptoms, she was administered intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) at a total dose of 2 g/kg over 4 days. Her creatine kinase (CK) levels demonstrated significant improvement within a 2-week period (see figure 1). She was discharged following an inpatient stay of nearly 4 weeks. However, 12 days post-discharge, she required readmission for 10 days due to septic shock secondary to a pseudomonal urinary tract infection, at which time her CK levels remained within the normal range.

She attended multiple outpatient follow-up appointments, during which her functional abilities and general well-being kept improving, with consistently normal CK readings. As a result, prednisone was tapered off quickly without issue. Considering both financial factors and her sustained progress after discontinuing semaglutide, she did not undergo further IVIG therapy beyond the initial inpatient administration.

Ten months following her initial assessment and commencement of therapy, she was most recently evaluated in the outpatient clinic, where she had returned to her previous level of functioning and had been free from immunosuppressive medications for the preceding eight months.

Investigations:

Her laboratory assessment revealed an elevated creatine kinase (CK) level of 1237 U/L at the time of evaluation, with a maximum value of 3992 U/L documented one week earlier at the referring institution. Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) was found to be within the reference range. Aspartate transaminase (AST) and alanine transaminase (ALT) were recorded at 191 U/L and 165 U/L, respectively, in the context of increased serum CK and a known history of hepatic steatosis. The autoantibody screen was remarkable for negative results in anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-Sjogren’s syndrome antibodies type A (anti-SSA), anti-Smith antibodies, anti-U1 ribonucleoprotein (anti-U1 RNP), anti-double-stranded DNA, antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), and anti-mitochondrial antibodies. Complement levels and creatinine were both unremarkable, and there were no signs of cytopenias. Her myositis-specific antibody panel was negative for SRP and HMGCR, as well as Jo-1, Pl-7, Pl-12, EJ, OJ, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-y, NXP-2, and cN-1A autoantibodies. The only positive serologic finding was a low-titre SS-B autoantibody measured at 2.4 (reference: <1.0). Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was increased at 22 mm/hour, as was C-reactive protein (CRP) at 22.8 mg/L. Of note, she also demonstrated an elevated high-sensitivity troponin (HS-Troponin) at 62 pg/mL (reference range ≤53 pg/mL), along with a pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (proBNP) of 4358 pg/mL (reference interval: 0–125 pg/mL).

Significant imaging studies included MRI scans of both femurs, echocardiography, cardiac MRI, Doppler liver ultrasound, high-resolution CT of the chest, and abdominal MRI. The MRI of the bilateral femurs identified mild and symmetric edema within both thighs’ musculature, most pronounced in the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps muscles, along with subcutaneous edema along the lateral sides of the thighs (figure 2). Echocardiography revealed a left ventricular ejection fraction estimated at 65%–70% and a right ventricular systolic pressure measuring 46 mm Hg. Cardiac MRI demonstrated mild hypokinesis of the mid-anteroseptal and anterior walls with evidence of myocardial edema, but did not reveal any definitive late gadolinium enhancement; these findings may suggest myocardial inflammation or early myocarditis, or perhaps localized stress-induced cardiomyopathy. There was no appreciable myocardial infarction or significant scar tissue, and valvular function was preserved. The liver ultrasound with Doppler indicated the presence of hepatic steatosis. High-resolution CT imaging of the chest was unremarkable for interstitial lung disease or lymphadenopathy. Abdominal MRI revealed hepatomegaly due to steatosis but was otherwise normal, without any lymphadenopathy.

Additional diagnostic evaluations included a second liver biopsy as well as a biopsy of the thigh musculature. The liver biopsy, conducted during hospitalization, revealed multiple fragments of hepatic tissue exhibiting macrovesicular steatosis, both micro- and macro-droplet forms, involving approximately 70% of the examined liver parenchyma. Importantly, there was no notable portal inflammatory infiltrate or interface hepatitis present. These histopathological features aligned with the patient’s established diagnosis of hepatosteatosis, but also indicated a mild progression to a NAFLD Activity Score of grade 5/8 and stage 1. Examination of a biopsy sample from the right vastus lateralis muscle uncovered scattered muscle fiber necrosis and regeneration (figure 3A,B). Immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated positivity for acid phosphatase (figure 3D) and cluster of Differentiation-68 (CD68), a marker for macrophages, alongside evidence of myophagocytosis (figure 4B). Further staining revealed deposition of the membrane attack complex on both necrotic and non-necrotic muscle fibers (figure 4C), indicative of an underlying inflammatory process. Scattered CD3-positive T cells (figure 4A) and inflammatory cells around blood vessels (figure 3C) were also noted. Collectively, the muscle biopsy results supported the diagnosis of IMNM.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient demonstrated a rapid and pronounced response to her first and only administration of semaglutide, indicating possible intolerance. She initially presented with gastrointestinal disturbances and developed renal failure, presumed to be due to hypovolaemia. Although her creatine kinase (CK) was only mildly elevated and did not align with the criteria for rhabdomyolysis at presentation, her progressive deterioration in the absence of further semaglutide exposure, along with the pattern of muscle weakness and a 12-fold escalation in CK levels within four months post-semiglutide, are indicative of an IMNM likely related to semaglutide. Additionally, histopathological findings consistent with drug-induced IMNM, absence of signs characteristic of an underlying connective tissue disorder, and ongoing clinical improvement following both withdrawal of semaglutide and the initiation of immunosuppressive therapy, lend further credence to this link.

It is noteworthy that the patient exhibited elevated transaminase levels before initiating semaglutide therapy, a feature sometimes observed in myopathic conditions. Nonetheless, a liver biopsy performed three years before commencing semaglutide confirmed hepatosteatosis, and the mild increase in AST/ALT remained even after normalization of CK concentrations. This suggests that her chronically raised transaminases are most plausibly attributed to hepatic etiology rather than muscle injury. Additionally, her cardiac assessment did not provide definitive evidence for active myocarditis. Considering her clinical course and swift recovery, a stress-induced myopathy also represents a plausible explanation in this context.

The presence of a positive SS-B antibody, despite negative ANA and SS-A results, was unanticipated. To our knowledge, no literature exists documenting cases of isolated SS-B antibody positivity in conjunction with negative ANA and SS-A, culminating in IMNM. A baseline CK value prior to the administration of semaglutide was regrettably unavailable, making it impossible to rule out the possibility of a subclinical autoimmune IMNM preceding semaglutide exposure. Nevertheless, the marked twelve-fold elevation in CK and the gradual development of proximal muscle weakness following semaglutide use suggest that the onset of IMNM in this case was likely medication-associated. Moreover, the patient's significant IMNM rapidly resolved after a brief course of corticosteroids and IVIG, in combination with discontinuation of semaglutide. This trajectory is more consistent with drug-induced IMNM, which often shows resolution following short-term immunosuppressive therapy and cessation of the trigger, as opposed to idiopathic IMNM, which typically necessitates extended and more aggressive immunosuppressive management.

Treatment:

The patient received 1000 mg of intravenous methylprednisolone daily for 5 days, after which she transitioned to oral prednisone at a dose of 1 mg/kg per day. Owing to ongoing weakness and pronounced symptoms, IVIG was administered at a total dose of 2 g/kg, distributed over 4 days. Subsequent follow-up visits in the outpatient setting showed consistent enhancements in her functional abilities and general health, along with continuously normal CK levels. These factors facilitated a successful and relatively swift tapering of prednisone, completed within 2 months. Given the cost implications and her stable improvement without semaglutide, no further IVIG was administered after her initial hospital course.

Outcome and follow-up:

At her most recent follow-up, which occurred 10 months after the initial assessment and commencement of therapy, the patient had returned to her previous level of functioning and had been off immunosuppressive medication for 7 months. Her CK levels continued to be stable and within the normal limits after her hospitalization and immunosuppressive therapy. Repeat echocardiogram and cardiac MRI demonstrated resolution of earlier detected abnormalities. Furthermore, she had no further episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation.

Discussion:

GLP-1 receptor agonists—including semaglutide—are increasingly prescribed and have shown efficacy in treating metabolic conditions like obesity and type 2 diabetes. While gastrointestinal side effects are commonly reported, there is also some evidence linking these agents to reductions in muscle mass and, in rare cases, rhabdomyolysis. In the current case, however, the patient’s progression aligns more closely with IMNM rather than rhabdomyolysis. Specifically, creatine kinase (CK) concentrations rose 12-fold across four months after a single semaglutide dose; in contrast, if semaglutide’s direct toxicity were responsible—as observed in other instances of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis—CK levels would typically return to baseline within a few weeks. Furthermore, a prior report of rhabdomyolysis with tirzepatide exhibited the classic clinical progression: the patient had a dramatic acute spike in CK (CK=57 014 at presentation) and then showed rapid clinical and biochemical improvement within days of drug discontinuation and hydration, with no need for immunosuppressive treatment.

According to the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) Causality Categories and Criteria, the association between semaglutide administration and IMNM in this instance is best classified as probable. This assessment is based on the presence of an appropriate temporal association, the low likelihood that other illnesses or medications are responsible for the IMNM, and the observed improvement upon discontinuation of semaglutide. A rechallenge was not performed due to the severity of the adverse reaction and associated ethical concerns. Nonetheless, definitive causality cannot be established in this case. Given the rapidly increasing use of semaglutide, this case report seeks to alert clinicians to this possible adverse reaction and to underscore the importance of ongoing pharmacovigilance. Additional research will be necessary to determine whether this relationship is causal or merely coincidental.

The factors that may have contributed to our patient’s pronounced response to semaglutide are still not well understood. As the use of GLP-1 receptor agonists continues to increase, it will be crucial for future research to investigate potential predisposing factors, especially if additional incidents emerge that occur shortly after semaglutide administration.
