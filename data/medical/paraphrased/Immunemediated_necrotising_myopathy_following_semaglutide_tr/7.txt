Title: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

Brian Lam, Eleni Tiniakou, Xinhai Robert Zhang, Shervin Assassi

Abstract:

Summary: This case details a woman in her 60s who developed immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) following exposure to semaglutide. She experienced progressively worsening dysphagia, dysphonia, proximal muscle weakness, and elevated creatine kinase levels over a period of four months, symptoms that began after she received a single dose of semaglutide. Diagnostic evaluation revealed evidence of necrotising myopathy, including inflammatory infiltrates observed on muscle biopsy. The patient demonstrated clinical improvement following treatment with immunosuppressive therapy, specifically corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG). Ultimately, she was able to regain her previous level of functioning after discontinuing immunosuppression for several months and continuing to avoid glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists. This report documents what appears to be the first case of IMNM likely linked to GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy, with favorable response to a brief period of immunosuppression and IVIG.

Background:

Over the past few years, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have become widely utilized in the management of metabolic conditions such as type 2 diabetes and obesity. One notable adverse event associated with these agents is a reduction in skeletal muscle mass. A recent case report has documented rhabdomyolysis exhibiting necrotising characteristics linked to tirzepatide, a GLP-1 receptor agonist frequently prescribed. In that report, the patient experienced a marked increase in creatine kinase (CK) levels, reaching the thousands, during ongoing therapy. After cessation of the drug, the CK values began to decrease within several days, aligning with a drug-induced etiology for rhabdomyolysis.

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) represents a specific type of muscle disease, marked by rapid onset proximal muscle weakness, necrotic muscle fibers, and minimal lymphocytic infiltration observed on histological examination. IMNM is strongly linked to autoantibodies such as anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (SRP). Statin therapy, commonly prescribed for metabolic disorders, has a pronounced correlation with HMGCR autoantibody positivity. Historically, multiple individuals treated with statins developed symptoms characteristic of necrotising myopathy and were typically diagnosed as having toxic myopathy or polymyositis before the connection with HMGCR autoantibodies was established. Many of these patients exhibited ongoing myopathic symptoms and elevated creatine kinase (CK) levels even after discontinuing statins. These clinical features, alongside positive responses to immunosuppressive therapy, pointed toward an autoimmune etiology, leading to the recognition of HMGCR autoantibody as a defining feature of this condition. It is important to note that approximately one-third of IMNM cases are seronegative. The absence of autoantibodies in this group introduces further challenges to diagnosis and indicates that additional related autoantibodies and possible precipitating factors remain unidentified.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 60s with a background of obesity, hepatic steatosis, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and a distant history of deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism presented with gradually worsening proximal muscle weakness, as well as difficulty swallowing and speaking.

Prior to her current decline, she had enjoyed good health and was fully capable of performing her daily activities independently. Three years before her symptoms worsened, she was being evaluated as an outpatient for elevated liver transaminases. At that time, her aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels were mildly raised, each about two times the upper limit of normal, with ALT exceeding AST. Her alkaline phosphatase, albumin, bilirubin, and platelet levels were all within normal ranges. She reported no alcohol consumption and was not using any potentially hepatotoxic drugs. An abdominal ultrasound detected enlarged liver with diffusely increased echogenicity, suggestive of hepatic steatosis. Screening for viral hepatitis and autoimmune liver disease, including anti-nuclear, anti-mitochondrial, anti-smooth muscle, and liver-kidney microsomal antibodies, yielded negative results. Liver biopsy demonstrated steatohepatitis in keeping with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) and an NAFLD Activity Score of 4/8, with stage 0 fibrosis. She received guidance to maintain a low-calorie diet and to engage in regular physical activity.

Because of ongoing fatty liver disease and obesity 2.5 years following her NAFLD diagnosis, semaglutide was started. A few hours after the initial subcutaneous dose (0.25 mg), she developed nausea and vomiting. These symptoms continued, resulting in hospital admission two days later with complaints of nausea, vomiting, poor oral intake, dyspnoea, acute renal failure, and pronounced metabolic acidosis. Management included intravenous fluids, bicarbonate, and anti-emetic therapy. She required a brief transfer to the intensive care unit for non-invasive ventilatory support. Hydration led to improvement in her renal parameters, and there was no necessity for haemofiltration or dialysis. Her admission creatine kinase (CK) was elevated at 337 U/L. She did not report associated manifestations such as rash, muscle soreness, or dark (cola-coloured) urine. During the hospitalization, she developed episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, though her serum troponin was not elevated. Discharge occurred after a 5-day stay; by then, gastrointestinal symptoms had abated, but she remained deconditioned. Over the next 4 months, she experienced increasing weakness. She denied previous statin therapy, significant intake of other substances associated with IMNM such as mushrooms or red yeast rice, or any other dietary supplements. She also reported no relevant family history of myopathy, no recent intense or extended physical exertion, and no alcohol or recreational drug use.

Four months after receiving her initial and only administration of semaglutide, her condition continued to deteriorate. She was unable to stand or sit without support, and for several days experienced increasing difficulty swallowing both liquids and solids. This led her to seek care at a different hospital, where her creatine kinase (CK) was found to be elevated at 3992 U/L (normal reference: 12–191 U/L) upon admission. Neurology was consulted through telemedicine, and she was given intravenous methylprednisolone at 1000 mg daily for five days. Subsequently, she was transferred to a tertiary care facility for evaluation by rheumatology. Upon arrival, she was afebrile, had normal blood pressure, and appeared comfortable overall. Examination demonstrated pronounced weakness, most notable in proximal muscles (see table 1). There were no skin rashes, changes in skin appearance, lung crackles, signs of synovitis, oedema, sicca symptoms, oral ulcers, or any additional neurological abnormalities.

After receiving pulse methylprednisolone, the patient was started on oral prednisone at a dose of 1 mg/kg per day. She also received intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) at 2 g/kg, administered over 4 days, because of ongoing weakness and pronounced symptoms. Over a period of two weeks, her CK levels showed significant improvement (see figure 1). She was released following an almost 4-week stay in the hospital. Twelve days post-discharge, she was readmitted for 10 days with septic shock caused by a pseudomonal urinary tract infection, during which her CK was noted to be within the normal range.

She attended multiple outpatient follow-up visits, during which her functional capacity and general health steadily improved, and her CK levels remained within normal limits. This allowed for a rapid discontinuation of prednisone. Considering both financial constraints and her favorable response after discontinuing semaglutide, she received no further IVIG beyond the initial hospitalization course.

At her most recent outpatient appointment, conducted 10 months following the start of therapy, she had fully returned to her baseline level of function and had been without immunosuppressive therapy for 8 months.

Investigations:

Her laboratory findings revealed an elevated CK of 1237 U/L at the time of assessment, with a maximum value of 3992 U/L recorded one week earlier at the referring facility. TSH levels were within the normal range. In the context of increased serum CK and a known history of hepatic steatosis, AST measured 191 U/L and ALT 165 U/L. The autoantibody profile showed negative results for anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-Sjogren’s syndrome antibodies type A (anti-SSA), anti-Smith antibodies, anti-U1 ribonucleoprotein antibodies (anti-U1 RNP), anti-double-stranded DNA, ANCA, and anti-mitochondrial antibodies. Complement levels and creatinine were normal, and there were no signs of cytopenia. A myositis panel yielded negative results for SRP, HMGCR, as well as Jo-1, Pl-7, Pl-12, EJ, OJ, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-y, NXP-2, and cN-1A autoantibodies. However, SS-B autoantibody was detected at a low titer of 2.4 (normal: <1.0). Inflammatory markers were raised, with ESR at 22 mm/hour and C-reactive protein at 22.8 mg/L. Additionally, HS-Troponin was elevated at 62 pg/mL (reference: ≤53 pg/mL), and proBNP was 4358 pg/mL (reference range: 0–125 pg/mL).

Key imaging studies included MRI scans of both femurs, echocardiography, cardiac MRI, liver ultrasound with Doppler, high-resolution chest CT, and abdominal MRI. The MRI of both femurs demonstrated mild, symmetric edema within the muscles bilaterally, most pronounced in the proximal regions of the vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps, alongside subcutaneous edema located on the lateral aspects of both thighs (figure 2). Echocardiographic findings included an estimated left ventricular ejection fraction between 65% and 70%, as well as a right ventricular systolic pressure measured at 46 mm Hg. Cardiac MRI identified mild hypokinesis involving the mid-anteroseptal and anterior walls, accompanied by myocardial edema, without clear late gadolinium enhancement; these findings could correspond to myocardial inflammation or early myocarditis, or possibly focal stress-induced cardiomyopathy. There was no evidence of significant myocardial scarring, infarction, or substantial valvular abnormalities. Doppler liver ultrasound indicated the presence of hepatosteatosis. High-resolution chest CT revealed no interstitial lung disease or lymphadenopathy. Abdominal MRI showed steatotic hepatomegaly but was otherwise unremarkable and did not show any lymphadenopathy.

Additional diagnostic evaluations included a second liver biopsy and a biopsy of the thigh muscle. The liver biopsy obtained during the hospital stay revealed multiple fragments of hepatic tissue, showing both small and large droplet macrovesicular steatosis covering approximately 70% of the sampled liver parenchyma, without significant portal inflammation or interface hepatitis. These results were in line with her prior diagnosis of hepatosteatosis, now showing mild progression with a NAFLD Activity Score of 5 out of 8 and a stage 1 classification. Biopsy of the right vastus lateralis muscle identified scattered necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers (figure 3A,B), and immunohistochemical staining demonstrated acid phosphatase (figure 3D) and cluster of Differentiation-68 (CD68), a marker for macrophages, along with evidence of myophagocytosis (figure 4B). The presence of membrane attack complex positive staining on both necrotic and non-necrotic muscle fibers (figure 4C) indicated an ongoing inflammatory reaction. There were also infrequent CD3-positive cells, indicating T-lymphocytes (figure 4A), as well as perivascular inflammatory cells (figure 3C). Overall, these muscle biopsy results were diagnostic for immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM).

Differential diagnosis:

The patient exhibited a pronounced and rapid response following her first and only administration of semaglutide, indicating possible intolerance. She initially developed gastrointestinal symptoms along with renal failure, likely due to hypovolemia. Although her creatine kinase (CK) was only mildly increased and did not fit rhabdomyolysis upon presentation, her progressive deterioration off semaglutide, the pattern of muscle weakness, and a twelvefold rise in CK over four months after semaglutide exposure point toward an immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) potentially linked to semaglutide. Additionally, biopsy findings consistent with drug-induced IMNM, absence of other signs of connective tissue disease, and continued improvement after immunosuppressive therapy and withdrawal of semaglutide, further reinforce this connection.

It is significant that elevated transaminase levels were present before she began semaglutide therapy, a feature observed in some myopathies. Nonetheless, a liver biopsy conducted three years prior to starting semaglutide confirmed hepatosteatosis, and her mild increases in AST and ALT remained after CK levels returned to normal. Therefore, her chronic transaminase elevations likely stem from a hepatic cause rather than muscle injury. Additionally, cardiac evaluation did not definitively demonstrate active myocarditis. In this clinical context, stress-induced myopathy would also be a plausible consideration, particularly given her swift improvement.

Her isolated positivity for SS-B antibody, despite negative ANA and SS-A, was unanticipated. No literature exists documenting IMNM associated with SS-B positivity alone in the absence of ANA and SS-A. Regrettably, we were not able to obtain a CK value prior to the start of semaglutide therapy, so it remains impossible to fully rule out a preexisting, slow-progressing autoimmune IMNM. Nonetheless, the twelvefold increase in CK as well as the worsening proximal muscle weakness subsequent to semaglutide exposure suggest that the IMNM episode was likely triggered by the medication. Moreover, the patient's marked IMNM improved with a brief regimen of corticosteroids and IVIG in addition to discontinuation of semaglutide. This sequence is more consistent with drug-induced IMNM, which often responds to short-term immunosuppression following cessation of the causative agent, in contrast to idiopathic IMNM, which typically necessitates extended and aggressive immunosuppressive treatment.

Treatment:

She received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for 5 days, after which oral prednisone was started at 1 mg/kg per day. Because of ongoing weakness and pronounced clinical symptoms, IVIG was administered at a total dose of 2 g/kg, divided over 4 days. The patient was monitored regularly in outpatient clinic visits, during which she showed continuous gains in both her functional ability and overall well-being, and her CK levels remained within the normal range. Owing to this progress, prednisone was tapered and discontinued over a 2-month period. Since she experienced continued clinical benefit after stopping semaglutide and for reasons related to cost, further IVIG beyond the initial inpatient regimen was not provided.

Outcome and follow-up:

Ten months after her initial assessment and the start of therapy, she returned to the outpatient clinic, having recovered her baseline functional abilities and had been without immunosuppressive therapy for seven months. Her creatine kinase values stayed within normal limits after hospital discharge and immunosuppressive treatment. Follow-up echocardiogram and cardiac MRI demonstrated resolution of earlier abnormal findings. Additionally, there was no recurrence of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation.

Discussion:

GLP-1 receptor agonists, including semaglutide, are increasingly utilized and have shown efficacy in treating metabolic conditions such as obesity and type 2 diabetes. Although gastrointestinal side effects are most frequently observed, these agents can also be linked to reductions in muscle mass and, in some cases, rhabdomyolysis. In this report, the patient’s progression aligns more closely with IMNM rather than rhabdomyolysis. The patient's CK rose 12-fold over a period of 4 months after a single administration of semaglutide; if semaglutide toxicity was responsible, CK levels would be expected to return to normal within several weeks, as documented in other instances of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis. Of interest, a previously documented case of tirzepatide-induced rhabdomyolysis exhibited the standard clinical trajectory: a significant and acute spike in CK (CK=57 014 at the time of presentation) that resolved swiftly following cessation of the medication and hydration over a few days, without immunosuppressive therapy.

According to the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) Causality Categories and Criteria, there is a likely association between semaglutide administration and IMNM in our patient, as the temporal correlation is appropriate, other potential causes or medications are unlikely explanations for IMNM, and symptom improvement was observed following discontinuation of semaglutide. A rechallenge with semaglutide would not be ethically permissible due to the serious adverse event experienced. Nonetheless, we recognize that this report does not establish definitive causality. With the rapid increase in semaglutide use, our intent is to alert clinicians to this potential complication and to encourage careful monitoring for adverse reactions. Further research is necessary to determine whether this link is causal or coincidental.

In addition, the factors contributing to our patient’s pronounced response to semaglutide remain unknown. As GLP-1 receptor agonists are increasingly utilized, future investigations into susceptibility factors will become particularly important, especially if additional cases arise with a similar temporal association to semaglutide.
