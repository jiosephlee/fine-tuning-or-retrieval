Title: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

Brian Lam, Eleni Tiniakou, Xinhai Robert Zhang, Shervin Assassi

Abstract:

Summary: This case report outlines a woman in her sixties who developed immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) following administration of semaglutide. Over a four-month period, she experienced worsening dysphagia, dysphonia, proximal muscle weakness, and elevated creatine kinase. Her symptoms began after a single dose of semaglutide. Diagnostic evaluation showed necrotising myopathy with inflammatory cell infiltration on muscle biopsy. Treatment with corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) resulted in improvement. She subsequently returned to her previous level of functioning, remained off immunosuppressive therapy for several months, and continued to avoid glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists. This report presents the first known case of IMNM likely linked to GLP-1 receptor agonist exposure, demonstrating a favorable response to a brief immunosuppressive regimen and IVIG.

Background:

Over the past few years, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have been frequently utilized in the management of metabolic disorders such as type 2 diabetes and obesity. One of the adverse reactions linked to this class includes a reduction in skeletal muscle mass. A recent case report documented rhabdomyolysis with necrotizing characteristics connected to tirzepatide, a widely prescribed GLP-1 receptor agonist. In this instance, the patient presented with significantly elevated creatine kinase (CK) levels, reaching into the thousands, during ongoing therapy, and the CK values began to decline within several days following discontinuation of the medication, aligning with a diagnosis of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis.

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) represents a unique form of myopathy, distinguished by rapidly progressive weakness affecting proximal muscles and histologically marked by necrotising muscle fibres with minimal lymphocytic infiltration. Two autoantibodies, anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (SRP), demonstrate a strong association with IMNM. Statins, frequently prescribed for metabolic conditions, have been linked closely with the presence of HMGCR autoantibodies. Prior to the identification of the relationship with HMGCR autoantibodies, several patients on statins exhibited necrotising myopathy characteristics and were commonly misclassified as having toxic myopathy or polymyositis. Notably, in these individuals, muscle weakness and elevated CK persisted even after discontinuation of statin therapy. Subsequent improvement following treatment with immunosuppressive agents pointed toward an autoimmune etiology, which ultimately led to HMGCR autoantibody recognition as a defining feature of the condition. However, it should be emphasized that approximately one-third of IMNM patients do not exhibit detectable autoantibodies. The absence of an autoantibody in this cohort presents further diagnostic obstacles and implies the existence of unidentified autoantibodies and precipitating factors.

Case presentation:

A woman in her sixties, whose past medical history included obesity, fatty liver disease, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and a previous episode of deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism, presented with worsening weakness affecting the proximal muscles, difficulty swallowing, and impaired voice.

Prior to the onset of her symptoms, she was generally healthy and was able to perform all daily activities independently. Three years before her current decline, she was evaluated in the outpatient setting due to elevated liver transaminases. At that time, laboratory tests revealed mildly increased levels of aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT), about twice the upper limit of normal with ALT being higher than AST. Alkaline phosphatase, albumin, bilirubin, and platelet counts remained within normal values. She reported no alcohol consumption and was not taking medications known to be hepatotoxic. Abdominal ultrasound revealed an enlarged liver with diffusely increased echogenicity suggestive of steatosis. Results for viral hepatitis serologies, anti-nuclear antibody, anti-mitochondrial antibody, anti-smooth muscle antibody, and liver-kidney microsomal antibody testing were all negative. Liver biopsy findings demonstrated steatohepatitis characteristic of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), with a NAFLD Activity Score of 4 out of 8 and stage 0. She was counseled to adopt a calorie-restricted diet and maintain regular physical activity.

Ongoing hepatic steatosis and obesity were noted 2.5 years post-NAFLD diagnosis, prompting initiation of semaglutide therapy. Shortly after administration of the initial dose (0.25 mg subcutaneously), she developed nausea and vomiting, which persisted. Two days later, she was admitted to hospital with ongoing nausea, vomiting, decreased oral intake, dyspnea, acute renal failure, and marked metabolic acidosis. Management included intravenous fluids, bicarbonate, and anti-emetic medication. She required temporary transfer to the intensive care unit for non-invasive ventilation. Her kidney function improved following hydration, and renal replacement therapies such as hemofiltration or dialysis were not necessary. Admission labs revealed elevated creatine kinase (CK) of 337 U/L, although she did not report symptoms such as rash, myalgia, or dark-colored urine. During her hospital stay, she developed intermittent atrial fibrillation, but serum troponin measurements remained within normal limits. After five days, she was discharged; at discharge, gastrointestinal complaints had resolved, but she remained physically deconditioned. Over the next four months, she experienced gradually worsening weakness. She reported no prior statin exposure, significant ingestion of supplements linked with IMNM (e.g., mushrooms or red yeast rice), nor other nutritional supplements. She denied notable family history of myopathy, recent excessive or sustained physical exertion, or use of alcohol or illicit drugs.

Four months after her initial and sole administration of semaglutide, her condition worsened. She was unable to stand or remain seated upright without help and reported several days of trouble swallowing both liquids and solids. This led her to seek care at a different hospital, where her creatine kinase (CK) was measured at 3992 U/L (reference range: 12–191 U/L) upon admission. Neurology was consulted via telemedicine, and she received intravenous methylprednisolone 1000 mg daily for five days. Subsequently, she was transferred to a tertiary care facility for further rheumatology assessment. On arrival, her vital signs were stable—she was afebrile and normotensive—and she displayed no acute distress. Examination showed pronounced weakness, most prominent in the proximal muscles (see table 1). She lacked rashes, skin changes, pulmonary crackles, synovitis, edema, sicca symptoms, oral ulcers, or other neurologic abnormalities.

After initial therapy with pulse methylprednisolone, she was subsequently started on oral prednisone at a dosage of 1 mg/kg per day. Because of ongoing significant weakness and severe clinical manifestations, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) at a dose of 2 g/kg was administered over 4 days. Over a two-week period, her creatine kinase (CK) levels improved considerably (figure 1). She was discharged following nearly four weeks of inpatient care. However, 12 days post-discharge, she required readmission for 10 days due to septic shock secondary to a pseudomonal urinary tract infection, during which her CK remained within normal limits.

She had multiple outpatient follow-up visits, during which her functional abilities and general health progressively improved, and her CK levels remained within the normal range. Consequently, her prednisone was tapered quickly and discontinued without issue. Given both financial constraints and her significant recovery after discontinuing semaglutide, she did not undergo further IVIG therapy beyond the first inpatient course.

At her most recent outpatient appointment, 10 months following initial assessment and commencement of treatment, she had returned to her pre-illness functional status and had been without immunosuppressive therapy for 8 months.

Investigations:

Her laboratory assessment revealed a CK level of 1237 U/L at the time of evaluation, which had reached a maximum of 3992 U/L one week earlier at the referring facility. TSH was found to be within normal limits. AST measured 191 U/L and ALT was 165 U/L, in the context of elevated serum CK and a history of hepatic steatosis. Her autoantibody screening showed negative results for anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-Sjogren’s syndrome type A antibodies (anti-SSA), anti-Smith antibodies, anti-U1 ribonucleoprotein (anti-U1 RNP) antibodies, anti-double-stranded DNA, antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), and anti-mitochondrial antibodies. Complement levels and creatinine were within normal limits, and no cytopenias were observed. The myositis panel was negative for SRP, HMGCR, Jo-1, Pl-7, Pl-12, EJ, OJ, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-y, NXP-2, and cN-1A autoantibodies. She was positive for SS-B autoantibody at a low titre (2.4; reference range <1.0). Erythrocyte sedimentation rate was elevated at 22 mm/hour, and C-reactive protein was 22.8 mg/L. Of note, she demonstrated an increased HS-Troponin at 62 pg/mL (reference ≤53 pg/mL) and a proBNP level of 4358 pg/mL (reference range: 0–125 pg/mL).

Imaging studies performed included MRI of both femurs, echocardiography, cardiac MRI, liver ultrasound with Doppler, high-resolution CT of the chest, and MRI of the abdomen. MRI of both femurs identified mild, symmetrical muscular edema bilaterally, most prominent in the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps regions, along with subcutaneous edema at the lateral sides of both thighs (figure 2). Echocardiography demonstrated an estimated left ventricular ejection fraction between 65% and 70%, and a right ventricular systolic pressure of 46 mm Hg. Cardiac MRI noted mild hypokinesis of the mid-anteroseptal and anterior wall, accompanied by myocardial edema without clear late gadolinium enhancement; these findings were considered possibly indicative of myocardial inflammation or early myocarditis, or alternatively a focal stress-induced cardiomyopathy. There was no evidence of significant myocardial scarring or infarction, nor significant valvular abnormalities. Liver ultrasound with Doppler showed hepatosteatosis. High-resolution CT of the chest showed no interstitial lung disease or lymphadenopathy. Abdominal MRI revealed steatotic enlargement of the liver, but was otherwise unremarkable with no lymphadenopathy.

Comprehensive diagnostic evaluation included a second liver biopsy and a biopsy of the thigh muscle. The liver biopsy, carried out during hospitalization, revealed several tissue cores showing both small and large droplet macrovesicular steatosis, involving roughly 70% of the hepatic parenchyma submitted, without notable portal inflammation or evidence of interface hepatitis. These results aligned with the patient’s known hepatosteatosis and indicated slight worsening of her liver pathology, yielding a NAFLD Activity Score grade of 5/8, stage 1. Analysis of a biopsy from the right vastus lateralis showed scattered necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers (figure 3A,B); immunohistochemical stains demonstrated positive acid phosphatase (figure 3D) and CD68, a macrophage marker, as well as evidence of myophagocytosis (figure 4B). Additionally, necrotizing and non-necrotizing muscle fibers exhibited positive staining for the membrane attack complex (figure 4C), indicative of inflammation. A small number of CD3-positive cells, marking T lymphocytes (figure 4A), and perivascular inflammatory infiltrates (figure 3C) were also observed. Overall, the muscle biopsy findings supported a diagnosis of IMNM.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient displayed a pronounced and immediate adverse response following her first and only administration of semaglutide, indicating drug intolerance. She initially developed gastrointestinal symptoms and acute renal failure, which was thought to be due to hypovolaemia. At presentation, her creatine kinase (CK) was only mildly raised, inconsistent with rhabdomyolysis, but her condition deteriorated even after discontinuation of semaglutide. The evolving pattern of muscle weakness and a twelve-fold rise in CK over the subsequent four months point toward the development of immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) potentially linked to semaglutide. Additionally, muscle biopsy findings compatible with drug-induced IMNM, absence of alternative clinical evidence for underlying connective tissue disease, and persistent improvement following immunosuppressive therapy and withdrawal of semaglutide further substantiate this association.

It is important to point out that she exhibited elevated transaminases before beginning semaglutide, a feature sometimes present in myopathies. Nevertheless, a liver biopsy conducted three years prior to semaglutide initiation confirmed hepatosteatosis, and the slight increase in AST/ALT remained even after creatine kinase values returned to normal. Thus, her chronic transaminase elevation is most likely attributable to hepatic causes rather than muscle injury. Additionally, her cardiac assessment did not provide definitive evidence of ongoing myocarditis. Considering the circumstances and her swift improvement, stress-induced myopathy is also a plausible explanation.

The detection of SS-B antibody in the absence of both ANA and SS-A was surprising. To date, no studies have documented IMNM arising from isolated SS-B positivity with concurrent negative ANA and SS-A results. Regrettably, a baseline CK value prior to the administration of semaglutide was not available, precluding definitive exclusion of a preexisting, subclinical autoimmune IMNM before semaglutide therapy. Nonetheless, the marked increase in CK by a factor of twelve and the gradual onset of proximal muscle weakness following semaglutide exposure suggest that this episode of IMNM was most likely drug-induced. Moreover, the patient's significant IMNM resolved after a brief course of corticosteroids and IVIG, combined with discontinuation of semaglutide. This clinical response is more consistent with medication-related IMNM, which generally improves after short-term immunosuppression and removal of the culprit drug, as opposed to idiopathic IMNM, which typically necessitates extended, intensive immunosuppressive treatment.

Treatment:

She received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for 5 days, after which she was transitioned to oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg per day. Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at a total dose of 2 g/kg, divided over 4 days because of ongoing profound weakness and significant clinical manifestations. She underwent multiple outpatient follow-up assessments, during which she consistently demonstrated improvement in both functional status and overall health, as well as persistently normal creatine kinase (CK) levels. This allowed for a successful and relatively rapid prednisone taper completed over a 2-month period. Considering her ongoing clinical stability after stopping semaglutide and taking into account financial factors, no further IVIG infusions were given following her initial inpatient treatment course.

Outcome and follow-up:

At her most recent outpatient follow-up, conducted 10 months post initial assessment and commencement of therapy, she had returned to her original level of functioning and had discontinued immunosuppressive agents for 7 months. Her CK values stayed within normal limits after her hospital admission and immunosuppressive treatment. Subsequent echocardiography and cardiac MRI demonstrated resolution of previously noted abnormalities. There was also no further occurrence of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation.

Discussion:

GLP-1 receptor agonists, including semaglutide, are increasingly utilized and have shown efficacy in treating metabolic conditions such as obesity and type 2 diabetes. While gastrointestinal symptoms are among the most frequent adverse events, these agents can also be linked to reduced muscle mass and possibly to rhabdomyolysis. In this instance, the patient's disease progression aligns more closely with IMNM rather than rhabdomyolysis. The patient's CK rose twelvefold across four months after a single semaglutide dose; if semaglutide toxicity were the cause, a return to normal CK would typically occur within weeks, similar to other episodes of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis. Of note, the previously documented tirzepatide-associated rhabdomyolysis case followed a classic course: the individual exhibited a sudden and pronounced CK surge (CK=57 014 at the time of presentation), with rapid recovery after discontinuing the medication and administering hydration within days, and did not require immunosuppressive therapy.

Applying the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) Causality Categories and Criteria to this case, there appears to be probable causality between semaglutide administration and the development of IMNM. This assessment is supported by an appropriate temporal association, the unlikelihood that other illnesses or medications were responsible, and an appropriate clinical improvement upon discontinuation of semaglutide. Reintroducing the drug would not be ethically justified given the severity of the adverse event observed. Nevertheless, we recognize that the definitive causal link in this instance cannot be established. In light of the widespread use of semaglutide, our case seeks to alert healthcare professionals to this potential adverse reaction and bolster efforts in pharmacovigilance. Additional research will be required to determine whether the observed association is causal or merely coincidental.

The specific factors contributing to our patient’s pronounced response to semaglutide remain unidentified. As GLP-1 receptor agonists continue to rise in usage, investigations into predisposing variables will be increasingly important, especially if further instances showing a close temporal relationship to semaglutide exposure are documented.
