Title: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

Brian Lam, Eleni Tiniakou, Xinhai Robert Zhang, Shervin Assassi

Abstract:

Summary: This case report presents a woman in her 60s who developed immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) following exposure to semaglutide. She experienced progressive symptoms over a 4-month period, including worsening dysphagia, dysphonia, proximal muscle weakness, and increased creatine kinase levels. These manifestations arose after a single administration of semaglutide. Diagnostic evaluation showed findings indicative of necrotising myopathy with an inflammatory component on muscle biopsy. Treatment with corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) led to improvement. The patient gradually returned to her baseline functional level and remained well off immunosuppressive therapy for multiple months, provided she continued to avoid glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists. This report details the first known instance of IMNM likely related to GLP-1 receptor agonist exposure, demonstrating clinical improvement following brief immunosuppression and IVIG therapy.

Background:

In recent years, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have become frequently prescribed for managing metabolic conditions such as type 2 diabetes and obesity. Among the reported adverse reactions associated with these drugs is a reduction in skeletal muscle mass. One recent case report detailed an instance of rhabdomyolysis exhibiting necrotizing features that was linked to tirzepatide, a widely utilized GLP-1 receptor agonist. The patient involved experienced creatine kinase (CK) levels rising into the thousands during ongoing therapy, and this elevation began to resolve within several days of stopping the medication, a pattern indicative of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis.

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) represents a unique form of myopathy, defined by rapidly progressive proximal muscle weakness and muscle biopsy findings of necrotic muscle fibers with minimal lymphocytic infiltration. The autoantibodies anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (SRP) show a strong correlation with IMNM. Statin therapy, often prescribed for metabolic conditions, is particularly linked to the development of HMGCR autoantibodies. Before recognizing the association with HMGCR autoantibodies, numerous patients taking statins presented with necrotising myopathy and were frequently misclassified as having toxic myopathy or polymyositis. In such cases, muscle weakness and elevated creatine kinase levels typically persisted even after stopping statin therapy. Resolution of symptoms following immunosuppressive treatment pointed toward an underlying autoimmune myopathy and subsequently contributed to the recognition of HMGCR autoantibody as a defining feature of this condition. It is important to highlight that approximately one-third of individuals with IMNM test negative for these autoantibodies. The absence of identifiable autoantibodies in this group introduces further complexity in diagnosis and indicates the likely presence of additional, yet unidentified, autoantibodies or triggers.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 60s with a medical history significant for obesity, hepatic steatosis, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and a distant episode of deep venous thrombosis/pulmonary embolism presented with gradually worsening weakness in her proximal muscles, as well as difficulty swallowing and speaking.

Prior to this episode, she had been in good health and maintained independence with daily activities. Three years before her current decline, she was being evaluated as an outpatient for elevated transaminase levels. During that period, her aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) were mildly raised—approximately two times the upper limit of normal, with ALT higher than AST. Other laboratory results, including alkaline phosphatase, albumin, bilirubin, and platelet count, were all within normal parameters. She reported no alcohol consumption and denied use of medications associated with liver toxicity. An abdominal ultrasound revealed an enlarged liver with diffusely increased echogenicity, findings most consistent with hepatic steatosis. Tests for viral hepatitis, as well as anti-nuclear, anti-mitochondrial, anti-smooth muscle, and liver-kidney microsomal antibodies, returned negative results. Histopathological examination via liver biopsy indicated steatohepatitis, in keeping with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), with a NAFLD Activity Score of 4/8 and stage 0 fibrosis. She was subsequently counseled to adhere to a low-calorie diet and to engage in regular physical activity.

Owing to ongoing hepatic steatosis and obesity 2.5 years post-NAFLD diagnosis, semaglutide therapy was commenced. Within several hours after her initial subcutaneous dose of semaglutide (0.25 mg), she developed nausea and vomiting. Her symptoms did not abate, and she was admitted to the hospital two days later for intractable nausea, vomiting, inadequate oral intake, shortness of breath, acute kidney injury, and pronounced metabolic acidosis. Management included intravenous fluids, bicarbonate administration, and anti-emetic agents. She required a short-term transfer to the intensive care unit for non-invasive respiratory support. Subsequently, with intravenous hydration her renal function recovered, obviating the necessity for haemofiltration or dialysis. On admission, her creatine kinase (CK) was found to be elevated at 337 U/L. She exhibited no additional features such as skin rash, myalgia, or dark (cola-coloured) urine. During this admission, she experienced episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, though her serum troponin remained within normal limits. Discharge occurred five days later, with resolution of gastrointestinal symptoms; however, she remained physically deconditioned. Over the next four months, she developed gradually worsening weakness. She reported no prior statin use, notable consumption of IMNM-associated supplements (e.g., mushrooms or red yeast rice), or other nutritional supplements. She also denied significant familial myopathy, recent extreme or prolonged exercise, as well as alcohol or illicit drug use.

Four months after receiving her first and only administration of semaglutide, her condition progressively worsened. She lost the ability to stand or maintain an upright sitting position without support and developed several days of dysphagia affecting both liquids and solids. Due to these symptoms, she sought care at an external hospital where laboratory work-up revealed a creatine kinase level of 3992 U/L (reference range: 12–191 U/L) on admission. Neurology, consulted via telemedicine, recommended treatment with intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for five days. Subsequently, she was transferred to a tertiary care center for rheumatological assessment. Upon arrival, she was afebrile, maintained normal blood pressure, and appeared comfortable otherwise. Examination demonstrated pronounced weakness, particularly in the proximal muscles (see table 1). There were no signs of rash, skin abnormalities, pulmonary crackles, synovitis, edema, sicca symptoms, oral ulcers, or additional neurologic impairments.

After receiving pulse methylprednisolone, she was transitioned to daily oral prednisone at a dosage of 1 mg/kg. Because her weakness persisted and her clinical presentation remained severe, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at a total dose of 2 g/kg, divided across 4 days. Over a period of 2 weeks, there was a significant decline in her CK levels (figure 1). She was discharged following an almost 4-week hospital stay. Twelve days post-discharge, she was readmitted for 10 days with septic shock secondary to a pseudomonal urinary tract infection; during this episode, her CK levels were found to be normal.

During multiple outpatient follow-up visits, she exhibited further improvement in her functional capacity and general well-being, with consistently normal CK values. This facilitated a rapid taper and discontinuation of her prednisone. Because of financial considerations and her sustained improvement without semaglutide, no further courses of IVIG were administered after her initial inpatient treatment.

She was last seen in the outpatient setting 10 months after her initial evaluation and treatment initiation and had regained her baseline functional status, off immunosuppression for 8 months.

Investigations:

Her laboratory investigations revealed a creatine kinase (CK) level of 1237 U/L at the time of assessment, with a highest recorded value of 3992 U/L one week earlier at the referring hospital. Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) was within the reference range. Aspartate aminotransferase (AST) was found to be 191 U/L and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) 165 U/L, interpreted in the context of elevated serum CK and a known history of hepatic steatosis. Autoantibody screening was significant for negative results in anti-nuclear antibody (ANA), anti-Sjogren’s syndrome antigen A (anti-SSA), anti-Smith, anti-U1 ribonucleoprotein (anti-U1 RNP), anti-double-stranded DNA, antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA), and anti-mitochondrial antibody testing. Complement levels, serum creatinine, and peripheral blood cell counts were all within normal limits; there was no evidence of cytopenia. The myositis-specific antibody panel was negative for SRP, HMGCR, Jo-1, Pl-7, Pl-12, EJ, OJ, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-γ, NXP-2, and cN-1A autoantibodies. She did have a low-titer SS-B (La) antibody at 2.4 (reference <1.0). Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was raised to 22 mm/hour, and C-reactive protein (CRP) was elevated at 22.8 mg/L. Of note, high-sensitivity troponin was increased at 62 pg/mL (reference range ≤53 pg/mL), and N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (proBNP) measured 4358 pg/mL (reference interval: 0–125 pg/mL).

Significant imaging studies included MRI scans of both femurs, echocardiography, cardiac MRI, liver Doppler ultrasound, high-resolution chest CT, and abdominal MRI. The femoral MRI demonstrated mild, bilateral muscular edema with symmetrical distribution, most prominent in the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps muscles, alongside subcutaneous edema on the lateral sides of both thighs (see figure 2). Echocardiography revealed a left ventricular ejection fraction estimated at 65%–70%, with a right ventricular systolic pressure of 46 mm Hg. Cardiac MRI showed mild hypokinesis involving the mid-anteroseptal and anterior wall, associated with myocardial edema; there was no definite late gadolinium enhancement. These findings could be suggestive of early myocarditis or focal stress-induced cardiomyopathy. There was no notable myocardial scar or infarction, and valvular function appeared preserved. Liver Doppler ultrasound identified hepatosteatosis. High-resolution chest CT ruled out interstitial lung disease and lymphadenopathy. Abdominal MRI demonstrated steatotic hepatomegaly with otherwise normal findings and no lymphadenopathy.

Further diagnostic evaluation included a repeat hepatic biopsy and a biopsy of the thigh musculature. The liver biopsy, obtained during her hospital stay, showed multiple fragments of hepatic tissue displaying macrovesicular steatosis with both small and large fat droplets involving about 70% of the liver parenchyma, without notable portal inflammation or interface hepatitis. These pathological findings aligned with her preexisting hepatosteatosis and indicated mild worsening of her chronic liver condition, resulting in a NAFLD Activity Score of 5 out of 8, stage 1. Analysis of a biopsy taken from the right vastus lateralis identified scattered areas of muscle fiber necrosis and regeneration (figure 3A,B). Immunohistochemical assessment demonstrated positive staining for acid phosphatase (figure 3D) and CD68, a macrophage-specific marker, with evidence of myophagocytosis (figure 4B). Additionally, immunostaining was positive for the membrane attack complex in both necrotic and non-necrotic myofibers (figure 4C), indicative of an inflammatory process. A few CD3-positive (T-cell marker) cells (figure 4A) and perivascular inflammatory infiltrates (figure 3C) were also observed. Collectively, the muscle biopsy findings were diagnostic of an immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM).

Differential diagnosis:

The patient exhibited a pronounced and immediate adverse response following her first and only administration of semaglutide, indicative of an intolerance. She initially developed gastrointestinal complaints and renal failure, which was attributed to presumed hypovolemia. Although her creatine kinase (CK) level was only slightly elevated at presentation—making rhabdomyolysis unlikely—her ongoing deterioration after discontinuing semaglutide, the pattern of muscular weakness, and a twelve-fold rise in CK over four months post-exposure point toward an immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) likely linked to semaglutide. Additionally, histopathology consistent with drug-induced IMNM, absence of alternative connective tissue disease features, and persistent clinical improvement after immunosuppressive therapy and semaglutide withdrawal further corroborate this association.

It is worth mentioning that elevated transaminase levels were present before she initiated semaglutide therapy, a finding that can occur in muscle disorders. Nevertheless, she had been diagnosed with hepatosteatosis by liver biopsy three years prior to starting semaglutide, and her marginally increased AST and ALT values persisted even after normalization of CK. This suggests that her chronically raised transaminases most likely stem from a hepatic source rather than muscular injury. Additionally, her cardiac evaluation did not yield definitive evidence of ongoing myocarditis. Given her swift clinical improvement, a stress-related myopathy would also represent a plausible explanation in this scenario.

The presence of SS-B antibody alone, with negative ANA and SS-A antibodies, was unusual in this context. To date, no cases have been reported of isolated SS-B positivity with negative ANA and SS-A antibodies resulting in IMNM. We did not have access to a baseline CK value before semaglutide therapy, so we cannot entirely rule out pre-existing, low-grade autoimmune IMNM prior to starting semaglutide. Nonetheless, the 12-fold elevation in CK and the worsening proximal muscle weakness that arose following semaglutide administration suggest that the IMNM episode was likely induced by the medication. Furthermore, the patient’s severe IMNM was effectively treated with a brief course of corticosteroids and IVIG along with discontinuation of semaglutide. This pattern of rapid improvement is more characteristic of drug-induced IMNM—which may respond to short-term immunosuppression once the causative drug is stopped—than of idiopathic IMNM, which typically necessitates prolonged, aggressive immunosuppressive treatment.

Treatment:

She received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for 5 days, then transitioned to oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg per day. Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at a total dose of 2 g/kg, divided across 4 days in response to her ongoing profound weakness and significant clinical features. Multiple outpatient follow-up visits noted her progressive functional and overall health improvement, as well as consistently normal CK levels. In light of these findings, prednisone was tapered off promptly over a 2-month span. Owing to cost factors and her stable condition after discontinuing semaglutide, no further IVIG was administered apart from the initial inpatient course.

Outcome and follow-up:

Ten months after her initial assessment and commencement of therapy, she was evaluated again in the outpatient clinic, having restored her baseline level of functioning and remained off immunosuppressive therapy for seven months. Her creatine kinase measurements stayed within the normal limits since her hospital admission and immunosuppressive treatment. Follow-up echocardiography and cardiac MRI demonstrated resolution of the abnormalities that had previously been noted. She also remained free of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation recurrence.

Discussion:

GLP-1 receptor agonists like semaglutide are increasingly prescribed and have shown efficacy in treating metabolic diseases such as type 2 diabetes and obesity. Although gastrointestinal adverse effects are most frequently reported, these agents have also been linked to reductions in muscle mass and, on occasion, rhabdomyolysis. In this case, the patient exhibited a clinical trajectory more indicative of IMNM rather than rhabdomyolysis. Specifically, creatine kinase levels rose twelvefold over a four-month period after a single dose of semaglutide, whereas, if the drug had exerted a direct myotoxic effect, CK values would typically return to baseline within several weeks, as described in other instances of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis. Of note, in a previously documented episode of rhabdomyolysis associated with tirzepatide, the clinical progression was characteristic: the individual developed a pronounced acute increase in CK (CK=57,014 at presentation), which rapidly declined following cessation of the medication and intravenous fluids over a matter of days, and there was no requirement for immunosuppressive therapy.

According to the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) Causality Categories and Criteria, there is a probable link between semaglutide administration and the development of IMNM in this patient, based on an appropriate temporal association, the improbability of IMNM being explained by other conditions or medications, and a clinically plausible improvement upon discontinuation of the drug. Re-exposure to semaglutide would not be justified in light of the severity of the adverse effect observed. Nevertheless, we recognize that a definitive causal link cannot be established in this case. Given the rapidly increasing use of semaglutide, the intention of this report is to alert healthcare providers to this potential side effect and to support heightened pharmacovigilance. Further research will be necessary to determine if this relationship is indeed causative or merely coincidental.

Additionally, the factors that might have rendered our patient susceptible to this severe reaction with semaglutide remain unidentified. With the surge in use of GLP-1 receptor agonists, investigating risk factors for adverse reactions will become increasingly important if additional reports with similar timing relative to semaglutide administration emerge.
