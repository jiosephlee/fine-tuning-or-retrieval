Title: Could semaglutide therapy play a role in the onset of immune-mediated necrotising myopathy?

Brian Lam, Eleni Tiniakou, Xinhai Robert Zhang, Shervin Assassi

Abstract:

Summary: This paper presents the case of a woman in her 60s who developed immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) following administration of semaglutide. Over a period of four months, she experienced progressive symptoms including dysphagia, dysphonia, proximal muscle weakness, and increased creatine kinase levels. These manifestations began after a single exposure to semaglutide. Diagnostic evaluation identified necrotising myopathy with evidence of inflammatory infiltrates on muscle biopsy. Treatment with corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) led to clinical improvement. The patient regained her prior level of function and maintained this recovery for several months while off immunosuppressive therapy and by abstaining from further use of glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists. This report documents what appears to be the first instance of IMNM potentially linked to GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy, with remission achieved after a brief regimen of immunosuppression and IVIG.

Background:

In recent times, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have become widely prescribed for managing metabolic disorders such as type 2 diabetes and obesity. However, these agents have been linked to several adverse reactions, including reductions in skeletal muscle mass. Notably, a recent case report identified an instance of rhabdomyolysis exhibiting necrotizing characteristics in association with tirzepatide, a frequently utilized GLP-1 receptor agonist. In this report, the individual experienced a marked elevation of creatine kinase (CK) into the thousands during active therapy. Following cessation of the medication, CK levels began to decrease within several days, supporting a diagnosis of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis.

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) constitutes a specific subset of myopathies that is marked by rapidly progressive proximal muscle weakness, notable necrosis of muscle fibers, and minimal lymphocytic infiltration seen on muscle biopsy. Among the autoantibodies most closely linked to IMNM are anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (SRP). Notably, there is a strong correlation between the development of HMGCR autoantibodies and prior statin exposure, a class of medications widely prescribed for metabolic conditions. Before HMGCR autoantibodies were recognized as a disease marker, several patients using statins presented with signs consistent with necrotising myopathy, and these cases were often misclassified as either toxic myopathy or polymyositis. Importantly, many such patients continued to experience myopathy and elevated creatine kinase (CK) levels even after discontinuing statin therapy. The subsequent clinical response to immunosuppressive treatment pointed towards an underlying autoimmune etiology, ultimately leading to the identification of the HMGCR autoantibody as a defining feature of this disorder. Nevertheless, approximately one third of individuals diagnosed with IMNM do not have detectable autoantibodies. This seronegative group not only presents further complexities in reaching a diagnosis but also raises the possibility that there are additional, as yet unidentified, autoantibodies or precipitating factors involved in IMNM.

Case presentation:

A woman in her sixth decade, whose past medical history includes obesity, hepatosteatosis, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and a distant episode of deep venous thrombosis/pulmonary embolism, reported experiencing gradually worsening proximal muscle weakness, along with dysphagia and dysphonia.

The patient had been in good health and was entirely self-sufficient in performing daily tasks. Three years before her clinical decline, she was assessed in an outpatient setting for elevated transaminases. At that stage, laboratory tests revealed a modest increase in aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT), both at roughly twice the upper limit of normal, with ALT surpassing AST. Levels of alkaline phosphatase, albumin, bilirubin, and platelets were all within standard reference ranges. She reported no history of alcohol consumption and had not been prescribed any medications known to cause liver toxicity. An abdominal ultrasound demonstrated an enlarged liver with uniformly heightened echotexture, findings most consistent with hepatic steatosis. Serologic investigations, including those for viral hepatitis and autoantibodies such as anti-nuclear, anti-mitochondrial, anti-smooth muscle, and liver-kidney microsomal antibodies, were negative. Histopathological analysis of a liver biopsy revealed steatohepatitis, aligning with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) with a NAFLD Activity Score of grade 4/8 and stage 0 fibrosis. Recommendations included adherence to a calorie-restricted diet and engagement in regular physical activity.

As a result of ongoing obesity and fatty liver persisting 2.5 years after her initial NAFLD diagnosis, semaglutide therapy was started. Shortly after administration of her initial semaglutide dose (0.25 mg subcutaneously), she developed nausea and vomiting. These symptoms did not resolve and, after two days, she required hospital admission due to ongoing nausea, vomiting, decreased oral intake, dyspnoea, acute kidney injury, and pronounced metabolic acidosis. Management involved intravenous fluids and bicarbonate therapy, alongside anti-emetic medications. She was briefly moved to the intensive care unit to receive non-invasive ventilation. Following hydration, her kidney function recovered, and renal replacement therapies such as haemofiltration or haemodialysis were not necessary. On admission, her creatine kinase was measured at 337 U/L, though she did not report muscle aches, rashes, or dark-colored urine. During her stay, she developed paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, although troponin assays remained within normal limits. She was discharged on day 5. At discharge, gastrointestinal complaints had subsided, but she remained physically deconditioned. Over the next four months, she developed gradually worsening muscle weakness. She reported no prior exposure to statins, ingestion of known IMNM-associated supplements such as mushrooms or red yeast rice, or use of other dietary supplements. She also had no significant family history of myopathy, had not engaged in unusually intense or prolonged physical exertion, and reported no alcohol or illicit substance use.

Four months following her initial and sole administration of semaglutide, the patient experienced progressive deterioration. She lost the ability to maintain an upright posture, either standing or sitting, without support. Additionally, she developed dysphagia to both solids and liquids over several days, which led her to seek care at an outside hospital. Upon presentation, laboratory analysis showed a creatine kinase (CK) level of 3992 U/L (reference range: 12–191 U/L). The neurology service, via telemedicine, initiated treatment with intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for five days. Subsequent transfer to a tertiary care center for rheumatology assessment revealed a patient who was afebrile, had stable blood pressure, and did not appear to be in distress. On examination, marked weakness was most apparent in the proximal muscle groups (table 1). No rashes, skin abnormalities, crackles, synovitis, edema, sicca symptoms, oral ulcers, or other neurological impairments were observed.

After receiving pulse methylprednisolone therapy, the patient was subsequently administered oral prednisone at a dosage of 1 mg/kg per day. Owing to ongoing muscle weakness and pronounced clinical manifestations, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at a total dose of 2 g/kg over the course of 4 days. Over the subsequent two weeks, a significant reduction in CK levels was noted (figure 1). She was ultimately discharged after an almost 4-week hospital stay. However, 12 days post-discharge, she was readmitted for 10 days due to septic shock secondary to a pseudomonal urinary tract infection; during this period, her CK was found to be within normal limits.

The patient was monitored through multiple outpatient visits, during which she consistently exhibited enhancements in both her functional capacity and general health, alongside stable, normal CK levels. As a result, she was able to discontinue prednisone quickly without issue. Owing to financial constraints and sustained clinical progress after stopping semaglutide, no further IVIG treatments were administered following her initial hospital course.

At her most recent outpatient follow-up, conducted 10 months following the commencement of her initial assessment and therapy, she had returned to her previous level of functioning and had been free from immunosuppressive medication for the past 8 months.

Investigations:

On laboratory evaluation, her creatine kinase (CK) was found to be elevated at 1237 U/L, with a prior peak recorded at 3992 U/L one week before transfer. Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) remained within reference values. Liver enzymes were also raised, with AST at 191 U/L and ALT at 165 U/L, coinciding with high serum CK and a medical history of hepatic steatosis. Immunological studies revealed that tests for anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-Sjogren’s syndrome antibody A (anti-SSA), anti-Smith, anti-U1 ribonucleoprotein (anti-U1 RNP), anti-double-stranded DNA, ANCA, and anti-mitochondrial antibodies were all negative. Complement levels and creatinine were normal, and there were no cytopenias observed. The extended myositis autoantibody panel returned negative for SRP, HMGCR, as well as for Jo-1, Pl-7, Pl-12, EJ, OJ, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-y, NXP-2, and cN-1A antibodies. However, there was a low-titre positivity for SS-B autoantibody at 2.4 (reference: <1.0). Inflammatory markers were increased, with erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) at 22 mm/hour and C-reactive protein at 22.8 mg/L. Additionally, high-sensitivity troponin was mildly elevated at 62 pg/mL (reference: ≤53 pg/mL), and pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (proBNP) was significantly increased at 4358 pg/mL (reference: 0–125 pg/mL).

Key imaging assessments comprised MRI scans of both femurs, echocardiography, cardiac MRI, liver ultrasound with Doppler, high-resolution chest CT, and abdominal MRI. The MRI of the bilateral femurs demonstrated mild, symmetric edema in the muscles of both thighs, predominantly within the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps, along with subcutaneous edema localized to the lateral regions of the thighs (figure 2). Echocardiographic evaluation revealed a left ventricular ejection fraction estimated at 65%–70% and a right ventricular systolic pressure measuring 46 mm Hg. Cardiac MRI indicated mild hypokinesis in the mid-anteroseptal and anterior myocardial walls in conjunction with myocardial oedema, absent definitive late gadolinium enhancement; these findings could reflect early myocardial inflammation/myocarditis or a localized stress-induced cardiomyopathy. There were no signs of substantial myocardial scarring, infarction, or notable valvular abnormalities. Liver ultrasound with Doppler detected hepatosteatosis. The high-resolution CT of the chest did not reveal evidence of interstitial lung disease or lymphadenopathy. Abdominal MRI confirmed the presence of steatotic hepatomegaly, but was otherwise unremarkable and showed no lymphadenopathy.

Further diagnostic evaluation included repetition of the liver biopsy and performance of a thigh muscle biopsy. The repeat liver biopsy, conducted during the patient's hospitalization, revealed several samples of hepatic tissue exhibiting both small and large droplet macrovesicular steatosis affecting about 70% of the hepatic parenchyma submitted for examination. There was an absence of notable portal inflammation or interface hepatitis. These histological results aligned with her known history of hepatosteatosis, and indicated a mild advancement of her preexisting liver disease, now categorized as NAFLD Activity Score grade 5/8, stage 1. The right vastus lateralis muscle biopsy identified scattered necrotic as well as regenerating muscle fibers (figure 3A,B). Immunohistochemical analysis showed acid phosphatase (figure 3D) and cluster of Differentiation-68 (CD68), a macrophage marker, associated with evidence of myophagocytosis (figure 4B). Additionally, staining for membrane attack complex was positive in both necrotic and non-necrotic myofibers (figure 4C), suggestive of an active inflammatory process. Occasional CD3 positive cells, indicative of T-cell infiltration (figure 4A), were seen alongside perivascular inflammatory infiltrates (figure 3C). The collective muscle biopsy findings were indicative of IMNM.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient exhibited an abrupt and pronounced response following her first and only administration of semaglutide, indicative of drug intolerance. Initially, she presented with gastrointestinal disturbances and renal failure, presumed to be related to hypovolaemia. Although her creatine kinase (CK) levels were only mildly elevated—insufficient for a diagnosis of rhabdomyolysis—during her first assessment, she subsequently deteriorated after discontinuing semaglutide. The pattern of her muscle weakness, accompanied by a twelvefold elevation in CK over four months post-exposure, points toward an immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) likely linked to semaglutide. Additional support for this association comes from histopathological findings characteristic of drug-induced IMNM, the absence of signs typical of a connective tissue disorder, and her persistent recovery following cessation of semaglutide and initiation of immunosuppressive therapy.

It is worth noting that the patient exhibited transaminaemia before beginning semaglutide therapy, a finding sometimes associated with myopathies. Nevertheless, she was diagnosed with hepatosteatosis through biopsy three years prior to initiating semaglutide, and her mildly elevated AST/ALT levels continued even after normalization of CK values. This suggests that the persistent elevation of her transaminases most likely stems from a hepatic source, rather than being a consequence of muscle injury. Additionally, her cardiac assessment did not yield definitive evidence of ongoing myocarditis. Considering her clinical context and swift recovery, stress-induced myopathy would also represent a plausible explanation.

The detection of a positive SS-B antibody despite negative ANA and SS-A results was an unexpected finding. To date, there are no documented cases of isolated SS-B positivity with concomitantly negative ANA and SS-A resulting in IMNM. A baseline CK value prior to initiation of semaglutide therapy was unavailable, limiting our ability to completely rule out the presence of subclinical autoimmune IMNM before exposure to semaglutide. Nonetheless, the observed 12-fold increase in CK alongside gradually worsening proximal muscle weakness following the commencement of semaglutide suggests that this episode of IMNM was likely precipitated by the medication. Furthermore, the patient’s severe IMNM remitted following a brief treatment period with corticosteroids and IVIG and through discontinuation of semaglutide. This progression is more consistent with the course seen in drug-induced IMNM—wherein improvement often occurs with transient immunosuppression after cessation of the causative drug—rather than idiopathic IMNM, which typically necessitates extended and intensive immunosuppressive therapy.

Treatment:

She initially received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for 5 days, after which she was transitioned to oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg per day. Due to ongoing significant weakness and pronounced clinical features, IVIG was administered at a total dose of 2 g/kg, divided over 4 days. Subsequent follow-up visits in the outpatient clinic demonstrated steady gains in her functional ability and general well-being, with consistently normal CK levels. Owing to these positive developments, her prednisone therapy was tapered and discontinued rapidly over a 2-month period. Additionally, taking into account both the expense and her stable improvement in the absence of semaglutide, no further IVIG infusions were administered after her initial hospitalization.

Outcome and follow-up:

At her most recent outpatient follow-up, conducted 10 months post-initial assessment and commencement of therapy, she had returned to her pre-illness functional capacity, having discontinued immunosuppressive medication 7 months prior. Her CK levels stayed stable within the normal limits after discharge and during her course of immunosuppressive therapy. Subsequent echocardiography and cardiac MRI demonstrated resolution of previously noted cardiac abnormalities. Furthermore, there were no further episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation.

Discussion:

GLP-1 receptor agonists like semaglutide are increasingly prescribed, having shown proven efficacy in treating metabolic conditions such as obesity and type 2 diabetes. While gastrointestinal symptoms are the most frequent side effects, these agents have also been implicated in reducing muscle mass and, on occasion, inducing rhabdomyolysis. In this report, however, the patient's presentation aligns more with IMNM than with rhabdomyolysis. Following a single administration of semaglutide, the patient’s CK values rose twelvefold over a period of four months, contrasting with the rapid normalization of CK generally expected within weeks if semaglutide-induced muscle toxicity were the cause, as typically observed in cases of drug-related rhabdomyolysis. Furthermore, a previously documented incident of rhabdomyolysis attributed to tirzepatide exemplified the usual trajectory: an abrupt and significant rise in CK levels (CK=57 014 at the initial assessment) followed by swift resolution upon cessation of the medication and supportive hydration, all without necessitating immunosuppressive therapy.

Applying the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) Causality Categories and Criteria, the relationship between semaglutide administration and IMNM in our patient can be classified as probable. This assessment is based on the presence of an appropriate temporal association, the lack of alternative explanations involving other conditions or medications, and the notable clinical improvement after discontinuation of semaglutide. A rechallenge was not performed due to ethical concerns, given the severity of the adverse reaction observed. Nonetheless, we recognize that definitive causality cannot be established in this instance. Given the rapidly increasing use of semaglutide, this case report seeks to inform clinicians of this potential adverse event and to strengthen pharmacovigilance efforts. Further research will be needed to determine whether this observed association reflects true causation or mere coincidence.

The underlying reasons for our patient's pronounced reaction to semaglutide are still not well understood. As GLP-1 receptor agonists gain broader use, research aimed at identifying risk factors will be essential, particularly if further instances with a strong temporal association to semaglutide administration are observed.
