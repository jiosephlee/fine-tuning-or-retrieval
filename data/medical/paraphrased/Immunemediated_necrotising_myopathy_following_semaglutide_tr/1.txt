Title: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

Brian Lam, Eleni Tiniakou, Xinhai Robert Zhang, Shervin Assassi

Abstract:

Summary: This case report details a woman in her sixties who developed immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) after exposure to semaglutide. She presented with worsening dysphagia, dysphonia, proximal muscle weakness, and elevated creatine kinase levels spanning a period of four months. These clinical manifestations emerged following a single administration of semaglutide. Diagnostic evaluation demonstrated necrotising myopathy with inflammatory infiltrates on muscle biopsy. The patient showed improvement with immunosuppressive therapy consisting of corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG). She experienced gradual recovery and was able to return to her baseline level of functioning, maintaining stability off immunosuppressive agents for several months while continuing to avoid glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists. This represents the first published instance of IMNM likely linked to GLP-1 receptor agonist exposure, with apparent resolution following a brief course of corticosteroids and IVIG.

Background:

GLP-1 receptor agonists have become widely utilized in the management of metabolic conditions such as type 2 diabetes and obesity in recent years. Among the documented side effects of these agents is a reduction in skeletal muscle mass. A recent case report highlighted an instance of rhabdomyolysis exhibiting necrotizing characteristics in association with tirzepatide, another frequently prescribed GLP-1 receptor agonist. In this report, the patient experienced a marked increase in creatine kinase (CK) levels, reaching the thousands, during ongoing therapy. Notably, CK concentrations began to decline within a few days after cessation of the medication, supporting a diagnosis of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis.

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) represents a unique form of myopathy marked by rapidly progressive weakness affecting proximal muscles, with histological evidence of necrotic muscle fibers and minimal lymphocytic infiltration. Two autoantibodies, anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (SRP), show a strong association with IMNM. Notably, statin medications, frequently prescribed for metabolic conditions, are closely linked to the presence of HMGCR autoantibodies. Prior to the recognition of the connection between statin exposure and HMGCR autoantibody positivity, several individuals who developed necrotising myopathy while on statins were often misclassified as having toxic myopathy or polymyositis. Many of these patients continued to exhibit muscle symptoms and elevated creatine kinase (CK) levels despite discontinuing statin therapy. The subsequent clinical response to immunosuppressive treatment pointed to an underlying autoimmune process and ultimately led to the identification of HMGCR autoantibody as a defining feature of IMNM. It is important to note, however, that approximately one-third of IMNM cases do not exhibit identifiable autoantibodies. The absence of detectable autoantibodies in these individuals presents further diagnostic difficulties and indicates the possibility that other associated autoantibodies or triggers have yet to be characterized.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 60s, with a past medical history including obesity, hepatosteatosis, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and a distant episode of deep vein thrombosis/pulmonary embolism, presented with gradually worsening proximal muscle weakness, difficulty swallowing, and changes in her voice.

She had previously maintained good health and remained fully capable in her daily self-care. Three years before her current decline, she underwent outpatient workup due to elevated liver transaminases. At that point, laboratory tests revealed mildly increased aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT), each about two times above the upper normal limit, with ALT exceeding AST. Alkaline phosphatase, albumin, bilirubin, and platelet levels were unremarkable. The patient reported abstaining from alcohol and was not on any liver-toxic drugs. An abdominal ultrasound revealed an enlarged liver with diffusely increased echogenicity, suggestive of hepatic steatosis. Laboratory testing for viral hepatitis as well as anti-nuclear, anti-mitochondrial, anti-smooth muscle, and liver-kidney microsomal antibodies was negative. Liver biopsy confirmed steatohepatitis matching non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD)/NAFLD Activity Score grade 4 out of 8, stage 0. She was counseled to adhere to a calorie-restricted diet and to exercise regularly.

Because her hepatic steatosis and obesity persisted 2.5 years after she was diagnosed with NAFLD, semaglutide therapy was started. A few hours following the initial subcutaneous administration of semaglutide (0.25 mg), she developed nausea and vomiting. These symptoms continued, leading to her hospitalisation two days later for ongoing nausea and vomiting, decreased oral intake, dyspnoea, acute renal insufficiency, and severe metabolic acidosis. Management included intravenous fluids, bicarbonate therapy, and antiemetics. She required a brief transfer to the intensive care unit to receive non-invasive ventilatory support. With intravenous hydration, her renal parameters improved, eliminating the need for haemofiltration or dialysis. Creatine kinase (CK) was measured upon admission and was found to be elevated at 337 U/L. She exhibited no other related complaints such as rash, myalgia, or dark-colored urine. While hospitalised, she developed paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, although her serum troponin remained within normal limits. She was discharged after five days; at the time of discharge her gastrointestinal manifestations had resolved, although she remained deconditioned. Over the next four months, she developed gradually worsening weakness. She reported no prior statin usage, significant intake of mushrooms, red yeast rice, or other nutritional supplements linked to IMNM, and denied a relevant family history of myopathy, recent intensive or prolonged physical exertion, or use of alcohol or illicit drugs.

Four months after her first and only administration of semaglutide, her condition continued to worsen. She became unable to stand or maintain an upright sitting position without support, and developed difficulties swallowing both liquids and solids over several days. This led her to seek care at an outside hospital, where her admission laboratory studies revealed a creatine kinase (CK) level of 3992 U/L (reference range: 12–191 U/L). Neurology was consulted remotely, and she received intravenous methylprednisolone at 1000 mg daily for five days. Subsequently, she was transferred to a tertiary facility for rheumatological assessment, during which she was afebrile, had normal blood pressure, and did not display signs of distress. On examination, she was found to have pronounced weakness, especially affecting the proximal muscles (see table 1). No rashes, changes in skin, crackles, synovitis, oedema, sicca symptoms, oral ulcers, or additional neurological deficits were observed.

After receiving pulse methylprednisolone, the patient was placed on oral prednisone at a dose of 1 mg/kg per day. Given her ongoing weakness and significant clinical manifestations, she was administered intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) at a total dose of 2 g/kg, divided across 4 days. Over a 2-week period, there was substantial reduction in her CK levels, as illustrated in figure 1. She was discharged following nearly 4 weeks of hospitalization. Twelve days post-discharge, she was readmitted for 10 days with septic shock secondary to a pseudomonal urinary tract infection, during which her CK was noted to be within the normal range.

She attended multiple outpatient follow-up appointments, demonstrating ongoing gains in functional capacity and general well-being, along with stable, normal creatine kinase measurements. These factors enabled a successful and expedited taper of her prednisone. As a result of improved health without semaglutide and cost considerations, she did not undergo further IVIG beyond her original hospitalization regimen.

Her most recent outpatient evaluation occurred 10 months post initial assessment and commencement of therapy, at which point she had returned to her baseline level of function and had been without immunosuppressive therapy for 8 months.

Investigations:

Her laboratory evaluation revealed a CK level of 1237 U/L at presentation, with a maximum of 3992 U/L recorded a week earlier at the referring facility. Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) was within the reference range. Aspartate aminotransferase (AST) was measured at 191 U/L and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) at 165 U/L, interpreted in the context of her elevated CK and prior diagnosis of fatty liver. Autoantibody screening demonstrated negative results for anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-Sjogren’s syndrome antibodies type A (anti-SSA), anti-Smith, anti-U1 ribonucleoprotein (anti-U1 RNP), anti-double-stranded DNA, antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), and anti-mitochondrial antibodies. Complement levels and creatinine were unremarkable, and there was no evidence of cytopenias. Myositis-specific autoantibody panel returned negative for antibodies against SRP, HMGCR, Jo-1, Pl-7, Pl-12, EJ, OJ, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-y, NXP-2, and cN-1A. However, testing identified a low-titer positive SS-B antibody at 2.4 (reference <1.0). Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was found to be elevated at 22 mm/hour, and C-reactive protein measured 22.8 mg/L. Additionally, high-sensitivity troponin was increased at 62 pg/mL (reference ≤53 pg/mL) and pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (proBNP) was elevated at 4358 pg/mL (reference: 0–125 pg/mL).

Key imaging studies encompassed MRI of both femurs, echocardiography, cardiac MRI, liver ultrasound with Doppler, high-resolution CT of the chest, and abdominal MRI. MRI of the femurs demonstrated mild, symmetric edema of the muscles bilaterally, most prominent in the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps, along with subcutaneous edema on the lateral thighs bilaterally (see figure 2). Echocardiography revealed an estimated left ventricular ejection fraction of 65%–70% and a right ventricular systolic pressure measuring 46 mm Hg. Cardiac MRI identified mild hypokinesis involving the mid-anteroseptal and anterior walls, accompanied by myocardial edema without clear late gadolinium enhancement, suggesting possible myocardial inflammation or early myocarditis, or potentially a localized stress cardiomyopathy. There was no notable evidence of myocardial scarring, infarction, or relevant valvular abnormality. Liver ultrasound with Doppler detected hepatic steatosis. High-resolution chest CT did not demonstrate interstitial lung disease or lymphadenopathy. Abdominal MRI revealed steatotic hepatomegaly but was otherwise unremarkable without lymphadenopathy.

Additional diagnostic evaluations included a repeat liver biopsy as well as a biopsy of the thigh muscle. The liver biopsy, conducted during her hospital stay, showed several sections of hepatic tissue exhibiting macrovesicular steatosis with both small and large fat droplets, which involved about 70% of the examined liver parenchyma, without evidence of significant portal inflammation or interface hepatitis. These results aligned with her prior diagnosis of hepatosteatosis, indicating mild advancement of her hepatic pathology to a NAFLD Activity Score of 5/8, stage 1. A biopsy taken from the right vastus lateralis revealed scattered necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers (figure 3A,B), and immunohistochemical analysis identified acid phosphatase staining (figure 3D) and positive staining for cluster of Differentiation-68 (CD68), a marker for macrophages, demonstrating myophagocytosis (figure 4B). Furthermore, membrane attack complex staining was present on both necrotic and non-necrotic muscle fibers (figure 4C), indicative of an inflammatory reaction. There were occasional cells positive for CD3, a marker for T-cells (figure 4A), along with perivascular inflammatory infiltrates (figure 3C). The collective muscle biopsy findings were consistent with the diagnosis of immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM).

Differential diagnosis:

The patient exhibited a notable and rapid onset of symptoms following her first and only administration of semaglutide, indicating intolerance. She initially developed gastrointestinal disturbances and acute renal failure, likely due to hypovolemia. Although creatine kinase (CK) levels were only mildly increased initially and did not indicate rhabdomyolysis, her condition continued to deteriorate even after stopping semaglutide, with progressive muscle weakness and a 12-fold elevation in CK over the subsequent four months, pointing toward an immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) likely linked to semaglutide. Histopathological findings characteristic of drug-induced IMNM, absence of clinical signs suggestive of a concurrent connective tissue disorder, and persistent clinical improvement following initial immunosuppression and discontinuation of semaglutide further reinforce this possible association.

It is worth noting that transaminitis was present before initiation of semaglutide, a finding observed in some myopathies. Nevertheless, three years before starting semaglutide, she had histologically confirmed hepatic steatosis, and her mildly increased AST and ALT remained even after CK values returned to normal. This suggests that her chronically raised transaminases were likely hepatic in etiology rather than attributable to muscle injury. Additionally, her cardiac evaluation did not yield definitive evidence of ongoing myocarditis. Given her swift improvement, stress-related myopathy would also be a plausible consideration in this context.

The detection of SS-B antibody in isolation, with negative ANA and SS-A, was unanticipated. No previous literature has reported cases where sole positivity for SS-B antibody alongside negative ANA and SS-A results in IMNM. A CK level prior to starting semaglutide was not available, precluding confirmation of whether a subclinical autoimmune IMNM existed before semaglutide therapy began. Nonetheless, the twelvefold increase in CK and the onset of progressive proximal muscle weakness subsequent to semaglutide administration suggest that the IMNM episode may be related to the medication. Moreover, the patient's severe IMNM resolved following a brief course of corticosteroids and IVIG, in combination with discontinuation of semaglutide. This clinical response is more suggestive of drug-induced IMNM, which often improves with short-term immunosuppression upon cessation of the inciting agent, as opposed to idiopathic IMNM, which typically necessitates extended and more intensive immunosuppressive treatment.

Treatment:

She received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for 5 days, after which she was transitioned to oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg per day. Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at a total dose of 2 g/kg, divided over 4 days, in response to ongoing muscle weakness and pronounced disease severity. During multiple outpatient visits, she demonstrated ongoing gains in function and overall well-being, with creatine kinase levels remaining within normal limits. As a result, her prednisone was able to be tapered successfully and quickly over a 2-month period. Owing to financial factors and persistent improvement after discontinuing semaglutide, no further IVIG infusions were required beyond her initial hospital treatment.

Outcome and follow-up:

At her most recent outpatient follow-up, occurring 10 months after the first assessment and the commencement of therapy, she had returned to her pre-illness level of function and had been without immunosuppressive medications for 7 months. Her creatine kinase levels stayed within normal limits after hospitalization and immunosuppressive treatment. Subsequent echocardiogram and cardiac MRI demonstrated resolution of the previously noted abnormalities. Additionally, paroxysmal atrial fibrillation did not recur.

Discussion:

GLP-1 receptor agonists like semaglutide are increasingly utilized and have shown efficacy in managing metabolic conditions such as obesity and type 2 diabetes. These agents most frequently cause gastrointestinal side effects, but they may also contribute to reduced muscle mass and, in some cases, rhabdomyolysis. In this instance, the patient’s presentation aligns more closely with IMNM rather than rhabdomyolysis. The patient’s CK levels rose by a factor of 12 over a four-month period after a single semaglutide dose; by contrast, a direct drug toxicity from semaglutide would typically lead to normalization of CK within a few weeks, as observed in other drug-induced rhabdomyolysis reports. Importantly, a prior report of rhabdomyolysis secondary to tirzepatide featured the usual pattern: the patient exhibited an acute and significant elevation of CK (CK=57 014 at presentation), followed by swift improvement after discontinuing the drug and initiating hydration over several days, without immunosuppressive therapy.

Based on the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) Causality Categories and Criteria, the association between semaglutide administration and the onset of IMNM in this patient is likely, as the temporal relationship is plausible, alternative causes or medications are improbable, and the withdrawal of semaglutide led to an appropriate clinical improvement. Rechallenging the patient would not be ethical, considering the severity of the adverse event associated with semaglutide. Nonetheless, we recognize that a definitive causal link cannot be established from this single case. Given the rapidly increasing utilization of semaglutide, the purpose of this report is to alert clinicians to this possible side effect and promote increased pharmacovigilance. Ongoing and future research will help determine if this association is indeed causal or merely coincidental.

The underlying factors that may have contributed to our patient's heightened sensitivity to semaglutide, resulting in this pronounced reaction, remain uncertain. With the widespread use of GLP-1 receptor agonists, investigations into susceptibility factors will be critical, especially if additional reports of temporally related cases with semaglutide arise.
