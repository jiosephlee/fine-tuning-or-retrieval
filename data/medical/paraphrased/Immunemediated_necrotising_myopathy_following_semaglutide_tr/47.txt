Title: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

Brian Lam, Eleni Tiniakou, Xinhai Robert Zhang, Shervin Assassi

Abstract:

Summary: This case report details a woman in her 60s who developed immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) following administration of semaglutide. She experienced a 4-month progression of dysphagia, dysphonia, proximal muscle weakness, and elevated creatine kinase. Her clinical presentation was preceded by a single dose of semaglutide. Diagnostic evaluation revealed necrotising myopathy with inflammatory infiltrates on muscle biopsy. The patient was treated with corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), resulting in gradual symptom improvement. She ultimately regained her baseline functional abilities after discontinuing immunosuppressive therapy for several months and continued avoiding glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists. This represents the first documented instance of IMNM likely linked to GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy, demonstrating apparent benefit from brief immunosuppressive and IVIG treatment.

Background:

Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have gained widespread use in the management of metabolic disorders such as type 2 diabetes and obesity. Although generally well tolerated, this medication class has been linked to adverse outcomes, including reductions in skeletal muscle mass. Recently, tirzepatide—another frequently prescribed GLP-1 receptor agonist—was implicated in a case of rhabdomyolysis with necrotizing features. The patient in question experienced a marked elevation in creatine kinase (CK) levels, reaching values in the thousands during active therapy. Following cessation of tirzepatide, CK levels began to decline within a few days, a response that aligns with drug-induced rhabdomyolysis.

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) represents a unique form of muscle disease, typified by rapidly progressive weakness affecting the proximal muscles, accompanied by necrosis of muscle fibers with few lymphocytic infiltrates evident upon histological examination. IMNM is strongly linked to two autoantibodies: anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (SRP). Notably, statins—frequently prescribed for metabolic conditions—are closely connected to the presence of HMGCR autoantibodies. Prior to recognition of this relationship, individuals on statins who developed necrotising myopathy were often categorized under toxic myopathy or polymyositis. In numerous instances, affected patients experienced ongoing muscle symptoms and elevated creatine kinase levels even after discontinuing statin therapy. These persistent findings, in combination with a favorable response to immunosuppressive treatment, implied an autoimmune etiology and ultimately facilitated the identification of HMGCR autoantibody as a defining marker for IMNM. It is important to note, however, that approximately one-third of IMNM patients do not have detectable autoantibodies. The absence of known autoantibodies in these cases adds complexity to diagnosis and indicates that there may be additional related autoantibodies and precipitating factors yet to be identified.

Case presentation:

A woman in her sixth decade of life, whose medical background included obesity, hepatic steatosis, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and a remote episode of deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism, presented with gradually worsening weakness of the proximal muscles, difficulty swallowing, and voice changes.

Prior to the onset of her current symptoms, she had enjoyed good health and was fully autonomous in daily functioning. Three years before her decline, she underwent ambulatory assessment due to elevated liver enzymes. During this period, laboratory findings revealed mild increases in AST and ALT, both approximately double the normal threshold, with ALT exceeding AST. Levels of alkaline phosphatase, albumin, bilirubin, and platelets remained within normal parameters. She reported no alcohol consumption and was not exposed to any hepatotoxic drugs. Imaging via abdominal ultrasound revealed an enlarged liver with diffusely increased echogenicity, likely indicative of hepatic steatosis. Serological testing for viral hepatitis, along with anti-nuclear, anti-mitochondrial, anti-smooth muscle, and liver-kidney microsomal antibodies, yielded negative results. Histological examination of the liver demonstrated steatohepatitis compatible with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) and an NAFLD Activity Score of 4 out of 8, with stage 0 fibrosis. Lifestyle recommendations included adherence to a caloric restriction diet and routine physical activity.

Owing to ongoing fatty liver and obesity 2.5 years post-NAFLD diagnosis, semaglutide therapy was started. Shortly after administration of her initial semaglutide dose (0.25 mg subcutaneously), she developed nausea and vomiting. These symptoms persisted, leading to hospital admission 2 days later with continued nausea, vomiting, reduced oral intake, dyspnoea, acute renal failure, and pronounced metabolic acidosis. Management included intravenous fluids, bicarbonate, and anti-emetics. She was temporarily transferred to the intensive care unit for non-invasive ventilation. Her renal function subsequently recovered with hydration and did not require haemofiltration or haemodialysis. On admission, creatine kinase (CK) was measured at 337 U/L, but she did not report associated symptoms such as rash, muscle pain, or dark-coloured urine. During her stay, she developed paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, although her serum troponin remained within normal limits. She was discharged after 5 days; gastrointestinal symptoms had resolved, though she was deconditioned. Over the next 4 months, she noticed progressive weakness. She reported no previous statin use, no significant intake of supplements linked to IMNM (e.g., mushrooms or red yeast rice), or any other nutritional supplements. She also denied a notable family history of myopathy, recent excessive or sustained physical exertion, and alcohol or illicit drug use.

Four months following her first and only semaglutide dose, her condition continued to worsen. She required assistance to stand or sit upright and began to experience difficulty swallowing both liquids and solids over several days. As a result, she sought care at an external hospital where her creatine kinase level was found to be elevated at 3992 U/L (reference range: 12–191 U/L) upon admission. Neurology, consulted via telemedicine, recommended a regimen of intravenous methylprednisolone 1000 mg daily for 5 days. Subsequently, she was transferred to a tertiary care center for rheumatology assessment, at which time she appeared afebrile, had normal blood pressure, and showed no signs of distress. Examination demonstrated marked weakness, predominantly affecting proximal muscle groups (see table 1). No rashes, skin abnormalities, lung crackles, synovitis, edema, sicca symptoms, oral ulcers, or other neurological deficits were observed.

After receiving pulse methylprednisolone, the patient was placed on a daily oral prednisone regimen at 1 mg/kg. Due to ongoing muscle weakness and pronounced symptoms, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at 2 g/kg over 4 days. Over the subsequent 2 weeks, her CK levels demonstrated substantial improvement (see figure 1). She was discharged following an inpatient stay of nearly 4 weeks. Twelve days post-discharge, she was readmitted for 10 days with septic shock secondary to a pseudomonal urinary tract infection, during which her CK was found to be within normal limits.

She attended multiple outpatient visits where she demonstrated ongoing progress in both functional ability and general health, with CK levels remaining within the normal range throughout. This allowed for a swift tapering of prednisone. Given financial considerations and her significant recovery following cessation of semaglutide, she did not undergo any further IVIG therapy beyond the initial regimen administered during her hospital stay.

At her most recent outpatient appointment, which took place 10 months after initial assessment and commencement of treatment, she had fully returned to her baseline level of function and had been free from immunosuppressive therapy for 8 months.

Investigations:

Her laboratory evaluation demonstrated a creatine kinase (CK) level of 1237 U/L at the time of assessment, with a previous maximum value of 3992 U/L recorded one week earlier at the referring hospital. Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) was found to be within the normal range. Aspartate aminotransferase (AST) measured 191 U/L and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) was 165 U/L, in the context of increased CK levels and a known history of fatty liver disease. Autoantibody testing revealed negative results for anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-Sjögren’s syndrome antibodies type A (anti-SSA), anti-Smith antibodies, anti-U1 ribonucleoprotein (anti-U1 RNP), anti-double-stranded DNA, antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), and anti-mitochondrial antibodies. Complement levels and creatinine were normal, and there was no evidence of cytopenia. A myositis antibody panel returned negative for signal recognition particle (SRP), HMG-CoA reductase (HMGCR), as well as Jo-1, Pl-7, Pl-12, EJ, OJ, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-γ, NXP-2, and cN-1A antibodies. However, SS-B autoantibody was positive at a low titer of 2.4 (reference range <1.0). Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was raised at 22 mm/hour, and C-reactive protein was also increased at 22.8 mg/L. Additionally, high-sensitivity troponin (HS-Troponin) was elevated to 62 pg/mL (reference ≤53 pg/mL), and pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (proBNP) was significantly increased at 4358 pg/mL (reference: 0–125 pg/mL).

Relevant imaging studies consisted of MRI scans of both femurs, echocardiogram, cardiac MRI, liver ultrasound with Doppler, high-resolution chest CT, and abdominal MRI. The MRI of the bilateral femurs demonstrated mild, symmetric edema in both muscles, most prominently in the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps, as well as subcutaneous edema lateral to both thighs (figure 2). Echocardiographic assessment revealed a left ventricular ejection fraction estimated at 65%–70% and a right ventricular systolic pressure measured at 46 mm Hg. The cardiac MRI identified mild hypokinesis of the mid-anteroseptal and anterior walls, in conjunction with myocardial edema, but no clear late gadolinium enhancement. These findings may suggest myocardial inflammation or early myocarditis, or alternatively, a localized stress-induced cardiomyopathy. There was no evidence of significant myocardial scar formation, infarction, or major valvular abnormalities. Liver ultrasound with Doppler indicated the presence of hepatosteatosis. The high-resolution chest CT did not reveal interstitial lung disease or lymphadenopathy. Abdominal MRI showed hepatomegaly with steatosis but was otherwise unremarkable and free of lymphadenopathy.

Further specialized testing included a second liver biopsy and a biopsy of thigh muscle. The liver biopsy, conducted during her hospitalization, revealed several samples of hepatic tissue showing both small and large droplet macrovesicular steatosis involving about 70% of the examined parenchyma, without notable portal inflammation or interface hepatitis. These results aligned with her known hepatosteatosis and suggested mild progression of the underlying hepatic disorder, reflected by a NAFLD Activity Score of 5 out of 8 and stage 1. Biopsy from the right vastus lateralis showed scattered necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers (figure 3A,B). Immunohistochemical analysis displayed positive acid phosphatase staining (figure 3D) and CD68, a marker for macrophages, indicating myophagocytosis (figure 4B). Membrane attack complex staining was observed on both necrotic and intact muscle fibers (figure 4C), indicative of an inflammatory process. Additionally, a small number of CD3-positive T cells (figure 4A) and perivascular inflammatory infiltrates (figure 3C) were identified. Altogether, these muscle biopsy results supported the diagnosis of immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM).

Differential diagnosis:

The patient exhibited a rapid and pronounced response following her first and only administration of semaglutide, indicative of drug intolerance. Her presenting manifestations involved gastrointestinal disturbances and acute renal failure, presumed secondary to hypovolemia. At the outset, her creatine kinase (CK) was only mildly elevated and did not meet criteria for rhabdomyolysis. Despite discontinuation of semaglutide, her condition deteriorated, with progressive weakness in a pattern consistent with immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM), and her CK increased twelvefold over four months subsequent to semaglutide exposure, pointing toward IMNM likely linked to the medication. Additionally, pathology findings demonstrated features typical of drug-induced IMNM, and absence of other signs of connective tissue disease, coupled with continued improvement after initial immunosuppressive therapy and withdrawal of semaglutide, further reinforce the proposed relationship.

It is significant that elevated transaminases were present before she began semaglutide therapy, a phenomenon that may occur in myopathies. Nevertheless, hepatosteatosis had been confirmed by liver biopsy three years prior to semaglutide initiation, and mild increases in AST and ALT continued even after her CK returned to normal. Therefore, it is probable that her chronic transaminase elevation is attributable to hepatic causes rather than muscle injury. Additionally, her cardiac evaluation did not definitively indicate ongoing myocarditis. Considering the clinical context and her swift improvement, stress-related myopathy remains a plausible explanation.

Her isolated SS-B antibody positivity alongside negative ANA and SS-A results was unusual. The literature does not document cases where sole SS-B antibody presence, with negative ANA and SS-A, precedes IMNM. Regrettably, we did not have access to a CK value prior to the initiation of semaglutide, making it impossible to rule out the existence of subclinical autoimmune IMNM before semaglutide therapy. Nonetheless, the 12-fold increase in CK and gradual proximal muscle weakness observed subsequent to starting semaglutide suggest this IMNM episode is likely medication-induced. Furthermore, her severe IMNM improved after a brief course of corticosteroids and IVIG in combination with discontinuing semaglutide. This clinical trajectory is consistent with drug-associated IMNM, which typically responds to short-term immunosuppressive treatment following cessation of the causative medication, as opposed to idiopathic IMNM, which generally requires sustained and intensive immunosuppression.

Treatment:

She received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for 5 days, then transitioned to oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg per day. Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at 2 g/kg, divided over 4 days, because of her ongoing significant weakness and severe symptoms. Outpatient follow-up occurred multiple times, demonstrating continued gains in functional ability and general health, with creatine kinase (CK) levels remaining within the normal range throughout. Based on this progress, prednisone was tapered off successfully over a 2-month period. No further IVIG was provided after the initial hospitalization course, due to her ongoing clinical improvement without semaglutide and consideration of costs.

Outcome and follow-up:

Ten months after her initial assessment and commencement of therapy, she attended her final outpatient visit and had returned to her pre-illness level of functioning, having discontinued immunosuppressive medications for 7 months. Her creatine kinase concentrations continued to be within normal limits after hospitalization and immunosuppressive treatment. Subsequent echocardiogram and cardiac MRI demonstrated resolution of previously observed abnormalities. She likewise had no further episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation.

Discussion:

GLP-1 receptor agonists like semaglutide are increasingly prescribed and have shown efficacy in treating metabolic conditions such as obesity and type 2 diabetes. While gastrointestinal side effects are frequent, these agents may also be linked to reduced muscle mass and, in rare cases, rhabdomyolysis. In this report, the patient’s presentation aligns more closely with IMNM rather than rhabdomyolysis. Creatine kinase (CK) levels rose to 12 times the baseline over a four-month period after a single semaglutide dose, whereas a toxic reaction to semaglutide resulting in rhabdomyolysis would typically lead to CK normalization within a few weeks, as described in other drug-induced rhabdomyolysis cases. Importantly, the previously documented instance of rhabdomyolysis associated with tirzepatide followed the expected clinical trajectory: the individual had a substantial acute CK spike (CK=57 014 at admission) and a rapid recovery upon discontinuation of the drug and administration of hydration, achieved within days and without the need for immunosuppressive therapy.

According to the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) Causality Categories and Criteria, there is probable causality linking semaglutide administration to the development of IMNM in our patient. This assessment is based on an appropriate temporal association, the low likelihood that IMNM arose from other illnesses or medications, and an expected improvement following discontinuation of semaglutide. Conducting a rechallenge would not be ethically acceptable in this context due to the severity of the adverse event. Nonetheless, we recognize that causality cannot be definitively established in this instance. With the increasing use of semaglutide, this case report seeks to inform clinicians about this possible side effect and promote greater pharmacovigilance. Additional research will be necessary to determine whether this relationship is indeed causal or merely coincidental.

The reason our patient may have been susceptible to this pronounced reaction after starting semaglutide remains uncertain. As GLP-1 receptor agonists gain wider use, investigations into potential predisposing factors will be especially important if additional cases exhibiting a similar timing of symptom onset in relation to semaglutide therapy are documented.
