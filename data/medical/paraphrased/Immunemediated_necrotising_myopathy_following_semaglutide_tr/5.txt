Title: Semaglutide therapy and the onset of immune-mediated necrotising myopathy: a possible association?

Brian Lam, Eleni Tiniakou, Xinhai Robert Zhang, Shervin Assassi

Abstract:

Summary: This case report presents a woman in her 60s who developed immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) following administration of semaglutide. Over a period of four months, she experienced worsening symptoms including dysphagia, dysphonia, proximal muscle weakness, and elevated creatine kinase levels, all of which began after a single semaglutide dose. Diagnostic evaluation showed evidence of necrotising myopathy with inflammatory cell infiltration on muscle biopsy. Treatment with corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) led to clinical improvement. She eventually recovered to her pre-illness functional state and maintained this without ongoing immunosuppressive therapy, provided she continued to avoid glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists. This report documents, to our knowledge, the first case of IMNM potentially linked to GLP-1 receptor agonist exposure, demonstrating a favorable response to brief immunosuppressive and IVIG therapy.

Background:

Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have gained widespread application in the management of metabolic disorders such as type 2 diabetes and obesity in recent years. However, treatment with these agents has been associated with certain adverse outcomes, including the reduction of skeletal muscle mass. Notably, a recent case report detailed an instance of rhabdomyolysis with necrotising characteristics linked to tirzepatide, another GLP-1 receptor agonist widely utilized in clinical practice. In this report, the patient exhibited marked elevations in creatine kinase (CK) levels—reaching into the thousands—during ongoing therapy. Importantly, cessation of the medication led to a prompt decline in CK levels over the subsequent days, supporting the diagnosis of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis.

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) represents a unique subtype of myopathy marked by severe weakness of proximal muscles, the presence of necrotic muscle fibers, and minimal lymphocytic infiltration on histological analysis. The autoantibodies anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (SRP) display a strong correlation with IMNM. Notably, statin medications, widely prescribed for metabolic conditions, are closely linked with the appearance of HMGCR autoantibodies. Prior to the recognition of the association between HMGCR autoantibodies and IMNM, individuals who exhibited necrotising myopathy following statin exposure were often misclassified as having either toxic myopathy or polymyositis. In these cases, muscle symptoms and elevated CK levels frequently persisted even after discontinuing statin therapy. The observation that such patients improved with immunosuppressive treatment indicated an underlying autoimmune mechanism, eventually culminating in the identification of the HMGCR autoantibody as a defining marker of IMNM. It should be emphasized, however, that approximately one third of IMNM cases do not have detectable autoantibodies. This seronegativity not only complicates diagnostic efforts, but also implies the existence of as yet unidentified autoantibodies or triggers involved in IMNM pathogenesis.

Case presentation:

A woman in her sixties, whose past medical history includes obesity, hepatosteatosis, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and a distant episode of deep venous thrombosis and pulmonary embolism, presented with gradually worsening proximal muscle weakness, difficulty swallowing, and voice changes.

Previously, the patient enjoyed good health and maintained full independence in her daily activities. Approximately three years before her clinical decline, she was evaluated as an outpatient for elevated transaminases. During this period, laboratory tests revealed mildly increased levels of aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT), both up to about two times the upper normal limit, with ALT exceeding AST. Results for alkaline phosphatase, albumin, bilirubin, and platelet counts remained within normal parameters. She reported no alcohol consumption and was not on any medications known to induce liver toxicity. Imaging via abdominal ultrasound identified hepatomegaly accompanied by a diffuse increase in echotexture, findings most consistent with hepatic steatosis. Serological testing for viral hepatitis and autoantibodies—including anti-nuclear, anti-mitochondrial, anti-smooth muscle, and liver-kidney microsomal antibodies—were all negative. Liver biopsy demonstrated steatohepatitis indicative of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), with a NAFLD Activity Score of grade 4/8 and stage 0 fibrosis. The patient was counseled to adopt a low-calorie diet and engage in regular physical activity.

Owing to persistent fatty liver and ongoing obesity 2.5 years after her NAFLD diagnosis, semaglutide therapy was commenced. Shortly after her initial administration of semaglutide (0.25 mg subcutaneously), she developed symptoms of nausea and vomiting. These symptoms continued, prompting hospital admission two days later due to persistent nausea, vomiting, insufficient oral intake, shortness of breath, acute renal failure, and marked metabolic acidosis. Management included intravenous fluids, bicarbonate administration, and anti-emetic medications. She required brief transfer to the intensive care unit for non-invasive ventilatory support. Following intravenous hydration, her renal function recovered without requiring haemodialysis or haemofiltration. Creatine kinase levels at admission were elevated at 337 U/L, though she did not exhibit other features such as rash, muscle discomfort, or dark (cola-colored) urine. During her stay, she experienced paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, though troponin levels remained within normal limits. She was discharged after five days; her gastrointestinal symptoms had resolved, though she remained physically deconditioned. Over the next four months, she noted gradually worsening weakness. She reported no previous statin exposure, no significant ingestion of IMNM-associated supplements such as mushrooms or red yeast rice, and no other dietary supplements. There was no family history of myopathy, nor had she engaged in excessive or unaccustomed physical activity, or used alcohol or illicit substances.

Four months following her initial and sole administration of semaglutide, the patient’s condition further deteriorated. She became unable to either stand or maintain an upright sitting position without assistance and reported experiencing dysphagia to both liquids and solids over the preceding several days. As a result, she sought care at an external hospital, where laboratory testing showed her CK level was elevated at 3992 U/L (reference interval: 12–191 U/L) at the time of admission. Management included administration of intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for five days, overseen by neurology through telemedicine consultation. Subsequently, she was transferred to a tertiary care facility for further rheumatological assessment, during which she was afebrile, had normal blood pressure, and appeared generally well. On physical exam, marked weakness was documented, particularly affecting the proximal muscles (see table 1). No skin findings such as rashes or changes, respiratory abnormalities (crackles), joint findings (synovitis), peripheral oedema, sicca symptoms, oral ulcers, or other neurologic deficits were observed.

After receiving pulse methylprednisolone, the patient began a daily regimen of oral prednisone at a dose of 1 mg/kg. Due to ongoing weakness and pronounced symptoms, she was administered intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) at 2 g/kg, divided across 4 days. Over the subsequent 2 weeks, her CK levels improved significantly (figure 1). She was ultimately discharged following an almost 4-week hospital stay. Twelve days post-discharge, she required readmission for 10 days because of septic shock secondary to a pseudomonal urinary tract infection; during this episode, her CK levels remained within the normal range.

She underwent multiple outpatient follow-up visits, during which her functional status and general health continued to progress, and her CK levels remained consistently within the normal range. Consequently, her prednisone was tapered and discontinued in a short period. Given both the financial implications and her sustained recovery following the cessation of semaglutide, she did not require further IVIG beyond the initial treatment she received while hospitalized.

At her most recent outpatient follow-up, conducted 10 months following her initial assessment and commencement of therapy, she had returned to her prior level of functioning and had been without immunosuppressive medication for the preceding 8 months.

Investigations:

Her laboratory investigations revealed an elevated CK level of 1237 U/L at the time of assessment, with a prior maximum value of 3992 U/L recorded one week earlier at the referring facility. Thyroid function, as measured by TSH, was unremarkable and within normal range. Liver enzymes were increased, with an AST of 191 U/L and ALT of 165 U/L, in the context of raised serum CK and a documented history of fatty liver. The patient’s autoantibody screening showed negative results for anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-Sjogren’s syndrome antibodies type A (anti-SSA), anti-Smith antibodies, anti-U1 ribonucleoprotein antibodies (anti-U1 RNP), anti-double-stranded DNA, ANCA, and anti-mitochondrial antibodies. Complement components and serum creatinine were both within normal limits, and no evidence of cytopenias was found. Her myositis antibody panel did not detect autoantibodies to SRP, HMGCR, Jo-1, Pl-7, Pl-12, EJ, OJ, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-y, NXP-2, or cN-1A. However, SS-B autoantibody was detected at a low titre (2.4; normal range: <1.0). Both ESR and C-reactive protein were raised, at 22 mm/hour and 22.8 mg/L, respectively. Additionally, notable findings included an elevated HS-Troponin at 62 pg/mL (reference: ≤53 pg/mL) and an increased pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (proBNP) of 4358 pg/mL (reference interval: 0–125 pg/mL).

The patient underwent a comprehensive imaging workup, including MRI scans of both femurs, echocardiography, cardiac MRI, liver ultrasound with Doppler, high-resolution CT of the chest, and MRI of the abdomen. The femoral MRI indicated mild and symmetric oedema within the muscles of both legs, especially pronounced in the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps, along with subcutaneous oedema on the lateral sides of both thighs (figure 2). Echocardiographic assessment demonstrated a left ventricular ejection fraction estimated at 65%–70%, with a right ventricular systolic pressure recorded at 46 mm Hg. Findings from the cardiac MRI included mild hypokinesis localized to the mid-anteroseptal and anterior myocardial wall, accompanied by myocardial oedema but lacking definite late gadolinium enhancement, raising suspicion for myocardial inflammation or early myocarditis versus a focal, stress-induced cardiomyopathy. There was no evidence of substantial myocardial infarct, scar formation, or significant valvular abnormality. The liver ultrasound with Doppler revealed the presence of hepatosteatosis. High-resolution CT imaging of the chest excluded both interstitial lung disease and lymphadenopathy. Abdominal MRI confirmed hepatomegaly due to steatosis, with otherwise normal findings and no evidence of lymphadenopathy.

Further diagnostic evaluation included conducting another liver biopsy as well as a biopsy of the thigh muscle. The liver biopsy, which was performed during her hospital stay, showed multiple cores of hepatic tissue displaying both small and large droplet macrovesicular steatosis that involved roughly 70% of the sampled hepatic parenchyma. There was an absence of significant portal inflammation or interface hepatitis. These results aligned with the patient's established diagnosis of hepatosteatosis, with mild advancement to NAFLD Activity Score grade 5/8, stage 1. Examination of the right vastus lateralis muscle biopsy revealed scattered areas of muscle fiber necrosis and regeneration (figure 3A,B). Immunohistochemical analyses demonstrated positivity for acid phosphatase (figure 3D) as well as cluster of Differentiation-68 (CD68), a macrophage-associated marker, indicative of myophagocytosis (figure 4B). Additionally, a membrane attack complex was identified on both necrotic and non-necrotic myofibres (figure 4C), suggesting an inflammatory process. A few CD3-positive cells, which are indicative of T-cells (figure 4A), along with perivascular infiltrates of inflammatory cells (figure 3C), were also observed. Overall, the muscle biopsy findings were indicative of IMNM.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient displayed an acute and pronounced adverse response following her first and only administration of semaglutide, indicating possible intolerance to the medication. Her early presentation involved gastrointestinal disturbances and acute renal failure, which was presumed to be a consequence of hypovolaemia. Although her creatine kinase (CK) levels were only mildly elevated at the time of initial assessment—levels not typical for rhabdomyolysis—her clinical status worsened despite discontinuation of semaglutide. This progression, coupled with the anatomical distribution of muscle weakness and a twelvefold escalation in CK over four months after cessation of the drug, points to the development of IMNM potentially triggered by semaglutide. Additionally, biopsy findings demonstrated histopathological patterns compatible with drug-induced IMNM. The absence of clinical indications for an underlying connective tissue disorder and the marked and persistent improvement following both withdrawal of semaglutide and initiation of immunosuppression provide further corroboration for this association.

It is important to highlight that the patient exhibited transaminaemia before commencing semaglutide, a phenomenon observed in certain myopathies. Nonetheless, a liver biopsy performed three years earlier confirmed hepatosteatosis, and her mildly elevated AST/ALT persisted even after normalization of CK levels. This suggests that the persistent transaminase elevation likely stemmed from a hepatic source rather than from muscle injury. Furthermore, her cardiac assessment did not provide definitive evidence for active myocarditis. Considering the rapid improvement observed, a diagnosis of stress-induced myopathy would also be plausible in this context.

The presence of a positive SS-B antibody alongside negative ANA and SS-A results was an unusual finding. To date, there are no documented cases reporting isolated SS-B positivity with negative ANA and SS-A preceding the development of IMNM. Regrettably, we were unable to obtain CK levels from before the patient began semaglutide therapy, so a pre-existing, subclinical autoimmune IMNM cannot be ruled out with certainty. Nevertheless, the twelvefold elevation in CK and the emergence of progressive proximal muscle weakness after initiating semaglutide use strongly suggest a medication-induced episode of IMNM. Furthermore, the patient's severe IMNM responded rapidly to a brief course of corticosteroids and IVIG, combined with discontinuation of semaglutide. Such a clinical trajectory is more characteristic of drug-induced IMNM, which typically shows improvement with short-term immunosuppression following withdrawal of the causative medication, as opposed to idiopathic IMNM, which generally requires long-term, aggressive immunosuppressive management.

Treatment:

She received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for 5 days, then transitioned to oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg per day. In addition, IVIG was administered at a total dose of 2 g/kg, divided across 4 days because of ongoing weakness and pronounced symptoms. During multiple outpatient follow-up appointments, she exhibited progressive enhancement in both functional capacity and general well-being, with creatine kinase (CK) levels remaining within the normal range. Owing to this sustained clinical improvement, prednisone tapering was accomplished successfully and relatively quickly over 2 months. Taking into account financial factors and her stable improvement after discontinuation of semaglutide, no further IVIG therapy was provided beyond the initial hospital course.

Outcome and follow-up:

At her most recent outpatient follow-up, conducted 10 months after her original assessment and the start of therapy, she had returned to her pre-illness level of functioning and had been without immunosuppressive therapy for 7 months. Her CK values stayed within the normal limits subsequent to her hospital stay and immunosuppressive treatment. Follow-up echocardiogram and cardiac MRI demonstrated resolution of the abnormalities previously identified. Additionally, there was no recurrence of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation.

Discussion:

GLP-1 receptor agonists, including semaglutide, are increasingly prescribed and have shown efficacy in treating metabolic conditions such as type 2 diabetes and obesity. While gastrointestinal side effects are most frequently reported, there is also emerging evidence that these agents may contribute to reduced muscle mass and, in rare instances, rhabdomyolysis. In the current case, the clinical progression aligns more closely with IMNM than with rhabdomyolysis. After a single dose of semaglutide, the patient’s CK values rose twelvefold over four months, whereas drug-induced rhabdomyolysis would typically manifest with a return of CK to normal within several weeks if a direct toxic mechanism from semaglutide were responsible. In comparison, a prior report describing rhabdomyolysis due to tirzepatide detailed a classic presentation: a sharp, acute rise in CK (CK=57 014 at initial assessment), followed by rapid clinical improvement upon cessation of the drug and initiation of hydration therapy, occurring within a few days and without requiring immunosuppressive treatment.

According to the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) Causality Categories and Criteria, the link between semaglutide administration and the development of IMNM in this patient appears probable. This assessment is based on a plausible temporal association, the lack of alternative explanations such as other diseases or medications, and significant clinical improvement following discontinuation of semaglutide. Ethical concerns preclude re-exposure (rechallenge) to semaglutide due to the severity of the initial reaction. Nonetheless, we recognize that definitive causality cannot be established in this instance. Given the rapidly increasing use of semaglutide, our report seeks to alert clinicians to this potential serious adverse effect and to promote enhanced pharmacovigilance. Further research will be necessary to determine whether this observed association is causal or merely coincidental.

The factors that may have contributed to our patient's pronounced response to semaglutide are still not well understood. As the use of GLP-1 receptor agonists becomes increasingly widespread, future research that investigates underlying susceptibility will be essential, particularly if more instances with a clear temporal association to semaglutide administration arise.
