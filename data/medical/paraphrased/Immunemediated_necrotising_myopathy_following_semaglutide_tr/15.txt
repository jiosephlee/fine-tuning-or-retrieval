Title: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

Brian Lam, Eleni Tiniakou, Xinhai Robert Zhang, Shervin Assassi

Abstract:

Summary: This case report presents a woman in her 60s who developed immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) following administration of semaglutide. Over four months, she experienced worsening dysphagia, dysphonia, proximal muscle weakness, and increased creatine kinase levels. Her symptoms emerged after a single semaglutide dose. Diagnostic evaluation demonstrated findings characteristic of necrotising myopathy, including inflammatory infiltration on muscle biopsy. She exhibited clinical improvement following treatment with corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG). Ultimately, the patient regained her baseline level of function after discontinuing immunosuppression for several months and refraining from further use of glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists. This report details the first known instance of IMNM potentially linked to GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy, with symptomatic resolution following a brief course of immunosuppression and IVIG.

Background:

In recent years, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have been widely prescribed for metabolic conditions such as type 2 diabetes and obesity. Loss of skeletal muscle mass is among the side effects reported for this medication class. A recent case report noted rhabdomyolysis with necrotizing characteristics linked to tirzepatide, another GLP-1 receptor agonist frequently used. In that report, the patient experienced creatine kinase (CK) elevations into the thousands during ongoing therapy, with levels beginning to decrease within several days of stopping the medication, findings that align with drug-induced rhabdomyolysis.

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) represents a unique subtype of myopathy defined by rapidly progressive proximal muscle weakness, the presence of necrotic muscle fibers, and minimal lymphocytic infiltration observed on histopathology. IMNM is strongly associated with two autoantibodies: anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (SRP). Notably, statin therapy—a common treatment for metabolic conditions—is closely linked to HMGCR autoantibody-positive IMNM. Before the recognition of the HMGCR autoantibody’s role, patients who developed necrotising myopathy after statin exposure were frequently misclassified as having toxic myopathy or polymyositis. In such cases, myopathy and elevated creatine kinase levels persisted even after stopping the statin, yet these patients responded to immunosuppressive therapy—findings consistent with autoimmune myopathy—which ultimately contributed to the identification of the HMGCR autoantibody as a diagnostic marker. Importantly, it is estimated that around one-third of IMNM patients do not have detectable autoantibodies. The absence of a specific autoantibody in this group not only makes diagnosis more complex but also indicates that there may be additional, as-yet-unidentified autoantibodies or triggers associated with IMNM.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 60s, whose medical history included obesity, hepatic steatosis, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and a distant episode of deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism, presented with gradually worsening proximal muscle weakness, difficulty swallowing, and voice changes.

She had been in good health prior to the onset of these symptoms and maintained complete independence in her daily activities. Three years before her current decline, she was evaluated as an outpatient due to increased transaminase levels. At that time, she exhibited mild elevations in aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT), with values approximately double the upper limit of normal and ALT exceeding AST. Alkaline phosphatase, albumin, bilirubin, and platelet count were all found to be within normal parameters. She reported no alcohol consumption and was not on any medications known to cause liver toxicity. Abdominal ultrasonography demonstrated enlarged liver with diffusely increased echogenicity, suggestive of hepatic steatosis. Viral hepatitis markers, as well as tests for anti-nuclear, anti-mitochondrial, anti-smooth muscle, and liver-kidney microsomal antibodies, returned negative results. Liver biopsy was consistent with steatohepatitis, matching a diagnosis of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) with a NAFLD Activity Score of 4/8 and stage 0 fibrosis. She was counseled to adopt a reduced-calorie diet and to engage in regular exercise.

Because of ongoing hepatic steatosis and obesity 2.5 years following her NAFLD diagnosis, semaglutide therapy was initiated. Several hours after her initial dose of semaglutide (0.25 mg administered subcutaneously), she developed nausea and vomiting. These symptoms did not abate, and two days later she was admitted to the hospital for intractable nausea and vomiting, decreased oral intake, shortness of breath, acute renal insufficiency, and marked metabolic acidosis. Management included intravenous fluids and bicarbonate administration, in addition to anti-emetic agents. She required a brief stay in the intensive care unit for non-invasive ventilatory support. Renal function subsequently improved with fluid resuscitation, and neither haemofiltration nor haemodialysis was necessary. Creatine kinase was measured on admission and was elevated at 337 U/L. She had no accompanying findings such as myalgia, cutaneous eruption, or darkened urine. During this admission, she developed episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, though serum troponin was within normal limits. The patient was discharged five days later. By discharge, her gastrointestinal complaints had subsided, though she remained physically deconditioned. Over the ensuing 4 months, she noticed increasing weakness. She reported never having used statins or taken significant amounts of mushrooms, red yeast rice, or other supplements linked to IMNM, nor had she used any other nutritional products. She also denied a family history of muscle disease, any recent periods of excessive or intense exercise, and any alcohol or illicit substance use.

Four months after receiving her first and only dose of semaglutide, the patient’s condition continued to deteriorate. She became unable to stand or sit upright without help, and for several days had developed dysphagia for both solids and liquids. Consequently, she sought care at another hospital, where laboratory work revealed an elevated creatine kinase (CK) level of 3992 U/L (reference: 12–191 U/L) on admission. Treatment was initiated with intravenous methylprednisolone 1000 mg daily for five days, under the guidance of a neurology consultant via telemedicine. Subsequently, she was transferred to a tertiary care center for assessment by rheumatology. Upon arrival, she was afebrile, maintained normal blood pressure, and appeared comfortable. Examination demonstrated marked muscle weakness, especially in proximal groups (see table 1). No rash, skin changes, pulmonary crackles, synovitis, edema, sicca symptoms, oral ulcers, or additional neurologic deficits were observed.

After receiving pulse methylprednisolone, she was started on oral prednisone at a dosage of 1 mg/kg per day. Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at 2 g/kg divided over 4 days, in response to her ongoing weakness and significant clinical severity. Over the subsequent two weeks, her creatine kinase (CK) levels showed substantial decline (figure 1). She was discharged following an inpatient stay of nearly 4 weeks. However, 12 days after discharge, she was re-admitted for 10 days with septic shock secondary to a pseudomonal urinary tract infection, during which her CK remained within normal limits.

She attended several outpatient follow-up visits, which demonstrated progressive improvement in both her functional ability and general health, as well as sustained normalization of CK values. As a result, prednisone was successfully and rapidly tapered. Due to both financial considerations and her significant clinical recovery off semaglutide, no further IVIG was administered after her initial inpatient course.

She was last seen in the outpatient setting 10 months after her initial evaluation and treatment initiation and had regained her baseline functional status, off immunosuppression for 8 months.

Investigations:

Her laboratory evaluation revealed a creatine kinase (CK) level of 1237 U/L at the time of assessment, with a maximum value of 3992 U/L documented one week earlier at the referring facility. Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) was within normal parameters. Aspartate aminotransferase (AST) was measured at 191 U/L and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) at 165 U/L, findings interpreted in the context of both increased CK and a known history of hepatic steatosis. Autoantibody screening demonstrated negative results for anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-Sjogren’s syndrome antibodies type A (anti-SSA), anti-Smith antibodies, anti-U1 ribonucleoprotein (anti-U1 RNP), anti-double-stranded DNA (dsDNA), anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), and anti-mitochondrial antibodies. Complement levels were within normal range, renal function was normal, and there was no evidence of cytopenias. Myositis-specific and myositis-associated antibody testing was negative for SRP, HMGCR, Jo-1, Pl-7, Pl-12, EJ, OJ, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-y, NXP-2, and cN-1A. However, a low-titer positive SS-B antibody was identified at 2.4 (reference range: <1.0). Inflammatory markers were increased, with an erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) of 22 mm/hour and a C-reactive protein (CRP) of 22.8 mg/L. Additional findings included a high-sensitivity troponin (HS-Troponin) level of 62 pg/mL (reference: ≤53 pg/mL) and a pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (proBNP) concentration of 4358 pg/mL (reference range: 0–125 pg/mL).

Key imaging studies performed included MRI scans of both femurs, an echocardiogram, cardiac MRI, liver ultrasound with Doppler, high-resolution chest CT, and abdominal MRI. MRI of the femurs showed mild, bilateral, symmetric muscle edema predominantly affecting the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps, along with subcutaneous edema on the lateral sides of both thighs (figure 2). The echocardiogram demonstrated a left ventricular ejection fraction between 65%–70%, and a right ventricular systolic pressure of 46 mm Hg. Cardiac MRI detected mild hypokinesis involving the mid-anteroseptal and anterior walls, accompanied by myocardial edema, without definite late gadolinium enhancement—findings which may represent myocardial inflammation, early myocarditis, or possibly a localized stress-induced cardiomyopathy. No evidence of notable myocardial scarring, infarction, or significant valvular dysfunction was present. Liver ultrasound with Doppler revealed hepatosteatosis. The high-resolution chest CT did not identify interstitial lung disease or lymphadenopathy. Abdominal MRI confirmed the presence of steatotic hepatomegaly but was otherwise unremarkable, with no detected lymphadenopathy.

Additional advanced diagnostic evaluations involved performing a repeat liver biopsy as well as a biopsy of the thigh muscle. The liver biopsy, conducted during her hospitalization, revealed several cores of hepatic tissue showing both small and large droplet macrovesicular steatosis occupying roughly 70% of the examined hepatic parenchyma, without notable portal inflammation or evidence of interface hepatitis. These observations matched her previous diagnosis of hepatosteatosis, indicating mild progression of her chronic liver disease and were categorized as a NAFLD Activity Score of 5 out of 8, stage 1. Biopsy of the right vastus lateralis muscle showed scattered areas of necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers (figure 3A,B). Immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated positive staining for acid phosphatase (figure 3D) and cluster of Differentiation-68 (CD68), a macrophage marker, with accompanying myophagocytosis (figure 4B). Furthermore, there was deposition of membrane attack complex in both necrotic and non-necrotic muscle fibers (figure 4C), indicating an ongoing inflammatory process. Occasional CD3-positive T cells (figure 4A) and perivascular collections of inflammatory cells (figure 3C) were also present. Collectively, these muscle biopsy findings were compatible with a diagnosis of immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM).

Differential diagnosis:

The patient developed a pronounced and rapid adverse response following her first and only administration of semaglutide, indicative of intolerance. Initial manifestations were gastrointestinal disturbances and renal failure believed to be secondary to hypovolemia. Although creatine kinase was only slightly increased at presentation, which did not meet criteria for rhabdomyolysis, her ongoing deterioration off semaglutide, the pattern of muscle weakness, and a 12-fold rise in creatine kinase over the 4 months following semaglutide exposure point toward an immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) likely linked to semaglutide. Additionally, muscle biopsy findings characteristic of drug-induced IMNM, the absence of signs typical of an underlying connective tissue disorder, and marked improvement following immunosuppression and discontinuation of semaglutide further corroborate this relationship.

It is important to recognize that she exhibited elevated transaminase levels before beginning semaglutide, a finding that may occur in certain myopathies. Nevertheless, a liver biopsy conducted three years before initiation of semaglutide confirmed hepatosteatosis, and her mildly increased AST and ALT values remained after normalization of CK concentrations. This suggests that her chronic transaminase elevations most likely originate from hepatic pathology rather than muscle injury. Additionally, her cardiac assessment did not provide definitive evidence of ongoing myocarditis. Considering her swift improvement, stress-related myopathy would also be a plausible diagnosis in this clinical scenario.

Detection of SS-B antibodies alone, despite negative ANA and SS-A results, was an unanticipated finding in this context. To date, the literature does not describe cases of isolated SS-B antibody positivity with negative ANA and SS-A culminating in IMNM. A creatine kinase value prior to initiating semaglutide therapy was not available, so the presence of a low-grade autoimmune IMNM before starting semaglutide cannot be conclusively ruled out. Nonetheless, a twelvefold increase in CK levels and the worsening of proximal muscle weakness subsequent to semaglutide administration suggest a stronger possibility of the IMNM episode being medication-induced. Moreover, the patient's pronounced IMNM responded well to a brief regimen of corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin, along with discontinuation of semaglutide. This recovery pattern is more consistent with drug-induced IMNM—which often improves with temporary immunosuppression following removal of the causative medication—than with idiopathic IMNM, which usually necessitates extended and aggressive immunosuppressive treatment.

Treatment:

She received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for 5 days, after which she was transitioned to oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg per day. Given ongoing significant weakness, IVIG was administered at a total dose of 2 g/kg, divided over 4 days. Subsequent outpatient evaluations revealed continuous improvement in both her functional capacity and general health, with her CK levels remaining consistently within normal limits. As a result of these findings, prednisone was able to be tapered successfully and discontinued over a 2-month period. Owing to a combination of financial constraints and her stable improvement following discontinuation of semaglutide, no further IVIG infusions were provided beyond her initial hospitalization.

Outcome and follow-up:

At her most recent outpatient follow-up, which occurred 10 months after her initial assessment and start of therapy, she had returned to her previous level of function and had been off immunosuppressive medication for 7 months. Her creatine kinase levels stayed within normal limits after her hospitalization and immunosuppressive treatment. Repeat cardiac imaging, including echocardiogram and cardiac MRI, demonstrated resolution of the previously observed abnormalities. Additionally, she had no further episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation.

Discussion:

GLP-1 receptor agonists like semaglutide are increasingly prescribed and have shown efficacy in treating metabolic conditions such as obesity and type 2 diabetes. While gastrointestinal side effects are frequently reported, there is also potential for these drugs to induce loss of muscle mass and, in some cases, rhabdomyolysis. In this case, however, the patient's progression is more aligned with IMNM rather than rhabdomyolysis. The creatine kinase (CK) concentration rose twelvefold over four months after a single dose of semaglutide, a pattern that does not fit the expected rapid CK normalization seen within a few weeks if semaglutide-induced muscle toxicity were responsible, as described in other incidents of drug-related rhabdomyolysis. Of note, the previously published case of rhabdomyolysis linked to tirzepatide exhibited the expected clinical progression: the individual developed a pronounced acute CK elevation (CK=57,014 at presentation), followed by prompt resolution after discontinuing the medication and providing hydration, without the use of immunosuppressive therapy.

According to the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) Causality Categories and Criteria, there appears to be a probable link between semaglutide administration and the development of IMNM in this patient. This assessment is based on the appropriate temporal association, the low likelihood of alternative explanations from other diseases or medications, and the notable clinical improvement following cessation of semaglutide. Re-exposing the patient to semaglutide would be considered unethical due to the severity of the adverse reaction observed. Nonetheless, we recognize that the causative relationship in this scenario cannot be considered definitive. Given the rising use of semaglutide, our report aims to alert clinicians to this potential side effect and strengthen pharmacovigilance efforts. Further investigations are necessary to determine whether this relationship is indeed causal or merely coincidental.

The underlying factors that might have predisposed this patient to such a severe response to semaglutide remain unknown. As GLP-1 receptor agonists become more widely prescribed, it will be crucial for future research to investigate risk factors for adverse reactions, particularly if more cases emerge with a similar close temporal association to semaglutide therapy.
