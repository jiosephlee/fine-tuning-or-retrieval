Title: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

Brian Lam, Eleni Tiniakou, Xinhai Robert Zhang, Shervin Assassi

Abstract:

Summary: This case details a woman in her 60s who developed immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) following administration of semaglutide. Over a four-month period, she presented with worsening dysphagia, dysphonia, proximal muscle weakness, and elevated creatine kinase. Her symptom onset was temporally linked to a single dose of semaglutide. Diagnostic evaluation showed findings compatible with necrotising myopathy, including biopsy evidence of inflammatory infiltrates. Treatment with corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) resulted in improvement. Over time, she regained her previous level of function without ongoing immunosuppression, while continuing to abstain from glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists. This report introduces the first documented occurrence of IMNM likely related to GLP-1 receptor agonist exposure, showing an apparent response to brief immunosuppression and IVIG therapy.

Background:

In recent times, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have become widely prescribed for metabolic conditions such as type 2 diabetes and obesity. One of the reported side effects of these agents is a reduction in skeletal muscle mass. A recent case report detailed rhabdomyolysis with necrotising characteristics in association with tirzepatide, a frequently utilized GLP-1 receptor agonist. In the described patient, creatine kinase (CK) levels rose into the thousands during therapy, and began to decrease within several days following withdrawal of the medication, which is consistent with drug-related rhabdomyolysis.

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) represents a unique type of myopathic disorder that manifests with pronounced proximal muscle weakness and histological evidence of myofibre necrosis, accompanied by minimal lymphocytic infiltration. The presence of two specific autoantibodies—anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (SRP)—is strongly correlated with IMNM. Notably, the administration of statins, frequently prescribed for metabolic conditions, has a well-established connection to the development of HMGCR autoantibody positivity. Historically, patients receiving statins who subsequently developed necrotising myopathy were frequently misdiagnosed as having toxic myopathy or polymyositis before the link to the HMGCR autoantibody was recognized. Many of these individuals continued to experience myopathic symptoms and raised creatine kinase levels after discontinuation of statin therapy. The subsequent clinical improvement with immunosuppressive treatment supported the presence of an autoimmune myopathy and ultimately contributed to the identification of HMGCR autoantibody as a key feature of this disorder. It should be emphasized that approximately one-third of IMNM cases are seronegative. This absence of detectable autoantibodies presents further diagnostic hurdles and indicates the likely existence of additional, as-yet-unidentified autoantibodies and triggering factors.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 60s with a medical background of obesity, fatty liver disease, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and a distant history of deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism presented with gradually worsening proximal muscle weakness, difficulty swallowing, and voice changes.

Previously, she enjoyed good health and managed all daily tasks independently. Three years before her current decline, she was being evaluated in the outpatient setting for elevated transaminases. At that time, laboratory tests revealed slight increases in aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT), each approximately two times the upper limit of normal, with ALT exceeding AST. Alkaline phosphatase, albumin, bilirubin, and platelets remained within normal parameters. She reported no alcohol consumption and was not using any potentially hepatotoxic drugs. An abdominal ultrasound demonstrated an enlarged liver with diffuse echogenicity, likely indicating hepatic steatosis. Screening for viral hepatitis and autoantibodies—including anti-nuclear, anti-mitochondrial, anti-smooth muscle, and liver-kidney microsomal—were all negative. Liver biopsy findings were consistent with steatohepatitis associated with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), showing a NAFLD Activity Score of 4 out of 8 and stage 0 disease. She was counseled to adhere to a reduced-calorie diet and maintain regular physical activity.

Given ongoing fatty liver and obesity 2.5 years post-NAFLD diagnosis, semaglutide therapy was initiated. Shortly after administration of the initial 0.25 mg subcutaneous dose, she developed nausea and vomiting. These symptoms persisted, leading to hospital admission two days later with complaints of nausea, vomiting, limited oral intake, dyspnea, acute renal insufficiency, and marked metabolic acidosis. Management included intravenous fluids, bicarbonate, and antiemetics. She required temporary transfer to the intensive care unit for non-invasive ventilatory support. Her renal parameters improved with hydration alone, without the necessity for haemofiltration or dialysis. Creatine kinase was measured on admission and found to be elevated at 337 U/L. She exhibited no additional symptoms such as rash, myalgia, or dark (cola-colored) urine. During her hospitalization, she also experienced paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, yet troponin levels remained within the normal range. She was discharged after five days, at which point gastrointestinal symptoms had abated, though she remained physically deconditioned. Progressive muscle weakness developed over the subsequent four months. She reported no previous use of statins, no significant ingestion of supplements commonly associated with IMNM—including mushrooms, red yeast rice—or other nutritional supplements. There was no notable family history of myopathy, nor history of recent substantial or prolonged exercise, or use of alcohol or recreational drugs.

Four months following her first and only administration of semaglutide, her condition progressively worsened. She became dependent on assistance to stand or sit upright, and reported experiencing difficulty swallowing both solids and liquids over several days. This led her to seek care at another hospital, where laboratory testing revealed an elevated creatine kinase (CK) level of 3992 U/L (reference range: 12–191 U/L) at the time of admission. Neurology was consulted remotely, and she received intravenous methylprednisolone 1000 mg daily for five days. Subsequently, she was transferred to a tertiary center for rheumatological assessment. Upon arrival, she was afebrile, had normal blood pressure, and did not appear to be in distress. Examination demonstrated marked weakness, particularly pronounced in the proximal muscle groups (see table 1). There were no findings of rash, skin changes, pulmonary crackles, synovitis, edema, sicca symptoms, oral ulcers, or other neurological abnormalities.

After receiving pulse methylprednisolone, oral prednisone was initiated at a dose of 1 mg/kg per day. Because of her ongoing weakness and severe clinical presentation, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at a total dose of 2 g/kg over the span of 4 days. Over a period of 2 weeks, her CK showed substantial improvement (figure 1). She was discharged following an inpatient stay of almost 4 weeks. Twelve days post-discharge, she was admitted again for 10 days because of septic shock resulting from a pseudomonal urinary tract infection, during which her CK was found to be normal.

She attended multiple outpatient follow-up visits, during which she demonstrated ongoing progress in her functional abilities and general health, along with stable, normal CK measurements. As a result, prednisone was tapered off quickly. Because of financial considerations and her sustained improvement following discontinuation of semaglutide, no further IVIG was administered after her initial inpatient course.

Her most recent outpatient evaluation occurred 10 months following the start of treatment, at which time she had returned to her pre-illness level of function and remained free from immunosuppressive therapy for 8 months.

Investigations:

Her laboratory investigations revealed an increased CK level of 1237 U/L at the time of assessment, with a maximum value of 3992 U/L recorded one week earlier at the referring institution. Thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH) was within standard limits. Transaminases showed AST of 191 U/L and ALT of 165 U/L, findings observed alongside elevated CK and a prior diagnosis of fatty liver. Autoantibody screening was remarkable for absence of anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-Sjogren’s syndrome antibodies type A (anti-SSA), anti-Smith antibodies, anti-U1 ribonucleoprotein antibodies (anti-U1 RNP), anti-double-stranded DNA, ANCA, and anti-mitochondrial antibodies. Complement levels were normal, creatinine was within reference range, and no cytopenias were identified. The myositis-specific antibody panel returned negative results for SRP and HMGCR, as well as Jo-1, Pl-7, Pl-12, EJ, OJ, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-y, NXP-2, and cN-1A autoantibodies. There was a low titre positivity for SS-B autoantibody at 2.4 (reference range: <1.0). Her ESR was raised at 22 mm/hour and CRP at 22.8 mg/L. Additionally, a high-sensitivity troponin was elevated at 62 pg/mL (reference: ≤53 pg/mL) and pro-B-type natriuretic peptide measured 4358 pg/mL (reference range: 0–125 pg/mL).

Imaging assessments included bilateral femur MRI, echocardiogram, cardiac MRI, liver ultrasound with Doppler, high-resolution chest CT, and abdominal MRI. MRI of the femurs showed mild and symmetric muscle edema, primarily involving the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps, as well as subcutaneous edema over the lateral aspects of both thighs (see figure 2). Echocardiography demonstrated a left ventricular ejection fraction estimated at 65%–70% and a right ventricular systolic pressure of 46 mm Hg. Cardiac MRI identified mild hypokinesis in the mid-anteroseptal and anterior myocardial walls accompanied by myocardial edema, without clear late gadolinium enhancement; these findings could reflect myocardial inflammation/early myocarditis or a focal, stress-induced cardiomyopathy. There was an absence of significant myocardial scarring, infarction, or notable valvular disease. Liver ultrasound with Doppler revealed findings consistent with hepatic steatosis. High-resolution CT of the chest showed no evidence of interstitial lung disease or lymphadenopathy. Abdominal MRI demonstrated hepatomegaly due to steatosis, but was otherwise normal and did not show any lymphadenopathy.

Additional advanced investigations included a repeat liver biopsy as well as a muscle biopsy of the thigh. The liver biopsy, performed during hospitalization, revealed multiple samples of hepatic tissue exhibiting both small and large droplet macrovesicular steatosis involving roughly 70% of the examined liver parenchyma, without significant portal inflammation or evidence of interface hepatitis. These results were in line with her prior diagnosis of hepatosteatosis and indicated mild worsening of her liver pathology, now classified as NAFLD Activity Score grade 5/8, stage 1. Biopsy of the right vastus lateralis muscle showed scattered necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers (figure 3A,B). Immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated staining for acid phosphatase (figure 3D) and cluster of Differentiation-68 (CD68), which is a macrophage marker, revealing myophagocytosis (figure 4B). There was also positivity for membrane attack complex on both necrotizing and non-necrotizing muscle fibers (figure 4C), indicative of an inflammatory response. In addition, infrequent cells expressing CD3, a T-cell marker, were present (figure 4A), along with perivascular inflammatory infiltrates (figure 3C). The findings from the muscle biopsy supported a diagnosis of IMNM.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient had a pronounced and immediate reaction following her first and only administration of semaglutide, indicative of intolerance. Initial manifestations involved gastrointestinal disturbances and renal failure believed to be secondary to hypovolaemia. Although her CK was only mildly raised, inconsistent with rhabdomyolysis at presentation, her ongoing deterioration after discontinuation of semaglutide, the pattern of muscle weakness, and a twelvefold CK increase over four months post-exposure point toward an IMNM likely related to semaglutide. Additionally, biopsy findings consistent with drug-induced IMNM, absence of clinical signs suggestive of an underlying connective tissue disorder, and lasting improvement after immunosuppressive therapy and stopping semaglutide further reinforce this potential association.

It is worth mentioning that the patient exhibited elevated transaminases before starting semaglutide therapy, a phenomenon observed in some muscle disorders. Nevertheless, a liver biopsy conducted three years prior to semaglutide initiation confirmed hepatosteatosis, and her marginally increased AST/ALT levels remained even after normalization of CK. This suggests that her persistent transaminase abnormalities are likely attributable to liver disease rather than muscle injury. Additionally, her cardiac evaluation did not definitively indicate ongoing myocarditis. Given the clinical context and the swift resolution of her symptoms, stress-induced myopathy also represents a plausible explanation.

The detection of SS-B antibodies in the absence of ANA and SS-A was surprising. To date, the literature does not document cases in which isolated SS-B positivity, combined with negative ANA and SS-A, is associated with IMNM. We were unable to identify a CK level measured before the patient began taking semaglutide, so it is not possible to entirely rule out the presence of a slowly progressive autoimmune IMNM preceding semaglutide administration. Nonetheless, the twelve-fold increase in CK and worsening proximal muscle weakness after starting semaglutide support the hypothesis that IMNM was triggered by the medication. Moreover, the patient’s severe IMNM resolved after a brief regimen of corticosteroids and IVIG, combined with discontinuation of semaglutide. This clinical trajectory is characteristic of drug-induced IMNM, which often responds to temporary immunosuppression following cessation of the causative drug, in contrast to idiopathic IMNM, which usually necessitates extended and aggressive immunosuppressive therapy.

Treatment:

She received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for 5 days, followed by oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg per day. Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at 2 g/kg, divided across 4 days, owing to ongoing weakness and pronounced clinical manifestations. Subsequent outpatient follow-up demonstrated progressive improvement in her functional capacity and overall health, as well as consistently normal CK values. As a result, prednisone was tapered rapidly and discontinued within 2 months. No further IVIG was given after her initial hospitalization, due to both financial considerations and the sustained clinical response following cessation of semaglutide.

Outcome and follow-up:

At her most recent outpatient follow-up, conducted 10 months after beginning treatment, the patient had returned to her pre-illness level of functioning and had discontinued immunosuppressive therapy for the past 7 months. Her creatine kinase levels stayed within normal limits after hospital admission and immunosuppressive management. Repeat assessment with echocardiogram and cardiac MRI showed resolution of previous abnormal findings. Additionally, she had no further episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation.

Discussion:

GLP-1 receptor agonists such as semaglutide are increasingly prescribed and have shown efficacy in metabolic conditions including obesity and type 2 diabetes. While gastrointestinal side effects are frequently observed, these agents may also contribute to reductions in muscle mass and, in rare instances, rhabdomyolysis. In this particular case, the patient's presentation aligns more closely with IMNM than with rhabdomyolysis. After a single dose of semaglutide, CK levels rose by a factor of 12 over four months; if semaglutide-induced toxicity were responsible, we would expect CK values to return to normal within weeks, paralleling the course of other drug-induced rhabdomyolysis cases. For example, a prior report describing rhabdomyolysis linked to tirzepatide demonstrated the usual clinical pattern: the patient had a significant acute increase in CK (CK=57 014 at initial presentation) and rapid improvement within days following cessation of the drug and hydration therapy, with no requirement for immunosuppression.

According to the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) Causality Categories and Criteria, the association between semaglutide administration and IMNM in our patient appears to be probable, as the temporal correlation is appropriate, alternative etiologies such as other conditions or medications are unlikely, and discontinuation resulted in a clinically plausible improvement. Rechallenge in this instance would not be ethical due to the serious adverse event linked to semaglutide. Nevertheless, we recognize that definitive causation cannot be established in this report. As semaglutide usage increases rapidly, this case serves to alert clinicians to this possible adverse event and to promote heightened pharmacovigilance. Further research will be needed to determine if this relationship is truly causal or merely coincidental.

The factors contributing to our patient's susceptibility to this severe reaction from semaglutide remain undetermined. With the rising use of GLP-1 receptor agonists, identifying predisposing risk factors will be critical, especially if additional cases showing a tight temporal connection to semaglutide administration are documented going forward.
