Title: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

Brian Lam, Eleni Tiniakou, Xinhai Robert Zhang, Shervin Assassi

Abstract:

Summary: This case report details a woman in her sixth decade who developed immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) following exposure to semaglutide. She presented with progressive dysphagia, dysphonia, proximal muscle weakness, and elevated creatine kinase over a period of 4 months, with the onset of symptoms occurring after a single administration of semaglutide. Diagnostic evaluation revealed findings indicative of necrotising myopathy with inflammatory infiltrates seen on muscle biopsy. The patient demonstrated clinical improvement following treatment with corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG). Over time, she regained her pre-illness functional status, maintained for several months without immunosuppressive therapy, and by continued avoidance of glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists. This report presents the first documented instance of IMNM likely related to GLP-1 receptor agonist exposure, showing resolution with brief immunosuppressive therapy and IVIG.

Background:

Over the past few years, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have become widely utilized for the management of metabolic conditions such as type 2 diabetes and obesity. Among the reported side effects for these medications is a reduction in skeletal muscle mass. A recent published case highlighted an occurrence of rhabdomyolysis with necrotising characteristics linked to tirzepatide, a commonly prescribed GLP-1 receptor agonist. In that instance, the patient experienced an increase in creatine kinase (CK) into the thousands during ongoing therapy, with CK levels beginning to decline within a few days following discontinuation of the medication, indicative of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis.

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) represents a unique form of myopathy distinguished by rapidly progressive weakness affecting proximal muscles, accompanied by necrotising muscle fibers and minimal lymphocytic infiltration noted on histological examination. The autoantibodies anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (SRP) have a strong correlation with IMNM. Statin therapy, frequently employed in the management of metabolic conditions, is especially linked to the presence of HMGCR autoantibodies. Historically, some individuals treated with statins developed manifestations consistent with necrotising myopathy and were routinely categorized as having toxic myopathy or polymyositis prior to recognition of the relationship with HMGCR autoantibodies. In several such instances, patients continued to exhibit myopathic symptoms and elevated creatine kinase levels even after discontinuing statin use. Subsequent improvement following immunosuppressive therapy indicated an autoimmune etiology, which ultimately led to identification of HMGCR autoantibodies as a defining feature of this disorder. It is important to note that approximately one-third of patients diagnosed with IMNM do not test positive for any identifiable autoantibody. This seronegativity not only complicates the diagnostic process but also implies the existence of other associated autoantibodies and as yet unidentified precipitating factors.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 60s with a history of obesity, fatty liver, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and a distant episode of deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism presented with gradually worsening weakness in her proximal muscles, as well as difficulty swallowing and speaking.

She had previously enjoyed good health and was entirely independent in daily activities. Three years before her current decline, she had been evaluated as an outpatient for elevated transaminases. At that time, her aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels were mildly increased, roughly twice the upper normal limit, with ALT exceeding AST. Alkaline phosphatase, albumin, bilirubin, and platelet count were all within normal ranges. She reported no alcohol consumption and was not on any medications known to harm the liver. Abdominal ultrasound revealed an enlarged liver with diffuse increase in echogenicity, most likely indicating hepatic steatosis. Serologic tests for viral hepatitis, anti-nuclear, anti-mitochondrial, anti-smooth muscle, and liver-kidney microsomal antibodies were negative. Liver biopsy confirmed steatohepatitis consistent with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), NAFLD Activity Score grade 4/8, stage 0. She was advised to adopt a reduced-calorie diet and to engage in regular physical activity.

Because her fatty liver and obesity persisted for 2.5 years following her NAFLD diagnosis, semaglutide therapy was started. Soon after administering the initial dose of semaglutide (0.25 mg subcutaneously), she developed nausea and vomiting. These symptoms continued, leading to her hospital admission two days later for ongoing nausea, vomiting, poor oral intake, dyspnoea, acute renal failure, and marked metabolic acidosis. Management included intravenous fluids and bicarbonate, alongside anti-emetic treatment. She was transferred to the intensive care unit briefly to receive non-invasive ventilatory support. Her kidney function subsequently improved after hydration, with no requirement for haemofiltration or haemodialysis. At presentation, her CK was elevated at 337 U/L, though she reported no accompanying symptoms such as rash, muscle pain, or dark urine. During her admission, she experienced paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, yet her serum troponin levels remained within normal range. She was discharged from the hospital after a five-day stay. By discharge, her gastrointestinal issues had resolved, but she remained physically deconditioned. Over the ensuing four months, she developed worsening weakness. She denied prior statin usage, notable intake of other IMNM-associated supplements including mushrooms or red yeast rice, or any other nutritional supplements. Additionally, she reported no significant family history of myopathy, recent excessive or extended physical exertion, or use of alcohol or illicit substances.

Four months following administration of her initial and only semaglutide dose, her condition continued to worsen. She became unable to stand or sit upright without support, and had developed difficulty swallowing both liquids and solids over the preceding days. These symptoms led her to seek care at an outside hospital, where laboratory results revealed a creatine kinase (CK) level of 3992 U/L (reference range: 12–191 U/L) at admission. Neurology was consulted via telemedicine, and she received 1000 mg intravenous methylprednisolone daily for five days. Subsequently, she was transferred to a tertiary facility for rheumatological assessment. Upon arrival, her vital signs indicated she was afebrile and normotensive, and she appeared comfortable. The physical examination demonstrated marked weakness, particularly involving proximal muscle groups (see table 1). No rash, skin changes, crackles, synovitis, edema, sicca symptoms, oral ulcers, or other neurologic abnormalities were observed.

After receiving pulse methylprednisolone, she commenced oral prednisone at a dosage of 1 mg/kg per day. Because of ongoing muscle weakness and significant symptoms, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at 2 g/kg, divided across 4 days. Over a 2-week period, her creatine kinase (CK) showed substantial improvement (see figure 1). She was discharged following nearly 4 weeks of inpatient care. Twelve days later, she was re-admitted for a 10-day hospitalization secondary to septic shock caused by pseudomonal urinary tract infection, during which her CK levels remained within the normal range.

She attended multiple outpatient follow-up visits, demonstrating ongoing progress in both her functional capacity and general health, with CK levels remaining within the normal range. Consequently, prednisone was tapered and discontinued swiftly without issue. Considering her clinical improvement following discontinuation of semaglutide and financial factors, no further IVIG therapy was administered beyond the initial hospitalization course.

At her most recent outpatient appointment, conducted 10 months after her first evaluation and start of treatment, she had returned to her baseline level of function and had been without immunosuppressive therapy for 8 months.

Investigations:

Her laboratory evaluation revealed a CK level of 1237 U/L upon assessment, with the highest recorded value being 3992 U/L one week earlier at the referring institution. Thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH) was within reference ranges. AST measured 191 U/L and ALT was 165 U/L in the context of increased serum CK and a history of hepatic steatosis. Autoantibody testing showed negative results for anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-Sjogren’s syndrome antibody type A (anti-SSA), anti-Smith antibodies, anti-U1 ribonucleoprotein (anti-U1 RNP), anti-double-stranded DNA, antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), and anti-mitochondrial antibodies. Complement levels were normal, renal function was preserved, and cytopenias were absent. The myositis panel did not detect SRP or HMGCR, nor Jo-1, Pl-7, Pl-12, EJ, OJ, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-y, NXP-2, or cN-1A autoantibodies. She demonstrated a low titer SS-B autoantibody (2.4; reference range <1.0). Erythrocyte sedimentation rate was raised to 22 mm/hour and C-reactive protein was elevated at 22.8 mg/L. Of note, high-sensitivity troponin was above normal at 62 pg/mL (reference ≤53 pg/mL), and pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (proBNP) was markedly increased at 4358 pg/mL (reference range: 0–125 pg/mL).

The patient's imaging workup consisted of MRI scans of both femurs, echocardiography, cardiac MRI, liver ultrasound with Doppler, high-resolution chest CT, and abdominal MRI. MRI of the femurs identified mild, bilateral, and symmetric muscle oedema, most prominent in the proximal portions of the vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps, along with subcutaneous oedema on the lateral sides of both thighs (see figure 2). The echocardiogram revealed a left ventricular ejection fraction estimated at 65%–70% and a right ventricular systolic pressure measuring 46 mm Hg. Cardiac MRI showed mild hypokinesis involving the mid-anteroseptal and anterior walls, accompanied by myocardial oedema, but no late gadolinium enhancement, suggesting possible myocardial inflammation/early myocarditis or potentially localized stress-induced cardiomyopathy. There was no evidence for significant myocardial scarring, infarction, or notable valvular abnormalities. Liver ultrasound with Doppler demonstrated hepatosteatosis. High-resolution CT of the chest did not identify interstitial lung disease or lymphadenopathy. Abdominal MRI depicted steatotic hepatomegaly, otherwise was unremarkable, and showed no lymphadenopathy.

Additional advanced testing included a repeat liver biopsy along with a biopsy of the thigh muscle. The liver biopsy, conducted during hospitalization, revealed multiple cores of hepatic tissue exhibiting both small and large droplet macrovesicular steatosis, affecting roughly 70% of the examined hepatic parenchyma, without notable portal inflammation or interface hepatitis. These results aligned with her established hepatosteatosis, showing mild progression to a NAFLD Activity Score of 5/8, stage 1. A biopsy of the right vastus lateralis muscle showed scattered necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers (figure 3A,B). Immunohistochemical analysis indicated acid phosphatase activity (figure 3D) and positive staining for cluster of Differentiation-68 (CD68), a macrophage-specific marker, along with evidence of myophagocytosis (figure 4B). The muscle tissue also exhibited membrane attack complex positivity on both necrotic and non-necrotic myofibers (figure 4C), suggesting an inflammatory reaction. Additionally, rare CD3-positive cells, indicating T lymphocytes (figure 4A), and perivascular inflammatory infiltrates (figure 3C) were observed. The collective muscle biopsy results supported a diagnosis of IMNM.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient exhibited a pronounced and rapid adverse response following her first and only administration of semaglutide, indicating possible intolerance. She initially presented with gastrointestinal disturbances and acute renal failure presumed to be due to hypovolemia. Although her creatine kinase (CK) was only mildly elevated and not suggestive of rhabdomyolysis during her first assessment, her ongoing clinical deterioration in the absence of further semaglutide exposure, the pattern of muscle weakness, and a 12-fold rise in CK over four months post-exposure point toward the development of an immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) likely linked to semaglutide. Additionally, muscle biopsy findings characteristic of drug-induced IMNM, absence of other signs of connective tissue disease, and persistent recovery after initiating immunosuppression and discontinuing semaglutide, provide further evidence for this relationship.

It is important to note that elevated transaminase levels were present before she began taking semaglutide, a pattern that may occur in myopathic conditions. Nonetheless, a liver biopsy conducted three years before semaglutide initiation confirmed hepatosteatosis, and the mild increases in AST and ALT remained even after creatine kinase concentrations returned to normal. Therefore, the chronic transaminase elevation most likely arises from hepatic sources rather than muscle injury. Cardiac evaluation did not yield definitive evidence for ongoing myocarditis. Given her swift improvement, stress-related myopathy is also a plausible explanation within this clinical context.

The presence of a positive SS-B antibody alongside negative ANA and SS-A results was unusual. To date, the literature does not contain reports of IMNM arising from isolated SS-B positivity with negative ANA and SS-A. Regrettably, we were unable to obtain a CK value from before the patient began semaglutide therapy, so we cannot conclusively rule out a pre-existing, low-grade autoimmune IMNM prior to semaglutide administration. Nevertheless, the 12-fold elevation in CK and the worsening proximal muscle weakness that occurred following semaglutide initiation suggest that this instance of IMNM was likely induced by the medication. Furthermore, the patient’s marked IMNM responded to a brief course of corticosteroids and IVIG, as well as discontinuation of semaglutide. This pattern of recovery is more characteristic of drug-induced IMNM—which tends to resolve with short-term immunosuppression after cessation of the causative drug—compared to idiopathic IMNM, which generally necessitates prolonged and aggressive immunosuppressive treatment.

Treatment:

She received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for 5 days, then transitioned to oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg each day. Owing to ongoing significant weakness and severe symptoms, IVIG was administered at 2 g/kg divided over 4 days. Subsequent outpatient follow-up appointments revealed continued gains in her physical function and overall health, with creatine kinase levels remaining within normal limits. This allowed for a successful and relatively rapid prednisone taper over a two-month period. Based on both financial factors and her stable improvement after discontinuing semaglutide, she did not undergo any further IVIG treatment aside from the initial hospital administration.

Outcome and follow-up:

Ten months following her initial assessment and commencement of therapy, she returned for an outpatient visit and had recovered her usual level of function, having discontinued immunosuppressive therapy for the past 7 months. Her creatine kinase values stayed within normal limits after her hospitalization and immunosuppression. Subsequent echocardiography and cardiac MRI demonstrated resolution of previously detected abnormalities. She also remained free of additional episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation.

Discussion:

GLP-1 receptor agonists, including semaglutide, are increasingly utilized and have shown efficacy in treating metabolic conditions such as obesity and type 2 diabetes. While gastrointestinal side effects are most frequently reported, there is evidence linking these agents to reduced muscle mass and, in rare instances, rhabdomyolysis. In this particular case, the clinical progression aligns more closely with IMNM than with rhabdomyolysis. After a single dose of semaglutide, CK concentrations rose by a factor of 12 over a four-month span; had semaglutide-induced toxicity been responsible, CK values would typically return to normal within weeks, as observed in drug-induced rhabdomyolysis cases. Importantly, the previously documented rhabdomyolysis incident related to tirzepatide showed a characteristic pattern: an abrupt and significant spike in CK (CK=57 014 upon presentation) with prompt resolution after discontinuation and hydration, occurring within days and not requiring immunosuppressive therapy.

Applying the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) Causality Categories and Criteria, our case demonstrates probable causality between semaglutide administration and IMNM, as there was an appropriate temporal association, other possible etiologies including diseases and medications are improbable, and clinical improvement was observed upon discontinuation. A rechallenge was not performed due to the severity of the adverse reaction, making it ethically unacceptable. Nevertheless, we recognize that definitive causation cannot be established in this instance. With semaglutide usage increasing rapidly, this case report seeks to alert healthcare providers to the potential risk and to contribute to ongoing pharmacovigilance efforts. Further research is required to determine whether the observed association is causal or merely coincidental.

Additionally, it is uncertain what factors may have made our patient susceptible to such a marked response to semaglutide. As GLP-1 receptor agonists gain widespread usage, investigations into risk factors will be critical, particularly if additional cases with a close temporal link to semaglutide exposure emerge.
