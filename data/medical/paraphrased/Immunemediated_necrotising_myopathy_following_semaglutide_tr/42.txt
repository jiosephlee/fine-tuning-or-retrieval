Title: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

Brian Lam, Eleni Tiniakou, Xinhai Robert Zhang, Shervin Assassi

Abstract:

Summary: This case report details a woman in her 60s who developed immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) following exposure to semaglutide. Over a period of four months, she experienced worsening dysphagia, dysphonia, proximal muscle weakness, and elevated creatine kinase levels. The onset of her symptoms followed administration of a single semaglutide dose. Diagnostic evaluation demonstrated findings characteristic of necrotising myopathy, including inflammatory infiltrates observed on muscle biopsy. The patient showed clinical improvement after treatment with corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG). Over time, she regained her previous level of function and remained stable off immunosuppressive therapy for several months, avoiding further GLP-1 receptor agonist medications. This represents the first documented instance of IMNM likely linked to GLP-1 receptor agonist exposure, which appeared to respond favorably to a brief period of immunosuppression and IVIG.

Background:

Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have increasingly been prescribed for the management of metabolic conditions such as type 2 diabetes and obesity in recent years. Among the adverse events reported for this medication class, reductions in skeletal muscle mass have been noted. One recent case report highlighted a presentation of rhabdomyolysis with necrotising characteristics associated with tirzepatide, a widely used GLP-1 receptor agonist. The patient in question experienced creatine kinase (CK) elevations reaching thousands during active therapy; notably, CK levels began to decrease within days following cessation of the drug, indicating a likely drug-related rhabdomyolysis.

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) represents a unique form of myopathy, typified by rapidly progressive weakness of proximal muscles, histological evidence of myofibre necrosis, and minimal lymphocytic infiltration. Two autoantibodies, anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (SRP), are strongly linked to IMNM. Notably, statin therapy—widely prescribed for metabolic conditions—is closely correlated with the presence of HMGCR autoantibodies. Prior to recognition of the HMGCR autoantibody, numerous patients exposed to statins developed clinical and histological signs consistent with necrotising myopathy and were typically diagnosed as having toxic myopathy or polymyositis. In these instances, individuals often exhibited sustained muscle pathology and elevated creatine kinase (CK) levels even after discontinuing statin treatment. The persistence of these symptoms, combined with their responsiveness to immunosuppressive therapy, provided evidence of an underlying autoimmune process, ultimately leading to the identification of the HMGCR autoantibody as a diagnostic feature. It is important to note that approximately one-third of IMNM patients do not exhibit detectable autoantibodies. This seronegativity complicates diagnosis and indicates that additional autoantibodies or inciting factors may still be unknown.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 60s with a background of obesity, hepatic steatosis, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and a distant history of deep vein thrombosis/pulmonary embolism presented with gradually worsening proximal muscle weakness, swallowing difficulties, and voice changes.

Previously, she maintained good health and was entirely independent regarding daily activities. Three years before her current decline, she underwent outpatient assessment for elevated liver enzymes. At that time, she exhibited mildly increased aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT), with ALT levels exceeding AST and both approximately two times the upper reference limit. Her alkaline phosphatase, albumin, bilirubin, and platelet counts were all normal. She reported no alcohol consumption and denied using any medications known to cause liver toxicity. Abdominal ultrasonography demonstrated an enlarged liver with uniformly increased echogenicity, suggesting fatty infiltration. Serologic tests for viral hepatitis, as well as anti-nuclear, anti-mitochondrial, anti-smooth muscle, and liver-kidney microsomal antibodies, yielded negative results. A liver biopsy revealed steatohepatitis in keeping with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), with a NAFLD Activity Score of 4 out of 8 and fibrosis stage 0. The patient was instructed to adhere to a reduced-calorie diet and engage in regular exercise.

Because her fatty liver disease and obesity persisted 2.5 years following her NAFLD diagnosis, semaglutide therapy was initiated. Soon after the first subcutaneous administration of semaglutide at 0.25 mg, she began to experience nausea and vomiting. The symptoms continued and, two days later, she was admitted to hospital for nausea, vomiting, decreased oral intake, dyspnoea, acute renal failure, and severe metabolic acidosis. Management included intravenous fluids and bicarbonate, in addition to anti-emetics. She required a brief transfer to the intensive care unit for non-invasive ventilatory support. Her kidney function subsequently recovered with hydration alone; neither haemofiltration nor haemodialysis was necessary. Her admission creatine kinase was measured at 337 U/L, but she exhibited no other signs, such as rash, myalgia, or dark urine. During this hospital stay, she experienced paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, yet troponin levels remained within normal limits. She was discharged after 5 days; gastrointestinal complaints had abated, though she remained physically deconditioned. Progressive weakness developed over the ensuing 4 months. She reported no history of statin use, substantial intake of other IMNM-associated supplements including mushrooms or red yeast rice, or any other nutritional products. She also denied relevant family history of myopathy, recent strenuous or sustained exercise, or use of alcohol or illicit substances.

Four months following her initial and only administration of semaglutide, her condition continued to worsen. She became unable to stand or sit upright without help, and had experienced trouble swallowing both liquids and solids over several days. These developments led her to seek care at an external hospital, where admission testing revealed a creatine kinase (CK) level of 3992 U/L (reference range: 12–191 U/L). She received 1000 mg of intravenous methylprednisolone daily for five days, with neurology oversight provided via telemedicine. Afterwards, she was transferred to a tertiary care center for rheumatology assessment. On arrival, she was afebrile and normotensive, and appeared otherwise comfortable. The physical exam demonstrated marked weakness, most pronounced in proximal muscle groups (table 1). There were no observed rashes, skin alterations, lung crackles, synovitis, edema, sicca symptoms, oral ulcers, or additional neurological findings.

After receiving pulse methylprednisolone, she was placed on oral prednisone at a dose of 1 mg/kg per day. Because of ongoing muscle weakness and significant clinical findings, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at 2 g/kg, divided across 4 days. Her CK levels showed substantial reduction over a 2-week period (see figure 1). She was released following an approximately 4-week hospital stay. Twelve days following discharge, she was readmitted for 10 days with septic shock caused by a pseudomonal urinary tract infection, during which her CK was within normal limits.

She attended multiple outpatient follow-up visits, during which her functional abilities and general health continued to improve, and her CK levels remained consistently within normal limits. These findings enabled a prompt tapering and discontinuation of prednisone. Owing to both financial considerations and her sustained recovery after cessation of semaglutide, she did not undergo further IVIG therapy beyond the initial series administered during her hospitalization.

At her most recent outpatient appointment, occurring 10 months post initial assessment and start of therapy, she had returned to her prior baseline functional capacity and had been without immunosuppressive treatment for the previous 8 months.

Investigations:

Her laboratory evaluation revealed an elevated CK level of 1237 U/L at the time of assessment, with a maximum value of 3992 U/L documented one week earlier at the referring facility. TSH was within normal parameters. Her AST measured 191 U/L and ALT 165 U/L, findings attributed to increased serum CK as well as a history of fatty liver. Autoimmune serologies were notable for negative results, including anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-Sjogren’s syndrome antibodies type A (anti-SSA), anti-Smith antibodies, anti-U1 ribonucleoprotein antibodies (anti-U1 RNP), anti-double-stranded DNA, ANCA, and anti-mitochondrial antibodies. Complement and creatinine were both within normal ranges and there was no evidence of cytopenia. A myositis-specific antibody panel was negative for SRP, HMGCR, as well as Jo-1, Pl-7, Pl-12, EJ, OJ, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-y, NXP-2, and cN-1A autoantibodies. However, she did exhibit a low-titer positive SS-B autoantibody at 2.4 (reference: <1.0). Inflammatory markers were increased, with ESR at 22 mm/hour and C-reactive protein at 22.8 mg/L. She was also found to have a raised HS-Troponin of 62 pg/mL (normal ≤53 pg/mL) and a pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (proBNP) of 4358 pg/mL (reference: 0–125 pg/mL).

The patient's imaging workup encompassed MRI scans of both femurs, echocardiography, cardiac MRI, liver ultrasound with Doppler, high-resolution chest CT, and abdominal MRI. MRI findings of the femurs indicated mild, symmetric oedema in both thigh muscles, predominantly seen in the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps, along with subcutaneous oedema located laterally in both thighs (see figure 2). Echocardiography showed the left ventricular ejection fraction was estimated between 65%–70%, and right ventricular systolic pressure measured at 46 mm Hg. Cardiac MRI revealed mild hypokinesis involving the mid-anteroseptal and anterior cardiac wall associated with myocardial oedema, without clear evidence of late gadolinium enhancement. These findings may reflect early myocarditis or localized stress-induced cardiomyopathy. No significant scarring, infarction, or substantial valvular abnormalities were present. Liver ultrasound with Doppler identified hepatosteatosis. High-resolution CT of the chest ruled out interstitial lung disease and lymphadenopathy. Abdominal MRI detected enlarged, fatty liver but was otherwise unremarkable, with no lymphadenopathy noted.

Additional advanced tests included both a repeat liver biopsy and a biopsy of the thigh muscle. The liver biopsy, carried out during her hospitalization, revealed several cores of hepatic tissue containing small and large droplet macrovesicular steatosis, affecting roughly 70% of the examined hepatic parenchyma, without notable portal inflammation or evidence of interface hepatitis. This pattern corresponded with her history of hepatosteatosis, demonstrating a slight worsening of her liver disease classified as NAFLD Activity Score grade 5 out of 8, stage 1. A biopsy of the right vastus lateralis muscle showed scattered areas of muscle fibre necrosis and regeneration (figure 3A,B). Immunohistochemical analysis displayed positive staining for acid phosphatase (figure 3D) and for cluster of Differentiation-68 (CD68), a marker for macrophages, accompanied by evidence of myophagocytosis (figure 4B). Furthermore, staining for the membrane attack complex was positive on both necrotising and non-necrotising myofibres (figure 4C), indicating an inflammatory process. A few cells positive for CD3, a T-cell marker (figure 4A), and perivascular inflammatory infiltrates (figure 3C) were also present. Collectively, the muscle biopsy findings were diagnostic of IMNM.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient developed an immediate and pronounced adverse response following her first and only administration of semaglutide, indicative of drug intolerance. She initially presented with gastrointestinal complaints and renal insufficiency likely attributable to hypovolemia. Although her creatine kinase (CK) level was only slightly increased at presentation and did not fulfill criteria for rhabdomyolysis, the subsequent progression of her symptoms off semaglutide, combined with the pattern of muscle weakness and a twelvefold rise in CK over the four months following semaglutide exposure, point towards an immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) potentially related to semaglutide. Additionally, biopsy findings characteristic of medication-induced IMNM, absence of other clinical signs of connective tissue disease, and persistent recovery following cessation of semaglutide and initiation of immunosuppressive therapy further strengthen this presumed connection.

It is worth mentioning that elevated transaminases were present before she began semaglutide therapy, a finding sometimes associated with myopathic conditions. Nonetheless, three years before starting semaglutide, she underwent a liver biopsy confirming hepatosteatosis, and her slightly increased AST/ALT values remained even after CK normalized. Therefore, her chronic transaminase elevations are likely attributable to hepatic causes rather than muscle injury. In addition, her cardiac assessment did not provide definitive evidence for ongoing myocarditis. Considering her swift improvement, stress-induced myopathy could also plausibly explain her presentation in this clinical scenario.

The detection of SS-B antibody positivity in the absence of both ANA and SS-A antibodies was unexpected. To date, there are no reports in the literature documenting isolated SS-B antibody positivity with negative ANA and SS-A in connection with the development of IMNM. A limitation of our case is the lack of a creatine kinase (CK) value preceding the introduction of semaglutide, preventing us from definitively ruling out the presence of subclinical autoimmune IMNM before semaglutide therapy commenced. Nevertheless, the twelvefold increase in CK and the gradual emergence of proximal muscle weakness following the initiation of semaglutide suggest a higher probability that the IMNM episode was drug-induced. Furthermore, the patient's severe IMNM resolved rapidly after a brief course of corticosteroids and IVIG, coupled with discontinuation of semaglutide. This clinical evolution is more characteristic of drug-induced IMNM, which may respond to short-term immunosuppression after withdrawal of the precipitating medication, as opposed to idiopathic IMNM, which typically necessitates more prolonged and aggressive immunosuppressive treatment.

Treatment:

The patient received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for 5 days, after which she was started on oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg per day. Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at 2 g/kg, divided over 4 days, in light of her ongoing weakness and pronounced symptoms. She was subsequently monitored in the outpatient clinic on multiple occasions, demonstrating ongoing gains in functional capacity and overall well-being, with her creatine kinase (CK) levels remaining within normal limits throughout. Owing to her positive response, prednisone was tapered off over a 2-month interval. Considerations related to cost and her continued stability off semaglutide led to the decision not to provide further IVIG following her initial inpatient treatment.

Outcome and follow-up:

At her most recent outpatient follow-up, which occurred 10 months after her initial assessment and commencement of therapy, she had returned to her usual level of functioning and had been without immunosuppressive medications for the preceding 7 months. Post-hospitalisation and immunosuppressive treatment, her creatine kinase concentrations consistently stayed within the normal limits. Subsequent echocardiography and cardiac MRI demonstrated resolution of previously observed abnormalities. Additionally, she did not have any further episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation.

Discussion:

GLP-1 receptor agonists like semaglutide are increasingly utilized and have shown efficacy in treating metabolic conditions such as obesity and type 2 diabetes. While gastrointestinal adverse effects are most frequently observed, these agents can also be linked to reduced muscle mass and, in some instances, rhabdomyolysis. In this particular case, the patient's progression is more indicative of IMNM rather than rhabdomyolysis. Following a single administration of semaglutide, CK levels rose by a factor of 12 over a four-month period; this is in contrast to the anticipated normalization of CK levels within several weeks if semaglutide toxicity were responsible, which is characteristic of other drug-related rhabdomyolysis episodes. Of note, the rhabdomyolysis case previously documented with tirzepatide exhibited a classic presentation, in which the patient showed a dramatic, acute increase in CK (CK=57 014 at onset) and achieved rapid improvement after stopping the medication and providing hydration within days, all without the need for immunosuppressive therapy.

According to the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) Causality Categories and Criteria, there appears to be a probable causal link between semaglutide administration and IMNM in our patient. This assessment is based on an appropriate temporal association, the lack of alternative explanations from other diseases or medications, and the clinically logical improvement observed after discontinuation of semaglutide. Reintroducing the drug would not be ethically acceptable due to the severity of the adverse reaction encountered. Nonetheless, we recognize that causation cannot be definitively established in this instance. In light of the rapidly increasing use of semaglutide, our report intends to alert clinicians to this potential adverse reaction and underscore the importance of pharmacovigilance. Further research will determine whether the observed association is causal or merely coincidental.

The underlying factors contributing to our patient’s susceptibility to such a pronounced reaction from semaglutide are still unknown. With GLP-1 receptor agonists becoming increasingly prevalent, investigating potential risk factors will be critical if additional cases with a similar temporal connection to semaglutide use are observed in the future.
