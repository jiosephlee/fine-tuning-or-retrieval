Title: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

Brian Lam, Eleni Tiniakou, Xinhai Robert Zhang, Shervin Assassi

Abstract:

Summary: This case details a woman in her 60s who developed immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) subsequent to receiving semaglutide. Over four months, she experienced worsening dysphagia, dysphonia, proximal muscle weakness, and an increase in creatine kinase levels. The onset of her clinical features was temporally associated with a single semaglutide dose. Diagnostic investigations demonstrated necrotising myopathy with evidence of inflammatory infiltrates on muscle biopsy. Her condition improved with immunosuppressive therapy consisting of corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG). She gradually regained her pre-illness level of function, maintaining remission for several months after stopping immunosuppression and avoiding glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists. This report presents what is, to our knowledge, the first instance of IMNM potentially linked to GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy, which showed a favorable response to brief immunosuppressive management with IVIG.

Background:

Over the past several years, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have been widely prescribed to manage metabolic disorders such as type 2 diabetes and obesity. Notably, one adverse effect reported with this class of drugs is a reduction in skeletal muscle mass. Recently, there was a case report of rhabdomyolysis with necrotizing characteristics linked to tirzepatide, another frequently utilized GLP-1 receptor agonist. In this report, the patient experienced a marked increase in creatine kinase (CK) levels, reaching the thousands, while on therapy; the CK levels began to decrease within a few days of stopping the medication, consistent with a diagnosis of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis.

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) represents a unique form of muscle disease marked by rapidly progressing weakness predominantly affecting the proximal muscles, with muscle biopsies showing myofibre necrosis and minimal lymphocytic infiltration. The autoantibodies most strongly linked to IMNM are anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (SRP). Notably, statin medications, frequently prescribed for metabolic conditions, have a well-documented association with HMGCR autoantibody positivity. Historically, cases involving statin exposure where patients developed necrotising muscle symptoms were usually labeled as toxic myopathy or polymyositis before the identification of the connection to HMGCR autoantibodies. In such scenarios, many patients continued to experience muscle symptoms and elevated creatine kinase (CK) levels even after discontinuation of the statin. These persistent findings, in conjunction with a favorable response to immunosuppressive therapy, supported an autoimmune mechanism and ultimately contributed to the recognition of HMGCR autoantibodies as a defining feature of this disorder. It is important to recognize, however, that roughly one-third of IMNM patients test negative for known autoantibodies. This seronegative group presents further diagnostic hurdles and raises the possibility that additional as-yet unidentified autoantibodies or initiating factors may play a role.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 60s, whose medical background includes obesity, fatty liver disease, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and a distant history of deep vein thrombosis/pulmonary embolism, presented with gradually worsening proximal muscle weakness along with difficulty swallowing and changes in voice.

Prior to the onset of these symptoms, she had been in stable health and maintained full independence in daily activities. Three years before her current decline, she was assessed as an outpatient due to elevated liver transaminases. At that time, both aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) were mildly elevated to roughly twice the normal upper limit, with ALT values surpassing those of AST. Other liver function tests, such as alkaline phosphatase, albumin, bilirubin, and platelet counts, remained within the normal reference range. She reported no alcohol consumption and was not using any medications known to cause liver toxicity. Abdominal ultrasonography identified an enlarged liver with uniformly increased echotexture, which was most compatible with hepatic steatosis. Serological workup for viral hepatitis, as well as tests for anti-nuclear, anti-mitochondrial, anti-smooth muscle, and liver-kidney microsomal antibodies, all yielded negative results. Liver biopsy findings confirmed steatohepatitis, matching the diagnosis of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) with a NAFLD Activity Score of 4 out of 8 and stage 0 fibrosis. Recommendations included adopting a calorie-restricted diet and engaging in regular physical activity.

Because her fatty liver and obesity persisted 2.5 years following her NAFLD diagnosis, semaglutide therapy was started. Shortly after her initial administration of semaglutide (0.25 mg subcutaneously), she developed nausea and vomiting. These symptoms continued, prompting admission to the hospital two days later for ongoing nausea, vomiting, inadequate oral intake, shortness of breath, acute renal failure, and marked metabolic acidosis. Management included intravenous fluids, bicarbonate, and anti-emetic agents. She required a brief stay in the intensive care unit to receive non-invasive ventilation. Her renal function subsequently recovered with hydration alone, with no necessity for haemofiltration or haemodialysis. On admission, her creatine kinase level was elevated to 337 U/L, though she exhibited no other related symptoms such as rash, muscle pain, or dark-colored urine. During her hospitalization, paroxysmal atrial fibrillation also occurred, although serum troponin levels remained within normal limits. She was released after a five-day inpatient stay. By discharge, her gastrointestinal complaints had abated, but she remained physically deconditioned. Over the next four months, she developed increasing weakness. She reported no history of statin therapy, noteworthy use of supplements associated with IMNM—including mushrooms or red yeast rice—or any other dietary supplements. She also denied any family history of myopathies, substantial or extended physical exertion recently, or use of alcohol or illicit substances.

Four months following her first and only administration of semaglutide, her condition progressively worsened. She became unable to either stand or sit upright independently and developed dysphagia affecting both liquids and solids over several days. These issues led her to seek care at an external hospital, where her admission labs revealed a creatine kinase (CK) level of 3992 U/L (reference range: 12–191 U/L). Management included intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for 5 days, administered under the direction of neurology consultants via telemedicine. She was subsequently transferred to a tertiary care facility for assessment by the rheumatology team. Upon evaluation, she was afebrile, maintained normal blood pressure, and showed no signs of distress. Physical exam demonstrated pronounced muscle weakness, most pronounced in proximal muscles (see table 1). No rash, skin abnormalities, pulmonary crackles, joint inflammation, edema, sicca symptoms, oral ulcers, or other neurological deficits were found.

After receiving pulse methylprednisolone, she was transitioned to oral prednisone at a dosage of 1 mg/kg per day. Given her ongoing weakness and pronounced symptoms, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at 2 g/kg, divided over 4 days. Over a period of 2 weeks, her CK levels showed significant reduction (see figure 1). She was discharged following an almost 4-week hospital stay. Twelve days post-discharge, she was readmitted for 10 days due to septic shock stemming from a pseudomonal urinary tract infection, during which her CK remained within normal limits.

Multiple outpatient follow-up visits documented continual progress in both her physical function and general health, with CK measurements consistently in the normal range. This allowed for prompt tapering and discontinuation of her prednisone. She did not receive any further IVIG beyond the initial inpatient course, owing both to the expense and her continued clinical improvement after stopping semaglutide.

She was last seen in the outpatient setting 10 months after her initial evaluation and treatment initiation and had regained her baseline functional status, off immunosuppression for 8 months.

Investigations:

Her laboratory assessment revealed a creatine kinase (CK) level of 1237 U/L at the time of evaluation, with a maximum value of 3992 U/L documented one week earlier at the referring facility. Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) was within the normal reference range. Aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) were elevated at 191 U/L and 165 U/L, respectively, in the context of both increased CK and a background of fatty liver disease. The autoantibody panel was significant for absence of anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-Sjogren’s syndrome antibodies type A (anti-SSA), anti-Smith antibodies, anti-U1 ribonucleoprotein antibodies (anti-U1 RNP), anti-double-stranded DNA antibodies, antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), and anti-mitochondrial antibodies. Complement levels and creatinine were unremarkable, and no cytopenias were noted. The myositis-specific antibody panel was negative for SRP, HMGCR, Jo-1, Pl-7, Pl-12, EJ, OJ, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-y, NXP-2, and cN-1A. She was found to have a low-titer positive SS-B antibody at 2.4 (normal <1.0). Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was raised at 22 mm/hour and C-reactive protein measured 22.8 mg/L. Of note, high-sensitivity troponin (HS-Troponin) was mildly increased at 62 pg/mL (reference ≤53 pg/mL), and her pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (proBNP) level was substantially elevated at 4358 pg/mL (normal: 0–125 pg/mL).

Key imaging studies consisted of MRI of both femurs, echocardiogram, cardiac MRI, liver Doppler ultrasound, high-resolution chest CT, and abdominal MRI. The MRI of both femurs demonstrated mild, symmetric muscular edema in both legs, predominantly involving the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps muscles, along with subcutaneous edema on the lateral aspects of both thighs (see figure 2). Echocardiography showed a left ventricular ejection fraction estimated at 65%–70%, and right ventricular systolic pressure measured at 46 mm Hg. Cardiac MRI revealed mild hypokinesis in the mid-anteroseptal and anterior cardiac walls with associated myocardial edema, without definitive late gadolinium enhancement, findings possibly indicative of myocardial inflammation, early myocarditis, or possibly focal stress-induced cardiomyopathy. There was no evidence of significant myocardial infarction, scarring, or notable valvular abnormalities. Liver Doppler ultrasound revealed hepatosteatosis. High-resolution chest CT was unremarkable for interstitial lung disease or lymphadenopathy. Abdominal MRI demonstrated hepatomegaly with hepatic steatosis but was otherwise normal, showing no lymphadenopathy.

Further investigations included a repeat liver biopsy and a biopsy of the thigh muscle. The liver biopsy, obtained during her hospitalization, showed several samples of hepatic tissue exhibiting both small and large droplet macrovesicular steatosis involving roughly 70% of the examined liver parenchyma, without notable portal inflammation or interface hepatitis. These results aligned with her known hepatosteatosis, revealing slight advancement of her hepatic condition, classified as NAFLD Activity Score 5 out of 8, stage 1. The right vastus lateralis muscle biopsy revealed scattered necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers (figure 3A,B). Immunohistochemical analysis highlighted acid phosphatase (figure 3D) and cluster of Differentiation-68 (CD68), a macrophage-associated marker, accompanied by evidence of myophagocytosis (figure 4B). In addition, there was membrane attack complex positivity seen on both necrotic and non-necrotic myofibers (figure 4C), indicative of an inflammatory process. Occasional CD3-positive cells, marking T lymphocytes (figure 4A), as well as perivascular inflammatory infiltrates (figure 3C), were also observed. The collective muscle biopsy findings supported a diagnosis of IMNM.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient developed an acute and pronounced response following her first and only administration of semaglutide, indicative of intolerance. Initially, she exhibited gastrointestinal symptoms and renal failure believed to be secondary to hypovolemia. At the time of her first assessment, her creatine kinase (CK) was only slightly elevated, which did not align with a diagnosis of rhabdomyolysis. However, despite discontinuing semaglutide, she continued to deteriorate, and over the subsequent four months, her muscle weakness progressed in distribution alongside a twelvefold rise in CK levels, pointing towards an immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) likely related to semaglutide exposure. Additionally, histopathological evidence compatible with drug-induced IMNM, absence of clinical manifestations characteristic of connective tissue disease, and significant recovery following initial immunosuppressive therapy and withdrawal of semaglutide further strengthen this association.

It is important to highlight that the patient exhibited elevated transaminases before commencing semaglutide therapy, a finding sometimes associated with myopathies. Nevertheless, a liver biopsy performed three years prior to semaglutide initiation confirmed hepatosteatosis, and the mild increases in AST and ALT remained after creatine kinase concentrations returned to normal. Therefore, her chronic transaminase elevations most likely stem from hepatic causes rather than muscle injury. Additionally, her cardiac assessment did not provide definitive evidence of ongoing myocarditis. Considering the clinical context and her swift improvement, stress-related myopathy would also be a plausible explanation.

The detection of isolated SS-B antibody positivity with negative ANA and SS-A was unanticipated in this case. To date, there are no reports in the literature describing isolated SS-B seropositivity with negative ANA and SS-A as a cause of IMNM. It was not possible to obtain a CK level from before the initiation of semaglutide. Thus, we cannot fully rule out the possibility that a subclinical autoimmune IMNM was present prior to starting semaglutide. Nevertheless, the twelvefold elevation in CK and onset of progressive proximal muscle weakness following the introduction of semaglutide support the probability that this episode of IMNM was induced by the medication. Furthermore, the patient’s severe IMNM subsided after a brief regimen of corticosteroids and IVIG, accompanied by discontinuation of semaglutide. This pattern is more consistent with drug-induced IMNM, which often remits with short-term immunosuppression after withdrawal of the causative drug, as opposed to idiopathic IMNM, which typically necessitates extended, aggressive immunosuppressive treatment.

Treatment:

She received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for 5 days, then transitioned to oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg per day. Owing to ongoing weakness and significant clinical severity, IVIG was administered at a total dose of 2 g/kg, divided across 4 days. She underwent several outpatient follow-up visits, during which she demonstrated continued progress in functional capacity and general health, as well as consistently normal CK levels. This allowed for a successful and expedited taper of prednisone, completed over a 2-month period. Given financial constraints and maintained clinical improvement without semaglutide, she did not require further IVIG after her initial hospitalization course.

Outcome and follow-up:

At her most recent outpatient follow-up, which took place 10 months after her initial assessment and the start of therapy, she had returned to her previous level of functioning and had been off immunosuppressive medications for the past 7 months. Her creatine kinase levels stayed within the normal limits following her hospitalization and immunosuppressive therapy. Repeat echocardiography and cardiac MRI demonstrated resolution of the previously noted abnormalities. Additionally, she had no further episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation.

Discussion:

GLP-1 receptor agonists, such as semaglutide, are being utilized with increasing frequency and have shown efficacy in treating metabolic conditions like obesity and type 2 diabetes. Gastrointestinal adverse effects are commonly observed, but these agents may also be linked to reductions in muscle mass and, on rare occasions, rhabdomyolysis. In this case, the progression of the patient’s symptoms aligns more closely with IMNM than with rhabdomyolysis. After a single dose of semaglutide, the patient’s CK rose twelvefold over a 4-month period, a pattern inconsistent with what would be expected in drug-induced rhabdomyolysis secondary to direct toxicity, where normalization of CK typically occurs within weeks. In contrast, a previously documented instance of rhabdomyolysis related to tirzepatide demonstrated the expected clinical trajectory: an acute, pronounced rise in CK (CK = 57,014 at presentation) followed by a rapid decrease with discontinuation of the medication and administration of intravenous fluids over a matter of days, without the need for immunosuppressive therapy.

According to the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) causality criteria, semaglutide likely has a probable causal relationship with the IMNM observed in this patient, as the timeline of symptom development is appropriate, alternative explanations such as other diseases or medications are unlikely, and there was a clinically appropriate improvement following discontinuation of the drug. A rechallenge was not performed due to the severity of the adverse effect, as such an action would not be ethically justified. Nonetheless, we recognize that causation cannot be definitively confirmed in this case. With the rapidly increasing use of semaglutide, this report seeks to alert clinicians to this potential side effect and emphasize the need for vigilant pharmacovigilance. Further research is required to determine whether the observed association is causal or merely coincidental.

The reason why our patient experienced such a marked adverse reaction to semaglutide is still undetermined. As the utilization of GLP-1 receptor agonists rises, it will be critical to investigate factors that may increase susceptibility, particularly if additional cases temporally associated with semaglutide administration are documented going forward.
