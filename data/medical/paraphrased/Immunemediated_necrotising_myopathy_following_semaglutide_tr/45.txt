Title: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

Brian Lam, Eleni Tiniakou, Xinhai Robert Zhang, Shervin Assassi

Abstract:

Summary: This case report details a woman in her 60s who developed immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) following administration of semaglutide. She presented with worsening dysphagia, dysphonia, proximal muscle weakness, and elevated creatine kinase levels over a four-month period. These manifestations emerged after she received a single dose of semaglutide. Diagnostic evaluation demonstrated necrotising myopathy with inflammatory infiltrates on muscle biopsy. The patient was treated with immunosuppressive therapy including corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), which resulted in clinical improvement. She subsequently regained her baseline level of function, remained off immunosuppressive agents for several months, and continued to abstain from glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists. This represents the first documented instance of IMNM likely linked to GLP-1 receptor agonist exposure, demonstrating a favorable outcome with short-term immunosuppression and IVIG therapy.

Background:

Over the past several years, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have been widely administered for metabolic conditions such as type 2 diabetes and obesity. Loss of skeletal muscle mass has been among the documented side effects for this group of drugs. A recent case report detailed an episode of rhabdomyolysis with necrotizing characteristics linked to tirzepatide, a frequently prescribed GLP-1 receptor agonist. The patient in this report experienced a marked increase in creatine kinase (CK) levels into the thousands during ongoing therapy, which began to normalize within a few days following discontinuation of the medication, aligning with the presentation of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis.

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) represents a unique form of myopathy that presents with severe weakness of the proximal muscles, accompanied by necrosis of muscle fibers and minimal lymphocytic infiltration on histological examination. Autoantibodies such as anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (SRP) are strongly linked to IMNM. Of note, statins, widely prescribed for metabolic conditions, are closely correlated with HMGCR autoantibody positivity. Prior to the recognition of this association, several individuals taking statins developed symptoms of necrotising myopathy and were frequently misclassified as having toxic myopathy or polymyositis. In these cases, numerous patients continued to exhibit myopathy and elevated CK levels even after discontinuing statin therapy. The subsequent clinical improvement following immunosuppressive treatment indicated an autoimmune component, ultimately resulting in the identification of the HMGCR autoantibody as a defining feature of this condition. It is important to highlight that roughly one-third of IMNM patients do not exhibit detectable autoantibodies. This seronegativity not only presents further difficulties in diagnosis but also implies that there may be undiscovered autoantibodies and triggers involved.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 60s with past medical conditions including obesity, fatty liver disease, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and a remote history of deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism, presented with worsening proximal muscle weakness, difficulty swallowing, and changes in her voice.

Previously, she maintained good health and was fully capable of performing daily tasks independently. Three years before the onset of her symptoms, she underwent an outpatient workup for elevated transaminases. During that evaluation, she exhibited mildly increased aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels, both roughly two times the upper limit of normal, with ALT exceeding AST. Alkaline phosphatase, albumin, bilirubin, and platelet counts remained within normal values. She denied alcohol consumption and had not been using any hepatotoxic agents. Abdominal ultrasonography identified an enlarged liver with diffuse increased echogenicity, suggestive of hepatic steatosis. Laboratory tests, including viral hepatitis panels and antibodies—anti-nuclear, anti-mitochondrial, anti-smooth muscle, and liver-kidney microsomal—were all negative. Liver biopsy revealed steatohepatitis compatible with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), NAFLD Activity Score grade 4/8, stage 0. She was counseled to adopt a reduced-calorie diet and to exercise regularly.

Approximately two and a half years following her NAFLD diagnosis, persistent hepatic steatosis and obesity led to the initiation of semaglutide therapy. After administering her first subcutaneous dose of semaglutide (0.25 mg), she developed nausea and vomiting within a few hours. The symptoms continued, prompting hospital admission two days later due to persistent nausea and vomiting, inadequate oral intake, shortness of breath, acute renal failure, and marked metabolic acidosis. Management included intravenous fluids and bicarbonate, alongside anti-emetic therapy. She required a brief stay in the intensive care unit for non-invasive ventilatory support. Subsequent hydration resulted in improved renal function, eliminating the need for haemofiltration or haemodialysis. Upon admission, her creatine kinase level was elevated at 337 U/L. She did not report additional symptoms such as rash, myalgias, or dark urine. During the admission, she also experienced paroxysmal atrial fibrillation; however, her serum troponin remained within normal limits. After five days, she was discharged; her gastrointestinal complaints had resolved but she exhibited physical deconditioning. Over the ensuing four months, she experienced gradually worsening weakness. She denied previous statin use, substantial intake of supplements linked to IMNM including mushrooms or red yeast rice, or use of other nutritional supplements. She also reported no notable family history of myopathy, no recent excessive or prolonged exercise, nor alcohol or illicit drug consumption.

Four months after receiving her first and only dose of semaglutide, her condition continued to worsen. She became unable to stand or sit upright independently and reported experiencing dysphagia with both liquids and solids over the preceding several days. As a result, she sought care at an external hospital, where initial labs indicated an elevated CK of 3992 U/L (reference range: 12–191 U/L). Neurology provided remote consultation and she was administered intravenous methylprednisolone 1000 mg daily for 5 days. Subsequently, she was transferred to a tertiary care center for rheumatological assessment, at which time she was found to be afebrile, normotensive, and appeared comfortable. Physical examination demonstrated pronounced muscle weakness, predominantly affecting proximal muscle groups (see table 1). She showed no evidence of rash, skin abnormalities, pulmonary crackles, synovitis, edema, sicca symptoms, oral ulcers, or any other neurological impairments.

After receiving pulse methylprednisolone, oral prednisone was administered at a dose of 1 mg/kg per day. Owing to ongoing muscle weakness and significant disease manifestations, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was provided at a total dose of 2 g/kg over 4 days. Over the subsequent two weeks, her creatine kinase levels showed substantial improvement (figure 1). She was discharged following an inpatient stay of nearly 4 weeks. Twelve days post-discharge, she was re-admitted for 10 days with septic shock secondary to a pseudomonal urinary tract infection; during this second hospitalization, her creatine kinase was within the normal range.

She underwent multiple outpatient follow-up visits, during which her functional capacity and general health continued to improve, with sustained normal CK values. Consequently, prednisone was discontinued swiftly without complications. Given both the financial considerations and her substantial progress after stopping semaglutide, she did not require further IVIG following the initial series administered during hospitalization.

At her most recent outpatient assessment, 10 months after starting therapy and initial evaluation, she had returned to her baseline functional level, remaining free from immunosuppressive agents for the preceding 8 months.

Investigations:

Her laboratory evaluation revealed a creatine kinase (CK) level of 1237 U/L at presentation, having reached a maximum of 3992 U/L one week earlier at the referring facility. Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) was within reference limits. Aspartate aminotransferase (AST) measured 191 U/L and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) 165 U/L, both elevated in the context of increased CK and a past medical history of fatty liver disease. Autoantibody testing demonstrated negative results for anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-Sjogren’s syndrome antibody type A (anti-SSA), anti-Smith antibody, anti-U1 ribonucleoprotein (anti-U1 RNP), anti-double-stranded DNA, anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA), and anti-mitochondrial antibodies. Complement levels were normal, renal function (creatinine) was within standard limits, and there was no evidence of cytopenias. Myositis-specific antibody panel was negative for signal recognition particle (SRP), HMGCR, Jo-1, Pl-7, Pl-12, EJ, OJ, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-y, NXP-2, and cN-1A autoantibodies. A low-titer positive SS-B autoantibody was identified at 2.4 (reference range: <1.0). Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was increased at 22 mm/hour, and C-reactive protein was elevated at 22.8 mg/L. Additionally, high-sensitivity troponin (HS-Troponin) was raised at 62 pg/mL (normal: ≤53 pg/mL), and pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (proBNP) was markedly elevated at 4358 pg/mL (reference: 0–125 pg/mL).

Imaging studies performed included MRI of both femurs, echocardiogram, cardiac MRI, liver ultrasound with Doppler, high-resolution CT of the chest, and MRI of the abdomen. The MRI of the femurs identified mild, symmetric muscle edema bilaterally, most pronounced in the proximal portions of the vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps muscles, along with subcutaneous edema along the lateral regions of both thighs (figure 2). Echocardiography showed a left ventricular ejection fraction estimated at 65%–70%, with a right ventricular systolic pressure measured at 46 mm Hg. Cardiac MRI displayed mild hypokinesis of the mid-anteroseptal and anterior cardiac wall, which was accompanied by myocardial edema but lacked definitive late gadolinium enhancement; these findings could reflect myocardial inflammation or early myocarditis, or alternatively, a focal stress-induced cardiomyopathy. There was no evidence of significant myocardial scarring, infarction, or valvular abnormality. Liver ultrasound with Doppler revealed hepatosteatosis. High-resolution chest CT ruled out interstitial lung disease and did not detect lymphadenopathy. MRI of the abdomen showed steatotic hepatomegaly; otherwise, it was unremarkable and without lymphadenopathy.

Additional advanced diagnostic tests included another liver biopsy as well as a biopsy of the thigh muscle. The liver biopsy, conducted during her hospitalization, showed multiple hepatic tissue samples with both small and large droplet macrovesicular steatosis, affecting about 70% of the examined hepatic parenchyma, without notable portal inflammation or evidence of interface hepatitis. These results correlated with her prior diagnosis of hepatosteatosis, demonstrating mild advancement to an NAFLD Activity Score grade 5/8, stage 1. Biopsy of the right vastus lateralis muscle revealed scattered necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers (figure 3A,B). Immunohistochemistry showed positive staining for acid phosphatase (figure 3D) and cluster of Differentiation-68 (CD68), which marks macrophages, along with evidence of myophagocytosis (figure 4B). Staining for the membrane attack complex was observed in both necrotizing and non-necrotizing myofibers (figure 4C), indicative of an inflammatory reaction. A small number of CD3-positive T-cells (figure 4A) and perivascular inflammatory cells (figure 3C) were also noted. The muscle biopsy findings were consistent with a diagnosis of IMNM.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient developed an abrupt and notable adverse response following her first and only administration of semaglutide, indicating an intolerance. Her presenting complaints involved gastrointestinal disturbances and acute renal failure likely due to hypovolemia. Although her creatine kinase (CK) level was only slightly increased, which did not meet criteria for rhabdomyolysis during her initial assessment, persistent symptoms despite discontinuation of semaglutide, a pattern of muscle weakness, and a twelvefold elevation in CK over the subsequent four months point toward an immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) potentially related to semaglutide. Additionally, biopsy findings compatible with drug-induced IMNM, absence of clinical indicators for connective tissue disease, and continued clinical improvement after immunosuppressive therapy and withdrawal of semaglutide, all strengthen the evidence for this association.

It is significant that elevated transaminase levels were present before semaglutide administration, a feature observed in myopathies. Nonetheless, three years prior to starting semaglutide, she underwent a liver biopsy confirming hepatosteatosis, and her mild increase in AST and ALT remained even after normalization of CK values. Therefore, her chronic transaminase elevation is most likely attributable to hepatic causes rather than muscle injury. Additionally, cardiac assessments did not definitively indicate active myocarditis. In this context, a stress-induced myopathy would also be a plausible explanation, especially considering her swift improvement.

The detection of SS-B antibody positivity in the absence of ANA and SS-A antibodies was unusual. To date, the literature lacks reports of isolated SS-B antibody positivity accompanied by negative ANA and SS-A resulting in IMNM. Unfortunately, we did not have access to a CK value measured prior to initiating semaglutide, which limits our ability to definitively rule out the possibility of pre-existing, subclinical autoimmune IMNM before semaglutide commencement. Nevertheless, the twelvefold increase in CK levels along with gradually worsening proximal muscle weakness following semaglutide administration suggest that this IMNM episode is likely medication-associated. Furthermore, the patient’s significant IMNM improved after a brief period of corticosteroid and IVIG treatment in combination with discontinuation of semaglutide. This clinical response is more characteristic of drug-induced IMNM, which frequently resolves with temporary immunosuppression and cessation of the offending medication, as opposed to idiopathic IMNM, which generally demands sustained and aggressive immunosuppressive therapy.

Treatment:

She received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for 5 days, then transitioned to daily oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg. Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at a total dose of 2 g/kg, divided over 4 days, given her ongoing weakness and pronounced symptoms. Multiple outpatient follow-up visits showed ongoing gains in her functional abilities and general well-being, with her CK levels remaining within the normal range throughout. As a result, prednisone was able to be tapered off over a 2-month period. Owing to both financial factors and her sustained clinical improvement after discontinuing semaglutide, no further IVIG treatments were given following her initial hospitalization course.

Outcome and follow-up:

She was most recently evaluated as an outpatient 10 months after her original assessment and beginning therapy, at which time she had returned to her usual functional capacity and had been free from immunosuppressive medication for 7 months. Her CK values stayed normal after hospitalization and immunosuppressive therapy. Follow-up echocardiography and cardiac MRI demonstrated resolution of the previously observed abnormalities. In addition, she did not have any further episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation.

Discussion:

GLP-1 receptor agonists, including semaglutide, are increasingly utilized and have shown efficacy in metabolic conditions such as obesity and type 2 diabetes. While gastrointestinal symptoms are the most frequently reported side effects, these agents may also contribute to reduced muscle mass and, on occasion, rhabdomyolysis. In this report, the patient's progression aligns more closely with IMNM than with rhabdomyolysis. The patient's CK levels rose by a factor of 12 over a period of four months following a single administration of semaglutide; in contrast, normalization of CK would typically be expected within weeks if semaglutide exerted a direct myotoxic effect, as documented in cases of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis. Importantly, a previously described case of rhabdomyolysis with tirzepatide followed the expected pattern: the individual presented with a significant acute increase in CK (CK=57,014) and exhibited rapid improvement after cessation of the drug and administration of hydration in a matter of days, without immunosuppressive therapy.

According to the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) Causality Categories and Criteria, the likelihood of a causal connection between semaglutide administration and IMNM in this patient is considered probable. This determination is based on the appropriate temporal association, the low probability that IMNM was attributable to alternative medications or underlying conditions, and the noticeable clinical improvement following discontinuation of semaglutide. Rechallenge was not pursued due to the severity of the adverse event, making it ethically unacceptable. Nevertheless, we recognize that definitive causality cannot be established in this case. With semaglutide’s rapid increase in usage, this case highlights the need for clinicians to be alert to this possible complication and to strengthen pharmacovigilance practices. Further research is needed to ascertain whether this relationship is truly causal or merely coincidental.

The underlying factors contributing to our patient’s pronounced reaction to semaglutide remain unknown. As GLP-1 receptor agonists become increasingly common, future investigations into susceptibility factors will be critical, particularly if additional cases with similar timing in relation to semaglutide exposure are observed.
