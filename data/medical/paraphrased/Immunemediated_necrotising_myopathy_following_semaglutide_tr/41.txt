Title: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

Brian Lam, Eleni Tiniakou, Xinhai Robert Zhang, Shervin Assassi

Abstract:

Summary: This case report details a woman in her 60s who developed immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) subsequent to semaglutide exposure. She experienced worsening dysphagia, dysphonia, proximal muscle weakness, and elevated creatine kinase levels over four months. Her symptoms began shortly after receiving a single semaglutide dose. Diagnostic evaluation revealed necrotising myopathy with inflammatory infiltrates on muscle biopsy. Treatment with corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) resulted in clinical improvement. Over time, she achieved full recovery to her premorbid functional status, remaining off immunosuppressive therapy for several months and continuing to avoid glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists. This report documents the first known case of IMNM likely linked to GLP-1 receptor agonist use, demonstrating notable responsiveness to brief immunosuppressive and IVIG treatment.

Background:

Over the past few years, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have been widely utilized to manage metabolic conditions such as type 2 diabetes and obesity. Notably, adverse effects for this medication class have included reductions in skeletal muscle mass. Recently, a case report detailed rhabdomyolysis exhibiting necrotising characteristics linked to tirzepatide, a commonly prescribed GLP-1 receptor agonist. In that instance, the patient experienced a rise in creatine kinase (CK) levels into the thousands during ongoing therapy, and the CK concentrations began to decline within several days following cessation of the drug, supporting the diagnosis of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis.

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) represents a unique form of myopathy distinguished by rapid onset proximal muscle weakness, histopathologic evidence of necrotising muscle fibers, and minimal lymphocytic infiltration. The autoantibodies anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (SRP) are closely linked to IMNM. Statin medications, frequently prescribed for metabolic disorders, have a strong association with HMGCR autoantibody positivity. Prior to recognition of the HMGCR autoantibody relationship, several statin-exposed patients developed manifestations of necrotising myopathy and were commonly diagnosed as toxic myopathy or polymyositis. Many of these individuals continued to experience myopathy and elevated creatine kinase levels despite discontinuing statin therapy. Subsequent response to immunosuppressive treatment supported an autoimmune etiology, ultimately leading to the identification of HMGCR autoantibody as a signature feature of this condition. Of note, approximately one-third of individuals with IMNM are seronegative. The absence of detectable autoantibodies in this population adds complexity to diagnostic evaluation and indicates that additional autoantibodies and possible precipitants may remain unidentified.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 60s with obesity, hepatic steatosis, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and a distant history of deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism presented with worsening proximal muscle weakness, difficulty swallowing, and changes in her voice.

She had previously been in good health and able to independently perform all daily activities. Three years before the onset of her worsening symptoms, she was evaluated in the outpatient setting for elevated liver enzymes. During this assessment, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels were mildly increased to about twice the upper normal limit, with ALT higher than AST. Alkaline phosphatase, albumin, bilirubin, and platelet counts were normal. She reported no alcohol consumption and was not on any hepatotoxic drugs. Abdominal ultrasound indicated hepatomegaly with a uniformly increased echotexture, suggestive of hepatic steatosis. Serological testing for viral hepatitis, as well as anti-nuclear, anti-mitochondrial, anti-smooth muscle, and liver-kidney microsomal antibodies, were all negative. Liver biopsy demonstrated steatohepatitis consistent with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), with a NAFLD Activity Score of 4 out of 8 and stage 0 fibrosis. The patient was counseled to maintain a low-calorie diet and engage in regular physical activity.

As fatty liver and obesity persisted for 2.5 years following her NAFLD diagnosis, semaglutide therapy was started. Shortly after administration of her initial 0.25 mg subcutaneous dose, she began experiencing nausea and vomiting. Her symptoms continued and two days later she was admitted to hospital for ongoing nausea, vomiting, inadequate oral intake, dyspnoea, acute renal failure, and pronounced metabolic acidosis. Management included intravenous fluids and bicarbonate alongside anti-emetic medications. She underwent a brief transfer to the intensive care unit for non-invasive ventilation. After treatment, her renal function improved with hydration, obviating the need for haemofiltration or haemodialysis. On admission, her CK level was measured at 337 U/L. She did not report any other related manifestations, such as rash, muscle pain, or dark urine. During her hospital stay, she developed intermittent atrial fibrillation, though serum troponin remained within normal limits. She was released after five days. By discharge, her gastrointestinal complaints had subsided, although she was physically weakened. In the following four months, she experienced steadily worsening weakness. She stated she had never taken statins, nor did she consume supplements linked to IMNM, such as mushrooms or red yeast rice, or any other nutritional products. She denied any relevant family history of myopathy, recent strenuous or prolonged exercise, and alcohol or recreational drug use.

Four months following her initial and solitary administration of semaglutide, her condition continued to deteriorate. She lost the ability to stand or maintain an upright seated position without support and experienced several days of difficulty swallowing both liquids and solids. This led her to seek care at an external hospital, where laboratory evaluation revealed a creatine kinase (CK) of 3992 U/L (reference range: 12–191 U/L) on admission. Management included daily intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg for 5 days, with neurology oversight provided through telemedicine. Subsequently, she was transferred to a tertiary care facility for assessment by rheumatology. On arrival, she was afebrile, had normal blood pressure, and appeared comfortable. Examination demonstrated pronounced muscle weakness, most marked in proximal muscle groups (see table 1). There were no observed rashes, skin abnormalities, crackles, synovitis, edema, sicca symptoms, oral ulcers, or other neurological impairments.

After completing a course of intravenous methylprednisolone pulses, oral prednisone was initiated at a daily dose of 1 mg/kg. Because of ongoing muscle weakness and pronounced clinical findings, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at a total dose of 2 g/kg, divided across 4 days. Her creatine kinase levels demonstrated significant improvement over a two-week period (see figure 1). She was ultimately discharged following an almost four-week inpatient stay. Twelve days later, she required rehospitalisation for 10 days due to septic shock secondary to a pseudomonal urinary tract infection; during this second admission, her CK remained within normal limits.

She underwent multiple outpatient follow-up visits, during which she demonstrated ongoing enhancements in both functional capacity and general well-being, accompanied by sustained normal CK measurements. Consequently, prednisone tapering was accomplished quickly. Given financial constraints and her continued progress in the absence of semaglutide, no further IVIG was administered beyond the initial hospitalization course.

Her final outpatient assessment occurred 10 months following the beginning of evaluation and treatment, by which time she had fully restored her baseline functional abilities and had remained off immunosuppressive therapy for 8 months.

Investigations:

Her laboratory findings revealed a CK of 1237 U/L during her assessment, with a prior peak of 3992 U/L recorded one week earlier at the referring hospital. TSH was found to be within the reference range. AST measured 191 U/L and ALT was 165 U/L in the context of increased serum CK and a history of hepatic steatosis. Her autoantibody screening showed negative results for anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-Sjogren’s syndrome antibodies type A (anti-SSA), anti-Smith, anti-U1 ribonucleoprotein (anti-U1 RNP), anti-double-stranded DNA, ANCA, and anti-mitochondrial antibodies. Complement levels and creatinine were normal, and there was no evidence of cytopenias. The myositis antibody panel was negative for SRP, HMGCR, Jo-1, Pl-7, Pl-12, EJ, OJ, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-y, NXP-2, and cN-1A autoantibodies. There was a low-titer positive SS-B autoantibody present at 2.4 (reference <1.0). ESR was raised at 22 mm/hour and C-reactive protein at 22.8 mg/L. Additionally, high-sensitivity troponin was elevated to 62 pg/mL (reference ≤53 pg/mL) and proBNP was measured at 4358 pg/mL (reference: 0–125 pg/mL).

The imaging workup comprised bilateral femur MRI, echocardiography, cardiac MRI, liver ultrasound with Doppler, high-resolution chest CT, and abdominal MRI. The MRI of both femurs demonstrated mild, bilateral, and symmetric muscular edema, predominantly involving the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps muscles, along with subcutaneous edema along the lateral regions of both thighs (see figure 2). Echocardiography indicated a left ventricular ejection fraction between 65% and 70%, and a right ventricular systolic pressure measured at 46 mm Hg. Cardiac MRI identified mild hypokinesis in the mid-anteroseptal and anterior myocardial walls with corresponding myocardial edema and an absence of significant late gadolinium enhancement, findings that could be associated with myocardial inflammation or early-stage myocarditis, or possibly a focal stress cardiomyopathy. No substantial myocardial scar tissue, infarction, or notable valvular abnormalities were detected. The liver ultrasound with Doppler was remarkable for hepatic steatosis. High-resolution CT of the chest revealed no evidence of interstitial lung disease or lymphadenopathy. Abdominal MRI showed hepatomegaly due to steatosis but was otherwise normal, with no lymphadenopathy identified.

Additional advanced diagnostic testing included a repeat liver biopsy and a biopsy of thigh muscle. The liver biopsy obtained during her hospitalization revealed several hepatic tissue cores with both small and large droplet macrovesicular steatosis affecting about 70% of the examined liver parenchyma, without notable portal inflammation or interface hepatitis. These pathological features aligned with her known hepatosteatosis, indicating mild progression to a NAFLD Activity Score of grade 5/8, stage 1. Biopsy of the right vastus lateralis muscle showed scattered necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers (figure 3A,B). Immunohistochemical staining identified acid phosphatase (figure 3D) and cluster of Differentiation-68 (CD68), a marker for macrophages, accompanied by evidence of myophagocytosis (figure 4B). Staining for the membrane attack complex revealed positive results on both necrotic and intact muscle fibers (figure 4C), suggestive of an inflammatory response. Occasional CD3-positive cells, which indicate T-cells (figure 4A), and inflammatory cells surrounding blood vessels (figure 3C) were also observed. The muscle biopsy findings were indicative of IMNM.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient exhibited a pronounced and acute response to her first and only administration of semaglutide, raising concern for intolerance. She initially presented with gastrointestinal adverse effects and renal insufficiency likely attributable to hypovolemia. Although her creatine kinase was only slightly increased and did not meet criteria for rhabdomyolysis at presentation, the progression of her symptoms despite discontinuation of semaglutide, the specific pattern of muscle weakness, and a twelvefold elevation in CK measured over four months after semaglutide exposure, point toward an IMNM likely related to the medication. Additionally, the muscle biopsy findings compatible with drug-induced IMNM, absence of signs typical of an underlying connective tissue disorder, and the durable clinical improvement following initial immunosuppressive therapy along with withdrawal of semaglutide further reinforce this connection.

It is worth noting that she exhibited elevated transaminase levels before initiating semaglutide therapy, a finding sometimes associated with myopathies. Nevertheless, she was diagnosed with hepatosteatosis by liver biopsy three years before starting semaglutide, and her mildly increased AST and ALT persisted even after CK values returned to normal. Therefore, her chronic transaminase elevation most likely stems from hepatic rather than muscular causes. Additionally, her cardiac assessment did not provide definitive evidence of ongoing myocarditis. In this clinical context, stress-related myopathy is also plausible, particularly considering her swift improvement.

The finding of positive SS-B antibody alongside negative ANA and SS-A was unanticipated. To date, the literature does not report cases of isolated SS-B antibody positivity with concurrent negative ANA and SS-A resulting in IMNM. We did not have access to a CK value prior to the initiation of semaglutide, so it is not possible to conclusively rule out a pre-existing, indolent autoimmune IMNM before starting this medication. Nonetheless, the twelvefold increase in CK and the worsening proximal muscle weakness that manifested after semaglutide administration support the likelihood that the IMNM episode was precipitated by the drug. Moreover, the patient’s severe IMNM resolved following a brief treatment with corticosteroids and IVIG, coupled with cessation of semaglutide. This clinical trajectory is more consistent with medication-induced IMNM, which tends to respond to short-term immunosuppression following removal of the causative agent, in contrast to idiopathic IMNM, typically requiring sustained, intensive immunosuppressive therapy.

Treatment:

She received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for 5 days, after which she was transitioned to oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg per day. Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at a total dose of 2 g/kg, divided over 4 days, in light of her ongoing weakness and severe clinical presentation. She underwent multiple outpatient follow-up visits, during which she showed continued gains in functional abilities and general health, along with consistently normal creatine kinase (CK) measurements. As a result, her prednisone could be tapered off successfully within a 2-month period. Given both financial factors and her ongoing improvement after stopping semaglutide, no additional IVIG was administered following her initial hospital course.

Outcome and follow-up:

At her most recent outpatient follow-up, which occurred 10 months post initial assessment and commencement of therapy, she had returned to her baseline level of function and had been without immunosuppressive agents for 7 months. Her creatine kinase concentrations stayed normal after discharge and immunosuppressive treatment. Subsequent echocardiogram and cardiac MRI demonstrated resolution of the earlier noted abnormalities. She likewise had no further episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation.

Discussion:

GLP-1 receptor agonists, including semaglutide, are increasingly utilized and have shown efficacy in treating metabolic conditions such as obesity and type 2 diabetes. While gastrointestinal side effects are frequent, these agents might also be linked to reduced muscle mass and, on occasion, rhabdomyolysis. In this report, the patient's presentation aligns more closely with IMNM rather than rhabdomyolysis. CK levels rose twelvefold over a period of four months after a single semaglutide dose, contrasting with the expected normalization of CK levels within weeks should semaglutide toxicity have been responsible, as observed in cases of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis. Importantly, a previously documented instance of tirzepatide-related rhabdomyolysis displayed a classic pattern: the individual showed a pronounced acute CK increase (CK=57 014 at initial assessment) and rapid improvement following cessation of the medication and hydration, all within several days and without requiring immunosuppressive therapy.

According to the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) Causality Categories and Criteria, our case demonstrates a probable causal link between semaglutide administration and the development of IMNM, owing to an appropriate temporal association, the low likelihood that IMNM resulted from other conditions or medications, and a clinically plausible improvement upon discontinuation of semaglutide. Furthermore, re-exposing the patient to semaglutide would be inappropriate, given the severity of the adverse event. Nonetheless, we recognize that causality has not been conclusively established in this instance. Given the rapidly increasing use of semaglutide, the purpose of this report is to alert healthcare professionals to this potential complication and to contribute to improved pharmacovigilance. Further investigations will be required to determine whether the observed relationship is causal or merely coincidental.

The factors that may have rendered our patient susceptible to this pronounced reaction to semaglutide remain uncertain. With the continued widespread adoption of GLP-1 receptor agonists, research identifying possible risk factors will be essential should more cases emerge exhibiting a close temporal association with semaglutide administration.
