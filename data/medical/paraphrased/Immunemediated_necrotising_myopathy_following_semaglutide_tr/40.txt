Title: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

Brian Lam, Eleni Tiniakou, Xinhai Robert Zhang, Shervin Assassi

Abstract:

Summary: This case details a woman in her 60s who developed immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) following administration of semaglutide. Over four months, she exhibited worsening dysphagia, dysphonia, proximal muscle weakness, and elevated creatine kinase, with these manifestations emerging after just one dose of semaglutide. Investigations revealed necrotising myopathy characterized by inflammatory cell infiltration on muscle biopsy. The patient showed clinical improvement when treated with corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG). She slowly returned to her normal level of function and remained stable for several months without immunosuppression, as long as she avoided glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists. This report presents the first documented case of IMNM likely associated with GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy, demonstrating a favorable response to a brief course of immunosuppressive treatment and IVIG.

Background:

In recent years, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have become widely utilized for the management of metabolic conditions such as type 2 diabetes and obesity. Loss of skeletal muscle mass has been identified as one of the potential side effects associated with these agents. A recent case report detailed an episode of rhabdomyolysis with necrotizing characteristics linked to tirzepatide, a GLP-1 receptor agonist frequently prescribed. In the reported case, the patient experienced a creatine kinase (CK) increase reaching several thousand units during ongoing therapy, and the CK levels began to decline within days following cessation of the medication, consistent with rhabdomyolysis induced by the drug.

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) represents a specific form of myopathy marked by severe weakness of proximal muscles, necrosis of muscle fibers, and limited presence of lymphocytes on histopathological examination. The autoantibodies anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (SRP) are strongly correlated with IMNM. Statins, widely prescribed for metabolic conditions, have a well-documented association with HMGCR autoantibody positivity. Prior to the recognition of the link with HMGCR autoantibody, multiple individuals receiving statins who developed necrotising myopathy features were often misdiagnosed as having toxic myopathy or polymyositis. In numerous such cases, muscle weakness and elevated creatine kinase levels persisted even after discontinuing statin therapy. Clinical improvement upon introduction of immunosuppressive agents indicated an underlying autoimmune mechanism and contributed to the eventual identification of HMGCR autoantibody as a key feature of this pathology. It is important to recognize that approximately one-third of IMNM cases do not exhibit detectable autoantibodies. The absence of autoantibodies in this population introduces further complexity to diagnosis and implies the existence of additional, as yet unidentified, associated autoantibodies or triggers.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 60s, with past medical history notable for obesity, hepatic steatosis, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and a distant episode of deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism, presented with worsening proximal muscle weakness, difficulty swallowing, and changes in her voice.

Prior to the onset of symptoms, she was in stable health and able to perform her daily activities independently. Three years before her clinical decline, she had been evaluated as an outpatient for elevated liver enzymes. At that time, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) were mildly elevated, reaching approximately two times the upper limit of normal, with ALT higher than AST. Alkaline phosphatase, albumin, bilirubin, and platelet levels were all within normal ranges. The patient denied any alcohol intake and was not using medications known to cause liver toxicity. Abdominal ultrasound revealed an enlarged liver with increased overall echogenicity, most consistent with hepatic steatosis. Laboratory testing for viral hepatitis, anti-nuclear, anti-mitochondrial, anti-smooth muscle, and liver-kidney microsomal antibodies were all negative. Liver biopsy results demonstrated steatohepatitis in line with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), showing a NAFLD Activity Score of 4 out of 8, with stage 0 fibrosis. Lifestyle recommendations included adoption of a reduced-calorie diet and routine physical activity.

Owing to ongoing fatty liver and obesity two and a half years after being diagnosed with NAFLD, semaglutide therapy was started. After receiving her first dose of semaglutide (0.25 mg subcutaneous), she developed nausea and vomiting within a few hours. The symptoms continued, and two days later she was admitted to hospital for persistent nausea, vomiting, inadequate oral intake, shortness of breath, acute renal failure, and profound metabolic acidosis. Management included intravenous fluids, bicarbonate, and anti-emetics. She was briefly transferred to the intensive care unit for non-invasive ventilation. Her renal function improved following hydration, and neither haemofiltration nor haemodialysis was required. Upon admission, her creatine kinase (CK) was elevated at 337 U/L. She did not report other symptoms such as rash, muscle pain, or dark urine. During this admission, she developed episodes of atrial fibrillation, although her serum troponin remained within the normal range. She was discharged after five days, at which point her gastrointestinal symptoms had subsided, but she exhibited deconditioning. Over the next four months, she experienced gradually worsening weakness. She stated she had never used statins, nor consumed mushrooms, red yeast rice, or any other supplements associated with IMNM, or other nutritional supplements. She also reported no relevant family history of muscle disease, recent excessive or prolonged physical exertion, and denied use of alcohol or illicit drugs.

Four months following her initial and sole dose of semaglutide, the patient's condition continued to deteriorate. She became unable to stand or sit upright without aid and developed dysphagia to both liquids and solids over several days. This led her to seek care at an outside hospital, where admission laboratory studies revealed an elevated creatine kinase (CK) of 3992 U/L (reference: 12–191 U/L). She was started on 1000 mg intravenous methylprednisolone daily for five days, managed remotely by neurology via telemedicine. Subsequently, she was transferred to a tertiary center for rheumatology assessment. Upon arrival, she was afebrile, had normal blood pressure, and displayed no signs of distress. Physical exam demonstrated profound weakness, predominantly affecting proximal muscles (see table 1). There were no findings of rashes, skin alterations, crackles, synovitis, edema, sicca symptoms, oral ulcers, or other neurological abnormalities.

After administration of pulse methylprednisolone, she commenced daily oral prednisone at a dose of 1 mg/kg. Because of ongoing muscle weakness and significant clinical severity, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at 2 g/kg, divided over 4 days. Her creatine kinase (CK) levels showed considerable reduction within a 2-week period (figure 1). She was released from the hospital following nearly 4 weeks of inpatient care. Twelve days post-discharge, she was readmitted for 10 days with septic shock secondary to a pseudomonal urinary tract infection; during this hospitalization, her CK levels were within the normal range.

She had multiple outpatient follow-up visits, during which her functional capacity and general health kept improving, and her CK values remained within normal limits. Consequently, prednisone tapering was accomplished quickly and successfully. Given both financial considerations and sustained clinical improvement after discontinuing semaglutide, no further IVIG was administered beyond the first hospital course.

Her most recent outpatient evaluation occurred 10 months after beginning treatment; at that visit, she had returned to her original functional level and had not received immunosuppressive therapy for the preceding 8 months.

Investigations:

Her laboratory evaluation revealed a creatine kinase (CK) level of 1237 U/L upon assessment, with a maximum value of 3992 U/L recorded one week earlier at the referring facility. Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) was within the reference range. Aspartate aminotransferase (AST) measured 191 U/L and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) was 165 U/L, in the context of elevated serum CK and a history of fatty liver disease. Autoantibody screening demonstrated negative results for anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-Sjogren’s syndrome antibodies type A (anti-SSA), anti-Smith antibodies, anti-U1 ribonucleoprotein antibodies (anti-U1 RNP), anti-double-stranded DNA, antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), and anti-mitochondrial antibodies. Complement levels were normal, creatinine was within expected limits, and cytopenias were absent. Her myositis autoantibody panel was negative for SRP and HMGCR, as well as for Jo-1, Pl-7, Pl-12, EJ, OJ, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-y, NXP-2, and cN-1A autoantibodies. A low-titer positive SS-B autoantibody was noted at 2.4 (reference range: <1.0). Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was elevated at 22 mm/hour, and C-reactive protein measured 22.8 mg/L. Importantly, high-sensitivity troponin was raised at 62 pg/mL (normal ≤53 pg/mL), and pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (proBNP) was elevated at 4358 pg/mL (normal range: 0–125 pg/mL).

The patient underwent several significant imaging studies, including MRI of both femurs, echocardiography, cardiac MRI, Doppler liver ultrasound, high-resolution chest CT, and abdominal MRI. Bilateral femur MRI demonstrated mild, symmetric muscle edema, most pronounced in the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps, with additional subcutaneous edema along the lateral thighs (figure 2). Echocardiography revealed a left ventricular ejection fraction estimated at 65%–70%, along with a right ventricular systolic pressure measuring 46 mm Hg. Cardiac MRI identified mild hypokinesis involving the mid-anteroseptal and anterior walls in conjunction with myocardial edema and an absence of clear late gadolinium enhancement. These findings were suggestive of either myocardial inflammation/early myocarditis or potentially localized stress-induced cardiomyopathy. There was no evidence of substantial myocardial infarction, scarring, or major valvular abnormality. The liver ultrasound with Doppler indicated hepatic steatosis. High-resolution chest CT did not reveal interstitial lung disease or lymphadenopathy. MRI of the abdomen showed steatotic enlargement of the liver but was otherwise normal, with no lymphadenopathy detected.

Additional advanced diagnostic testing included both a repeat liver biopsy and a biopsy of thigh muscle. The liver biopsy, completed during her hospitalization, showed several samples of hepatic tissue containing both small and large droplet macrovesicular steatosis, affecting roughly 70% of the examined liver parenchyma. No substantial portal inflammation or interface hepatitis was present. This histopathology aligned with her prior diagnosis of hepatosteatosis and indicated mild advancement of her liver disease, reflected by a NAFLD Activity Score of 5 out of 8 and stage 1 classification. The right vastus lateralis muscle biopsy revealed scattered muscle fibers undergoing necrosis and regeneration (figure 3A,B). Immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated acid phosphatase staining (figure 3D) and CD68 positivity, marking macrophages with evidence of myophagocytosis (figure 4B). Additionally, membrane attack complex staining was identified on both necrotizing and non-necrotizing myofibers (figure 4C), indicative of an inflammatory reaction. Occasional CD3-positive T cells (figure 4A) were seen, as well as perivascular inflammatory infiltrates (figure 3C). The collective muscle biopsy results were consistent with a diagnosis of IMNM.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient exhibited a pronounced and immediate adverse response after her first and only administration of semaglutide, indicating intolerance to the drug. She initially developed gastrointestinal symptoms along with renal failure, which was thought to be related to hypovolaemia. Although creatine kinase (CK) was only slightly raised at the first assessment—making rhabdomyolysis unlikely—she continued to deteriorate despite discontinuation of semaglutide. The pattern of muscle weakness and a twelvefold CK elevation over the subsequent four months post-semaglutide are suggestive of immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) likely linked to semaglutide exposure. Additionally, muscle biopsy findings indicative of drug-associated IMNM, the absence of clinical evidence pointing to a connective tissue disorder, and significant recovery following initial immunosuppressive therapy along with withdrawal of semaglutide all further support this association.

It is worth noting that transaminase elevation was present before she initiated semaglutide, a finding that may occur in myopathies. Nevertheless, three years prior to starting semaglutide, she was diagnosed with hepatosteatosis via biopsy, and her mild AST/ALT increases continued even after her CK values returned to normal. Therefore, her chronic transaminase elevations are most likely hepatic in origin rather than resulting from muscle injury. Additionally, cardiac assessments did not conclusively demonstrate ongoing myocarditis. In this clinical scenario, stress-induced myopathy remains a plausible consideration, especially in light of her prompt improvement.

Her isolated SS-B antibody positivity, in the context of negative ANA and SS-A results, was unanticipated. To date, there are no documented cases in the literature describing IMNM associated solely with SS-B positivity while ANA and SS-A are negative. Regrettably, we did not have access to a creatine kinase (CK) value from before semaglutide initiation, so we cannot categorically rule out the presence of a low-level, pre-existing autoimmune IMNM prior to starting semaglutide. Nonetheless, the 12-fold elevation in CK and the onset of gradually worsening proximal muscle weakness following the initiation of semaglutide increase the probability that this episode of IMNM was induced by the drug. Furthermore, her pronounced IMNM improved following a brief regimen of corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), along with discontinuation of semaglutide. The patient’s clinical improvement corresponds more typically with drug-induced IMNM—which often resolves with short-term immunosuppression after stopping the causative medication—as opposed to idiopathic IMNM, which usually necessitates extended and aggressive immunosuppressive management.

Treatment:

She received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for 5 days, after which oral prednisone was initiated at 1 mg/kg per day. Owing to her ongoing muscular weakness and pronounced symptoms, IVIG was administered at 2 g/kg, divided across 4 days. Multiple outpatient follow-up visits demonstrated continued gains in function and overall wellbeing, with consistently normal creatine kinase levels. Consequently, she was able to taper off prednisone quickly within a 2-month period. Because of a combination of financial factors and sustained clinical stability after discontinuation of semaglutide, no further IVIG was given apart from the initial treatment during her hospitalization.

Outcome and follow-up:

She had her most recent outpatient follow-up 10 months following her initial assessment and start of therapy and had returned to her baseline level of function, having discontinued immunosuppressive agents for the past 7 months. Her creatine kinase measurements stayed normal after hospital discharge and immunosuppressive therapy. Subsequent echocardiogram and cardiac MRI demonstrated resolution of previously observed abnormalities. Additionally, there was no recurrence of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation.

Discussion:

GLP-1 receptor agonists like semaglutide are increasingly prescribed, with proven efficacy for metabolic conditions such as obesity and type 2 diabetes. Gastrointestinal symptoms are the most frequently reported adverse reactions, but these agents have also been linked to reductions in muscle mass and, in some cases, rhabdomyolysis. In the current report, the progression observed in the patient aligns more closely with IMNM rather than rhabdomyolysis. The patient's CK concentration rose 12 times above baseline across a four-month period after a single semaglutide administration; had a direct toxic effect from semaglutide been responsible, we would typically anticipate CK normalization within a few weeks, similar to other documented instances of drug-related rhabdomyolysis. Importantly, a prior rhabdomyolysis report related to tirzepatide followed the expected course, where the individual had a substantial acute CK increase (CK=57 014 at initial evaluation) and exhibited swift improvement after discontinuing the drug and initiating hydration, all within days and without any requirement for immunosuppressive therapy.

Utilizing the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) Causality Categories and Criteria, our case demonstrates probable causality between semaglutide administration and the development of IMNM, based on a plausible temporal association, the low likelihood that IMNM is attributable to alternative diseases or medications, and a clinically appropriate response following discontinuation of semaglutide. Reintroduction of the drug would be ethically unacceptable, considering the serious adverse reaction observed. Nonetheless, we recognize that definitive causality cannot be established in this instance. In light of the rapid increase in semaglutide usage, this case report seeks to inform clinicians about this possible adverse event and promote greater pharmacovigilance. Ongoing research will determine whether this relationship is causal or merely coincidental.

Additionally, the factors that may have predisposed this patient to such a marked response to semaglutide remain uncertain. As GLP-1 receptor agonists gain wider adoption, investigations into potential risk factors will be essential if further cases with close chronological association to semaglutide therapy are documented.
