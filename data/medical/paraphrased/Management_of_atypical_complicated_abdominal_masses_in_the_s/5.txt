Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

Rosalba De Nola, Emma Bassi, Cataldo Paletta, Mario Vicino

Abstract:

A woman in her 40s, who had not previously given birth, experienced abdominal pain and shortness of breath during her second trimester of pregnancy. Imaging via ultrasonography identified two substantial abdominal masses: the superior mass occupied the entire left subphrenic region and extended towards the uterine fundus, while the inferior mass was located in the pouch of Douglas. The patient was treated with laparotomy, leaving the lower mass undisturbed within the pelvis. Following surgery, she received routine prenatal care and underwent a planned caesarean section, which led to favorable maternal and neonatal outcomes. When encountering sizable, symptomatic masses during pregnancy, thorough assessment—including bimanual examination and safe imaging techniques like ultrasound and non-contrast MRI—is critical. Surgical intervention should be reserved for cases where there is a suspicion of malignancy or if clinical complications arise, with priority given to maintaining the pregnancy and minimizing unnecessary procedures. The decision regarding the method of delivery should take into account the number, size, and anatomical distribution of the uterine masses.

Background:

The identification and treatment of unusual or symptomatic abdominal masses in pregnant patients pose notable clinical difficulties. Diagnostic modalities are confined to physical examination, ultrasound (US) with colour Doppler, and non-contrast MRI. Preoperative US plays a critical role in localizing symptomatic uterine leiomyomas positioned on the anterior wall of the pregnant uterus, regardless of whether they are submucosal or pedunculated. MRI, on the other hand, provides enhanced diagnostic precision for cases involving multiple, sizable, intramural, or posteriorly situated leiomyomas by accurately delineating the spatial relationship between the myoma and the uterine cavity.

The utility of serum tumour markers during gestation continues to be contentious. In particular, CA-125 is known to physiologically increase in the first trimester before returning to baseline levels, rendering single measurements non-specific. However, serial assessments can yield informative trends that assist both in differential diagnosis and longitudinal monitoring. Effective management hinges on comprehensive multidisciplinary evaluation of risks to appropriately weigh obstetric considerations against the infrequent and intricate nature of these cases.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 40s, presenting with obesity (body mass index of 33) and gravida 1 para 0 abortus 1 status, sought medical attention at 21 weeks gestation (second trimester) due to intense and sudden abdominal pain located in the upper left quadrant and pelvis (Visual Analogue Scale=5 at both locations), accompanied by mild shortness of breath. Routine screening from the first and second trimesters indicated low risk for aneuploidies, corroborated by non-invasive prenatal testing, and revealed normal fetal morphology.

Initially, she was evaluated in the emergency department through a multidisciplinary approach. Her oxygen saturation was within normal limits (median: 97%). Thoracic examination showed a regular heart rate (median: 74 bpm) and blood pressure (median: 100/60 mm Hg). The ECG was unremarkable. Subsequently, she was transferred to the Obstetrical and Gynaecological Division. Examination revealed her abdomen was more distended than would be anticipated for her gestational age, with a firm, tense-elastic mass, measuring 18 cm and occupying the entire upper left quadrant, without clinical evidence of ascites.

Bimanual vaginal assessment revealed a fixed, rounded, hard mass approximately 9 cm in size located in the lesser pelvis, partially protruding into the posterior fornix.

Analgesia was managed with intravenous paracetamol 1000 mg twice daily, along with semi-orthostatic positioning to potentially mitigate diaphragmatic pressure from the gravid uterus and large abdominal mass. This regimen provided moderate symptom relief, diminishing pain scores from 5 to 1 on the Visual Analogue Scale, without requiring supplemental oxygen or ventilation.

During her admission, she developed a mild fever. Polymerase chain reaction testing via nasopharyngeal swab for severe acute respiratory syndrome Coronavirus 2 returned negative; however, urine culture identified a Klebsiella pneumoniae infection. Laboratory findings included iron deficiency anemia, as indicated by haemoglobin (Hb) at 9.9 g/L (normal range: 12.0–16.6 g/L) and serum ferritin at 25 mcg/L (normal range: 15–300 mcg/L), mildly elevated C reactive protein at 1.39 mg/dL (normal range: 0–0.3 mg/dL), and increased neutrophil count (white blood cells 11.29×10^3 /uL [normal range: 4–10×10^3 /uL]; neutrophils 9.27×10^3 /uL [normal range: 2.8–5.5×10^3 /uL]), findings considered compatible with physiological changes associated with pregnancy.

Investigations:

Ultrasound assessment revealed the presence of two sizable, unilocular cystic lesions characterized by smooth margins and vascularization at the periphery. The superior lesion had dimensions of 18×12×7 cm and spanned from the left subphrenic space down to the uterine fundus. The inferior lesion, measuring 9.5×8×8 cm, appeared to be attached to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration (58×71 mm).

A panel of tumour markers, including CA 19–9, CA 15–3, CEA, β2-microglobulin, and LDH, returned values within normal reference ranges. However, CA-125 was found to be elevated at 41.8 U/mL (normal <35 U/mL), and this increase continued from a previously recorded value of 36 U/mL two weeks prior, suggesting a mild upward trend in its serum levels. Subsequently, a non-contrast MRI scan was performed, revealing cystic lesions with thickened walls (1.4 cm), inhomogeneous fluid signals on both T1 and T2 fat-saturated sequences, and regions with focal hyperintensity on T1-T2 weighted imaging in solid areas (refer to figure 1; only T2 fat-sat images provided). The separation between the caudal lesion and adjacent tissues was indistinct, giving the impression of a continuum with the previously ultrasonographically detected posterior leiomyoma. No lymphadenopathy was detected on MRI.

Differential diagnosis:

During the US assessment, the cystic lesions closely resembled two large decidualised endometriomas; notably, the lower (caudal) cyst was seen in proximity to, and appeared adherent with, an intramural-subserosal leiomyoma located on the posterior wall of the uterus, which measured 58×71 mm.

MRI revealed the presence of two well-circumscribed, unilocular adnexal cysts exhibiting vascular hypertrophy and heterogeneous internal fluid (refer to figure 1). The lower lesion maintained apparent adherence to a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma demonstrating cystic degeneration, measuring 58×71 mm.

Owing to the spatial dominance of the gravid uterus and the substantial size of the masses, neither a distinct connecting stalk nor a vascular supply to the upper lesion could be delineated, thus leaning the diagnostic consideration toward ovarian origin—particularly endometriomas—given the mild increase in CA-125 during the second trimester of pregnancy (see discussion).

Integrating ultrasonographic and MRI findings, the differential diagnoses encompassed decidualised endometriomas and cystically degenerating leiomyomas: while the modest elevation in CA-125 was more indicative of an endometrioma for the upper cyst, the direct association with uterine tissue favored a myomatous etiology for the lower mass. The patient's dyspnoea was interpreted as secondary to the space-occupying effect of the extensive abdominal masses, in the absence of signs of pulmonary or cardiac pathology.

Treatment:

Given the progressive deterioration of the patient's clinical status (characterized by mass-induced abdominal pain and dyspnoea attributed to restrictive lung disease resulting from the expanding abdominal masses), alongside the imperative for a definitive diagnosis, the possibility of adverse events (such as rupture, torsion, or infection), and anticipated uterine growth corresponding with advancing gestation, a decision was made to perform a laparotomy via a longitudinal xiphoid-to-pubic incision at 21 weeks and 6 days gestation, following acquisition of informed consent.

Initially, two aliquots of free peritoneal fluid were obtained for microbiological culture and cytological analysis. Subsequent exploration of the abdominal cavity confirmed ongoing fetal cardiac activity.

Unexpectedly, the cranial mass proved to be a large, pedunculated uterine leiomyoma of atypical cystic and taut-elastic consistency, exhibiting considerable vascularity, partial torsion, and evidence of leakage, findings that likely accounted for the patient’s fever during hospitalization (refer to figure 2). As a result, the surgical team proceeded with careful excision of the mass from its uterine attachment, utilizing a double clamp technique (see figure 2). Following transection of the stalk, the exposed base was closed meticulously using a single absorbable suture. The caudal lesion, emerging from the uterine torus, was intentionally preserved in situ to avoid increasing obstetric risk; it displayed characteristics consistent with a large cystic intramural-subserosal leiomyoma, including fixation, a smooth exterior, and a soft texture.

The operative procedure took 2 hours and 25 minutes in total. Prophylactic administration of progesterone (100 mg intramuscularly) was initiated intraoperatively and continued at the same dosage daily until hospital discharge. Subsequently, the regimen shifted to intramuscular hydroxyprogesterone caproate (341 mg, one vial, every three days as specified in the package instructions) and was maintained until scheduled delivery. Perioperative prophylactic antibiotics included intravenous cefazolin 2 g intraoperatively, followed by postoperative treatment comprising intravenous cefazolin 1 g three times daily along with clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily, guided by the antibiogram of the urine culture (which revealed Klebsiella pneumoniae highly susceptible to cephalosporins and clarithromycin).

Outcome and follow-up:

Following the surgical intervention, the patient developed worsening anaemia (Hb 7.5 g/L, HCT 24.3%) alongside ongoing iron deficiency, necessitating intravenous administration of ferric carboxymaltose (1 g) as well as two successful blood transfusions (resulting in Hb 10.0 g/L, HCT 30.4%). She was discharged in stable condition without any postoperative complications 10 days later and subsequently placed under close obstetrical surveillance every 3 weeks. Ongoing evaluations documented appropriate fetal growth (at the 86th percentile according to the WHO fetal growth chart calculator, accessible online at https://srhr.org/fetalgrowthcalculator/#/), in addition to monitoring of the amniotic fluid index.

Analysis of the peritoneal fluid did not reveal any abnormalities on cultural or cytological testing. Histopathological evaluation identified benign uterine leiomyomas, with regions of ischaemic necrosis adjacent to the torsed pedicle.

The position and size of the caudal myoma would have created a significant barrier to descent of the fetal head during the second stage of labour. Accordingly, an elective caesarean section was planned and conducted along the previous surgical incision once the pregnancy reached 37 weeks’ gestation, thereby reducing the likelihood of uterine scar dehiscence and resulting in an excellent neonatal prognosis.

At present, the patient does not exhibit symptoms attributable to the myoma, and the remaining pelvic myoma has diminished slightly in size to 7.5 cm. She has established successful breastfeeding, but opts to defer any further surgical interventions due to the observed partial postpartum regression of the leiomyomas, her current asymptomatic state, and a sense of completion regarding motherhood. The infant continues to grow and develop appropriately.

Discussion:

Myomas can often be misdiagnosed as adnexal masses: the incidence during pregnancy is 0.3–10.1% and 1–6%, respectively .13

In this particular instance, the initial assessment identified abdominal masses that resembled two large decidualised endometriomas. However, the lower solid portion of the pelvic mass presented as a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma of the uterus, exhibiting cystic degeneration with dimensions of 58×71 mm on both ultrasound and MRI studies. Given the lack of established management protocols for pelvic masses during pregnancy, a multidisciplinary approach led to the decision that planned surgical intervention might reduce the likelihood of preterm labor.

Typically, maternal serum CA-125 exhibits two distinct peaks throughout pregnancy: one during the first trimester (median value 23 U/mL, p<0.00001) and another during the third trimester (median value 21 U/mL, p<0.001), with both values being significantly higher than that of the second trimester (median 14.0 U/mL). Contrastingly, in this case, the observed CA-125 level was 41.8 U/mL, surpassing the average concentration expected in the second trimester.

CA-125 is predominantly utilized as a tumor marker in the detection of ovarian malignancies, with a conventional cut-off point of ≥35 U/mL, particularly in women above 50 years of age where it yields improved diagnostic accuracy in terms of area under the curve, positive predictive value, sensitivity, and specificity. Nevertheless, elevated CA-125 levels can also be found in numerous benign scenarios, including endometriosis and adenomyosis (commonly >19 U/mL), pelvic inflammatory disease, menstruation, early and late stages of pregnancy (median 23 and 21 U/mL, respectively), increased risk of miscarriage, as well as in cases of myoma (generally <19 U/mL).57

The primary factors influencing clinical management included the detection of large, highly vascularized masses exhibiting both solid and cystic features, which presented an immediate risk of complications such as rupture, torsion, or infection. Additional considerations comprised the patient’s deteriorating symptoms—particularly escalating pain and dyspnoea—the potential for continued growth of the masses with an increased likelihood of hypoxia, necrosis, and degeneration, a mildly elevated oncological marker level (CA-125=41.8 U/mL), and the limitation of intra-abdominal space necessary for subsequent physiological expansion of the uterus.

Given the uncertain etiology and possible neoplastic nature of the masses, the patient subsequently received a non-contrast MRI to enhance diagnostic precision following ultrasonography. In alignment with contemporary guidelines, the administration of gadolinium contrast during pregnancy is generally contraindicated unless absolutely necessary for specific indications, such as rheumatological, inflammatory, or infiltrative skin disorders, or when there is risk of stillbirth or neonatal death. Consequently, the multidisciplinary team elected to perform a non-contrast MRI, ensuring that the imaging did not exceed a 1.5-T field strength, as this is regarded as a safe approach post-first trimester.

In this case, a collaborative team including surgeons, gynaecologists, and obstetricians made the decision to undertake a longitudinal laparotomic approach after securing formal written informed consent from the patient. Intraoperative findings revealed a pedicle associated with the superior lesion, creating a considerable risk for acute, classic complications. This risk assessment justified restricting the intervention to an upper myomectomy only. Additionally, repeated and interval-based transvaginal ultrasonography was employed during the procedure to assess ongoing fetal viability, with intraoperative progesterone administered as a preventive measure.

As reported in a comprehensive multicenter analysis by Zhao et al, it is essential to assess and record the fetal heart rate prior to, during, and following surgical intervention.

Ordinarily, surgical intervention is postponed until after delivery in favour of medical or watchful waiting. Nevertheless, Sakinci et al recently carried out a study at a tertiary institution, finding no statistically significant differences between women who underwent caesarean section (CS) with myomectomy and those with only CS without myomas, with respect to both preoperative and postoperative haemoglobin values or requirements for blood transfusion (\(p > 0.05\)). Conversely, the duration of both hospitalisation and surgery was notably increased in patients who received CS plus myomectomy (\(p = 0.001\) and \(p = 0.001\), respectively). The average myoma size observed was \(8.3 \pm 4.1\) cm, and a significant positive correlation was noted between myoma size and the length of hospital stay (\(p = 0.01\)).

The same investigation identified a statistically significant negative association between myoma size and gestational age at delivery ($p=0.035$).

During pregnancy and the puerperium, uterine leiomyomas exhibit a non-linear pattern in terms of size variation. Specifically, leiomyomas grow at a rate of 6.90% per week: between the first and second trimesters the increase is 6.12% ($p<0.001$), while between the second and third trimesters the growth slows to 3.10%±0.39% ($p<0.05$). Consequently, the most pronounced enlargement occurs during the first trimester as opposed to subsequent stages of pregnancy. Conversely, a marked reduction in myoma size is observed in the third trimester and at 6 weeks postpartum (mean decrease 44.25%±36.05%, $p<0.05$).

These findings underscore the necessity of a thorough ultrasonographic assessment of uterine myomas—including their size, anatomical position, quantity, and proximity to the placenta—prior to any surgical intervention.

When uterine myomas are identified in pregnancy, the standard management is typically conservative, owing to the significant risks associated with surgical procedures, such as major hemorrhage, loss of pregnancy, preterm labor, and potential uterine rupture. Nonetheless, myomectomy may become necessary if the patient develops complications such as torsion of a pedunculated myoma, or if persistent symptoms do not resolve with conservative measures. Surgical intervention should be as brief as possible to maximize pregnancy preservation, and can be safely performed laparoscopically, especially via the open Hasson technique, when gestational age is low (first or early second trimester), the lesion is relatively small, and the myoma's location is not intramural. This strategy offers improved outcomes regarding maternal morbidity and increased likelihood of vaginal delivery. Nonetheless, as demonstrated in the present case, there is a frequent tendency to opt for laparotomy instead of laparoscopy in instances where the myomas are larger, multiple, intramural, or occupy critical sites, factors which are linked to elevated cesarean section rates. This is particularly relevant when a substantial myoma is attached to the posterior isthmic portion of the uterus, potentially obstructing the vaginal birth pathway.
