Title: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

Rosalba De Nola, Emma Bassi, Cataldo Paletta, Mario Vicino

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her 40s, who had not previously given birth, presented in her second trimester with complaints of abdominal pain and shortness of breath. Ultrasound imaging identified two sizable intra-abdominal masses: one situated in the left subphrenic region extending up to the uterine fundus, and another located within the pouch of Douglas. She was managed surgically via laparotomy, during which the pelvic (caudal) mass was left untouched within the pelvis. The patient received routine obstetric care afterwards and underwent a planned caesarean section, achieving an optimal outcome for both mother and newborn. For pregnant patients with large, symptomatic abdominal masses, thorough assessment with bimanual examination and imaging modalities considered safe in pregnancy—such as ultrasound and non-contrast MRI—is crucial. Surgical intervention should be reserved for situations where there is suspicion of malignancy or the presence of clinical complications, with the priority being to maintain pregnancy and prevent unnecessary interventions. The decision regarding delivery method should be guided by the size, number, and anatomical position of the uterine lesions.

Background:

Diagnosing and managing atypical or symptomatic abdominal masses in pregnant patients involves considerable clinical complexity. The range of diagnostic modalities is constrained to physical examination, ultrasound (US) with colour Doppler, and non-contrast magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Preoperative US serves a key role in localizing symptomatic uterine leiomyomas on the anterior aspect of the pregnant uterus, whether they are submucosal or pedunculated. For cases involving multiple, large, intramural, or posterior leiomyomas, MRI provides greater diagnostic precision by accurately delineating the relationship between the leiomyoma and the uterine cavity.

The utility of serum tumour markers in pregnancy, particularly in the context of diagnosis, continues to be a matter of discussion. CA-125, for instance, naturally increases during the first trimester before returning to baseline, rendering single measurements unreliable for diagnosis. However, serial measurements may yield patterns beneficial for distinguishing among potential diagnoses and for ongoing monitoring. Achieving optimal management demands a comprehensive multidisciplinary risk assessment in order to weigh the associated obstetric risks against the infrequency and intricacy of these clinical presentations.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 40s with obesity (body mass index 33), gravida 1 para 0 abortus 1, presented to our clinic at 21 weeks gestation with intense, sudden onset abdominal pain affecting the left upper quadrant and pelvic region (Visual Analogue Scale score of 5 at each site), as well as mild shortness of breath. Routine screening tests during her first and second trimesters indicated low risk for chromosomal abnormalities, a finding later confirmed by non-invasive prenatal testing, and fetal morphology was reported as normal.

Initially, she presented to the emergency department for comprehensive assessment by a multidisciplinary team. Her oxygen saturation was within normal limits (median 97%). Thoracic examination showed a regular heart rate (median 74 bpm) and normal blood pressure (median 100/60 mm Hg). The ECG demonstrated no abnormalities. Subsequently, she was moved to the Obstetrical and Gynecological Division. On examination, her abdomen appeared more distended than anticipated for her gestational age, with a tender swelling and a firm, elastic mass measuring 18 cm occupying the entire upper left quadrant; there was no clinical evidence of ascites.

The bimanual vaginal examination showed the presence of a fixed mass in the lesser pelvis of about 9 cm, rounded and hard, that partially bulged at the posterior fornix.

Analgesic management with intravenous paracetamol at a dose of 1000 mg twice daily, combined with positioning the patient in a semi-orthostatic manner—intended to minimize diaphragmatic compression from both the gravid uterus and extensive abdominal masses—led to modest improvement in symptoms. This approach alleviated pain from a score of 5 down to 1 on the Visual Analogue Scale without the need for supplemental oxygen or ventilatory assistance.

While hospitalized, the patient experienced a mild fever. A nasopharyngeal swab for SARS-CoV-2 using PCR yielded a negative result; however, urine cultures identified the presence of a Klebsiella pneumoniae infection. Laboratory investigations further revealed iron deficiency anemia, with hemoglobin measured at 9.9 g/L (reference range 12.0–16.6 g/L) and serum ferritin at 25 mcg/L (normal values 15–300 mcg/L). There was a modest elevation in C reactive protein at 1.39 mg/dL (reference 0–0.3 mg/dL), as well as increased neutrophil count: total white blood cells at 11.29×10³ /uL (reference 4–10×10³ /uL) and neutrophils at 9.27×10³ /uL (reference 2.8–5.5×10³ /uL), in keeping with typical pregnancy-related hematological changes.

Investigations:

Ultrasound revealed two sizable, unilocular cystic lesions characterized by smooth borders and peripheral blood flow. The superior lesion measured 18×12×7 cm and spanned from the left subphrenic area down to the fundus of the uterus. The inferior lesion, measuring 9.5×8×8 cm, appeared attached to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration (58×71 mm).

Serum tumor marker levels—including CA 19–9, CA 15–3, CEA, β2-microglobulin, and LDH—were all within normal ranges. However, the CA-125 level was raised at 41.8 U/mL (reference <35 U/mL), having increased from 36 U/mL two weeks prior. Thus, a mild upward trend was observed for this marker. As a result, the patient underwent non-contrast MRI, which identified lesions with thickened walls (1.4 cm), heterogeneous internal fluid on T1- and T2-weighted fat-saturated sequences, and areas of focal hyperintensity within both T1 and T2-weighted solid regions (refer to figure 1; only T2 fat-sat images provided). The interface of the lower mass was poorly defined and appeared contiguous with the posterior leiomyoma previously noted on ultrasound. MRI did not demonstrate any lymphadenopathy.

Differential diagnosis:

During the ultrasound assessment, the cystic lesions appeared similar to two large decidualised endometriomas; additionally, the more inferior cyst seemed attached to an intramural-subserosal leiomyoma located on the posterior uterine wall, measuring 58×71 mm.

Magnetic resonance imaging revealed two well-defined, unilocular adnexal cysts featuring enlarged vessels and heterogeneous fluid content (refer to figure 1). The caudal lesion was observed to be adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration, also measuring 58×71 mm.

Because the size of the gravid uterus and the large lesions obscured visualization, neither a connecting stalk nor a vascular supply to the upper mass could be demonstrated, making ovarian masses—particularly endometriomas—more likely, in light of the mild elevation in CA-125 observed during the second trimester (see discussion).

The differential diagnosis, according to ultrasound and MRI findings, included both decidualized endometriomas and cystic degenerating leiomyomas. The modest increase in CA-125 supported endometrioma as the diagnosis for the upper cystic mass, whereas the caudal lesion’s contiguity with uterine tissue indicated it likely originated from a leiomyoma. The patient’s dyspnoea was attributed to the large abdominal masses exerting mechanical pressure, as there was no indication of pulmonary or cardiac impairment.

Treatment:

Given the progression of symptoms—including abdominal pain associated with the masses and dyspnoea from restrictive lung disease secondary to mass size—as well as the need for definitive diagnosis, concerns about potential complications such as rupture, torsion, or infection, and the expected increase in uterine size with fetal development, the patient consented to and subsequently underwent a laparotomy via a longitudinal xiphoid-to-pubic incision at 21 weeks and 6 days of gestation.

First, we collected two samples of the free peritoneal fluid for cultural and cytological purposes. Then, we explored the abdominal cavity, verifying the presence of fetal cardiac activity.

Unexpectedly, the cranial mass was identified as a large, pedunculated, cystic leiomyoma of the uterus with an unusual taut-elastic consistency and rich vascular supply; it was initially twisted and exhibiting leakage, likely explaining the febrile episodes observed during hospitalization (refer to figure 2). Consequently, the surgical team opted to carefully resect the mass at its site of uterine attachment using a double clamp technique (refer to figure 2). After transecting the stalk, the exposed base was securely closed with a single absorbable suture. The caudal mass, which arose from the uterine torus, was not removed in order to reduce obstetric complications; this lesion presented as immobile, with a smooth surface and soft, cystic texture, resembling a large intramural-subserosal leiomyoma.

The surgical procedure took 2 hours and 25 minutes. Progesterone prophylaxis was administered at a dose of 100 mg intramuscularly beginning during the operation, and subsequently continued with daily 100 mg intramuscular injections until the patient was discharged. Following discharge, intramuscular hydroxyprogesterone caproate (341 mg, 1 vial intramuscularly every three days as indicated in the package insert) was given up to the planned delivery. For intraoperative antibiotic prophylaxis, cefazolin 2 g was delivered intravenously, followed by a postoperative regimen consisting of cefazolin 1 g intravenously three times daily in combination with clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily for the duration of the hospital admission. This regimen was based on the urine culture antibiogram, which indicated Klebsiella pneumoniae with marked susceptibility to both cephalosporins and clarithromycin.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following the operation, the patient's anemia worsened (Hb 7.5 g/L, HCT 24.3%) and iron deficiency remained, necessitating intravenous administration of ferric carboxymaltose (1 g) in addition to two successful blood transfusions, which raised her Hb to 10.0 g/L and HCT to 30.4%. She was discharged in stable condition 10 days postoperatively, with no complications, and began close obstetric monitoring every 3 weeks. Subsequent assessments demonstrated appropriate fetal growth (86th percentile per WHO fetal growth chart calculator, https://srhr.org/fetalgrowthcalculator/#/) and normal amniotic fluid index.

Both microbiological cultures and cytological assessment of the peritoneal fluid yielded negative results. Histopathological analysis identified benign uterine leiomyomas and regions of ischemic necrosis adjacent to the area of the twisted pedicle.

The presence of the caudal myoma would have significantly obstructed descent of the fetal head during the second stage of labor. Therefore, an elective cesarean section was carried out at 37 weeks of gestation using the existing surgical incision, effectively reducing the risk of uterine scar rupture and resulting in an optimal neonatal outcome.

The patient remains asymptomatic with respect to her myoma, and the remaining pelvic myoma has diminished modestly in size to 7.5 cm. She is successfully breastfeeding, but opts against immediate scheduling of further surgery, given the partial postpartum shrinkage of the residual leiomyomas, her lack of symptoms, and her completion of childbearing goals. The infant is developing normally.

Discussion:

Myomas can often be misdiagnosed as adnexal masses: the incidence during pregnancy is 0.3–10.1% and 1–6%, respectively .13

In this case, the first assessment revealed abdominal masses resembling two large decidualized endometriomas, with the exception of the lower solid part of the pelvic mass, which resembled a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration, measuring 58×71 mm as seen on both ultrasound and MRI. Given the lack of established protocols for managing pelvic masses during pregnancy, the multidisciplinary team determined that planned surgical intervention might lower the likelihood of preterm birth.

Throughout gestation, serum CA-125 levels typically exhibit two peaks: the first occurs in the initial trimester (median value of 23 U/mL, p<0.00001), while the second is observed in the third trimester (median value of 21 U/mL, p<0.001), both elevated in comparison to the second trimester (median of 14.0 U/mL). Conversely, in this patient, the measured value was 41.8 U/mL during the second trimester, exceeding the median for this period.

CA-125 primarily serves as a tumor marker for ovarian cancer, with the current standard threshold set at ≥35 U/mL. Its diagnostic performance—including area under the curve, positive predictive value, sensitivity, and specificity—is notably higher in women over 50 years of age. Nevertheless, elevated CA-125 levels can also be observed in several benign conditions, such as endometriosis and adenomyosis (levels typically exceeding 19 U/mL), pelvic inflammatory disease, menstruation, as well as during early and late stages of pregnancy (median values of 23 and 21 U/mL, respectively), risk of miscarriage, and in cases of myoma, where levels are usually below 19 U/mL.57

The primary factors influencing the clinical management included the identification of large, vascularized lesions exhibiting both solid and cystic features, which posed an immediate risk for complications such as rupture, torsion, or infection. Additional considerations were the patient’s deteriorating symptoms, including increasing pain and shortness of breath, the potential for further mass enlargement increasing the likelihood of hypoxia, necrosis, and degenerative changes, a mild elevation of tumor markers (CA-125 at 41.8 U/mL), and the potential for space limitations that could hinder normal uterine growth.

Given the potential neoplastic etiology and indeterminate source of the masses, the patient subsequently underwent a non-contrast MRI following the ultrasound evaluation to improve diagnostic precision. Based on current guidelines, gadolinium-enhanced MRI is contraindicated during pregnancy except in situations of compelling necessity, such as rheumatologic, inflammatory, or infiltrative dermatologic disorders, and concerns regarding stillbirth or neonatal mortality. Consequently, the multidisciplinary care team opted for a non-contrast MRI and avoided using MRI systems exceeding 1.5-Tesla, which are generally regarded as acceptably safe following the first trimester.

In this case, the multidisciplinary team—including surgeons, gynecologists, and obstetricians—chose to perform a longitudinal laparotomic surgical approach after securing specific written informed consent from the patient. During the operation, the upper lesion was found to possess a pedicle, which posed a notable threat for a common acute complication. This risk led to the decision to restrict the intervention to the upper myomectomy. Additionally, repeated and intermittent transvaginal ultrasound examinations were utilized intraoperatively to assess fetal well-being, complemented by prophylactic intraoperative progesterone administration.

According to a large multicentric study conducted by Zhao et al, it is mandatory to check and document the fetal heart rate before, during and after the surgery.

Typically, surgical intervention is postponed until after delivery, with preference given to conservative and expectant management. Nevertheless, a recent investigation by Sakinci et al at a tertiary care facility demonstrated that there were no statistically significant differences regarding preoperative and postoperative hemoglobin levels or requirements for blood transfusion (p >0.05) between women who underwent caesarean section (CS) with concurrent myomectomy and those without myomas who had only CS. However, both hospital stay and operative time were notably longer in patients receiving CS with myomectomy (p = 0.001 for both). The average myoma size measured 8.3 ± 4.1 cm, and a significant positive correlation was identified between myoma size and duration of hospitalisation (p = 0.01).

The same research study also showed a statistically significant and inverse correlation between the size of the myoma and the delivery week (p = 0.035).

Uterine leiomyomas exhibit a non-linear pattern of size fluctuation throughout pregnancy and the postpartum period. Specifically, leiomyoma volume increases by 6.90% per week, with a growth rate of 6.12% (p<0.001) from the first to the second trimester and 3.10%±0.39% between the second and third trimesters (p<0.05). Consequently, leiomyomas enlarge at a notably greater rate during the first trimester compared to later stages of gestation. Conversely, the dimensions of myomas significantly regress during the third trimester and by six weeks postpartum, demonstrating a mean reduction of 44.25%±36.05% (p<0.05).

These papers showed the importance of an accurate US examination of the size, location, number and distance of uterine myomas from the placenta before the surgical approach.

When uterine myomas are identified during pregnancy, management typically favors a conservative strategy over surgical intervention, owing to concerns over significant hemorrhage, miscarriage, preterm delivery, and potential uterine rupture. Nevertheless, myomectomy may be warranted in situations involving complicated lesions, such as torsion of a pedunculated fibroid, or when persistent symptoms are unresponsive to conservative measures. Surgical intervention should be kept as brief as possible to protect the pregnancy, and laparoscopic removal is feasible, especially employing the open Hasson entry technique during the first or early second trimester, for masses of smaller size and when the myoma’s location is extra-mural rather than intramural. This minimally invasive approach is associated with reduced maternal morbidity and improved prospects for vaginal delivery. Nonetheless, the case described here illustrates a typical tendency to select laparotomy rather than laparoscopy for larger, multiple, intramural, or critically positioned fibroids—decisions often driven by increased difficulty and concerns about outcomes. In this instance, the presence of a massive myoma attached to the posterior wall at the uterine isthmus could act as a barrier to vaginal delivery, potentially raising the likelihood of cesarean section.
