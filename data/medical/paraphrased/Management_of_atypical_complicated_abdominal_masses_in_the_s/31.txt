Title: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

Rosalba De Nola, Emma Bassi, Cataldo Paletta, Mario Vicino

Abstract:

A woman in her 40s, who had not given birth previously, presented with abdominal pain and shortness of breath during her second trimester. Ultrasound imaging detected two sizeable abdominal masses: the superior mass filled the entire left subphrenic region and extended toward the uterine fundus, while the inferior mass occupied the pouch of Douglas. Laparotomy was performed, but the lower mass was retained within the pelvis. The patient subsequently continued routine prenatal monitoring and later underwent planned caesarean section, achieving excellent maternal and neonatal outcomes. When large symptomatic masses arise during pregnancy, thorough evaluation—including bimanual examination and safe imaging methods such as ultrasonography or non-contrast MRI—is crucial. Surgical intervention should be reserved for cases with suspicion of malignancy or clinical complications, prioritizing pregnancy preservation and avoiding unnecessary procedures. The choice of delivery method should be guided by the number, dimensions, and anatomical positions of uterine masses.

Background:

Diagnosing and treating atypical or symptomatic abdominal masses in pregnancy poses considerable clinical difficulties. Available diagnostic tools are limited to physical examination, ultrasound (US) with colour Doppler, and non-contrast MRI. Preoperative ultrasound is especially useful in detecting symptomatic uterine leiomyomas on the anterior wall of the pregnant uterus, including both submucosal and pedunculated types. MRI is more effective for diagnosing multiple, large, intramural, or posterior leiomyomas, as it can accurately measure the distance between a myoma and the uterine cavity.

The utility of serum tumour markers in pregnant patients is still controversial. Specifically, CA-125 levels naturally increase during the first trimester and return to normal thereafter, making single measurements non-diagnostic; however, repeated assessments may reveal patterns helpful for distinguishing causes and for monitoring. Appropriate management involves thorough multidisciplinary evaluation to weigh obstetric risks against the uncommon and complex nature of these situations.

Case presentation:

A woman in her forties, G1P0A1, with obesity (body mass index 33), presented at 21 weeks gestational age during her second trimester with intense and sudden onset abdominal pain in the upper left quadrant and pelvis (both rated as 5 on the Visual Analogue Scale), accompanied by mild shortness of breath. Routine first and second trimester screenings indicated a low likelihood for aneuploidies, corroborated by non-invasive prenatal testing, and revealed normal fetal morphology.

She was initially assessed in the emergency department by a multidisciplinary team. Her oxygen saturation was within normal limits (median 97%). Examination of the chest showed a normal heart rate (median 74 bpm) and blood pressure (median 100/60 mm Hg). The electrocardiogram appeared normal. Subsequently, she was admitted to the Obstetrical and Gynecological Division. Her abdomen appeared more distended than appropriate for the gestational age, displaying a tender, taut, and elastic swelling filling the entire upper left quadrant, measuring 18 cm, but without signs suggestive of ascites.

During bimanual vaginal examination, a fixed mass approximately 9 cm in size, hard and round, was noted in the lesser pelvis, partially protruding into the posterior fornix.

Analgesia was administered with intravenous paracetamol (1000 mg twice daily), alongside a semi-orthostatic positioning intended to reduce diaphragmatic compression caused by the pregnant uterus and sizable abdominal masses. This approach yielded a slight improvement in her symptoms, with pain decreasing from 5 to 1 on the Visual Analogue Scale, without the need for additional oxygen or ventilatory assistance.

The patient subsequently developed a mild fever during hospitalization. SARS-CoV-2 PCR testing via nasopharyngeal swab was negative, whereas urine cultures identified Klebsiella pneumoniae infection. Laboratory results indicated iron deficiency anemia, with hemoglobin at 9.9 g/L (reference range 12.0–16.6 g/L) and serum ferritin 25 mcg/L (reference range 15–300 mcg/L); C reactive protein was slightly elevated at 1.39 mg/dL (reference range 0–0.3 mg/dL); and leukocyte counts were increased, with white blood cells at 11.29×10^3/uL (reference range 4–10×10^3/uL) and neutrophils at 9.27×10^3/uL (reference range 2.8–5.5×10^3/uL), consistent with physiological changes related to pregnancy.

Investigations:

Ultrasound revealed two sizable, unilocular cystic lesions displaying smooth borders and vascularization at their periphery. The superior lesion measured 18×12×7 cm and extended from the left subphrenic area to the uterine fundus. The inferior lesion, measuring 9.5×8×8 cm, appeared to be adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration (58×71 mm).

Assessment of tumor markers (CA 19–9, CA 15–3, CEA, β2-microglobulin, and LDH) returned values within standard ranges; however, CA-125 was increased (41.8 U/mL; normal <35 U/mL), rising from 36 U/mL recorded two weeks prior. This indicated a mild upward trend in serum CA-125 levels. The patient subsequently underwent an MRI without contrast, which revealed thickened walls (1.4 cm), inhomogeneous fluid as seen in T1 and T2 fat-saturated sequences, and focal areas of solid hyperintensity on T1-T2 weighted images (see figure 1; only T2 fat-saturated images are included). The demarcation of the lower mass was unclear and appeared contiguous with the posterior leiomyoma previously noted on ultrasound. No lymphadenopathy was detected on MRI.

Differential diagnosis:

During ultrasonographic assessment, the cystic lesions closely resembled two large decidualised endometriomas; the lower cyst was noted to be attached to an intramural-subserosal fibroid located on the posterior aspect of the uterus, measuring 58×71 mm.

Magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated two rounded, unilocular cysts in the adnexal region, both exhibiting hypertrophied vascularity and heterogeneous intracystic fluid (refer to figure 1). The inferior mass appeared connected to a posterior uterine intramural-subserosal myoma that displayed cystic degeneration, sized 58×71 mm.

The considerable bulk of the gravid uterus and the presence of substantial masses prevented identification of any connecting stalk or vascular supply to the superior cyst, leading to a diagnostic inclination towards ovarian masses—primarily endometriomas—given the mild CA-125 elevation during the second trimester (see discussion).

Sonographic and MRI characteristics suggested a differential including decidualised endometriomas and cystic degeneration of leiomyomas. Slightly increased CA-125 supported an endometrioma for the upper cyst, while direct anatomical continuity with uterine tissue favored a myomatous etiology for the caudal lesion. Dyspnoea was thought to result from the mechanical effect of the voluminous abdominal masses, with no evidence indicating pulmonary or cardiac compromise.

Treatment:

Given the deterioration in clinical status—manifested by abdominal discomfort from the masses and dyspnoea secondary to restrictive lung disease induced by the masses’ size—as well as the necessity for definitive diagnostic clarification, potential risk for complications (such as rupture, torsion, and infection), and anticipated uterine enlargement with ongoing fetal growth, the patient was scheduled for a laparotomy with a longitudinal incision from xiphoid to pubic symphysis at 21 weeks plus 6 days after obtaining informed consent.

Initially, two samples of free peritoneal fluid were obtained for microbiological culture and cytological evaluation. Subsequent inspection of the abdominal cavity confirmed fetal cardiac activity.

Unexpectedly, the mass located cranially was identified as an extensive pedunculated, atypical cystic leiomyoma of the uterus, demonstrating taut-elastic consistency and significant vascularization; it was found to be initially twisted and leaking, potentially explaining the patient’s fever during her admission (refer to figure 2). Accordingly, the surgical team elected to remove the mass from its uterine attachment by careful application of a double clamp (see figure 2). Upon transecting the stalk, the exposed base was meticulously secured with a single absorbable suture. The caudally situated mass, which arose from the uterine torus, was preserved in place to limit obstetric complications. This lesion appeared immobile, exhibiting a smooth exterior and soft texture consistent with a large cystic intramural-subserosal leiomyoma.

The procedure duration was 2 hours and 25 minutes. Of note, the patient was administered 100 mg progesterone intramuscularly as prophylaxis, initiated intraoperatively and continued at 100 mg intramuscularly daily until discharge. Subsequently, hydroxyprogesterone caproate was given intramuscularly (341 mg per vial, every 3 days, as outlined in the package insert) until planned delivery. Cefazolin 2 g was provided intravenously as intraoperative antibiotic prophylaxis, followed postoperatively by cefazolin 1 g intravenously three times daily plus clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily during hospitalization, aligned with urine culture antibiogram results (Klebsiella pneumoniae highly susceptible to cephalosporins and clarithromycin).

Outcome and follow-up:

Following the operation, the patient’s anaemia worsened (Hb 7.5 g/L, HCT 24.3%) with ongoing iron deficiency, necessitating administration of intravenous ferric carboxymaltose (1 g) and two successful blood transfusions (Hb increased to 10.0 g/L, HCT to 30.4%). She was released from hospital without adverse events 10 days post-surgery, then underwent strict obstetric monitoring every 3 weeks. Fetal growth was tracked (at the 86th percentile per WHO fetal growth chart calculator, available at https://srhr.org/fetalgrowthcalculator/#/), along with assessment of amniotic fluid index.

Analysis of peritoneal fluid showed negative results in both culture and cytology. Histopathological evaluation confirmed benign uterine leiomyomas, with regions of ischaemic necrosis adjacent to the twisted pedicle.

The location of the caudal myoma would likely have obstructed fetal head descent during labour’s expulsive stage. Therefore, an elective caesarean section was scheduled on the previous surgical scar once the fetus reached 37 weeks’ gestation, reducing the risk of uterine scar rupture and resulting in excellent neonatal outcomes.

At present, the patient reports no myoma-related symptoms, though the size of the remaining pelvic myoma has marginally decreased to 7.5 cm. She continues breastfeeding successfully but opts to defer further surgical intervention, citing partial postpartum regression of the remaining leiomyomas, absence of symptoms, and fulfillment of maternal goals. The child continues to develop appropriately.

Discussion:

Myomas can often be misdiagnosed as adnexal masses: the incidence during pregnancy is 0.3–10.1% and 1–6%, respectively .13

In this patient, initial assessment revealed abdominal masses that resembled two large, decidualised endometriomas, aside from the lower solid portion of the pelvic mass, which was suggestive of a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma with cystic degeneration measuring 58×71 mm as observed on both ultrasound and MRI. Given that there are no established guidelines for managing pelvic masses during pregnancy, the multidisciplinary team decided that performing elective surgery might reduce the likelihood of preterm birth.

Typically, serum CA-125 levels during pregnancy exhibit two peaks: one in the first trimester (median 23 U/mL, p<0.00001) and a second in the third trimester (median 21 U/mL, p<0.001), both higher compared to the second trimester (median 14.0 U/mL). However, in this particular case, the measured CA-125 was 41.8 U/mL, exceeding the typical median for the second trimester.

CA-125 is predominantly utilized as a tumor marker for ovarian cancer, with the current standard threshold set at ≥35 U/mL, especially in women over age 50, where it demonstrates greater area under the curve, positive predictive value, sensitivity, and specificity. Nonetheless, elevated CA-125 levels can also be associated with several benign conditions, including endometriosis and adenomyosis (>19 U/mL), pelvic inflammatory disease, menstruation, early and late pregnancy (median values 23 U/mL and 21 U/mL, respectively), miscarriage risk, as well as myoma (cut-off <19 U/mL).57

The primary factors influencing the management plan included the identification of large, highly vascularized masses featuring both solid and cystic components, which carried a significant risk for complications such as rupture, torsion, or infection; the patient’s deteriorating clinical status with increasing pain and dyspnoea; the likelihood of the masses enlarging further, which could increase the potential for hypoxia, necrosis, and degeneration; mildly elevated tumor markers (CA-125=41.8 U/mL); and limitations in intra-abdominal space that could impede continued normal uterine expansion.

Given the suspicion for neoplastic etiology and unclear nature of the masses, the patient received a non-contrast MRI following the ultrasonographic evaluation to enhance diagnostic precision. Consistent with current guidelines, gadolinium-based contrast MRI is contraindicated in pregnancy except when absolutely essential—for example, in rheumatological, inflammatory, or infiltrative dermatological disorders, or when there is a risk of fetal demise or neonatal death. As a result, the multidisciplinary team opted for a non-contrast MRI, taking care to avoid the use of MRI machines exceeding 1.5 Tesla, which is regarded as acceptably safe following the first trimester.

In this case, a multidisciplinary group including surgeons, gynecologists, and obstetricians elected to carry out a longitudinal laparotomic surgical intervention after obtaining specific written informed consent from the patient. Intraoperatively, the upper lesion was found to possess a pedicle, which posed a considerable risk for a classic, acute complication. As a result, the team decided to restrict the procedure to an upper myomectomy. Throughout the operation, fetal viability was assessed using repeated and periodic transvaginal ultrasound examinations, and intraoperative progesterone was administered prophylactically.

A substantial multicenter study by Zhao et al underscores the necessity of evaluating and recording fetal heart rate prior to, during, and after surgical intervention.

Typically, surgery is postponed until postpartum, with a preference for medical and expectant management. Nevertheless, Sakinci et al recently demonstrated in a tertiary care setting that no statistically significant differences were found in preoperative and postoperative hemoglobin values or blood transfusion requirements (p >0.05) between women who underwent cesarean section (CS) with myomectomy and those without myoma undergoing CS alone. Conversely, hospitalization duration and operative time were notably longer in the group undergoing CS with myomectomy (p =0.001 and p =0.001, respectively). The average myoma diameter measured 8.3 ± 4.1 cm, with a significant positive association between myoma size and length of hospital stay (p =0.01).

The same study reported a statistically significant inverse association between myoma size and gestational age at delivery (p = 0.035).

Uterine leiomyomas demonstrate a non-linear pattern of size changes throughout pregnancy and the postpartum period. Specifically, leiomyoma size expands by 6.90% per week, with an increase of 6.12% (p < 0.001) between the first and second trimesters and an increase of 3.10% ± 0.39% between the second and third trimesters (p < 0.05). Thus, growth rates of uterine leiomyomas are notably higher in the first trimester compared with subsequent gestational periods. Conversely, in the third trimester and up to 6 weeks postpartum, myoma size showed a significant reduction (mean decrease 44.25% ± 36.05%, p < 0.05).

These articles highlight the necessity for meticulous ultrasonographic assessment of uterine myomas’ size, number, location, and their proximity to the placenta prior to any surgical intervention.

When uterine myomas are detected during pregnancy, conservative management is generally preferred over surgical intervention due to potential risks such as major hemorrhage, pregnancy loss, preterm labor, and uterine rupture. Nonetheless, myomectomy may be indicated in cases of complicated masses—for instance, if a pedunculated myoma undergoes torsion or if persistent symptoms do not resolve with conservative measures. The operative procedure should be as brief as feasible to safeguard the pregnancy, and it can be performed laparoscopically, ideally utilizing the open Hasson technique, particularly for earlier gestational ages (first and early second trimester), smaller myomas, and non-intramural locations. This method tends to offer superior outcomes with regard to maternal morbidity and likelihood of vaginal delivery. Nevertheless, the current case exemplifies a typical scenario where selection bias favors laparotomy over laparoscopy for larger, multiple, intramural, or critically positioned myomas, which may be associated with increased rates of cesarean section. This is due to substantial myomas attached to the posterior wall of the isthmus, posing significant obstruction to vaginal delivery.
