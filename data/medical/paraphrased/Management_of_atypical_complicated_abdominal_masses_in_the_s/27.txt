Title: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

Rosalba De Nola, Emma Bassi, Cataldo Paletta, Mario Vicino

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her 40s with no history of childbirth presented during her second trimester with abdominal pain and shortness of breath. Ultrasound imaging identified two sizable masses in her abdomen: the upper mass filled the entire left subphrenic region and extended toward the uterine fundus, while the lower mass occupied the pouch of Douglas. She underwent a laparotomy in which the lower pelvic lesion was left undisturbed. The patient received regular obstetric monitoring and later had a planned caesarean section, both mother and infant having an optimal outcome. When large, symptomatic masses arise in pregnancy, thorough assessment—including bimanual examination and safe imaging techniques such as ultrasound and non-contrast MRI—is critical. Surgery should only be pursued when there is suspicion for malignancy or clinical complications, with an emphasis on preserving the pregnancy and minimizing unnecessary intervention. The delivery method should be based on the number, dimensions, and anatomical position of uterine masses.

Background:

The identification and treatment of unusual or symptomatic abdominal masses during gestation pose considerable clinical difficulties. Available diagnostic methods include physical assessment, ultrasound (US) with color Doppler, and non-contrast MRI. Preoperative US is particularly helpful for detecting symptomatic uterine leiomyomas situated on the anterior uterine wall in pregnant patients, regardless of whether they are submucosal or pedunculated. MRI delivers enhanced diagnostic precision for multiple, sizable, intramural, or posterior leiomyomas, allowing for accurate evaluation of the separation between the myoma and the uterine cavity.

The utility of serum tumor markers as a diagnostic tool during pregnancy remains controversial. CA-125, in particular, shows a physiological increase during the first trimester, then returns to baseline levels. As such, single measurements lack diagnostic significance, while repeated testing may offer valuable insights for distinguishing diagnoses and monitoring. Proper management calls for comprehensive multidisciplinary risk analysis to ensure a balance between obstetric considerations and the uncommon and complex nature of these cases.

Case presentation:

An obese woman in her forties (body mass index: 33), with a gravidity of 1, parity of 0, and one previous abortion (G1P0A1), presented at 21 weeks of gestation during her second trimester with intense, sudden abdominal pain in the upper left quadrant and pelvic region (Visual Analogue Scale=5 for both areas), accompanied by mild shortness of breath. Routine screenings from both the first and second trimesters indicated a low risk for aneuploidies, as confirmed by non-invasive prenatal testing, and demonstrated normal fetal morphology.

She was initially evaluated in the emergency department by a multidisciplinary team. Her oxygen saturation was within normal limits (median 97%), physical examination revealed a regular heart rate (median 74 bpm), and blood pressure (median 100/60 mm Hg). An ECG showed no abnormalities. The patient was subsequently transferred to the Obstetrics and Gynecology Division. Abdominal assessment revealed enlargement beyond expected for the current gestational age, with a palpable, tender, taut-elastic mass in the entire upper left quadrant, measuring 18 cm, absent ascites on clinical examination.

On bimanual vaginal assessment, a fixed, approximately 9 cm mass was detected in the lesser pelvis, which was firm, round, and partially protruded into the posterior fornix.

Analgesic management with intravenous paracetamol 1000 mg twice daily and a semi-orthostatic positioning—intended to minimize diaphragmatic pressure from the uterus and large abdominal masses—provided slight symptom relief, with pain scores dropping from 5 to 1 on the Visual Analogue Scale. No supplemental oxygen or respiratory support was required.

While hospitalized, she experienced a mild fever. SARS-CoV-2 PCR testing by nasopharyngeal swab yielded a negative result, while a urinary tract infection due to Klebsiella pneumoniae was identified. Laboratory findings revealed iron deficiency anaemia, evidenced by haemoglobin at 9.9 g/L (normal range 12.0–16.6 g/L) and serum ferritin at 25 mcg/L (normal range 15–300 mcg/L). There was a modest elevation in C-reactive protein (CRP: 1.39 mg/dL; normal range 0–0.3 mg/dL) and an increase in neutrophil count (white blood cells 11.29×10^3 /uL [normal 4–10×10^3 /uL]; neutrophils 9.27×10^3 /uL [normal 2.8–5.5×10^3 /uL]), in keeping with physiological changes observed during pregnancy.

Investigations:

Ultrasound examination revealed two sizeable, unilocular cystic lesions with smooth borders and peripheral vascularity. The superior mass measured 18×12×7 cm and extended from the left subphrenic space down to the uterine fundus. The inferior mass, measuring 9.5×8×8 cm, appeared to be attached to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration (58×71 mm).

Laboratory analysis showed tumour markers including CA 19–9, CA 15–3, CEA, β2-microglobulin, and LDH all within reference ranges, while CA-125 was raised at 41.8 U/mL (normal <35 U/mL), representing an upward trend from 36 U/mL recorded two weeks previously. Consequently, a non-contrast MRI was performed, revealing cyst walls with a thickness of 1.4 cm, heterogenous fluid content on T1 and T2 fat-saturated sequences, and localized hyperintense solid areas on both T1 and T2-weighted images (refer to figure 1; only T2 fat-sat images displayed). The caudal lesion's separation was indistinct and it appeared contiguous with the posterior leiomyoma previously seen on ultrasound. No lymphadenopathy was observed on MRI.

Differential diagnosis:

During ultrasonographic assessment, the cystic lesions resembled two large decidualized endometriomas; the more inferior cyst was seen contiguous with a posterior uterine wall intramural-subserosal leiomyoma measuring 58×71 mm.

Magnetic resonance imaging identified two rounded, unilocular adnexal cystic lesions featuring hypertrophic vascularity and heterogeneous internal fluid (refer to figure 1). The caudal cystic mass appeared attached to a uterine posterior wall intramural-subserosal myoma with cystic degeneration, also measuring 58×71 mm.

Given both the sizeable gravid uterus and the substantial masses, imaging could not delineate any connecting stalk or tumor-supplying vessel for the superior lesion. This appearance suggested an ovarian mass, particularly an endometrioma, considering the mildly increased CA-125 during the second trimester of pregnancy (see discussion).

Interpretation of sonographic and MRI characteristics led to differential considerations of decidualized endometriomas versus cystic degenerated leiomyomas. The mildly raised CA-125 supported an endometriotic nature for the superior cyst, whereas the attachment to myometrial tissue favored a myomatous origin for the caudal mass. The patient's dyspnoea was considered the result of mechanical pressure from the large intra-abdominal lesions, as cardiopulmonary pathology was not evident.

Treatment:

Owing to the progression of the patient’s symptoms—including abdominal discomfort associated with the mass and dyspnoea secondary to restrictive lung disease caused by the increasing size of the abdominal masses—as well as the need for definitive diagnosis, potential for complications such as rupture, torsion, and infection, and anticipation of uterine enlargement in tandem with fetal growth, laparotomy was performed at 21 weeks and 6 days with a longitudinal incision from the xiphoid process to the pubic symphysis, following informed consent.

Initially, two specimens of free peritoneal fluid were obtained for culture and cytological analysis. Subsequently, the abdominal cavity was inspected and ongoing fetal cardiac activity was confirmed.

Unexpectedly, the cranial lesion turned out to be a markedly enlarged, pedunculated, atypical cystic and taut-elastic uterine leiomyoma, displaying significant vascularity, initial torsion, and evidence of leakage. This finding could explain the fever noted during the hospital stay (refer to figure 2). As a result, the surgical team elected to remove the mass from its uterine attachment with the aid of a double clamp (see figure 2). Following division of the stalk, the exposed base was meticulously sutured with a single absorbable stitch. The caudal mass, which originated from the uterine torus, was left undisturbed in an effort to reduce obstetric risk; this lesion was fixed, had a smooth contour, and soft consistency, resembling a large cystic intramural-subserosal leiomyoma.

The procedure lasted 2 hours and 25 minutes. Of note, progesterone prophylaxis was initiated intraoperatively at a dose of 100 mg intramuscularly and continued daily until discharge. Subsequently, the patient was administered intramuscular hydroxyprogesterone caproate (341 mg, 1 ampoule every 3 days, in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions) until scheduled delivery. Antibiotic prophylaxis with intravenous cefazolin 2 g was given intraoperatively, followed by postoperative cefazolin 1 g intravenously three times daily plus clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily throughout the hospitalization, as guided by the urine culture antibiogram (Klebsiella pneumoniae highly susceptible to cephalosporins and clarithromycin).

Outcome and follow-up:

Following the operation, the patient experienced worsening anemia (Hb 7.5 g/L, HCT 24.3%) with ongoing iron deficiency, necessitating intravenous administration of ferric carboxymaltose (1 g) and two blood transfusions, which were effective (Hb increased to 10.0 g/L, HCT to 30.4%). She was discharged without complications 10 days post-surgery and began intensive obstetric monitoring every 3 weeks, demonstrating fetal growth (86th percentile per the WHO fetal growth chart calculator, available online at https://srhr.org/fetalgrowthcalculator/#/) and stable amniotic fluid index.

Testing of peritoneal fluid yielded negative results for both culture and cytology. Pathology revealed benign uterine leiomyomas and zones of ischemic necrosis adjacent to the twisted pedicle.

The lower uterine myoma would have severely impeded descent of the fetal head during the labor expulsive phase. Therefore, an elective cesarean section was undertaken on the pre-existing surgical incision at 37 weeks gestation, thereby reducing the risk of uterine scar rupture and resulting in excellent neonatal outcomes.

At present, the patient reports no myoma-related symptoms, with the residual pelvic myoma decreasing slightly in size to 7.5 cm. She is breastfeeding successfully, and, owing to partial postpartum regression of the remaining leiomyomas, absence of symptoms, and her satisfaction with motherhood, she prefers not to schedule further surgical intervention at this time. The infant is developing well.

Discussion:

Myomas can often be misdiagnosed as adnexal masses: the incidence during pregnancy is 0.3–10.1% and 1–6%, respectively .13

In this case, the initial assessment identified abdominal masses that resembled two large decidualized endometriomas. However, the lower solid portion of the pelvic mass was suggestive of a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration measuring 58×71 mm, as seen on both ultrasonography and MRI. Given the lack of established protocols for managing pelvic masses during pregnancy, the multidisciplinary team judged that planned surgery might lower the risk of preterm birth.

During pregnancy, serum CA-125 levels typically show two peaks: one in the first trimester (median 23 U/mL, p<0.00001) and another in the third trimester (median 21 U/mL, p<0.001), with these values being higher compared to those measured in the second trimester (median 14.0 U/mL). Conversely, this patient’s CA-125 level was 41.8 U/mL, exceeding the second trimester median.

CA-125 is predominantly utilized as a tumor marker for ovarian cancer, with a standard diagnostic threshold of ≥35 U/mL, particularly providing greater diagnostic accuracy, positive predictive value, sensitivity, and specificity in women over 50. Nevertheless, elevated CA-125 levels may also occur in a number of benign conditions, including endometriosis and adenomyosis (>19 U/mL), pelvic inflammatory disease, menstruation, early and late pregnancy (median 23 and 21 U/mL, respectively), risk of miscarriage, as well as myomas (cut-off <19 U/mL).57

The main factors guiding clinical management included the observation of large, vascular masses displaying both solid and cystic components, which posed a current risk for complications such as rupture, torsion, or infection; the patient’s worsening clinical symptoms, specifically increased pain and dyspnoea; potential for the masses to enlarge, increasing the likelihood of hypoxia, necrosis, and degeneration; mild elevation of oncological markers (CA-125=41.8 U/mL); and limited space available for subsequent normal uterine expansion.

Given the suspicion of neoplastic origin and the indeterminate nature of the masses, the patient underwent a non-contrast MRI following the ultrasound in order to enhance diagnostic precision. Based on current guidelines, gadolinium-enhanced MRI is contraindicated during pregnancy except in cases of strict necessity related to rheumatological, inflammatory, or infiltrative skin disorders, or risk of stillbirth or neonatal death. Thus, the multidisciplinary team opted for a non-contrast MRI, and avoided scanning with a strength greater than 1.5-T, which is considered reasonably safe after the first trimester.

In this case, the multidisciplinary team—including surgeons, gynecologists, and obstetricians—chose to perform a longitudinal laparotomic surgery after securing specific written informed consent from the patient. During the surgical evaluation, the uppermost lesion demonstrated a pedicle, which posed a considerable risk for an acute, classic complication. This finding supported the decision to restrict the intervention to only an upper myomectomy. In addition, repeated and interval-based transvaginal ultrasounds were utilized to assess fetal viability throughout the operation, accompanied by prophylactic intraoperative progesterone administration.

A comprehensive multicenter investigation by Zhao et al. highlights that monitoring and documentation of fetal heart rate before, during, and after the surgical procedure are essential.

Typically, surgical intervention is postponed until postpartum, with preference given to expectant and medical management. Nevertheless, Sakinci et al. recently conducted a study at a tertiary facility, finding no significant differences between women who had caesarean section (CS) combined with myomectomy and those without myomas undergoing only CS in terms of preoperative or postoperative hemoglobin measurements, as well as rates of blood transfusion (p >0.05). However, both hospital stay and operation time were markedly longer among the group subjected to CS with myomectomy (p =0.001 and p =0.001, respectively). The average myoma dimension measured 8.3 ± 4.1 cm, with a statistically significant positive relationship observed between myoma size and length of hospitalization (p =0.01).

The same study identified a statistically significant, inverse association between myoma size and the week of delivery (p = 0.035).

Uterine fibroids exhibit a non-linear pattern of size variation throughout pregnancy and the postpartum period. Specifically, leiomyoma dimensions increase by 6.90% per week, with a 6.12% growth rate (p < 0.001) observed between the first and second trimesters, and a 3.10% ± 0.39% increase between the second and third trimesters (p < 0.05). Thus, the rate of leiomyoma enlargement is notably greater in the first trimester compared to later stages of gestation. Conversely, a marked reduction in myoma size occurs during the third trimester and up to six weeks postpartum, averaging 44.25% ± 36.05% (p < 0.05).

These studies emphasize the need for precise ultrasonographic assessment of the dimensions, position, quantity, and proximity of uterine myomas to the placenta prior to undertaking surgical intervention.

When uterine myomas are detected in pregnancy, management is generally conservative rather than surgical, due to the potential risks such as significant hemorrhage, miscarriage, preterm birth, and uterine rupture. Nonetheless, myomectomy may be indicated in cases of complicated lesions—such as torsion of a pedunculated myoma—or when symptoms persist and do not respond to conservative management. The surgical procedure should be as brief as possible to maintain the pregnancy and can be performed laparoscopically, ideally utilizing an open Hasson technique when dealing with early gestational ages (first and early second trimesters), smaller fibroid size, and in selected locations (excluding intramural myomas). This strategy is associated with improved maternal outcomes and higher rates of vaginal delivery. The present case, however, illustrates a common selection bias favoring laparotomy over laparoscopy in instances involving larger, multiple, intramural, or high-risk locations, conditions that are frequently linked to increased cesarean section rates, particularly when a sizable myoma adherent to the posterior isthmic uterine wall could hinder vaginal delivery.
