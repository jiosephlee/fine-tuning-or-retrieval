Title: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

Rosalba De Nola, Emma Bassi, Cataldo Paletta, Mario Vicino

Abstract:

Summary: A nulliparous woman in her 40s reported abdominal discomfort and shortness of breath in the second trimester. Ultrasound imaging identified two sizable masses: the superior mass filled the entire left subphrenic region and extended toward the uterine fundus, while the inferior mass was situated within the pouch of Douglas. The patient underwent laparotomy, with the lower pelvic mass remaining untouched. She continued with routine obstetric monitoring and ultimately underwent an elective caesarean section, which resulted in a favorable outcome for both mother and infant. For pregnant patients with substantial symptomatic masses, thorough assessment involving bimanual palpation and safe imaging techniques—such as ultrasonography and non-contrast MRI—is vital. Surgical intervention should be reserved for cases with malignancy risk or when clinical complications occur, with efforts focused on maintaining pregnancy and avoiding unnecessary procedures. The choice of delivery should be determined based on the masses’ size, number, and precise location.

Background:

The identification and treatment of unusual or symptomatic abdominal masses in pregnancy pose notable clinical difficulties. Available diagnostic tools are restricted to physical examination, ultrasound (US) with color Doppler, and non-contrast magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Preoperative US is especially helpful for detecting symptomatic uterine leiomyomas situated on the anterior aspect of the pregnant uterus, irrespective of their being submucosal or pedunculated. In contrast, MRI provides enhanced accuracy for detecting numerous, sizable, intramural, or posterior leiomyomas, as it can precisely delineate the separation between the fibroid and the endometrial cavity.

The utility of serum tumor markers in diagnosis during pregnancy remains controversial. CA-125, in particular, is known to rise physiologically during the initial trimester, before returning to baseline levels thereafter. Consequently, single measurements of CA-125 are not diagnostic, although sequential assessments may reveal helpful trends for differential diagnosis and surveillance. Appropriate management necessitates thorough multidisciplinary risk evaluation to judiciously balance obstetric concerns against the infrequent and complex nature of such presentations.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 40s, obese with a body mass index of 33 and obstetric history of G1P0A1, presented during her second trimester (21 weeks gestation) reporting severe, acute abdominal pain localized to the upper left quadrant and pelvis (Visual Analogue Scale score of 5 for each region), as well as mild dyspnea. Routine screening in both the first and second trimesters indicated low risk for aneuploidies, verified by non-invasive prenatal testing, and fetal morphology was normal.

Upon initial presentation, she was evaluated in the emergency department through a multidisciplinary approach. Her oxygen saturation was within normal limits (median value 97%), and thoracic assessment revealed normal heart rate (median 74 bpm) and blood pressure (median 100/60 mmHg). ECG findings were unremarkable. Subsequently, she was moved to the Obstetrical and Gynecological Division. Examination revealed that her abdomen appeared more distended than anticipated for her gestational age, with a tender, taut-elastic swelling in the upper left quadrant measuring 18 cm and lacking signs suggestive of ascites.

Bimanual pelvic examination identified a fixed, round, hard mass approximately 9 cm in size within the lesser pelvis, protruding partially into the posterior fornix.

Analgesic treatment (paracetamol 1000 mg IV, twice daily) and maintaining a semi-orthostatic position, thought to decrease diaphragmatic pressure from the uterus and sizable abdominal masses, led to moderate symptom relief; no supplemental oxygen or respiratory support was required, and pain decreased from 5 to 1 on the Visual Analogue Scale.

During hospitalization, the patient developed a mild fever. PCR testing for SARS-CoV-2 by nasopharyngeal swab was negative, but urine cultures identified Klebsiella pneumoniae infection. Laboratory studies demonstrated iron deficiency anemia (haemoglobin 9.9 g/L [normal range 12.0–16.6 g/L], serum ferritin 25 mcg/L [normal range 15–300 mcg/L]), a mild elevation of C-reactive protein at 1.39 mg/dL [normal range 0–0.3 mg/dL], and increased neutrophil count (total white blood cells 11.29×10³/uL [normal range 4–10×10³/uL], neutrophils 9.27×10³/uL [normal range 2.8–5.5×10³/uL]), in line with typical physiological changes in pregnancy.

Investigations:

Ultrasound revealed two sizable, unilocular cystic lesions characterized by smooth borders and peripheral blood flow. The superior mass, measuring 18×12×7 cm, spanned from the left subphrenic area down to the uterine fundus. The inferior mass was 9.5×8×8 cm and appeared to be attached to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration (58×71 mm).

Tumour markers including CA 19–9, CA 15–3, CEA, β2-microglobulin, and LDH were found within the reference range. In contrast, CA-125 was elevated at 41.8 U/mL (reference <35 U/mL), with a progressive increase from 36 U/mL two weeks previously, indicating a modest upward trend in this marker’s serum levels. Subsequently, an MRI without contrast was performed, demonstrating thick walls (1.4 cm), heterogeneous fluid on T1 and T2 fat-sat sequences, and areas of solid tissue appearing hyperintense in both T1 and T2 weighted images (refer to figure 1; only T2 fat-sat images displayed). The caudal mass's delineation was indistinct and it appeared to merge with the posterior leiomyoma previously noted on ultrasound. MRI showed no evidence of lymphadenopathy.

Differential diagnosis:

During ultrasonography, the cystic lesions resembled two large decidualised endometriomas; additionally, the inferior cyst appeared to be attached to an intramural-subserosal leiomyoma located on the posterior uterine wall, measuring 58×71 mm.

Magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated two spherical, unilocular adnexal cysts associated with enlarged vessels and heterogeneous internal fluid (refer to figure 1). The inferior lesion appeared contiguous with a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma, which exhibited cystic degeneration and measured 58×71 mm.

Owing to the considerable size of the gravid uterus and the bulky masses, neither a connecting pedicle nor a feeder vessel could be discerned for the superior mass. This inclined the diagnostic consideration toward ovarian pathology, particularly endometriomas, supported by mild elevation of CA-125 observed during the second trimester (see discussion).

Considering the ultrasonographic and MRI findings, the primary differentials included decidualised endometriomas and leiomyomas with cystic degeneration. The moderate increase in CA-125 was suggestive of an endometrioma for the superior cyst, whereas the anatomical continuity with the uterus implied the caudal lesion originated from a myoma. The patient’s dyspnoea was thought to result from direct pressure exerted by the large intra-abdominal masses, in the absence of any pulmonary or cardiac impairment.

Treatment:

Given the deterioration in the patient's clinical status—characterized by abdominal pain attributable to the mass and dyspnoea related to restrictive lung disease from the expanding abdominal lesions—together with the need for a definitive diagnosis, the elevated risks of adverse events (such as rupture, torsion, or infection), and anticipated uterine enlargement as the pregnancy advanced, surgical intervention was undertaken. A laparotomy was performed with a longitudinal incision extending from the xiphoid process to the pubis at 21 weeks and 6 days gestation, following the patient’s informed consent.

Initially, two samples of free peritoneal fluid were obtained for both cultural and cytological assessments. Subsequently, an intra-abdominal exploration was conducted, during which fetal cardiac activity was confirmed.

Unexpectedly, the superior mass was identified as a massive, pedunculated, atypical cystic, and taut-elastic uterine leiomyoma. This tumor displayed prominent vascularity and appeared to be partially twisted and oozing, potentially accounting for the fever observed during the patient’s hospitalization (refer to figure 2). Consequently, the surgical team opted for a cautious excision of the mass from its site of uterine attachment by employing a double clamp technique (as shown in figure 2). Following transection of the stalk, the exposed base was meticulously secured using a single absorbable suture. The inferior mass, originating from the uterine torus, was deliberately left in place to minimize obstetric complications. This lesion demonstrated a fixed position, smooth external surface, and a soft consistency, resembling a sizable cystic intramural-subserosal leiomyoma.

The operation lasted 2 hours and 25 minutes. The patient received prophylactic progesterone at a dose of 100 mg intramuscularly during the surgery, which was continued daily via the same route until hospital discharge. Subsequent management included administration of hydroxyprogesterone caproate (341 mg, 1 ampoule intramuscularly every 3 days per the prescribing information) until elective delivery. Intraoperative antibiotic prophylaxis consisted of 2 g intravenous cefazolin, followed in the postoperative period by cefazolin 1 g intravenously three times daily plus clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily. This regimen was selected in accordance with the urine culture antibiogram, which demonstrated Klebsiella pneumoniae highly susceptible to both cephalosporins and clarithromycin.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following the operation, the patient's anaemia worsened (Hb 7.5 g/L, HCT 24.3%) and iron deficiency persisted, necessitating intravenous iron therapy with ferric carboxymaltose (1 g) and two successful blood transfusions (resulting in Hb 10.0 g/L, HCT 30.4%). Ten days post-surgery, the patient was discharged without any complications and commenced an obstetric follow-up every three weeks. During these visits, fetal growth was observed (86th percentile as per the WHO fetal growth chart calculator, available at https://srhr.org/fetalgrowthcalculator/#/) along with assessment of the amniotic fluid index.

Examination of peritoneal fluid found no evidence of infection or malignancy in both culture and cytology. Pathologic evaluation identified benign uterine leiomyomas and regions of ischaemic necrosis adjacent to the twisted pedicle.

The caudal myoma would have posed a significant hindrance to fetal head passage during labour's expulsive phase. Consequently, an elective caesarean section was carried out through the previous surgical incision at 37 weeks gestation, reducing the likelihood of uterine scar rupture, with a favorable neonatal outcome.

Currently, the patient is asymptomatic regarding myoma, and the remaining pelvic myoma has diminished slightly in size to 7.5 cm. She is breastfeeding successfully and has chosen to defer any further surgical intervention, citing partial postpartum regression of the leiomyomas, absence of symptoms, and satisfaction with motherhood. The child continues to thrive.

Discussion:

Myomas can often be misdiagnosed as adnexal masses: the incidence during pregnancy is 0.3–10.1% and 1–6%, respectively .13

In this case, the initial assessment identified abdominal masses that resembled two large decidualised endometriomas, except for a lower solid part of the pelvic mass, which appeared consistent with a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma undergoing cystic degeneration, measuring 58×71 mm as visualized on both ultrasound and MRI. Due to the lack of established management protocols for pelvic masses during pregnancy, a multidisciplinary team determined that elective surgery might lower the likelihood of preterm birth.

Typically, maternal serum CA-125 exhibits two surges during gestation: one during the first trimester (median 23 U/mL, p<0.00001) and another in the third trimester (median 21 U/mL, p<0.001) in comparison to levels in the second trimester (median 14.0 U/mL). However, in this instance, the CA-125 value was measured at 41.8 U/mL, surpassing the median level observed during the second trimester.

CA-125 is predominantly utilized as a tumor marker for ovarian malignancy (with a conventional threshold of ≥35 U/mL), particularly in women older than 50 years, in whom higher diagnostic accuracy, positive predictive value, sensitivity, and specificity have been documented. Nevertheless, elevated CA-125 can also be seen in various benign conditions, including endometriosis and adenomyosis (>19 U/mL), pelvic inflammatory disease, menstruation, early and near-term pregnancy (medians 23 and 21 U/mL, respectively), increased miscarriage risk, and myoma (with a threshold <19 U/mL).57

The main factors influencing the clinical approach included the detection of large, highly vascular masses displaying both solid and cystic areas, presenting an imminent risk of complications such as rupture, torsion, or infection; the patient’s worsening symptoms of pain and shortness of breath; the potential for continued mass enlargement increasing the likelihood of hypoxia, tissue necrosis, and degeneration; mild elevation in tumor markers (CA-125 at 41.8 U/mL); and the restricted intra-abdominal space available to support further normal uterine expansion.

Given the uncertain etiology and possible neoplastic nature of the masses, the patient underwent a non-contrast MRI following the ultrasound to improve diagnostic accuracy. Based on current guidelines, gadolinium-enhanced MRI is contraindicated during pregnancy except in cases of absolute necessity related to rheumatologic, inflammatory, or infiltrative skin disorders, or heightened risk of stillbirth or neonatal death. Consequently, the multidisciplinary team proceeded with a non-contrast MRI, refraining from using field strengths greater than 1.5-T, which is generally considered acceptably safe following the first trimester.

In this case, the multidisciplinary team, including surgeons, gynecologists, and obstetricians, decided to proceed with longitudinal laparotomic surgical intervention after obtaining the patient's written informed consent. During surgery, the upper mass was found to have a pedicle, representing a considerable risk for acute complications, which justified restricting the intervention to the upper myomectomy. Fetal viability was consistently assessed intraoperatively using serial and intermittent transvaginal ultrasound, with additional prophylactic intraoperative administration of progesterone.

As reported by Zhao et al. in a large multicenter study, it is imperative to verify and record fetal heart rate prior to, during, and following surgical procedures.

Generally, surgical treatment is deferred until after delivery, favoring medical or expectant management. Nonetheless, a recent study by Sakinci et al. performed in a tertiary center demonstrated no statistically significant differences in preoperative or postoperative hemoglobin values, nor in blood transfusion rates (p > 0.05), between women who underwent cesarean section (CS) with concurrent myomectomy and those who had CS alone without myoma. However, both hospitalization length and operative time were notably increased in the CS myomectomy group (p = 0.001 for both comparisons). The average myoma size was 8.3 ± 4.1 cm, with a significant positive correlation between myoma size and duration of hospital stay (p = 0.01).

The same study found a statistically significant inverse association between myoma size and gestational age at delivery (p = 0.035).

Uterine leiomyomas exhibit a non-linear progression in size throughout pregnancy and the postpartum period. Specifically, their dimensions increase by 6.90% weekly: 6.12% (p<0.001) from the first to the second trimester and 3.10%±0.39% from the second to the third trimester (p<0.05). Consequently, leiomyomas tend to enlarge more rapidly during the first trimester compared to subsequent stages of pregnancy. Conversely, the size of myomas decreases substantially in the third trimester and up to 6 weeks post-delivery, with a mean reduction of 44.25%±36.05% (p<0.05).

These studies highlight the necessity for thorough ultrasound assessment regarding the size, position, quantity, and proximity of uterine myomas to the placenta prior to surgical intervention.

For uterine myomas detected during gestation, management typically favors conservative treatment rather than surgery, considering the risks of severe hemorrhage, pregnancy loss, preterm labor, and uterine rupture. Nevertheless, myomectomy should be contemplated for complicated lesions such as torsion of a pedunculated myoma or ongoing symptoms unresponsive to conservative measures. The operation should be kept brief to support pregnancy preservation, and may be performed via laparoscopy, ideally using the open Hasson technique when gestational age is lower (first or early second trimester), the lesion volume is smaller, and the myoma is not intramural. This method yields improved outcomes regarding maternal morbidity and likelihood of vaginal delivery. Despite this, the current case illustrates a frequent bias toward choosing laparotomy over laparoscopy in patients with larger, multiple, intramural, or critically located myomas. This preference is often associated with a higher rate of cesarean section, particularly when a substantial myoma attached to the posterior wall of the uterine isthmus could impede vaginal birth.
