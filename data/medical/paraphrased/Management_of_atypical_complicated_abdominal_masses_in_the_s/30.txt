Title: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

Rosalba De Nola, Emma Bassi, Cataldo Paletta, Mario Vicino

Abstract:

A woman in her forties, with no prior pregnancies, presented during her second trimester with abdominal pain and shortness of breath. Ultrasound imaging detected two sizable masses in the abdomen: one was situated in the left subphrenic region and extended up to the uterine fundus, while the other occupied the pouch of Douglas. The patient underwent a laparotomy, with the lower mass remaining in the pelvis. She continued routine obstetric follow-up and later had an elective cesarean section, which led to favorable maternal and neonatal outcomes. When large symptomatic masses are encountered during pregnancy, thorough assessment with bimanual examination and safe imaging methods, such as ultrasound and non-contrast MRI, is crucial. Surgical intervention should be reserved for cases where malignancy is suspected or complications arise, focusing on maintaining the pregnancy and minimizing unnecessary procedures. Decisions regarding delivery should consider the size, number, and specific anatomical position of uterine masses.

Background:

Identifying and treating atypical or symptomatic abdominal masses in pregnancy poses substantial clinical difficulties. Available diagnostic modalities are restricted to physical assessment, ultrasound (US) with colour Doppler, and non-contrast magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). US prior to surgery is particularly instrumental in delineating symptomatic uterine leiomyomas found on the anterior aspect of the enlarged uterus, regardless of whether they are submucosal or pedunculated. MRI delivers higher diagnostic precision when evaluating numerous, sizeable, intramural, or posterior leiomyomas, allowing accurate determination of their spatial relationship to the uterine cavity.

The utility of serum tumour markers as diagnostic tools in pregnancy is still controversial. For instance, CA-125 concentrations naturally increase in early pregnancy and return to baseline thereafter. Thus, a single CA-125 value does not establish diagnosis; however, serial measurements may offer informative trends for distinguishing between possible conditions and for monitoring. Effective clinical management involves comprehensive, multidisciplinary assessment of risks, aiming to weigh obstetric dangers against the unusual and complex nature of these cases.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 40s, obese with a body mass index of 33, gravida 1, para 0, abortus 1, presented during the second trimester of her pregnancy (21 weeks’ gestation) with severe, sudden abdominal pain localized to both the upper left quadrant and pelvic region (each site rated as 5 on the Visual Analogue Scale) and accompanied by mild shortness of breath. Her routine screening in both the first and second trimesters indicated a low risk for chromosomal abnormalities, which was corroborated by non-invasive prenatal testing, and her fetus demonstrated normal morphology.

Upon arrival at the emergency department, she underwent comprehensive evaluation by various specialties. Oxygen saturation was within normal limits (median 97%). Examination of her chest revealed a median heart rate of 74 bpm and blood pressure of 100/60 mmHg. Her ECG was unremarkable. She was subsequently admitted to the Obstetrical and Gynaecological Division. On examination, her abdomen appeared disproportionately enlarged for her gestational age, with a tender swelling and a firm, elastic mass spanning the entire upper left quadrant, measuring 18 cm, but no clinical evidence of ascites.

A bimanual vaginal examination detected a fixed mass within the lesser pelvis, approximately 9 cm in size, round, firm, and protruding partially into the posterior fornix.

For analgesia, she was given intravenous paracetamol 1000 mg twice daily and positioned semi-orthostatically to potentially minimize diaphragmatic compression from the gravid uterus and large abdominal masses, which led to modest symptom improvement. There was no requirement for supplemental oxygen or ventilation; the pain decreased from 5 to 1 on the Visual Analogue Scale.

During her hospitalization, the patient developed a mild fever. SARS-CoV-2 PCR testing via nasopharyngeal swab was negative; however, urine cultures identified a Klebsiella pneumoniae infection. Laboratory findings revealed iron deficiency anemia, with hemoglobin at 9.9 g/L (normal range 12.0–16.6 g/L) and serum ferritin at 25 mcg/L (normal range 15–300 mcg/L); C reactive protein was slightly elevated at 1.39 mg/dL (normal range 0–0.3 mg/dL); and white blood cell count was 11.29×10^3/uL (normal range 4–10×10^3/uL) with neutrophils at 9.27×10^3/uL (normal range 2.8–5.5×10^3/uL), findings that were considered within the spectrum of pregnancy-related physiological changes.

Investigations:

Ultrasound revealed two sizable, unilocular cystic lesions with smooth borders and vascularisation at their periphery. The superior lesion measured 18×12×7 cm, stretching from the left subphrenic space down to the fundus of the uterus. The inferior lesion measured 9.5×8×8 cm and appeared to be attached to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration (58×71 mm).

Tumour marker tests (CA 19–9, CA 15–3, CEA, β2-microglobulin, and LDH) were within standard ranges; however, CA-125 was raised at 41.8 U/mL (normal <35 U/mL) and displayed an upward trajectory, having risen from 36 U/mL two weeks prior. Thus, the patient underwent an MRI scan without contrast administration, which demonstrated thick walls (1.4 cm), inhomogeneous fluid content on T1 and T2 fat-sat sequences, and solid elements that were hyperintense in both T1 and T2 weighted images (see figure 1; only T2 fat-sat images provided). The boundaries of the caudal lesion were indistinct, appearing to be contiguous with the posterior leiomyoma that had been noted on the prior ultrasound. The MRI did not reveal any adenopathy.

Differential diagnosis:

On ultrasonographic assessment, the cystic lesions resembled two large decidualised endometriomas, with the inferior cyst appearing attached to an intramural-subserosal leiomyoma on the posterior uterine wall measuring 58×71 mm.

Magnetic resonance imaging revealed two well-defined, unilocular adnexal cysts containing hypertrophic vessels and heterogeneous fluid (refer to figure 1). The lower lesion appeared adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration, measuring 58×71 mm.

The considerable size of both the gravid uterus and masses precluded identification of any connecting stalk or nourishing vessel for the upper lesion, thus steering the presumptive diagnosis toward ovarian origins—primarily endometriomas—supported by mild CA-125 positivity noted in the second trimester (refer to discussion).

Given the sonographic and MRI characteristics, possible diagnoses included decidualised endometriomas and leiomyomas undergoing cystic degeneration. The mild rise in CA-125 supported an endometrioma diagnosis for the superior cyst, whereas the continuity with the uterus indicated a likely myomatous nature for the lower mass. The patient’s dyspnoea was considered secondary to mechanical compression from the large abdominal masses, with no indication of underlying pulmonary or cardiac compromise.

Treatment:

Given the deterioration of the patient’s condition, manifested by abdominal pain attributed to the mass and dyspnea secondary to restrictive lung disease resulting from the large abdominal masses, as well as the need to establish a definitive diagnosis, potential complications (such as rupture, torsion, and infection), and anticipated uterine enlargement alongside fetal growth, a laparotomy was performed at 21 weeks and 6 days gestation via a longitudinal incision from the xiphoid process to the pubis after obtaining informed consent.

Initially, two samples of free peritoneal fluid were collected for culture and cytological evaluation. Subsequent inspection of the abdominal cavity confirmed ongoing fetal cardiac activity.

Unexpectedly, the cranial mass was identified as a large, pedunculated, atypically cystic leiomyoma of the uterus with a taut, elastic consistency and pronounced vascularity. The mass was initially twisted and leaking, which likely accounted for the fever observed during the hospitalisation (refer to figure 2). As a result, the surgical team proceeded to carefully excise the mass from its uterine attachment, utilizing a double clamp (see figure 2). After the stalk was severed, the exposed base was closed with a single absorbable suture. The caudal mass, emanating from the uterine torus, was preserved in situ to reduce obstetric risk. It exhibited fixation, a smooth surface, and a soft consistency characteristic of a sizable cystic intramural-subserosal leiomyoma.

The procedure lasted 2 hours and 25 minutes. The patient was initiated on progesterone prophylaxis intraoperatively with 100 mg intramuscularly, continuing with daily 100 mg intramuscular injections until discharge. Thereafter, intramuscular caproate hydroxyprogesterone (341 mg 1 fl every 3 days, per package instructions) was administered until scheduled delivery. Cefazolin 2 g intravenously was provided as intraoperative antibiotic prophylaxis, followed postoperatively by cefazolin 1 g intravenously three times daily combined with clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily throughout the inpatient stay, as determined by the urine culture antibiogram (Klebsiella pneumoniae highly sensitive to cephalosporins and clarithromycin).

Outcome and follow-up:

Following the operation, progressive anaemia (Hb 7.5 g/L, HCT 24.3%) combined with ongoing iron deficiency necessitated intravenous administration of ferric carboxymaltose (1 g) as well as two successful blood transfusions (Hb increased to 10.0 g/L, HCT to 30.4%). The patient was discharged in stable condition, without complications, ten days post-operatively, and began a routine obstetric follow-up every three weeks. During these visits, fetal growth was monitored (86th percentile, based on the WHO fetal growth chart calculator, available at https://srhr.org/fetalgrowthcalculator/#/), along with evaluation of the amniotic fluid index.

Analysis of peritoneal fluid yielded negative results on both bacteriological and cytological testing. Pathologic assessment identified benign uterine leiomyomas and regions of ischemic necrosis adjacent to the twisted pedicle.

The lower, caudal myoma posed a significant barrier to fetal descent during the second stage of labour; as a result, a planned caesarean section was performed along the previous surgical incision when the fetus reached 37 weeks gestation, thereby minimizing the risk of uterine scar rupture and yielding an optimal neonatal outcome.

At present, the patient reports no myoma-related symptoms. The residual pelvic myoma has marginally decreased in size to 7.5 cm. She is breastfeeding successfully and chooses not to pursue further scheduled surgical intervention because of partial postpartum regression of the remaining leiomyomas, lack of symptoms, and having achieved her motherhood goals. The infant continues to develop appropriately.

Discussion:

Myomas can often be misdiagnosed as adnexal masses: the incidence during pregnancy is 0.3–10.1% and 1–6%, respectively .13

In this case, the initial assessment revealed abdominal masses that resembled two large decidualized endometriomas. However, the lower solid portion of the pelvic mass appeared to be a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma with cystic degeneration measuring 58×71 mm, as observed on both ultrasound and MRI scans. Due to the lack of established protocols for managing pelvic masses during pregnancy, a multidisciplinary team determined that scheduled surgery might lower the likelihood of preterm birth.

Throughout pregnancy, serum CA-125 levels generally display two peaks: one during the first trimester (median 23 U/mL, p<0.00001) and another in the third trimester (median 21 U/mL, p<0.001), in contrast to lower values in the second trimester (median 14.0 U/mL). In the current case, the measured CA-125 was 41.8 U/mL, exceeding the typical median for the second trimester.

CA-125 serves primarily as a tumor marker for ovarian cancer, with a recognized threshold of ≥35 U/mL—most notably among women over 50 years of age (showing higher area under the curve, positive predictive value, sensitivity, and specificity). Nevertheless, elevated CA-125 may also be associated with several benign conditions, including endometriosis and adenomyosis (>19 U/mL), pelvic inflammatory disease, menstruation, early and late stages of pregnancy (median 23 and 21 U/mL, respectively), risk of miscarriage, and uterine myoma (cut-off <19 U/mL).57

The main factors guiding the clinical approach included the identification of large, vascular masses exhibiting both solid and cystic elements that presented an immediate risk of complications such as rupture, torsion, or infection. The patient's condition was marked by increasing pain and dyspnea, with potential for further tumor growth that could lead to hypoxia, necrosis, and degeneration. There was also a slight elevation in tumor marker levels (CA-125=41.8 U/mL) and limited space available to support ongoing physiological uterine enlargement.

Given the possibility that the masses were neoplastic and their etiology remained unclear, the patient underwent a non-contrast MRI to provide greater diagnostic clarity following the initial ultrasound. In line with current guidelines, MRI with gadolinium contrast is not recommended in pregnancy unless absolutely necessary in certain conditions, such as severe rheumatological, inflammatory, or skin infiltrative disease, or risk of stillbirth or neonatal mortality. For this reason, the multidisciplinary team opted for an unenhanced MRI and avoided using magnets stronger than 1.5-T, as this is considered reasonably safe in the second trimester and beyond.

In this case, the multidisciplinary team—including surgeons, gynecologists, and obstetricians—chose to perform longitudinal laparotomy for surgical management after securing specific written informed consent from the patient. The upper lesion demonstrated a pedicle during intraoperative evaluation, which substantially increased the likelihood of an acute complication. This factor underpinned the decision to restrict intervention to the upper myomectomy. Additionally, fetal viability was continuously and intermittently tracked via transvaginal ultrasound during the procedure, with intraoperative progesterone administered prophylactically.

Zhao et al, in a large multicenter analysis, emphasized the necessity of documenting fetal heart rate measurements prior to, throughout, and following surgical intervention.

In general, surgical intervention is postponed until after delivery, with medical and expectant management often preferred. Notably, Sakinci et al reported in a recent tertiary center study that there were no statistically significant differences regarding preoperative and postoperative hemoglobin values or blood transfusion rates (p >0.05) between women undergoing combined caesarean section (CS) and myomectomy and those with no myoma who had CS only; however, both hospital stay and operative time were significantly greater in the CS myomectomy cohort (p =0.001 and p =0.001, respectively). The average myoma diameter measured 8.3 ± 4.1 cm, with a statistically significant positive correlation between myoma size and length of hospital admission (p =0.01).

The same study identified a statistically significant negative correlation between myoma size and gestational age at delivery (p = 0.035).

Uterine leiomyomas exhibit a non-linear pattern in their size progression during gestation and the postpartum period. Specifically, leiomyomas increase in size by 6.90% per week overall: 6.12% (p<0.001) from the first to the second trimester, and 3.10%±0.39% from the second to the third trimester (p<0.05). Consequently, the rate of leiomyoma enlargement is significantly greater in the initial trimester compared to later stages of pregnancy. Conversely, a notable reduction in myoma size is observed during the third trimester and up to six weeks post-delivery (mean decrease 44.25%±36.05%, p<0.05).

The aforementioned studies emphasize the necessity of meticulous ultrasonographic assessment of uterine myomas’ size, location, number, and spatial relationship to the placenta prior to any surgical intervention.

When uterine myomas are detected during pregnancy, management typically favors a conservative strategy over surgical removal, due to the increased risks of substantial hemorrhage, pregnancy loss, premature labor, and uterine rupture. Nonetheless, surgical intervention (myomectomy) should be contemplated when dealing with complicated tumors, such as those with torsion of a pedunculated myoma or persistent, refractory symptoms unresponsive to conservative measures. In such cases, the duration of surgery should be minimized to protect the ongoing pregnancy, and laparoscopic methods—with an open Hasson entry technique—may be utilized, especially during early gestation (first and early second trimesters), for smaller, non-intramural masses in suitable locations. This strategy is associated with improved maternal morbidity profiles and facilitates the possibility of vaginal delivery. However, the current case exemplifies a frequent selection bias favoring laparotomy rather than laparoscopy in scenarios involving larger, multiple, intramural, or precariously situated myomas, which may necessitate cesarean section due to the presence of a sizable myoma adherent to the posterior isthmic uterine wall, potentially impeding vaginal delivery.
