Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

Rosalba De Nola, Emma Bassi, Cataldo Paletta, Mario Vicino

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her 40s, who had not previously given birth, experienced abdominal pain and shortness of breath while in her second trimester of pregnancy. Imaging by ultrasonography identified two substantial abdominal masses: one superior mass extending from the entire left subphrenic area down to the uterine fundus, and an inferior mass occupying the pouch of Douglas. The patient underwent a laparotomy, during which the caudal mass was retained within the pelvis. Following the procedure, she received routine antenatal care and ultimately underwent an elective caesarean section, yielding favorable outcomes for both mother and child. In situations where large, symptomatic masses are detected during pregnancy, thorough assessment involving bimanual examination and reliable imaging techniques, such as ultrasound and non-contrast MRI, is critical. Surgical intervention should be restricted to cases where there is clinical suspicion of malignancy or the emergence of complications, with the overarching goal of maintaining pregnancy and preventing unnecessary interventions. Decisions regarding the route of delivery should be guided by the mass's size, quantity, and anatomical positioning within the uterus.

Background:

The evaluation and treatment of unusual or symptomatic abdominal masses in pregnant patients pose notable clinical difficulties. Diagnostic modalities are primarily restricted to physical assessment, ultrasound (US) incorporating colour Doppler imaging, and non-contrast magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Preoperative US is especially helpful for detecting symptomatic uterine leiomyomas situated on the anterior surface of the pregnant uterus, including those with submucosal or pedunculated characteristics. For the assessment of numerous, sizable, intramural, or posteriorly located leiomyomas, MRI provides greater diagnostic precision by accurately measuring the separation between the myoma and the endometrial cavity.

The utility of serum tumour markers during gestation remains controversial. In particular, CA-125 concentrations naturally increase during early pregnancy and later return to baseline, making single time-point values nondiagnostic. Instead, longitudinal assessment of these markers may reveal important trends that aid in differential diagnosis and ongoing surveillance. Effective management depends on a thorough, multidisciplinary risk evaluation, aiming to carefully weigh obstetric considerations against the uncommon and complex nature of these cases.

Case presentation:

A woman in her forties with obesity (body mass index of 33) and a pregnancy history of G1P0A1 was referred to our department during her second trimester (at 21 weeks of gestation) due to acute, severe pain in the upper left abdomen and pelvis (Visual Analogue Scale score of 5 at both locations), alongside mild shortness of breath. Her prior first and second trimester screenings for aneuploidies indicated low risk, corroborated by non-invasive prenatal testing, and fetal structural evaluations were normal.

Initially, she presented to the emergency department for comprehensive, multidisciplinary assessment. Upon evaluation, her oxygen saturation was within normal limits (median 97%). Cardiothoracic examination yielded normal findings, including a median heart rate of 74 beats per minute and blood pressure of 100/60 mm Hg. Her ECG was unremarkable. She was subsequently transferred to the Obstetrical and Gynaecological Division. Abdominal examination revealed more distention than typically anticipated for her gestational age, featuring a sensitive swelling and a firm, elastic mass in the entire upper left abdominal quadrant, measuring 18 cm in diameter, but without clinical evidence of ascites.

On bimanual vaginal examination, a fixed, rounded, hard mass approximately 9 cm in diameter was palpated in the lesser pelvis, partly protruding into the posterior fornix.

Administration of intravenous paracetamol (1000 mg twice daily) in combination with a semi-orthostatic positioning—intended to reduce diaphragmatic pressure from the gravid uterus and the substantial abdominal masses—resulted in partial symptomatic relief, lowering her pain score from 5 to 1 on the Visual Analogue Scale, all without additional oxygen or ventilatory support.

During her admission, the patient developed a low-grade fever. A nasopharyngeal swab PCR test for severe acute respiratory syndrome, Coronavirus 2, was negative. However, urine cultures identified a Klebsiella pneumoniae infection. Laboratory investigations also revealed iron deficiency anaemia (haemoglobin of 9.9 g/L, normal range 12.0–16.6 g/L; serum ferritin 25 mcg/L, normal range 15–300 mcg/L), a modest elevation in C-reactive protein (1.39 mg/dL, normal value 0–0.3 mg/dL), and increased neutrophil count (total white blood cell count 11.29×10^3 /uL [n.v. 4–10×10^3 /uL], neutrophils 9.27×10^3 /uL [n.v. 2.8–5.5×10^3 /uL]), the latter findings compatible with expected physiological changes during pregnancy.

Investigations:

Ultrasound examination revealed two sizable, single-chamber cystic lesions characterized by smooth contours and peripheral vascular supply. The superior cystic mass measured 18×12×7 cm, extending from the left subphrenic space down to the fundus of the uterus. The inferior mass, with dimensions of 9.5×8×8 cm, appeared to be attached to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration (58×71 mm).

Laboratory analysis of tumour markers (CA 19–9, CA 15–3, CEA, β2-microglobulin, and LDH) revealed values within normal ranges, while CA-125 was found to be elevated at 41.8 U/mL (reference range <35 U/mL), having risen from 36 U/mL in the previous 2 weeks, indicating a slight upward trend in this marker’s serum level. Consequently, an MRI scan without contrast was performed, which demonstrated thick cystic walls (1.4 cm), with T1 and T2 fat-saturated sequences revealing inhomogeneous fluid contents as well as focal regions of high signal intensity on both T1- and T2-weighted images (see figure 1; only T2 fat-sat sequences are presented). The lower mass’s borders were indistinct, appearing to merge with the posterior leiomyoma that had previously been identified on ultrasound. MRI assessment did not reveal any lymphadenopathy.

Differential diagnosis:

During the ultrasound assessment, the cystic lesions resembled two large decidualised endometriomas; additionally, the lower (caudal) cyst appeared attached to an intramural-subserosal leiomyoma located on the posterior uterine wall, with dimensions of 58×71 mm.

Magnetic Resonance Imaging revealed the presence of two well-defined, unilocular adnexal cysts characterized by prominent vascularization and heterogeneous intraluminal content (refer to figure 1). The caudal lesion was contiguous with a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma undergoing cystic degeneration, also measuring 58×71 mm.

Given the spatial displacement from the enlarged gravid uterus and the substantial size of the masses, it was not possible to visualize any pedicle or distinct vascular supply to the superior mass. These features led to a presumptive diagnosis of ovarian masses, particularly endometriomas, further supported by mildly elevated CA-125 levels noted in the second trimester (see discussion).

Integrating findings from both sonography and MRI, the main differential diagnoses included decidualised endometriomas and leiomyomas with cystic degeneration. While the slight increase in CA-125 was more consistent with an endometrioma as the etiology of the upper cyst, the anatomical continuity between the caudal lesion and the uterine wall favored a diagnosis of myomatous origin for the caudal mass. The patient's experience of dyspnoea was attributed to compressive effects of the voluminous abdominal masses, with no indications of cardiopulmonary compromise.

Treatment:

As the patient's clinical status deteriorated—marked by abdominal pain associated with the masses and dyspnoea attributable to restrictive lung disease caused by the mass effect—a definitive diagnosis became imperative. Additionally, concerns regarding potential complications such as rupture, torsion, and infection, as well as anticipated uterine expansion with fetal development, prompted the surgical team to proceed with a laparotomy. The procedure, performed via a longitudinal xiphos-pubic incision, was undertaken at 21 weeks and 6 days of gestation following informed consent.

The initial intraoperative step involved collecting two samples of peritoneal fluid for both cytological examination and culture. Subsequent exploration of the abdominal cavity confirmed the persistence of fetal cardiac activity.

Unexpectedly, the cranial mass was identified as a giant pedunculated, atypical cystic, and firm-elastic uterine leiomyoma. This lesion was highly vascularised, exhibited initial twisting, and was leaking, findings that likely accounted for the febrile episodes observed during hospitalization (see figure 2). In response, the surgical team delicately excised the mass at its point of uterine attachment utilizing a double-clamp technique (see figure 2). After transecting the stalk, meticulous closure of the exposed base was achieved using a single absorbable suture. To reduce obstetric risk, the caudal mass, which originated from the uterine torus and demonstrated fixation, a smooth contour, and a soft consistency resembling a large cystic intramural-subserosal leiomyoma, was left unremoved.

The entire operation lasted 2 hours and 25 minutes. Prophylactic progesterone (100 mg intramuscularly) was initiated during surgery and subsequently administered daily via the intramuscular route until discharge. Thereafter, maintenance therapy involved intramuscular caproate hydroxyprogesterone (341 mg, 1 ampoule intramuscularly every 3 days, as per prescribing information) up to the scheduled delivery. For antimicrobial prophylaxis, cefazolin 2 g was administered intravenously during surgery, followed by a postoperative regimen of cefazolin 1 g intravenously three times daily plus clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily. This regimen was selected according to the urine culture antibiogram, which demonstrated that Klebsiella pneumoniae was highly susceptible to both cephalosporins and clarithromycin.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following the operation, the patient's anaemia worsened (Hb 7.5 g/L, HCT 24.3%) and iron deficiency persisted, necessitating intravenous iron therapy with ferric carboxymaltose (1 g) alongside two successful blood transfusions (resulting in Hb 10.0 g/L, HCT 30.4%). The postoperative course was uneventful, and she was discharged 10 days after surgery. Thereafter, she was placed on close obstetric surveillance at three-week intervals, which demonstrated satisfactory fetal growth (86th percentile according to the WHO fetal growth chart calculator: https://srhr.org/fetalgrowthcalculator/#/) and stable amniotic fluid index.

Testing of the peritoneal fluid returned negative results for both microbiological and cytological analyses. Histopathological evaluation identified benign uterine leiomyomas and showed regions of ischaemic necrosis adjacent to the twisted pedicle.

The caudally located myoma would have significantly obstructed fetal descent during the second stage of labour. Therefore, when the fetus attained 37 weeks of gestation, an elective caesarean section was performed via the pre-existing surgical incision, thereby lowering the likelihood of uterine scar rupture and resulting in a favorable neonatal outcome.

At present, the patient remains asymptomatic with respect to the myoma, although the residual pelvic mass has marginally decreased in size to 7.5 cm. She has established effective breastfeeding and prefers to defer further surgery, given the partial regression of the leiomyomas postpartum, her lack of symptoms, and having achieved her maternal goals. The infant is exhibiting normal development.

Discussion:

Myomas can often be misdiagnosed as adnexal masses: the incidence during pregnancy is 0.3–10.1% and 1–6%, respectively .13

In this instance, initial assessments revealed abdominal masses that resembled two large decidualised endometriomas, with the exception of a caudal solid portion within the pelvic mass, which appeared consistent with a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration, measuring 58×71 mm on both US and MRI scans. Given the lack of established management protocols for pelvic masses during pregnancy, a multidisciplinary team judged that planned surgical intervention might lower the risk of preterm birth.

Throughout pregnancy, serum CA-125 typically presents two peaks: one in the first trimester (median 23 U/mL, p<0.00001) and another in the third trimester (median 21 U/mL, p<0.001), both significantly higher than median values in the second trimester (median 14.0 U/mL). By contrast, in this patient, the observed value of 41.8 U/mL exceeded the median normally recorded in the second trimester.

CA-125 primarily serves as a tumor marker for ovarian malignancy (standard cut-off ≥35 U/mL), with its diagnostic performance—area under the curve, sensitivity, specificity, and positive predictive value—being highest in women over 50. Nevertheless, CA-125 concentrations may also increase due to benign conditions, including endometriosis and adenomyosis (>19 U/mL), pelvic inflammatory disease, menstruation, early and late-stage pregnancy (median values of 23 U/mL and 21 U/mL, respectively), elevated risk of miscarriage, and myoma (cut-off <19 U/mL).57

The main factors influencing the clinical management included the identification of large, vascularized masses exhibiting both solid and cystic elements, which posed a significant risk for potential complications such as rupture, torsion, or infection. The patient's worsening symptoms—namely escalating pain and dyspnoea—further guided intervention, alongside concerns regarding potential further enlargement of the masses, which could increase the likelihood of hypoxia, necrosis, or degeneration. Additionally, mildly elevated levels of oncological markers (CA-125=41.8 U/mL) and concerns about spatial constraints affecting normal uterine growth were also considered in decision making.

Given the suspicion of a neoplastic process and the ambiguous nature of the lesions, the patient received an MRI without contrast subsequent to the ultrasound to enhance diagnostic clarity. Current guidelines advise against the use of gadolinium-enhanced MRI during pregnancy except in cases of absolute necessity—such as specific rheumatologic, inflammatory, or infiltrative dermal conditions, or when there is risk of stillbirth or neonatal mortality. Consequently, the multidisciplinary care team opted for a non-contrast MRI, using a magnet strength of no more than 1.5-T, which is generally regarded as safe in pregnancies beyond the first trimester.

In this case, a multidisciplinary team consisting of surgeons, gynaecologists, and obstetricians elected to perform longitudinal laparotomic surgery after securing explicit written informed consent from the patient. Intraoperatively, the superior lesion was found to possess a pedicle, increasing the potential for an acute, characteristic complication. This intraoperative finding was the basis for restricting the intervention to an upper myomectomy. Throughout the procedure, serial and intermittent transvaginal ultrasonography was employed to assess ongoing fetal viability, complemented by the prophylactic use of intraoperative progesterone.

A substantial multicentre investigation by Zhao et al highlighted the necessity of monitoring and documenting fetal heart rate before, during, and after the operative intervention.

Typically, surgical intervention is deferred until after delivery, favouring a conservative or medical management strategy. However, a recent study by Sakinci et al at a tertiary care centre indicated that no statistically significant differences existed between women undergoing caesarean section (CS) with concurrent myomectomy and those undergoing CS without myoma in terms of preoperative and postoperative haemoglobin levels or the requirement for blood transfusion (p >0.05). Nevertheless, the length of hospital stay and operative time were markedly longer in the cohort subjected to CS with myomectomy (p =0.001 and p =0.001, respectively). The average myoma diameter was 8.3 ± 4.1cm, and there was a statistically significant positive correlation between myoma size and the duration of hospitalisation (p =0.01).

The same study identified a statistically significant inverse relationship between myoma size and the gestational age at delivery ($p = 0.035$).

Uterine leiomyomas exhibit a non-linear progression in size throughout pregnancy and the postpartum period. Specifically, leiomyoma dimensions increase by 6.90% per week: 6.12% ($p<0.001$) from the first to second trimesters, and 3.10% $\pm$ 0.39% ($p<0.05$) from the second to third trimesters. Hence, leiomyomas demonstrate a markedly greater enlargement during the first trimester relative to subsequent gestational stages. Conversely, in the third trimester and six weeks following delivery, myoma size shows a statistically significant reduction (mean decrease of 44.25% $\pm$ 36.05%, $p<0.05$).

These studies underscore the necessity for precise ultrasonographic assessment regarding the size, anatomical position, count, and proximity of uterine myomas to the placenta prior to any surgical intervention.

When uterine myomas are detected during pregnancy, conservative management is typically favored over surgical procedures, given the increased risk of severe hemorrhage, fetal loss, preterm delivery, and uterine rupture. Surgical removal (myomectomy), however, should be contemplated in cases involving complicated masses such as torsion of pedunculated myomas, or when persistent symptoms fail to respond to conservative therapy. Surgical intervention should be kept as brief as possible to optimize pregnancy outcomes, and minimally invasive techniques such as laparoscopy—with a preference for the open Hasson entry technique—are advisable in instances of lower gestational age (first and early second trimester), reduced myoma volume, and when the mass is not intramural. This method is associated with decreased maternal morbidity and improved rates of vaginal delivery. Nonetheless, the case presented exemplifies a frequent bias towards selecting laparotomy over laparoscopy for larger, multiple, intramural, or critically situated myomas, which is linked to a higher incidence of cesarean section due to large myomas adhered to the posterior isthmic region of the uterus, thereby obstructing the possibility of vaginal birth.
