Title: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

Rosalba De Nola, Emma Bassi, Cataldo Paletta, Mario Vicino

Abstract:

A nulliparous woman in her 40s presented during her second trimester with abdominal pain and shortness of breath. Ultrasonographic assessment identified two sizable masses in the abdomen: the superior mass occupied the entire left subphrenic region and extended to the uterine fundus, while the inferior mass was located in the pouch of Douglas. The patient underwent laparotomy, and the pelvic lesion was left undisturbed. She continued with routine obstetric monitoring and later underwent a planned caesarean section, resulting in favorable maternal and neonatal outcomes. In pregnant patients with large symptomatic masses, thorough evaluation with bimanual examination and safe imaging approaches, including ultrasonography and non-contrast MRI, is crucial. Surgery should be reserved for cases where there is suspicion for malignancy or clinical complications, prioritizing pregnancy preservation and avoiding unnecessary intervention. Decisions regarding delivery method should be individualized based on the characteristics and location of uterine masses.

Background:

Diagnosing and treating unusual or symptomatic abdominal masses in pregnant patients involves considerable clinical difficulty. Diagnostic modalities are restricted to physical assessment, ultrasound (US) with colour Doppler, and MRI without contrast. Preoperative US is especially helpful in detecting symptomatic uterine leiomyomas that are situated on the anterior wall of the pregnant uterus, whether they are submucosal or pedunculated. MRI is particularly advantageous in cases of multiple, large, intramural, or posterior leiomyomas, as it can accurately measure the distance between the fibroid and the endometrial cavity.

The utility of serum tumour markers during pregnancy remains controversial. For instance, CA-125 concentrations physiologically increase during the initial trimester before returning to baseline levels. Thus, single measurements are not diagnostic, but serial assessments may reveal trends valuable for distinguishing causes and monitoring patients. Optimal care requires a thoughtful, multidisciplinary risk evaluation to appropriately weigh obstetric risks against the unusual nature and complexity of these cases.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 40s, G1P0A1, who was obese with a body mass index of 33, presented during her second trimester of pregnancy at 21 weeks gestation with intense, sudden abdominal pain in both the upper left quadrant and pelvic region (Visual Analogue Scale score of 5 for each area), along with mild shortness of breath. Her first and second trimester routine screenings indicated a low probability of aneuploidies, confirmed by non-invasive prenatal testing, and fetal morphology was found to be normal.

She was initially seen in the emergency department, where a comprehensive multidisciplinary assessment was conducted. Oxygen saturation was within the normal range (median 97%). Chest examination revealed a normal heart rate (median 74 bpm) and blood pressure (median 100/60 mm Hg). ECG findings were unremarkable. Subsequently, she was admitted to the Obstetrical and Gynaecological Division. Physical examination showed an abdomen more distended than expected for her gestational age, characterized by a tender swelling and a firm, taut-elastic mass occupying the entire upper left quadrant, measuring 18 cm, without any signs of ascites on clinical evaluation.

A bimanual vaginal assessment revealed a fixed, rounded, hard mass approximately 9 cm in size within the lesser pelvis, partially protruding into the posterior fornix.

Pain management included intravenous paracetamol at a dose of 1000 mg twice daily, combined with a semi-orthostatic posture intended to possibly minimize diaphragmatic compression from the gravid uterus and substantial abdominal masses. These measures provided moderate relief, reducing pain from a score of 5 to 1 on the Visual Analogue Scale, and did not require supplemental oxygen or ventilation.

During hospitalization, the patient developed a mild fever. Polymerase chain reaction testing for severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 from a nasopharyngeal swab returned negative, while urine cultures revealed a Klebsiella pneumoniae infection. Laboratory investigations showed iron deficiency anemia with a haemoglobin level of 9.9 g/L (normal range 12.0–16.6 g/L) and serum ferritin of 25 mcg/L (normal range 15–300 mcg/L). There was a mild elevation of C-reactive protein at 1.39 mg/dL (normal range 0–0.3 mg/dL) and increased neutrophil count—total white blood cells 11.29×10^3 /uL (normal range 4–10×10^3 /uL) and neutrophils 9.27×10^3 /uL (normal range 2.8–5.5×10^3 /uL)—in line with physiological changes of pregnancy.

Investigations:

Ultrasound revealed two sizable, unilocular cystic lesions with smooth borders and peripheral blood flow. The superior lesion measured 18×12×7 cm, stretching from the left subphrenic area down to the uterine fundus. The inferior lesion measured 9.5×8×8 cm and appeared attached to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration (58×71 mm).

Laboratory evaluation showed that tumour markers CA 19-9, CA 15-3, CEA, β2-microglobulin, and LDH were all within reference ranges. However, CA-125 was elevated at 41.8 U/mL (normal <35 U/mL), with a progression from 36 U/mL recorded two weeks prior, indicating a mild upward trend in this marker’s serum levels. Subsequently, the patient underwent MRI without contrast, which revealed thick walls measuring 1.4 cm, inhomogeneous fluid on T1 and T2 fat-sat weighted sequences, and areas of focal hyperintensity in T1-T2 weighted solid components (refer to figure 1; only T2 fat-sat images provided). The caudal lesion’s boundary was indistinct, and it appeared contiguous with the posterior leiomyoma previously described by ultrasound. MRI showed no evidence of lymphadenopathy.

Differential diagnosis:

During ultrasonography, the cystic lesions closely resembled two large decidualised endometriomas. The cyst located caudally appeared to be attached to a posterior intramural-subserosal leiomyoma in the uterine wall, measuring 58×71 mm.

Magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated two rounded, unilocular cysts in the adnexa with enlarged blood vessels and uneven fluid contents (see figure 1). The caudal lesion was contiguous with a posterior uterine intramural-subserosal myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration and a diameter of 58×71 mm.

Because the substantial volume of the gravid uterus and the sizeable masses precluded visualization of any connecting stalk or vascular supply to the superior cystic lesion, the diagnostic focus shifted to ovarian masses—most notably endometriomas, supported by mildly increased CA-125 levels during the second trimester (see discussion).

Sonographic and MRI characteristics prompted a differential diagnosis that included both decidualised endometriomas and cystic degenerating leiomyomas. While the modest CA-125 elevation favored an endometriomatous origin for the upper cyst, anatomical continuity with the uterine tissue indicated that the caudal mass was likely a myomatous lesion. The patient's dyspnoea was considered secondary to physical compression by the large abdominal masses, with no signs of cardiopulmonary compromise.

Treatment:

Given the deterioration of the patient's clinical status—including abdominal pain attributed to the mass and dyspnoea secondary to restrictive lung disease from the masses’ size—along with the need for definitive diagnostic clarification, risk for potential complications (such as rupture, torsion, or infection), and the possibility of uterine enlargement with fetal growth, laparotomy was performed at 21 weeks and 6 days following acquisition of informed consent. The incision extended longitudinally from the xiphoid process to the pubic symphysis.

At the onset of the procedure, two samples of free peritoneal fluid were obtained for both culture and cytology. Subsequent examination of the abdominal cavity confirmed ongoing fetal cardiac activity.

Unexpectedly, the mass in the cranial location presented as a markedly large, pedunculated uterine leiomyoma characterized by atypical cystic and tense-elastic properties, high vascularity, initial torsion, and evidence of leakage—potentially explaining the patient’s fever during hospitalization (refer to figure 2). The surgical team, therefore, opted to excise this mass from its uterine attachment using a double clamp technique (refer to figure 2). After severing the pedicle, the base was closed with a single absorbable suture. The caudal mass, which originated from the uterine torus, was left undisturbed to reduce obstetric risk. This lesion appeared firmly attached, with a smooth surface and soft consistency resembling a sizable intramural-subserosal cystic leiomyoma.

The operative period lasted 2 hours and 25 minutes. Of note, the patient was administered prophylactic progesterone intramuscularly at 100 mg beginning during surgery and continued at 100 mg intramuscular daily until discharge. Subsequently, intramuscular hydroxyprogesterone caproate (341 mg per ampule intramuscular every 3 days, as indicated in the manufacturer's instructions) was provided until planned delivery. Prophylactic antibiotics were initiated intraoperatively with intravenous cefazolin 2 g, followed by postoperative intravenous cefazolin 1 g three times daily and clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily throughout the hospital stay per the urine culture antibiogram (demonstrating Klebsiella pneumoniae with high susceptibility to cephalosporins and clarithromycin).

Outcome and follow-up:

Following surgery, the patient’s anaemia worsened (Hb 7.5 g/L, HCT 24.3%), and persistent iron deficiency necessitated intravenous iron administration (ferric carboxymaltose 1 g) along with two successful blood transfusions (Hb 10.0 g/L, HCT 30.4%). She was discharged without complications 10 days postoperatively and began close obstetric follow-up every 3 weeks, demonstrating ongoing fetal growth (86th percentile according to the WHO fetal growth chart calculator, available online at https://srhr.org/fetalgrowthcalculator/#/), as well as monitoring of amniotic fluid index.

Peritoneal fluid analysis yielded negative results for both culture and cytology. Pathologic assessment identified benign uterine leiomyomas and areas of ischemic necrosis adjacent to the twisted pedicle.

The inferior myoma would have severely impeded passage of the fetal head during labor's expulsive phase; thus, an elective caesarean section was carried out at 37 weeks gestation, utilising the same surgical incision, thereby reducing the likelihood of uterine scar rupture and resulting in an optimal neonatal outcome.

Presently, the patient does not report symptoms attributable to the myoma, though the remaining pelvic myoma has diminished slightly in size to 7.5 cm. She is breastfeeding without difficulty, and prefers not to schedule additional surgery at this time due to partial postpartum shrinkage of the remaining leiomyomas, lack of symptoms, and the completion of her maternal goals. The infant continues to develop normally.

Discussion:

Myomas can often be misdiagnosed as adnexal masses: the incidence during pregnancy is 0.3–10.1% and 1–6%, respectively .13

In this case, the initial assessment revealed abdominal masses resembling two large decidualized endometriomas. However, the lower solid portion of the pelvic mass was consistent with a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration, measuring 58×71 mm on both ultrasound and MRI studies. As there are no established guidelines for the management of pelvic masses during pregnancy, the multidisciplinary team determined that planned surgery might reduce the likelihood of preterm birth.

Normally, serum CA-125 concentrations during pregnancy display two peaks: the first during the initial trimester (median 23 U/mL, p<0.00001) and the second in the third trimester (median 21 U/mL, p<0.001), as compared to the second trimester median (14.0 U/mL). In contrast, the patient in this report exhibited a CA-125 level of 41.8 U/mL, which exceeds the typical median value observed in the second trimester.

CA-125 is most commonly used as a tumor marker for ovarian cancer (with a standard threshold of ≥35 U/mL), particularly in women aged over 50 years, where it offers improved area under the curve, positive predictive value, sensitivity, and specificity. Nonetheless, elevated CA-125 is also associated with a range of benign conditions including endometriosis and adenomyosis (values exceeding 19 U/mL), pelvic inflammatory disease, menstruation, early and late pregnancy (median 23 and 21 U/mL, respectively), increased risk of miscarriage, and myoma (cut-off <19 U/mL).57

The primary factors guiding the clinical management included the identification of large, vascularized lesions with both solid and cystic components, which posed a current risk for complications such as rupture, torsion, or infection. Additional considerations were the patient's deteriorating symptoms—specifically worsening pain and dyspnea—as well as the potential for the masses to further enlarge, thereby increasing the likelihood of hypoxia, necrosis, or degeneration. Slight elevations in oncologic markers (CA-125=41.8 U/mL) and limited intra-abdominal space, which could impede continued physiological uterine growth, were also influential in the decision-making process.

Given the suspected neoplastic nature and indeterminate etiology of the masses, an MRI without contrast was performed following the ultrasound to enhance diagnostic clarity. In alignment with current guidelines, gadolinium-enhanced MRI was not employed during pregnancy unless absolutely necessary—such as in cases involving rheumatological, inflammatory, or infiltrative skin disorders, or a risk of stillbirth or neonatal death. Consequently, the multidisciplinary team opted for a non-contrast MRI, ensuring not to exceed a 1.5-Tesla field strength, a modality considered acceptably safe after the first trimester.

In this case, the multidisciplinary team—including surgeons, gynecologists, and obstetricians—chose a longitudinal laparotomic surgical approach after obtaining a formal written informed consent from the patient. Intraoperative evaluation revealed a pedicle on the upper lesion, which posed a considerable risk for an acute, classic complication, thus leading to the decision to restrict the procedure to an upper myomectomy. Fetal viability was tracked throughout the operation via intermittent and serial transvaginal ultrasounds, complemented by prophylactic intraoperative progesterone administration.

A multicenter study by Zhao et al emphasizes the necessity of assessing and documenting fetal heart rate prior to, during, and following surgery.

Typically, surgical intervention is postponed until after delivery, favoring medical and expectant management. Nevertheless, Sakinci et al recently reported in a tertiary center that women who underwent caesarean section (CS) with concurrent myomectomy demonstrated no statistically significant differences compared to those undergoing CS without myomectomy with respect to preoperative and postoperative hemoglobin levels or need for blood transfusion (p > 0.05). However, the duration of hospitalization and surgery was notably longer in the CS myomectomy group (p = 0.001 for both). The average myoma size was 8.3 ± 4.1 cm, with a significant positive relationship between myoma size and length of hospital stay (p = 0.01).

The same study found a statistically significant inverse relationship between myoma size and gestational age at delivery (p = 0.035).

Uterine leiomyomas exhibit a non-linear pattern of size fluctuation during both pregnancy and the puerperium. Specifically, leiomyoma dimensions expand by 6.90% per week, with a 6.12% weekly increase observed between the first and second trimesters (p<0.001), and a 3.10%±0.39% increase per week between the second and third trimesters (p<0.05). Consequently, uterine leiomyomas demonstrate a markedly faster growth rate in the first trimester than at later gestational stages. In contrast, myoma dimensions decrease significantly during the third trimester and by 6 weeks postpartum, with a mean reduction of 44.25%±36.05% (p<0.05).

These studies underscore the necessity for precise ultrasonographic assessment of the dimensions, position, count, and distance from the placenta of uterine myomas prior to undertaking surgical intervention.

When uterine myomas are identified during pregnancy, conservative management is typically favored over surgical options because of the heightened risks for heavy bleeding, pregnancy loss, preterm delivery, and uterine rupture. Nevertheless, surgical removal should be contemplated if there are complicated presentations such as torsion of a pedunculated myoma or ongoing symptoms not responsive to conservative management. When surgery is warranted, the procedure should be kept as brief as possible to maintain pregnancy viability, and a laparoscopic technique—particularly the open Hasson approach—is advisable for cases in the first or early second trimester, with smaller myomas located outside the uterine wall (non-intramural). This method offers improved outcomes concerning maternal morbidity and likelihood of vaginal delivery. The current case, however, exemplifies the frequent selection bias towards laparotomy rather than laparoscopy in the context of larger, multiple, intramural, or critically situated myomas, which may be associated with increased cesarean section rates. In this scenario, a sizeable myoma attached to the posterior isthmic region of the uterus posed a physical barrier to vaginal delivery.
