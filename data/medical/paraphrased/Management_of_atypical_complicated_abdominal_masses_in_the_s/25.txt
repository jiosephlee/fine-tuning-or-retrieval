Title: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

Rosalba De Nola, Emma Bassi, Cataldo Paletta, Mario Vicino

Abstract:

Summary: A nulliparous woman in her 40s experienced abdominal discomfort and shortness of breath during the second trimester of pregnancy. Ultrasound identified two sizable abdominal masses: the upper mass filled the entire left subphrenic area and extended toward the uterine fundus, while the lower mass occupied the pouch of Douglas. The patient underwent laparotomy, leaving the lower pelvic mass intact. She then received regular prenatal care and an elective cesarean section, leading to favorable maternal and neonatal outcomes. When large, symptomatic masses occur during pregnancy, thorough assessment—including bimanual examination and imaging modalities such as ultrasound and non-contrast MRI—is critical. Surgery should be reserved for cases with oncological concern or clinical complications, with the goal of maintaining the pregnancy and avoiding unnecessary interventions. The delivery approach should be chosen based on the size, quantity, and anatomical location of the uterine masses.

Background:

Identifying and treating unusual or symptomatic abdominal masses in pregnant patients is clinically demanding. Available diagnostic modalities include physical assessment, ultrasound (US) with color Doppler, and non-contrast magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Preoperative US proves especially useful for detecting symptomatic uterine leiomyomas positioned on the anterior aspect of the pregnant uterus, regardless of whether they are submucosal or pedunculated in nature. MRI delivers enhanced diagnostic precision for cases involving multiple, sizable, intramural, or posterior fibroids, due to its ability to accurately delineate the spatial relationship between the fibroid and the uterine cavity.

The use of serum tumor markers for diagnosis during gestation is still a topic of ongoing discussion. In particular, CA-125 concentrations naturally increase during the first trimester before returning to baseline. As a result, single measurements lack diagnostic value, while serial assessments may reveal patterns helpful in distinguishing diagnoses and monitoring disease progression. Effective clinical management entails thorough multidisciplinary evaluation to weigh obstetric risks against the uncommon and complex nature of these presentations.

Case presentation:

A woman in her forties, classified as obese with a body mass index of 33, G1P0A1, presented during her second trimester at 21 weeks gestation due to intense, sudden-onset abdominal pain localized to the upper left quadrant and pelvic region, with each site scoring 5 on the Visual Analogue Scale. She also experienced mild dyspnoea. Her routine first and second trimester screenings indicated a low probability of aneuploidy (validated by non-invasive prenatal testing), and fetal morphology appeared normal.

Upon arrival, she underwent a comprehensive multidisciplinary assessment in the emergency department: oxygen saturation was within normal limits (median 97%), thoracic examination showed a regular heart rate (median 74 bpm) and blood pressure (median 100/60 mm Hg). The ECG yielded normal results. Subsequently, she was transferred to the Obstetrics and Gynecology department. Clinically, her abdomen was more distended than anticipated for her gestational stage, with a palpable, tender swelling and a taut-elastic mass, 18 cm in size, occupying the entire upper left quadrant, and no clinical evidence of ascites.

Bimanual vaginal examination identified a fixed, firm mass in the lesser pelvis measuring approximately 9 cm, rounded in shape, and protruding slightly towards the posterior fornix.

For pain management, she was treated with intravenous paracetamol (1000 mg twice daily) and maintained in a semi-upright position, presumed to lessen diaphragmatic pressure caused by the gravid uterus and sizeable abdominal masses. These interventions led to a modest improvement in symptoms, reducing her pain from 5 to 1 on the Visual Analogue Scale, without requiring supplemental oxygen or ventilatory support.

During hospitalization, she developed a mild fever. A PCR test for severe acute respiratory syndrome, Coronavirus 2 via nasopharyngeal swab returned negative, whereas urinary samples revealed a Klebsiella pneumoniae infection. Laboratory investigations showed iron deficiency anaemia with a haemoglobin concentration of 9.9 g/L (reference range 12.0–16.6 g/L) and serum ferritin of 25 mcg/L (reference range 15–300 mcg/L). There was a slight elevation in C reactive protein at 1.39 mg/dL (reference range 0–0.3 mg/dL), along with increased neutrophil counts (white blood cells 11.29×10^3 /uL [reference 4–10×10^3 /uL], neutrophils 9.27×10^3 /uL [reference 2.8–5.5×10^3 /uL]), in line with changes attributable to pregnancy.

Investigations:

Ultrasound revealed two sizable, unilocular cystic lesions characterized by smooth borders and peripheral vascularization. The superior mass measured 18×12×7 cm, spanning from the left subphrenic area down to the uterine fundus. The inferior mass was 9.5×8×8 cm and appeared to be adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration (58×71 mm).

Serum tumour markers including CA 19–9, CA 15–3, CEA, β2-microglobulin, and LDH were all within reference ranges. CA-125, however, was elevated at 41.8 U/mL (normal <35 U/mL), and had risen from 36 U/mL measured two weeks previously, indicating a mild upward trend. Accordingly, an MRI examination without contrast was performed, revealing thick walls (1.4 cm), with T1 and T2 fat-saturated sequences showing inhomogeneous fluid within the masses as well as focal solid areas that were hyperintense on T1 and T2-weighted images (see figure 1; only T2 fat-saturated images are depicted). The caudal mass had indistinct boundaries and appeared to be continuous with the previously identified posterior leiomyoma on ultrasound. MRI found no evidence of lymphadenopathy.

Differential diagnosis:

On ultrasound assessment, the cystic lesions resembled two large decidualised endometriomas. The lower cyst appeared attached to an intramural-subserosal leiomyoma located on the posterior uterine wall, which measured 58×71 mm.

Magnetic resonance imaging revealed two spherical, unilocular adnexal cysts characterized by enlarged vessels and heterogeneous fluid content (see figure 1). The lower lesion appeared to be connected to a posterior uterine intramural-subserosal myoma displaying cystic degeneration, also measuring 58×71 mm.

Owing to the significant size of the pregnant uterus and the substantial masses present, neither a connecting stalk nor a nutrient vessel for the upper lesion could be visualized, leading to consideration of ovarian masses, predominantly endometriomas. This was further supported by the mild elevation in CA-125 observed during the second trimester (see discussion).

The imaging findings from ultrasound and MRI prompted a differential diagnosis of either decidualised endometriomas or cystic degenerating leiomyomas. Slightly raised CA-125 levels suggested the upper cyst was most consistent with an endometrioma, while anatomical continuity with uterine tissue indicated a myomatous origin for the caudal mass. The patient's shortness of breath was linked to physical compression from the large abdominal masses, with no evidence of respiratory or cardiac impairment.

Treatment:

Given the deterioration in the patient's clinical status (including abdominal pain associated with the masses and dyspnoea due to restrictive lung disease secondary to the mass size), the requirement for definitive diagnosis, concerns over possible complications (such as rupture, torsion, or infection), and anticipated uterine expansion with fetal growth, a laparotomy was performed at 21 weeks and 6 days gestation after obtaining informed consent, using a longitudinal xiphoid to pubic incision.

Initially, two samples of peritoneal fluid were obtained for microbiological and cytological analysis. The abdominal cavity was subsequently inspected, and fetal cardiac activity was confirmed.

Unexpectedly, the cranial mass was identified as a large, pedunculated, atypical cystic uterine leiomyoma with taut-elastic consistency and significant vascularity; it was initially twisted and leaking, potentially accounting for the patient's fever during hospitalization (refer to figure 2). The surgical team opted to remove the mass carefully from its attachment to the uterus with a double clamp (see figure 2). After dividing the pedicle, the exposed base was meticulously closed using a single absorbable suture. To minimise obstetric risk, the caudal mass, which originated from the uterine torus, was left in place; it was immobile, with a smooth surface and soft feel, resembling a large cystic intramural-subserosal leiomyoma.

The procedure lasted for 2 hours and 25 minutes. The patient was administered prophylactic progesterone, beginning with 100 mg intramuscularly during the operation, and continued at 100 mg intramuscularly daily until discharge. Afterwards, she received intramuscular hydroxyprogesterone caproate (341 mg, one ampule intramuscularly every 3 days as per the package insert) until scheduled delivery. Intraoperative antibiotic prophylaxis with cefazolin 2 g intravenously was followed by postoperative antibiotics (cefazolin 1 g intravenously three times daily plus clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily) during hospitalization, in accordance with urine culture results (Klebsiella pneumoniae highly sensitive to cephalosporins and clarithromycin).

Outcome and follow-up:

Following the surgical procedure, the patient's anaemia worsened (Hb 7.5 g/L, HCT 24.3%) and iron deficiency remained, necessitating intravenous iron therapy (ferric carboxymaltose 1 g) as well as two successful blood transfusions (post-transfusion Hb 10.0 g/L, HCT 30.4%). She was discharged in stable condition 10 days postoperatively and placed under close obstetrical surveillance every 3 weeks, during which fetal growth (86th percentile per WHO fetal growth chart calculator, https://srhr.org/fetalgrowthcalculator/#/) and amniotic fluid levels were monitored.

Analysis of the peritoneal fluid yielded negative results for both culture and cytology. Histopathological evaluation showed benign uterine leiomyomas and areas of ischemic necrosis adjacent to the twisted pedicle.

The position of the caudal myoma was anticipated to present significant impediment to fetal head descent during labor. As a result, an elective cesarean section was performed at the same incision site upon the fetus attaining 37 weeks gestational age, thereby reducing risk of uterine scar rupture, which led to an optimal neonatal outcome.

Currently, the patient experiences no symptoms attributable to the myoma, although the residual pelvic myoma has decreased slightly in size to 7.5 cm. She is breastfeeding successfully and has chosen not to schedule further surgical intervention at this time, citing partial postpartum involution of the remaining leiomyomas, absence of symptoms, and satisfaction with motherhood. The infant’s development remains favorable.

Discussion:

Myomas can often be misdiagnosed as adnexal masses: the incidence during pregnancy is 0.3–10.1% and 1–6%, respectively .13

In this instance, the patient’s initial assessment identified abdominal masses that resembled two large decidualized endometriomas, aside from the lower solid portion of the pelvic mass, which was suggestive of a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma with cystic degeneration measuring 58×71 mm, as detected on both ultrasound and MRI. Due to the lack of established management protocols for pelvic masses during pregnancy, the multidisciplinary team determined that elective surgical intervention might lower the likelihood of preterm birth.

Serum CA-125 levels in pregnancy typically exhibit two peaks: one during the first trimester (median 23 U/mL, p<0.00001) and a second during the third trimester (median 21 U/mL, p<0.001), both of which are higher compared to the second trimester values (median 14.0 U/mL). In contrast, the current case presented a CA-125 concentration of 41.8 U/mL, surpassing the median value seen in the second trimester.

CA-125 is predominantly utilized as a tumor marker for ovarian malignancy (with a common cut-off of ≥35 U/mL), particularly in women above 50 years old, in whom diagnostic accuracy—including area under the curve, positive predictive value, sensitivity, and specificity—is greater. Nevertheless, this marker may also be elevated in various benign conditions, such as endometriosis and adenomyosis (>19 U/mL), pelvic inflammatory disease, menstruation, both early and late pregnancy (median values of 23 U/mL and 21 U/mL, respectively), increased risk of miscarriage, and myomas (cut-off <19 U/mL).57

The main factors guiding clinical management included the detection of large, highly vascular masses exhibiting both solid and cystic features, which posed an immediate risk for complications such as rupture, torsion, or infection. Additionally, the patient's worsening symptoms—specifically pain and dyspnoea—the likelihood of further enlargement of the masses that could lead to hypoxia, necrosis, and degeneration, slight elevation in tumor markers (CA-125=41.8 U/mL), and physical limitation on the uterus's capacity for normal growth were all considered.

Because the origin of the masses was uncertain and a tumorous cause was possible, an MRI without contrast was performed following ultrasonography in order to improve diagnostic specificity. According to current guidelines, gadolinium-enhanced MRI is not recommended during pregnancy unless absolutely required for rheumatological, inflammatory, or infiltrative dermatological conditions, or when there is a risk of stillbirth or neonatal mortality. Thus, the multidisciplinary team opted for a non-contrast MRI and ensured that the scan did not exceed 1.5-T, which is regarded as sufficiently safe after the first trimester.

In this case, the multidisciplinary team—including surgeons, gynecologists, and obstetricians—decided to undertake a longitudinal laparotomy after acquiring specific written informed consent from the patient. During the operative evaluation, the superior mass demonstrated a pedicle, thereby posing a substantial risk for the development of an acute and characteristic complication. This observation underpinned the decision to restrict surgical intervention to an upper myomectomy. Additionally, ongoing and periodic transvaginal ultrasonography was used intraoperatively to assess fetal viability, and preventive progesterone was administered during the procedure.

A large multicenter trial by Zhao et al emphasizes the necessity of assessing and documenting fetal heart rate prior to, during, and after surgical intervention.

Typically, surgical treatment is postponed until after delivery, favoring medical management and observation. Nevertheless, a recent investigation by Sakinci et al at a tertiary care center indicated no statistically significant difference in preoperative or postoperative hemoglobin levels or blood transfusion requirements (p >0.05) between women undergoing caesarean section (CS) with concurrent myomectomy and those undergoing CS alone, without myomas. However, the group receiving CS myomectomy had significantly longer hospital stays and operative times (p = 0.001 and p = 0.001, respectively). The average size of the myomas was 8.3 ± 4.1 cm, and there was a statistically significant positive correlation between myoma size and length of hospitalization (p = 0.01).

The aforementioned study identified a statistically significant inverse relationship between myoma size and gestational age at delivery (p = 0.035).

Uterine leiomyomas demonstrate a non-linear progression in size throughout pregnancy and the postpartum period. Specifically, their size increases weekly by 6.90%: with a 6.12% increase (p<0.001) from the first to the second trimester and a 3.10%±0.39% increase from the second to the third trimester (p<0.05). Thus, the rate of leiomyoma growth is notably greater during the initial trimester than during later stages of pregnancy. Conversely, during the third trimester and the 6-week period following delivery, there is a marked reduction in myoma size (mean decrease 44.25%±36.05%, p<0.05).

These findings underscore the necessity of precise ultrasonographic assessment of the size, location, quantity, and proximity of uterine myomas to the placenta prior to surgical intervention.

For uterine myomas identified during pregnancy, conservative management is generally favored over surgical treatment because of the risk of significant hemorrhage, miscarriage, preterm labor, and uterine rupture. Nevertheless, myomectomy may be warranted in cases involving complicated masses, such as torsion of a pedunculated myoma, or persistent symptoms unresponsive to conservative measures. Surgery should be minimized in duration to support ongoing pregnancy, and can be performed laparoscopically, ideally using the open Hasson technique for patients in the first or early second trimester, with smaller myomas and those not classified as intramural. This method is associated with reduced maternal morbidity and improved likelihood of vaginal delivery. Despite this, the current case illustrates a prevalent selection bias favoring laparotomy over laparoscopy when confronting larger, multiple, intramural, or critically located myomas. Such factors may contribute to a greater incidence of cesarean section, particularly when a large myoma is adherent to the posterior wall of the uterine isthmus, potentially impeding vaginal delivery.
