Title: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

Rosalba De Nola, Emma Bassi, Cataldo Paletta, Mario Vicino

Abstract:

A woman in her 40s who had not previously given birth presented in her second trimester with complaints of abdominal pain and shortness of breath. Ultrasound imaging identified two sizable abdominal masses: one superior mass occupied the entire left subphrenic region and extended to the uterine fundus, while the inferior mass was located in the pouch of Douglas. The patient proceeded to laparotomy, during which the lower pelvic mass was left in place. She continued with standard obstetric monitoring and later underwent a scheduled caesarean section, with both maternal and neonatal outcomes being highly favorable. For pregnant patients with substantial symptomatic masses, thorough assessment—including bimanual examination and use of safe imaging techniques such as ultrasound and non-contrast MRI—is crucial. Surgical intervention should be limited to situations involving suspected malignancy or the development of clinical complications, with an emphasis on maintaining the pregnancy and preventing unnecessary treatment. The decision regarding delivery should be tailored based on the quantity, dimensions, and anatomical positions of uterine masses.

Background:

The evaluation and treatment of unusual or symptomatic abdominal masses in pregnancy poses substantial clinical difficulties. Diagnostic tools are limited to physical examination, ultrasound (US) with color Doppler, and non-contrast magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Preoperative US is especially useful for detecting symptomatic uterine leiomyomas located on the anterior aspect of the pregnant uterus, whether these are submucosal or pedunculated in nature. MRI, on the other hand, provides greater accuracy for diagnosing multiple, sizable, intramural, or posteriorly situated leiomyomas by allowing precise assessment of the separation between the myoma and the uterine cavity.

The utility of serum tumor markers during gestation remains controversial. CA-125, in particular, increases physiologically during the first trimester before returning to baseline levels. Consequently, a single measurement lacks diagnostic value, while serial monitoring may help track changes and assist with both differential diagnosis and ongoing follow-up. Successful management involves a meticulous multidisciplinary evaluation of risks, aiming to carefully weigh obstetric considerations against the infrequency and complexity inherent in these clinical presentations.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 40s, gravida 1 para 0 aborta 1, with obesity (body mass index of 33), presented to us during her second trimester of pregnancy at 21 weeks’ gestation. She reported sudden, intense abdominal pain localized to the left upper quadrant and pelvic region, scoring 5 on the Visual Analogue Scale at both locations, along with mild shortness of breath. Routine screening in both the first and second trimesters indicated a low risk of aneuploidy, as verified by non-invasive prenatal testing, and fetal morphology was found to be normal.

Initially, she presented to the emergency department for comprehensive multidisciplinary assessment. Her oxygen saturation was within normal limits (median 97%). On thoracic examination, heart rate and blood pressure were both normal (median values of 74 bpm and 100/60 mm Hg, respectively). The ECG demonstrated no abnormalities. Subsequently, she was admitted to the Obstetrical and Gynaecological Division. Examination revealed the abdomen appeared more distended than anticipated for her gestational age, characterized by a tender, swelling, taut-elastic mass measuring 18 cm that occupied the entire upper left quadrant; there was no clinical evidence of ascites.

The bimanual vaginal examination showed the presence of a fixed mass in the lesser pelvis of about 9 cm, rounded and hard, that partially bulged at the posterior fornix.

Pain management consisted of intravenous paracetamol at a dose of 1000 mg administered twice daily, combined with positioning the patient in a semi-orthostatic posture, which was thought to alleviate diaphragmatic compression from both the gravid uterus and sizable abdominal masses. These interventions modestly improved symptoms in the absence of supplemental oxygen or ventilatory assistance, decreasing pain from a score of 5 to 1 based on the Visual Analogue Scale.

While hospitalized, the patient experienced a mild elevation in temperature. Consequently, a nasopharyngeal swab for PCR detection of severe acute respiratory syndrome Coronavirus 2 was conducted and returned negative. Conversely, urine cultures identified a Klebsiella pneumoniae infection. Laboratory assessment revealed iron deficiency anemia with a hemoglobin level of 9.9 g/L (normal range 12.0–16.6 g/L) and serum ferritin measuring 25 mcg/L (normal range 15–300 mcg/L). There was a mild elevation in C-reactive protein to 1.39 mg/dL (normal range 0–0.3 mg/dL) and an increased neutrophil count, with total white blood cells at 11.29×10^3 /uL (normal range 4–10×10^3 /uL) and neutrophils at 9.27×10^3 /uL (normal range 2.8–5.5×10^3 /uL), reflecting changes commonly observed in pregnancy.

Investigations:

Ultrasonography revealed two sizable, unilocular cystic lesions characterized by smooth margins and peripheral blood flow. The superior lesion measured 18×12×7 cm, reaching from the left subphrenic area down to the uterine fundus. The inferior lesion had dimensions of 9.5×8×8 cm and was contiguous with a posterior intramural-subserosal fibroid exhibiting cystic degeneration (58×71 mm).

Assessment of tumor markers—including CA 19–9, CA 15–3, CEA, β2-microglobulin, and LDH—demonstrated values within their respective reference ranges; however, CA-125 was found to be elevated at 41.8 U/mL (normal <35 U/mL), and this elevation had progressed from 36 U/mL over a 2-week period, indicating a mildly rising trend. Consequently, an MRI scan without contrast was performed, demonstrating thickened walls (1.4 cm), with T1- and T2 fat-saturated sequences revealing inhomogeneous fluid within the masses and discrete focal areas of T1-T2 weighted hyperintensity corresponding to solid elements (see figure 1; only T2 fat-saturated images displayed). The lower mass could not be distinctly separated, appearing continuous with the previously identified posterior leiomyoma on ultrasound assessment. No lymphadenopathy was present on MRI.

Differential diagnosis:

On ultrasound examination, the cystic lesions resembled two large decidualized endometriomas, with the lower cyst appearing attached to an intramural-subserosal leiomyoma on the posterior uterine wall measuring 58×71 mm.

Magnetic resonance imaging revealed two spherical, unilocular cysts in the adnexa, characterized by prominent blood vessels and heterogeneous internal fluid (refer to figure 1). The caudal lesion appeared to be adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration, measuring 58×71 mm.

Because the substantial size of both the gravid uterus and the large masses obscured anatomical details, no connecting stalk or tumor-feeding vessel was visualized for the upper mass. This finding, along with mildly increased CA-125 levels in the second trimester, favored an ovarian origin, particularly endometriomas (refer to discussion).

Imaging by ultrasound and MRI led to consideration of decidualised endometriomas versus cystic degeneration of leiomyomas. The mild elevation of CA-125 supported the possibility of endometrioma for the upper cystic lesion; however, the caudal mass was seen to be continuous with uterine tissue, making a myomatous lesion more likely in that location. The patient's dyspnea was believed to result from mass effect due to the sizeable intra-abdominal tumors, as no cardiac or pulmonary impairment was detected.

Treatment:

Given the progression of her symptoms—including abdominal discomfort attributable to the masses and dyspnoea linked to restrictive lung disease from the masses’ dimensions—as well as the need for definitive diagnostic clarification, the potential for adverse events such as rupture, torsion, or infection, and the anticipated further increase in uterine size with fetal development, the patient underwent an open abdominal procedure via a longitudinal xiphoid-to-pubic incision at 21 weeks and 6 days of gestation, following informed consent.

First, we collected two samples of the free peritoneal fluid for cultural and cytological purposes. Then, we explored the abdominal cavity, verifying the presence of fetal cardiac activity.

Unexpectedly, the cranial mass was identified as a large pedunculated, atypical cystic uterine leiomyoma exhibiting a tense, elastic consistency and marked vascularity. The mass demonstrated evidence of initial torsion and leakage, which likely explained the patient's febrile episodes during hospitalization (refer to figure 2). Consequently, the surgical team elected to remove the mass from its uterine base by careful double-clamp technique (see figure 2). Following division of the stalk, the exposed base was meticulously closed with a single absorbable suture. The caudal mass, which arose from the uterine torus, was retained in order to reduce obstetric risk. This mass was immobile and exhibited a smooth exterior with a soft consistency, resembling a sizable cystic intramural-subserosal leiomyoma.

The surgical procedure took 2 hours and 25 minutes to complete. The patient was administered progesterone prophylaxis at a dose of 100 mg intramuscularly initiated during surgery, with this regimen maintained at 100 mg intramuscularly daily until hospital discharge. Following discharge, intramuscular hydroxyprogesterone caproate was given at 341 mg (one ampoule) every three days, as per the instructions provided in the package leaflet, up to the time of planned delivery. For infection prevention, the patient received intraoperative cefazolin 2 g intravenously, succeeded by a postoperative course of cefazolin 1 g intravenously three times daily, combined with clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily throughout hospitalization. This antimicrobial therapy was selected based on the urine culture antibiogram, which showed Klebsiella pneumoniae that was highly susceptible to both cephalosporins and clarithromycin.

Outcome and follow-up:

Postoperatively, the patient's anaemia continued to deteriorate (Hb 7.5 g/L, HCT 24.3%) and iron deficiency persisted, necessitating administration of intravenous ferric carboxymaltose (1 g) and two units of blood, resulting in improved haemoglobin (Hb 10.0 g/L) and haematocrit (HCT 30.4%). The patient experienced an uncomplicated recovery and was discharged 10 days following the operation, subsequently undergoing close obstetric monitoring every 3 weeks. Assessments indicated fetal growth at the 86th percentile per the WHO fetal growth chart calculator (accessible at https://srhr.org/fetalgrowthcalculator/#/) and tracking of the amniotic fluid index.

Both culture and cytology of the peritoneal fluid returned negative results. Histopathological analysis identified benign uterine leiomyomas, with regions of ischemic necrosis found adjacent to the site of the twisted pedicle.

The position of the caudal myoma would likely have created a significant barrier to the fetal head’s descent during the second stage of labor. Therefore, a planned caesarean section was scheduled at 37 weeks' gestation, utilizing the same surgical incision, to reduce the likelihood of uterine scar rupture and resulting in a favorable neonatal outcome.

The patient remains asymptomatic regarding her myoma, despite a minor decrease in the residual pelvic myoma size to 7.5 cm. She is successfully breastfeeding, but, due to the partial postpartum reduction in her remaining leiomyomas, lack of symptoms, and her satisfaction with motherhood, she chooses not to proceed with further surgical intervention at this time. Her baby is developing appropriately.

Discussion:

Myomas can often be misdiagnosed as adnexal masses: the incidence during pregnancy is 0.3–10.1% and 1–6%, respectively .13

In this case, the primary assessment revealed abdominal masses resembling two large decidualized endometriomas. Notably, the lower solid portion of the pelvic mass resembled a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine fibroid with cystic degeneration measuring 58×71 mm, as identified on both ultrasound and MRI. Since there are no established protocols for managing pelvic masses during pregnancy, the multidisciplinary team determined that planned surgical intervention might lower the risk of preterm birth.

Throughout pregnancy, serum CA-125 typically exhibits two peak levels: the first occurs during the initial trimester (median 23 U/mL, p<0.00001), and the second during the third trimester (median 21 U/mL, p<0.001), both surpassing levels measured in the second trimester (median 14.0 U/mL). In contrast, the current case demonstrated a CA-125 measurement of 41.8 U/mL during the second trimester, which exceeds the reported median for this gestational period.

CA-125 primarily serves as a tumor marker for ovarian cancer, with the current standard threshold set at ≥35 U/mL. Its diagnostic accuracy—including area under the curve, positive predictive value, sensitivity, and specificity—is particularly greater in women over 50 years of age. However, elevated CA-125 levels are also associated with various benign conditions such as endometriosis and adenomyosis (often >19 U/mL), pelvic inflammatory disease, menstruation, early and late pregnancy (median values of 23 and 21 U/mL, respectively), increased miscarriage risk, and myomas (typically with a cut-off below 19 U/mL).57

The main factors guiding the clinical management included the identification of large, highly vascularized masses exhibiting both solid and cystic features, which posed an immediate risk for complications such as rupture, torsion, or infection. Additional considerations included the patient's deteriorating symptoms (increasing pain and dyspnea), the potential for tumor enlargement that could elevate the likelihood of hypoxia, necrosis, and degeneration, marginal elevation of tumor markers (CA-125 at 41.8 U/mL), as well as limited intra-abdominal space that could impede further normal uterine growth.

Given the potential neoplastic nature and indeterminate cause of the masses, an MRI without contrast was performed following the ultrasound to improve diagnostic clarity. Based on current guidelines, the use of gadolinium-enhanced MRI is not advised during pregnancy unless there are compelling indications, such as for certain rheumatologic, inflammatory, or infiltrative dermatological disorders, or in situations where there is a risk of stillbirth or neonatal mortality. Consequently, the multidisciplinary care team proceeded with a non-contrast MRI, taking care not to exceed a 1.5-Tesla magnetic field, which is generally regarded as safe beyond the first trimester.

In this case, following specific written informed consent from the patient, the multidisciplinary team—including surgeons, gynecologists, and obstetricians—chose to perform an open longitudinal laparotomy. Intraoperatively, the upper lesion displayed a pedicle, indicating a high potential for an acute complication typical of such presentations. This risk justified restricting the intervention to an upper myomectomy alone. Additionally, serial and periodic transvaginal ultrasound examinations were utilized intraoperatively to monitor fetal viability, complemented by prophylactic intraoperative progesterone administration.

According to a large multicentric study conducted by Zhao et al, it is mandatory to check and document the fetal heart rate before, during and after the surgery.

Typically, surgical intervention is postponed until after delivery, with preference given to conservative and expectant medical management. Nevertheless, Sakinci et al recently reported a study conducted at a tertiary center, demonstrating no statistically significant differences between women undergoing caesarean section (CS) with simultaneous myomectomy and those without myoma who had CS alone regarding preoperative and postoperative hemoglobin levels or the need for blood transfusion (p >0.05). In contrast, both hospitalization and operative times were notably longer for those undergoing CS with myomectomy (p =0.001 and p =0.001, respectively). The average size of myomas was 8.3 ± 4.1 cm, and a significant positive correlation was found between myoma size and length of hospital stay (p =0.01).

The same research study also showed a statistically significant and inverse correlation between the size of the myoma and the delivery week (p = 0.035).

Uterine leiomyomas exhibit a non-linear pattern of growth in both pregnancy and the puerperium. Specifically, their dimensions expand by 6.90% per week, with an increase of 6.12% (p<0.001) occurring from the first to the second trimester, and a rise of 3.10%±0.39% noted between the second and third trimesters (p<0.05). This indicates that the growth rate of uterine leiomyomas is markedly greater in the first trimester compared to later gestational periods. Conversely, there is a statistically significant reduction in myoma size during the third trimester and at 6 weeks postpartum, with an average decrease of 44.25%±36.05% (p<0.05).

These papers showed the importance of an accurate US examination of the size, location, number and distance of uterine myomas from the placenta before the surgical approach.

For uterine myomas identified during pregnancy, conservative management is typically favored over surgical intervention because of the potential risks, such as significant hemorrhage, loss of pregnancy, preterm labor, and uterine rupture. Nonetheless, myomectomy may be warranted when the myoma is complicated—such as in cases of torsion of a pedunculated fibroid—or when symptoms persist despite conservative measures. In such scenarios, surgical intervention should aim to minimize operative time to support ongoing pregnancy, and may be carried out laparoscopically, particularly using the open Hasson entry technique for patients in the first or early second trimester, with smaller lesions, and when the myoma is not intramural. This minimally invasive approach offers advantages in terms of lower maternal morbidity and a higher likelihood of vaginal delivery. Despite this, the current case illustrates a frequent selection bias, whereby laparotomy is chosen over laparoscopy for larger, multiple, intramural, or critically positioned myomas, as these characteristics are linked with increased cesarean section rates. Specifically, the large myoma adherent to the posterior wall at the uterine isthmus in this case would likely have obstructed the passage for vaginal delivery.
