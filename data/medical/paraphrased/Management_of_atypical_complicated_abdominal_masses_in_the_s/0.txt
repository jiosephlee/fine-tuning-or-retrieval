Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

Rosalba De Nola, Emma Bassi, Cataldo Paletta, Mario Vicino

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her 40s with no prior pregnancies presented in her second trimester with complaints of abdominal discomfort and shortness of breath. Ultrasonographic assessment identified two sizable masses within the abdomen: one, situated superiorly, filled the entire left subphrenic region and extended down to the uterine fundus, while the other was located inferiorly, occupying the pouch of Douglas. The patient was treated surgically via laparotomy, leaving the lower pelvic mass undisturbed. She was subsequently monitored through routine obstetric assessments and ultimately underwent a planned caesarean section, yielding favorable maternal and neonatal outcomes. In instances where pregnant patients present with large, symptomatic masses, thorough assessment—including bimanual examination and safe imaging options such as ultrasonography and non-contrast MRI—is crucial. Surgical intervention should be reserved for cases with indications suggestive of malignancy or the development of clinical complications, with an emphasis on maintaining pregnancy and preventing unnecessary procedures. The chosen delivery method should be individualized, taking into account the size, quantity, and precise anatomical distribution of the uterine masses.

Background:

The identification and treatment of unusual or symptomatic abdominal masses in pregnant patients constitute notable clinical difficulties. The available diagnostic modalities are restricted to physical examination, ultrasound (US) enhanced with colour Doppler, and non-contrast MRI. Preoperative US serves an essential function in detecting symptomatic uterine leiomyomas, particularly those situated on the anterior wall of the pregnant uterus, including submucosal and pedunculated variants. In comparison, MRI is more effective for evaluating cases with numerous, sizeable, intramural, or posterior leiomyomas, as it offers high precision in mapping the spatial relationship between a myoma and the uterine cavity.

The utility of serum tumour markers during gestation is still contentious. For instance, CA-125 concentrations typically increase physiologically in the first trimester and return to baseline afterwards. Consequently, single-point measurements have limited diagnostic value, whereas repeated assessments over time may help elucidate diagnostic patterns and support longitudinal monitoring. Comprehensive management demands a multidisciplinary approach to carefully weigh obstetric considerations against the uncommon and intricate nature of these cases.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 40s, classified as obese with a body mass index of 33 and obstetric history of G1P0A1, was evaluated in her second trimester (21 weeks gestation) due to intense, sudden-onset pain in the upper left abdominal quadrant and pelvic region, both registering a score of 5 on the Visual Analogue Scale, alongside mild shortness of breath. Her previous first- and second-trimester screening assessments indicated a low likelihood of aneuploidies, as verified by non-invasive prenatal testing, and no abnormalities in fetal morphology were detected.

Upon presentation, she was initially admitted to the emergency department for comprehensive multidisciplinary assessment. Oxygen saturation was within normal range (median 97%), with physical examination indicating a stable heart rate (median 74 bpm) and blood pressure (median 100/60 mm Hg). Her ECG findings were unremarkable. She was subsequently moved to the Obstetrical and Gynaecological Division. Abdominal inspection revealed significant distension exceeding what would be anticipated for the gestational age, with palpable swelling and a firm, elastic mass occupying the entire upper left quadrant, measuring 18 cm; there were no clinical indicators of ascites.

Bimanual vaginal examination identified a fixed, approximately 9 cm rounded and solid mass in the lesser pelvis, partially protruding into the posterior fornix.

The patient was managed with intravenous paracetamol (1000 mg twice daily) and maintained in a semi-orthostatic posture, aiming to reduce diaphragmatic pressure exerted by both the gravid uterus and large intra-abdominal masses. This approach led to a modest reduction in symptoms, lowering the pain score from 5 to 1 on the Visual Analogue Scale, without need for supplemental oxygen or ventilatory assistance.

During her hospitalisation, she developed a low-grade fever. Testing for severe acute respiratory syndrome Coronavirus 2 via nasopharyngeal PCR was negative, but urinary cultures identified a Klebsiella pneumoniae infection. Hematological investigations revealed iron deficiency anaemia, with haemoglobin measured at 9.9 g/L (reference range 12.0–16.6 g/L) and serum ferritin at 25 mcg/L (reference 15–300 mcg/L). There was a mild elevation in C reactive protein (1.39 mg/dL, reference 0–0.3 mg/dL), as well as increased neutrophil counts (white blood cells 11.29×10³ /uL [normal 4–10×10³ /uL], neutrophils 9.27×10³ /uL [normal 2.8–5.5×10³ /uL]), consistent with expected physiological changes of pregnancy.

Investigations:

Ultrasound assessment revealed two sizable, unilocular cystic lesions, each displaying well-defined borders and peripheral vascularity. The superior lesion measured 18×12×7 cm, stretching from the left subphrenic area down to the uterine fundus. The inferior lesion measured 9.5×8×8 cm and appeared attached to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration (58×71 mm).

Serological tests for tumour markers (CA 19–9, CA 15–3, CEA, β2-microglobulin, and LDH) all yielded results within the reference range; however, CA-125 was found to be elevated (41.8 U/mL; reference <35 U/mL), and had risen from 36 U/mL recorded two weeks earlier, thus demonstrating a mild upward trend. Subsequently, the patient underwent MRI without the use of contrast agents, which identified thickened cyst walls (1.4 cm), T1- and T2-fat-saturated sequences with inhomogeneous fluid characteristics, and discrete solid regions showing hyperintensity on both T1- and T2-weighted images (refer to figure 1; only T2 fat-sat sequences provided). The demarcation of the caudal lesion was indistinct, and it appeared to be contiguous with the posterior leiomyoma already identified during ultrasound. No lymphadenopathy was observed on MRI.

Differential diagnosis:

During the ultrasound examination, the cystic lesions appeared similar to two large decidualised endometriomas. The lower cyst was observed to be adherent to a posterior uterine wall intramural-subserosal leiomyoma, which measured 58×71 mm.

MRI identified two well-defined, unilocular adnexal cysts featuring hypertrophied vessels and heterogeneous internal fluid (refer to figure 1). The lower lesion appeared attached to a posterior uterine intramural-subserosal myoma displaying cystic degeneration, also measuring 58×71 mm.

Owing to the considerable size of the gravid uterus and the voluminous cystic masses, a distinct connecting stalk or vascular pedicle feeding the upper lesion could not be visualised. This finding, along with mildly increased CA-125 levels in the second trimester, suggested an ovarian origin—most likely endometriomas (see discussion).

The combination of sonographic and MRI observations raised differential diagnostic considerations of decidualised endometriomas versus cystic degeneration of leiomyomas. A modest rise in CA-125 supported an endometrioma diagnosis for the upper cystic mass, while the caudal lesion’s anatomical continuity with uterine tissue was more indicative of a myomatous lesion. The patient’s dyspnoea was ascribed to the compressive effect of the extensive abdominal masses, in the absence of any signs pointing toward pulmonary or cardiac involvement.

Treatment:

Owing to the progressive decline in the patient's clinical status—characterized by abdominal pain associated with mass effect and dyspnoea related to restrictive lung pathology secondary to the size of the abdominal tumors—together with the necessity for definitive diagnosis, potential complication risks (including rupture, torsion, and infection), and anticipated uterine expansion with ongoing fetal growth, a decision was made to proceed with laparotomy. This was performed via a longitudinal xiphos-pubic incision at 21 w+6 d following the acquisition of informed consent.

Initially, two aliquots of free peritoneal fluid were obtained for both cytological assessment and microbial culture. Subsequent exploration of the abdominal cavity was conducted, during which fetal cardiac activity was confirmed.

Unexpectedly, the cranial mass was identified as an extremely large, pedunculated, atypically cystic, and taut-elastic uterine leiomyoma exhibiting marked vascularity. The mass was also initially twisted and demonstrated evidence of leakage, likely accounting for the febrile episodes observed during the hospital admission (refer to figure 2). Consequently, the surgical team proceeded to carefully detach the mass from its uterine base utilizing a double-clamp technique (see figure 2). Following division of the pedicle, the exposed origin was securely ligated with a single absorbable suture. In order to limit obstetric risk, the caudal mass, which originated from the uterine torus, was left in place. This lesion was observed to be fixed, with a smooth contour and a soft, cystic, intramural-subserosal leiomyoma-like consistency.

The entire procedure was completed in 2 hours and 25 minutes. The patient received intraoperative progesterone prophylaxis (100 mg intramuscularly), which was continued postoperatively at a dosage of 100 mg intramuscularly per day until hospital discharge. Afterward, intramuscular hydroxyprogesterone caproate (341 mg 1 fl every 3 days, as indicated in the package insert) was administered until planned delivery. Intraoperative prophylaxis with cefazolin (2 g intravenously) was followed by postoperative antibiotic management with cefazolin (1 g intravenously three times daily) in conjunction with clarithromycin (500 mg twice daily) throughout the hospital stay, in accordance with urine culture antibiogram findings (Klebsiella pneumoniae, highly susceptible to cephalosporins and clarithromycin).

Outcome and follow-up:

Following the surgical intervention, the patient’s anaemia worsened (Hb 7.5 g/L, HCT 24.3%), and iron deficiency persisted, necessitating intravenous administration of ferric carboxymaltose (1 g) in addition to two blood transfusions. These measures resulted in improved haematological parameters (Hb 10.0 g/L, HCT 30.4%). She was discharged in stable condition 10 days post-operation, and was subsequently enrolled in an obstetric surveillance program with visits every 3 weeks. Serial assessments demonstrated sustained fetal growth (86th percentile per the WHO fetal growth chart calculator: https://srhr.org/fetalgrowthcalculator/#/) as well as monitoring of the amniotic fluid index.

Analysis of the peritoneal fluid yielded negative results for both microbial cultures and cytological assessment. Histopathological examination confirmed benign uterine leiomyomas with evidence of ischaemic necrosis adjacent to the site of pedicle torsion.

The location of the caudal myoma posed a significant potential impediment to the descent of the fetal head during the second stage of labour. Therefore, once the fetus reached 37 weeks gestation, a planned caesarean section was conducted at the site of the prior uterine incision to reduce the likelihood of uterine scar rupture, resulting in a favourable neonatal outcome.

At present, the patient remains asymptomatic in relation to the myoma, though the residual pelvic leiomyoma has decreased marginally to 7.5 cm in diameter. She is breastfeeding without difficulty, and prefers not to schedule additional surgery at this time, owing to partial regression of the remaining leiomyomas postpartum, lack of symptoms, and the fulfilment of her maternal aspirations. The infant is also exhibiting normal growth and development.

Discussion:

Myomas can often be misdiagnosed as adnexal masses: the incidence during pregnancy is 0.3–10.1% and 1–6%, respectively .13

In this case, initial assessments identified large abdominal masses resembling two significantly decidualised endometriomas. Notably, the lower solid component of the pelvic mass was suggestive of a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma of the uterus, exhibiting cystic degeneration and measuring 58×71 mm, as observed on both US and MRI scans. Given the lack of established treatment protocols for pelvic masses during pregnancy, the multidisciplinary team judged that elective surgical intervention might help reduce the likelihood of preterm labor.

Throughout pregnancy, serum CA-125 typically demonstrates two peak values: the first during the initial trimester (median 23 U/mL, p<0.00001) and the second in the third trimester (median 21 U/mL, p<0.001), in comparison with the lower median observed in the second trimester (median 14.0 U/mL). In contrast, the current case revealed a CA-125 concentration of 41.8 U/mL, markedly exceeding the median for the second trimester.

CA-125 is predominantly employed as a tumor marker in the context of ovarian cancer (with the conventional threshold set at ≥35 U/mL), most notably in women over 50, where diagnostic accuracy is heightened (demonstrated by improved area under the curve, positive predictive value, sensitivity, and specificity). Nevertheless, elevated CA-125 levels may also be associated with several non-malignant conditions, including endometriosis and adenomyosis (often >19 U/mL), pelvic inflammatory disease, menstruation, early and late-stage pregnancy (median 23 and 21 U/mL, respectively), miscarriage risk, as well as myomas (typically cut-off <19 U/mL).57

The principal factors guiding clinical management included the identification of large, highly vascularized masses featuring both solid and cystic elements, which presented a tangible risk for complications such as rupture, torsion, or infection; the patient’s deteriorating clinical state, characterized by increased pain and shortness of breath; the potential for continued mass enlargement, potentially increasing the likelihood of hypoxia, necrosis, and tissue degeneration; mildly elevated oncological markers (CA-125=41.8 U/mL); and spatial constraints that could impede further normal uterine expansion.

Given the suspicion of neoplasia and the indeterminate etiology of the masses, the patient subsequently underwent a non-contrast MRI following the initial ultrasound to enhance diagnostic precision. In line with current guidelines, gadolinium-contrast MRI is generally contraindicated in pregnancy except under strict indications such as severe rheumatologic, inflammatory, or infiltrative dermatologic diseases, or in cases where there is significant risk of stillbirth or neonatal mortality. As such, the multidisciplinary team opted for a non-contrast MRI, adhering to the use of a 1.5-Tesla machine or lower, which is considered acceptably safe after the first trimester.

In this case, the multidisciplinary team—including surgeons, gynecologists, and obstetricians—chose to undertake a longitudinal laparotomic surgical intervention, having first secured written informed consent from the patient. During surgery, the uppermost lesion was found to possess a pedicle, thereby increasing the likelihood of a sudden, conventional complication. This finding justified restricting the surgical procedure to an upper myomectomy. Additionally, serial and periodic transvaginal ultrasound examinations were employed to assess fetal viability during the operation, complemented by the prophylactic intraoperative administration of progesterone.

Zhao et al, in an extensive multicenter study, emphasized that documenting and monitoring the fetal heart rate before, during, and following surgery is imperative.

Typically, surgical intervention is deferred until after delivery, with preference given to conservative medical management. Nevertheless, a recent investigation at a tertiary center by Sakinci et al reported no statistically significant differences between women undergoing combined caesarean section (CS) and myomectomy versus those without myomas receiving only CS, in terms of preoperative and postoperative hemoglobin levels or rates of blood transfusion ($p > 0.05$). However, both hospitalization and surgical time were notably prolonged in patients receiving CS myomectomy ($p = 0.001$ and $p = 0.001$, respectively). The average myoma measured $8.3 \pm 4.1$ cm, and there was a significant positive association between myoma size and length of hospital stay ($p = 0.01$).

The same study identified a statistically significant negative correlation between myoma size and the gestational age at delivery ($p = 0.035$).

Uterine leiomyomas exhibit a non-linear pattern of size fluctuations throughout pregnancy and the puerperium. Specifically, leiomyoma size increases by 6.90% per week, with a growth of 6.12% ($p < 0.001$) between the first and second trimesters, and 3.10% ± 0.39% between the second and third trimesters ($p < 0.05$). Consequently, the expansion rate of uterine leiomyomas is markedly greater in the first trimester compared to later stages of gestation. Conversely, a significant reduction in myoma size occurs during the third trimester and persists 6 weeks postpartum (mean decrease of 44.25% ± 36.05%, $p < 0.05$).

These publications underscore the necessity for thorough ultrasonographic assessment prior to surgical intervention, specifically evaluating myoma size, anatomical location, quantity, and proximity to the placenta.

When uterine myomas are detected during pregnancy, management is predominantly conservative due to potential complications such as significant hemorrhage, pregnancy loss, preterm delivery, and uterine rupture associated with surgical intervention. Nevertheless, myomectomy may become warranted in instances of complex masses, for example, when torsion of a pedunculated myoma occurs or when symptoms persist despite conservative therapy. In such cases, surgical intervention should be as brief as possible to safeguard the ongoing pregnancy, and may be accomplished via laparoscopy—preferably employing the open Hasson technique in cases of early gestation (first or early second trimester), smaller myomas, and those located away from the uterine wall (non-intramural). This minimally invasive approach is associated with improved maternal outcomes and greater likelihood of vaginal delivery. The present case, however, illustrates a typical selection bias favoring laparotomy rather than laparoscopy for patients with larger, multiple, intramural, or critically situated myomas, circumstances often linked to increased caesarean section rates because a large myoma adherent to the posterior isthmic region of the uterus can obstruct vaginal delivery.
