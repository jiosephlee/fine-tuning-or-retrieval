Title: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

Rosalba De Nola, Emma Bassi, Cataldo Paletta, Mario Vicino

Abstract:

A woman in her 40s who had not previously given birth presented in her second trimester with abdominal discomfort and shortness of breath. Ultrasound imaging identified two sizable masses within the abdomen: the superior mass filled the entire left subphrenic region and extended toward the uterine fundus, while the inferior mass was situated in the pouch of Douglas. Laparotomy was performed, leaving the pelvic lesion undisturbed. Following this, she received routine prenatal care and delivered by planned caesarean section, achieving favorable outcomes for both mother and child. When encountering large symptomatic masses during pregnancy, thorough assessment via bimanual examination and safe imaging techniques such as ultrasound and non-contrast MRI is vital. Surgical intervention should be reserved for cases with suspected malignancy or clinical complications, with careful attention to preserving the pregnancy and preventing unnecessary treatment. Decisions regarding delivery should take into account the dimensions, quantity, and anatomical placement of uterine masses.

Background:

Diagnosing and managing atypical or symptomatic abdominal masses in pregnant patients pose notable clinical difficulties. The diagnostic approach is generally restricted to physical examination, ultrasound (US) with colour Doppler, and non-contrast magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Preoperative US is particularly effective in detecting symptomatic uterine leiomyomas along the anterior uterine wall of a gravid uterus, including those that are submucosal or pedunculated. For identifying multiple, large, intramural, or posterior leiomyomas, MRI provides greater diagnostic precision by accurately measuring the separation between the fibroid and the uterine cavity.

The utility of serum tumour markers during gestation is still controversial. Specifically, CA-125 concentrations tend to physiologically increase during the first trimester and then return to baseline. Thus, single assessments are not definitive for diagnosis, whereas monitoring levels over time may assist in distinguishing between causes and in patient surveillance. Managing these cases optimally necessitates a thorough multidisciplinary evaluation of risks to appropriately weigh obstetric considerations against the uncommon and complex nature of these presentations.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 40s with obesity (body mass index 33), gravida 1 para 0 abortus 1, presented at 21 weeks gestation, during her second trimester, due to intense and sudden abdominal discomfort in the upper left quadrant and pelvis (Visual Analogue Scale score of 5 at both sites) accompanied by mild shortness of breath. Routine screening in the first and second trimesters had demonstrated a low probability for aneuploidy, as confirmed by non-invasive prenatal testing, with normal fetal structural findings.

Upon arrival to the emergency department, a comprehensive multidisciplinary assessment was performed. Her oxygen saturation was within normal limits (median value 97%). Chest examination showed a heart rate of 74 beats per minute and blood pressure of 100/60 mm Hg (median values), both normal, and the ECG was unremarkable. Subsequently, she was admitted to the Obstetrics and Gynecology unit. Her abdominal girth was greater than anticipated for her gestational stage, with a noticeable tender, firm, elastic mass encompassing the upper left quadrant, measuring 18 cm, and no clinical evidence of ascites.

Pelvic examination by bimanual technique revealed a fixed, solid, rounded mass approximately 9 cm in size within the lesser pelvis, protruding partially into the posterior fornix.

Pain management consisted of intravenous paracetamol 1000 mg administered twice daily and positioning in a semi-upright manner, aimed at decreasing diaphragmatic compression from the gravid uterus and sizable abdominal masses. These interventions resulted in modest symptom relief, without the need for supplemental oxygen or respiratory support, reducing the pain score from 5 to 1 according to the Visual Analogue Scale.

During her inpatient period, she developed a mild fever. PCR testing via nasopharyngeal swab for severe acute respiratory syndrome Coronavirus 2 was negative; however, a urine culture was positive for Klebsiella pneumoniae infection. Laboratory evaluation revealed iron deficiency anemia with haemoglobin 9.9 g/L (normal range 12.0–16.6 g/L) and serum ferritin 25 mcg/L (normal range 15–300 mcg/L). There was a slight elevation of C-reactive protein (1.39 mg/dL, normal range 0–0.3 mg/dL), and increased neutrophils (total white blood cells 11.29×10^3/uL [normal range 4–10×10^3/uL], neutrophils 9.27×10^3/uL [normal range 2.8–5.5×10^3/uL]), findings compatible with physiological changes seen in pregnancy.

Investigations:

Ultrasound revealed two sizable, unilocular cystic lesions exhibiting smooth borders and vascularization at their periphery. The superior lesion was 18×12×7 cm, spanning from the left subphrenic area down to the uterine fundus. The inferior lesion measured 9.5×8×8 cm and appeared attached to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma demonstrating cystic degeneration (58×71 mm).

Tumour marker levels—including CA 19–9, CA 15–3, CEA, β2-microglobulin, and LDH—were within standard ranges; however, CA-125 was raised at 41.8 U/mL (reference <35 U/mL), reflecting a gradual rise from 36 U/mL documented two weeks earlier. This indicated a mild upward trend in the marker’s serum levels. Subsequently, the patient underwent a contrast-free MRI, which depicted thickened walls (1.4 cm), inhomogeneous fluid on T1 and T2 fat-saturated sequences, and discrete hyperintense T1-T2 weighted solid portions (see figure 1; only T2 fat-sat images are shown). The delineation of the lower mass was unclear, and it appeared to merge with the posterior leiomyoma previously visualized by ultrasound. MRI did not reveal any lymphadenopathy.

Differential diagnosis:

During ultrasonography, the cystic lesions resembled two large decidualised endometriomas; additionally, the lower cyst appeared attached to an intramural-subserosal leiomyoma located on the posterior uterine wall, which measured 58×71 mm.

MRI identified two rounded, unilocular adnexal cysts featuring enlarged vessels and heterogenous fluid signal (refer to figure 1). The inferior lesion was apparently adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration and measuring 58×71 mm.

The substantial size of both the gravid uterus and the abdominal masses prevented identification of a connecting stalk or any feeding vessel to the upper lesion, steering the diagnosis toward ovarian masses, specifically endometriomas, given a mild elevation of CA-125 observed during the second trimester (see discussion).

Imaging findings from ultrasound and MRI raised the possibility of either decidualised endometriomas or cystically degenerating leiomyomas. The moderate rise in CA-125 supported an endometrioma etiology for the upper cyst, while the lower mass’s direct association with uterine tissue pointed toward a myomatous source. The patient’s dyspnoea was ascribed to compressive effects of the bulky abdominal formations; no pulmonary or cardiac abnormalities were detected.

Treatment:

Given the progression of clinical symptoms—namely, abdominal pain from the masses, dyspnoea attributable to restrictive lung disease from the size of the abdominal tumors—and the imperative for definitive diagnosis, as well as the risk of complications such as rupture, torsion, and infection, coupled with the expectation of uterine expansion with fetal growth, the patient consented to and underwent a laparotomy using a longitudinal xiphoid-to-pubic incision at 21 weeks and 6 days gestation.

Initially, two peritoneal fluid samples were collected for microbiological and cytological analysis. The surgical team then assessed the abdominal cavity, confirming ongoing fetal cardiac activity.

Unexpectedly, the upper mass was identified as a markedly enlarged pedunculated uterine leiomyoma with atypical cystic features and a firm, tense-elastic consistency. It was highly vascular, showing early signs of torsion and leakage, which likely accounted for the patient's in-hospital febrile episodes (see figure 2). As a result, the team carefully detached the mass from its uterine origin with a double clamp (see figure 2), and after dividing the stalk, closed the base meticulously using a single absorbable suture. The inferior mass, attached at the uterine torus, was left in place to reduce risk to the pregnancy. This lesion had a smooth exterior, was non-mobile, and had a soft consistency, consistent with a large cystic leiomyoma exhibiting both intramural and subserosal involvement.

The operative procedure lasted 2 hours and 25 minutes. Of note, progesterone prophylaxis was initiated intraoperatively at a dose of 100 mg intramuscularly and continued at 100 mg IM daily until hospital discharge. Afterward, the patient was switched to intramuscular hydroxyprogesterone caproate, 341 mg (1 fl IM) every 3 days as per the product instructions, continuing through until scheduled delivery. Antibiotic prophylaxis during surgery consisted of 2 g cefazolin intravenously, followed by postoperative administration of cefazolin 1 g IV three times daily in conjunction with clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily, tailored to urine culture results (Klebsiella pneumoniae with high sensitivity to cephalosporins and clarithromycin).

Outcome and follow-up:

Following the operation, the patient’s anaemia worsened (Hb 7.5 g/L, HCT 24.3%), and ongoing iron deficiency necessitated intravenous iron administration with ferric carboxymaltose 1 g, along with two successful blood transfusions (Hb increased to 10.0 g/L, HCT 30.4%). She was discharged without complications 10 days postoperatively and subsequently underwent close obstetric monitoring every three weeks, which demonstrated appropriate fetal growth (86th percentile per the WHO fetal growth chart calculator at https://srhr.org/fetalgrowthcalculator/#/) and normal amniotic fluid index.

Analysis of the peritoneal fluid yielded negative results for both microbiological cultures and cytology. Histopathology confirmed benign uterine leiomyomas and areas of ischemic necrosis adjacent to the twisted pedicle.

The position of the caudal myoma would have constituted a significant obstruction to fetal head passage during the second stage of labour. Therefore, an elective caesarean section was scheduled on the existing surgical scar once gestational age reached 37 weeks, thereby reducing the risk of uterine rupture and resulting in an excellent neonatal outcome.

At present, the patient does not report any myoma-related symptoms, although the size of the remaining pelvic myoma has slightly decreased to 7.5 cm. She has been breastfeeding successfully, but wishes to defer any further surgical intervention owing to the partial spontaneous reduction of the residual leiomyomas postpartum, absence of symptoms, and the attainment of her motherhood goals. The child is developing normally.

Discussion:

Myomas can often be misdiagnosed as adnexal masses: the incidence during pregnancy is 0.3–10.1% and 1–6%, respectively .13

In this instance, the initial assessment identified abdominal masses resembling two large decidualized endometriomas, aside from the lower solid portion of the pelvic mass, which appeared consistent with a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration measuring 58×71 mm as seen on both ultrasound and MRI studies. Due to the lack of established protocols for managing pelvic masses during pregnancy, the multidisciplinary team determined that planned surgical intervention might reduce the probability of preterm birth.

Normally, serum CA-125 levels during pregnancy demonstrate two peaks: the first occurs in the initial trimester (median 23 U/mL, p<0.00001), and the second in the third trimester (median 21 U/mL, p<0.001), both significantly elevated compared to the second trimester (median 14.0 U/mL). However, this patient exhibited a CA-125 of 41.8 U/mL, surpassing the typical median value for the second trimester.

CA-125 is predominantly utilized as a tumor marker in ovarian carcinoma, with the accepted threshold being ≥35 U/mL, particularly relevant in women over 50 years of age (providing higher values for area under the curve, positive predictive value, sensitivity, and specificity). Nonetheless, increased CA-125 can be attributed to multiple benign disorders such as endometriosis and adenomyosis (>19 U/mL), pelvic inflammatory disease, menstruation, early and late stages of pregnancy (median values at 23 U/mL and 21 U/mL respectively), risk for spontaneous abortion, as well as myoma (cut-off <19 U/mL).57

The main factors influencing clinical management included the presence of large, vascular masses exhibiting both solid and cystic features, which carried an immediate risk for complications such as rupture, torsion, or infection; the patient's deteriorating symptoms with increasing pain and shortness of breath; potential for further mass enlargement that could result in hypoxia, necrosis, and degeneration; mildly elevated tumor markers (CA-125 measured at 41.8 U/mL); and insufficient space for normal uterine expansion.

Given the suspicion of neoplastic origin and the ambiguous nature of the masses, the patient underwent a non-contrast MRI following the ultrasound to improve diagnostic precision. According to current guidelines, gadolinium-enhanced MRI is contraindicated during pregnancy unless there is a strict necessity related to rheumatological, inflammatory, or infiltrative skin diseases, or elevated risk of stillbirth or neonatal mortality. As a result, the multidisciplinary team opted for a non-contrast MRI, avoiding magnetic field strengths above 1.5-T, which is regarded as reasonably safe once the first trimester has passed.

In this case, the multidisciplinary team—including surgeons, gynecologists, and obstetricians—decided to undertake longitudinal laparotomic surgery after obtaining specific written informed consent from the patient. During the procedure, the upper lesion was found to have a pedicle, which posed a considerable risk for an acute, classical complication; thus, the decision was made to restrict the intervention to the upper myomectomy. Fetal viability was tracked throughout the operation by intermittent transvaginal ultrasonography, and intraoperative progesterone was administered prophylactically.

A large multicenter investigation by Zhao et al emphasized that it is essential to assess and record fetal heart rate before, during, and after surgical intervention.

Typically, surgical management is postponed until after delivery, favoring conservative and medical strategies. Notably, Sakinci et al, in a recent tertiary center study, reported no statistically significant differences between women who underwent caesarean section (CS) combined with myomectomy and those who had CS alone without myoma in terms of preoperative and postoperative hemoglobin levels or rates of blood transfusion (p >0.05); however, both hospitalization and surgery times were markedly increased for the group receiving CS myomectomy (p =0.001 and p =0.001, respectively). The average myoma diameter measured 8.3 ± 4.1 cm, and there was a statistically significant positive association between myoma size and duration of hospital stay (p =0.01).

The same study reported a statistically significant negative association between myoma size and gestational age at delivery (p = 0.035).

Uterine leiomyomas exhibit a non-linear pattern of size variation throughout pregnancy and the puerperium. Specifically, their dimensions increase weekly by 6.90% overall; this includes a 6.12% rise (p<0.001) from the first to second trimester and a 3.10%±0.39% increment between the second and third trimesters (p<0.05). Thus, the rate of myoma enlargement during pregnancy is markedly greater in the first trimester compared to subsequent gestational periods. In contrast, myoma size significantly declines during the third trimester and within six weeks postpartum, with a mean reduction of 44.25%±36.05% (p<0.05).

These studies emphasize the necessity for precise ultrasound evaluation of uterine myomas—assessing their size, location, quantity, and proximity to the placenta—prior to considering surgical intervention.

When uterine myomas are identified during pregnancy, non-surgical management is generally favored due to the risk of substantial hemorrhage, miscarriage, preterm delivery, and uterine rupture associated with surgical procedures. Nonetheless, myomectomy may be warranted for complicated cases such as pedunculated myomas with torsion or persistent symptoms unresponsive to conservative therapy. The procedure should be kept as brief as possible to optimize pregnancy preservation, and laparoscopic surgery is an option, ideally utilizing the open Hasson method when the gestational age is lower (first or early second trimester), the myoma is smaller, and its location is not intramural. This technique offers improved outcomes regarding maternal morbidity and the possibility of vaginal delivery. However, the current case illustrates a frequent bias toward choosing laparotomy over laparoscopy for larger, multiple, intramural, or critically positioned myomas, which are often associated with a higher incidence of cesarean section. This is due to a substantial myoma attached to the posterior isthmic region of the uterus, which could hinder vaginal delivery.
