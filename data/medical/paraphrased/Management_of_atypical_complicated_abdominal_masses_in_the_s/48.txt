Title: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

Rosalba De Nola, Emma Bassi, Cataldo Paletta, Mario Vicino

Abstract:

A woman in her 40s, who had not previously given birth, presented during her second trimester with abdominal pain and shortness of breath. Ultrasound imaging identified two sizeable masses within the abdomen: the upper lesion occupied the left subphrenic region and extended up to the uterine fundus, while the lower mass was situated in the pouch of Douglas. The patient underwent a laparotomy, during which the lower mass was left undisturbed within the pelvic cavity. Following surgery, she had routine obstetric monitoring and underwent a scheduled caesarean section, both mother and infant experiencing favorable outcomes. For pregnant patients with large symptomatic masses, thorough assessment utilizing bimanual examination as well as safe imaging methods—such as ultrasound and non-contrast MRI—is crucial. Surgical intervention should be reserved for cases where malignancy is suspected or complications develop, with the primary objective to maintain pregnancy and prevent unnecessary treatment. The chosen method of delivery should take into account the mass's size, number, and location in relation to the uterus.

Background:

The evaluation and treatment of unusual or symptomatic abdominal masses in pregnancy involve considerable clinical difficulty. Available diagnostic modalities include physical examination, ultrasound (US) with color Doppler, and non-contrast MRI. Preoperative US is especially helpful for detecting symptomatic uterine leiomyomas situated on the anterior surface of the pregnant uterus, whether submucosal or pedunculated in nature. For cases involving multiple, large, intramural, or posterior leiomyomas, MRI provides enhanced diagnostic precision by accurately assessing the separation between the myoma and the uterine cavity.

The utility of serum tumor markers during pregnancy as diagnostic tools remains controversial. Specifically, CA-125 concentrations naturally increase during the initial trimester and then return to baseline. Consequently, single measurements do not serve as definitive diagnostic indicators, whereas sequential assessments may offer informative patterns for both differential diagnosis and ongoing monitoring. Effective management demands a thorough multidisciplinary risk evaluation to appropriately weigh obstetric risks against the uncommon and complicated nature of these cases.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 40s with obesity (BMI 33) and a reproductive history of G1P0A1 presented at 21 weeks gestation (second trimester) with intense, sudden onset abdominal pain localized to the upper left quadrant and pelvis (Visual Analogue Scale=5 for each site), accompanied by mild shortness of breath. Her first and second trimester screening had indicated a low probability for aneuploidy, which was further substantiated by non-invasive prenatal testing, and fetal morphology was within normal parameters.

Upon arrival at the emergency department, a multidisciplinary assessment was performed: she had normal oxygen saturation (average 97%), thoracic examination revealed a regular heart rate (average 74 bpm) and blood pressure (average 100/60 mm Hg), and her ECG results were unremarkable. She was subsequently admitted to the Obstetrics and Gynecology unit. Her abdomen was observed to be more distended than expected for her gestational age, with a palpable, tender, taut-elastic mass measuring 18 cm occupying the entire upper left quadrant, but there were no signs of ascites.

On bimanual vaginal examination, a fixed, hard, rounded mass measuring about 9 cm was palpated in the lesser pelvis, protruding partly into the posterior fornix.

The patient received analgesic treatment (intravenous paracetamol 1000 mg twice daily) and was positioned in a semi-orthostatic posture, which theoretically reduced diaphragmatic compression from the gravid uterus and large abdominal masses. This regimen led to a modest improvement in symptoms, decreasing pain scores from 5 to 1 according to the Visual Analogue Scale, without requiring supplemental oxygen or mechanical ventilation.

During her hospitalization, she developed a mild fever. Therefore, a nasopharyngeal swab PCR for SARS-CoV-2 was performed, yielding a negative result. However, urine cultures identified a Klebsiella pneumoniae infection. Laboratory analyses revealed iron deficiency anemia with a haemoglobin (Hb) level of 9.9 g/L (reference 12.0–16.6 g/L) and serum ferritin of 25 mcg/L (reference 15–300 mcg/L). C-reactive protein was mildly elevated at 1.39 mg/dL (reference 0–0.3 mg/dL) and neutrophils were increased (total white blood cells 11.29×10^3/uL [reference 4–10×10^3/uL], neutrophils 9.27×10^3/uL [reference 2.8–5.5×10^3/uL]), findings that are consistent with physiological changes in pregnancy.

Investigations:

Ultrasound revealed two sizable, unilocular cystic lesions exhibiting smooth borders and vascularization at their periphery. The superior mass measured 18×12×7 cm and extended from the left subphrenic region down to the uterine fundus. The inferior mass measured 9.5×8×8 cm and appeared to be attached to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration (dimensions 58×71 mm).

Assessment of tumor markers—including CA 19–9, CA 15–3, CEA, β2-microglobulin, and LDH—found values within reference ranges. However, CA-125 was elevated at 41.8 U/mL (normal <35 U/mL), displaying a progressive increase compared to 36 U/mL measured two weeks prior. Thus, there was a moderate upward trend in CA-125 levels. Consequently, an MRI without contrast was performed, which demonstrated thickened walls (1.4 cm), heterogeneous fluid characteristics on both T1 and T2 fat-saturated sequences, and localized hyperintense solid areas on T1 and T2-weighted images (refer to figure 1; only T2 fat-saturated images are presented). The delineation between the caudal lesion and adjacent structures was ambiguous, with apparent continuity between the caudal mass and the posterior leiomyoma previously identified on ultrasound. No lymphadenopathy was detected by MRI.

Differential diagnosis:

On ultrasound examination, the cystic lesions resembled two large decidualized endometriomas, with the inferior cyst appearing to be attached to an intramural-subserosal leiomyoma located on the posterior uterine wall, which measured 58×71 mm.

MRI identified two rounded, single-chamber adnexal cysts with enlarged vessels and heterogeneous fluid contents (see figure 1); the lower lesion appeared contiguous with a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration and measuring 58×71 mm.

The significant size and spatial occupation (‘steric bulk’) of the gravid uterus and the extensive masses made it impossible to distinguish a connecting stalk or a vascular supply for the superior mass, thereby favoring an ovarian mass as the diagnosis—most notably endometriomas, particularly given the mildly elevated CA-125 during the second trimester (see discussion).

The imaging findings on ultrasound and MRI yielded a differential diagnosis including decidualized endometriomas and cystic degenerating leiomyomas; the moderate rise in CA-125 supported an endometrioma for the superior cyst, whereas the continuous relationship with uterine tissue pointed towards a myomatous nature for the lower lesion. The patient's dyspnoea was ascribed to physical compression from the voluminous abdominal masses, with no detected pulmonary or cardiac pathology.

Treatment:

Given the progression of the patient’s symptoms—namely abdominal pain attributed to the mass and dyspnoea secondary to restrictive lung mechanics from the abdominal tumors—as well as the pressing need for a definitive diagnosis, potential complication risks (including rupture, torsion, and infection), and the expectation of further uterine expansion with continued fetal development, an exploratory laparotomy via a longitudinal xiphoid-to-pubic incision was performed at 21 weeks and 6 days of gestation following informed consent.

Initially, two samples of peritoneal fluid were obtained for both culture and cytology. Subsequently, inspection of the abdominal cavity was undertaken, confirming fetal cardiac activity.

Unexpectedly, the mass in the cranial location was identified as a very large pedunculated leiomyoma with atypical features; it appeared cystic and had a tense-elastic consistency, demonstrating significant vascularity, evidence of early torsion, and leakage, which may have accounted for the fevers observed during hospitalization (refer to figure 2). Accordingly, the surgical team proceeded to carefully excise the mass at its point of uterine attachment utilizing a double clamp technique (refer to figure 2). Following transection of the pedicle, the base was closed meticulously using a single absorbable suture. The caudal mass, originating from the uterine torus, was not removed in order to reduce obstetric risk; this lesion was fixed in place, with a smooth contour and a soft consistency characteristic of a large cystic intramural-subserosal leiomyoma.

The operation required 2 hours and 25 minutes. Prophylactic progesterone (100 mg intramuscularly) was administered beginning intraoperatively and was continued at 100 mg IM daily until the patient was discharged. Thereafter, the patient was transitioned to hydroxyprogesterone caproate, administered intramuscularly (341 mg, 1 vial every 3 days as specified in the package insert), continuing until planned delivery. For infection prophylaxis, cefazolin 2 g was given intravenously intraoperatively, followed by postoperative antibiotics consisting of cefazolin 1 g IV three times daily combined with clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily during hospitalization, in accordance with urine culture sensitivity demonstrating Klebsiella pneumoniae highly susceptible to both cephalosporins and clarithromycin.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following the operation, the patient’s anaemia worsened (Hb 7.5 g/L, HCT 24.3%) and iron deficiency persisted, necessitating intravenous administration of ferric carboxymaltose (1 g) and two successful blood transfusions (resulting in Hb 10.0 g/L, HCT 30.4%). Ten days post-surgery, the patient was discharged in stable condition and subsequently attended close obstetric follow-up every three weeks. These assessments demonstrated fetal growth in the 86th percentile as per the WHO fetal growth chart calculator (https://srhr.org/fetalgrowthcalculator/#/) and maintained amniotic fluid index.

Analysis of the peritoneal fluid yielded negative results on both culture and cytology. Histopathological examination identified benign uterine leiomyomas, with ischaemic necrosis observed adjacent to the twisted pedicle.

The caudal myoma would have constituted a significant barrier to the descent of the fetal head during the second stage of labor. Therefore, once gestation reached 37 weeks, an elective caesarean section was scheduled using the previous surgical incision, effectively reducing the risk of uterine scar rupture and resulting in an optimal neonatal outcome.

Currently, the patient reports no myoma-associated symptoms, although the residual pelvic myoma has diminished slightly in size to 7.5 cm. She has experienced successful breastfeeding and, given the partial regression of leiomyomas postpartum, lack of symptoms, and satisfaction with motherhood, has opted against immediate further surgery. The infant is progressing well.

Discussion:

Myomas can often be misdiagnosed as adnexal masses: the incidence during pregnancy is 0.3–10.1% and 1–6%, respectively .13

In this patient, the initial workup identified abdominal masses that presented as two large, decidualized endometriomas. However, the inferior solid segment of the pelvic mass was suggestive of a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma demonstrating cystic degeneration, measuring 58×71 mm as visualized on both ultrasound and MRI scans. Given the absence of clear management protocols for pelvic masses during gestation, the multidisciplinary team opted for planned surgical intervention, weighing the potential reduction in preterm birth risk.

Typically, serum CA-125 levels during pregnancy exhibit two peaks: one occurring in the first trimester (median 23 U/mL, p<0.00001) and another in the third trimester (median 21 U/mL, p<0.001), both higher than levels in the second trimester (median 14.0 U/mL). Contrasting this pattern, the current patient demonstrated a CA-125 level of 41.8 U/mL, exceeding the standard second trimester median.

CA-125 serves predominantly as a tumor marker for ovarian carcinoma, with a commonly accepted threshold of ≥35 U/mL, particularly effective in women older than 50 years due to increased area under the curve, predictive value, sensitivity, and specificity. Nonetheless, CA-125 levels may be elevated in a variety of non-malignant conditions, such as endometriosis and adenomyosis (values >19 U/mL), pelvic inflammatory disease, menstruation, early and late stages of pregnancy (median 23 and 21 U/mL, respectively), heightened miscarriage risk, and in cases of myoma (cut-off <19 U/mL).57

The primary factors guiding the clinical management included the identification of large, highly vascularized lesions comprising both solid and cystic elements, which carried a substantial risk of complications such as rupture, torsion, or infection. Additional considerations were the patient’s deteriorating symptoms—namely, increasing pain and shortness of breath—as well as the potential for further enlargement of the masses, raising concerns for the onset of hypoxia, necrosis, or degenerative changes. Slight elevations in tumor markers (CA-125=41.8 U/mL) and limitations in intra-abdominal space—potentially impeding subsequent normal expansion of the uterus—were also influential.

Given the suspicion of neoplastic origin and unclear etiology of these lesions, the patient subsequently received an MRI without contrast to improve diagnostic precision following sonographic evaluation. Current guidelines advise against using gadolinium contrast-enhanced MRI during pregnancy unless absolutely required, especially for rheumatologic, inflammatory, or infiltrative dermatological conditions, or in situations involving risks of stillbirth or neonatal mortality. Thus, the multidisciplinary team opted for a non-contrast MRI, refraining from the use of imaging above 1.5-Tesla, which is regarded as acceptably safe post-first trimester.

In this case, the multidisciplinary team—which included surgeons, gynecologists, and obstetricians—decided on a longitudinal laparotomic approach following acquisition of a written informed consent from the patient. Intraoperatively, the superior mass was found to possess a pedicle, thereby conferring an elevated risk of an acute and classic complication. This observation influenced the decision to restrict the intervention to upper myomectomy. In addition, serial and periodic transvaginal ultrasounds were employed during the procedure to continuously assess fetal viability, supplemented with intraoperative prophylactic progesterone administration.

Zhao et al’s multicenter investigation underscores the necessity of recording fetal heart rate before, during, and after surgical intervention.

Typically, surgical intervention is postponed until after delivery, favoring medical management or an expectant strategy. Nevertheless, Sakinci et al recently reported from a tertiary institution that no statistically significant differences existed between women undergoing caesarean section (CS) with myomectomy and those undergoing only CS (absent myomas) in terms of preoperative or postoperative hemoglobin levels and blood transfusion requirements (p > 0.05). However, both hospital stay and operative time were markedly greater among patients receiving CS with myomectomy (p = 0.001 and p = 0.001, respectively). The average myoma diameter was 8.3 ± 4.1 cm, and a statistically significant positive correlation was noted between myoma size and duration of hospitalization (p = 0.01).

The aforementioned study also found a statistically significant, inverse relationship between myoma size and gestational week at delivery (p = 0.035).

Uterine leiomyomas exhibit a non-linear pattern of size variation throughout pregnancy and postpartum. Specifically, leiomyomas expand by 6.90% each week: 6.12% (p < 0.001) from the first to second trimesters, and 3.10% ± 0.39% from the second to third trimesters (p < 0.05). This indicates that the rate of leiomyoma growth is most pronounced in the first trimester compared to subsequent stages of pregnancy. Conversely, during the third trimester and at six weeks postpartum, myoma size markedly regressed (average reduction 44.25% ± 36.05%, p < 0.05).

These studies emphasize the necessity for meticulous ultrasonographic assessment of the size, site, number, and proximity of uterine myomas to the placenta prior to any surgical intervention.

When uterine myomas are detected during pregnancy, management is generally conservative due to heightened risk for severe hemorrhage, pregnancy loss, preterm labor, and uterine rupture with surgical intervention. Myomectomy may, however, be warranted in cases involving complicated masses such as pedunculated myoma torsion or ongoing symptoms unresponsive to conservative measures. The operative procedure should be minimized in duration to maintain pregnancy viability and may be conducted laparoscopically, preferably utilizing the open Hasson technique, especially in the early gestational period (first and early second trimesters), smaller mass volumes, and non-intramural locations. This method is associated with improved maternal morbidity outcomes and higher likelihood of vaginal delivery. The case described illustrates a frequently observed selection bias favoring laparotomy over laparoscopy when confronted with larger, multiple, intramural, or critically positioned myomas, which are more closely linked with increased cesarean section rates. This is exemplified by the presence of a substantial myoma adhered to the posterior isthmic uterine wall, creating a potential barrier to vaginal delivery.
