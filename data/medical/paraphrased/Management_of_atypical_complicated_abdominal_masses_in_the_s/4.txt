Title: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

Rosalba De Nola, Emma Bassi, Cataldo Paletta, Mario Vicino

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her 40s, with no prior pregnancies, experienced abdominal discomfort and shortness of breath in her second trimester. Ultrasound imaging detected two sizable abdominal masses: one superior lesion extended throughout the entire left subphrenic region up to the uterine fundus, while the other occupied the pouch of Douglas. Surgical exploration via laparotomy was performed, leaving the mass located in the pelvis untouched. The patient then continued with routine prenatal monitoring and later underwent a planned caesarean section, yielding favorable outcomes for both mother and infant. For pregnant patients presenting with substantial, symptomatic masses, a thorough assessment—including bimanual examination and safe imaging techniques like ultrasound and non-contrast MRI—is crucial. Surgical intervention should be reserved for cases where malignancy is suspected or when clinical complications develop, with an emphasis on maintaining pregnancy and minimizing unnecessary procedures. The chosen method of delivery should be guided by the masses’ size, count, and anatomical distribution.

Background:

Diagnosing and treating atypical or symptomatic abdominal masses in pregnant patients involves considerable clinical complexity. The range of diagnostic modalities is restricted primarily to physical examination, ultrasound (US) incorporating colour Doppler, and non-contrast MRI. Preoperative US serves an important role in the detection of symptomatic uterine leiomyomas, particularly those situated on the anterior aspect of the gravid uterus, regardless of whether they are submucosal or pedunculated. In contrast, MRI demonstrates enhanced diagnostic capability, especially in the assessment of multiple, sizeable, intramural, or posteriorly located leiomyomas, as it enables accurate delineation of the myoma's proximity to the uterine cavity.

The utility of serum tumour markers as a diagnostic aid in pregnancy remains controversial. For instance, CA-125 is known to exhibit a physiological increase in the first trimester, followed by a return to baseline levels. Consequently, single-point measurements lack diagnostic utility, whereas serial monitoring may provide meaningful patterns to assist in differential diagnosis and ongoing assessment. Effective management strategies necessitate thorough multidisciplinary risk evaluation to appropriately balance obstetric considerations against the infrequency and intricacy of these cases.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 40s with obesity (body mass index 33) and a history of G1P0A1 presented to our clinic at 21 weeks gestation, experiencing sudden and intense pain in the upper left abdominal quadrant and pelvic region (Visual Analogue Scale=5 at both locations), along with mild shortness of breath. Previous first and second trimester screenings had indicated a low likelihood of aneuploidies—this was further corroborated by non-invasive prenatal testing—and fetal morphology assessments were unremarkable.

Upon initial presentation to the emergency department, she underwent a comprehensive multidisciplinary evaluation. Her oxygen saturation was stable (median: 97%). Thoracic examination showed a normal heart rate (median: 74 bpm) and blood pressure (median: 100/60 mm Hg); her ECG did not reveal any abnormalities. Subsequently, she was transferred to the Division of Obstetrics and Gynecology. Notably, her abdominal enlargement appeared disproportionate to her gestational age, with a palpably tense and elastic mass extending throughout the upper left quadrant, measuring 18 cm and lacking clinical indicators of ascites.

A bimanual vaginal examination identified a fixed, rounded, and firm mass, approximately 9 cm in diameter, located in the lesser pelvis and partially protruding into the posterior fornix.

Administration of intravenous paracetamol (1000 mg twice daily) and maintenance in a semi-orthostatic position—aimed at potentially minimizing diaphragmatic compression from both the gravid uterus and large intra-abdominal masses—produced modest symptom relief. This intervention diminished pain levels from 5 to 1 on the Visual Analogue Scale without requiring supplemental oxygen or ventilatory assistance.

During her hospitalization, she developed a low-grade fever. PCR testing for severe acute respiratory syndrome Coronavirus 2 using a nasopharyngeal swab was negative, but urine cultures were positive for Klebsiella pneumoniae. Hematological analysis revealed iron deficiency anemia, with hemoglobin at 9.9 g/L (n.v. 12.0–16.6 g/L) and serum ferritin of 25 mcg/L (n.v. 15–300 mcg/L); C-reactive protein was mildly elevated at 1.39 mg/dL (n.v. 0–0.3 mg/dL); and leukocyte counts were slightly increased, with total white blood cells at 11.29×10^3 /uL (n.v. 4–10×10^3 /uL) and neutrophils at 9.27×10^3 /uL (n.v. 2.8–5.5×10^3 /uL), in line with pregnancy-associated hematological adaptations.

Investigations:

Ultrasound evaluation revealed the presence of two substantial, unilocular cystic lesions, both exhibiting smooth borders and vascularity at their periphery. The superior lesion, measuring 18×12×7 cm, spanned from the left subphrenic space down to the level of the uterine fundus. The inferior lesion had dimensions of 9.5×8×8 cm and appeared to be adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma displaying cystic degeneration (measuring 58×71 mm).

Analysis of tumour markers, including CA 19–9, CA 15–3, CEA, β2-microglobulin, and LDH, revealed values within reference ranges. In contrast, CA-125 was found to be elevated (41.8 U/mL; reference <35 U/mL), and serial measurements indicated a rise from 36 U/mL two weeks prior. This indicated a gradual increase in the serum level of CA-125. As a result, the patient was further evaluated with a non-contrast MRI scan, which detected cystic structures with thickened walls (1.4 cm), heterogeneous signal intensity on T1 and T2 fat-suppressed images, and regions of focal hyperintensity on both T1 and T2-weighted sequences (refer to figure 1; only T2 fat-suppressed sequences are illustrated). The demarcation between the inferior mass and adjacent structures was indistinct, with the mass appearing contiguous with the previously noted posterior leiomyoma identified during ultrasound assessment. MRI revealed no evidence of lymphadenopathy.

Differential diagnosis:

During the ultrasound examination, the cystic lesions resembled two large decidualised endometriomas, with the more caudal cyst appearing attached to an intramural-subserosal leiomyoma located on the posterior uterine wall, which measured 58×71 mm.

Magnetic resonance imaging revealed the presence of two well-defined, unilocular adnexal cysts characterized by prominent vessels and heterogeneous fluid contents (refer to figure 1). The inferior mass appeared to be attached to a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma demonstrating cystic degeneration, with dimensions of 58×71 mm.

The considerable size of the gravid uterus and the bulky masses precluded identification of a connecting stalk or a vascular pedicle feeding the upper lesion, steering the diagnostic impression toward ovarian tumors—most notably endometriomas—supported by mildly increased CA-125 levels in the second trimester of pregnancy (refer to the discussion).

Integrating findings from ultrasonography and MRI, the main differential considerations included decidualised endometriomas and leiomyomas with cystic degeneration. The moderate rise in CA-125 was more consistent with endometrioma for the superior cyst, whereas the anatomical continuity with the uterine wall of the caudal mass pointed toward a myomatous etiology. The patient’s dyspnoea was attributed to the compressive effect of these extensive intra-abdominal masses, in the absence of pulmonary or cardiac involvement.

Treatment:

Given the progression of the patient's clinical symptoms—specifically, pain related to abdominal mass effect and respiratory difficulties owing to restrictive lung disease induced by the size of the abdominal masses—as well as the imperative to establish a definitive diagnosis, the likelihood of complications (including rupture, torsion, and infection), and the anticipated increase in uterine volume with advancing gestation, a decision was made to perform a laparotomy at 21 weeks and 6 days gestation, following the acquisition of informed consent. The incision extended longitudinally from the xiphoid process to the pubic region.

Initial intraoperative steps included the collection of two aliquots of free peritoneal fluid for both microbiological and cytological analyses. The surgical team then systematically evaluated the abdominal contents, confirming ongoing fetal cardiac activity.

Unexpectedly, the cranial mass was identified as an exceptionally large, pedunculated, cystic, taut-elastic uterine leiomyoma with atypical features, marked by significant vascularity. This mass exhibited partial torsion and serous leakage, likely accounting for the fever observed during hospitalization (refer to figure 2). Consequently, the surgical approach entailed cautious enucleation of the mass from its uterine base using a double clamp technique (see figure 2). Following transection of the stalk, the base was meticulously secured with a single layer of absorbable sutures. The caudal mass, which originated from the uterine torus, was deliberately left undisturbed to reduce potential obstetric complications. This lesion exhibited immobility, a smooth contour, and a soft consistency, resembling a large cystic leiomyoma with intramural and subserosal components.

The operative duration was 2 hours and 25 minutes. Progesterone prophylaxis (100 mg intramuscularly) was initiated intraoperatively, continued daily until discharge, after which the regimen was transitioned to intramuscular hydroxyprogesterone caproate (341 mg, administered every 3 days in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions) until the planned delivery. Perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis with cefazolin 2 g intravenously was administered, succeeded by a postoperative regimen consisting of cefazolin 1 g intravenously thrice daily and clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily during hospitalization. This was tailored based on urine culture sensitivity (Klebsiella pneumoniae, highly susceptible to both cephalosporins and clarithromycin).

Outcome and follow-up:

Following the surgical procedure, the patient's anaemia deteriorated (Hb 7.5 g/L, HCT 24.3%) and iron deficiency persisted, necessitating administration of intravenous ferric carboxymaltose (1 g) alongside two blood transfusions. These interventions resulted in improved haemoglobin (Hb 10.0 g/L) and haematocrit (HCT 30.4%) values. The patient experienced an uneventful recovery and was discharged 10 days postoperatively. She subsequently underwent close obstetric monitoring at 3-week intervals, which documented fetal growth at the 86th percentile (according to the WHO fetal growth chart calculator: https://srhr.org/fetalgrowthcalculator/#/) and normal amniotic fluid index.

Analysis of peritoneal fluid yielded negative results for both microbial cultures and cytology. Histopathological examination confirmed benign uterine leiomyomas and identified regions of ischaemic necrosis adjacent to the torsed pedicle.

The location of the caudal myoma posed a significant barrier to fetal head descent during the second stage of labour. Consequently, an elective caesarean section was conducted at the site of the previous surgical scar upon fetal maturation at 37 weeks’ gestation, thereby reducing the likelihood of uterine scar rupture and resulting in a favorable neonatal outcome.

Currently, the patient reports no myoma-related symptoms, and the residual pelvic myoma has modestly regressed to 7.5 cm in diameter. She continues to breastfeed successfully and opts to defer further surgical intervention due to partial postpartum involution of the remaining leiomyomas, her asymptomatic status, and fulfillment of her reproductive goals. The infant is developing appropriately.

Discussion:

Myomas can often be misdiagnosed as adnexal masses: the incidence during pregnancy is 0.3–10.1% and 1–6%, respectively .13

In this case, the initial assessment identified abdominal masses that resembled two large decidualised endometriomas. However, the caudal solid portion of the pelvic mass was suggestive of a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration, measuring 58×71 mm on both US and MRI. Given the lack of established guidelines for managing pelvic masses during pregnancy, the multidisciplinary team determined that elective surgical intervention might reduce the likelihood of preterm birth.

Typically, serum CA-125 concentrations in pregnancy exhibit two peaks: one in the first trimester (median 23 U/mL, p<0.00001) and another in the third trimester (median 21 U/mL, p<0.001), with the second trimester showing lower values (median 14.0 U/mL). Contrasting these expected patterns, this patient’s CA-125 level was 41.8 U/mL, notably exceeding the median observed during the second trimester.

CA-125 is primarily recognized as a tumor marker for ovarian carcinoma, with a commonly accepted threshold of ≥35 U/mL, particularly yielding greater diagnostic accuracy in women over 50 years (increased area under the curve, positive predictive value, sensitivity, and specificity). Nonetheless, CA-125 elevations can occur in several nonmalignant situations, such as endometriosis and adenomyosis (>19 U/mL), pelvic inflammatory disease, menstruation, pregnancy (early and late third trimester, medians 23 and 21 U/mL, respectively), increased risk of miscarriage, as well as with myomas (cut-off <19 U/mL).57

The primary factors influencing clinical decision-making included the identification of large, vascularized masses featuring both solid and cystic elements, which posed a tangible risk for complications such as rupture, torsion, or infection. Additional considerations comprised the patient's deteriorating symptoms (increasing pain and dyspnoea), the potential for further tumor enlargement leading to hypoxia, necrosis, or degeneration, a mild elevation in oncological marker levels (CA-125=41.8 U/mL), and concerns regarding limited pelvic space potentially impeding normal uterine expansion.

Given the potential neoplastic nature and indeterminate origin of the lesions, an MRI without contrast was performed to obtain greater diagnostic clarity following ultrasonography. In alignment with current clinical guidelines, gadolinium-enhanced MRI was avoided during pregnancy unless absolutely warranted—for example, in the context of rheumatological, inflammatory, or infiltrative skin disorders, or the risk of stillbirth or neonatal death. As a result, the interdisciplinary care team proceeded with a non-contrast MRI, restricting field strength to no more than 1.5-T, a level regarded as acceptably safe beyond the first trimester.

In this case, the multidisciplinary team—including surgeons, gynaecologists, and obstetricians—chose to undertake a longitudinal laparotomic surgical intervention after securing specific written informed consent from the patient. Upon surgical examination, the superior lesion was found to possess a pedicle, which notably heightened the risk of an acute and conventional complication. As a result, the decision was made to confine the intervention to an upper myomectomy. Additionally, fetal viability was continuously assessed during the operation by means of serial transvaginal ultrasounds, supplemented by the preventative intraoperative use of progesterone.

A comprehensive multicentre investigation by Zhao et al. recommends strict monitoring and documentation of fetal heart rate preoperatively, intraoperatively, and postoperatively.

Surgical treatment is generally deferred until postpartum, with a preference for conservative and medical management; nevertheless, a recent study at a tertiary center by Sakinci et al. reported no statistically significant differences in preoperative and postoperative hemoglobin levels or the necessity for blood transfusions between women undergoing caesarean section (CS) with concurrent myomectomy and those undergoing CS without myoma (p >0.05). However, both hospital stay and operative duration were significantly extended in the CS myomectomy group (p =0.001 and p =0.001, respectively). The average myoma diameter measured 8.3 ± 4.1 cm, and a significant positive correlation was observed between myoma size and the length of hospitalisation (p =0.01).

The same study identified a statistically significant negative association between myoma size and gestational age at delivery (p = 0.035).

Uterine leiomyomas demonstrate a non-linear pattern of size variation throughout gestation and the puerperium. Specifically, leiomyomas expand in size by 6.90% per week: an increment of 6.12% (p<0.001) is observed from the first to the second trimester, and a 3.10%±0.39% increase is detected between the second and third trimesters (p<0.05). This data suggests that leiomyomas grow most rapidly during the first trimester, with a slower increase in subsequent trimesters. Conversely, a significant reduction in myoma size is observed in the third trimester and at 6 weeks postpartum, with an average decrease of 44.25%±36.05% (p<0.05).

The reviewed literature emphasizes the critical role of meticulous ultrasonographic evaluation for determining the dimensions, position, quantity, and proximity of uterine myomas to the placenta prior to planning surgical intervention.

When uterine myomas are discovered during pregnancy, a conservative management approach is typically favored over surgery due to potential complications, such as substantial hemorrhage, fetal loss, preterm birth, and uterine rupture. Nevertheless, surgical intervention should be contemplated in instances involving complicated myomas, such as those with pedunculated torsion or refractory symptoms unresponsive to conservative methods. In these scenarios, minimizing surgical duration is essential to protect the ongoing pregnancy, and laparoscopic techniques—preferably utilizing the open Hasson method—are advisable for cases characterized by earlier gestational ages (first and early second trimester), smaller mass sizes, and extramural (non-intramural) myoma locations. This minimally invasive strategy is associated with improved maternal outcomes and increased likelihood of vaginal delivery. However, the current case exemplifies the tendency to opt for laparotomy rather than laparoscopy when confronted with larger, multiple, intramural, or critically located myomas, conditions which may elevate the risk of cesarean section. In the described scenario, a sizable myoma attached to the posterior wall of the isthmic uterine region represented a significant barrier to vaginal birth.
