Title: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

Rosalba De Nola, Emma Bassi, Cataldo Paletta, Mario Vicino

Abstract:

A woman in her 40s with no previous pregnancies presented in her second trimester, reporting abdominal pain and shortness of breath. Ultrasound imaging identified two substantial abdominal lesions: the superior mass occupied the entire left subphrenic area and extended up to the uterine fundus, while the inferior mass was located in the pouch of Douglas. The patient had a laparotomy, during which the lower pelvic lesion was left untouched. She received standard obstetric care afterward and later underwent a planned caesarean section, leading to highly favorable outcomes for both mother and infant. When pregnant patients have sizeable, symptomatic abdominal masses, a thorough assessment should include a bimanual exam and safe imaging, like ultrasound and non-contrast MRI. Surgery ought to be reserved for cases with concern for malignancy or clinical issues, with the intent to maintain pregnancy and prevent unnecessary interventions. Decisions regarding delivery method should factor in the number, dimensions, and anatomical distribution of uterine masses.

Background:

Diagnosing and treating unusual or symptomatic abdominal masses in pregnancy is clinically complex. Available diagnostic tools include physical examination, ultrasound (US) with color Doppler, and non-contrast MRI. Preoperative US is especially helpful in detecting symptomatic uterine leiomyomas situated along the anterior uterine wall, whether submucosal or pedunculated. MRI is more accurate for identifying numerous, large, intramural, or posterior leiomyomas, as it can accurately measure the distance between the myoma and the uterine cavity.

The utility of serum tumor markers for diagnosis in pregnancy is still debated. CA-125 levels tend to increase physiologically during the first trimester and return to normal afterwards. As such, single measurements are not considered diagnostic; however, monitoring CA-125 over time may reveal trends that aid in differential diagnosis and follow-up. Management should involve a thorough multidisciplinary evaluation of risk to appropriately weigh obstetric concerns against the uncommon and complex nature of these cases.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 40s with obesity (body mass index of 33), G1P0A1, presented in her second trimester of pregnancy at 21 weeks gestation with intense and sudden onset abdominal pain localized to the upper left quadrant and pelvic region (Visual Analogue Scale score of 5 at both locations), accompanied by mild shortness of breath. Routine screenings during both the first and second trimesters indicated a low risk for aneuploidy, as confirmed by non-invasive prenatal testing, and normal fetal morphology.

Initially, she was evaluated in the emergency department by a multidisciplinary team. Her oxygen saturation was within normal limits (median 97%). Physical examination of her thorax demonstrated a regular heart rate (median 74 beats per minute) and blood pressure (median 100/60 mm Hg). Electrocardiogram findings were unremarkable. Subsequently, she was admitted to the Obstetrical and Gynecological ward. Her abdomen appeared more distended than typically expected at this stage of pregnancy, with a palpable, tender swelling and a taut, elastic mass measuring 18 cm occupying the entire upper left quadrant, absent signs of ascites.

On bimanual vaginal examination, a fixed, firm, rounded mass measuring approximately 9 cm was identified in the lesser pelvis, partly protruding into the posterior fornix.

The patient was treated with intravenous paracetamol (1000 mg twice daily) and placed in a semi-upright position to potentially mitigate diaphragmatic pressure from the gravid uterus and large abdominal mass. These measures resulted in modest symptom relief; pain scores decreased from 5 to 1 on the Visual Analogue Scale, with no need for supplemental oxygen or ventilatory assistance.

While hospitalized, she experienced a low-grade fever. PCR testing for SARS-CoV-2 via nasopharyngeal swab was negative; however, Klebsiella pneumoniae was isolated from urinary samples. Laboratory tests revealed iron deficiency anemia with hemoglobin at 9.9 g/L (normal range 12.0–16.6 g/L) and serum ferritin at 25 mcg/L (normal range 15–300 mcg/L); C-reactive protein was elevated at 1.39 mg/dL (normal range 0–0.3 mg/dL); and leukocyte counts were slightly raised with white blood cells at 11.29×10^3/uL (normal range 4–10×10^3/uL) and neutrophils at 9.27×10^3/uL (normal range 2.8–5.5×10^3/uL), findings attributed to physiologic changes in pregnancy.

Investigations:

Ultrasound evaluation revealed two sizable, unilocular cystic lesions with smooth borders and peripheral blood flow. The superior lesion measured 18×12×7 cm, extending from the left subphrenic area down to the uterine fundus. The inferior lesion was 9.5×8×8 cm in size and appeared attached to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration, measuring 58×71 mm.

Tumour markers including CA 19–9, CA 15–3, CEA, β2-microglobulin, and LDH were all within reference ranges. In contrast, CA-125 was increased at 41.8 U/mL (normal <35 U/mL), with a gradual rise noted from 36 U/mL two weeks prior. This indicated a modest upward trend in the serum CA-125 level. Consequently, the patient underwent a non-contrast MRI, which revealed thickened walls (1.4 cm), heterogeneous fluid signal on T1 and T2 fat-saturated sequences, as well as localized hyperintense T1-T2 weighted solid areas (refer to figure 1; only T2 fat-sat images are presented). The boundary between the caudal lesion and surrounding tissues was indistinct, suggesting possible continuity with the posterior leiomyoma previously identified by ultrasound. There was no evidence of lymphadenopathy on MRI.

Differential diagnosis:

On ultrasound examination, the cystic lesions resembled two large decidualised endometriomas; the lower cyst appeared to be attached to a posterior uterine wall intramural-subserosal leiomyoma, which measured 58×71 mm.

Magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated two oval, unilocular adnexal cysts exhibiting hypertrophied vasculature and heterogeneous internal fluid (refer to figure 1). The inferior mass was contiguous with a posterior uterine intramural-subserosal myoma undergoing cystic degeneration, sized 58×71 mm.

Given the spatial volume of the gravid uterus and the substantial size of the masses, no connecting pedicle or vascular supply was discerned for the superior mass. This led to suspicion of ovarian masses—particularly endometriomas—considering the modest rise in CA-125 observed in the second trimester (see discussion).

Sonographic and MRI findings prompted differential diagnoses including decidualised endometriomas and leiomyomas with cystic degeneration. The mild increase in CA-125 supported the possibility of endometrioma for the upper cyst, whereas the physical continuity with the uterine wall favored a myomatous origin for the lower mass. Respiratory symptoms were ascribed to physical compression from the large abdominal masses, with no detected pulmonary or cardiac compromise.

Treatment:

Owing to deterioration in her clinical status—including abdominal pain caused by mass effect and dyspnoea from restrictive pulmonary disease linked to the size of her abdominal masses—combined with the need for a definitive diagnosis, potential complications (such as rupture, torsion, and infection), and the likelihood of uterine expansion with advancing gestation, the patient underwent laparotomy via a midline xiphoid-to-pubic incision at 21 weeks and 6 days gestation, following informed consent.

Initially, two samples of free peritoneal fluid were obtained for cultural and cytological analysis. Subsequent inspection of the abdomen confirmed ongoing fetal cardiac activity.

Unexpectedly, the cranial mass proved to be a large pedunculated cystic leiomyoma of the uterus with taut-elastic characteristics, pronounced vascularity, initial torsion, and evidence of leakage—likely correlating with the patient’s febrile episodes during admission (refer to figure 2). Consequently, the surgical team carefully resected the mass from its uterine site using a double clamp technique (see figure 2). After transecting the stalk, the stump was secured with a single absorbable suture. The caudal mass, which arose from the uterine torus, was preserved in situ to avoid obstetric complications; this lesion had a firm, smooth surface and soft consistency, resembling a substantial cystic intramural-subserosal leiomyoma.

The procedure required 2 hours and 25 minutes. Importantly, the patient was given prophylactic progesterone (100 mg intramuscularly) intraoperatively and continued on daily intramuscular doses of 100 mg until discharge. Subsequently, she received hydroxyprogesterone caproate (341 mg intramuscularly, one ampoule every 3 days, as per package insert) until planned delivery. Intraoperative antimicrobial prophylaxis was provided with intravenous cefazolin 2 g; postoperatively, she was treated with cefazolin 1 g IV three times daily and clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily during her hospitalization, in accordance with urine culture antibiogram findings (Klebsiella pneumoniae highly sensitive to cephalosporins and clarithromycin).

Outcome and follow-up:

Following the operation, the patient’s anemia worsened (Hb 7.5 g/L, HCT 24.3%) and iron deficiency persisted, necessitating intravenous administration of ferric carboxymaltose (1 g) and two successful blood transfusions, resulting in improvement (Hb 10.0 g/L, HCT 30.4%). She was discharged without adverse events 10 days post-surgery and began close obstetric follow-up every 3 weeks, with monitoring that demonstrated fetal growth at the 86th percentile (per the WHO fetal growth chart calculator, https://srhr.org/fetalgrowthcalculator/#/) and appropriate amniotic fluid index.

Testing of the peritoneal fluid returned negative results for both cultures and cytology. Pathological analysis identified benign uterine leiomyomas and areas of ischemic necrosis adjacent to the twisted pedicle.

Due to the location of the caudal myoma, there would have been significant obstruction to fetal head passage during the expulsive stage of labor. Therefore, an elective caesarean section was scheduled at 37 weeks gestation, using the existing surgical scar, which minimized the risk of uterine scar rupture and resulted in a favorable neonatal outcome.

At present, the patient reports no myoma-related symptoms. The remaining pelvic myoma has decreased in size to 7.5 cm. She continues to breastfeed successfully and has chosen to defer further surgical intervention, given partial postpartum regression of the residual leiomyomas, lack of symptoms, and satisfaction with her maternal status. The infant is developing normally.

Discussion:

Myomas can often be misdiagnosed as adnexal masses: the incidence during pregnancy is 0.3–10.1% and 1–6%, respectively .13

In this case, initial assessment revealed abdominal masses consistent with two large decidualised endometriomas, aside from a caudal solid section of the pelvic lesion that resembled a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration measuring 58×71 mm, as observed on both ultrasound and MRI. With a lack of established guidelines for the management of pelvic masses during pregnancy, the multidisciplinary team determined that elective surgical intervention might reduce the risk of preterm birth.

Typically, serum CA-125 levels demonstrate two peaks throughout pregnancy: one during the first trimester (median 23 U/mL, p<0.00001) and another in the third trimester (median 21 U/mL, p<0.001), both significantly higher than levels seen in the second trimester (median 14.0 U/mL). However, the value observed in this patient was 41.8 U/mL, exceeding the median for the second trimester.

CA-125 is predominantly utilized as a tumor marker for ovarian carcinoma (current standard threshold ≥35 U/mL), particularly in women older than 50 years, with increased area under the curve, positive predictive value, sensitivity, and specificity. Nevertheless, it is also elevated in various benign conditions including endometriosis and adenomyosis (>19 U/mL), pelvic inflammatory disease, menstruation, early and near-term pregnancy (medians 23 and 21 U/mL, respectively), risk of miscarriage, and myoma (cut-off <19 U/mL).57

The main factors guiding the clinical management included the existence of large, highly vascular masses displaying both solid and cystic features, which carried a real risk for complications such as rupture, torsion, or infection; the worsening clinical symptoms, including pain and shortness of breath; the potential for further enlargement of the masses, leading to an increased likelihood of hypoxia, necrosis, and degeneration; mildly elevated tumor markers (CA-125=41.8 U/mL); and concerns about limited space available for continued physiological expansion of the uterus.

Given the possible neoplastic nature and indeterminate etiology of the masses, the patient underwent a non-contrast MRI following the ultrasound assessment to improve diagnostic precision. In line with current guidelines, gadolinium-enhanced MRI is not recommended during pregnancy unless strictly indicated for specific rheumatological, inflammatory, or infiltrative skin disorders, or in cases of risk for stillbirth or neonatal death. Consequently, the multidisciplinary team chose to perform a non-contrast MRI, avoiding use of MRI above 1.5-T, which is considered appropriately safe in the period following the first trimester.

In this case, the multidisciplinary team—including surgeons, gynecologists, and obstetricians—chose to implement a longitudinal laparotomic surgical approach after obtaining specific written informed consent from the patient. During surgical evaluation, the superior mass was noted to have a pedicle, which posed an elevated risk for a classic acute complication. For this reason, only the upper myomectomy was performed. Continuous and periodic transvaginal ultrasonography was used throughout the operation to track fetal viability, alongside prophylactic intraoperative progesterone administration.

Zhao et al., in a large multicenter study, emphasized the necessity of recording and verifying the fetal heart rate before, during, and after surgery.

Surgical intervention is typically postponed until postpartum, favoring a medical or expectant strategy. Nonetheless, Sakinci et al. recently reported from a tertiary center that there were no statistically significant differences in preoperative and postoperative hemoglobin levels or blood transfusion rates between women who underwent cesarean section (CS) with concurrent myomectomy and those without myoma who had only CS (p >0.05). However, both hospital stay and operative time were markedly longer in the group receiving CS myomectomy (p = 0.001 for each outcome). The average myoma size was 8.3 ± 4.1 cm, with a statistically significant and positive association between myoma size and hospital length of stay (p = 0.01).

The same investigation found a statistically significant negative correlation between myoma size and gestational age at delivery (p = 0.035).

Uterine leiomyomas demonstrate non-linear alterations in size throughout pregnancy and the postpartum period. Specifically, the leiomyoma diameter enlarges by an average of 6.90% per week, with increases of 6.12% (p<0.001) observed between the first and second trimesters and 3.10%±0.39% between the second and third trimesters (p<0.05). Accordingly, the growth rate of uterine leiomyomas is most pronounced in the first trimester compared to later stages of gestation. By contrast, a significant reduction in myoma size occurs during the third trimester and at 6 weeks postpartum, with a mean decrease of 44.25%±36.05% (p<0.05).

These studies highlight the necessity for thorough ultrasound assessment of leiomyoma size, position, quantity, and their proximity to the placenta prior to undertaking surgical intervention.

When uterine myomas are identified in pregnancy, a conservative strategy is commonly favored due to the potential for significant hemorrhage, miscarriage, preterm delivery, and uterine rupture associated with surgery. Nonetheless, myomectomy should be contemplated in cases with complicated lesions, such as torsion of a pedunculated myoma or in the presence of persistent, unremitting symptoms not responding to conservative measures. Surgical intervention should be minimized in duration to safeguard the ongoing pregnancy and is feasible via laparoscopy, particularly utilizing an open Hasson entry technique for cases of lower gestational age (first or early second trimester), smaller tumors, and specific non-intramural locations. This method affords better maternal outcomes and supports the feasibility of vaginal delivery. However, the case presented underscores a prevalent bias towards choosing laparotomy over laparoscopy for larger, multiple, intramural, or critically located myomas, which may increase the frequency of cesarean section, particularly when a large myoma adheres to the posterior wall at the isthmic region, potentially hindering vaginal delivery.
