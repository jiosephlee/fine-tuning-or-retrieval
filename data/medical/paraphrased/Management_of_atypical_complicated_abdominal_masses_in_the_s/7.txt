Title: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

Rosalba De Nola, Emma Bassi, Cataldo Paletta, Mario Vicino

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her 40s, who had never given birth, experienced abdominal pain and shortness of breath during her second trimester of pregnancy. Imaging with ultrasonography identified two substantial abdominal masses: one in the upper abdomen extended throughout the entire left subphrenic region up to the uterine fundus, while the second mass occupied the pouch of Douglas. Laparotomy was performed, leaving the lower mass in the pelvis. The patient was subsequently monitored through routine obstetric assessments and underwent a planned caesarean section, both she and her newborn faring well. For pregnant patients presenting with large, symptomatic masses, thorough assessment—including bimanual pelvic examination and safe imaging techniques like ultrasound and non-contrast MRI—is crucial. Surgical intervention should be reserved for situations involving suspected malignancy or clinical deterioration, with priority given to safeguarding the pregnancy and preventing unnecessary procedures. The choice of delivery method should be individualized, taking into account the characteristics and anatomical placement of the uterine masses.

Background:

The evaluation and treatment of atypical or symptomatic abdominal masses in pregnant patients are associated with considerable clinical complexities. Diagnostic modalities are largely confined to physical examination, ultrasound (US) enhanced with colour Doppler, and non-contrast MRI. Among these, preoperative US proves especially effective in detecting symptomatic uterine leiomyomas situated along the anterior uterine wall, regardless of whether they are submucosal or pedunculated in nature. When dealing with multiple, sizable, intramural, or posterior leiomyomas, MRI is notably more precise, as it facilitates accurate measurement of the distance between a leiomyoma and the uterine cavity.

The utility of serum tumour markers for diagnosis during gestation remains controversial. For example, CA-125 concentrations naturally increase during early pregnancy and return to baseline later, rendering single measurements diagnostically unreliable; however, monitoring levels over time may assist with differential diagnosis and patient monitoring. Managing these cases most effectively necessitates a comprehensive, multidisciplinary evaluation of risks, aimed at judiciously weighing obstetric hazards against the rare and intricate nature of such presentations.

Case presentation:

A woman in her forties, classified as obese with a body mass index of 33 and obstetric history of G1P0A1, presented at 21 weeks gestation with severe, acute abdominal pain localized to the upper left quadrant and pelvic area, both sites scoring 5 on the Visual Analogue Scale, alongside mild dyspnoea. Routine first and second trimester screenings had previously indicated a low risk for aneuploidies, findings that were corroborated by non-invasive prenatal testing, and fetal morphology was reported as normal.

Upon arrival at the emergency department, the patient underwent comprehensive assessment by a multidisciplinary team. Oxygen saturation was within normal limits (median 97%), thoracic examination noted a normal heart rate (median 74 bpm) and blood pressure (median 100/60 mm Hg). Electrocardiography yielded unremarkable results. Subsequent transfer to the Obstetrical and Gynaecological Division revealed abdominal distension greater than anticipated for her gestational age. Physical examination identified a firm, elastic mass measuring 18 cm in the upper left quadrant, without clinical evidence of ascites.

Bimanual vaginal examination detected a fixed, rounded, and firm mass approximately 9 cm in diameter, occupying the lesser pelvis and protruding partially at the posterior fornix.

The patient was managed with intravenous paracetamol (1000 mg twice daily) and advised to maintain a semi-orthostatic position, intended to decrease diaphragmatic compression from both the gravid uterus and large abdominal mass. These interventions provided mild symptomatic relief, reducing pain scores from 5 to 1 on the Visual Analogue Scale, without the need for supplemental oxygen or ventilatory support.

During hospitalization, she developed a low-grade fever. Severe acute respiratory syndrome Coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) was ruled out with a negative PCR nasopharyngeal swab; however, urinary culture grew Klebsiella pneumoniae. Laboratory evaluation revealed iron deficiency anemia, with a haemoglobin level of 9.9 g/L (reference range 12.0–16.6 g/L) and serum ferritin of 25 mcg/L (reference 15–300 mcg/L). Additionally, there was a mild elevation in C-reactive protein (1.39 mg/dL; reference 0–0.3 mg/dL) and neutrophil count (white blood cells 11.29×10^3 /uL [reference 4–10×10^3 /uL]; neutrophils 9.27×10^3 /uL [reference 2.8–5.5×10^3 /uL]), findings interpreted as consistent with physiological changes during pregnancy.

Investigations:

Ultrasonography revealed the presence of two substantial, unilocular cystic lesions characterized by smooth boundaries and vascular flow at the periphery. The superior cyst measured 18×12×7 cm, spanning from the left subphrenic space to the uterine fundus. The inferior cyst, sized at 9.5×8×8 cm, appeared to be adherent to a posteriorly located intramural-subserosal myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration (58×71 mm).

Assessment of tumour markers (CA 19–9, CA 15–3, CEA, β2-microglobulin, and LDH) yielded results within the reference range, while CA-125 was mildly elevated at 41.8 U/mL (normal <35 U/mL), continuing an upward trend from 36 U/mL recorded two weeks prior. Thus, a gradual increase in the serum concentration of CA-125 was observed. As a result, the patient subsequently underwent a non-contrast MRI, which demonstrated thickened cystic walls (1.4 cm), inhomogeneous fluid on T1 and T2 fat-saturated sequences, and focal solid regions with T1-T2 hyperintensity (refer to figure 1; only T2 fat-sat images are displayed). The interface between the caudal cyst and adjacent tissue was indistinct, and the lesion appeared contiguous with the posterior leiomyoma previously identified on ultrasound. No lymphadenopathy was observed on MRI.

Differential diagnosis:

During ultrasonographic assessment, the cystic neoplasms appeared as two large decidualised endometriomas, with the inferior cyst showing apparent adherence to a posteriorly located intramural-subserosal leiomyoma of the uterus, measuring 58×71 mm.

Magnetic Resonance Imaging revealed the presence of two well-circumscribed, unilocular adnexal cysts exhibiting hypertrophied vasculature and heterogeneous fluid content (refer to figure 1); the lower lesion appeared attached to a posterior uterine myoma with cystic degeneration, measuring 58×71 mm.

Given the considerable spatial presence of the gravid uterus and the voluminous masses, no discernible connecting pedicle or vascular supply to the superior mass was detected, thereby raising suspicion for ovarian lesions, in particular endometriomas, especially considering a slight increase in CA-125 levels during the second trimester (see discussion).

Interpretation of both the ultrasonographic and MRI findings required differential consideration of decidualised endometriomas versus cystically degenerating leiomyomas: while mild elevation of CA-125 lent support to the diagnosis of endometrioma for the superior cyst, the anatomical continuity with uterine tissue for the caudal lesion indicated a myomatous etiology. The patient's dyspnoea was attributed to the mechanical pressure exerted by the expansive intra-abdominal lesions, as no respiratory or cardiac impairment was detected.

Treatment:

In light of the deterioration of clinical symptoms—including abdominal pain from mass effect and dyspnoea due to restrictive lung disease caused by the expanding abdominal masses—the imperative to establish a definitive diagnosis, the potential for serious complications (such as rupture, torsion, or infection), and the anticipated increase in uterine size concomitant with fetal development, the patient underwent a laparotomy via a longitudinal xiphos-pubic incision at 21 weeks and 6 days of gestation, following the provision of informed consent.

Initially, two samples of free peritoneal fluid were obtained for both microbiological cultures and cytological examination. Subsequent exploration of the abdomen was performed, during which fetal cardiac activity was confirmed.

Unexpectedly, the cranial mass was identified as a large, pedunculated, atypically cystic, and taut-elastic uterine leiomyoma. This tumor was highly vascularized, showed initial signs of torsion and leakage, likely accounting for the patient’s fever during admission (refer to figure 2). Accordingly, the surgical team opted for careful removal of the mass from its uterine attachment using a double-clamp technique (see figure 2). After transection of the stalk, the exposed base was meticulously sutured with a single absorbable stitch. The caudal mass, arising from the uterine torus, was retained in situ in order to reduce obstetric risks. This lesion presented as immobile, smooth-surfaced, and soft, resembling a sizable intramural-subserosal cystic leiomyoma.

The operative procedure lasted 2 hours and 25 minutes. Importantly, progesterone prophylaxis (100 mg intramuscularly) was initiated intraoperatively and maintained daily at the same dose until discharge. Subsequently, the patient received hydroxyprogesterone caproate (341 mg, 1 ampoule intramuscularly every 3 days per manufacturer’s instructions) until the planned delivery. Antibiotic prophylaxis during surgery consisted of cefazolin 2 g administered intravenously, followed postoperatively by cefazolin 1 g intravenously three times daily plus clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily throughout the hospital stay, guided by the antibiogram results from the urine culture (Klebsiella pneumoniae highly sensitive to cephalosporins and clarithromycin).

Outcome and follow-up:

Following surgery, the patient’s anaemia worsened (Hb 7.5 g/L, HCT 24.3%) and iron deficiency persisted, necessitating intravenous administration of ferric carboxymaltose (1 g) as well as two successful blood transfusions (resulting in Hb 10.0 g/L, HCT 30.4%). The patient experienced no complications and was discharged 10 days postoperatively. She subsequently underwent close obstetric monitoring every three weeks, during which assessments indicated appropriate fetal growth (86th percentile as per the WHO fetal growth chart calculator, accessible at https://srhr.org/fetalgrowthcalculator/#/) and normal amniotic fluid index.

Microbiological and cytological evaluation of the peritoneal fluid yielded negative results. Histopathological analysis identified benign uterine leiomyomas and regions of ischaemic necrosis in proximity to the twisted pedicle.

The presence of the caudal myoma would have posed a substantial impediment to fetal head descent in the second stage of labour. Accordingly, an elective caesarean section was scheduled and performed using the prior surgical incision once the pregnancy reached 37 weeks gestation, thereby minimizing the risk of uterine scar rupture. The procedure resulted in an optimal neonatal outcome.

Presently, the patient remains asymptomatic with regard to myoma, although the residual pelvic leiomyoma has decreased slightly in size, now measuring 7.5 cm. She is breastfeeding successfully but has chosen to defer any further surgical interventions at this stage due to a partial reduction in the size of the remaining leiomyomas, lack of related symptoms, and satisfaction with her maternal experience. The child continues to develop normally.

Discussion:

Myomas can often be misdiagnosed as adnexal masses: the incidence during pregnancy is 0.3–10.1% and 1–6%, respectively .13

In this particular case, initial assessments identified abdominal masses that resembled two large decidualised endometriomas. However, the lower solid portion of the pelvic mass exhibited imaging characteristics consistent with a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma demonstrating cystic degeneration, with a size of 58×71 mm, as seen on both US and MRI. Given the lack of established protocols for the management of pelvic masses during pregnancy, the multidisciplinary team determined that planned surgical intervention might potentially lower the risk of preterm birth.

Physiologically, serum CA-125 concentrations in pregnancy typically display two peak values: one in the first trimester (median 23 U/mL, p<0.00001) and another in the third trimester (median 21 U/mL, p<0.001), which are significantly elevated compared to the second trimester (median 14.0 U/mL). In contrast, this case revealed a CA-125 level of 41.8 U/mL, markedly above the median level commonly observed during the second trimester.

Although CA-125 is predominantly utilized as a tumor marker in the diagnosis and monitoring of ovarian carcinoma (current standard threshold ≥35 U/mL), especially in women over 50 years of age (yielding increased area under the curve, predictive value, sensitivity, and specificity), its levels can also be elevated in numerous benign scenarios, including endometriosis and adenomyosis (>19 U/mL), pelvic inflammatory disease, menstruation, early and late stages of pregnancy (median 23 and 21 U/mL, respectively), increased risk of miscarriage, as well as in cases of myoma (cut-off <19 U/mL).57

The principal factors influencing the clinical decision included the identification of large, highly vascularized masses exhibiting both solid and cystic characteristics, which posed an imminent risk for complications such as rupture, torsion, or infection. Additionally, the patient’s worsening symptoms—specifically increasing pain and dyspnoea—alongside the potential for further mass enlargement, which could heighten the risk of hypoxia, necrosis, and tissue degeneration, were significant considerations. Slight elevations in oncological markers (CA-125=41.8 U/mL) and spatial limitations that could impede normal uterine growth were also critical factors.

Given the suspicion of a tumorous process and the ambiguous etiology of the masses, the patient subsequently underwent an MRI scan without contrast enhancement following the initial ultrasound, to achieve greater diagnostic clarity. Consistent with current guidelines, administration of gadolinium-enhanced MRI is not advised during pregnancy except in situations involving rheumatological, inflammatory, or infiltrative dermatologic diseases, or when there is a heightened risk of stillbirth or neonatal mortality. Thus, a multidisciplinary approach led to the decision to perform a non-contrast MRI, ensuring the use of no more than 1.5-T magnetic field strength, which is regarded as relatively safe beyond the first trimester.

In this case, the multidisciplinary team—including surgeons, gynaecologists, and obstetricians—decided to employ a longitudinal laparotomic approach after securing the patient’s written informed consent. Upon intraoperative examination, the superior mass was found to possess a pedicle, posing a high risk of acute, characteristic complications. This risk provided the justification for restricting the intervention to an upper myomectomy. Throughout the surgery, fetal well-being was regularly evaluated by repeated transvaginal ultrasonography, and intraoperative progesterone was administered prophylactically.

A major multicenter investigation by Zhao et al underscores the necessity of assessing and recording fetal heart rate prior to, during, and following operative procedures.

Standard practice often favors postponing surgical intervention until postpartum, prioritizing medical management or watchful waiting. Nevertheless, recent findings from Sakinci et al, based on data from a tertiary care center, revealed no significant difference in preoperative or postoperative hemoglobin levels or rates of blood transfusion (p >0.05) between women undergoing caesarean section (CS) with concurrent myomectomy and those having CS alone in the absence of myoma. Conversely, the cohort undergoing CS with myomectomy experienced significantly increased surgical duration and length of hospitalization (p =0.001 and p =0.001, respectively). The average myoma measured 8.3 ± 4.1cm, and a statistically significant positive relationship emerged between myoma size and duration of hospital stay (p =0.01).

The aforementioned study also identified a statistically significant inverse relationship between myoma size and gestational age at delivery ($p = 0.035$).

Uterine leiomyomas exhibit non-linear patterns in their dimensional changes throughout pregnancy and the puerperium. Specifically, leiomyoma dimensions increase at a rate of 6.90% per week: 6.12% ($p < 0.001$) between the first and second trimesters, and 3.10% ± 0.39% from the second to the third trimester ($p < 0.05$). Consequently, leiomyoma growth is considerably more pronounced during the first trimester compared to later gestational periods. Conversely, there is a notable reduction in myoma size observed during the third trimester and within six weeks postpartum (average decrease of 44.25% ± 36.05%, $p < 0.05$).

These studies underscore the necessity of a thorough ultrasonographic evaluation of the dimensions, anatomical location, quantity, and placental proximity of uterine myomas prior to surgical intervention.

For uterine myomas detected during gestation, conservative management is typically favored over surgical treatment due to potential complications such as severe hemorrhage, fetal loss, preterm birth, and uterine rupture. Nonetheless, myomectomy may be warranted in cases involving complicated masses, such as torsion of a pedunculated myoma, or when symptoms persist despite conservative therapy. Surgical intervention should be minimized to ensure pregnancy preservation and can be undertaken laparoscopically, ideally utilizing an open Hasson technique in early gestation (first and early second trimesters), when the myoma is smaller, and in selected anatomical sites (excluding intramural myomas). This method is associated with improved maternal outcomes and an increased likelihood of vaginal delivery. The present case, however, exemplifies the frequent selection bias that leads clinicians to choose laparotomy over laparoscopy in cases involving large, multiple, intramural, or strategically situated myomas—circumstances which are linked to increased cesarean section rates due to substantial myomas adherent to the posterior isthmic region of the uterus, which can obstruct vaginal birth.
