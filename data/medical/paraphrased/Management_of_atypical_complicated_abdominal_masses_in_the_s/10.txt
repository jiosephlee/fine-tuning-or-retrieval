Title: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

Rosalba De Nola, Emma Bassi, Cataldo Paletta, Mario Vicino

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her 40s who had not previously given birth presented during her second trimester with abdominal pain and shortness of breath. Ultrasound demonstrated two sizable abdominal masses: one located superiorly, filling the entire left subphrenic region and extending to the uterine fundus, and another inferiorly, occupying the pouch of Douglas. Laparotomy was performed, but the pelvic (caudal) mass was left undisturbed within the pelvis. The patient continued with standard prenatal monitoring and subsequently delivered via planned caesarean section, achieving favorable outcomes for both mother and infant. For pregnant patients with large, symptomatic masses, thorough assessment—including bimanual examination and safe imaging options like ultrasound and non-contrast MRI—is critical. Surgical intervention should be limited to situations involving suspicion of malignancy or clinical complications, with the intent to maintain the pregnancy and prevent unnecessary procedures. The choice of delivery method should take into account the characteristics and anatomical position of the uterine masses.

Background:

Diagnosing and treating unusual or symptomatic abdominal masses in pregnant patients involves notable clinical difficulties. Diagnostic modalities are restricted primarily to physical assessment, ultrasound (US) with colour Doppler, and non-contrast magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Preoperative US is especially useful for detecting symptomatic uterine leiomyomas situated on the anterior wall of the pregnant uterus, including submucosal and pedunculated forms. For multiple, large, intramural, or posteriorly located leiomyomas, MRI is preferred because it more accurately delineates the proximity of the fibroid to the endometrial cavity.

The utility of serum tumor markers in pregnancy is still a matter of discussion. For instance, CA-125 is known to increase physiologically in the first trimester and then return to baseline levels; thus, single measurements are non-specific, while trends from serial assessments may aid in differential diagnosis and ongoing monitoring. Management strategies must incorporate careful, multidisciplinary evaluation to weigh maternal and fetal risks given the uncommon and complex nature of these presentations.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 40s with obesity (BMI 33) and a gynecologic history of G1P0A1 presented to our clinic at 21 weeks’ gestation, experiencing severe, acute pain in both the upper left abdomen and pelvic regions (Visual Analogue Scale score of 5 at each site), accompanied by mild shortness of breath. Routine screening tests during the first and second trimesters had indicated low risk for fetal aneuploidies, as verified by non-invasive prenatal testing, and ultrasound had shown normal fetal anatomy.

Initially, she presented to the emergency department for comprehensive evaluation by a multidisciplinary team. Her oxygen saturation was within normal limits (median 97%), and examination of the chest showed a normal heart rate (median 74 bpm) along with stable blood pressure readings (median 100/60 mm Hg). An ECG was unremarkable. Subsequently, she was admitted to the Obstetrics and Gynaecology Department. On abdominal assessment, distension appeared greater than what would be expected for her gestational age, characterized by a tender, stretched-elastic mass occupying the entire upper left abdominal quadrant and measuring 18 cm, with no clinical evidence of ascites.

The bimanual vaginal examination showed the presence of a fixed mass in the lesser pelvis of about 9 cm, rounded and hard, that partially bulged at the posterior fornix.

Analgesia consisting of intravenous paracetamol 1000 mg administered twice daily, along with placing the patient in a semi-orthostatic position—intended to reduce diaphragmatic compression from the gravid uterus and large abdominal masses—led to a modest improvement in symptoms without the need for supplemental oxygen or ventilatory assistance, lowering the reported pain from 5 to 1 on the Visual Analogue Scale.

While hospitalized, the patient experienced a mild fever. Polymerase chain reaction testing for severe acute respiratory syndrome Coronavirus 2 was conducted via nasopharyngeal swab, which yielded a negative result. In contrast, urine cultures identified a Klebsiella pneumoniae infection. Additionally, laboratory investigations demonstrated iron deficiency anaemia with haemoglobin at 9.9 g/L (reference range: 12.0–16.6 g/L) and serum ferritin of 25 mcg/L (reference range: 15–300 mcg/L); there was a mild elevation in C-reactive protein at 1.39 mg/dL (reference range: 0–0.3 mg/dL), and the white blood cell count was elevated at 11.29×10^3 /uL (reference range: 4–10×10^3 /uL) with neutrophils at 9.27×10^3 /uL (reference range: 2.8–5.5×10^3 /uL), findings in line with physiological changes of pregnancy.

Investigations:

Ultrasound revealed two sizable, unilocular cystic lesions exhibiting smooth borders and peripheral blood flow. The superior lesion measured 18×12×7 cm and extended from the left subphrenic space down to the uterine fundus. The inferior lesion measured 9.5×8×8 cm and appeared adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma demonstrating cystic degeneration (58×71 mm).

Assessment of tumor markers including CA 19–9, CA 15–3, CEA, β2-microglobulin, and LDH returned values within normal ranges. However, CA-125 was increased at 41.8 U/mL (normal <35 U/mL), and had risen from 36 U/mL two weeks previously, indicating a mild upward trend in serum CA-125. Consequently, MRI was performed without contrast, revealing cysts with thickened walls (1.4 cm), inhomogeneous fluid contents on T1- and T2-weighted fat-suppressed images, and focal areas of hyperintensity on both T1- and T2-weighted sequences suggestive of solid components (refer to figure 1; only T2 fat-sat images are provided). The caudal mass’s separation was indistinct, with apparent continuity to the posterior leiomyoma previously noted on ultrasound. No lymphadenopathy was detected on MRI.

Differential diagnosis:

During ultrasonographic assessment, the cystic lesions closely resembled two large decidualized endometriomas, with the more caudal cyst seemingly attached to a posterior intramural-subserosal leiomyoma of the uterus that measured 58×71 mm.

Magnetic resonance imaging revealed two well-defined, unilocular adnexal cysts characterized by enlarged blood vessels and heterogeneous internal fluid (refer to figure 1). The caudal lesion appeared to be adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma demonstrating cystic degeneration, measuring 58×71 mm.

Because of the significant size of both the gravid uterus and the large masses, no stalk or vessel supplying the upper lesion was visualized, which pointed towards an ovarian origin—most notably endometriomas—especially given the mildly elevated CA-125 observed during the second trimester (see discussion).

The differential diagnosis, considering imaging via ultrasound and MRI, included both decidualized endometriomas and cystic degenerating leiomyomas. The minor increase in CA-125 was more supportive of an endometrioma for the superior cystic mass; however, the lower mass showed continuity with uterine tissue, aligning more with a leiomyomatous origin. The patient's dyspnoea was thought to result from mechanical compression exerted by the voluminous abdominal tumors, as there was no clinical evidence of cardiopulmonary impairment.

Treatment:

Given the deterioration in the patient's status (including abdominal pain attributable to the masses and dyspnea from restrictive lung disease secondary to the masses' dimensions), the need to establish a definitive diagnosis, potential complication risks (such as rupture, torsion, and infection), and anticipated uterine growth in parallel with fetal development, a laparotomy with a vertical midline incision from the xiphoid process to the pubic symphysis was performed at 21 weeks and 6 days gestation following the acquisition of informed consent.

First, we collected two samples of the free peritoneal fluid for cultural and cytological purposes. Then, we explored the abdominal cavity, verifying the presence of fetal cardiac activity.

Unexpectedly, the cranial lesion was identified as a large, pedunculated, atypical leiomyoma of the uterus, exhibiting a cystic and firm, elastic consistency with significant vascularity; it showed initial signs of torsion and leakage, which may account for the fever noted during hospitalization (see figure 2). As a result, the surgical team chose to gently remove the mass at its uterine attachment using a double-clamp technique (see figure 2). After transecting the stalk, the exposed base was meticulously secured with a single absorbable suture. The caudal lesion, which arose from the uterine torus, was retained in place to reduce potential obstetric complications. This lesion was immobile, with a smooth contour and soft quality, resembling a sizable cystic leiomyoma of intramural-subserosal origin.

The surgical procedure took 2 hours and 25 minutes to complete. The patient was administered progesterone prophylaxis at a dose of 100 mg intramuscularly beginning during the operation, which was continued at 100 mg intramuscularly daily until discharge. Subsequently, she was given hydroxyprogesterone caproate intramuscularly (341 mg in a single vial every three days as specified in the package insert) up to the scheduled delivery. For intraoperative antibiotic prophylaxis, cefazolin 2 g was given intravenously, and this was succeeded by postoperative intravenous cefazolin 1 g three times per day combined with clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily throughout her hospital stay, guided by the urine culture antibiogram, which revealed Klebsiella pneumoniae with high sensitivity to both cephalosporins and clarithromycin.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following the operation, the patient experienced worsening anemia (Hb 7.5 g/L, HCT 24.3%), along with persistent iron deficiency, necessitating intravenous iron therapy (ferric carboxymaltose 1 g) and the administration of two units of blood. This resulted in improved laboratory values (Hb 10.0 g/L, HCT 30.4%). She was discharged without any complications 10 days postoperatively and began close obstetrical monitoring every three weeks. Follow-up assessments demonstrated fetal growth in the 86th percentile, as per the WHO fetal growth chart calculator (https://srhr.org/fetalgrowthcalculator/#/), as well as continued surveillance of the amniotic fluid index.

Both microbiological cultures and cytological analysis of the peritoneal fluid yielded negative results. Histopathological assessment demonstrated benign uterine leiomyomas with zones of ischemic necrosis adjacent to the twisted pedicle.

The location of the caudal myoma would likely have significantly impeded descent of the fetal head during the second stage of labor. Therefore, a planned cesarean section was carried out at 37 weeks’ gestation along the previous surgical incision, thereby reducing the likelihood of uterine scar rupture and achieving an optimal neonatal outcome.

The patient remains asymptomatic regarding her myoma, despite a minor decrease in the residual pelvic myoma’s size to 7.5 cm. She is breastfeeding successfully, yet wishes not to proceed with further surgical intervention at this time, given the partial reduction of the remaining leiomyomas following childbirth, her lack of symptoms, and satisfaction with her current status as a mother. The infant is developing appropriately.

Discussion:

Myomas can often be misdiagnosed as adnexal masses: the incidence during pregnancy is 0.3–10.1% and 1–6%, respectively .13

In this case, the primary assessment revealed abdominal masses resembling two large decidualized endometriomas, with the exception of the lower solid portion of the pelvic lesion, which was suggestive of a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine fibroid with cystic degeneration measuring 58 × 71 mm, as identified by both ultrasound and MRI. Given the lack of established protocols for managing pelvic masses during pregnancy, the multidisciplinary team judged that planned surgical intervention might reduce the likelihood of preterm labor.

Throughout pregnancy, serum CA-125 levels typically exhibit two peaks: the first occurs during the first trimester (median 23 U/mL, p<0.00001), and the second during the third trimester (median 21 U/mL, p<0.001), both significantly higher in comparison to the second trimester median level (14.0 U/mL). In contrast, the patient in this report demonstrated a CA-125 level of 41.8 U/mL, which exceeds the median value seen in the second trimester.

CA-125 is primarily utilized as a tumor marker for ovarian carcinoma, with a commonly accepted threshold of ≥35 U/mL, particularly yielding greater diagnostic accuracy—including higher area under the curve, positive predictive value, sensitivity, and specificity—in women over the age of 50. Nonetheless, elevated CA-125 levels may also be observed in various non-malignant conditions, such as endometriosis and adenomyosis (typically exceeding 19 U/mL), pelvic inflammatory disease, menstrual periods, both early and late stages of pregnancy (median values of 23 U/mL and 21 U/mL, respectively), risk of miscarriage, as well as uterine myomas (where levels usually remain below 19 U/mL).57

The main factors guiding the clinical management included the identification of large, highly vascularized masses containing both solid and cystic regions, which posed a real risk for complications such as rupture, torsion, or infection. Additionally, the patient's condition was deteriorating, as evidenced by increasing pain and difficulty breathing. There was also a concern about potential mass enlargement, which could elevate the likelihood of hypoxia, necrosis, and degenerative changes. Laboratory findings showed a mild elevation in tumor markers (CA-125 = 41.8 U/mL). Finally, the size of the mass was likely to restrict the necessary physiological expansion of the uterus.

Given the potential neoplastic nature and indeterminate origin of the lesions, a non-contrast MRI was performed following the ultrasound to improve diagnostic precision. Current guidelines advise against the use of gadolinium-based contrast agents in MRI during pregnancy unless absolutely indicated for certain rheumatologic, inflammatory, or infiltrative dermatoses, or when there is a threat of stillbirth or neonatal mortality. As a result, the multidisciplinary team opted for an MRI study without contrast and avoided imaging at field strengths above 1.5-T, which is regarded as reasonably safe beyond the first trimester.

In this case, the multidisciplinary team—including surgeons, gynecologists, and obstetricians—chose to perform a longitudinal laparotomic surgical approach after securing specific written informed consent from the patient. During the surgical evaluation, the superior mass was found to have a pedicle, which posed a substantial risk for an acute, classic complication. This observation justified restricting the intervention to removal of the upper myoma only. In addition, fetal viability was monitored intraoperatively through repeated and periodic transvaginal ultrasonography, accompanied by the preventive use of progesterone administered during surgery.

According to a large multicentric study conducted by Zhao et al, it is mandatory to check and document the fetal heart rate before, during and after the surgery.

Typically, surgical intervention is postponed until postpartum, favoring a conservative and medical management strategy. Nevertheless, Sakinci et al recently reported findings from a tertiary center indicating no statistically significant differences in preoperative and postoperative hemoglobin levels or the need for blood transfusions (p >0.05) between women who underwent caesarean section (CS) with myomectomy and those without fibroids undergoing CS alone. However, both hospitalization duration and operative time were markedly increased in the CS myomectomy group (p =0.001 and p =0.001, respectively). The average diameter of the myomas was 8.3 ± 4.1 cm, and a statistically significant positive association was found between the size of the myoma and length of hospital stay (p =0.01).

The same research study also showed a statistically significant and inverse correlation between the size of the myoma and the delivery week (p = 0.035).

Uterine leiomyomas exhibit a non-linear pattern of size variation throughout pregnancy and the postpartum period. Specifically, leiomyoma size enlarges by 6.90% weekly: showing an increase of 6.12% (p<0.001) from the first to the second trimester and an increase of 3.10%±0.39% from the second to the third trimester (p<0.05). Thus, the rate of leiomyoma growth is significantly greater in the first trimester compared to later stages of gestation. Conversely, a notable reduction in myoma size occurs during the third trimester and up to 6 weeks postpartum, with an average decrease of 44.25%±36.05% (p<0.05).

These papers showed the importance of an accurate US examination of the size, location, number and distance of uterine myomas from the placenta before the surgical approach.

When uterine myomas are identified during pregnancy, management is typically conservative rather than surgical because of potential complications such as significant hemorrhage, pregnancy loss, preterm delivery, and uterine rupture. Nevertheless, myomectomy may become necessary in situations involving complicated lesions, such as torsion of a pedunculated myoma, or in cases with ongoing symptoms unresponsive to conservative measures. Surgical intervention should be as brief as possible to help maintain the pregnancy and can be performed laparoscopically, ideally utilizing the open Hasson technique, particularly when gestational age is low (first and early second trimester), the mass is of limited size, and the myoma is located in a non-intramural region. This minimally invasive strategy is associated with reduced maternal morbidity and increased likelihood of vaginal delivery. Despite these advantages, the current case highlights the frequent selection bias towards laparotomy over laparoscopy for larger, multiple, intramural, or critically located myomas—circumstances often linked with an elevated rate of cesarean section. In this instance, a large myoma adherent to the posterior wall at the uterine isthmus would have presented a substantial barrier to vaginal birth.
