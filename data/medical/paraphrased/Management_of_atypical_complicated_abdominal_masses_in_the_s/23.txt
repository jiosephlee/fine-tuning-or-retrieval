Title: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

Rosalba De Nola, Emma Bassi, Cataldo Paletta, Mario Vicino

Abstract:

A woman in her 40s, who had not previously given birth, presented in her second trimester with abdominal pain and shortness of breath. Ultrasound imaging identified two sizable abdominal masses: the superior mass extended throughout the left subphrenic area and reached the uterine fundus, while the inferior mass occupied the pouch of Douglas. The patient underwent laparotomy, and the pelvic mass was left undisturbed. She received routine obstetric care and later underwent a scheduled caesarean section, which yielded a favorable outcome for both mother and child. For pregnant patients with significant symptomatic masses, thorough assessment via bimanual examination and safe imaging techniques, including ultrasound and non-contrast MRI, is crucial. Surgical intervention should be reserved for cases with suspicion of malignancy or clinical complications, with a priority on maintaining the pregnancy and avoiding unnecessary procedures. The approach to delivery should be determined by the dimensions, number, and anatomical positioning of the uterine masses.

Background:

Identifying and treating atypical or symptomatic abdominal masses in pregnant patients involves substantial clinical complexity. Available diagnostic tools include physical examination, ultrasound (US) with colour Doppler, and non-contrast magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Preoperative US is especially helpful in detecting symptomatic uterine leiomyomas situated on the anterior aspect of the gravid uterus, either submucosal or pedunculated in nature. For multiple, sizable, intramural, or posterior leiomyomas, MRI provides enhanced diagnostic accuracy, allowing for precise measurement of the separation between the leiomyoma and the uterine cavity.

The usefulness of serum tumour markers during pregnancy remains controversial. For example, CA-125 concentrations naturally increase during the first trimester and subsequently return to baseline, rendering isolated values insufficient for diagnosis. However, monitoring levels serially may yield important information to distinguish diagnoses or for ongoing surveillance. Effective management hinges on thorough multidisciplinary risk evaluation, aiming to balance obstetric considerations against the unusual and intricate nature of such clinical presentations.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 40s, obese with a body mass index of 33, and G1P0A1, presented during the second trimester of her pregnancy (21 weeks gestational age) with intense, sudden onset abdominal pain localized to the upper left quadrant and pelvic region, each rated as 5 on the Visual Analogue Scale, accompanied by mild shortness of breath. Routine screenings from her first and second trimesters indicated a low risk for aneuploidies (as confirmed by non-invasive prenatal testing) and normal fetal morphology.

She was first evaluated in the emergency department by a multidisciplinary team. Her oxygen saturation was normal (median 97%), thoracic examination showed a normal heart rate (median 74 bpm) and blood pressure (median 100/60 mm Hg), and the ECG findings were unremarkable. Subsequently, she was admitted to the Obstetrical and Gynecological unit. The abdomen appeared disproportionately enlarged for her gestational age, with a tender swelling and a firm, taut mass measuring 18 cm occupying the entire upper left quadrant, absent clinical signs of ascites.

Bimanual vaginal examination revealed a stationary, firm, rounded pelvic mass measuring approximately 9 cm, partially protruding at the posterior fornix.

The patient was treated with intravenous paracetamol (1000 mg twice daily) and placed in a semi-upright position, theoretically to decrease diaphragmatic compression caused by the gravid uterus and large abdominal mass. This resulted in slight symptom improvement, with pain scores decreasing from 5 to 1 on the Visual Analogue Scale, without any need for supplemental oxygen or ventilation.

While hospitalized, she developed a low-grade fever. A nasopharyngeal swab PCR for severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 was negative, but a urinary tract infection with Klebsiella pneumoniae was identified. Blood tests showed iron deficiency anemia with hemoglobin at 9.9 g/L (reference range 12.0–16.6 g/L) and serum ferritin of 25 mcg/L (reference range 15–300 mcg/L), a mild increase in C-reactive protein (1.39 mg/dL; reference range 0–0.3 mg/dL), and elevated neutrophil counts (white blood cells 11.29×10^3/uL [normal range 4–10×10^3/uL], neutrophils 9.27×10^3/uL [normal range 2.8–5.5×10^3/uL]), which were interpreted as pregnancy-related.

Investigations:

Ultrasound revealed two sizeable, unilocular cystic masses with smooth borders and peripheral blood flow. The superior mass measured 18×12×7 cm, spanning from the left subphrenic space down to the uterine fundus. The inferior mass measured 9.5×8×8 cm and appeared to be adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration (58×71 mm).

Serum tumour markers, including CA 19–9, CA 15–3, CEA, β2-microglobulin, and LDH, were found to be within reference ranges. In contrast, CA-125 was increased at 41.8 U/mL (reference <35 U/mL) and demonstrated a gradual rise from 36 U/mL recorded two weeks prior. This suggested a mild upward trend in CA-125 levels. The patient subsequently underwent MRI without administration of contrast, which revealed masses with thick walls (1.4 cm), fluid content that appeared heterogeneous on T1 and T2 fat-saturated sequences, and solid areas that were focally hyperintense on both T1 and T2-weighted images (see figure 1; only T2 fat-saturated images presented). The demarcation of the caudal mass was indistinct, as it seemed contiguous with the posterior leiomyoma previously observed by ultrasound. MRI did not show any lymphadenopathy.

Differential diagnosis:

During ultrasonographic assessment, the cystic lesions resembled two large decidualized endometriomas, and the inferior cyst appeared to be adherent to an intramural-subserosal leiomyoma located on the posterior uterine wall, measuring 58×71 mm.

Magnetic resonance imaging revealed two rounded, unilocular adnexal cysts characterized by hypertrophied vessels and heterogeneous fluid content (refer to figure 1). The lower mass was observed to be attached to a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration and measuring 58×71 mm.

Owing to the spatial obstruction caused by the gravid uterus and the substantial size of the masses, no identifiable pedicle or nutrient vessels for the upper lesion were visualized, suggesting a diagnosis of ovarian masses, particularly endometriomas, in light of mild CA-125 elevation detected during the second trimester of gestation (see discussion).

Considering the sonographic and MRI characteristics, the differential diagnosis comprised decidualized endometriomas and cystic degeneration within leiomyomas. Slightly raised CA-125 levels favored endometrioma as the etiology for the upper cyst, whereas the anatomical continuity with uterine tissue pointed toward a myomatous nature for the caudal mass. The patient’s dyspnea was ascribed to mechanical compression from the voluminous intra-abdominal lesions, with no indications of pulmonary or cardiac compromise.

Treatment:

Given the progression of clinical symptoms—including pain from the mass and shortness of breath caused by restrictive lung disease related to the abdominal masses—as well as the need to establish a definitive diagnosis, concerns about potential complications (such as rupture, torsion, or infection), and the likelihood of increasing uterine size with ongoing fetal development, a laparotomy was performed at 21 weeks and 6 days gestation following informed consent, using a longitudinal incision from the xiphoid to pubic region.

Initially, two samples of peritoneal fluid were obtained for both cultural and cytological examination. Subsequent inspection of the abdominal cavity confirmed ongoing fetal cardiac activity.

Unexpectedly, the superior mass consisted of an enormous pedunculated leiomyoma of the uterus, which was cystic, atypical, tense-elastic, and highly vascular, and appeared initially twisted with leakage, likely responsible for the patient’s fever during admission (refer to figure 2). As a result, the surgical team opted to resect the mass from its uterine base with gentle application of a double clamp (see figure 2). The base was severed and subsequently closed using a single absorbable suture. The inferior mass, arising from the uterine torus, remained in place to limit obstetric risk. This lesion was immobile, with a smooth exterior and soft consistency, resembling a large cystic intramural-subserosal leiomyoma.

The procedure lasted 2 hours and 25 minutes. Importantly, the patient received intramuscular progesterone prophylaxis at a dose of 100 mg, commencing during surgery and continued at 100 mg intramuscularly daily until discharge. Afterwards, intramuscular hydroxyprogesterone caproate was administered (341 mg 1 vial intramuscularly every 3 days, following the manufacturer’s instructions) until the planned delivery. Cefazolin 2 g was given intravenously as antibiotic prophylaxis during surgery, followed by postoperative antibiotics: cefazolin 1 g intravenously three times daily plus clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily for the duration of hospitalization, tailored to the urine culture antibiogram, which identified Klebsiella pneumoniae as highly susceptible to cephalosporins and clarithromycin.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following the operation, the patient’s anaemia worsened (Hb 7.5 g/L, HCT 24.3%) and iron deficiency persisted, prompting the administration of intravenous ferric carboxymaltose (1 g) and two successful blood transfusions, resulting in improved haemoglobin (Hb 10.0 g/L) and haematocrit (HCT 30.4%). She was discharged in stable condition 10 days postoperatively and began rigorous obstetric monitoring every three weeks. The follow-up demonstrated ongoing fetal growth (86th percentile per the WHO fetal growth chart calculator at https://srhr.org/fetalgrowthcalculator/#/) and normal amniotic fluid index.

Testing of peritoneal fluid yielded negative results for both microbiological cultures and cytology. Pathology identified benign uterine leiomyomas and regions of ischaemic necrosis close to the twisted pedicle.

The caudal myoma would have greatly hindered the passage of the fetal head during the expulsion phase of labour. Consequently, an elective caesarean section was performed at the previous surgical site once the fetus reached 37 weeks’ gestation, thus lowering the risk of uterine scar rupture and resulting in an excellent neonatal outcome.

The patient currently experiences no symptoms attributable to the myoma. The residual pelvic myoma has slightly decreased in size to 7.5 cm. She is breastfeeding successfully and has chosen not to schedule additional surgery at this time, given the partial postpartum regression of the remaining leiomyomas, lack of symptoms, and satisfaction with her maternal role. The infant continues to thrive.

Discussion:

Myomas can often be misdiagnosed as adnexal masses: the incidence during pregnancy is 0.3–10.1% and 1–6%, respectively .13

In this case, the initial assessment revealed abdominal masses that resembled two large decidualised endometriomas, apart from a lower solid section of the pelvic mass which was suggestive of a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration measuring 58×71 mm, as observed on both ultrasound and MRI scans. Given the lack of specific guidelines for managing pelvic masses during pregnancy, the multidisciplinary team judged that elective surgical intervention might reduce the likelihood of preterm birth.

Throughout pregnancy, serum CA-125 concentrations typically exhibit two peaks: one during the first trimester (median 23 U/mL, p<0.00001) and another in the third trimester (median 21 U/mL, p<0.001), in contrast to levels in the second trimester (median 14.0 U/mL). In this particular case, however, the CA-125 level was 41.8 U/mL, which exceeds the typical second trimester median.

CA-125 is predominantly utilized as a tumor marker for ovarian carcinoma (current standard threshold ≥35 U/mL), with greater diagnostic efficacy—area under the curve, positive predictive value, sensitivity, and specificity—in women aged over 50. Nevertheless, elevated CA-125 levels may also be associated with several benign conditions including endometriosis and adenomyosis (>19 U/mL), pelvic inflammatory disease, menstruation, early as well as late pregnancy (median values of 23 and 21 U/mL, respectively), increased risk of miscarriage, and myoma (cut-off <19 U/mL).57

The primary factors influencing clinical management included the detection of large, vascularized lesions exhibiting both solid and cystic components, which carried a current risk for complications such as rupture, torsion, or infection; the patient’s deteriorating symptoms, including escalating pain and worsening dyspnea; potential for further tumor growth increasing the likelihood of hypoxia, tissue necrosis, or degeneration; mildly elevated tumor marker levels (CA-125 at 41.8 U/mL); and the concern that the mass could hinder normal uterine expansion.

Given the suspicion for neoplastic pathology and ambiguity regarding the lesions’ origin, the patient subsequently underwent a non-contrast MRI to improve diagnostic clarity following sonographic assessment. As per current guidelines, gadolinium-enhanced MRI is not recommended during pregnancy unless absolutely indicated—for example, in cases involving rheumatologic, inflammatory, or infiltrative dermatologic disorders, or when there is a risk for stillbirth or neonatal demise. Consequently, the care team elected to conduct a non-contrast MRI, using equipment below 1.5-T, as this modality is generally accepted as safe beyond the first trimester.

In this case, the multidisciplinary team—including surgeons, gynecologists, and obstetricians—decided to perform a longitudinal laparotomic surgical intervention after receiving specific written informed consent from the patient. During intraoperative evaluation, the upper lesion was found to possess a pedicle, which posed a considerable risk for developing an acute, classic complication. Consequently, the decision was made to limit the procedure to an upper myomectomy. Continuous and intermittent transvaginal ultrasound examinations were used to assess fetal viability throughout the surgery, with the addition of prophylactic intraoperative progesterone administration for further support.

A large multicenter investigation by Zhao et al emphasizes the necessity of documenting fetal heart rate prior to, during, and following the operative procedure.

Typically, surgical intervention is postponed until the postpartum period, favoring medical management and observation. Nonetheless, Sakinci et al recently reported from a tertiary center that there were no statistically significant differences regarding preoperative and postoperative hemoglobin levels or rates of blood transfusion (p >0.05) between women undergoing caesarean section (CS) combined with myomectomy and those undergoing CS alone for cases without myomas. However, both length of hospital stay and operative time were notably increased in the cohort who had CS with myomectomy (p =0.001 for both). The average myoma diameter was 8.3 ± 4.1 cm, with a statistically significant positive association between myoma size and duration of hospitalization (p =0.01).

The same study identified a statistically significant, inverse association between myoma size and gestational age at delivery (p = 0.035).

Uterine leiomyomas exhibit a non-linear pattern of size variation throughout pregnancy and the puerperium. Specifically, leiomyoma size grows by 6.90% per week: increasing by 6.12% (p<0.001) between the first and second trimesters, and by 3.10% ± 0.39% from the second to third trimesters (p<0.05). Thus, the rate of leiomyoma growth is notably higher in the first trimester compared with subsequent stages of gestation. Conversely, during the third trimester and within 6 weeks postpartum, myoma size decreased significantly (average reduction 44.25% ± 36.05%, p<0.05).

These publications highlight the critical role of thorough ultrasonographic evaluation of uterine myomas—including assessment of size, number, location, and proximity to the placenta—prior to surgical intervention.

When uterine myomas are detected in pregnancy, conservative management is typically favored over surgical options, due to concerns for severe hemorrhage, pregnancy loss, preterm labor, and uterine rupture. Nonetheless, myomectomy may be warranted for complicated cases such as torsion of a pedunculated fibroid or unrelenting symptoms refractory to conservative therapy. Surgical procedures should be kept as brief as possible to safeguard the pregnancy, with laparoscopy using the open Hasson technique preferred in early gestational ages (first and early second trimester), smaller lesion volumes, and non-intramural locations. This minimally invasive method is associated with reduced maternal morbidity and a greater likelihood of vaginal delivery. Nevertheless, as illustrated in the current report, there remains a frequent tendency to select laparotomy instead of laparoscopy when treating larger, multiple, intramural, or critically sited myomas—a decision that may increase rates of cesarean section (CS), particularly with substantial myomas adherent to the posterior wall of the uterine isthmus, which can impede vaginal delivery.
