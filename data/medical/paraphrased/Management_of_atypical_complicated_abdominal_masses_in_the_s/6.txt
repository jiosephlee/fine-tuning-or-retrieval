Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

Rosalba De Nola, Emma Bassi, Cataldo Paletta, Mario Vicino

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her 40s, who had never given birth, reported experiencing abdominal pain and shortness of breath in her second trimester. Ultrasound imaging identified two sizable abdominal masses: the superior lesion spanned the entire left subphrenic region and reached up to the uterine fundus, while the inferior lesion occupied the pouch of Douglas. The patient was managed with laparotomy, during which the lower mass was left undisturbed in the pelvis. She was subsequently monitored with standard obstetric care and later underwent a planned caesarean section, which led to favorable maternal and neonatal results. When encountering substantial symptomatic masses in pregnancy, a thorough assessment utilizing bimanual palpation along with safe imaging approaches—such as ultrasonography and non-contrast MRI—is crucial. Surgical intervention should be reserved for situations with oncological concern or complications, with a continual emphasis on maintaining pregnancy and preventing unnecessary procedures. The decision regarding the method of delivery ought to be based on the characteristics—size, quantity, and anatomical position—of the uterine masses.

Background:

Managing and diagnosing atypical or symptomatic abdominal masses in pregnant patients pose notable clinical difficulties. Available diagnostic modalities include physical examination, ultrasound (US) with color Doppler imaging, and non-contrast MRI, with more invasive approaches being contraindicated. Preoperative US is especially effective for localizing symptomatic uterine leiomyomas situated on the anterior aspect of the gravid uterus, regardless of whether they are submucosal or pedunculated in nature. MRI, however, demonstrates greater precision in the detection and characterization of multiple, sizable, intramural, or posteriorly positioned leiomyomas by accurately measuring the separation between the myoma and the uterine cavity.

The clinical utility of serum tumour markers during gestation is still contentious. For instance, CA-125 values are known to rise physiologically during the first trimester before returning to baseline, which renders single-point measurements non-diagnostic. In contrast, serial assessments may reveal meaningful patterns to support differential diagnoses and guide follow-up. A multidisciplinary approach to risk evaluation is essential, taking into account the infrequent and complex nature of these cases while carefully weighing obstetric considerations.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 40s with obesity (body mass index of 33), gravidity 1, parity 0, and one abortion (G1P0A1), was evaluated during her second trimester of pregnancy at 21 weeks' gestation due to the onset of severe, acute abdominal pain localized in the upper left quadrant and pelvic region (Visual Analogue Scale=5 for both), accompanied by mild shortness of breath. Both her first- and second-trimester prenatal screenings indicated a low likelihood for aneuploidies, a result also supported by non-invasive prenatal testing, and revealed typical fetal morphology.

She initially presented to the emergency department for a comprehensive, multidisciplinary assessment. Her oxygen saturation was within the normal range (median 97%), while thoracic examination demonstrated a regular heart rate (median 74 bpm) and stable blood pressure (median 100/60 mm Hg). The electrocardiogram results were unremarkable. She was then admitted to the Obstetrical and Gynaecological Division. The abdominal examination revealed disproportionate distension for her gestational age, with an exquisitely tender, taut-elastic mass, approximately 18 cm in size, occupying the entire upper left abdominal quadrant, but no clinical evidence of ascites.

A bimanual vaginal assessment identified a fixed, roughly 9 cm, rounded and firm mass within the lesser pelvis, partially protruding into the posterior fornix.

Analgesia (paracetamol 1000 mg administered intravenously twice daily) and maintaining a semi-orthostatic position—presumably to decrease diaphragmatic pressure from the gravid uterus and significant intra-abdominal masses—offered partial symptom relief. Her pain score decreased from 5 to 1 on the Visual Analogue Scale without the need for supplemental oxygen or respiratory support.

During her inpatient course, the patient developed a low-grade fever. Consequently, a nasopharyngeal PCR test for severe acute respiratory syndrome Coronavirus 2 was performed and found negative; conversely, a urine culture identified a Klebsiella pneumoniae infection. Laboratory investigations further revealed iron deficiency anaemia, as evidenced by a haemoglobin (Hb) of 9.9 g/L (reference range 12.0–16.6 g/L) and serum ferritin of 25 mcg/L (reference range 15–300 mcg/L). There was also a mild elevation of C reactive protein to 1.39 mg/dL (reference range 0–0.3 mg/dL), along with leukocytosis—white blood cell count 11.29×10^3 /uL (reference 4–10×10^3 /uL) and neutrophils 9.27×10^3 /uL (reference 2.8–5.5×10^3 /uL)—which was consistent with physiological changes typical of pregnancy.

Investigations:

Ultrasound evaluation revealed the presence of two sizable, unilocular cystic lesions characterized by smooth margins and peripheral vascularization. The superior mass measured 18×12×7 cm, spanning from the left subphrenic space to the level of the uterine fundus. The inferior mass measured 9.5×8×8 cm and appeared to be adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma that exhibited cystic degeneration (58×71 mm).

Assessment of tumour markers (CA 19–9, CA 15–3, CEA, β2-microglobulin, and LDH) returned values within normal parameters; however, CA-125 was found to be elevated (41.8 U/mL; normal <35 U/mL), with an incremental rise noted from 36 U/mL measured two weeks previously. This indicated a mild upward trend in the serum CA-125 concentration. Consequently, the patient was further evaluated with a non-contrast MRI, which demonstrated cystic lesions with thickened walls (1.4 cm), inhomogeneous fluid signals on both T1 and T2 fat-saturated sequences, and discrete solid components showing focal hyperintensity on T1-T2 weighted images (refer to figure 1; only T2 fat-sat sequences provided). The anatomical delineation of the caudal mass was ambiguous, and it appeared to be contiguous with the posterior leiomyoma previously noted on ultrasound. No lymphadenopathy was detected on MRI.

Differential diagnosis:

During the ultrasound examination, the cystic lesions closely resembled two large decidualised endometriomas. The lower (caudal) cyst appeared to be attached to an intramural-subserosal leiomyoma located on the posterior wall of the uterus, which measured 58×71 mm.

MRI imaging revealed two rounded, unilocular adnexal cysts characterized by enlarged vessels and heterogeneous internal fluid (refer to figure 1). The caudal lesion seemed adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration, also measuring 58×71 mm.

Owing to the spatial constraints imposed by the gravid uterus and the considerable size of the masses, a connecting stalk or a vascular pedicle feeding the upper mass was not visualized. This led to a diagnostic leaning toward ovarian masses—most notably, endometriomas—particularly in the context of mildly elevated CA-125 levels during the second trimester (see discussion).

Taking into account the sonographic and MRI findings, the main differential considerations included decidualised endometriomas and leiomyomas undergoing cystic degeneration. The mild increase in CA-125 supported the likelihood of an endometrioma for the superior cyst; however, its anatomic association with uterine tissue made a myomatous etiology more plausible for the caudal mass. The patient’s dyspnoea was considered a result of mechanical pressure exerted by the large abdominal masses, in the absence of any detectable pulmonary or cardiac pathology.

Treatment:

Given the progression of clinical symptoms—including abdominal pain attributable to the mass and dyspnoea caused by restrictive lung disease secondary to the size of the abdominal masses—along with the need for definitive diagnostic clarification, the potential for complications such as rupture, torsion, or infection, and anticipated uterine enlargement with fetal growth, the patient consented to and underwent a laparotomy with a longitudinal xiphos-pubic incision at 21 weeks and 6 days gestation.

Initially, two samples of free peritoneal fluid were obtained for both culture and cytology. Subsequently, the abdominal cavity was systematically examined, confirming ongoing fetal cardiac activity.

Unexpectedly, the cranial lesion proved to be a substantial pedunculated leiomyoma of the uterus, displaying atypical cystic features, a taut-elastic consistency, marked vascularity, initial torsion, and evidence of leakage—findings likely responsible for the patient's febrile episodes during admission (see figure 2). Consequently, the surgical team proceeded with cautious excision of the mass at its uterine base using a double clamp technique (see figure 2). After transection of the stalk, meticulous hemostasis was achieved by closing the exposed base with a single absorbable suture. The caudal mass, which originated from the uterine torus, was left undisturbed to minimize obstetric risk. This lesion presented as immobile, with a smooth surface and soft, cystic intramural-subserosal consistency.

The operation was completed in 2 hours and 25 minutes. Prophylactic progesterone (100 mg intramuscularly) was initiated during surgery and continued daily until discharge. Following this, the patient was switched to intramuscular caproate hydroxyprogesterone (341 mg every three days, as per manufacturer’s guidelines) through to scheduled delivery. For infection prophylaxis, cefazolin 2 g was administered intravenously intraoperatively, followed by a postoperative regimen of cefazolin 1 g IV three times daily and clarithromycin 500 mg orally twice daily, tailored to the antibiogram from urine culture (Klebsiella pneumoniae, highly sensitive to cephalosporins and clarithromycin) for the duration of her hospital stay.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following surgery, the patient's anaemia worsened (Hb 7.5 g/L, HCT 24.3%) and iron deficiency persisted, necessitating intravenous administration of ferric carboxymaltose (1 g) and two successful blood transfusions, which improved haemoglobin and haematocrit values (Hb 10.0 g/L, HCT 30.4%). She was discharged without incident 10 days postoperatively and commenced close obstetric monitoring at 3-week intervals. This surveillance demonstrated appropriate fetal development (86th percentile per the WHO fetal growth chart calculator, https://srhr.org/fetalgrowthcalculator/#/), and stable amniotic fluid measurements.

Analysis of the peritoneal fluid returned negative results on both cultural and cytological assays. Histopathological evaluation identified benign uterine leiomyomas and showed regions of ischaemic necrosis in proximity to the twisted pedicle.

The position of the caudal myoma posed a significant barrier to fetal head descent during the second stage of labour. Consequently, an elective caesarean section was conducted via the existing surgical incision once the fetus reached 37 weeks gestation, thereby reducing the likelihood of uterine scar rupture and resulting in a favourable neonatal outcome.

Currently, the patient is asymptomatic with respect to the myoma, though the residual pelvic myoma has marginally diminished to 7.5 cm in diameter. She has been breastfeeding successfully and has chosen not to schedule further surgery at this time, given the observed partial regression of the residual leiomyomas postpartum, her lack of symptoms, and satisfaction with her motherhood experience. The infant is developing normally.

Discussion:

Myomas can often be misdiagnosed as adnexal masses: the incidence during pregnancy is 0.3–10.1% and 1–6%, respectively .13

In this instance, initial assessments identified abdominal masses that primarily resembled two large decidualised endometriomas. However, the inferior solid portion of the pelvic mass exhibited characteristics consistent with a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma displaying cystic degeneration, measuring 58×71 mm on both US and MRI scans. Given the lack of established protocols for the management of pelvic masses during pregnancy, the multidisciplinary team determined that planned surgical intervention might reduce the likelihood of preterm birth.

Typically, maternal serum CA-125 concentrations peak twice during gestation: first in the initial trimester (median 23 U/mL, p<0.00001), and again in the third trimester (median 21 U/mL, p<0.001), both significantly elevated compared to second trimester values (median 14.0 U/mL). In contrast, the patient discussed here exhibited a CA-125 concentration of 41.8 U/mL, which surpasses the typical median for the second trimester.

While CA-125 serves predominantly as a tumor marker in the diagnosis of ovarian carcinoma (with a standard cut-off value of ≥35 U/mL), especially for women older than 50 years (where it has higher diagnostic accuracy and predictive metrics), it is also elevated in numerous benign gynecological conditions. These include endometriosis and adenomyosis (commonly >19 U/mL), pelvic inflammatory disease, menstruation, early and late pregnancy (median levels 23 and 21 U/mL, respectively), risk of miscarriage, and myomas (usually with a threshold of <19 U/mL).57

Several critical factors influenced the clinical decision-making process. These included the identification of large, highly vascularized masses displaying both solid and cystic areas, which posed an immediate risk for complications such as rupture, torsion, or infection. The patient's clinical presentation—marked by increasing pain and progressive dyspnoea—along with the potential for further growth of the masses and the consequent risks of hypoxia, necrosis, and tissue degeneration, were important considerations. Additionally, a mild elevation in tumor markers (CA-125=41.8 U/mL) and the limited intra-abdominal space available to accommodate continued physiological uterine expansion were also taken into account.

Given the uncertain etiology of the masses and their potential neoplastic nature, a non-contrast MRI was performed after the initial ultrasound to improve diagnostic precision. Following current guidelines, gadolinium-enhanced MRI is generally contraindicated during pregnancy and reserved only for situations involving significant rheumatological, inflammatory, or skin-infiltrative diseases, or when there is a risk of stillbirth or neonatal mortality. For these reasons, the multidisciplinary team elected to conduct a non-contrast MRI, ensuring that the field strength did not exceed 1.5-T, a threshold regarded as sufficiently safe beyond the first trimester.

In this case, the collaborative team—including surgeons, gynaecologists, and obstetricians—chose to perform a longitudinal laparotomic surgical intervention after securing explicit written informed consent from the patient. Intraoperative evaluation revealed that the superior lesion possessed a pedicle, thus posing a heightened likelihood of a sudden, classic complication. This observation served as the main justification for restricting the intervention to only an upper myomectomy. Continuous and periodic transvaginal ultrasound examinations were used to monitor fetal viability during the procedure, supplemented by the preventive use of intraoperative progesterone.

A comprehensive multicentre study by Zhao et al. stipulates that it is essential to monitor and record fetal heart rate before, throughout, and following the surgical procedure.

Conventionally, surgery is postponed until after delivery in favour of conservative medical management; however, a recent study conducted by Sakinci et al. in a tertiary care setting reported no statistically significant differences between women undergoing caesarean section (CS) plus myomectomy and those with no myoma undergoing only CS regarding both preoperative and postoperative haemoglobin levels or the need for blood transfusions ($p > 0.05$). Nevertheless, durations of surgery and hospital stay were considerably extended in the CS myomectomy group ($p = 0.001$ and $p = 0.001$, respectively). The mean diameter of the myoma was $8.3 \pm 4.1\,\text{cm}$, and a statistically significant positive correlation was found between the size of the myoma and the length of hospitalisation ($p = 0.01$).

The same study identified a statistically significant inverse relationship between myoma size and gestational age at delivery (p = 0.035).

Uterine leiomyomas exhibit a non-linear pattern of size variation throughout pregnancy and the postpartum period. Specifically, leiomyoma size was found to increase by 6.90% per week, with a 6.12% growth rate (p<0.001) between the first and second trimesters, and a more moderate weekly increase of 3.10%±0.39% between the second and third trimesters (p<0.05). This indicates that the most pronounced enlargement of uterine leiomyomas occurs in the first trimester, with the rate of growth diminishing in subsequent trimesters. Conversely, a marked reduction in myoma size was observed during the third trimester and within six weeks postpartum, with a mean decrease of 44.25%±36.05% (p<0.05).

These findings emphasize the necessity for meticulous ultrasonographic assessment of uterine myomas, particularly evaluating their dimensions, anatomical placement, quantity, and proximity to the placenta prior to surgical intervention.

When uterine myomas are detected during pregnancy, the standard management strategy favors conservative therapy rather than surgery, owing to the elevated risks of significant hemorrhage, miscarriage, preterm delivery, and uterine rupture. Nonetheless, myomectomy may be indicated in situations involving complicated lesions—such as torsion of a pedunculated myoma—or persistent symptomatology unresponsive to conservative measures. Surgical intervention should be brief to minimize risk to the pregnancy and may be accomplished using laparoscopy, especially when employing an open Hasson entry technique. This is most suitable for early gestational ages (first and early second trimesters), smaller myomas, and non-intramural locations. This minimally invasive method is associated with improved maternal outcomes and higher likelihood of vaginal delivery. In contrast, as demonstrated in the present case, there is often a selection bias toward performing laparotomy for larger, more numerous, intramural, or critically situated myomas. This approach is frequently chosen because such cases are linked to increased rates of cesarean section, particularly when a substantial myoma is attached to the posterior isthmic region of the uterus, creating a physical barrier to vaginal birth.
