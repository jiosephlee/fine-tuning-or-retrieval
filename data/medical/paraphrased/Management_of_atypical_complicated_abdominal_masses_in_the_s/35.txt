Title: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

Rosalba De Nola, Emma Bassi, Cataldo Paletta, Mario Vicino

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her 40s with no previous pregnancies presented in her second trimester with abdominal discomfort and shortness of breath. Ultrasound identified two sizable abdominal masses: the superior mass occupied the left subphrenic space and extended to the uterine fundus, while the inferior mass was located in the pouch of Douglas. She underwent a laparotomy, during which the pelvic mass remained undisturbed. Afterwards, routine obstetric monitoring was maintained, and an elective caesarean section was performed, yielding favorable maternal and neonatal outcomes. For pregnant patients with large symptomatic masses, thorough assessment utilizing bimanual examination and safe imaging, including ultrasonography and non-contrast MRI, is critical. Surgery should be reserved for cases with suspected malignancy or when complications arise, prioritizing pregnancy preservation and minimizing unnecessary interventions. Decisions about delivery should be guided by the mass size, quantity, and anatomical relationship to the uterus.

Background:

Managing atypical or symptomatic abdominal masses in pregnant patients involves considerable clinical difficulties. Available diagnostic tools are restricted to physical examination, ultrasound (US) with colour Doppler, and non-contrast MRI. Preoperative US is highly useful for detecting symptomatic uterine leiomyomas situated along the anterior wall of the uterus during pregnancy, including those with submucosal or pedunculated growth patterns. In comparison, MRI delivers improved diagnostic precision for cases involving numerous, large, intramural, or posterior fibroids, as it can accurately assess the separation between the myoma and the uterine cavity.

The clinical significance of serum tumour markers in pregnancy is still controversial. Specifically, CA-125 typically increases during the initial trimester and returns to baseline thereafter. Single measurements are, therefore, unreliable for diagnosis, whereas repeated assessments may offer informative trends to aid differential diagnosis and ongoing monitoring. Effective management hinges on a thorough, multidisciplinary evaluation of risks, aiming to weigh the potential obstetric complications against the unusual nature and intricacies of these presentations.

Case presentation:

An overweight woman in her 40s with a body mass index of 33 and a history of G1P0A1 presented to our facility during her second trimester at 21 weeks gestation, experiencing severe, acute abdominal discomfort localized to the left upper quadrant and pelvic regions (Visual Analogue Scale score 5 at each site), accompanied by mild shortness of breath. Routine screening tests from both the first and second trimesters indicated a reduced risk for aneuploidy, confirmed by non-invasive prenatal testing, and fetal anatomical assessments were unremarkable.

She initially sought care in the emergency department, where a comprehensive multidisciplinary work-up was performed. Her oxygen saturation averaged 97%, thoracic physical examination findings included a normal heart rate (median 74 bpm) and blood pressure (median 100/60 mm Hg), and her ECG was unremarkable. Subsequently, she was transferred to the Obstetrics and Gynecology service. Examination revealed that her abdominal girth was greater than expected for her gestational stage, with a firm, tender, taut-elastic mass spanning the entire left upper quadrant, measuring 18 cm, absent clinical indicators of ascites.

Upon bimanual vaginal examination, a fixed, rounded, firm mass approximately 9 cm in diameter was detected in the lesser pelvis, with partial protrusion into the posterior fornix.

Administration of intravenous paracetamol (1000 mg twice daily) in conjunction with positioning the patient semi-upright—which may have helped relieve diaphragmatic compression by the gravid uterus and substantial abdominal masses—led to mild symptomatic improvement. There was no need for supplemental oxygen or respiratory support, and pain scores decreased from 5 to 1 on the Visual Analogue Scale.

While hospitalized, the patient experienced a mild fever. Polymerase chain reaction testing for SARS-CoV-2 via nasopharyngeal swab returned negative; however, urinalysis identified a Klebsiella pneumoniae infection. Additional laboratory findings showed iron deficiency anemia with hemoglobin at 9.9 g/L (normal range 12.0–16.6 g/L), serum ferritin of 25 mcg/L (normal range 15–300 mcg/L), a slight elevation in C-reactive protein (1.39 mg/dL; normal range 0–0.3 mg/dL), and increased neutrophil count [total white blood cells 11.29×10^3/uL (normal range 4–10×10^3/uL); neutrophils 9.27×10^3/uL (normal range 2.8–5.5×10^3/uL)], consistent with expected changes during pregnancy.

Investigations:

Ultrasound revealed two sizable, unilocular cystic masses featuring smooth borders and blood flow at their periphery. The superior mass measured 18×12×7 cm, reaching from the left subphrenic area down to the fundus of the uterus. The inferior mass measured 9.5×8×8 cm and appeared attached to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma undergoing cystic degeneration (58×71 mm).

Tumor marker assays including CA 19–9, CA 15–3, CEA, β2-microglobulin, and LDH showed results within the reference range. However, CA-125 levels were elevated at 41.8 U/mL (reference: <35 U/mL) and had risen from 36 U/mL two weeks previously, indicating a mild upward trend. As a result, the patient underwent MRI without administration of contrast, which demonstrated thick-walled lesions (wall thickness of 1.4 cm), fluid with heterogeneous signals on T1 and T2 fat-suppressed sequences, and distinct hyperintense solid components on both T1 and T2 weighted images (refer to figure 1; only T2 fat-sat images provided). The caudal mass appeared to be inseparable from the posterior leiomyoma observed on ultrasound, with unclear delineation between the two. MRI detected no lymphadenopathy.

Differential diagnosis:

During ultrasonography, the cystic lesions appeared similar to two large decidualised endometriomas, with the lower cyst observed to be attached to a posterior uterine intramural-subserosal leiomyoma measuring 58×71 mm.

Magnetic resonance imaging identified two spherical, unilocular adnexal cysts exhibiting prominent vasculature and non-uniform fluid signals (see figure 1). The lower mass appeared contiguous with a posterior uterine intramural-subserosal myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration, also measuring 58×71 mm.

Owing to the large size of the gravid uterus and the voluminous lesions, neither a connecting stalk nor a vascular supply to the upper mass was visualized, leading to a presumptive diagnosis of ovarian origin, and specifically endometriomas, supported by a mild increase in CA-125 during the second trimester (refer to discussion).

Considering imaging findings from ultrasound and MRI, the main differential diagnoses were decidualised endometriomas and leiomyomas with cystic degeneration: although the minor CA-125 elevation indicated an endometrioma in the superior cyst, the caudal lesion’s apparent association with the uterine wall favored a myomatous origin. The patient's shortness of breath was ascribed to physical compression from the large intra-abdominal masses, with no cardiopulmonary abnormalities detected.

Treatment:

Given the progressive decline in clinical status—manifested by abdominal pain due to the mass and dyspnoea from restrictive lung disease related to the mass size—as well as the need for definitive diagnosis, potential complication risks (including rupture, torsion, and infection), and anticipated uterine enlargement with fetal development, the patient underwent a laparotomy with a midline xiphoid-to-pubic incision at 21 weeks and 6 days gestation after providing informed consent.

Initially, two samples of free peritoneal fluid were obtained for both microbiological culture and cytological examination. The abdominal cavity was then inspected, confirming ongoing fetal cardiac activity.

Unexpectedly, the superior mass proved to be a large, pedunculated, unusual cystic, and firm-elastic uterine leiomyoma with significant vascularity; it was initially twisted and showed signs of leakage, which likely accounted for the patient's febrile episode during hospitalization (see figure 2). The surgical team therefore elected to remove the mass from its uterine attachment cautiously, utilizing a double clamp (see figure 2). Following transection of the stalk, the base was meticulously secured with a single absorbable suture. The lower mass, which arose from the uterine torus, was left untouched to reduce obstetric risks. This lesion was fixed, displayed a smooth surface, and had a soft consistency resembling a large cystic intramural-subserosal leiomyoma.

The operative procedure lasted 2 hours and 25 minutes. Progesterone prophylaxis (100 mg intramuscularly) was administered during surgery and continued at the same daily dose until discharge. Afterwards, intramuscular hydroxyprogesterone caproate (341 mg, 1 ampoule every 3 days as specified in the package insert) was initiated and maintained up to the scheduled delivery. Intraoperative antibiotic prophylaxis included intravenous cefazolin 2 g, followed by postoperative cefazolin 1 g intravenously three times daily combined with clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily during the inpatient period, aligned with the urine culture antibiogram showing Klebsiella pneumoniae highly susceptible to cephalosporins and clarithromycin.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following the surgical intervention, the patient experienced exacerbation of anemia (Hb 7.5 g/L, HCT 24.3%) and continued iron deficiency, necessitating intravenous administration of ferric carboxymaltose (1 g) and successful transfusion of two units of blood, resulting in improved laboratory values (Hb 10.0 g/L, HCT 30.4%). Ten days postoperatively, she was discharged without complications and commenced close obstetric surveillance at three-week intervals, which demonstrated ongoing fetal growth (86th percentile, per the WHO fetal growth chart calculator at https://srhr.org/fetalgrowthcalculator/#/) and appropriate amniotic fluid index.

Analysis of the peritoneal fluid yielded negative results for both culture and cytology. Histopathological evaluation identified benign uterine leiomyomas with evidence of ischemic necrosis adjacent to the twisted pedicle.

The presence of the caudal myoma posed a significant impediment to fetal head descent during the second stage of labor; therefore, an elective cesarean section was performed along the prior surgical incision at 37 weeks gestation, effectively reducing the risk of uterine scar rupture and resulting in an excellent neonatal outcome.

At present, the patient does not experience symptoms attributable to the myoma, with the remaining pelvic leiomyoma decreasing slightly in size to 7.5 cm. She is breastfeeding successfully and has elected to postpone further surgery, given the partial regression of the residual leiomyomas postpartum, lack of symptoms, and fulfillment of her maternal goals. The infant continues to thrive.

Discussion:

Myomas can often be misdiagnosed as adnexal masses: the incidence during pregnancy is 0.3–10.1% and 1–6%, respectively .13

In this instance, initial assessment identified abdominal masses resembling two large decidualized endometriomas. However, the lower solid portion of the pelvic mass was suggestive of a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration, measuring 58×71 mm on both ultrasonography and MRI. Given the lack of definitive guidelines for management of pelvic masses during pregnancy, the multidisciplinary team determined that elective surgical intervention might reduce the likelihood of preterm birth.

Throughout pregnancy, serum CA-125 typically demonstrates two peaks: the first occurs in the first trimester (median 23 U/mL, p<0.00001) and the second in the third trimester (median 21 U/mL, p<0.001), both of which are higher compared to the second trimester median of 14.0 U/mL. In contrast, this case reported a CA-125 value of 41.8 U/mL, surpassing the median observed during the second trimester.

CA-125 is most commonly employed as a tumor marker for ovarian cancer (standard cut-off ≥35 U/mL), particularly in women older than 50 years, as it offers superior area under the curve, positive predictive value, sensitivity, and specificity. Nonetheless, elevated levels of CA-125 can also be associated with a variety of benign conditions, including endometriosis and adenomyosis (values >19 U/mL), pelvic inflammatory disease, menstruation, pregnancy in early and late stages (median values of 23 U/mL and 21 U/mL, respectively), increased risk of miscarriage, and myoma (cut-off <19 U/mL).57

The primary factors guiding the clinical approach included the identification of large, highly vascular masses with both solid and cystic characteristics, which posed an immediate risk for complications such as rupture, torsion, or infection; the patient’s deteriorating symptoms, including escalating pain and respiratory distress; concerns regarding further mass enlargement that could increase the likelihood of hypoxia, tissue necrosis, or degeneration; a mildly elevated tumor marker (CA-125=41.8 U/mL); and restricted abdominal space compromising normal uterine growth.

Given the potential neoplastic nature and ambiguous etiology of the masses, the patient underwent a non-contrast MRI to improve diagnostic accuracy following ultrasonography. As per current recommendations, gadolinium-enhanced MRI is generally avoided during pregnancy unless absolutely essential due to conditions such as rheumatologic, inflammatory, or infiltrative dermatoses, or in cases presenting risk of stillbirth or neonatal loss. Consequently, the multidisciplinary team elected to proceed with a non-contrast MRI, while also limiting the study to a 1.5-Tesla scanner, a modality considered relatively safe beyond the first trimester.

In this case, the multidisciplinary team, consisting of surgeons, gynecologists, and obstetricians, decided to undertake a longitudinal laparotomic surgical approach after acquiring specific written informed consent from the patient. During intraoperative evaluation, the upper lesion was found to have a pedicle, which posed a considerable risk for a sudden and typical complication. This finding served as the basis for restricting the intervention to a myomectomy of the upper mass alone. Continuous and intermittent transvaginal ultrasound assessments were used to monitor fetal viability throughout the surgical procedure, and intraoperative progesterone was administered prophylactically.

A large multicenter study by Zhao et al emphasizes the necessity of assessing and recording fetal heart rate preoperatively, intraoperatively, and postoperatively.

Surgical intervention is typically postponed until after delivery, with medical management and expectant observation preferred. Nevertheless, Sakinci et al recently conducted research at a tertiary care center that demonstrated no statistically significant difference between women undergoing cesarean section (CS) with myomectomy and those undergoing CS alone for patients without myomas, regarding preoperative and postoperative hemoglobin levels or the need for blood transfusion (p > 0.05). Despite this, the group receiving CS with myomectomy experienced notably longer hospitalization and surgery durations (p = 0.001 for both). The mean myoma diameter measured 8.3 ± 4.1 cm, and there was a significant positive relationship between myoma size and length of hospital stay (p = 0.01).

The same study demonstrated a statistically significant inverse relationship between myoma size and gestational age at delivery (p = 0.035).

Uterine leiomyomas exhibit a non-linear progression in size throughout pregnancy and the puerperium. Specifically, leiomyoma dimensions increase by 6.90% per week: 6.12% (p<0.001) from the first to the second trimester, and 3.10%±0.39% from the second to the third trimester (p<0.05). Consequently, the rate of myoma growth is markedly elevated during the first trimester compared to later stages of pregnancy. Conversely, during the third trimester and up to six weeks postpartum, the size of the myomas decreased significantly (mean reduction 44.25%±36.05%, p<0.05).

These studies emphasize the necessity of a thorough ultrasound assessment prior to surgical intervention, evaluating myoma size, anatomical position, number, and distance from the placenta.

For uterine myomas identified during pregnancy, conservative management is generally favored over surgery due to increased risks of significant hemorrhage, fetal loss, preterm delivery, and uterine rupture. Nonetheless, myomectomy should be contemplated for cases involving complications such as torsion of pedunculated myomas or persistent symptoms unresponsive to conservative measures. Any surgical procedure should be minimized in duration to preserve pregnancy and can be performed laparoscopically, with preference for the open Hasson entry technique in patients at a lower gestational age (first and early second trimesters), with smaller myomas, and when the tumor is not intramural. This minimally invasive technique offers improved maternal morbidity and allows for a greater likelihood of vaginal delivery. The case described here, however, illustrates a common selection bias favoring laparotomy instead of laparoscopy for larger, multiple, intramural, or critically located leiomyomas, a decision that may be linked to higher cesarean section rates, as exemplified by the presence of a substantial myoma adherent to the posterior isthmic uterine wall that could obstruct vaginal delivery.
