Title: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

Rosalba De Nola, Emma Bassi, Cataldo Paletta, Mario Vicino

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her 40s with no history of childbirth presented in her second trimester with abdominal pain and shortness of breath. Ultrasound examination identified two sizable abdominal masses: one superior lesion occupied the entire left subphrenic region, extending up to the uterine fundus, while the inferior lesion was located in the pouch of Douglas. Laparotomy was performed, with the pelvic (caudal) mass left undisturbed in the pelvis. The patient continued with standard prenatal monitoring and underwent a scheduled caesarean section, achieving an optimal outcome for both mother and infant. When pregnant patients have large, symptomatic masses, a thorough assessment utilizing bimanual palpation and safe imaging techniques—such as ultrasonography or non-contrast MRI—is crucial. Surgical intervention should be reserved for cases with suspected malignancy or clinical complications, prioritizing pregnancy preservation and preventing unnecessary procedures. The chosen delivery method must be individualized based on the size, quantity, and precise location of uterine masses.

Background:

Diagnosing and treating atypical or symptomatic abdominal masses in pregnant patients is clinically challenging. Diagnostic modalities are restricted to physical examination, ultrasound (US) with colour Doppler, and non-contrast magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Preoperative US is especially useful for detecting symptomatic uterine leiomyomas situated on the anterior aspect of the enlarging uterus, including both submucosal and pedunculated variants. MRI yields higher accuracy in visualising multiple, large, intramural, or posteriorly located fibroids, as it enables precise measurement of the distance between a myoma and the uterine cavity.

The significance of serum tumour markers in pregnancy is still uncertain. For example, CA-125 levels tend to increase during the first trimester and return to normal afterward. As such, single assessments are not definitive for diagnosis, while monitoring levels over time may reveal helpful trends for differential diagnosis and ongoing assessment. Managing these cases optimally demands thorough multidisciplinary evaluation of risk, weighing obstetric considerations against the uncommon and complex nature of these presentations.

Case presentation:

A woman in her forties with obesity (BMI 33), G1P0A1, presented at 21 weeks gestation (second trimester) reporting severe, sudden-onset abdominal pain in the upper left quadrant and pelvic regions, both scoring 5 on the Visual Analogue Scale, and mild shortness of breath. Her routine prenatal screening in the first and second trimesters indicated a low risk for aneuploidies, which was further corroborated by non-invasive prenatal testing, and fetal anatomical assessment was normal.

Upon presentation, she was evaluated in the emergency department by a multidisciplinary team. Her oxygen saturation averaged 97%, with physical exam findings of thoracic stability, heart rate at 74 bpm (median), and blood pressure measuring 100/60 mm Hg (median). ECG results were within normal limits. Subsequently, she was moved to the Obstetrics and Gynecology unit. Examination revealed that her abdomen appeared disproportionately large for her gestational age, and there was a tender, taut-elastic swelling measuring 18 cm in the upper left quadrant, without indications of ascites.

On bimanual vaginal examination, a fixed, rounded, firm mass approximately 9 cm was noted in the lesser pelvis, protruding partially into the posterior fornix.

Pain management was initiated using intravenous paracetamol (1000 mg twice daily) and positioning her in a semi-orthostatic posture to potentially alleviate diaphragmatic pressure from the uterus and abdominal masses. This resulted in modest symptom improvement, lowering her pain score from 5 to 1, without the need for supplemental oxygen or ventilatory assistance.

During her hospitalization, she experienced a mild febrile episode. Testing with PCR for SARS-CoV-2 via nasopharyngeal swab returned negative; however, urine cultures identified a Klebsiella pneumoniae infection. Blood investigations revealed iron deficiency anaemia, with haemoglobin at 9.9 g/L (reference range 12.0–16.6 g/L), and serum ferritin at 25 mcg/L (reference range 15–300 mcg/L). There was a slight elevation in C-reactive protein at 1.39 mg/dL (reference range 0–0.3 mg/dL), alongside increased neutrophils (total WBC count 11.29×10^3 /uL [reference 4–10×10^3 /uL] and neutrophils 9.27×10^3 /uL [reference 2.8–5.5×10^3 /uL]), which were considered related to physiological changes in pregnancy.

Investigations:

Ultrasound evaluation revealed two sizable, unilocular cystic lesions characterized by smooth borders and peripheral blood flow. The superior cyst measured 18×12×7 cm and extended from the left subphrenic area to the uterine fundus. The inferior cyst was 9.5×8×8 cm in size and appeared to be attached to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration (58×71 mm).

Serum tumour markers including CA 19–9, CA 15–3, CEA, β2-microglobulin, and LDH were all within reference ranges. In contrast, CA-125 was elevated at 41.8 U/mL (normal <35 U/mL) and demonstrated a gradual rise, up from 36 U/mL two weeks prior. This indicated a mild upward trend in CA-125 levels. Consequently, the patient underwent a non-contrast MRI, which showed thickened walls (1.4 cm), inhomogeneous fluid contents on T1 and T2 fat-sat weighted sequences, and localized hyperintense solid areas on both T1 and T2 weighted images (refer to figure 1; only T2 fat-sat images displayed). The caudal cyst lacked distinct separation and appeared continuous with the posterior leiomyoma noted previously during ultrasound. MRI did not reveal any lymphadenopathy.

Differential diagnosis:

During the ultrasound assessment, the cystic lesions resembled two large decidualised endometriomas, with the more caudal cyst appearing attached to an intramural-subserosal fibroid located on the posterior uterine wall, which measured 58×71 mm.

Magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated two well-defined, unilocular adnexal cysts featuring enlarged vessels and uneven fluid content (see figure 1). The caudal lesion appeared contiguous with a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine fibroid exhibiting cystic degeneration, measuring 58×71 mm.

The substantial size of the gravid uterus and the sizable masses prevented visualization of any connecting pedicle or vascular supply to the upper lesion, which shifted diagnostic consideration towards ovarian origins, particularly endometriomas, supported by mildly increased CA-125 levels noted during the second trimester of pregnancy (see discussion).

Evaluation of sonographic and MRI characteristics prompted differential considerations including decidualised endometriomas and cystically degenerating fibroids: a slight CA-125 elevation was more indicative of endometrioma for the superior cyst, while the connection to uterine tissue pointed towards a myomatous etiology for the lower mass. The patient's dyspnoea was thought to be a consequence of significant abdominal mass effect, without findings consistent with pulmonary or cardiac compromise.

Treatment:

Given the progression of the patient's symptoms—specifically, abdominal pain related to the mass and dyspnoea from restrictive lung disease due to the mass size—as well as the need to secure a definitive diagnosis, prevent possible complications such as rupture, torsion, or infection, and in light of anticipated uterine enlargement with advancing gestation, a laparotomy with a longitudinal incision from xiphoid to pubic region was performed at 21 weeks and 6 days gestational age following informed consent.

Initially, two samples of free peritoneal fluid were obtained for both microbiological and cytological analysis. Subsequent inspection of the abdominal cavity confirmed ongoing fetal cardiac activity.

Unexpectedly, the superior mass revealed itself to be an enormous, pedunculated, atypical cystic leiomyoma exhibiting a tense, elastic consistency; it was markedly vascular, initially twisted, and exhibiting leakage, which may have contributed to the patient’s fever during hospitalization (see figure 2). The surgical team proceeded to excise the mass delicately from its uterine attachment using a double clamp (see figure 2). Following transection of the stalk, the base was meticulously closed with a single absorbable suture. The inferior mass, which arose from the uterine torus, was retained in order to avoid increasing obstetric risk. This mass appeared fixed, possessed a smooth surface, and was soft in consistency, consistent with a sizeable cystic intramural-subserosal leiomyoma.

The procedure was completed in 2 hours and 25 minutes. Progesterone prophylaxis, 100 mg administered intramuscularly, was initiated during the operation and subsequently continued daily at the same dosage until the patient’s discharge. Afterwards, the patient was given intramuscular hydroxyprogesterone caproate (341 mg, 1 fl, intramuscularly every three days per the package insert) until the scheduled delivery. Intraoperative prophylaxis with intravenous cefazolin (2 g) was followed by postoperative antibiotic therapy consisting of cefazolin 1 g intravenously three times daily and clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily throughout the hospital stay, according to urine culture antibiogram findings, which demonstrated Klebsiella pneumoniae highly susceptible to cephalosporins and clarithromycin.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following the operation, the patient’s anemia worsened (Hb 7.5 g/L, HCT 24.3%) and iron deficiency persisted, prompting administration of intravenous ferric carboxymaltose (1 g) as well as two blood transfusions, which improved her condition (Hb 10.0 g/L, HCT 30.4%). She was discharged after 10 days with no complications and enrolled in a close obstetrical follow-up every 3 weeks. Surveillance demonstrated appropriate fetal growth (86th percentile per the WHO fetal growth chart calculator, https://srhr.org/fetalgrowthcalculator/#/), and monitored amniotic fluid index.

Cultures and cytological analyses of the peritoneal fluid were negative. Histopathological evaluation identified benign uterine leiomyomas and regions of ischemic necrosis adjacent to the twisted pedicle.

The location of the caudal myoma would likely have significantly impeded fetal head descent during the expulsion stage of labor. Consequently, a planned cesarean section was carried out at the original surgical site when the fetus reached 37 weeks gestation, reducing the potential for uterine scar rupture and resulting in an optimal neonatal outcome.

At present, the patient does not experience symptoms attributable to myoma, although the residual pelvic myoma has decreased slightly in size to 7.5 cm. She is successfully breastfeeding, but has chosen to delay any further surgical intervention, given the partial postpartum regression of the remaining leiomyomas, lack of symptoms, and the achievement of motherhood. The infant is developing well.

Discussion:

Myomas can often be misdiagnosed as adnexal masses: the incidence during pregnancy is 0.3–10.1% and 1–6%, respectively .13

In this case, initial assessment identified large abdominal masses resembling two sizable decidualised endometriomas. However, the lower solid portion of the pelvic mass presented as if it were a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine fibroid exhibiting cystic degeneration, measuring 58×71 mm on both ultrasound and MRI studies. Given the lack of established protocols for treating pelvic masses in pregnancy, a multidisciplinary discussion concluded that scheduled surgical intervention might lower the likelihood of preterm birth.

Normally during gestation, serum CA-125 tends to demonstrate two elevations: one during the first trimester (median 23 U/mL, p<0.00001), and another in the third trimester (median 21 U/mL, p<0.001), when compared to the second trimester (median 14.0 U/mL). In contrast, in this patient, the CA-125 measured 41.8 U/mL, which surpasses the median value expected during the second trimester.

CA-125 is primarily utilized as a tumor marker for ovarian carcinoma (common threshold ≥35 U/mL), with particular relevance in women above the age of 50, in whom diagnostic performance is improved. Nevertheless, elevated CA-125 may be found in a variety of benign disorders including endometriosis and adenomyosis (>19 U/mL), pelvic inflammatory disease, menstruation, early and late pregnancy (median values 23 and 21 U/mL, respectively), increased miscarriage risk, and in cases of myoma (cut-off <19 U/mL).57

The principal factors guiding the clinical management included the existence of large, highly vascularized masses exhibiting both solid and cystic components, which posed a significant risk for complications such as rupture, torsion, or infection. Additional considerations were the patient’s worsening pain and increasing dyspnoea, the potential for mass enlargement with an associated increased risk of hypoxia, necrosis, and degeneration, mildly elevated tumor marker levels (CA-125=41.8 U/mL), and restricted intra-abdominal space that might impair further physiological uterine growth.

Given the potential neoplastic nature and indeterminate origin of the masses, the patient underwent a contrast-free MRI following the ultrasound assessment to improve diagnostic specificity. Contemporary guidelines advise against gadolinium-enhanced MRI in pregnant patients unless absolutely indicated for certain conditions such as rheumatological, inflammatory, or infiltrative dermatological diseases, or in the presence of risks for stillbirth or neonatal death. Consequently, the multidisciplinary team elected to perform a non-contrast MRI, ensuring the magnetic field strength did not exceed 1.5-T, which is regarded as safe beyond the first trimester.

In this case, the multidisciplinary team, consisting of surgeons, gynecologists, and obstetricians, decided to undertake a longitudinal laparotomic surgical approach after acquiring explicit written informed consent from the patient. Intraoperative evaluation revealed that the superior lesion possessed a pedicle, which posed a considerable threat for an acute, characteristic complication. Consequently, only an upper myomectomy was performed. Throughout the procedure, fetal viability was monitored using repeated and periodic transvaginal ultrasounds, supplemented by the preventive intraoperative use of progesterone.

As highlighted in the multicenter study by Zhao et al, documenting and monitoring the fetal heart rate before, during, and after surgical intervention is obligatory.

In general, surgical intervention is postponed until postpartum, with medical and expectant management typically preferred. Nevertheless, Sakinci et al’s recent study at a tertiary facility demonstrated no statistically significant differences in preoperative and postoperative hemoglobin levels or rates of blood transfusion (p >0.05) between women undergoing caesarean section (CS) combined with myomectomy and those without myoma undergoing only CS. However, patients in the CS myomectomy group experienced significantly longer hospitalizations and surgical times (p =0.001 and p =0.001, respectively). The average myoma diameter was 8.3 ± 4.1 cm, and there was a statistically significant positive association between myoma size and length of hospital stay (p =0.01).

The same study demonstrated a statistically significant inverse relationship between the size of myomas and the gestational week at delivery (p = 0.035).

Uterine leiomyomas exhibit a non-linear pattern in size alterations throughout pregnancy and the postpartum period. Specifically, leiomyoma size increases at a weekly rate of 6.90%; the increase is 6.12% (p<0.001) from the first to the second trimester, and 3.10%±0.39% from the second to the third trimester (p<0.05). This indicates that uterine leiomyomas grow more rapidly during the first trimester compared to subsequent stages of pregnancy. Conversely, in the third trimester and up to 6 weeks following delivery, myoma size decreases significantly, with a mean reduction of 44.25%±36.05% (p<0.05).

The referenced studies underscore the necessity for precise ultrasound evaluation of the size, location, quantity, and proximity of uterine myomas to the placenta prior to surgical intervention.

When uterine myomas are identified during pregnancy, conservative management is generally favored over surgery due to the associated risks of substantial hemorrhage, miscarriage, preterm labor, and uterine rupture. Nevertheless, myomectomy should be considered for complicated cases, such as torsion of a pedunculated myoma or when persistent symptoms fail to improve with conservative therapy. The surgical procedure should be as brief as possible to safeguard the pregnancy and may be conducted laparoscopically, ideally using the open Hasson technique during early gestation (first and early second trimester), with smaller masses, and for myomas not located intramurally. This method results in better maternal morbidity outcomes and greater likelihood of vaginal delivery. The case presented herein illustrates the common tendency toward selecting laparotomy instead of laparoscopy for larger, multiple, intramural, or critically positioned myomas, which is often associated with increased rates of cesarean section, particularly when a large myoma is adherent to the posterior isthmic region of the uterus and acts as a barrier to vaginal delivery.
