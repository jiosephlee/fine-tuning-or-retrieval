Title: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

Rosalba De Nola, Emma Bassi, Cataldo Paletta, Mario Vicino

Abstract:

A woman in her 40s, who had not previously given birth, presented during her second trimester with abdominal pain and shortness of breath. Ultrasonography identified two sizable masses within the abdomen: the superior mass occupied the entire left subphrenic region and extended toward the uterine fundus, while the inferior mass was located in the pouch of Douglas. The patient underwent laparotomy, during which the lower mass was retained in the pelvic cavity. Following surgery, she received standard antenatal care and underwent an elective caesarean section, leading to a favorable outcome for both mother and infant. For pregnant patients with large, symptomatic masses, a thorough assessment utilizing bimanual examination and safe imaging techniques—such as ultrasonography and non-contrast MRI—is critical. Surgical intervention should only be pursued in cases of suspected malignancy or clinical complications, with priority given to maintaining the pregnancy and avoiding unnecessary procedures. The choice of delivery method should be guided by the number, size, and anatomical positioning of the uterine masses.

Background:

Identifying and treating atypical or symptomatic abdominal masses in pregnant patients pose considerable clinical difficulties. Available diagnostic modalities are restricted to physical examination, ultrasound (US) with color Doppler imaging, and non-contrast magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Preoperative US is especially effective in visualizing symptomatic uterine leiomyomas situated on the anterior surface of the pregnant uterus, including both submucosal and pedunculated types. In contrast, MRI provides enhanced diagnostic precision in cases involving multiple, large, intramural, or posterior leiomyomas by accurately assessing the spatial relationship between the leiomyoma and the uterine cavity.

The utility of serum tumor markers for diagnosis during pregnancy is still controversial. For example, CA-125 concentrations naturally increase during the initial trimester and later return to baseline levels. Consequently, single measurements lack diagnostic value, while repeated assessments may offer informative trends for distinguishing diagnoses and ongoing monitoring. Effective management necessitates thorough, multidisciplinary evaluation of risks to appropriately weigh obstetric concerns against the infrequent and complex nature of these presentations.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 40s, obese with a body mass index of 33, gravida 1 para 0 abortus 1 (G1P0A1), presented in her second trimester of pregnancy at 21 weeks gestation, experiencing severe, acute abdominal pain localized to the upper left quadrant and pelvis (each site scored 5 on the Visual Analogue Scale), along with mild dyspnoea. Her routine first and second trimester screenings indicated a low risk for aneuploidy, as confirmed by non-invasive prenatal testing, and revealed normal fetal anatomy.

She was initially evaluated in the emergency department with a comprehensive multidisciplinary approach. Oxygen saturation was found to be normal (median 97%), and thoracic examination noted regular heart rate (median 74 bpm) and blood pressure (median 100/60 mm Hg). ECG results were unremarkable. Subsequently, she was admitted to the Obstetrical and Gynaecological Division. On examination, her abdomen appeared more distended than expected for 21 weeks gestation, presenting with a tender, taut-elastic mass measuring 18 cm occupying the entire upper left quadrant, without evidence of ascites.

Bimanual vaginal examination detected a fixed, rounded, firm mass about 9 cm in the lesser pelvis, protruding partially at the posterior fornix.

She received intravenous paracetamol at 1000 mg twice daily, together with semi-orthostatic positioning aimed at potentially mitigating diaphragmatic pressure from both the gravid uterus and sizable abdominal masses. This management led to mild symptomatic relief, lowering her pain from 5 to 1 on the Visual Analogue Scale, without the need for supplemental oxygen or ventilatory support.

During hospitalization, the patient developed a low-grade fever. Testing with a nasopharyngeal swab PCR for severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 was negative; however, urine analysis confirmed a Klebsiella pneumoniae infection. Laboratory investigations revealed iron deficiency anaemia with haemoglobin (Hb) at 9.9 g/L (reference range 12.0–16.6 g/L) and serum ferritin at 25 mcg/L (reference range 15–300 mcg/L). There was a mild elevation in C-reactive protein at 1.39 mg/dL (reference range 0–0.3 mg/dL), and an increased neutrophil count was observed (white blood cells 11.29×10^3/uL [normal: 4–10×10^3/uL], neutrophils 9.27×10^3/uL [normal: 2.8–5.5×10^3/uL]), consistent with changes seen in pregnancy.

Investigations:

Ultrasonography revealed two sizable, unilocular cystic lesions characterized by smooth borders and peripheral blood flow. The superior lesion measured 18×12×7 cm and extended from the left subphrenic area down to the uterine fundus. The inferior lesion had dimensions of 9.5×8×8 cm and appeared to be attached to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration (58×71 mm).

Assessment of tumour markers (CA 19–9, CA 15–3, CEA, β2-microglobulin, and LDH) showed values within the reference range, but the level of CA-125 was raised (41.8 U/mL; reference <35 U/mL), with a progressive increase observed from 36 U/mL two weeks prior. Thus, a mild upward trend in this marker was present. An MRI without contrast was subsequently performed, revealing thickened walls (1.4 cm), inhomogeneous fluid content on T1 and T2 fat-sat weighted sequences, and regions of hyperintense T1-T2 weighted solid tissue (refer to figure 1; only T2 fat-sat images are shown). The demarcation of the caudal lesion was indistinct, appearing contiguous with the previously identified posterior leiomyoma on ultrasonography. No lymphadenopathy was detected by MRI.

Differential diagnosis:

During the ultrasound examination, the cystic lesions appeared similar to two large decidualised endometriomas; additionally, the lower cyst was observed to be adherent to an intramural-subserosal leiomyoma located on the posterior wall of the uterus, measuring 58×71 mm.

Magnetic resonance imaging revealed two well-circumscribed, unilocular cysts within the adnexa, each showing enlarged vessels and heterogeneous fluid content (refer to figure 1). The lower lesion appeared attached to a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma that exhibited cystic degeneration, measuring 58×71 mm.

The substantial size of the gravid uterus and the considerable bulk of the masses impeded identification of a connecting stalk or any vascular supply to the upper mass, thereby shifting the diagnostic focus toward ovarian masses, particularly endometriomas, given the mildly elevated CA-125 detected in the second trimester of pregnancy (see discussion).

Evaluation of sonographic and MRI findings prompted consideration of decidualised endometriomas and cystic degeneration of leiomyomas as differential diagnoses. The mild increase in CA-125 supported the likelihood of endometrioma for the upper cyst, whereas the anatomical continuity with uterine tissue indicated a myomatous origin for the caudal lesion. The patient's dyspnoea was thought to result from the physical compression caused by the large abdominal masses, as there was no evidence pointing to pulmonary or cardiac compromise.

Treatment:

Given the deterioration of the patient’s clinical status—including mass-related abdominal pain and dyspnoea secondary to restrictive lung disease caused by the enlarging abdominal masses—a definitive diagnosis, potential complications such as rupture, torsion, and infection, and the likelihood of uterine growth paralleling fetal development, she underwent a laparotomy with a longitudinal xiphoid-pubic incision at 21 weeks and 6 days following informed consent.

Initially, two samples of free peritoneal fluid were obtained for both microbiological and cytological analyses. Upon further inspection of the abdominal cavity, fetal cardiac activity was confirmed.

Unexpectedly, the cranial mass proved to be a large, pedunculated, atypical cystic, and taut-elastic uterine leiomyoma, demonstrating significant vascularity; it was partially twisted and leaking, likely explaining the patient’s fever during admission (see figure 2). Subsequently, the surgical team opted to excise the mass from its uterine base using a double clamp technique (see figure 2). After division of the stalk, the exposed base was closed with a single absorbable suture. The caudal mass, which originated from the uterine torus, was left in place to avoid obstetric complications; this lesion had a smooth exterior and soft consistency, resembling a large cystic intramural-subserosal leiomyoma.

The operative time was 2 hours and 25 minutes. The patient received prophylactic progesterone (100 mg intramuscularly), begun intraoperatively and continued daily at 100 mg IM until discharge. Post-discharge, she was prescribed intramuscular hydroxyprogesterone caproate (341 mg per ampoule IM every 3 days per package instructions) until planned delivery. Cefazolin 2 g IV was administered intraoperatively for antibiotic prophylaxis, with subsequent postoperative regimen of cefazolin 1 g IV three times daily plus clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily, tailored to the urine culture antibiogram findings of Klebsiella pneumoniae (high sensitivity to cephalosporins and clarithromycin), for the duration of her hospitalisation.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following the operation, the patient's worsening anemia (Hb 7.5 g/L, HCT 24.3%), along with ongoing iron deficiency, necessitated intravenous iron therapy (ferric carboxymaltose 1 g) and the administration of two blood transfusions, which successfully raised hemoglobin to 10.0 g/L and hematocrit to 30.4%. The patient was released from the hospital without any complications 10 days post-surgery and began rigorous obstetric surveillance every 3 weeks. This monitoring confirmed fetal growth (86th percentile per the WHO fetal growth chart calculator available at https://srhr.org/fetalgrowthcalculator/#/) as well as the amniotic fluid index.

Examination of the peritoneal fluid revealed no abnormalities in culture or cytology. Histopathologic analysis identified benign uterine leiomyomas, with ischemic necrosis present near the twisted pedicle.

The lower (caudal) myoma was anticipated to significantly impede descent of the fetal head during the second stage of labor; therefore, a planned cesarean section was performed using the existing surgical incision once the fetus reached 37 weeks’ gestation. This approach minimized the possibility of uterine scar rupture and resulted in an optimal neonatal outcome.

Currently, the patient reports no symptoms related to the myoma, although the remaining pelvic myoma has decreased in size to 7.5 cm. She is breastfeeding effectively and chooses not to schedule further surgical intervention at this time, given the partial postpartum shrinkage of the residual leiomyomas, absence of related symptoms, and achievement of motherhood goals. The infant continues to thrive.

Discussion:

Myomas can often be misdiagnosed as adnexal masses: the incidence during pregnancy is 0.3–10.1% and 1–6%, respectively .13

In this case, the initial assessment identified abdominal masses that resembled two large decidualised endometriomas, except for the lower solid portion of the pelvic mass, which appeared consistent with a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration, measuring 58×71 mm on both ultrasound and MRI. Given the lack of clear evidence-based protocols for managing pelvic masses during pregnancy, the multidisciplinary team determined that scheduled surgery might reduce the likelihood of preterm birth.

Typically, during pregnancy, serum CA-125 levels show two peaks: one during the first trimester (median 23 U/mL, p<0.00001) and another in the third trimester (median 21 U/mL, p<0.001), compared to lower values in the second trimester (median 14.0 U/mL). In this patient, however, the CA-125 level was 41.8 U/mL, exceeding the median value expected in the second trimester.

CA-125 serves primarily as a tumor marker for ovarian malignancy, with the established cutoff being ≥35 U/mL, particularly in women over the age of 50, where it demonstrates enhanced area under the curve, positive predictive value, sensitivity, and specificity. Nonetheless, elevated CA-125 can also be seen in a range of benign conditions, including endometriosis and adenomyosis (>19 U/mL), pelvic inflammatory disease, menstruation, as well as early and late pregnancy (median values of 23 and 21 U/mL, respectively), threatened miscarriage, and in cases of myoma (cutoff <19 U/mL).57

The principal factors guiding the clinical approach included the existence of large, highly vascularized masses displaying mixed solid and cystic morphology, presenting a genuine risk for complications such as rupture, torsion, or infection; the patient’s worsening symptoms, namely increased pain and shortness of breath; potential for the masses to enlarge, heightening risks for hypoxia, necrosis, and degeneration; mildly elevated tumor marker levels (CA-125 at 41.8 U/mL); and limited space potentially restricting normal uterine expansion.

Owing to the suspected neoplastic nature and uncertain etiology of the masses, the patient underwent a non-contrast MRI following the ultrasound examination to enhance diagnostic precision. In accordance with current guidelines, gadolinium-enhanced MRI is generally avoided during pregnancy unless strictly warranted for specific rheumatologic, inflammatory, or infiltrative dermatologic conditions, or due to risk of stillbirth or neonatal demise. As such, the multidisciplinary team elected to proceed with a non-enhanced MRI, keeping magnetic field strength below 1.5 T, which is considered acceptably safe after the first trimester.

In this case, the multidisciplinary team—which included surgeons, gynecologists, and obstetricians—chose to perform longitudinal laparotomic surgery after securing specific written informed consent from the patient. During the operation, the upper lesion was found to have a pedicle, creating a considerable risk for developing a classic acute complication. As a result, the team elected to limit the intervention to removal of the superior myoma. Fetal viability was closely tracked through serial and intermittent transvaginal ultrasonography throughout the procedure, with intraoperative prophylactic progesterone also administered for support.

A large multicenter trial by Zhao et al concluded that it is essential to assess and record the fetal heart rate prior to, during, and after the surgical intervention.

Generally, surgical intervention is postponed until after delivery in favor of medical or expectant management. Nonetheless, Sakinci et al recently conducted a study in a tertiary care facility which demonstrated no statistically significant differences in preoperative or postoperative hemoglobin levels or the need for blood transfusion (p >0.05) when comparing women who underwent caesarean section (CS) with simultaneous myomectomy and those who had only CS without myoma. However, the CS with myomectomy group showed a significantly longer hospitalization and operative time (p = 0.001 for both). The average myoma diameter measured 8.3 ± 4.1 cm, and there was a significant positive association between myoma size and duration of hospital stay (p = 0.01).

The same study demonstrated a statistically significant inverse relationship between the size of the myoma and the gestational week at delivery (p = 0.035).

Uterine leiomyomas exhibit a non-linear pattern of size variation throughout pregnancy and the postpartum period. Specifically, myomas enlarge by 6.90% per week overall, with an increase of 6.12% (p<0.001) between the first and second trimesters, and a further growth of 3.10%±0.39% between the second and third trimesters (p<0.05). Consequently, the expansion rate of uterine leiomyomas is markedly greater in the first trimester compared to later stages of gestation. Conversely, during the third trimester and up to 6 weeks postpartum, myoma size decreases significantly, with an average reduction of 44.25%±36.05% (p<0.05).

These reports underscore the necessity of a thorough ultrasound assessment evaluating the dimensions, position, quantity, and proximity of uterine myomas relative to the placenta before any surgical intervention.

For uterine myomas identified during pregnancy, conservative management is generally favored over surgery, given the risks of significant hemorrhage, miscarriage, preterm delivery, and uterine rupture. Nonetheless, myomectomy should be contemplated when the lesion is complicated by pedunculated myoma torsion or when persistent symptoms fail to respond to conservative measures. The operative procedure should be kept brief to safeguard the ongoing pregnancy and may be carried out laparoscopically, preferably using the open Hasson method in instances of lower gestational age (first and early second trimester), smaller lesion volume, and when the myoma is located outside the uterine wall. This technique is associated with improved maternal morbidity and the likelihood of vaginal delivery. Still, the current case reflects a frequent bias where laparotomy is chosen over laparoscopy for larger, multiple, intramural, or otherwise critical myomas, which may be linked to higher cesarean section rates. This is particularly pertinent when a substantial myoma is attached to the posterior wall of the uterine isthmus, potentially acting as a barrier to vaginal delivery.
