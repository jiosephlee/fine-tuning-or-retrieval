Title: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

Rosalba De Nola, Emma Bassi, Cataldo Paletta, Mario Vicino

Abstract:

A woman in her 40s, who had not previously given birth, presented in her second trimester with abdominal pain and shortness of breath. Ultrasound imaging identified two sizable masses in her abdomen: the upper mass filled the entire left subphrenic region and extended to the uterine fundus, while the lower mass occupied the pouch of Douglas. The patient underwent a laparotomy, with the caudal mass remaining undisturbed in the pelvic cavity. She continued routine obstetric surveillance and ultimately had an elective caesarean section, which resulted in favorable outcomes for both mother and infant. When encountering large symptomatic masses during pregnancy, thorough assessment with bimanual examination and safe imaging modalities such as ultrasonography or non-contrast MRI is critical. Surgical intervention should be reserved for cases with concern for malignancy or clinical complications, with the priority of preserving pregnancy and avoiding unnecessary procedures. The approach to delivery should be guided by the mass size, quantity, and anatomical location.

Background:

Evaluating and treating atypical or symptomatic abdominal masses in pregnancy is a complex clinical task. Diagnostic modalities are restricted to physical examination, ultrasound (US) with color Doppler, and non-contrast MRI. Preoperative US is particularly helpful for detecting symptomatic uterine leiomyomas on the anterior aspect of the pregnant uterus, whether submucosal or pedunculated in type. MRI delivers enhanced diagnostic precision when assessing numerous, sizable, intramural, or posterior leiomyomas, as it can accurately measure the separation between the fibroid and the uterine cavity.

The use of serum tumor markers for diagnosis during pregnancy remains controversial. Specifically, CA-125 typically increases in the first trimester and returns to baseline levels thereafter. Thus, single CA-125 values are not diagnostic; however, repeated measurements over time may yield meaningful patterns useful for differential diagnosis and ongoing monitoring. Effective management necessitates thorough multidisciplinary risk evaluation, aiming to weigh obstetric risk against the uncommon and intricate nature of such cases.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 40s, classified as obese with a body mass index of 33, G1P0A1, presented in the second trimester of pregnancy (at 21 weeks gestation) due to sudden, intense abdominal pain affecting the upper left quadrant and pelvic region (both sites scoring 5 on the Visual Analogue Scale), along with mild shortness of breath. Routine screening for aneuploidies in both the first and second trimesters indicated low risk, as verified by non-invasive prenatal testing, and fetal anatomical development was normal.

She initially sought care in the emergency department, where a multidisciplinary team carried out a comprehensive assessment. Her oxygen saturation remained within the normal range (median 97%). Physical examination revealed that her heart rate was 74 bpm and blood pressure measured at 100/60 mm Hg. ECG results did not show any abnormalities. Subsequently, she was moved to the Obstetrics and Gynecology unit. Abdominal examination noted an enlargement exceeding what would be anticipated for her gestational age, with a palpable, tender, and taut-elastic mass occupying the entire upper left quadrant, approximately 18 cm in size, without evidence of ascites on clinical evaluation.

During bimanual vaginal assessment, a non-mobile mass of roughly 9 cm, firm and rounded, was felt in the lesser pelvis and partially protruded at the posterior fornix.

The patient received analgesia with intravenous paracetamol 1000 mg twice daily and was placed in a semi-upright position, presumed to decrease diaphragmatic compression caused by the gravid uterus and large abdominal masses. This approach led to a modest improvement in her symptoms, decreasing pain from 5 to 1 on the Visual Analogue Scale, without the need for additional oxygen or ventilation.

During hospitalization, she developed a mild fever. Severe acute respiratory syndrome Coronavirus 2 was ruled out with a negative PCR from a nasopharyngeal swab; conversely, a urinary tract infection with Klebsiella pneumoniae was confirmed. Blood tests revealed iron deficiency anaemia (haemoglobin 9.9 g/L; reference range 12.0–16.6 g/L; serum ferritin 25 mcg/L; reference range 15–300 mcg/L), a mild elevation in C-reactive protein (1.39 mg/dL; reference range 0–0.3 mg/dL), and an increased neutrophil count (white blood cells 11.29×10^3/uL; reference range 4–10×10^3/uL; neutrophils 9.27×10^3/uL; reference range 2.8–5.5×10^3/uL), consistent with changes commonly seen in pregnancy.

Investigations:

Ultrasonography revealed two substantial, unilocular cystic lesions characterized by smooth borders and vascularity along their periphery. The superior lesion measured 18×12×7 cm, reaching from the left subphrenic area down to the fundus of the uterus. The inferior lesion measured 9.5×8×8 cm and appeared attached to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma displaying cystic degeneration (58×71 mm).

Assessment of tumour markers, including CA 19–9, CA 15–3, CEA, β2-microglobulin, and LDH, all returned values within the normal range. In contrast, CA-125 was elevated at 41.8 U/mL (reference <35 U/mL), demonstrating a gradual increase from a level of 36 U/mL recorded two weeks prior. Thus, a mildly rising trend was observed in serum CA-125 levels. Consequently, the patient underwent an MRI scan without administration of contrast. This revealed the presence of thick cyst walls (1.4 cm), inhomogeneous fluid on T1 and T2 fat-sat weighted images, and regions of hyperintense solid tissue on both T1 and T2 weighted sequences (refer to figure 1; only T2 fat-sat images provided). The delineation between the caudal mass and adjacent tissues was indeterminate, and it appeared contiguous with the previously documented posterior leiomyoma from the ultrasonographic assessment. No lymphadenopathy was detected on MRI.

Differential diagnosis:

During the ultrasound assessment, the cystic lesions resembled two large, decidualised endometriomas; the more inferior cyst appeared to be adherent to an intramural-subserosal leiomyoma located on the posterior uterine wall, measuring 58×71 mm.

Magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated two round, unilocular adnexal cysts containing inhomogeneous fluid and surrounded by enlarged vessels (refer to figure 1). The lower lesion seemed attached to a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration, measuring 58×71 mm.

Because the physical size of the gravid uterus and the extensive masses obscured visualization, neither a connecting stalk nor a vessel supplying the lesion was seen in the upper mass, steering the diagnostic impression towards ovarian lesions—particularly endometriomas, considering a mild increase in CA-125 during the second trimester (see discussion).

Sonographic and MRI findings suggested a differential diagnosis between decidualised endometriomas and cystic degenerating leiomyomas; the slight CA-125 elevation supported endometrioma for the superior cyst, whereas the direct relationship with uterine tissue indicated a myomatous origin for the inferior lesion. The patient's dyspnoea was believed to result from compression caused by the voluminous abdominal masses, as there were no indications of cardiac or pulmonary pathology.

Treatment:

Given the progression of clinical symptoms—including pain associated with abdominal masses and dyspnea secondary to restrictive lung disease from the masses' size—as well as the need for definitive diagnosis, the possibility of complications such as rupture, torsion, or infection, and the likelihood of uterine expansion with ongoing fetal growth, a laparotomy with a midline xiphoid-to-pubic incision was performed at 21 weeks and 6 days following informed consent.

Initially, two samples of free peritoneal fluid were obtained for both culture and cytology. The abdomen was then explored, and fetal cardiac activity was confirmed.

Unexpectedly, the cranial lesion was identified as a large, pedunculated, atypically cystic, and tense-elastic uterine leiomyoma exhibiting significant vascularity, initial torsion, and leakage—features likely explaining the patient’s fever during hospitalization (see figure 2). Accordingly, the surgical team opted to excise this mass from its uterine attachment with cautious double clamping (see figure 2). After severing the stalk, the exposed base was meticulously closed using a single absorbable suture. The caudal mass, emanating from the uterine torus, was preserved in situ to minimize risks to the pregnancy. This mass was immobile, with a smooth outer surface and a soft consistency, suggestive of a large cystic intramural-subserosal leiomyoma.

The operative time totaled 2 hours and 25 minutes. Importantly, progesterone prophylaxis was initiated intraoperatively with 100 mg intramuscularly and subsequently continued at the same dose daily until discharge. Thereafter, the patient received hydroxyprogesterone caproate intramuscularly (341 mg, one ampoule, every 3 days, as per the package instructions) until planned delivery. Cefazolin 2 g intravenously was administered as intraoperative antibiotic prophylaxis, followed by postoperative antibiotics consisting of cefazolin 1 g intravenously three times daily and clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily, tailored to urine culture results demonstrating Klebsiella pneumoniae with high sensitivity to cephalosporins and clarithromycin.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following surgery, the patient’s anaemia worsened (Hb 7.5 g/L, HCT 24.3%) with ongoing iron deficiency, prompting the administration of intravenous ferric carboxymaltose (1 g) and two blood transfusions, which resulted in improvement (Hb 10.0 g/L, HCT 30.4%). She was discharged without incident 10 days postoperatively and began close obstetric monitoring at 3-week intervals, during which fetal growth was documented (86th percentile according to the WHO fetal growth chart calculator, https://srhr.org/fetalgrowthcalculator/#/), along with amniotic fluid index measurements.

Analysis of peritoneal fluid revealed negative results for both culture and cytology. Histopathological evaluation confirmed benign uterine leiomyomas with regions of ischaemic necrosis adjacent to the twisted pedicle.

The caudally located myoma posed a significant risk to fetal head descent during the second stage of labor. Therefore, an elective caesarean section was scheduled at the original surgical site when the fetus reached 37 weeks’ gestation, thereby reducing the possibility of uterine scar rupture and resulting in a favourable neonatal outcome.

Currently, the patient is asymptomatic in relation to her myoma, and imaging indicates that the remaining pelvic myoma has decreased to 7.5 cm in diameter. She is breastfeeding well and, due to partial postpartum regression of the leiomyomas, lack of symptoms, and having achieved her maternal goals, prefers to delay further surgical intervention. The infant continues to develop appropriately.

Discussion:

Myomas can often be misdiagnosed as adnexal masses: the incidence during pregnancy is 0.3–10.1% and 1–6%, respectively .13

In this case, initial assessment revealed abdominal masses resembling two large decidualised endometriomas, except for the lower solid portion of the pelvic mass, which was consistent with a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration measuring 58×71 mm on both ultrasound and MRI studies. Given the lack of established protocols for managing pelvic masses during pregnancy, the multidisciplinary team judged that elective surgical intervention might reduce the likelihood of preterm birth.

Typically, during gestation, serum CA-125 demonstrates two peaks: one in the first trimester (median 23 U/mL, p<0.00001) and another in the third trimester (median 21 U/mL, p<0.001) compared to lower concentrations in the second trimester (median 14.0 U/mL). Contrastingly, the present case exhibited a CA-125 level of 41.8 U/mL, which was higher than the second trimester median.

CA-125 is primarily employed as a tumor marker in ovarian carcinoma (standard threshold ≥35 U/mL), notably in women older than 50 years, where its area under the curve, positive predictive value, sensitivity, and specificity are enhanced. However, elevated CA-125 levels may also be observed in several benign conditions, including endometriosis and adenomyosis (>19 U/mL), pelvic inflammatory disease, menstruation, early and late pregnancy (median values of 23 and 21 U/mL, respectively), risk of miscarriage, as well as myomas (cut-off <19 U/mL).57

The primary factors guiding the clinical approach included the identification of large, vascular masses containing both solid and cystic elements, which posed immediate risks for complications such as rupture, torsion, or infection. Additionally, the patient's symptoms of escalating pain and shortness of breath, the potential for mass enlargement that could lead to increased likelihood of hypoxia, tissue necrosis, or degeneration, modest elevation of tumor marker levels (CA-125=41.8 U/mL), and limited intra-abdominal space threatening normal uterine expansion were all considered.

Given the suspicion of a neoplastic process and indeterminate nature of the masses, an MRI without contrast was performed following initial ultrasound evaluation to enhance diagnostic clarity. In keeping with current guidelines, gadolinium-based contrast MRI was avoided during pregnancy unless absolutely required for rheumatologic, inflammatory, or skin infiltrative disorders, or when there is a significant risk to the fetus. Consequently, the multidisciplinary team proceeded with a non-contrast MRI, ensuring that magnetic field strength did not exceed 1.5 T, which is deemed suitably safe after the first trimester of gestation.

In this case, the multidisciplinary team, which included surgeons, gynecologists, and obstetricians, elected to perform a longitudinal laparotomy after securing written informed consent from the patient. During the operation, the upper lesion was found to have a pedicle, posing an elevated risk for a classic, acute complication, which justified the decision to restrict the intervention to an upper myomectomy. Additionally, ongoing transvaginal ultrasound examinations were used at intervals throughout the procedure to assess fetal viability, and intraoperative progesterone was administered prophylactically.

As reported by Zhao et al in a large multicenter study, it is required to assess and record fetal heart rate prior to, during, and following the surgery.

Surgical intervention is commonly postponed until after delivery, with medical or expectant management preferred. Nonetheless, Sakinci et al recently reported findings from a tertiary center indicating no statistically significant differences in preoperative and postoperative hemoglobin levels or the need for blood transfusion (p >0.05) between women undergoing caesarean section (CS) plus myomectomy and those with only CS for pregnancies without myomas. Conversely, both hospitalization length and operative time were significantly greater in the CS myomectomy group (p = 0.001 for both comparisons). The average myoma measured 8.3 ± 4.1cm, and there was a significant positive relationship between myoma size and duration of hospital stay (p = 0.01).

The same study identified a statistically significant negative association between myoma size and the gestational age at delivery (p = 0.035).

Uterine leiomyomas exhibit a non-linear pattern of size variation throughout gestation and the postpartum period. Specifically, leiomyomas enlarge by approximately 6.90% each week, with an increase of 6.12% (p<0.001) observed between the first and second trimesters, and a 3.10%±0.39% increment from the second to the third trimester (p<0.05). Thus, the rate of myoma growth is markedly higher in the first trimester compared to later pregnancy stages. Conversely, a significant reduction in myoma size occurs during the third trimester and six weeks following delivery, with a mean decrease of 44.25%±36.05% (p<0.05).

These findings highlight the necessity for a meticulous ultrasonographic assessment of myoma size, location, number, and proximity to the placenta prior to deciding on a surgical intervention.

When uterine myomas are identified during pregnancy, conservative management is generally favored over surgery due to heightened risks of severe hemorrhage, pregnancy loss, preterm delivery, and uterine rupture. Nonetheless, myomectomy may be warranted in situations involving complicated tumors, such as torsion of a pedunculated myoma or ongoing symptoms unresponsive to conservative measures. If surgical intervention is required, the procedure should be minimized in duration to optimize pregnancy preservation; laparoscopy may be utilized, ideally employing the open Hasson technique in cases with earlier gestational age (first or early second trimester), smaller myoma size, or appropriate tumor location (non-intramural). This strategy has been associated with reduced maternal morbidity and better chances for vaginal delivery. However, the present case illustrates common selection bias, where laparotomy is chosen over laparoscopy for larger, multiple, intramural, or critically situated myomas—circumstances linked to higher cesarean section rates, as in this instance with a massive myoma adherent to the posterior isthmic uterine wall, which would present a mechanical barrier to vaginal delivery.
