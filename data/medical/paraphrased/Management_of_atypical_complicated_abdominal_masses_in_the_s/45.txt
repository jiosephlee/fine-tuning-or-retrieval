Title: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

Rosalba De Nola, Emma Bassi, Cataldo Paletta, Mario Vicino

Abstract:

Summary: A nulliparous woman in her forties presented during her second trimester with abdominal pain and shortness of breath. Ultrasound identified two sizable masses within the abdomen: the superior mass occupied the entire left subphrenic region extending toward the uterine fundus, while the inferior mass was situated within the pouch of Douglas. The patient underwent laparotomy, during which the pelvic mass was left untouched. She proceeded with standard obstetric surveillance and ultimately had an elective caesarean section, resulting in favourable outcomes for both mother and infant. When pregnant patients develop large symptomatic masses, thorough evaluation should include bimanual examination and the use of safe imaging techniques, such as ultrasonography and non-contrast MRI. Surgery should only be considered if there is suspicion of malignancy or clinical complications, with the primary goal of maintaining the pregnancy and preventing unnecessary intervention. The delivery approach should be tailored based on the size, quantity, and anatomical position of uterine masses.

Background:

Managing atypical or symptomatic abdominal masses in pregnant patients involves considerable clinical complexity. The available diagnostic modalities are restricted to physical examination, ultrasound (US) with color Doppler, and MRI without contrast. Preoperative US plays a key role in detecting symptomatic uterine leiomyomas situated on the anterior aspect of the pregnant uterus, including submucosal or pedunculated types. For cases involving multiple, large, intramural, or posterior leiomyomas, MRI provides greater diagnostic precision by accurately measuring the distance between the fibroid and the uterine cavity.

The application of serum tumor markers for diagnosis during pregnancy is still controversial. In particular, CA-125 levels increase physiologically during the initial trimester and then return to baseline. Thus, single measurements are not conclusive, whereas serial assessments may help monitor trends useful for differentiating diagnoses and tracking progress. Appropriate management hinges on thorough multidisciplinary evaluation of risks to adequately balance obstetric considerations with the uncommon and challenging nature of these presentations.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 40s, obese with a BMI of 33, G1P0A1, presented during the second trimester of pregnancy (21 weeks gestational age) due to sudden, severe abdominal pain localized to both the upper left quadrant and the pelvis (Visual Analogue Scale score 5 at each location), as well as mild shortness of breath. Her routine screenings in the first and second trimesters revealed a low risk for chromosomal abnormalities (validated by non-invasive prenatal testing) and normal fetal morphology.

Upon arrival, she was initially admitted to the emergency department for comprehensive evaluation by a multidisciplinary team. She exhibited normal oxygen saturation (median 97%), normal heart rate (median 74 bpm), and blood pressure (median 100/60 mm Hg) on thoracic examination. Electrocardiogram findings were unremarkable. She was subsequently transferred to the Obstetrical and Gynaecological Division. The abdomen appeared larger than expected for her gestational stage, with a tender swelling and a firm, elastic mass occupying the entire upper left quadrant, measuring 18 cm, but showing no signs of ascites.

On bimanual vaginal exam, a fixed, rounded, hard mass approximately 9 cm was noted in the lesser pelvis, partially protruding at the posterior fornix.

Intravenous analgesia (paracetamol 1000 mg twice daily) and semi-orthostatic positioning, thought to reduce diaphragmatic compression from the gravid uterus and the large abdominal mass, led to a slight improvement in symptoms, lowering pain from a score of 5 to 1 on the Visual Analogue Scale, without the need for supplemental oxygen or ventilation.

During hospitalization, the patient developed a mild fever. PCR testing for SARS-CoV-2 via nasopharyngeal swab returned negative; however, Klebsiella pneumoniae was identified in urine cultures. Laboratory assessment showed iron deficiency anemia with haemoglobin (Hb) at 9.9 g/L (normal range 12.0–16.6 g/L) and serum ferritin 25 mcg/L (normal range 15–300 mcg/L); C-reactive protein was slightly elevated at 1.39 mg/dL (normal range 0–0.3 mg/dL); leukocyte count was increased at 11.29×10^3/uL (normal range 4–10×10^3/uL), including neutrophils at 9.27×10^3/uL (normal range 2.8–5.5×10^3/uL), findings consistent with physiological changes in pregnancy.

Investigations:

Ultrasound revealed two sizable, single-chamber cystic lesions with smooth borders and vascularisation at the periphery. The superior lesion measured 18×12×7 cm and extended from the left subphrenic area to the uterine fundus. The inferior lesion measured 9.5×8×8 cm and appeared attached to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration (58×71 mm).

Tumor markers—including CA 19–9, CA 15–3, CEA, β2-microglobulin, and LDH—were found within normal ranges, but CA-125 was elevated at 41.8 U/mL (normal <35 U/mL) and had increased from 36 U/mL recorded two weeks prior. This indicated a mild upward trend in this marker's serum level. Consequently, the patient underwent MRI without administration of contrast. MRI demonstrated thick walls (1.4 cm), T1 and T2 fat-sat weighted heterogeneous fluid, and localized hyperintense solid areas on T1-T2 weighted sequences (refer to figure 1, where only T2 fat-sat images are provided). The separation between the caudal lesion and adjacent structures was unclear, appearing continuous with the posterior leiomyoma previously assessed during the ultrasound. No lymphadenopathy was noted on MRI.

Differential diagnosis:

On ultrasound examination, the cystic formations resembled two large decidualised endometriomas, with the lower cyst appearing to be attached to an intramural-subserosal leiomyoma located on the posterior uterine wall, which measured 58×71 mm.

Magnetic resonance imaging revealed two rounded, unilocular cysts in the adnexa with hypertrophic vessels and heterogeneous fluid within them (refer to figure 1); the inferior mass appeared adherent to a posterior uterine myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration measuring 58×71 mm.

The considerable size of the gravid uterus and these prominent masses prevented identification of a connecting stalk or nutrient vessel associated with the superior mass, leading clinicians to consider ovarian masses—particularly endometriomas—based on a mild rise in CA-125 noted during the second trimester (see discussion).

Considering the sonographic and MRI findings, the differential diagnosis comprised decidualised endometriomas and cystically degenerating leiomyomas. The slight increase in CA-125 lent support to the diagnosis of endometrioma in the upper cyst, whereas the caudal lesion’s continuity with uterine tissue indicated a possible myomatous origin. The patient’s dyspnoea was thought to result from the mechanical effect of the large abdominal masses, as there was no evidence pointing to underlying pulmonary or cardiac compromise.

Treatment:

Given the progression of the patient’s symptoms—including abdominal pain attributable to the masses and dyspnoea related to restrictive pulmonary mechanics due to their size—as well as the imperative to establish a definitive diagnosis, the risk of complications such as rupture, torsion, and infection, and anticipated uterine growth with advancing gestation, the decision was made to perform a laparotomy with a longitudinal xiphoid-to-pubis incision at 21 weeks and 6 days gestation, after securing informed consent.

Initially, two specimens of peritoneal fluid were obtained for both microbiological and cytological analysis. Subsequent intra-abdominal exploration confirmed ongoing fetal cardiac activity.

Unexpectedly, the superior mass was identified as a substantial pedunculated leiomyoma of the uterus, displaying atypical cystic and tense-elastic features, marked vascularity, initial torsion, and evidence of leakage—likely accounting for the fever observed during the patient's hospitalization (refer to figure 2). Consequently, the surgical team proceeded with careful removal of the mass at its uterine origin, employing a double clamp technique (see figure 2). Following transection of the stalk, the exposed base was meticulously sutured with a single absorbable stitch. The inferior mass, arising from the uterine torus, was left untouched to reduce obstetric risk; it presented as a fixed lesion with a smooth surface and soft consistency, reminiscent of a sizable cystic intramural-subserosal leiomyoma.

The operative duration was 2 hours and 25 minutes. Progesterone prophylaxis was administered, with 100 mg given intramuscularly during surgery and continued at the same dose daily until discharge. Thereafter, the patient received intramuscular hydroxyprogesterone caproate (341 mg, one ampoule every three days, per the manufacturer's instructions) until the scheduled delivery. Intraoperative antimicrobial prophylaxis consisted of 2 g cefazolin intravenously, followed postoperatively by cefazolin 1 g IV three times daily plus clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily, tailored to urinary culture sensitivity (Klebsiella pneumoniae highly susceptible to cephalosporins and clarithromycin).

Outcome and follow-up:

Following the surgical intervention, the patient's anaemia worsened (Hb 7.5 g/L, HCT 24.3%), with persistent iron deficiency necessitating intravenous administration of ferric carboxymaltose at a dose of 1 g and two units of blood transfusion. These measures were effective, resulting in improved haemoglobin (Hb 10.0 g/L) and haematocrit (HCT 30.4%) levels. The patient was discharged 10 days post-operatively without any complications and subsequently began intensive obstetric monitoring every three weeks. This follow-up documented fetal growth at the 86th percentile (based on WHO fetal growth chart calculator, available online at https://srhr.org/fetalgrowthcalculator/#/), as well as appropriate amniotic fluid index.

Peritoneal fluid analysis yielded negative results on both culture and cytology. Pathologic assessment identified benign uterine leiomyomas with zones of ischaemic necrosis adjacent to the twisted pedicle.

The inferiorly located myoma posed a substantial barrier to fetal head descent during the expulsive phase of labour. Therefore, an elective caesarean section was performed using the existing surgical incision once the fetus had reached 37 weeks' gestation, reducing the likelihood of uterine scar rupture and leading to a favourable neonatal outcome.

At present, the patient experiences no symptoms attributable to myoma, though the remaining pelvic myoma has decreased in size to 7.5 cm. Breastfeeding is proceeding well; however, the patient wishes to delay further surgical intervention due to partial postpartum regression of the residual leiomyomas, lack of symptoms, and fulfilment of motherhood goals. The infant continues to develop well.

Discussion:

Myomas can often be misdiagnosed as adnexal masses: the incidence during pregnancy is 0.3–10.1% and 1–6%, respectively .13

In this case, initial assessment identified abdominal masses that presented as two large decidualised endometriomas. However, the caudal solid portion of the pelvic mass resembled a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma with cystic degeneration, measuring 58×71 mm, as observed on both ultrasound and MRI scans. Since there are no established guidelines for the management of pelvic masses during pregnancy, the multidisciplinary team determined that elective surgery might lower the risk of preterm birth.

Serum CA-125 levels typically peak twice in pregnancy: once in the first trimester (median 23 U/mL, p<0.00001) and again in the third trimester (median 21 U/mL, p<0.001) when compared to second-trimester values (median 14.0 U/mL). In contrast, this patient had a CA-125 concentration of 41.8 U/mL, surpassing the median level typically observed during the second trimester.

CA-125 is predominantly utilized as a tumor marker for ovarian carcinoma, with a standard cutoff of ≥35 U/mL, and is most reliable in women over 50 due to increased area under the curve, positive predictive value, sensitivity, and specificity. Nonetheless, elevated CA-125 can also be associated with several benign conditions, including endometriosis and adenomyosis (>19 U/mL), pelvic inflammatory disease, menstruation, early and late pregnancy (median 23 U/mL and 21 U/mL, respectively), heightened risk of miscarriage, and also myomas (cut-off <19 U/mL).57

The primary factors guiding clinical management included the detection of large, highly vascularized masses composed of both solid and cystic areas, presenting a tangible risk for complications such as rupture, torsion, or infection; progressive symptoms (intensifying pain and shortness of breath); potential for further tumor growth increasing the likelihood of hypoxia, necrosis, and degenerative changes; mildly elevated tumor marker levels (CA-125 at 41.8 U/mL); and concerns regarding limited pelvic space that might restrict the normal expansion of the uterus.

Given the possible neoplastic nature and indeterminate origin of the lesions, an MRI without contrast was performed following ultrasound to enhance diagnostic precision. Based on current guidelines, gadolinium-enhanced MRI should generally be avoided in pregnancy unless clearly indicated for conditions such as severe rheumatologic, inflammatory, or infiltrative dermatological diseases, or in situations posing a risk of stillbirth or neonatal death. Consequently, the care team elected to proceed with a non-contrast MRI and ensured the MRI field strength did not exceed 1.5 Tesla, which is generally regarded as safe beyond the first trimester.

In this case, the multidisciplinary team, including surgeons, gynecologists, and obstetricians, chose to perform a longitudinal laparotomy after obtaining specific written informed consent from the patient. During surgical exploration, the superior lesion was found to have a pedicle, placing the patient at elevated risk for acute, characteristic complications. Consequently, the surgical intervention was restricted to upper myomectomy. Serial and intermittent transvaginal ultrasounds were utilized intraoperatively to evaluate fetal viability, complemented by the preventive use of progesterone during surgery.

A large multicenter investigation by Zhao et al emphasized that documenting the fetal heart rate before, during, and after the procedure is required.

Typically, surgery is postponed until postpartum, with a preference for medical or expectant management. Nonetheless, Sakinci et al recently performed a study in a tertiary care setting that demonstrated no statistically significant differences in preoperative or postoperative hemoglobin levels or rates of blood transfusion (p >0.05) between women who underwent cesarean section (CS) with myomectomy and those undergoing CS alone without myoma. However, the duration of both hospitalization and surgery was notably longer for the group undergoing CS myomectomy (p = 0.001 and p = 0.001, respectively). The average size of the myoma was 8.3 ± 4.1 cm, and a statistically significant positive association was observed between myoma size and length of hospital stay (p = 0.01).

The same study demonstrated a statistically significant inverse relationship between myoma size and gestational age at delivery (p = 0.035).

Uterine leiomyomas exhibit a non-linear pattern of size variation throughout pregnancy and the postpartum period. Specifically, myomas enlarge by 6.90% per week, with an increase of 6.12% (p < 0.001) observed between the first and second trimesters, and 3.10%±0.39% between the second and third trimesters (p < 0.05). Thus, the rate of growth of uterine leiomyomas is substantially greater during the first trimester compared to subsequent stages of pregnancy. Conversely, in the third trimester and at 6 weeks postpartum, myoma size decreased significantly, with an average reduction of 44.25%±36.05% (p < 0.05).

These publications highlight the necessity for comprehensive ultrasound assessment of myoma size, site, quantity, and proximity to the placenta prior to considering surgical intervention.

For uterine myomas identified during pregnancy, conservative management is typically favored over surgery, owing to risks such as substantial hemorrhage, pregnancy loss, preterm delivery, and uterine rupture. Nonetheless, myomectomy may be warranted for complicated cases, such as torsion of pedunculated myomas or ongoing symptoms unresponsive to conservative measures. The surgical procedure should be kept as brief as possible to safeguard the pregnancy and may be accomplished via laparoscopic techniques, ideally using the open Hasson method, especially in pregnancies of lower gestational age (first and early second trimester), smaller masses, and particular locations (excluding intramural myomas). This strategy is associated with improved outcomes regarding maternal morbidity and the likelihood of vaginal delivery. However, this case illustrates a frequent selection bias favoring laparotomy rather than laparoscopy in situations involving larger, multiple, intramural, or critical site myomas, which may increase the incidence of cesarean section, as seen when a sizeable myoma is adherent to the posterior isthmic area of the uterus and potentially obstructs vaginal birth.
