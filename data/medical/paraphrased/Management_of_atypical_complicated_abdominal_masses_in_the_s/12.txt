Title: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

Rosalba De Nola, Emma Bassi, Cataldo Paletta, Mario Vicino

Abstract:

A woman in her 40s, who had not previously given birth, presented during her second trimester with abdominal discomfort and shortness of breath. Ultrasonography demonstrated two substantial abdominal masses: the superior lesion occupied the entire left subphrenic region and extended to the uterine fundus, while the inferior lesion was located within the pouch of Douglas. The patient underwent a laparotomy, during which the lower pelvic mass was left undisturbed. Following surgery, she continued routine obstetric surveillance and subsequently had a planned caesarean section, yielding favorable outcomes for both mother and infant. For pregnant patients presenting with large, symptomatic masses, thorough assessment through bimanual examination and appropriate, safe imaging tools—such as ultrasound and non-contrast MRI—is crucial. Surgical intervention should be reserved for cases with suspected malignancy or complications, with the goal of maintaining the pregnancy and minimizing unnecessary treatment. The choice of delivery method should consider the masses’ size, quantity, and precise anatomical position.

Background:

Diagnosing and treating atypical or symptomatic abdominal masses in pregnant patients is clinically complex. Assessment is restricted to physical examination, ultrasound (US) with color Doppler imaging, and non-contrast magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Preoperative US is especially helpful for detecting symptomatic uterine leiomyomas situated on the anterior aspect of the pregnant uterus, including submucosal or pedunculated types. MRI is more effective for characterizing multiple, large, intramural, or posterior leiomyomas because it can accurately measure the space between the lesion and the uterine cavity.

The significance of serum tumor markers in pregnancy is still under discussion. For example, CA-125 concentrations naturally increase during the first trimester before returning to baseline later in gestation. As such, single test results lack diagnostic value, whereas sequential measurements may reveal patterns useful for distinguishing between causes and for surveillance. Careful multidisciplinary evaluation is critical in these cases to weigh obstetric hazards against the rarity and intricacies of the condition.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 40s, obese with a body mass index of 33 and a history of G1P0A1, presented to us during the second trimester at 21 weeks’ gestation, complaining of severe, sudden-onset abdominal pain localized to the upper left quadrant and pelvis (Visual Analogue Scale score of 5 at each site), accompanied by mild shortness of breath. Routine first and second trimester screenings indicated a low likelihood of aneuploidies—findings corroborated by non-invasive prenatal testing—and normal fetal anatomy was observed.

Initially, she presented to the emergency department, where a comprehensive multidisciplinary assessment was conducted. Oxygen saturation levels were within normal limits, with a median of 97%. On physical examination, the thorax exhibited a regular heart rate, with a median of 74 bpm, and blood pressure measured at a median of 100/60 mm Hg. ECG findings were unremarkable. Subsequently, she was moved to the Obstetrical and Gynaecological Division. Her abdomen appeared more distended than would be anticipated for her stage of pregnancy; palpation revealed a tender, taut-elastic mass, measuring 18 cm, occupying the entire upper left quadrant, in the absence of clinical evidence of ascites.

The bimanual vaginal examination showed the presence of a fixed mass in the lesser pelvis of about 9 cm, rounded and hard, that partially bulged at the posterior fornix.

Analgesic management with intravenous paracetamol at a dose of 1000 mg twice daily, in conjunction with maintaining a semi-orthostatic position—potentially reducing diaphragmatic pressure caused by the gravid uterus and substantial abdominal masses—led to a modest improvement in symptoms without the need for additional oxygen therapy or ventilatory assistance, lowering the patient's pain from 5 to 1 on the Visual Analogue Scale.

While hospitalized, the patient experienced a mild fever. Accordingly, a nasopharyngeal swab PCR test for severe acute respiratory syndrome Coronavirus 2 was conducted and found to be negative; however, urine analysis revealed a Klebsiella pneumoniae infection. Laboratory results also demonstrated iron deficiency anaemia with haemoglobin measured at 9.9 g/L (reference range: 12.0–16.6 g/L) and serum ferritin at 25 mcg/L (reference range: 15–300 mcg/L); C-reactive protein was mildly elevated at 1.39 mg/dL (reference range: 0–0.3 mg/dL); and there was a raised neutrophil count, with white blood cells at 11.29×10^3 /uL (reference range: 4–10×10^3 /uL) and neutrophils at 9.27×10^3 /uL (reference range: 2.8–5.5×10^3 /uL), findings consistent with physiological changes in pregnancy.

Investigations:

Ultrasonography revealed two sizable, unilocular cystic lesions featuring smooth borders and peripheral blood flow. The superior lesion measured 18×12×7 cm, originating in the left subphrenic area and extending to the fundus of the uterus. The inferior lesion measured 9.5×8×8 cm and appeared attached to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma that displayed cystic degeneration (58×71 mm).

Serum tumor markers, including CA 19–9, CA 15–3, CEA, β2-microglobulin, and LDH, remained within normal limits, while CA-125 was elevated at 41.8 U/mL (reference value <35 U/mL), with values rising from 36 U/mL recorded two weeks previously. This indicated a mild upward trend in CA-125 concentration. Consequently, the patient underwent non-contrast MRI, which revealed thickened walls (1.4 cm), areas of inhomogeneous fluid signal on T1 and T2 fat-suppressed sequences, and regions of focal hyperintensity in both T1- and T2-weighted solid components (refer to figure 1; only T2 fat-suppressed images are presented). The boundaries of the caudal lesion were indistinct and appeared continuous with the posterior leiomyoma previously visualized on ultrasound. No lymphadenopathy was detected on MRI.

Differential diagnosis:

On ultrasonography, the cystic lesions resembled two large decidualized endometriomas, with the lower cyst appearing attached to an intramural-subserosal leiomyoma located on the posterior uterine wall, measuring 58×71 mm.

Magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated two well-defined, unilocular adnexal cysts exhibiting thickened blood vessels and heterogeneous internal fluid (refer to figure 1); the inferior lesion appeared to be adherent to a posterior uterine intramural-subserosal myoma undergoing cystic degeneration, also measuring 58×71 mm.

Owing to the physical prominence of the gravid uterus and the sizeable abdominal masses, no connecting stalk or vascular supply to the upper mass was found, which favored an ovarian origin, particularly endometriomas, given the mild increase in CA-125 detected during the second trimester of pregnancy (refer to discussion).

Imaging findings on ultrasound and MRI raised considerations of decidualised endometriomas versus cystic degenerating leiomyomas. The mildly raised CA-125 supported the possibility of an endometrioma for the upper lesion, while the observed continuity with the myometrium in the lower mass pointed to a myomatous etiology. The patient's dyspnoea was ascribed to the compressive effects of the large abdominal masses, with no signs of underlying pulmonary or cardiac impairment.

Treatment:

Given the progression of symptoms—including abdominal pain from the mass and dyspnoea related to restrictive lung disease caused by the masses' dimensions—as well as the requirement for definitive diagnosis, the potential for complications such as rupture, torsion, and infection, and anticipated uterine enlargement as the fetus developed, the patient consented to a laparotomy. The procedure was performed via a longitudinal incision from the xiphoid to the pubis at 21 weeks and 6 days of gestation.

First, we collected two samples of the free peritoneal fluid for cultural and cytological purposes. Then, we explored the abdominal cavity, verifying the presence of fetal cardiac activity.

Unexpectedly, the cranial lesion was identified as a large, pedunculated, atypical cystic leiomyoma of the uterus with a tense, elastic consistency and marked vascularity. It was found to be twisted at its base and leaking, which likely explained the patient’s fever during hospitalization (see figure 2). The surgical team proceeded to carefully excise this mass from its attachment to the uterus by employing a double clamp technique (see figure 2). After severing the stalk, the exposed base was meticulously secured with a single absorbable suture. The caudal lesion, arising from the uterine torus, was deliberately left untouched to reduce the potential for obstetric complications. On examination, this mass was fixed, displayed a smooth surface, and was soft to the touch, consistent with a large cystic intramural-subserosal leiomyoma.

The operation was completed in 2 hours and 25 minutes. Progesterone prophylaxis was administered to the patient beginning intraoperatively with 100 mg intramuscularly, and subsequently continued at 100 mg intramuscularly daily until hospital discharge. Following discharge, the patient was given intramuscular hydroxyprogesterone caproate (341 mg, 1 ampoule intramuscularly every three days in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions) up until planned delivery. For infection prophylaxis during the procedure, cefazolin 2 g was given intravenously, and this was followed postoperatively by cefazolin 1 g intravenously three times daily in combination with clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily during hospitalization, guided by urine culture results demonstrating Klebsiella pneumoniae highly sensitive to both cephalosporins and clarithromycin.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following the operation, the patient's anaemia worsened (Hb 7.5 g/L, HCT 24.3%) and iron deficiency persisted, necessitating administration of intravenous ferric carboxymaltose (1 g) and two blood transfusions, which successfully increased Hb to 10.0 g/L and HCT to 30.4%. She was discharged 10 days post-surgery without any complications, and began close obstetrical monitoring every 3 weeks, during which evaluations confirmed appropriate fetal growth (86th percentile by the WHO fetal growth chart calculator, available online at https://srhr.org/fetalgrowthcalculator/#/) as well as normal amniotic fluid index.

Peritoneal fluid analysis showed no evidence of infection or malignancy on culture or cytology. Histopathological assessment identified benign uterine leiomyomas, with regions of ischemic necrosis adjacent to the torsed pedicle.

The location of the caudal myoma would have significantly hindered descent of the fetal head during the expulsion stage of labor. Consequently, an elective caesarean section was carried out at the previous surgical site once the pregnancy reached 37 weeks, thereby reducing the risk of rupture at the uterine scar and resulting in a favorable neonatal outcome.

At present, the patient is asymptomatic with respect to the myoma, and the remaining pelvic myoma has decreased modestly in size to 7.5 cm. She is successfully breastfeeding, but has expressed a preference to postpone further surgical intervention. This decision is influenced by the partial reduction in size of the residual leiomyomas observed after childbirth, her lack of symptoms, and her sense of maternal fulfillment. The infant is developing normally.

Discussion:

Myomas can often be misdiagnosed as adnexal masses: the incidence during pregnancy is 0.3–10.1% and 1–6%, respectively .13

In this case, the first assessment revealed abdominal masses consistent with two large decidualized endometriomas, with the exception of the lower solid portion of the pelvic lesion, which resembled a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma showing cystic degeneration measuring 58×71 mm on both ultrasound and MRI studies. Due to the lack of established management protocols for pelvic masses during pregnancy, the multidisciplinary team determined that planned surgical intervention might reduce the likelihood of preterm birth.

Throughout pregnancy, serum CA-125 levels typically display two elevations: the first occurs in the initial trimester (median 23 U/mL, p<0.00001), and the second takes place during the third trimester (median 21 U/mL, p<0.001), both significantly higher than levels seen in the second trimester (median 14.0 U/mL). In contrast, this case demonstrated a CA-125 concentration of 41.8 U/mL, which exceeds the reported median for the second trimester.

CA-125 is primarily utilized as a tumor marker for ovarian malignancy, with the accepted threshold being ≥35 U/mL. Its diagnostic utility is particularly notable in women over 50 years of age, showing improved area under the curve, positive predictive value, sensitivity, and specificity. However, elevated CA-125 levels may also be associated with various non-malignant conditions, including endometriosis and adenomyosis (typically above 19 U/mL), pelvic inflammatory disease, menstruation, and pregnancy in both early and late stages (median values of 23 and 21 U/mL, respectively), as well as risk of miscarriage and uterine myomas (with a cut-off below 19 U/mL).57

The main factors guiding clinical management included the identification of large, highly vascular masses featuring both solid and cystic regions and posing an immediate risk of complications such as rupture, torsion, or infection. Additional considerations were the patient's deteriorating symptoms, notably increasing pain and shortness of breath; the likelihood that further growth of the masses would heighten the risk of hypoxia, necrosis, and tissue degeneration; marginal elevation of tumor markers (CA-125 measured at 41.8 U/mL); and restricted intra-abdominal space, potentially compromising normal uterine expansion.

Given the potential neoplastic nature and ambiguous etiology of the lesions, the patient subsequently underwent a non-contrast MRI following ultrasonography to enhance diagnostic precision. Current guidelines advise against performing gadolinium-enhanced MRI in pregnant patients unless there is a compelling indication, such as rheumatological, inflammatory, or infiltrative dermal disorders, or increased risk of stillbirth or neonatal mortality. As a result, the multidisciplinary team proceeded with a non-contrast MRI scan, carefully ensuring that the magnetic field strength did not exceed 1.5 Tesla, as this approach is regarded as reasonably safe beyond the first trimester of pregnancy.

In this case, a multidisciplinary team that included surgeons, gynecologists, and obstetricians decided to undertake longitudinal laparotomic surgery after securing explicit written informed consent from the patient. During surgery, the upper lesion was found to have a pedicle, which was associated with a substantial risk for an acute and characteristic complication. This finding guided the decision to restrict the intervention to a myomectomy of the upper mass. Additionally, fetal viability was monitored intraoperatively using repeated transvaginal ultrasonography, with concurrent administration of prophylactic progesterone during the procedure.

According to a large multicentric study conducted by Zhao et al, it is mandatory to check and document the fetal heart rate before, during and after the surgery.

Typically, surgical intervention is postponed until after delivery, favoring a conservative, medically expectant strategy. Nevertheless, Sakinci et al recently conducted a study at a tertiary center indicating no statistically significant differences between women who had caesarean section (CS) with concurrent myomectomy and those with no myomas who underwent CS alone regarding preoperative and postoperative hemoglobin levels or need for blood transfusion (p >0.05). However, both length of hospitalization and duration of surgery were markedly higher in the group receiving CS with myomectomy (p = 0.001 and p = 0.001, respectively). The average myoma size was 8.3 ± 4.1 cm, and there was a significant positive association between the size of the myoma and the duration of hospitalization (p = 0.01).

The same research study also showed a statistically significant and inverse correlation between the size of the myoma and the delivery week (p = 0.035).

Uterine leiomyomas exhibit a non-linear pattern of size fluctuation throughout pregnancy and the postpartum period. Specifically, leiomyoma size grows by an average of 6.90% per week: an increase of 6.12% (p<0.001) is observed from the first to second trimester, and a rise of 3.10%±0.39% occurs between the second and third trimesters (p<0.05). This data indicates that the rate of leiomyoma growth is notably greater in the first trimester compared to subsequent stages of gestation. Conversely, during the third trimester and up to 6 weeks postpartum, the size of myomas significantly diminishes (mean reduction of 44.25%±36.05%, p<0.05).

These papers showed the importance of an accurate US examination of the size, location, number and distance of uterine myomas from the placenta before the surgical approach.

When uterine myomas are identified during pregnancy, management typically favors conservative therapy over surgical intervention, given the potential dangers such as significant hemorrhage, miscarriage, premature labor, and uterine rupture. Nonetheless, myomectomy may become necessary for complicated lesions, including torsion of a pedunculated fibroid or persistent symptoms that do not respond to conservative measures. Surgical intervention should be kept as brief as feasible to protect the ongoing pregnancy, and may be accomplished via laparoscopy, ideally utilizing an open Hasson entry in patients with lower gestational age (during the first or early second trimester), smaller lesions, and in non-intramural locations. This technique offers advantages for maternal morbidity and supports the possibility of vaginal delivery. Still, the case at hand illustrates the typical selection bias favoring laparotomy over laparoscopy for larger, multiple, intramural, or otherwise critical fibroid locations, which are associated with increased cesarean section rates, as demonstrated by the presence of a substantial myoma attached to the posterior isthmic uterine wall, which would likely have impeded vaginal delivery.
