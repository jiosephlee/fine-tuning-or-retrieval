Title: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

Rosalba De Nola, Emma Bassi, Cataldo Paletta, Mario Vicino

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her 40s, with no prior pregnancies, presented in the second trimester with abdominal pain and shortness of breath. Ultrasonography identified two sizable abdominal masses: one located in the left subphrenic region reaching up to the uterine fundus, and another occupying the pouch of Douglas. The patient underwent laparotomy, during which the more caudal mass was left undisturbed in the pelvis. She was subsequently monitored regularly through obstetric follow-up and proceeded to elective caesarean section, resulting in favorable outcomes for both mother and infant. For pregnant patients with large, symptomatic masses, thorough assessment utilizing bimanual examination along with safe imaging techniques such as ultrasound and non-contrast MRI is critical. Surgical intervention should be reserved for situations involving suspicion of malignancy or clinical complications, with the objective of preserving gestation and minimizing unnecessary procedures. The decision regarding mode of delivery should take into account the number, size, and anatomical location of uterine masses.

Background:

The identification and treatment of atypical or symptomatic abdominal masses in pregnant patients involve considerable clinical difficulties. Available diagnostic modalities are restricted to physical examination, ultrasound (US) with color Doppler, and non-contrast MRI. Preoperative US is especially helpful for detecting symptomatic uterine leiomyomas situated on the anterior aspect of the pregnant uterus, regardless of whether they are submucosal or pedunculated. For cases involving multiple, large, intramural, or posterior fibroids, MRI provides enhanced diagnostic precision by allowing accurate assessment of the separation between the myoma and the uterine cavity.

The utility of serum tumor markers in diagnosing conditions during pregnancy remains controversial. For example, CA-125 concentrations naturally increase during the first trimester and later return to baseline levels. Thus, a single measurement lacks diagnostic specificity, whereas serial assessments may reveal clinically meaningful trends for differential diagnosis and ongoing monitoring. Appropriate management strategies necessitate comprehensive multidisciplinary evaluation to carefully weigh the potential obstetric risks against the uncommon and complex nature of these cases.

Case presentation:

A woman in her forties with a body mass index of 33, gravida 1, para 0, abortus 1, presented during her second trimester at 21 weeks of gestation with intense, sudden onset pain in the left upper abdomen and pelvic region, both sites rating 5 on the Visual Analogue Scale, accompanied by mild shortness of breath. Routine prenatal screening from the first and second trimesters indicated low risk for chromosomal abnormalities, as corroborated by non-invasive prenatal testing, and fetal morphology appeared normal.

Initially, she presented to the emergency department for a comprehensive evaluation involving multiple specialties. Her oxygen saturation was within normal limits (median 97%). On chest examination, her heart rate was regular (median 74 bpm) and her blood pressure measured within normal range (median 100/60 mm Hg). The electrocardiogram demonstrated no abnormalities. Subsequently, she was moved to the Obstetrics and Gynecology Department. The abdomen appeared more distended than anticipated for her gestational age and exhibited a tender swelling with a firm, elastic mass spanning the entire upper left quadrant, measuring 18 cm, but there were no clinical indications of ascites.

The bimanual vaginal examination showed the presence of a fixed mass in the lesser pelvis of about 9 cm, rounded and hard, that partially bulged at the posterior fornix.

Analgesia with intravenous paracetamol 1000 mg administered twice daily, combined with maintaining the patient in a semi-orthostatic position—intended to potentially alleviate diaphragmatic compression from the gravid uterus and large intra-abdominal masses—resulted in a mild reduction of symptoms without the requirement for supplemental oxygen or ventilatory assistance. Pain levels, as measured by the Visual Analogue Scale, improved from 5 to 1.

While hospitalized, the patient experienced a low-grade fever. Consequently, a nasopharyngeal swab for PCR detection of severe acute respiratory syndrome Coronavirus 2 was undertaken and returned negative. Conversely, urine cultures indicated a Klebsiella pneumoniae infection. Laboratory findings were significant for iron deficiency anaemia, with haemoglobin measured at 9.9 g/L (reference range 12.0–16.6 g/L) and serum ferritin at 25 mcg/L (reference range 15–300 mcg/L); C-reactive protein was modestly elevated at 1.39 mg/dL (reference range 0–0.3 mg/dL); leukocytosis was noted, with total white blood cells at 11.29×10^3 /uL (normal 4–10×10^3 /uL) and neutrophils at 9.27×10^3 /uL (normal 2.8–5.5×10^3 /uL), which are findings often observed during pregnancy.

Investigations:

Ultrasound evaluation revealed two sizable, unilocular cystic lesions with smooth contours and peripheral blood flow. The superior lesion was 18×12×7 cm and spanned from the left subphrenic area to the uterine fundus. The inferior lesion measured 9.5×8×8 cm and appeared attached to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma exhibiting cystic changes (measuring 58×71 mm).

Serum tumor markers including CA 19–9, CA 15–3, CEA, β2-microglobulin, and LDH were found to be within normal ranges, but CA-125 was raised at 41.8 U/mL (reference <35 U/mL), demonstrating an upward trajectory from 36 U/mL two weeks prior. This indicated a modest increase in this marker’s serum level over time. Consequently, the patient underwent a non-contrast MRI scan, which detected cyst walls 1.4 cm in thickness, inhomogeneous fluid on both T1- and T2-weighted fat-saturated images, along with focal areas of hyperintensity within the solid portions on T1- and T2-weighted sequences (refer to figure 1; only T2 fat-saturated images are presented). The boundaries of the lower lesion were indistinct, suggesting possible continuity with the posterior leiomyoma seen on prior ultrasound. No lymphadenopathy was seen on MRI.

Differential diagnosis:

During ultrasonography, the cystic lesions resembled two large decidualized endometriomas, with the lower cyst observed to be attached to a 58×71 mm intramural-subserosal leiomyoma located on the posterior uterine wall.

Magnetic resonance imaging identified two well-defined, unilocular adnexal cysts characterized by hypertrophic vessels and heterogeneous internal fluid (see figure 1); the more caudal lesion appeared adherent to a posteriorly positioned intramural-subserosal uterine myoma displaying cystic degeneration, measuring 58×71 mm.

Owing to the physical obstruction presented by the enlarged gravid uterus and the substantial size of the masses, no connective stalk or tumor-feeding vessel was visualized in relation to the superior mass, which directed diagnostic consideration toward ovarian lesions—particularly endometriomas—in light of mildly increased CA-125 levels observed during the second trimester (refer to discussion).

Imaging with ultrasound and MRI yielded a differential diagnosis that included decidualized endometriomas as well as cystic degeneration within leiomyomas; the slight rise in CA-125 supported a diagnosis of endometrioma for the superior cyst, while the anatomical continuity with uterine tissue pointed to a myomatous etiology for the lower mass. The patient’s dyspnoea was attributed to the substantial abdominal masses exerting compressive effects, with no clinical findings suggestive of pulmonary or cardiac impairment.

Treatment:

Given the progression of symptoms—including abdominal pain associated with the masses and dyspnoea resulting from restrictive lung impairment due to the size of the abdominal tumors—along with the need to establish a definitive diagnosis, the potential for complications (such as rupture, torsion, or infection), and the anticipated uterine enlargement accompanying fetal development, the patient consented to a laparotomy using a longitudinal xiphoid-to-pubic incision at 21 weeks and 6 days of gestation.

First, we collected two samples of the free peritoneal fluid for cultural and cytological purposes. Then, we explored the abdominal cavity, verifying the presence of fetal cardiac activity.

Unexpectedly, the cranial mass was identified as a large pedunculated leiomyoma of the uterus, characterized by atypical cystic features and a tense, elastic consistency. It was highly vascularized, initially showed evidence of torsion and minor hemorrhage, which likely accounted for the fever observed during the patient’s hospitalization (see figure 2). Consequently, the surgical team elected to carefully excise the mass at its uterine attachment using a double clamp technique (see figure 2). Following transection of the stalk, the exposed base was meticulously secured with a single absorbable suture. The caudal mass, which arose from the uterine torus, was deliberately left in place to reduce potential obstetric complications. This lesion was immobile, exhibited a smooth contour, and had a soft consistency, resembling a sizable cystic leiomyoma with both intramural and subserosal components.

The operation was completed in 2 hours and 25 minutes. Of note, the patient began receiving intramuscular progesterone prophylaxis at a dose of 100 mg during surgery, which was continued daily at the same dose until hospital discharge. Subsequently, she was administered intramuscular hydroxyprogesterone caproate (341 mg per ampule intramuscularly every 3 days, in accordance with the instructions in the package insert) up to the planned delivery. For infection prophylaxis, cefazolin 2 g was given intravenously during surgery, which was followed postoperatively by cefazolin 1 g intravenously three times daily combined with clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily while hospitalized. This regimen was chosen based on the urine culture’s antibiogram, which indicated Klebsiella pneumoniae with marked susceptibility to both cephalosporins and clarithromycin.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following the surgical procedure, the patient's anaemia worsened (Hb 7.5 g/L, HCT 24.3%) and iron deficiency persisted, necessitating administration of intravenous ferric carboxymaltose (1 g) and two units of blood transfused, which led to improvement (Hb 10.0 g/L, HCT 30.4%). The patient was discharged 10 days postoperatively without any complications, and commenced a rigorous obstetric follow-up every 3 weeks. This monitoring demonstrated appropriate fetal growth (86th percentile per the WHO fetal growth chart calculator, accessible at https://srhr.org/fetalgrowthcalculator/#/) and satisfactory amniotic fluid index.

Both culture and cytology of the peritoneal fluid were negative. Histopathological analysis identified benign uterine leiomyomas and zones of ischaemic necrosis adjacent to the twisted pedicle.

The location of the caudal myoma would have significantly impeded descent of the fetal head during the expulsion stage of labor. Consequently, an elective cesarean section was scheduled using the previous surgical incision once gestational age reached 37 weeks, thereby reducing the chance of uterine scar rupture and resulting in an excellent neonatal outcome.

At present, the patient denies experiencing any symptoms attributable to myoma, despite a slight decrease in the remaining pelvic myoma’s size to 7.5 cm. She continues to breastfeed successfully; however, she opts not to plan further surgical intervention at this time, given the partial postpartum reduction of the residual leiomyomas, her lack of symptoms, and her completed childbearing. The infant is developing normally.

Discussion:

Myomas can often be misdiagnosed as adnexal masses: the incidence during pregnancy is 0.3–10.1% and 1–6%, respectively .13

In this case, the first assessment revealed large abdominal masses consistent with two extensive decidualized endometriomas. However, the lower solid portion of the pelvic mass was suggestive of a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration, measuring 58×71 mm on both ultrasound and MRI studies. Given the lack of established protocols for managing pelvic masses during pregnancy, the multidisciplinary team determined that planned surgical intervention might lower the likelihood of preterm birth.

Typically, serum CA-125 levels during pregnancy exhibit two peaks: one occurs in the first trimester (median 23 U/mL, p<0.00001), and another in the third trimester (median 21 U/mL, p<0.001), both of which are elevated relative to the second trimester (median 14.0 U/mL). Conversely, in this case, the measured value was 41.8 U/mL, which surpasses the median level observed in the second trimester.

CA-125 serves primarily as a tumor marker for ovarian cancer, with a commonly accepted threshold of ≥35 U/mL, particularly in women over the age of 50, in whom it demonstrates greater area under the curve, positive predictive value, sensitivity, and specificity. However, elevated CA-125 can also be observed in various benign conditions, including endometriosis and adenomyosis (typically >19 U/mL), pelvic inflammatory disease, menstruation, as well as early and late stages of pregnancy (median levels of 23 U/mL and 21 U/mL, respectively), risk of miscarriage, and uterine fibroids (commonly <19 U/mL).57

The primary factors guiding the clinical approach included the identification of large, highly vascular masses exhibiting both solid and cystic regions, which carried an immediate risk of complications such as rupture, torsion, or infection. Additional considerations were the patient's deteriorating clinical status, characterized by increasing pain and dyspnoea; the potential for further enlargement of the masses, raising the likelihood of hypoxia, necrosis, and degeneration; mildly elevated tumor marker levels (CA-125=41.8 U/mL); and the restricted intra-abdominal space, which could compromise the capacity for continued normal uterine growth.

Given the potential neoplastic nature and ambiguous source of the masses, an MRI without contrast was subsequently performed following the ultrasound to improve diagnostic precision. Current guidelines advise against the use of gadolinium-enhanced MRI in pregnant patients except when absolutely indicated, such as in cases involving rheumatological, inflammatory, or infiltrative dermatologic diseases, or when there is a risk of stillbirth or neonatal mortality. In light of these recommendations, the multidisciplinary team proceeded with a non-contrast MRI and refrained from using MRI systems exceeding 1.5-T, which are deemed acceptably safe post-first trimester.

In this case, the multidisciplinary team—which included surgeons, gynecologists, and obstetricians—chose to undertake a longitudinal laparotomic surgical approach after securing specific written informed consent from the patient. During intraoperative evaluation, the upper lesion was found to have a pedicle, representing a considerable risk for an acute and characteristic complication. As a result, the procedure was restricted to removal of the upper myoma. Additionally, ongoing fetal viability was assessed intraoperatively using repeated transvaginal ultrasound examinations, with prophylactic intraoperative progesterone administration used as an adjunct.

According to a large multicentric study conducted by Zhao et al, it is mandatory to check and document the fetal heart rate before, during and after the surgery.

Typically, surgical intervention is postponed until after delivery, with a preference for medical management and expectant observation. However, a recent study by Sakinci et al conducted at a tertiary care facility found no statistically significant differences between women undergoing caesarean section (CS) with concurrent myomectomy and those without myomas undergoing only CS regarding preoperative and postoperative hemoglobin levels or rates of blood transfusion (p >0.05). Nevertheless, both hospital stay and duration of surgery were significantly increased in the group receiving CS with myomectomy (p = 0.001 and p = 0.001, respectively). The average myoma diameter was 8.3 ± 4.1 cm, and there was a significant positive correlation between the size of the myoma and length of hospitalization (p = 0.01).

The same research study also showed a statistically significant and inverse correlation between the size of the myoma and the delivery week (p = 0.035).

Uterine leiomyomas exhibit a variable pattern of size alteration throughout pregnancy and the puerperium. Specifically, leiomyoma dimensions increase weekly by 6.90%, with an observed 6.12% rise (p<0.001) between the first and second trimesters and an increment of 3.10%±0.39% from the second to the third trimester (p<0.05). This indicates that the rate of leiomyoma growth is notably higher in the first trimester compared to subsequent trimesters. Conversely, during the third trimester and up to six weeks postpartum, myoma size showed a significant reduction, with an average decrease of 44.25%±36.05% (p<0.05).

These papers showed the importance of an accurate US examination of the size, location, number and distance of uterine myomas from the placenta before the surgical approach.

When uterine myomas are identified during pregnancy, management typically involves conservative measures rather than surgery, primarily because of the heightened risk of significant hemorrhage, pregnancy loss, preterm delivery, and uterine rupture. Nevertheless, surgical removal (myomectomy) may become necessary when there are complex masses—such as a pedunculated myoma with torsion—or when persistent symptoms fail to respond to non-surgical interventions. Surgery should be kept as brief as possible to minimize jeopardy to the ongoing pregnancy. It may also be undertaken via a laparoscopic approach, particularly utilizing the open Hasson entry method when gestational age is lower (during the first and early second trimesters), the mass is smaller in size, and the myoma is located in areas other than the myometrium. This technique has been associated with better outcomes regarding maternal complications and the likelihood of vaginal delivery. However, the present case illustrates a common bias in favoring laparotomy rather than laparoscopy when confronted with larger, multiple, intramural myomas or those situated in critical areas—scenarios often linked to an increased rate of cesarean section. In this situation, a large myoma adherent to the posterior wall at the uterine isthmus posed a mechanical impediment to vaginal delivery.
