Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

Rosalba De Nola, Emma Bassi, Cataldo Paletta, Mario Vicino

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her 40s, with no prior pregnancies, experienced abdominal pain and dyspnoea during her second trimester. Ultrasonographic examination identified two sizable masses in the abdomen: the superior mass spanned the left subphrenic region up to the uterine fundus, while the inferior mass occupied the pouch of Douglas. The patient proceeded to laparotomy, but the caudal mass was left undisturbed in the pelvis. Following this, she received routine prenatal care and later underwent an elective caesarean section, both mother and neonate achieving optimal outcomes. For pregnant patients with substantial, symptomatic masses, thorough assessment using bimanual palpation and appropriate, safe imaging techniques—such as ultrasonography and non-contrast MRI—is critical. Surgical intervention should be reserved for cases involving oncologic concern or emergent clinical complications, with a primary goal of preserving the pregnancy and avoiding unnecessary procedures. The decision on delivery method should take into account the uterine masses' number, dimensions, and spatial relationship to pelvic structures.

Background:

The identification and treatment of unusual or symptomatic abdominal masses in pregnant patients is clinically complex. Diagnostic tools are largely restricted to physical assessment, ultrasound (US) with colour Doppler imaging, and non-contrast MRI. Preoperative US serves as a crucial modality for detecting symptomatic uterine leiomyomas situated on the anterior surface of the gravid uterus, including submucosal or pedunculated variants. For patients with multiple, large, intramural, or posteriorly placed leiomyomas, MRI demonstrates greater diagnostic precision, as it enables accurate measurement of the separation between the myoma and the uterine cavity.

The utility of serum tumour markers for diagnosis during pregnancy is still a subject of ongoing discussion. In particular, CA-125 concentrations naturally increase in the first trimester before returning to baseline levels, meaning that single measurements are not definitive for diagnosis; however, serial monitoring may reveal diagnostic patterns and is valuable in distinguishing among possible conditions and tracking progression. Appropriate management strategies necessitate thorough multidisciplinary evaluation to carefully weigh the risks to the pregnancy against the low frequency and intricacy of these cases.

Case presentation:

A woman in her forties with obesity (BMI 33), gravida 1 para 0 abortus 1, was referred to our clinic at 21 weeks’ gestation due to severe, acute pain in the upper left abdominal quadrant and pelvic region (Visual Analogue Scale score of 5 for each site) as well as mild shortness of breath. Previous first and second trimester screenings indicated a low likelihood of fetal aneuploidy, as corroborated by non-invasive prenatal testing, and fetal anatomy appeared unremarkable.

Upon initial presentation, she was evaluated in the emergency department by a multidisciplinary team. Her oxygen saturation was within normal limits (median: 97%), cardiac and thoracic examinations revealed a regular heart rate (median: 74 bpm) and normotension (median blood pressure: 100/60 mm Hg), and ECG results were unremarkable. She was subsequently transferred to the Department of Obstetrics and Gynaecology. Clinical examination noted a distended abdomen larger than expected for her gestational stage, with a firm, taut-elastic mass measuring 18 cm that filled the entire upper left quadrant; there was no evidence of ascites.

A bimanual pelvic exam identified a fixed, spherical, and hard mass measuring approximately 9 cm located in the lesser pelvis, partially protruding into the posterior vaginal fornix.

She received intravenous paracetamol (1000 mg twice daily) for pain management and was maintained in a semi-orthostatic posture to possibly lessen diaphragmatic compression from the gravid uterus and the sizable abdominal masses. This approach yielded a mild improvement in symptoms, with pain scores decreasing from 5 to 1 on the Visual Analogue Scale, without the need for supplemental oxygen or ventilatory assistance.

During hospitalization, she developed a mild fever. PCR testing for SARS-CoV-2 using a nasopharyngeal swab returned negative; however, urine culture was positive for Klebsiella pneumoniae. Laboratory findings included iron deficiency anaemia with haemoglobin at 9.9 g/L (reference: 12.0–16.6 g/L) and serum ferritin of 25 mcg/L (reference: 15–300 mcg/L), a modest rise in C reactive protein at 1.39 mg/dL (reference: 0–0.3 mg/dL), and leukocytosis with neutrophilia (white blood cell count: 11.29×10^3 /uL [reference: 4–10×10^3 /uL], neutrophils: 9.27×10^3 /uL [reference: 2.8–5.5×10^3 /uL]), consistent with pregnancy-associated changes.

Investigations:

Ultrasound evaluation revealed two sizable, unilocular cystic lesions exhibiting smooth contours and vascularisation along their peripheries. The superior lesion was 18×12×7 cm in dimension, reaching from the left subphrenic space down to the uterine fundus. The inferior lesion measured 9.5×8×8 cm and appeared to be attached to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma, which demonstrated cystic degeneration (58×71 mm).

Assessment of tumour markers (CA 19–9, CA 15–3, CEA, β2-microglobulin, and LDH) found values within normal parameters; however, CA-125 was raised (41.8 U/mL; reference <35 U/mL), and demonstrated a slight increase compared to the value of 36 U/mL measured two weeks prior. In response to this finding, the patient underwent a non-contrast MRI, which detected thickened cyst walls (1.4 cm) and inhomogeneous intralesional fluid on T1 and T2 fat-saturated images, along with focally hyperintense T1-T2 weighted solid regions (refer to figure 1, with only T2 fat-sat images provided). The distinction of the lower mass from adjacent tissues was indistinct, suggesting continuity with the posterior leiomyoma already noted on ultrasound. MRI did not reveal any lymphadenopathy.

Differential diagnosis:

During the ultrasonographic assessment, the cystic lesions resembled two sizable decidualised endometriomas, with the lower cyst noted to be adherent to an intramural-subserosal leiomyoma located on the posterior aspect of the uterus, measuring 58×71 mm.

Magnetic resonance imaging revealed two round, unilocular adnexal cysts characterized by enlarged vessels and heterogeneous fluid content (see figure 1). The lower lesion appeared contiguous with a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma undergoing cystic degeneration, also measuring 58×71 mm.

Owing to the considerable spatial occupation by the gravid uterus and the voluminous nature of the masses, no visible connecting pedicle or prominent vascular supply was observed for the superior lesion. This finding led to a provisional diagnosis favoring ovarian pathology, particularly endometriomas, supported by a mild elevation in CA-125 during the second trimester (see discussion).

Taking into account both sonographic and MRI findings, the differential diagnosis included decidualised endometriomas and leiomyomas undergoing cystic degeneration. The modest increase in CA-125 levels supported an endometrioma for the superior cyst, while anatomical continuity with the uterine wall suggested a myomatous etiology for the lower lesion. The patient’s dyspnoea was thought to result from direct compression by these large abdominal masses, as there was no indication of cardiopulmonary compromise.

Treatment:

In light of deteriorating clinical symptoms—including abdominal pain attributable to mass effect and dyspnoea from restrictive lung disease associated with the enlarging abdominal masses—together with the imperative for definitive diagnosis, the potential for serious complications (such as rupture, torsion, and infection), and the anticipated increase in uterine size concurrent with fetal development, the patient underwent a laparotomy via a longitudinal xiphos-pubic incision at 21 weeks and 6 days of gestation, subsequent to obtaining informed consent.

Initially, two samples of free peritoneal fluid were taken for both cultural and cytological analyses. Subsequently, the surgical team assessed the abdominal cavity and confirmed the presence of fetal cardiac activity.

Unexpectedly, the cranial mass was found to be a very large, pedunculated uterine leiomyoma, which was atypically cystic, taut-elastic in consistency, and highly vascularized. This lesion was initially twisted and leaking, which likely explained the patient’s fever during hospitalization (refer to figure 2). Consequently, the surgeons elected to carefully remove the mass at its site of uterine attachment using a double clamp technique (see figure 2). After severing the stalk, the exposed base was closed meticulously with a single absorbable suture. The caudal mass, which originated from the uterine torus, was left in place to reduce obstetric risk. This lesion appeared immobile with a smooth external surface and soft, cystic consistency characteristic of a large intramural-subserosal leiomyoma.

The operative time was 2 hours and 25 minutes. Progesterone prophylaxis (100 mg intramuscularly) was initiated intraoperatively and then maintained at 100 mg intramuscularly per day until hospital discharge. Thereafter, the patient received intramuscular caproate hydroxyprogesterone (341 mg, 1 fl intramuscularly every 3 days, per package instructions) until planned delivery. For infection prophylaxis, cefazolin 2 g was administered intravenously during surgery, followed by postoperative cefazolin 1 g intravenously three times daily in addition to clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily throughout hospitalization, as indicated by the urine culture antibiogram (Klebsiella pneumoniae, which demonstrated high sensitivity to cephalosporins and clarithromycin).

Outcome and follow-up:

Following the surgical procedure, the patient experienced a worsening of anaemia (Hb 7.5 g/L, HCT 24.3%) and continued iron deficiency, necessitating intravenous administration of ferric carboxymaltose (1 g) and the transfusion of two units of blood, which resulted in improved laboratory values (Hb 10.0 g/L, HCT 30.4%). She was discharged without any adverse events 10 days post-operation and began close obstetric monitoring every 3 weeks. This follow-up indicated favorable fetal growth (86th percentile as determined by the WHO fetal growth chart calculator, accessible at https://srhr.org/fetalgrowthcalculator/#/) and maintained amniotic fluid index.

Analysis of the peritoneal fluid revealed negative results for both microbiological and cytological evaluation. Histopathological assessment identified benign uterine leiomyomas with regions of ischaemic necrosis adjacent to the twisted pedicle.

Given that the caudal myoma would have posed a considerable barrier to the passage of the fetal head during the expulsive stage of labor, an elective caesarean section was scheduled on the existing surgical incision site upon the fetus attaining 37 weeks’ gestation. This approach was selected to mitigate the risk of uterine scar rupture, yielding an excellent outcome for the neonate.

At present, the patient remains asymptomatic with respect to myoma-related complaints, though the remaining pelvic myoma has decreased in size to 7.5 cm. She continues to breastfeed successfully but has opted not to schedule further surgery at this time due to the partial regression of leiomyomas observed after childbirth, the lack of related symptoms, and her satisfaction with her maternal status. The infant is developing appropriately.

Discussion:

Myomas can often be misdiagnosed as adnexal masses: the incidence during pregnancy is 0.3–10.1% and 1–6%, respectively .13

In this instance, initial diagnostic imaging revealed abdominal masses characterized as two large decidualised endometriomas. However, the caudal solid portion of the pelvic mass exhibited imaging features consistent with a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma displaying cystic degeneration, measuring 58×71 mm as observed on both US and MRI scans. Due to the absence of established protocols for managing pelvic masses during pregnancy, the multidisciplinary team concluded that elective surgical intervention might reduce the likelihood of preterm labor.

Typically, serum CA-125 concentrations in pregnancy exhibit two distinct peaks: one during the first trimester (median 23 U/mL, p<0.00001) and another in the third trimester (median 21 U/mL, p<0.001), both significantly higher relative to second trimester values (median 14.0 U/mL). In contrast, this case presented a serum CA-125 level of 41.8 U/mL, exceeding the median expected during the second trimester.

CA-125 is most frequently employed as a tumor marker for ovarian carcinoma, with a conventional threshold of ≥35 U/mL, and demonstrates increased diagnostic accuracy (higher area under the curve, positive predictive value, sensitivity, and specificity) in women over 50 years of age. Nonetheless, CA-125 elevation may also be associated with several benign conditions, such as endometriosis and adenomyosis (>19 U/mL), pelvic inflammatory disease, menstruation, early and late-stage pregnancy (median 23 and 21 U/mL, respectively), risk of miscarriage, as well as myoma (cut-off <19 U/mL).57

The main factors influencing the clinical management included the detection of large, vascularized lesions featuring both solid and cystic elements, which posed an immediate risk of adverse events such as rupture, torsion, or infection; the patient’s deteriorating symptoms, particularly escalating pain and shortness of breath; concerns that the masses might increase in size, potentially leading to hypoxia, necrosis, or degeneration; a mild elevation in tumor markers (CA-125=41.8 U/mL); and concerns regarding limited intra-abdominal space necessary for normal uterine expansion.

Given the indeterminate and possibly neoplastic nature of the lesions, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) without contrast was performed after ultrasonography to achieve greater diagnostic clarity. Consistent with the latest clinical guidelines, gadolinium-based contrast-enhanced MRI is generally contraindicated during pregnancy unless absolutely warranted by specific circumstances such as rheumatological, inflammatory, or infiltrative dermatological disorders, or when there is a threat of stillbirth or neonatal demise. Accordingly, the multidisciplinary team opted for a non-contrast MRI, ensuring that the magnetic field strength did not exceed 1.5-T, a threshold regarded as relatively safe after the first trimester.

In this case, the multidisciplinary team—including surgeons, gynaecologists, and obstetricians—chose to proceed with a longitudinal laparotomic surgical approach after acquiring formal written informed consent from the patient. During intraoperative assessment, the upper lesion was found to possess a pedicle, thereby increasing the likelihood of an acute and classical complication. This finding justified restricting the intervention to an upper myomectomy. Throughout the procedure, ongoing fetal viability was confirmed using both serial and intermittent transvaginal ultrasounds, accompanied by prophylactic intraoperative progesterone administration.

As highlighted in a substantial multicenter investigation by Zhao et al, it is essential to record the fetal heart rate before, throughout, and following the surgery.

Surgical intervention is commonly postponed until after delivery, favoring expectant or medical management. Nonetheless, Sakinci et al recently reported from a tertiary center that no statistically significant differences were observed between women who underwent caesarean section (CS) with myomectomy and those undergoing CS alone without myoma, regarding preoperative and postoperative hemoglobin concentrations or blood transfusion rates ($p > 0.05$). In contrast, the CS myomectomy cohort demonstrated significantly prolonged surgery and hospitalization durations ($p = 0.001$ and $p = 0.001$, respectively). The average myoma dimension measured $8.3 \pm 4.1$cm, and a significant positive correlation was found between the size of the myoma and the length of hospital stay ($p = 0.01$).

The aforementioned study also identified a statistically significant negative correlation between myoma size and gestational age at delivery (\(p = 0.035\)).

The progression of uterine leiomyomas throughout pregnancy and the puerperium does not follow a linear pattern. Specifically, leiomyoma dimensions expand by 6.90% weekly, with an increase of 6.12% (\(p<0.001\)) from the first to the second trimester, and 3.10%±0.39% from the second to the third trimester (\(p<0.05\)). These findings highlight that the most pronounced enlargement of uterine leiomyomas occurs in the first trimester compared to later stages of gestation. Conversely, there is a notable reduction in myoma size during the third trimester and within 6 weeks postpartum, with a mean decrease of 44.25%±36.05% (\(p<0.05\)).

These studies underscore the necessity for thorough ultrasonographic evaluation regarding the size, anatomical location, number, and spatial relationship of uterine myomas to the placenta prior to any surgical intervention.

When uterine myomas are detected during pregnancy, conservative management is generally favored over surgical intervention, considering the risks such as major hemorrhage, fetal loss, premature labor, and uterine rupture. Nonetheless, myomectomy is warranted in cases involving complicated myomas, such as those presenting with pedunculated torsion or persistent, unrelieved symptoms despite conservative therapy. The procedure should be as brief as possible to support pregnancy preservation and may be performed laparoscopically, with the open Hasson entry technique recommended for lower gestational ages (first and early second trimesters), smaller myoma volumes, and non-intramural lesions. This strategy is associated with improved outcomes regarding maternal morbidity and likelihood of vaginal delivery. Despite these recommendations, the present case exemplifies the frequent tendency to opt for laparotomy instead of laparoscopy in scenarios involving large, multiple, intramural, or unfavorably situated myomas. Such choices are often linked to an increased incidence of cesarean sections, as exemplified by a large myoma adhered to the posterior isthmic wall, potentially impeding vaginal delivery.
