Title: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

Rosalba De Nola, Emma Bassi, Cataldo Paletta, Mario Vicino

Abstract:

A woman in her 40s, who had not previously given birth, presented in her second trimester with abdominal pain and shortness of breath. Ultrasound examination identified two sizable abdominal masses: the superior mass filled the entire left subphrenic area and extended toward the uterine fundus, while the inferior mass occupied the pouch of Douglas. The patient underwent laparotomy, leaving the lower pelvic mass untouched in situ. Subsequent routine obstetric monitoring led to a planned caesarean section, with both mother and newborn achieving excellent outcomes. When pregnant patients have large, symptomatic masses, thorough assessment with bimanual palpation and safe imaging techniques such as ultrasonography and non-contrast MRI is crucial. Surgical intervention should only be pursued if there are oncological concerns or clinical complications, prioritizing the preservation of pregnancy and preventing unnecessary procedures. The approach to delivery should be individualized, based on the dimensions, quantity, and anatomical placement of uterine masses.

Background:

Diagnosing and treating atypical or symptomatic abdominal masses in pregnant patients is clinically challenging. Available diagnostic modalities include clinical assessment, ultrasound (US) with color Doppler, and non-contrast MRI. Preoperative US is especially useful in detecting symptomatic uterine leiomyomas situated on the anterior aspect of the pregnant uterus, regardless of whether they are submucosal or pedunculated. For cases involving multiple, large, intramural, or posterior leiomyomas, MRI provides improved diagnostic precision by clearly delineating the spatial relationship between the leiomyoma and the uterine cavity.

The utility of serum tumor markers in pregnancy is still under consideration. In particular, CA-125 concentrations naturally increase during the first trimester, then return to baseline, making single value measurements unreliable for diagnosis. However, serial testing may reveal trends helpful for differential diagnosis and ongoing monitoring. Effective management depends on a thorough multidisciplinary evaluation of risks to weigh obstetric complications against the uncommon and intricate nature of these clinical presentations.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 40s with obesity (BMI 33) and a history of G1P0A1 presented at 21 weeks gestation during her second trimester, complaining of intense and acute pain in the upper left abdominal quadrant and pelvis (Visual Analogue Scale=5 at both locations), along with mild shortness of breath. Routine first and second trimester screening indicated a low probability for aneuploidy, as confirmed by non-invasive prenatal testing, and showed normal fetal anatomy.

She was initially evaluated in the emergency department through a multidisciplinary approach. Findings included oxygen saturation at a median of 97%, chest examination showing a regular heart rate (median 74 bpm) and blood pressure (median 100/60 mmHg). ECG results were unremarkable. Following this, she was admitted to the Obstetrical and Gynecological Division. Her abdomen appeared larger than expected for her gestational age, with a sensitive swelling and a taut, elastic mass occupying the entire upper left quadrant, measuring 18 cm, absent any clinical signs of ascites.

On bimanual vaginal examination, a non-mobile mass approximately 9 cm in diameter, rounded and firm, was palpated in the lesser pelvis, partially protruding into the posterior fornix.

For symptom management, she received paracetamol (1000 mg IV twice daily) and was placed in a semi-upright position to potentially alleviate diaphragmatic pressure from the gravid uterus and large abdominal masses. This intervention provided some relief, lowering her pain score from 5 to 1 on the Visual Analogue Scale, without the need for additional oxygen or ventilation.

During hospitalization, the patient developed a mild fever. SARS-CoV-2 PCR testing via nasopharyngeal swab was negative; however, urinary analysis revealed a Klebsiella pneumoniae infection. Blood work demonstrated iron deficiency anemia, with haemoglobin at 9.9 g/L (reference range 12.0–16.6 g/L) and serum ferritin of 25 mcg/L (reference range 15–300 mcg/L). C-reactive protein was mildly elevated at 1.39 mg/dL (reference range 0–0.3 mg/dL), and there was an increased neutrophil count—total white blood cells at 11.29×10^3/uL (reference range 4–10×10^3/uL), neutrophils at 9.27×10^3/uL (reference range 2.8–5.5×10^3/uL)—in line with physiological changes associated with pregnancy.

Investigations:

Ultrasound revealed two sizeable, single-chambered cystic lesions featuring smooth borders and vascularity at the periphery. The superior lesion measured 18×12×7 cm, spanning from the left subphrenic region to the uterine fundus. The inferior lesion, measuring 9.5×8×8 cm, appeared adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration (58×71 mm).

Tumour markers, including CA 19–9, CA 15–3, CEA, β2-microglobulin, and LDH, remained within reference ranges. However, CA-125 was raised (41.8 U/mL; reference <35 U/mL) and had increased from 36 U/mL two weeks prior, indicating a gradual rise in the serum level of this marker. Consequently, an MRI without contrast was performed, which revealed thickened walls (1.4 cm), T1 and T2 fat-saturated sequences with heterogeneous fluid content, and regions of hyperintense solid tissue on T1-T2 weighted imaging (refer to figure 1; only T2 fat-sat images depicted). The margin between the caudal lesion and its surroundings was indistinct, as it appeared continuous with the posterior leiomyoma identified during the ultrasound assessment. No lymphadenopathy was detected on MRI.

Differential diagnosis:

During the ultrasound assessment, the cystic lesions resembled two large decidualised endometriomas; additionally, the cyst located inferiorly appeared attached to an intramural-subserosal leiomyoma situated on the posterior wall of the uterus, measuring 58×71 mm.

Magnetic resonance imaging revealed two round, unilocular adnexal cysts exhibiting enlarged vessels and heterogeneous fluid contents (refer to figure 1). The lower mass appeared to be contiguous with a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma, demonstrating cystic degeneration, measuring 58×71 mm.

The considerable size of the gravid uterus and these large masses prevented identification of any connecting stalk or neoplastic blood supply to the upper mass, thereby favoring an ovarian origin—specifically endometrioma—supported by mild CA-125 elevation observed during the second trimester (see discussion).

Evaluation of the ultrasound and MRI findings yielded differential diagnoses that included decidualised endometriomas and leiomyomas undergoing cystic degeneration. The modest increase in CA-125 supported an endometrioma diagnosis for the superior cyst; however, continuity with uterine tissue implicated a myomatous origin for the lower mass. The patient's dyspnoea was attributed to mechanical pressure from the substantial abdominal masses, with no evidence for underlying pulmonary or cardiac pathology.

Treatment:

Owing to the deterioration in the patient's clinical status—marked by abdominal pain attributable to the masses and dyspnoea related to restrictive lung disease from the bulk of the abdominal lesions—as well as the need to establish a definitive diagnosis, the risk of adverse events such as rupture, torsion, and infection, and the anticipated increase in uterine size with advancing gestation, surgical intervention was undertaken. At 21 weeks and 6 days’ gestation, and after obtaining informed consent, a laparotomy with a longitudinal incision from the xiphoid process to the pubis was performed.

Initially, two samples of free peritoneal fluid were collected for microbiological and cytological assessment. Subsequently, an examination of the abdominal cavity was carried out, during which ongoing fetal cardiac activity was confirmed.

Unexpectedly, the cranial mass was identified as a large, pedunculated, atypical, cystic, and firm-elastic uterine leiomyoma with significant vascularization; the lesion was mildly twisted and exhibited leakage, potentially explaining the episode of fever during admission (refer to figure 2). The surgical team elected to resect the mass from its uterine base carefully utilizing a double clamp technique (see figure 2). Following transection of the stalk, the exposed base was closed meticulously with a single absorbable suture. The caudal mass, arising from the uterine torus, was left untouched to reduce the risk to the pregnancy; it appeared fixed, with a smooth contour and soft consistency, similar in character to a large cystic intramural-subserosal leiomyoma.

The operative procedure lasted 2 hours and 25 minutes. The patient was given progesterone prophylaxis at a dose of 100 mg intramuscularly, initiated during surgery and subsequently administered daily intramuscularly until discharge. Thereafter, therapy was continued with hydroxyprogesterone caproate 341 mg intramuscularly every three days (as described in the product information) up to the planned delivery. Cefazolin 2 g intravenously was administered intraoperatively for antibiotic prophylaxis, followed postoperatively by cefazolin 1 g IV three times daily and clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily, based on urine culture sensitivity results (Klebsiella pneumoniae with high susceptibility to cephalosporins and clarithromycin), throughout the inpatient stay.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following the operation, the patient's anaemia worsened (Hb 7.5 g/L, HCT 24.3%), and ongoing iron deficiency necessitated the administration of intravenous iron (ferric carboxymaltose 1 g) alongside two successful blood transfusions (Hb increased to 10.0 g/L, HCT to 30.4%). She was discharged without complications 10 days post-surgery and subsequently underwent intensive obstetrical monitoring every three weeks, which demonstrated fetal growth (86th percentile per the WHO fetal growth chart calculator, available online at https://srhr.org/fetalgrowthcalculator/#/), as well as assessment of the amniotic fluid index.

Examination of peritoneal fluid showed negative results for both cultures and cytology. Histopathological analysis identified benign uterine leiomyomas with regions of ischaemic necrosis adjacent to the twisted pedicle.

The presence of the caudal myoma would have significantly impeded the passage of the fetal head during labor's expulsive stage. Therefore, an elective caesarean section was carried out at the previous surgical site once the fetus achieved 37 weeks' gestation, thereby reducing the likelihood of uterine scar rupture and resulting in an excellent neonatal outcome.

Presently, the patient does not experience symptoms attributable to the myoma, although the residual pelvic myoma has diminished slightly to 7.5 cm. She has been breastfeeding well and opts to postpone any further surgical intervention, considering the partial regression of the remaining leiomyomas postpartum, lack of symptoms, and completion of her maternity goals. The child is developing well.

Discussion:

Myomas can often be misdiagnosed as adnexal masses: the incidence during pregnancy is 0.3–10.1% and 1–6%, respectively .13

In this case, initial assessment revealed large abdominal masses resembling two significant decidualised endometriomas, except for the lower solid portion of the pelvic mass, which on both ultrasound and MRI was suggestive of a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma with cystic degeneration, measuring 58×71 mm. Given the lack of established protocols for managing pelvic masses during pregnancy, the multidisciplinary team concluded that performing elective surgery might lower the likelihood of preterm birth.

Throughout pregnancy, serum CA-125 typically demonstrates two elevation points: the first occurs during the first trimester (median 23 U/mL, p<0.00001), and the second in the third trimester (median 21 U/mL, p<0.001), when compared with lower levels in the second trimester (median 14.0 U/mL). In contrast, in this case, the measured value was 41.8 U/mL, exceeding the typical median for the second trimester.

Although CA-125 is primarily utilized as a tumor marker in ovarian carcinoma, with a standard cut-off of ≥35 U/mL—especially in women older than 50 (yielding greater area under the curve, predictive value, sensitivity, and specificity)—it can also be elevated in various benign conditions. These include endometriosis and adenomyosis (typically >19 U/mL), pelvic inflammatory disease, menstrual periods, early and late stages of pregnancy (median values of 23 and 21 U/mL, respectively), risk of miscarriage, and myomas (with a cut-off of <19 U/mL).57

The principal factors influencing clinical management included the identification of large, highly vascular masses with both solid and cystic elements, which posed immediate risks such as rupture, torsion, or infection. The patient's symptomatology, specifically worsening abdominal pain and dyspnoea, potential for mass enlargement leading to hypoxia, necrosis, and degeneration, a mildly elevated oncological marker (CA-125=41.8 U/mL), and the likelihood of restricting the uterus’s physiological expansion, were also significant considerations.

Given the potential neoplastic nature and indeterminate etiology of these lesions, the patient underwent a non-contrast MRI following the initial ultrasound to improve diagnostic certainty. In line with current guidelines, gadolinium-enhanced MRI is generally avoided in pregnancy except when urgently indicated in cases such as rheumatological, inflammatory or infiltrative dermatoses, or when there is a significant risk of stillbirth or neonatal demise. Consequently, the multidisciplinary care team proceeded with a non-contrast MRI, ensuring the imaging field strength did not exceed 1.5-Tesla, a parameter deemed suitably safe for use beyond the first trimester.

In this case, the multidisciplinary team—including surgeons, gynecologists, and obstetricians—decided to pursue a longitudinal laparotomy following formal written informed consent obtained from the patient. During intraoperative evaluation, the superior mass was found to possess a pedicle, which carried a considerable risk for an acute and characteristic complication. Consequently, only an upper myomectomy was performed. Additionally, fetal viability was monitored intraoperatively with repeated and interval transvaginal ultrasound examinations, supplemented by prophylactic intraoperative progesterone administration.

A major multicenter investigation by Zhao et al. emphasized that monitoring and documentation of fetal heart rate is required before, throughout, and after the surgical intervention.

Typically, surgery is postponed until after delivery, favoring either medical or expectant management. Nonetheless, Sakinci et al. conducted a recent study at a tertiary facility revealing no statistically significant differences between the group undergoing combined caesarean section (CS) plus myomectomy versus those with only CS and no myoma, with regard to preoperative and postoperative hemoglobin levels or rates of blood transfusion (p >0.05). However, the CS myomectomy group experienced notably longer hospital stays and operative times (both p = 0.001). The average size of myoma was 8.3 ± 4.1 cm, and there was a significant positive correlation between myoma diameter and length of hospitalization (p = 0.01).

The same study found a statistically significant inverse association between myoma size and gestational age at delivery (p = 0.035).

Uterine leiomyomas exhibit a non-linear pattern of size fluctuation throughout pregnancy and the postpartum period. Specifically, leiomyoma size increases by 6.90% weekly: 6.12% (p<0.001) from the first to the second trimester, and 3.10%±0.39% from the second to the third trimester (p<0.05). Consequently, uterine leiomyomas grow more rapidly during the initial trimester compared to subsequent stages of pregnancy. Conversely, during the third trimester and up to 6 weeks postpartum, myoma size significantly decreased, with a mean reduction of 44.25%±36.05% (p<0.05).

The literature underscores the necessity for thorough ultrasound evaluation prior to surgical intervention, including assessment of myoma size, location, quantity, and proximity to the placenta.

For uterine myomas detected during pregnancy, conservative management is generally favored over surgical treatment, due to heightened risks of severe hemorrhage, pregnancy loss, premature labor, and uterine rupture. Nonetheless, myomectomy may be warranted when complicated masses, such as a torsioned pedunculated myoma or persistent symptoms unresponsive to conservative therapy, are present. Surgical procedures should be kept brief to safeguard the pregnancy, and laparoscopy, particularly utilizing the open Hasson method, is advised for cases involving early gestational ages (first and early second trimesters), smaller masses, or specific (non-intramural) locations. This technique is associated with improved maternal outcomes and greater likelihood of vaginal delivery. However, the current case illustrates a typical selection bias favoring laparotomy over laparoscopy in situations with larger, multiple, intramural, or problematic myoma locations, which may be linked to higher rates of cesarean section, particularly when a substantial myoma adheres to the posterior wall of the isthmic region of the uterus and poses a physical barrier to vaginal delivery.
