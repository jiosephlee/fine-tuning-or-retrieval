Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

Rosalba De Nola, Emma Bassi, Cataldo Paletta, Mario Vicino

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her 40s with no prior pregnancies experienced abdominal pain and shortness of breath during her second trimester. Imaging with ultrasonography identified two sizeable abdominal masses: the superior mass spanned the left subphrenic area and extended down to the fundus of the uterus, while the inferior mass was located in the pouch of Douglas. Surgical intervention via laparotomy was performed, but the pelvic mass was not removed and remained within the pelvis. The patient continued with routine obstetric care and ultimately underwent a planned caesarean section, leading to an excellent outcome for both mother and child. For pregnant patients presenting with large symptomatic abdominal masses, it is crucial to perform a thorough assessment that includes bimanual examination alongside safe imaging techniques such as ultrasonography and non-contrast MRI. Surgical intervention should be limited to scenarios where there is suspicion of malignancy or when clinical complications arise, with a consistent emphasis on preserving the pregnancy and minimizing unnecessary procedures. Decisions regarding the method of delivery should be guided by factors such as the dimensions, quantity, and specific anatomical positions of uterine masses.

Background:

The identification and treatment of unusual or symptomatic abdominal masses in pregnant patients pose considerable clinical difficulties. The range of diagnostic modalities is restricted to clinical evaluation, ultrasound (US) incorporating colour Doppler, and non-contrast MRI. Preoperative US plays an essential role in detecting symptomatic uterine leiomyomas along the anterior uterine wall, including submucosal and pedunculated forms. In contrast, MRI is particularly advantageous for delineating multiple, sizable, intramural, or posteriorly situated leiomyomas, as it can accurately assess the spatial relationship between the myoma and the uterine cavity.

The utility of serum tumour markers in pregnancy is still contentious. Specifically, CA-125 levels naturally increase during early gestation but return to baseline later, rendering single measurements non-informative. However, sequential assessments may be valuable for monitoring trends to support differential diagnosis and ongoing surveillance. Effective management hinges on a thorough, multidisciplinary evaluation of risks to judiciously balance obstetric considerations with the infrequency and intricacies inherent to such presentations.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 40s, gravida 1 para 0 aborta 1, with a body mass index of 33, was evaluated at 21 weeks’ gestation due to acute, severe pain localized to the upper left abdominal quadrant and pelvic region, both rated as 5 on the Visual Analogue Scale, alongside mild shortness of breath. Routine first and second trimester prenatal screening, including non-invasive prenatal testing, indicated low aneuploidy risk, and fetal anatomy appeared normal.

Initially, she was admitted to the emergency department for comprehensive multidisciplinary assessment. Her oxygen saturation remained within normal limits (median 97%), with a median heart rate of 74 bpm and a blood pressure of 100/60 mm Hg; cardiac auscultation and ECG results were unremarkable. Upon transfer to the Obstetrical and Gynaecological Division, her abdominal examination revealed distension greater than anticipated for her gestational age and a large, tender, elastic mass occupying the upper left quadrant measuring 18 cm in diameter, absent clinical evidence of ascites.

During bimanual vaginal examination, a fixed, firm, rounded mass measuring approximately 9 cm was detected in the lesser pelvis, protruding partially toward the posterior fornix.

Symptomatic management involved intravenous paracetamol (1000 mg twice daily) and maintaining a semi-orthostatic position, intended to alleviate diaphragmatic compression from both the gravid uterus and the sizable abdominal mass. These interventions modestly ameliorated symptoms, reducing the Visual Analogue Scale pain score from 5 to 1, without necessitating supplemental oxygen or ventilatory assistance.

The hospital course was notable for the development of a low-grade fever. SARS-CoV-2 PCR testing via nasopharyngeal swab was negative, while urine culture identified a Klebsiella pneumoniae infection. Laboratory analysis demonstrated iron deficiency anaemia (haemoglobin 9.9 g/L; reference range 12.0–16.6 g/L; serum ferritin 25 mcg/L; reference 15–300 mcg/L), a mild elevation in C reactive protein (1.39 mg/dL; reference 0–0.3 mg/dL), and leukocytosis with neutrophilia (white blood cells 11.29×10^3 /uL [reference 4–10×10^3 /uL]; neutrophils 9.27×10^3 /uL [reference 2.8–5.5×10^3 /uL]), all compatible with physiological changes observed in pregnancy.

Investigations:

Ultrasound evaluation identified two sizable, unilocular cystic lesions characterized by smooth margins and vascularization at their peripheries. The superior mass was measured at 18×12×7 cm, spanning from the left subphrenic region down to the uterine fundus. The inferior lesion measured 9.5×8×8 cm and appeared to be adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration (58×71 mm).

Laboratory assessment of tumour markers—including CA 19–9, CA 15–3, CEA, β2-microglobulin, and LDH—showed values within normal reference ranges. Conversely, CA-125 was elevated (41.8 U/mL; normal <35 U/mL), having risen from 36 U/mL two weeks previously, indicating a mild upward trend in serum levels of this marker. As a result, the patient underwent non-contrast MRI, which revealed lesions with thickened walls (1.4 cm), inhomogeneous fluid content on T1- and T2-weighted fat-suppressed images, and discrete hyperintense solid regions on both T1 and T2 sequences (refer to figure 1; only T2 fat-sat images depicted). The caudal mass demonstrated indistinct separation from adjacent structures and appeared continuous with the previously detected posterior leiomyoma on ultrasound. No lymphadenopathy was detected on the MRI scan.

Differential diagnosis:

During the ultrasound assessment, the cystic lesions resembled two sizable decidualised endometriomas. The more caudal cyst appeared to be attached to an intramural-subserosal leiomyoma located on the posterior uterine wall, which measured 58×71 mm.

MRI imaging revealed two well-defined, unilocular adnexal cysts characterized by thickened vascularization and heterogeneous internal fluid (refer to figure 1). The caudal lesion was closely associated with a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration and measured 58×71 mm.

Given the substantial size of the gravid uterus and the presence of voluminous masses, neither a pedicle nor a vascular supply to the upper mass could be discerned, suggesting an ovarian origin—particularly endometrioma—especially in light of a mildly increased CA-125 level during the second trimester (see discussion).

From the sonographic and MRI findings, the main considerations in the differential diagnosis were decidualised endometriomas and leiomyomas with cystic degeneration. The minimally raised CA-125 level was more compatible with an endometrioma for the superior cyst, while the anatomical continuity with the uterus pointed to a leiomyomatous etiology for the caudal mass. The patient's dyspnoea was thought to result from mass effect caused by these large abdominal structures, with no indications of underlying cardiac or pulmonary pathology.

Treatment:

Owing to the progression of clinical symptoms—specifically, abdominal pain attributable to mass effect and dyspnoea related to restrictive lung disease caused by the volume of the abdominal masses—as well as the requirement for definitive diagnosis, heightened risk of complications (including rupture, torsion, and infection), and the anticipated increase in uterine size associated with fetal growth, laparotomy was performed at 21 w+6 d following acquisition of informed consent. The surgical approach consisted of a longitudinal xiphos-pubic incision.

At the outset of surgery, two samples of free peritoneal fluid were obtained for microbiological and cytological examination. The abdominal cavity was then systematically inspected, and fetal cardiac activity was confirmed.

Unexpectedly, the cranial mass was identified as a large, pedunculated, cystic and taut-elastic uterine leiomyoma exhibiting atypical features. The mass was markedly vascularised, showed initial evidence of torsion, and was leaking fluid—features likely accounting for the patient’s febrile episodes during hospitalization (refer to figure 2). Consequently, the surgical team opted for careful removal of the mass from its point of attachment on the uterus using a double clamp technique (see figure 2). After transection of the stalk, the exposed base was meticulously secured with a single absorbable suture. The caudal mass, arising from the uterine torus, was not resected in order to reduce obstetric risks; it appeared immobile, possessed a smooth surface and soft consistency, and resembled a large cystic intramural-subserosal leiomyoma.

The operative time was 2 hours and 25 min. Of note, the patient received an initial dose of 100 mg intramuscular progesterone for prophylaxis during the operation, followed by daily intramuscular administration of 100 mg until discharge. Subsequently, intramuscular caproate hydroxyprogesterone was given (341 mg 1 fl intramuscularly every 3 days, per manufacturer instructions) and continued until planned delivery. Antibiotic prophylaxis intraoperatively involved 2 g intravenous cefazolin, with postoperative antibiotic therapy consisting of cefazolin 1 g intravenously three times daily, along with clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily, tailored according to the urine culture antibiogram, which identified Klebsiella pneumoniae highly sensitive to cephalosporins and clarithromycin.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following the surgical procedure, the patient's anaemia continued to deteriorate (Hb 7.5 g/L, HCT 24.3%), and iron deficiency persisted. This necessitated intravenous administration of ferric carboxymaltose (1 g) in addition to two units of blood transfusion, which effectively increased Hb to 10.0 g/L and HCT to 30.4%. Ten days post-surgery, the patient was discharged in stable condition, without any complications, and began intensive obstetric surveillance every 3 weeks. Serial assessments indicated ongoing fetal growth (86th percentile according to the WHO fetal growth chart calculator, accessible online at https://srhr.org/fetalgrowthcalculator/#/), as well as appropriate amniotic fluid index.

Analysis of the peritoneal fluid returned negative results in both microbiological and cytological investigations. Histopathological evaluation demonstrated benign uterine leiomyomas with foci of ischaemic necrosis adjacent to the region of the twisted pedicle.

The location of the caudal myoma posed a considerable risk as a mechanical obstruction to fetal descent during the second stage of labour. As a preventive measure, an elective caesarean section was scheduled at 37 weeks’ gestation, utilizing the same incision site as the initial surgery. This approach mitigated the risk of uterine rupture and resulted in a favorable neonatal outcome.

Currently, the patient does not experience symptoms attributable to the remaining myoma, and follow-up imaging has noted a mild decrease in the residual pelvic myoma size to 7.5 cm. She continues to breastfeed successfully and has opted to postpone further surgical intervention, owing to both the partial postpartum regression of her leiomyomas and her lack of symptoms, as well as her satisfaction with her completed family. The infant is developing well.

Discussion:

Myomas can often be misdiagnosed as adnexal masses: the incidence during pregnancy is 0.3–10.1% and 1–6%, respectively .13

In this instance, initial assessments identified abdominal masses resembling two large decidualised endometriomas, with the exception of the lower solid portion of the pelvic mass, which appeared consistent with a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma of the uterus exhibiting cystic degeneration and measuring 58×71 mm on both US and MRI imaging. Given the lack of established guidelines for the management of pelvic masses during pregnancy, the multidisciplinary team determined that planned surgical intervention might mitigate the risk of preterm birth.

Typically, serum CA-125 levels during pregnancy exhibit two peaks: one in the first trimester (median 23 U/mL, p<0.00001) and another in the third trimester (median 21 U/mL, p<0.001), in comparison to the second trimester (median 14.0 U/mL). However, the current case revealed a serum CA-125 concentration of 41.8 U/mL, which exceeds the median for the second trimester.

CA-125 is predominantly utilized as a tumor marker for ovarian cancer (current standard cut-off: ≥35 U/mL), particularly in women over 50 years of age, as its diagnostic metrics—including area under the curve, positive predictive value, sensitivity, and specificity—are higher in this demographic. Nevertheless, elevated CA-125 levels are also associated with several benign conditions such as endometriosis and adenomyosis (>19 U/mL), pelvic inflammatory disease, menstruation, as well as early and late-stage pregnancy (median values of 23 and 21 U/mL, respectively), an increased risk of miscarriage, and myoma (cut-off <19 U/mL).57

The clinical decision-making process was primarily influenced by several factors: the identification of large, vascularized masses exhibiting both solid and cystic characteristics and carrying a substantial risk of complications such as rupture, torsion, or infection; the patient’s worsening symptoms, including increased pain and dyspnoea; the potential for continued tumor growth, which could lead to further complications like hypoxia, necrosis, and tissue degeneration; mildly elevated oncological markers (CA-125=41.8 U/mL); and limited pelvic space that could hinder normal uterine expansion.

Given the suspicion of neoplastic pathology and ambiguity regarding the masses’ etiology, an additional MRI without contrast was obtained to refine diagnostic clarity following ultrasonography. Current guidelines advise against performing gadolinium-enhanced MRI during pregnancy unless strictly required due to conditions such as rheumatologic disorders, inflammation, or infiltrative dermatoses, as well as the risk of fetal demise or neonatal mortality. Consequently, the multidisciplinary team proceeded with a non-contrast MRI, opting not to exceed 1.5-Tesla field strength, a measure generally accepted as safe beyond the first trimester.

In this particular case, a multidisciplinary team—including surgeons, gynaecologists, and obstetricians—chose to perform a longitudinal laparotomic surgical intervention after securing explicit written informed consent from the patient. During intraoperative evaluation, the superior lesion was found to possess a pedicle, creating an elevated risk for an imminent, well-recognised complication. This risk justified restricting the operation to an upper myomectomy alone. Continuous monitoring of fetal well-being was achieved through repeated transvaginal ultrasound examinations throughout the procedure, alongside the preventive intraoperative use of progesterone.

As detailed in a large-scale multicenter investigation by Zhao et al, it is imperative to assess and record fetal heart rate preoperatively, intraoperatively, and postoperatively.

Generally, surgical intervention is deferred until after delivery in favor of conservative or medical management. Nevertheless, Sakinci et al, in a recent study conducted at a tertiary care facility, observed no statistically significant differences between women who underwent caesarean section (CS) with concurrent myomectomy and those undergoing CS without myoma with regard to preoperative and postoperative haemoglobin values or the necessity for blood transfusion ($p > 0.05$). In contrast, the combined CS myomectomy group experienced significantly longer surgery times and hospital stays ($p=0.001$ and $p=0.001$, respectively). The average myoma dimension measured $8.3 \pm 4.1$ cm, and a significant positive correlation was noted between myoma size and duration of hospitalisation ($p=0.01$).

The aforementioned study identified a statistically significant negative association between myoma size and gestational age at delivery (\(p=0.035\)).

Uterine leiomyomas display a non-linear trajectory in size alterations throughout gestation and the postpartum period. Specifically, leiomyomas grow at a weekly rate of 6.90%: an increase of 6.12% (\(p<0.001\)) between the first and second trimesters, and a rise of 3.10%±0.39% between the second and third trimesters (\(p<0.05\)). Thus, the expansion rate of uterine leiomyomas is substantially greater during the initial trimester compared to later stages of pregnancy. Conversely, during the third trimester and up to 6 weeks postpartum, there is a significant reduction in myoma size, with a mean decrease of 44.25%±36.05% (\(p<0.05\)).

Collectively, these findings underscore the value of precise ultrasonographic assessment regarding the dimensions, localization, count, and proximity of uterine myomas to the placenta prior to any surgical intervention.

For uterine myomas discovered during pregnancy, conservative management is generally favored over surgery, primarily due to elevated risks such as excessive hemorrhage, pregnancy loss, preterm birth, and uterine rupture. Nevertheless, surgical myomectomy should be contemplated in the presence of complicated lesions, for instance, torsion of a pedunculated myoma, or persistent symptoms unresponsive to conservative therapy. If surgery is deemed necessary, it should be brief to maximize pregnancy preservation. A laparoscopic approach—ideally using the open Hasson technique—is preferable when the gestational age is low (first or early second trimester), the myoma has a smaller volume, and is in a location that is not intramural. This strategy improves maternal outcomes and supports the likelihood of vaginal delivery. Nonetheless, the current case highlights a prevalent selection bias, whereby laparotomy is chosen over laparoscopy in scenarios involving large, multiple, intramural, or critically situated myomas. Such situations are often associated with increased rates of cesarean section, particularly when a substantial myoma is adherent to the posterior aspect of the isthmic uterine segment, effectively impeding vaginal delivery.
