Title: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

Rosalba De Nola, Emma Bassi, Cataldo Paletta, Mario Vicino

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her 40s with no previous pregnancies reported abdominal pain and shortness of breath during her second trimester. Ultrasonographic evaluation identified two sizable abdominal masses: the superior mass encompassed the entire left subphrenic area, extending up to the uterine fundus, while the inferior mass was localized within the pouch of Douglas. The patient underwent a laparotomy, leaving the pelvic mass untouched. She proceeded to receive standard obstetric care and an elective caesarean section, achieving favorable maternal and neonatal results. When large, symptomatic masses are found in pregnancy, thorough assessment with bimanual examination and safe imaging techniques, such as ultrasound and non-contrast MRI, is critical. Surgery should be reserved for situations involving oncological suspicion or clinical complications, with the primary objective of maintaining pregnancy and preventing unnecessary interventions. The choice of delivery should take into account the size, quantity, and location of uterine masses.

Background:

Diagnosing and treating atypical or symptomatic abdominal masses in pregnancy is associated with considerable clinical difficulties. Available diagnostic methods include physical examination, ultrasound (US) with colour Doppler, and non-contrast magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Preoperative US is instrumental in detecting symptomatic uterine leiomyomas situated on the anterior surface of the pregnant uterus, regardless of their submucosal or pedunculated nature. In comparison, MRI offers enhanced diagnostic detail, particularly for numerous, sizable, intramural, or posterior leiomyomas, enabling accurate measurement of the separation between the leiomyoma and the uterine cavity.

The use of serum tumour markers for diagnostic purposes during pregnancy is still controversial. Specifically, CA-125 concentrations tend to increase during the early pregnancy and return to normal later. As such, single values are not diagnostic, while serial assessment may reveal patterns helpful for differential diagnosis and ongoing surveillance. Appropriate management calls for thorough multidisciplinary evaluation of risks to carefully balance obstetric considerations against the unusual and intricate nature of these cases.

Case presentation:

An overweight female in her forties, with a BMI of 33 and obstetric history G1P0A1, presented during her second trimester at 21 weeks gestation, reporting severe and abrupt abdominal pain localized to the upper left quadrant and pelvis (both rated 5/10 on the Visual Analogue Scale), along with mild dyspnoea. Routine first and second trimester screenings indicated low probability for aneuploidies (confirmed by non-invasive prenatal testing), and normal fetal structural assessment.

She was initially evaluated in the emergency department by a multidisciplinary team. Oxygen saturation was stable (median 97%). Examination of her thorax showed a heart rate averaging 74 bpm, and blood pressure of 100/60 mm Hg. ECG findings were within normal limits. Following this, she was moved to the Obstetrics and Gynecology ward. Her abdomen appeared excessively enlarged relative to gestational age, with a tender, taut, and elastic mass occupying the entire upper left quadrant, measuring 18 cm, and no clinical evidence of ascites.

On bimanual vaginal assessment, a fixed, rounded, hard mass measuring approximately 9 cm was identified in the lesser pelvis, with partial protrusion into the posterior fornix.

Analgesic treatment consisted of intravenous paracetamol at 1000 mg twice daily and maintenance in a semi-orthostatic position, presumed to alleviate diaphragmatic compression by the gravid uterus and sizable abdominal masses; this approach yielded mild symptom improvement, as pain levels dropped from 5 to 1 on the Visual Analogue Scale, without supplemental oxygen or ventilatory support.

During her hospitalization, the patient developed a low-grade fever. PCR testing for SARS-CoV-2 via nasopharyngeal swab was negative. However, urine analysis identified infection with Klebsiella pneumoniae. Laboratory studies revealed iron deficiency anemia, with haemoglobin at 9.9 g/L (normal range 12.0–16.6 g/L) and ferritin at 25 mcg/L (normal range 15–300 mcg/L), as well as a modest elevation in C-reactive protein (1.39 mg/dL, reference 0–0.3 mg/dL). A leukocyte count of 11.29×10^3/uL (normal 4–10×10^3/uL) and neutrophil count at 9.27×10^3/uL (normal 2.8–5.5×10^3/uL) were also observed, which is consistent with physiological alterations associated with pregnancy.

Investigations:

Ultrasonography revealed two sizable, single-chamber cystic lesions with smooth borders and vascular flow at the periphery. The superior lesion measured 18×12×7 cm and extended from the left subphrenic area down to the uterine fundus. The inferior lesion was 9.5×8×8 cm in size and appeared to be attached to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma displaying cystic degeneration (58×71 mm).

Tumor marker levels (CA 19–9, CA 15–3, CEA, β2-microglobulin, and LDH) were within reference ranges, while CA-125 was elevated at 41.8 U/mL (normal <35 U/mL), demonstrating a rising trend from 36 U/mL two weeks prior. Thus, a slight upward trend in this serum marker concentration was observed. Consequently, the patient was evaluated with a non-contrast MRI, which revealed thickened walls (1.4 cm), inhomogeneous fluid on T1 and T2 fat-saturated sequences, and focal hyperintense solid regions on T1-T2 weighted images (refer to figure 1; only T2 fat-saturated images shown). The borders of the caudal lesion were indistinct, appearing contiguous with the posterior leiomyoma previously assessed by ultrasound. The MRI did not detect any lymphadenopathy.

Differential diagnosis:

During the ultrasound examination, the cystic lesions appeared similar to two large decidualised endometriomas, with the inferior cyst noted as being contiguous with a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine leiomyoma measuring 58×71 mm.

Magnetic resonance imaging identified two well-defined, unilocular adnexal cysts exhibiting enlarged vessels and heterogeneous fluid (refer to figure 1); the inferior lesion was closely associated with a posterior uterine intramural-subserosal myoma that exhibited cystic degeneration and measured 58×71 mm.

The considerable size of both the gravid uterus and the masses precluded the visualization of any connecting stalk or tumor-supplying vessels for the superior lesion, thus supporting a presumptive diagnosis of ovarian masses—predominantly endometriomas—given the mild CA-125 increase observed during the second trimester (see discussion).

Interpretation of sonographic and MRI findings led to consideration of decidualised endometriomas and cystic degenerated leiomyomas as potential diagnoses: the marginally elevated CA-125 lent support for endometrioma in the superior cyst, while the continuity with uterine tissue suggested a leiomyomatous etiology for the inferior mass. The patient's shortness of breath was considered to result from mechanical compression caused by the large abdominal lesions, with no signs of cardiac or pulmonary compromise detected.

Treatment:

Given the progression of symptoms—including abdominal pain associated with mass effect and dyspnoea secondary to restrictive lung disease caused by the size of the abdominal masses—as well as the need for definitive diagnosis, concern for potential complications such as rupture, torsion, or infection, and the likelihood of further uterine expansion concurrent with fetal development, the patient underwent a laparotomy utilizing a longitudinal xiphoid-to-pubic incision at 21 weeks and 6 days gestation following informed consent.

Initially, two samples of peritoneal fluid were taken for both culture and cytological assessment. The surgical team then inspected the abdomen, confirming ongoing fetal cardiac activity.

Unexpectedly, the superior mass was identified as a sizable, pedunculated, atypical cystic leiomyoma with taut-elastic consistency attached to the uterus, featuring marked vascularity and evidence of partial twisting and leakage; this likely accounted for the febrile episodes experienced during hospitalization (refer to figure 2). Consequently, the decision was made to delicately remove the mass from its uterine site utilizing a double clamp technique (see figure 2). After transecting the pedicle, the exposed base was meticulously sutured with an absorbable stitch. The inferior mass, which originated from the uterine torus, remained in place to reduce obstetric risk; it was observed to be immobile, with a smooth exterior and a soft consistency, consistent with a large intramural-subserosal cystic leiomyoma.

The procedure lasted 2 hours and 25 minutes. Importantly, progesterone prophylaxis was initiated intraoperatively with 100 mg administered intramuscularly, continued as 100 mg IM daily until the patient’s discharge. Subsequently, she was prescribed intramuscular hydroxyprogesterone caproate (341 mg, 1 vial IM every three days, as indicated by the package insert) until scheduled delivery. Perioperative antimicrobial prophylaxis was provided with intravenous cefazolin 2 g, followed by postoperative cefazolin 1 g IV three times daily combined with clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily throughout the hospital admission, based on antibiogram results from urine cultures (Klebsiella pneumoniae highly susceptible to cephalosporins and clarithromycin).

Outcome and follow-up:

Following the operation, the patient's anemia worsened (Hb 7.5 g/L, HCT 24.3%), and iron deficiency remained, necessitating intravenous iron administration (ferric carboxymaltose 1 g) along with two successful blood transfusions (Hb 10.0 g/L, HCT 30.4%). Ten days post-surgery, the patient was discharged with no complications and underwent close obstetric follow-up every 3 weeks. Serial assessments indicated fetal growth (86th percentile per WHO fetal growth chart calculator, available online at https://srhr.org/fetalgrowthcalculator/#/), as well as monitoring of the amniotic fluid index.

Peritoneal fluid analysis yielded negative results for both cultural and cytological evaluations. Histopathological findings demonstrated benign uterine leiomyomas with areas of ischemic necrosis adjacent to the twisted pedicle.

The location of the caudal myoma would have significantly impeded the descent of the fetal head during the expulsive phase of labor. Consequently, an elective cesarean section was undertaken along the previous surgical scar at 37 weeks’ gestation, reducing the likelihood of uterine scar rupture and resulting in an optimal neonatal outcome.

Presently, the patient is asymptomatic regarding her myoma, although the size of the residual pelvic myoma has moderately decreased to 7.5 cm. She is breastfeeding effectively and has opted not to schedule further surgical intervention, citing partial postpartum reduction of the remaining leiomyomas, lack of symptoms, and completion of her reproductive goals. The infant’s development remains favorable.

Discussion:

Myomas can often be misdiagnosed as adnexal masses: the incidence during pregnancy is 0.3–10.1% and 1–6%, respectively .13

In this case, the initial assessment identified abdominal masses, interpreted as two sizable decidualised endometriomas. However, the lower solid part of the pelvic mass was suggestive of a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma with cystic degeneration measuring 58×71 mm, as visualized on both ultrasound and MRI. Given the lack of established management protocols for pelvic masses during pregnancy, the multidisciplinary team determined that planned surgical intervention might reduce the risk of preterm birth.

Typically, during gestation, serum CA-125 levels display two peaks: one during the first trimester (median 23 U/mL, p<0.00001) and another in the third trimester (median 21 U/mL, p<0.001), compared to values in the second trimester (median 14.0 U/mL). By contrast, the CA-125 level in this patient was 41.8 U/mL, which exceeds the median for the second trimester.

CA-125 is predominantly utilized as a tumor marker for ovarian carcinoma (generally using a threshold of ≥35 U/mL), particularly in women older than 50 years, for whom the test demonstrates better discriminatory power, positive predictive value, sensitivity, and specificity. However, CA-125 may also be elevated in various benign disorders, such as endometriosis and adenomyosis (>19 U/mL), pelvic inflammatory disease, menstruation, the first and third trimesters of pregnancy (median values of 23 and 21 U/mL, respectively), increased miscarriage risk, as well as myoma (cut-off <19 U/mL).57

The primary factors guiding the clinical management included the identification of large, highly vascularized masses displaying both solid and cystic features, posing immediate risks such as rupture, torsion, or infection. Additional considerations comprised the patient’s deteriorating symptoms—specifically increasing pain and dyspnea—the potential for continued mass expansion resulting in elevated likelihood of hypoxia, necrosis, and degeneration, mild elevation in tumor markers (CA-125=41.8 U/mL), and the limited intra-abdominal space required for normal uterine growth.

Given the possible neoplastic nature and unclear etiology of the masses, an MRI without contrast was performed after the initial ultrasound to enhance diagnostic precision. Current guidelines advise against gadolinium-enhanced MRI during pregnancy except under imperative circumstances, such as in cases involving rheumatological, inflammatory, or infiltrative skin diseases, or significant risks of stillbirth or neonatal mortality. Consequently, the multidisciplinary team proceeded with a non-contrast MRI, ensuring the use of magnetic fields not exceeding 1.5-T, which are considered appropriately safe after the first trimester.

In this case, the multidisciplinary team—including surgeons, gynaecologists, and obstetricians—chose to undertake longitudinal laparotomic surgery after securing explicit written informed consent from the patient. During intraoperative evaluation, the superior mass was found to have a pedicle, thus posing a considerable risk for an acute, characteristic complication. This observation underpinned the decision to restrict the procedure to an upper myomectomy. Additionally, ongoing transvaginal ultrasound examinations were employed at intervals to verify fetal viability throughout the surgery, complemented by intraoperative prophylactic progesterone administration.

A multicentric study by Zhao et al underscores the necessity of recording and monitoring the fetal heart rate before, during, and after surgical intervention.

Typically, surgical treatment is postponed until after delivery, favoring medical management or an expectant strategy. Nevertheless, Sakinci et al, in a recent study at a tertiary institution, reported no statistically significant differences between women undergoing caesarean section (CS) combined with myomectomy and those without myomas who underwent only CS, with regard to preoperative and postoperative haemoglobin levels or the incidence of blood transfusions (p >0.05). However, patients receiving CS with myomectomy experienced notably longer hospital stays and operative times (p = 0.001 and p = 0.001, respectively). The average myoma size was 8.3 ± 4.1 cm, with a significant positive correlation observed between myoma size and length of hospitalisation (p = 0.01).

The same study identified a statistically significant inverse relationship between myoma size and gestational age at delivery (p = 0.035).

Uterine leiomyomas exhibit a non-linear pattern of size variation throughout pregnancy and the postpartum period. Specifically, leiomyomas enlarge by 6.90% per week, with a 6.12% increase (p<0.001) noted between the first and second trimesters, and a 3.10%±0.39% increase observed between the second and third trimesters (p<0.05). Thus, leiomyomas grow at a significantly greater rate during the first trimester compared to later stages of pregnancy. Conversely, the size of myomas in the third trimester and within 6 weeks postpartum declines substantially (mean reduction of 44.25%±36.05%, p<0.05).

These articles highlight the critical need for thorough ultrasonographic assessment of myoma size, position, quantity, and proximity to the placenta prior to surgical intervention.

For uterine myomas identified during pregnancy, management is typically conservative rather than surgical due to risks including significant hemorrhage, miscarriage, preterm birth, and uterine rupture. Nonetheless, myomectomy may be warranted for complicated cases involving pedunculated myomas with torsion or persistent symptoms refractory to conservative measures. The surgical procedure should be minimized in duration to safeguard the pregnancy and can also be performed laparoscopically, ideally utilizing an open Hasson entry technique for patients in the first or early second trimester, with smaller masses, or non-intramural myoma sites. This method offers improved maternal outcomes and increases the likelihood of vaginal delivery. Still, this particular case demonstrates the frequent selection bias favoring laparotomy over laparoscopic management in the presence of large, multiple, intramural, or critically positioned myomas, which is associated with an elevated risk of cesarean section, especially when a substantial myoma is adherent to the posterior wall of the uterine isthmus and could impede vaginal birth.
