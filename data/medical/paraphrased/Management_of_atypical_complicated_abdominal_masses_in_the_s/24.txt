Title: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

Rosalba De Nola, Emma Bassi, Cataldo Paletta, Mario Vicino

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her 40s with no history of childbirth presented in her second trimester with abdominal discomfort and shortness of breath. Ultrasonographic assessment identified two substantial abdominal masses: the upper mass occupied the entire left subphrenic area and extended to the fundus of the uterus, while the lower mass was located within the pouch of Douglas. Laparotomy was performed, leaving the pelvic mass intact. The patient was subsequently monitored routinely through obstetric visits and underwent an elective caesarean section, which yielded favorable outcomes for both mother and newborn. For large, symptomatic masses encountered during pregnancy, thorough assessment through bimanual examination and appropriate, safe imaging modalities—including ultrasound and non-contrast MRI—is critical. Surgical intervention should be reserved for cases with suspected malignancy or clinical complications, with the primary goal of maintaining the pregnancy and minimizing unnecessary procedures. Decisions regarding delivery should be tailored according to the mass's size, quantity, and anatomical distribution.

Background:

The identification and treatment of unusual or symptomatic abdominal masses encountered during pregnancy pose considerable clinical difficulties. Available diagnostic methods include physical examination, ultrasound (US) with colour Doppler, and non-contrast MRI. Preoperative US is especially useful for detecting symptomatic uterine leiomyomas situated on the anterior wall of the pregnant uterus, including submucosal and pedunculated types. In cases with multiple, sizable, intramural, or posterior leiomyomas, MRI proves more effective, as it accurately delineates the spatial relationship between the myoma and the uterine cavity.

The usefulness of serum tumour markers in pregnancy is still contentious. For example, CA-125 levels naturally increase during the first trimester before returning to baseline, making a single value non-diagnostic. Serial measurements may reveal important trends, aiding differential diagnosis and ongoing monitoring. Management requires coordinated multidisciplinary evaluation to appropriately weigh the obstetric risks against the infrequency and complexity inherent to these situations.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 40s with obesity (body mass index 33), G1P0A1, presented to our facility at 21 weeks gestation with intense and acute abdominal pain in the upper left quadrant and pelvis (Visual Analogue Scale score of 5 at each location), accompanied by mild shortness of breath. Routine screening in both the first and second trimesters showed no increased risk for aneuploidy (confirmed by non-invasive prenatal testing) and normal fetal morphology.

She was first evaluated in the emergency department by a multidisciplinary team. Oxygen saturation was within normal limits (median 97%). Examination of the thorax indicated regular heart rate (median 74 bpm) and blood pressure (median 100/60 mm Hg). ECG findings were normal. She was then sent to the Obstetrical and Gynecological Division. Abdominal examination revealed that the abdomen appeared larger than expected for her gestational stage, with a sensitive swelling and a tense, elastic mass filling the entire upper left quadrant, measuring 18 cm, absent any clinical evidence of ascites.

On bimanual vaginal exam, a fixed, round, firm mass of approximately 9 cm was found in the lesser pelvis, partially protruding into the posterior fornix.

Treatment with intravenous paracetamol at 1000 mg twice daily, together with maintaining a semi-orthostatic posture—presumed to alleviate diaphragmatic pressure from the gravid uterus and sizable abdominal masses—led to slight improvement of symptoms without need for supplemental oxygen or ventilation, decreasing pain scores from 5 to 1 on the Visual Analogue Scale.

While admitted, the patient developed a mild fever. Testing included a nasopharyngeal swab for severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 polymerase chain reaction (PCR), which was negative; however, urine samples revealed infection with Klebsiella pneumoniae. Blood work demonstrated iron deficiency anemia with hemoglobin (Hb) of 9.9 g/L (reference range 12.0–16.6 g/L) and serum ferritin at 25 mcg/L (reference range 15–300 mcg/L); C-reactive protein was modestly elevated at 1.39 mg/dL (reference range 0–0.3 mg/dL), and white blood cell count was elevated at 11.29×10^3/uL (reference range 4–10×10^3/uL) with neutrophils at 9.27×10^3/uL (reference range 2.8–5.5×10^3/uL), in line with pregnancy-associated changes.

Investigations:

Ultrasound revealed two sizeable, unilocular cystic lesions with smooth edges and blood flow along their periphery. The superior mass measured 18×12×7 cm and spanned from the left subphrenic area down to the uterine fundus. The inferior mass was 9.5×8×8 cm in size and appeared attached to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma showing cystic degeneration (measuring 58×71 mm).

Serological evaluation of tumour markers including CA 19–9, CA 15–3, CEA, β2-microglobulin, and LDH found values within reference ranges. However, CA-125 was raised at 41.8 U/mL (reference <35 U/mL), demonstrating an upward trajectory compared to the previous measurement of 36 U/mL two weeks prior. Consequently, a non-contrast MRI was performed, which detected thickened cyst walls (1.4 cm), inhomogeneous fluid with T1 and T2 fat-sat signal characteristics, and solid areas exhibiting focal hyperintensity on T1-T2 weighted sequences (refer to figure 1; only T2 fat-sat sequences shown). The boundary of the caudal mass was indistinct, with apparent continuity into the posterior leiomyoma identified previously on ultrasound. The MRI did not reveal any lymphadenopathy.

Differential diagnosis:

During ultrasonographic assessment, the cystic lesions resembled two large decidualised endometriomas. The lower cyst was noted to be attached to an intramural-subserosal fibroid on the posterior wall of the uterus, measuring 58×71 mm.

MRI findings revealed two rounded, unilocular adnexal cysts with prominent vessels and heterogeneous fluid (refer to figure 1). The inferior mass appeared adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration, also measuring 58×71 mm.

The substantial size of the pregnant uterus and the large masses precluded visualization of any connecting stalk or tumor-feeding vessel for the superior mass, thereby steering the diagnosis toward ovarian lesions, particularly endometriomas, as supported by a mildly increased CA-125 in the second trimester (see discussion).

Sonographic and MRI findings led to a differential diagnosis of decidualised endometriomas versus cystically degenerating leiomyomas. The mild rise in CA-125 was supportive of an endometrioma for the upper cyst; however, the lower mass’s continuity with uterine tissue implied a leiomyomatous etiology. Dyspnea was considered secondary to mechanical compression from the extensive abdominal masses, with no signs of cardiac or pulmonary impairment.

Treatment:

Owing to deterioration in clinical status—specifically, abdominal pain caused by the masses and dyspnoea from restrictive lung disease related to their size—as well as the need for a definitive diagnosis, concerns about potential complications (including rupture, torsion, and infection), and anticipated uterine enlargement with fetal development, a laparotomy with a longitudinal xiphoid-to-pubic incision was performed at 21 weeks and 6 days following informed consent.

Initial intraoperative steps included obtaining two samples of peritoneal fluid for both culture and cytological analysis. Subsequent examination of the abdominal cavity confirmed ongoing fetal cardiac activity.

Unexpectedly, the superior (cranial) mass was identified as a large, pedunculated, atypical cystic, and tense-elastic uterine leiomyoma with significant vascularity. The lesion was found twisted and leaking, which likely explained the fever during hospitalization (refer to figure 2). Accordingly, the surgical team proceeded with gentle excision of the mass at its uterine origin, utilizing a double-clamp technique (see figure 2). After division of the pedicle, the base was meticulously ligated with a single absorbable suture. The inferior (caudal) mass, which arose from the uterine torus, was left untouched to reduce obstetric risk. This lesion was immobile, displayed a smooth surface, and had a soft consistency resembling a large cystic intramural-subserosal leiomyoma.

The procedure lasted 2 hours and 25 minutes. Of note, prophylactic progesterone (100 mg intramuscular) was administered intraoperatively, then continued daily at the same dose until discharge. Thereafter, intramuscular hydroxyprogesterone caproate (341 mg per vial every 3 days, as described in the package insert) was given up to elective delivery. Intraoperative antibiotic prophylaxis was provided with cefazolin 2 g intravenously, and this was followed postoperatively with cefazolin 1 g intravenously three times daily in combination with clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily during hospitalization, guided by the urine culture antibiogram (Klebsiella pneumoniae highly sensitive to cephalosporins and clarithromycin).

Outcome and follow-up:

Following surgery, the patient's anaemia worsened (Hb 7.5 g/L, HCT 24.3%) and iron deficiency persisted, necessitating intravenous iron therapy with ferric carboxymaltose 1 g, as well as two successful blood transfusions (resulting in Hb 10.0 g/L, HCT 30.4%). Ten days postoperatively, the patient was discharged without complications and placed under close obstetric surveillance every 3 weeks. These follow-up assessments demonstrated fetal growth at the 86th percentile (according to the WHO fetal growth chart calculator available at https://srhr.org/fetalgrowthcalculator/#/) and maintained a satisfactory amniotic fluid index.

Analysis of the peritoneal fluid revealed negative results for both microbiological and cytological testing. Histopathological evaluation identified benign uterine leiomyomas with ischaemic necrosis adjacent to the twisted pedicle.

The location of the caudal myoma would have significantly impeded the passage of the fetal head during the expulsion phase of labour. Consequently, an elective caesarean section was performed at 37 weeks of gestation, utilising the same surgical incision to decrease the likelihood of uterine scar rupture, leading to an excellent neonatal outcome.

Currently, the patient does not experience any symptoms attributable to the myoma, although the remaining pelvic myoma has decreased in size to 7.5 cm. She continues to breastfeed without difficulty and elects to defer any further surgical intervention, given the partial postpartum regression of the residual leiomyomas, absence of symptoms, and having achieved her motherhood goals. The infant is developing appropriately.

Discussion:

Myomas can often be misdiagnosed as adnexal masses: the incidence during pregnancy is 0.3–10.1% and 1–6%, respectively .13

In this case, initial assessment revealed abdominal masses resembling two large decidualized endometriomas, except for the caudal solid portion of the pelvic mass, which mimicked a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma with cystic degeneration measuring 58×71 mm on both ultrasound and MRI. Given the lack of established protocols for the management of pelvic masses during pregnancy, the multidisciplinary team determined that planned surgical intervention might lower the likelihood of preterm birth.

Typically, during gestation, serum CA-125 concentrations exhibit two peaks: one in the first trimester (median 23 U/mL, p<0.00001) and another in the third trimester (median 21 U/mL, p<0.001), in contrast to second trimester levels (median 14.0 U/mL). However, in this patient, the CA-125 level was 41.8 U/mL, exceeding the median value noted during the second trimester.

CA-125 is primarily employed as a tumor marker for ovarian cancer, with a standard threshold of ≥35 U/mL, and it has improved diagnostic accuracy (greater area under the curve, positive predictive value, sensitivity, and specificity) in women over 50 years of age. Nevertheless, elevated CA-125 levels can also be seen in various benign conditions, such as endometriosis and adenomyosis (>19 U/mL), pelvic inflammatory disease, menstruation, early and late pregnancy (median 23 and 21 U/mL, respectively), increased miscarriage risk, as well as in the presence of myomas (cut-off <19 U/mL).57

The main factors influencing clinical management included the existence of large, highly vascularized masses displaying both solid and cystic components, posing immediate risks such as rupture, torsion, or infection. Additionally, the patient was experiencing worsening pain and dyspnoea, and there was concern that further enlargement of the masses could lead to increased risk of hypoxia, necrosis, and degeneration. Slight elevation in tumor markers (CA-125 at 41.8 U/mL) was noted, and the size of the masses was limiting the capacity for normal uterine expansion.

Given the uncertain nature and possible neoplastic origin of the lesions, an MRI without contrast was performed for more detailed evaluation following ultrasound imaging. Current guidelines advise against gadolinium-enhanced MRI during pregnancy except in cases of severe rheumatological, inflammatory, or infiltrative dermatological conditions, or risk of stillbirth or neonatal death. Thus, the multidisciplinary team proceeded with a non-contrast MRI, limiting magnetic field strength to under 1.5-T, which is regarded as acceptably safe after the first trimester.

In this case, the multidisciplinary team—including surgeons, gynecologists, and obstetricians—chose to perform a longitudinal laparotomic procedure after securing specific written informed consent from the patient. Intraoperatively, the superior lesion demonstrated a pedicle, leading to a considerable risk of acute, classical complications; thus, the intervention was restricted to an upper myomectomy. Continuous and intermittent transvaginal ultrasound examinations were utilized during surgery to assess fetal well-being, with intraoperative progesterone administered prophylactically.

Zhao et al, in a major multicenter investigation, underscore the necessity to evaluate and record fetal heart rate preoperatively, intraoperatively, and postoperatively.

Surgical intervention is generally deferred until postpartum, favoring medical management and observation. Nevertheless, Sakinci et al recently reported in a tertiary care setting that there were no statistically meaningful differences in pre- and postoperative hemoglobin levels or transfusion requirements (p >0.05) between women undergoing cesarean section (CS) with myomectomy and those having CS without myoma. However, those receiving CS plus myomectomy experienced notably longer operative times and hospital stays (p =0.001 for both outcomes). The average myoma diameter was 8.3 ± 4.1 cm, and a significant positive association was noted between myoma size and length of hospitalization (p =0.01).

The same study demonstrated a statistically significant inverse relationship between myoma size and gestational week at delivery (p = 0.035).

Uterine leiomyomas exhibit a non-linear pattern of size variation during pregnancy and the postpartum period. Specifically, leiomyoma size expands by 6.90% per week: 6.12% (p<0.001) between the first and second trimesters, and 3.10%±0.39% between the second and third trimesters (p<0.05). Consequently, the rate of growth is markedly greater in the first trimester compared to later stages of pregnancy. In contrast, leiomyoma size decreased significantly during the third trimester and at 6 weeks postpartum (mean reduction 44.25%±36.05%, p<0.05).

These studies highlight the necessity for thorough ultrasonographic evaluation of uterine myomas—including their size, position, quantity, and proximity to the placenta—prior to any surgical intervention.

When uterine myomas are identified during pregnancy, conservative management is generally favored over surgical procedures because of the risk for serious hemorrhage, pregnancy loss, premature labor, and uterine rupture. Nevertheless, myomectomy may be warranted in cases involving complicated masses such as twisted pedunculated myomas or when symptoms persist despite conservative therapy. Surgery should be kept brief to maintain the pregnancy, and laparoscopic myomectomy is feasible, preferably utilizing the open Hasson method in early pregnancy (first and early second trimester), when the mass is smaller and the myoma is located away from the intramural region. This technique provides improved maternal morbidity outcomes and supports vaginal delivery. Despite these recommendations, the current case illustrates a common selection bias favoring laparotomy over laparoscopy for larger, multiple, intramural, or critically positioned myomas, which may be associated with an increased rate of cesarean section, particularly when a large myoma adheres to the posterior isthmic region of the uterus and constitutes an obstruction to vaginal birth.
