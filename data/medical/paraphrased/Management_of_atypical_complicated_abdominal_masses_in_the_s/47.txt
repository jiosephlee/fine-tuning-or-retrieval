Title: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

Rosalba De Nola, Emma Bassi, Cataldo Paletta, Mario Vicino

Abstract:

A woman in her 40s, nulliparous, presented during her second trimester with abdominal pain and shortness of breath. Ultrasonographic assessment identified two sizable intra-abdominal masses: the superior mass extended throughout the entire left subphrenic region, reaching up to the uterine fundus, while the inferior mass occupied the pouch of Douglas. The patient underwent laparotomy, during which the pelvic mass was retained and not excised. She continued with routine prenatal monitoring and subsequently underwent an elective cesarean section, resulting in favorable maternal and neonatal outcomes. For pregnant patients with large, symptomatic masses, thorough evaluation including bimanual examination and safe imaging techniques—such as ultrasonography and non-contrast magnetic resonance imaging—is crucial. Surgical intervention is recommended only when there is suspicion of malignancy or the occurrence of clinical complications, prioritizing the maintenance of pregnancy and avoiding unnecessary procedures. The chosen method of delivery should be guided by the masses' size, quantity, and anatomical placement.

Background:

Managing and diagnosing atypical or symptomatic abdominal masses in pregnant patients poses considerable clinical difficulties. Available diagnostic tools are limited to physical examination, ultrasound (US) with color Doppler imaging, and non-contrast MRI. Preoperative US is especially useful for detecting symptomatic uterine leiomyomas positioned on the anterior aspect of the pregnant uterus, whether submucosal or pedunculated. MRI provides greater diagnostic precision for multiple, large, intramural, or posteriorly located leiomyomas, as it can accurately assess the distance between the lesion and the uterine cavity.

The utility of serum tumor markers in pregnancy remains contentious. For example, CA-125 levels physiologically increase during the first trimester and later return to baseline; thus, single values lack diagnostic significance, while serial measurements may yield helpful information for differential diagnosis and ongoing surveillance. Management should involve comprehensive, multidisciplinary risk evaluation to appropriately weigh obstetric risks against the uncommon and complex nature of these presentations.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 40s with obesity (BMI 33), gravida 1 para 0 abortus 1, presented to our facility at 21 weeks gestation for severe, sudden onset abdominal pain affecting the upper left quadrant and pelvic region (Visual Analogue Scale score of 5 at each location), accompanied by mild shortness of breath. Routine prenatal screens in the first and second trimesters had indicated a low likelihood of aneuploidy (also supported by non-invasive prenatal testing), and fetal anatomy was normal.

Initially, she was evaluated in the emergency department by a multidisciplinary team: her oxygen saturation was within normal limits (median 97%). Physical examination revealed normal thoracic findings, heart rate of 74 beats per minute (median), and blood pressure of 100/60 mm Hg (median). Her electrocardiogram was unremarkable. Subsequently, she was transferred to the Obstetrics and Gynecology Department. Her abdominal distension was greater than expected for her gestational age, with a sensitive, taut-elastic swelling throughout the upper left quadrant measuring 18 cm, and no clinical evidence of ascites.

Bimanual vaginal assessment revealed a fixed mass in the lesser pelvis, roughly 9 cm in size, firm and rounded, partially protruding into the posterior fornix.

Pain management consisted of paracetamol 1000 mg administered intravenously twice daily, along with a semi-orthostatic position intended to decrease diaphragmatic pressure from the gravid uterus and large abdominal masses; these measures led to modest symptom improvement, lowering pain scores from 5 to 1 on the Visual Analogue Scale, without a need for supplemental oxygen or ventilatory assistance.

While hospitalized, she developed a mild fever. Nasopharyngeal PCR for SARS-CoV-2 was negative, but a urinary infection with Klebsiella pneumoniae was identified. Blood testing showed iron deficiency anemia, with hemoglobin at 9.9 g/L (reference range 12.0–16.6 g/L) and serum ferritin at 25 mcg/L (reference range 15–300 mcg/L). There was a slight elevation in C-reactive protein, measuring 1.39 mg/dL (reference range 0–0.3 mg/dL), as well as an increased neutrophil count: total white blood cells were 11.29×10^3 /uL (reference range 4–10×10^3 /uL), with neutrophils at 9.27×10^3 /uL (reference range 2.8–5.5×10^3 /uL), consistent with physiological changes related to pregnancy.

Investigations:

Ultrasound assessment revealed two sizable, unilocular cystic lesions characterized by smooth borders and peripheral blood flow. The superior cyst measured 18×12×7 cm and extended from the left subphrenic area down to the uterine fundus. The inferior cyst measured 9.5×8×8 cm and appeared to be attached to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration (size: 58×71 mm).

Serum levels of tumor markers including CA 19–9, CA 15–3, CEA, β2-microglobulin, and LDH remained within reference ranges; however, CA-125 was raised at 41.8 U/mL (reference <35 U/mL), representing a mild upward trend from a value of 36 U/mL two weeks earlier. This indicated a gradual increase in this marker’s concentration. Consequently, the patient underwent magnetic resonance imaging without contrast. The MRI revealed thickened walls (1.4 cm), heterogeneous fluid signals on T1 and T2 fat-saturated sequences, and discrete, hyperintense solid portions on T1-T2 weighted images (see figure 1; only T2 fat-sat views depicted). The separation of the inferior lesion was unclear, with apparent continuity between this cyst and the previously identified posterior leiomyoma. No lymphadenopathy was detected on MRI.

Differential diagnosis:

During the ultrasound assessment, the cystic lesions resembled two large decidualised endometriomas; the more caudal cyst appeared to be attached to a posterior intramural-subserosal leiomyoma of the uterus measuring 58×71 mm.

Magnetic resonance imaging revealed two rounded, unilocular adnexal cystic lesions with enlarged vessels and heterogeneous fluid within them (refer to figure 1). The caudal lesion appeared contiguous with a posterior uterine intramural-subserosal myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration, also measuring 58×71 mm.

Owing to the volumetric effect of the gravid uterus and the size of the masses, a connecting stalk or nutrient vessel for the upper lesion was not visualized. This supported a presumptive diagnosis of ovarian masses, particularly endometriomas, corroborated by a mild increase in CA-125 during the second trimester (refer to discussion).

Evaluation of both ultrasound and MRI findings prompted consideration of decidualised endometriomas and cystic degenerating leiomyomas in the differential diagnosis: the upper cyst’s slight CA-125 elevation favored an endometrioma, whereas its connection to the uterine wall suggested a myomatous process for the caudal cyst. The patient’s dyspnoea was attributed to mechanical compression exerted by the large abdominal lesions, as there was no evidence of cardiac or pulmonary compromise.

Treatment:

Given the deterioration of the patient's clinical status—manifested by pain attributed to the mass effect in the abdomen and dyspnoea from restrictive lung disease secondary to the bulk of the abdominal masses—as well as the need for a definitive diagnosis, risk of potential complications (including rupture, torsion, and infection), and anticipated uterine growth as pregnancy progressed, the patient underwent a laparotomy with a longitudinal incision extending from the xiphoid process to the pubic symphysis at 21 weeks and 6 days after informed consent was obtained.

Initially, two samples of freely circulating peritoneal fluid were collected for culture and cytological evaluation. The surgical team then proceeded to examine the abdominal cavity, and confirmed the presence of fetal cardiac activity.

Unexpectedly, the superior mass was identified as a large, pedunculated, atypical cystic and tense-elastic leiomyoma of the uterus, demonstrating significant vascularity. It was found to be twisted and leaking, which may explain the patient’s fever during hospitalization (see figure 2). Consequently, the team chose to resect the mass from its uterine attachment carefully using double clamps (see figure 2). Following transection of the stalk, the exposed base was meticulously closed with a single absorbable suture. The inferior mass, arising from the uterine torus, was left undisturbed to reduce obstetric risks. This lesion was immobile, exhibited a smooth surface, and felt soft, consistent with a sizable cystic intramural-subserosal uterine leiomyoma.

The operative procedure took 2 hours and 25 minutes. Of note, progesterone prophylaxis was administered: 100 mg intramuscularly was initiated intraoperatively and continued daily until discharge. Subsequently, hydroxyprogesterone caproate was prescribed at a dosage of 341 mg (1 fl) intramuscularly every three days, as specified by the manufacturer's instructions, and maintained until the planned delivery. For antibiotic prophylaxis, 2 g cefazolin intravenously was given intraoperatively, followed by postoperative therapy consisting of cefazolin 1 g intravenously three times daily plus clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily during the hospital stay, consistent with the antibiogram from urine culture showing Klebsiella pneumoniae highly susceptible to cephalosporins and clarithromycin.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following the operation, the patient developed worsening anemia (Hb 7.5 g/L, HCT 24.3%) alongside continued iron deficiency, prompting administration of intravenous iron (ferric carboxymaltose 1 g) and two units of blood transfusion, which proved successful (Hb 10.0 g/L, HCT 30.4%). She was discharged 10 days postoperatively without any complications, and commenced close obstetric monitoring every three weeks. This follow-up demonstrated fetal growth at the 86th percentile based on the WHO fetal growth chart calculator (https://srhr.org/fetalgrowthcalculator/#/), as well as assessment of the amniotic fluid index.

Testing of the peritoneal fluid yielded negative results for both cultures and cytology. Pathological evaluation confirmed benign uterine leiomyomas, with ischemic necrosis noted near the twisted pedicle.

The caudal myoma would have posed a considerable barrier to fetal head descent during the second stage of labor. Therefore, an elective caesarean section was carried out at the previous surgical incision site once the fetus attained 37 weeks’ gestation, thus reducing the likelihood of uterine scar rupture and achieving an excellent neonatal outcome.

The patient presently reports no symptoms attributable to the myoma, though the residual pelvic myoma has decreased modestly to 7.5 cm. She is breastfeeding successfully and prefers not to schedule further surgery at this stage, given the partial postpartum reduction in the size of the remaining leiomyomas, absence of related symptoms, and satisfaction with her maternity experience. The infant is developing appropriately.

Discussion:

Myomas can often be misdiagnosed as adnexal masses: the incidence during pregnancy is 0.3–10.1% and 1–6%, respectively .13

In this instance, the initial assessment identified abdominal masses suggestive of two large decidualized endometriomas, with the exception of a caudal solid portion within the pelvic mass, which was reminiscent of a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration and measuring 58×71 mm on both ultrasonography and MRI. Given the lack of established protocols for managing pelvic masses during pregnancy, the multidisciplinary team judged that elective surgical intervention could potentially reduce the risk of preterm birth.

It is typical for serum CA-125 levels in pregnancy to display two peaks: one during the first trimester (median 23 U/mL, p<0.00001) and another in the third trimester (median 21 U/mL, p<0.001), when contrasted with second trimester levels (median 14.0 U/mL). However, in this patient, the measured CA-125 was 41.8 U/mL, surpassing the median second trimester reference value.

CA-125 is most commonly utilized as a tumor marker for ovarian cancer, with a current standard threshold of ≥35 U/mL, particularly in women over 50, in whom the test demonstrates higher area under the curve, positive predictive value, sensitivity, and specificity. Nonetheless, elevated CA-125 levels can be seen in various benign conditions, including endometriosis and adenomyosis (>19 U/mL), pelvic inflammatory disease, menstruation, early and late pregnancy (median values of 23 and 21 U/mL, respectively), threatened miscarriage, as well as with myomas (cut-off <19 U/mL).57

The primary factors guiding the clinical management included the detection of large, vascularized masses exhibiting both solid and cystic elements, which posed a tangible risk of adverse events such as rupture, torsion, or infection; the patient's deteriorating symptoms of pain and shortness of breath; the potential for further tumor enlargement with increased likelihood of hypoxia, necrosis, and degeneration; mildly elevated tumor markers (CA-125 = 41.8 U/mL); and concern that the mass could impede normal uterine expansion.

Given the indeterminate neoplastic potential and unclear etiology of the masses, an MRI without contrast was performed following the ultrasound to enhance diagnostic precision. Current guidelines advise against the use of gadolinium-enhanced MRI during pregnancy unless there is an overriding clinical need, as in certain rheumatological, inflammatory, or dermatological conditions, or in cases with risk of fetal demise. Consequently, the multidisciplinary team opted for an unenhanced MRI using a system not exceeding 1.5-T, as this is considered sufficiently safe after the first trimester.

In this case, the multidisciplinary team—including surgeons, gynecologists, and obstetricians—decided to undertake longitudinal laparotomic surgical intervention after securing formal written informed consent from the patient. During surgery, the upper lesion was noted to possess a pedicle, posing an elevated risk for a typical acute complication, which led to the decision to restrict the intervention to an upper myomectomy. Fetal viability was monitored throughout the procedure using repeated and intermittent transvaginal ultrasound assessments, accompanied by intraoperative prophylactic progesterone administration.

A large multicenter study by Zhao et al emphasized that documentation and evaluation of fetal heart rate before, during, and following the operation is required.

Typically, surgical intervention is postponed until after delivery, favoring conservative or medical management. However, Sakinci et al, in a recent study at a tertiary institution, reported no significant difference in preoperative and postoperative hemoglobin levels or blood transfusion requirements (p >0.05) between women undergoing caesarean section (CS) with myomectomy and those undergoing CS alone without myoma. Nevertheless, both hospitalization length and surgery duration were considerably increased among those receiving CS myomectomy (p =0.001 and p =0.001, respectively). The average myoma size was 8.3 ± 4.1cm, with a statistically significant positive correlation noted between myoma size and hospital stay (p =0.01).

The aforementioned study also identified a statistically significant negative association between myoma size and the gestational week at delivery (p = 0.035).

Uterine leiomyomas exhibit a non-linear pattern in size variation throughout pregnancy and the postpartum period. Specifically, leiomyomas demonstrate a weekly size increase of 6.90%, with a rate of 6.12% (p<0.001) between the first and second trimesters, and 3.10%±0.39% between the second and third trimesters (p<0.05). Consequently, the rate of growth for uterine leiomyomas is markedly greater during the first trimester compared to subsequent stages of pregnancy. Conversely, in the third trimester and up to 6 weeks postpartum, myoma size shows a substantial reduction, averaging 44.25%±36.05% (p<0.05).

These studies underscore the necessity for thorough ultrasound evaluation of myomas prior to surgical intervention, including assessment of their dimensions, location, quantity, and proximity to the placenta.

When uterine myomas are identified during pregnancy, management typically favors conservative strategies over surgical ones, given risks such as significant hemorrhage, miscarriage, preterm birth, and uterine rupture. Nonetheless, myomectomy is warranted in situations involving complicated lesions, such as twisted pedunculated myomas or persistent symptoms unresponsive to conservative measures. The procedure should be kept brief to safeguard the pregnancy, and laparoscopy—ideally utilizing the open Hasson technique—is suitable for lower gestational ages (first and early second trimester), smaller masses, and when the myoma is not intramural. This method contributes to reduced maternal morbidity and facilitates vaginal delivery. Despite this, the current case highlights a frequent bias in clinical decision-making, favoring laparotomy over laparoscopy for larger, multiple, intramural, or critical myomas. This bias is often associated with an increased rate of cesarean section, especially when a substantial myoma is adherent to the posterior isthmic region of the uterus, potentially impeding vaginal birth.
