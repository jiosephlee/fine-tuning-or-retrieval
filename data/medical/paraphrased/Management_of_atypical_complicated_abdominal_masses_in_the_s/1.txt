Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

Rosalba De Nola, Emma Bassi, Cataldo Paletta, Mario Vicino

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her 40s with no prior pregnancies presented in her second trimester experiencing abdominal discomfort and shortness of breath. Imaging via ultrasonography identified two substantial abdominal masses: the superior mass extended across the entire left subphrenic area up to the uterine fundus, while the inferior mass occupied the pouch of Douglas. The patient underwent a laparotomy, during which the more caudal mass was left undisturbed within the pelvis. She received standard obstetric care following the procedure and eventually delivered via planned caesarean section, with both mother and neonate experiencing favorable outcomes. When managing sizeable symptomatic masses during pregnancy, thorough assessment—including bimanual examination and the use of imaging modalities with established safety profiles, such as ultrasound and non-contrast MRI—is critical. Surgical intervention should be limited to scenarios involving suspicion of malignancy or acute clinical issues, prioritizing the preservation of gestation and minimizing unnecessary procedures. The determination of delivery method should be individualized, taking into account the number, dimensions, and anatomical sites of uterine masses.

Background:

The evaluation and treatment of atypical or symptomatic abdominal masses in pregnant patients is often clinically complex. Available diagnostic modalities include physical examination, ultrasound (US) complemented by colour Doppler, and non-contrast magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Preoperative US serves an important function in detecting symptomatic uterine leiomyomas on the anterior wall of the gravid uterus, regardless of whether they are submucosal or pedunculated in nature. For the assessment of multiple, sizable, intramural, or posterior leiomyomas, MRI provides greater diagnostic precision by accurately localising the myoma in relation to the uterine cavity.

The utility of serum tumour markers during pregnancy is controversial. In particular, physiological increases in CA-125 are observed during the first trimester, followed by a return to baseline. Consequently, single CA-125 measurements are not definitive for diagnosis, whereas serial monitoring may yield valuable information for differential diagnosis and ongoing assessment. Comprehensive multidisciplinary risk evaluation is essential to optimally manage these cases, balancing obstetric considerations against the infrequent and intricate nature of these presentations.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 40s, classified as obese with a body mass index of 33, gravida 1, para 0, abortus 1 (G1P0A1), presented during her second trimester (at 21 weeks’ gestation) with acute, severe abdominal pain in both the upper left quadrant and the pelvic region, each site scoring 5 on the Visual Analogue Scale, along with mild shortness of breath. Screening tests conducted in the first and second trimesters indicated low risk for aneuploidies, a finding also supported by non-invasive prenatal testing, and fetal structural assessment was unremarkable.

She was initially seen in the emergency department, where a multidisciplinary team conducted a comprehensive evaluation. Her oxygen saturation was stable (median 97%), cardiac exam revealed a regular heart rate (median 74 bpm), and blood pressure readings were within normal limits (median 100/60 mm Hg). Electrocardiography yielded normal findings. Subsequently, she was admitted to the Obstetrical and Gynaecological Division. On examination, her abdomen was distended more than expected for her gestational age; there was a firm, tender mass with a taut-elastic feel occupying the entire upper left quadrant and measuring 18 cm, in the absence of clinical ascites.

During a bimanual vaginal examination, a non-mobile, hard, rounded mass approximately 9 cm in diameter was palpated in the lesser pelvis, causing partial bulging at the posterior vaginal fornix.

She was treated with intravenous paracetamol (1000 mg twice daily) and encouraged to maintain a semi-orthostatic posture, with the aim of reducing diaphragmatic compression from both the gravid uterus and the large intra-abdominal masses. These interventions led to a modest alleviation of symptoms, as pain intensity decreased from 5 to 1 on the Visual Analogue Scale, without the need for supplemental oxygen or ventilation.

While hospitalized, the patient experienced a low-grade fever. A nasopharyngeal swab for SARS-CoV-2 PCR was performed and returned negative, whereas urine culture revealed an infection with Klebsiella pneumoniae. Blood analysis showed evidence of iron deficiency anemia, with a hemoglobin level of 9.9 g/L (reference range: 12.0–16.6 g/L) and serum ferritin at 25 mcg/L (reference: 15–300 mcg/L). Additionally, there was a mild elevation in C-reactive protein to 1.39 mg/dL (reference: 0–0.3 mg/dL) and leukocytosis, with total white blood cell count at 11.29×10^3 /uL (reference: 4–10×10^3 /uL) and neutrophils at 9.27×10^3 /uL (reference: 2.8–5.5×10^3 /uL), findings attributed to normal physiological changes of pregnancy.

Investigations:

Ultrasound assessment revealed the presence of two sizable, unilocular cystic lesions characterized by smooth borders and peripheral blood flow. The superior lesion was measured at 18×12×7 cm, spanning from the left subphrenic area down to the uterine fundus. The inferior lesion, measuring 9.5×8×8 cm, appeared to be attached to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration (58×71 mm).

Serological analysis indicated that tumour markers such as CA 19–9, CA 15–3, CEA, β2-microglobulin, and LDH remained within normal reference values; however, CA-125 levels were elevated at 41.8 U/mL (normal <35 U/mL), with an observable rise from 36 U/mL reported two weeks prior. This reflected a mild upward trajectory in this particular marker’s serum levels. Consequently, an MRI scan without contrast was performed, which detected thickened walls (1.4 cm) as well as inhomogeneous fluid contents on T1- and T2-weighted fat-saturated sequences and focal areas of hyperintensity in solid T1- and T2-weighted components (see figure 1; only T2 fat-saturated sequences presented). The lower mass’s plane of separation was indistinct, and it appeared to be continuous with the previously sonographically detected posterior leiomyoma. There was no evidence of lymphadenopathy on MRI.

Differential diagnosis:

During the ultrasonographic assessment, the cystic lesions closely resembled two large decidualised endometriomas, with the lower (caudal) cyst exhibiting adherence to an intramural-subserosal leiomyoma located on the posterior uterine wall and measuring 58×71 mm.

MRI identified two rounded, unilocular cystic structures within the adnexa, each displaying hypertrophied vasculature and heterogeneous internal fluid (refer to figure 1). The inferior mass appeared to be adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma with evidence of cystic degeneration of 58×71 mm.

Owing to the considerable size and spatial occupation ('steric bulk') of the gravid uterus in addition to the voluminous masses, neither a connecting stalk nor a tumour-supplying vessel could be discerned for the upper lesion. This observation directed diagnostic considerations primarily towards ovarian lesions—particularly endometriomas—supported by a mild increase in CA-125 detected during the second trimester (see discussion).

Interpreting the imaging findings from both ultrasound and MRI, differential diagnosis encompassed decidualised endometriomas and cystic degenerating leiomyomas. The mild rise in CA-125 was more indicative of an endometrioma in the upper cyst, yet the anatomical continuity with the uterus for the lower mass favored a leiomyomatous (myomatous) origin. The patient’s experience of dyspnoea was attributed to the significant mass effect of the large abdominal tumors, with no clinical or investigative evidence of pulmonary or cardiac compromise.

Treatment:

Owing to the progression of clinical symptoms—including mass-induced abdominal pain and dyspnoea attributable to restrictive lung disease from the increasing size of the abdominal masses—as well as the imperative for a definitive diagnosis, concerns about potential complications (such as rupture, torsion, and infection), and the expectation of uterine expansion concurrent with fetal growth, surgical intervention was undertaken. At 21 weeks and 6 days’ gestation, following the patient’s informed consent, a laparotomy with a midline xiphoid-to-pubic incision was performed.

Initially, two samples of free peritoneal fluid were obtained for both microbiological culture and cytological analysis. Subsequently, the surgical field was systematically inspected, and fetal cardiac activity was confirmed.

Unexpectedly, the cranial mass was identified as an enormous pedunculated, atypical, cystic, and taut-elastic uterine leiomyoma. The mass exhibited extensive vascularisation and appeared twisted and exuding fluid, features likely accounting for the febrile episodes observed during hospitalization (see figure 2). Accordingly, the surgical team proceeded with careful excision of the mass at its point of uterine attachment, employing a double-clamp technique (see figure 2). After transecting the stalk, meticulous hemostasis was achieved with a single absorbable suture applied to the exposed base. The caudal mass, which arose from the uterine torus, was deliberately left in place to reduce obstetric risks; it presented as immobile with a smooth surface and a soft, cystic consistency, suggestive of a large intramural-subserosal leiomyoma.

The operative procedure lasted 2 hours and 25 minutes. The patient received prophylactic progesterone, administered intramuscularly at 100 mg beginning intraoperatively and continued at the same daily dose until discharge. This regimen was followed by administration of intramuscular hydroxyprogesterone caproate (341 mg, 1 vial intramuscularly every three days as per the package instructions) up to the time of scheduled delivery. Perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis consisted of cefazolin 2 g intravenously during surgery, which was subsequently maintained postoperatively with cefazolin 1 g intravenously three times daily, plus clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily, throughout the hospital admission, guided by the antibiogram from urine cultures (showing Klebsiella pneumoniae highly susceptible to cephalosporins and clarithromycin).

Outcome and follow-up:

Following the surgical procedure, the patient's anaemia worsened (Hb 7.5 g/L, HCT 24.3%), and ongoing iron deficiency necessitated treatment with intravenous iron (ferric carboxymaltose 1 g) and two units of blood transfused, leading to clinical improvement (Hb 10.0 g/L, HCT 30.4%). She was discharged uneventfully 10 days post-operatively and subsequently enrolled in close obstetric monitoring every 3 weeks. Serial assessments demonstrated appropriate fetal growth (86th percentile, per the WHO fetal growth chart calculator, available online at https://srhr.org/fetalgrowthcalculator/#/) and normal amniotic fluid index.

Microbiological and cytological analyses of peritoneal fluid yielded negative results. Histopathological evaluation identified benign uterine leiomyomas and regions of ischaemic necrosis adjacent to the twisted pedicle.

The caudal myoma would likely have impeded descent of the fetal head during the second stage of labour. Consequently, an elective caesarean section was scheduled at 37 weeks of gestation on the same uterine incision to lower the risk of uterine rupture, resulting in a favorable neonatal outcome.

At present, the patient experiences no symptoms attributable to myoma, with the remaining pelvic leiomyoma slightly decreased in size to 7.5 cm. She is breastfeeding successfully and prefers to defer further surgical intervention, given partial postpartum regression of the myomas, absence of complaints, and having achieved her reproductive goals. The infant continues to develop appropriately.

Discussion:

Myomas can often be misdiagnosed as adnexal masses: the incidence during pregnancy is 0.3–10.1% and 1–6%, respectively .13

In this instance, the initial assessment identified abdominal masses resembling two large decidualised endometriomas. Notably, the inferior solid region of the pelvic mass was suggestive of a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration, measuring 58×71 mm, as confirmed by both US and MRI imaging. Given the lack of standardized treatment protocols for managing pelvic masses during pregnancy, the multidisciplinary team determined that planned surgical intervention might lower the likelihood of preterm birth.

Throughout gestation, serum CA-125 concentrations typically show two elevations: one in the first trimester (median 23 U/mL, p<0.00001) and another in the third trimester (median 21 U/mL, p<0.001), in comparison with values observed during the second trimester (median 14.0 U/mL). In contrast, this patient presented with a CA-125 level of 41.8 U/mL, which surpassed the median second-trimester value.

CA-125 is predominantly utilized as a tumor marker in the detection of ovarian cancer (with a conventional diagnostic threshold of ≥35 U/mL), particularly in women over the age of 50, where its diagnostic parameters—area under the curve, positive predictive value, sensitivity, and specificity—are optimized. Nevertheless, elevated CA-125 levels can also occur in various benign conditions, such as endometriosis and adenomyosis (>19 U/mL), pelvic inflammatory disease, menstruation, both early and late stages of pregnancy (median values of 23 and 21 U/mL, respectively), increased miscarriage risk, and myoma (cut-off <19 U/mL).57

Several factors influenced the clinical approach, including the identification of large, vascularized masses exhibiting both solid and cystic features, which posed an immediate risk for complications such as rupture, torsion, or infection. Additional considerations included the patient’s deteriorating symptoms (increased pain and dyspnoea), the possibility that the masses could further enlarge—heightening the risks of hypoxia, necrosis, and tissue degeneration—and mildly elevated tumour markers (CA-125 = 41.8 U/mL). Furthermore, there was concern that the size of the masses could restrict the normal expansion of the uterus.

Given both the potential neoplastic nature and uncertain etiology of the masses, an MRI scan without contrast was performed following the initial ultrasound to achieve improved diagnostic clarity. In line with current clinical guidelines, the use of gadolinium-enhanced MRI is generally discouraged during pregnancy, except in cases of clear clinical necessity related to rheumatological, inflammatory, or infiltrative dermatological conditions, or when there is significant risk to the fetus such as stillbirth or neonatal death. Accordingly, the multidisciplinary team proceeded with a non-contrast MRI scan, ensuring the magnetic field strength did not exceed 1.5-T, as this approach is generally accepted as safe following the first trimester.

In this case, the interdisciplinary medical team—including surgeons, gynaecologists, and obstetricians—decided to undertake a longitudinal laparotomic surgical intervention, following the acquisition of formal written informed consent from the patient. During intraoperative evaluation, the upper mass was found to possess a pedicle, which introduced an elevated risk of acute, conventional complications. This finding underpinned the decision to restrict surgical intervention to an upper myomectomy only. Additionally, the procedure incorporated serial, periodic transvaginal ultrasound assessments to ensure ongoing fetal viability, complemented by the prophylactic use of intraoperative progesterone administration.

As indicated by a comprehensive multicentre study by Zhao et al, it is essential to assess and record the fetal heart rate before, during, and following surgery.

Typically, surgical intervention is postponed until postpartum, favouring medical management and expectant observation. Nevertheless, a recent investigation conducted by Sakinci et al in a tertiary care setting revealed no statistically significant differences in preoperative and postoperative haemoglobin levels or blood transfusion requirements (p >0.05) between women undergoing caesarean section (CS) with concurrent myomectomy and those with CS alone and no myoma. However, those receiving CS myomectomy experienced notably longer operative and hospitalisation durations (p =0.001 and p =0.001, respectively). The mean size of the myoma was 8.3 ± 4.1 cm, and a statistically significant, positive relationship between myoma size and length of hospital stay was observed (p =0.01).

The same study identified a statistically significant inverse association between myoma size and gestational age at delivery (p = 0.035).

Uterine leiomyomas display a non-linear pattern of size fluctuation throughout pregnancy and the postpartum period. Specifically, leiomyomas enlarge by approximately 6.90% per week, with the increase being 6.12% (p<0.001) between the first and second trimesters, and 3.10%±0.39% between the second and third trimesters (p<0.05). Consequently, the rate of leiomyoma growth is markedly greater in the first trimester compared to subsequent stages of pregnancy. Conversely, during the third trimester and up to six weeks postpartum, the size of myomas diminishes significantly (mean reduction of 44.25%±36.05%, p<0.05).

These studies underscore the necessity for meticulous ultrasonographic assessment of uterine myomas with respect to their size, location, number, and proximity to the placenta prior to any surgical intervention.

For uterine myomas discovered during pregnancy, a conservative management strategy is generally advocated due to the heightened risk of complications such as severe hemorrhage, fetal loss, preterm birth, and uterine rupture associated with surgical intervention. Myomectomy, however, may be warranted in instances involving complicated myomas with torsion or intractable symptoms unresponsive to conservative measures. Surgical intervention should be minimized in duration to preserve pregnancy and may be conducted laparoscopically, with preference for the open Hasson technique when gestational age is low (first or early second trimester), the mass is smaller, and the myoma is not intramural. This method is associated with improved maternal outcomes and an increased likelihood of vaginal delivery. Nonetheless, the present case illustrates a prevalent bias wherein laparotomy is favored over laparoscopy in patients presenting with large, multiple, intramural, or critically positioned myomas, a practice that may increase the incidence of cesarean section due to substantial myomas attached to the posterior wall at the isthmic region, potentially obstructing vaginal delivery.
