Title: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

Rosalba De Nola, Emma Bassi, Cataldo Paletta, Mario Vicino

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her 40s with no prior pregnancies presented in her second trimester with abdominal pain and shortness of breath. Ultrasound identified two sizable abdominal masses: one lesion occupied the entire left subphrenic region and extended to the fundus of the uterus, while the other was located in the pouch of Douglas. The patient underwent laparotomy, but the lower mass was left undisturbed in the pelvis. She continued with standard obstetric monitoring and subsequently had an elective cesarean section, achieving excellent outcomes for both mother and child. For pregnant patients presenting with large, symptomatic masses, thorough assessment with bimanual examination and safe imaging techniques such as ultrasonography and non-contrast MRI is critical. Surgery should be reserved for situations involving suspicion of malignancy or clinical complications, with the objective of maintaining the pregnancy and preventing unnecessary intervention. Decisions regarding delivery should be based on the number, size, and anatomical position of uterine masses.

Background:

The evaluation and treatment of atypical or symptomatic abdominal masses in pregnancy pose notable clinical difficulties. Diagnostic modalities are primarily confined to physical assessment, ultrasonography (US) with color Doppler, and non-contrast MRI. Preoperative US plays a crucial role in detecting symptomatic uterine leiomyomas located on the anterior wall of the pregnant uterus, whether they are submucosal or pedunculated in nature. For the assessment of multiple, sizable, intramural, or posteriorly situated leiomyomas, MRI is superior due to its capacity to accurately delineate the relationship between the leiomyoma and the uterine cavity.

The application of serum tumor markers in the diagnostic process during pregnancy is controversial. CA-125, in particular, tends to be elevated during the initial trimester and returns to baseline levels later in gestation. As a result, single measurements lack diagnostic utility, although sequential testing may help in distinguishing diagnoses and in longitudinal monitoring. Appropriate management hinges on thorough, multidisciplinary risk evaluation to effectively weigh obstetric considerations against the infrequency and intricacy of such presentations.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 40s with obesity (body mass index 33), gravida 1, para 0, abortion 1, presented to our clinic at 21 weeks’ gestation during her second trimester, reporting severe, acute abdominal pain localized to both the upper left quadrant and the pelvic region (Visual Analogue Scale score of 5 at each site), in addition to mild shortness of breath. Routine screenings conducted during the first and second trimesters had indicated a low risk for aneuploidies—findings later corroborated by non-invasive prenatal testing—and fetal anatomy appeared unremarkable.

Initially, she presented to the emergency department for comprehensive multidisciplinary assessment: her oxygen saturation was within normal limits (median 97%), and thoracic examination showed a regular heart rate (median 74 bpm) and blood pressure (median 100/60 mm Hg). Her ECG demonstrated no abnormalities. Subsequently, she was admitted to the Obstetrics and Gynecology Department. Abdominal evaluation revealed distension greater than anticipated for her gestational stage, with a tender swelling and a firm, elastic mass measuring 18 cm filling the entire upper left abdominal quadrant, but without clinical evidence of ascites.

The bimanual vaginal examination showed the presence of a fixed mass in the lesser pelvis of about 9 cm, rounded and hard, that partially bulged at the posterior fornix.

Pain management included intravenous paracetamol 1000 mg administered twice daily and maintaining the patient in a semi-orthostatic position. This approach, theorized to decrease diaphragmatic compression from both the gravid uterus and sizable abdominal masses, led to some symptom relief without the necessity for supplemental oxygen or ventilatory assistance. Reported pain intensity diminished from 5 to 1 on the Visual Analogue Scale.

While hospitalized, the patient experienced a mild fever. As a result, a nasopharyngeal swab for SARS-CoV-2 using PCR testing was conducted and found to be negative. However, urine cultures were positive for a Klebsiella pneumoniae infection. Additionally, laboratory evaluation revealed iron deficiency anemia with hemoglobin measured at 9.9 g/L (normal range 12.0–16.6 g/L) and serum ferritin at 25 mcg/L (normal range 15–300 mcg/L). There was a modest elevation in C-reactive protein to 1.39 mg/dL (normal values 0–0.3 mg/dL), and an increased neutrophil count was present: total white blood cells 11.29×10^3 /µL (normal 4–10×10^3 /µL) and neutrophils 9.27×10^3 /µL (normal 2.8–5.5×10^3 /µL), consistent with physiological changes observed in pregnancy.

Investigations:

Ultrasound revealed two sizable unilocular cystic lesions exhibiting smooth borders and vascularity at their periphery. The superior lesion was 18×12×7 cm, located from the left subphrenic area down to the fundus of the uterus. The inferior lesion measured 9.5×8×8 cm, and appeared to be attached to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma demonstrating cystic degeneration (58×71 mm).

Tumor marker assessment—including CA 19–9, CA 15–3, CEA, β2-microglobulin, and LDH—yielded results within normal reference ranges. However, the CA-125 level was raised at 41.8 U/mL (normal <35 U/mL), up from 36 U/mL measured two weeks prior, indicating a mild upward trend in this marker’s serum concentration. Consequently, the patient was evaluated with a non-contrast MRI, which identified thickened walls (1.4 cm), heterogeneous fluid signal on T1- and T2-weighted fat-saturated sequences, and focal solid components that were hyperintense on T1- and T2-weighted images (see figure 1; T2 fat-sat sequences only depicted). The margins of the inferior mass were indistinct, appearing to be contiguous with the previously identified posterior leiomyoma on ultrasound. No lymphadenopathy was observed on MRI.

Differential diagnosis:

On ultrasound assessment, the cystic lesions closely resembled two large decidualized endometriomas, with the inferior cyst seen attached to a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine leiomyoma measuring 58×71 mm.

Magnetic resonance imaging revealed two oval-shaped, unilocular adnexal cysts containing hypertrophic blood vessels and heterogeneous fluid (refer to figure 1); the lower lesion appeared adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration with dimensions of 58×71 mm.

Owing to the physical obstruction created by the enlarged gravid uterus and sizable masses, no connecting stalk or feeding vessel supplying the superior mass could be detected, which pointed toward an ovarian origin—most notably endometriomas—given the mild increase in CA-125 levels during the second trimester (refer to discussion).

Imaging findings on ultrasound and MRI led to a differential diagnosis that considered both decidualised endometriomas and cystic degeneration of leiomyomas: the minor CA-125 elevation supported an endometrioma for the upper cystic lesion, whereas continuity with uterine tissue favored a myomatous etiology for the lower mass. The patient's shortness of breath was considered secondary to mechanical pressure from the large intra-abdominal masses, as neither pulmonary nor cardiac dysfunction was evident.

Treatment:

Given the progression of her symptoms—including abdominal pain from the mass and dyspnoea attributable to restrictive lung disease secondary to the size of the abdominal lesions—a definitive diagnosis became imperative. There was also an elevated risk of potential complications, such as rupture, torsion, and infection, in addition to anticipated further uterine enlargement as the fetus grew. Therefore, after obtaining informed consent, a laparotomy employing a midline xiphoid-to-pubic incision was performed at 21 weeks and 6 days of gestation.

First, we collected two samples of the free peritoneal fluid for cultural and cytological purposes. Then, we explored the abdominal cavity, verifying the presence of fetal cardiac activity.

Unexpectedly, the cranial mass was identified as a large, pedunculated, atypical leiomyoma of the uterus with cystic features and a firm, elastic consistency; it was highly vascularized, exhibited initial torsion, and showed evidence of leakage, which likely accounted for the patient’s fever during admission (refer to figure 2). Consequently, the surgical team opted to delicately remove the mass at its uterine attachment using a double-clamp technique (see figure 2). After transecting the pedicle, the exposed base was meticulously secured with a single absorbable suture. The caudal mass, which arose from the uterine torus, was preserved in place to reduce obstetric complications. This lesion was immobile, with a smooth contour and soft, cystic consistency reminiscent of a sizeable intramural-subserosal leiomyoma.

The operative procedure lasted for 2 hours and 25 minutes. Importantly, the patient was given prophylactic progesterone at a dose of 100 mg via intramuscular injection, initiated during the surgery and continued at 100 mg intramuscularly per day until hospital discharge. Subsequently, intramuscular hydroxyprogesterone caproate (341 mg in 1 ampule every 3 days, consistent with the manufacturer’s instructions) was administered up to the planned delivery. For antibiotic prophylaxis, cefazolin 2 g was administered intravenously intraoperatively, followed by postoperative intravenous cefazolin 1 g three times daily combined with clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily throughout the hospitalization. This regimen was selected based on the urine culture antibiogram demonstrating Klebsiella pneumoniae with high sensitivity to both cephalosporins and clarithromycin.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following the surgical procedure, the patient experienced worsening anemia (Hb 7.5 g/L, HCT 24.3%) and ongoing iron deficiency, necessitating the administration of intravenous ferric carboxymaltose (1 g) and two units of blood transfused, resulting in improved values (Hb 10.0 g/L, HCT 30.4%). She was discharged in stable condition 10 days postoperatively, with no complications, and was subsequently enrolled in close obstetrical monitoring every 3 weeks. During follow-up, assessments indicated fetal growth at the 86th percentile, based on the WHO fetal growth chart calculator (https://srhr.org/fetalgrowthcalculator/#/), as well as evaluation of the amniotic fluid index.

Both culture and cytology of the peritoneal fluid returned negative results. Histopathological analysis identified benign uterine leiomyomas, with zones of ischemic necrosis in proximity to the twisted pedicle.

The lower myoma was likely to significantly impede fetal head descent during the second stage of labor. Therefore, a planned caesarean section was scheduled at the prior surgical incision once the fetus reached 37 weeks of gestation, thereby reducing the chance of uterine scar dehiscence and resulting in a favorable neonatal outcome.

At present, the patient does not experience any myoma-associated symptoms, and the remaining pelvic myoma has marginally decreased in size to 7.5 cm. She continues to breastfeed successfully, but, given the partial regression of the residual leiomyomas postpartum, absence of clinical symptoms, and having achieved her desire for motherhood, she prefers not to plan additional surgical intervention at this time. Her baby is developing appropriately.

Discussion:

Myomas can often be misdiagnosed as adnexal masses: the incidence during pregnancy is 0.3–10.1% and 1–6%, respectively .13

In this case, the first assessment identified abdominal masses that resembled two large decidualized endometriomas, with the exception of a lower solid portion of the pelvic mass. This portion resembled a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration, measuring 58×71 mm on both ultrasound and MRI scans. Given the lack of specific management protocols for pelvic masses during pregnancy, the multidisciplinary team concluded that planned surgical intervention might reduce the likelihood of preterm birth.

Throughout pregnancy, serum CA-125 levels typically exhibit two distinct elevations: the first occurs in the initial trimester (median 23 U/mL, p<0.00001), and the second arises during the third trimester (median 21 U/mL, p<0.001), both significantly greater than values seen in the second trimester (median 14.0 U/mL). In contrast, in this patient, the CA-125 was measured at 41.8 U/mL, surpassing the typical median level observed in the second trimester.

CA-125 serves predominantly as a tumor marker for ovarian carcinoma, with a conventional threshold of ≥35 U/mL, particularly in women over the age of 50, where it demonstrates increased area under the curve, sensitivity, specificity, and positive predictive value. However, elevated CA-125 levels are also observed in a range of benign conditions, including endometriosis and adenomyosis (typically above 19 U/mL), pelvic inflammatory disease, menstruation, early or late pregnancy (with median values of 23 and 21 U/mL, respectively), risk of miscarriage, as well as in cases of myoma (with levels generally below 19 U/mL).57

The primary factors influencing the clinical management included the detection of large, vascular masses exhibiting both solid and cystic features, carrying a genuine risk for complications such as rupture, torsion, or infection. Additional considerations were the patient's worsening symptoms (increased pain and shortness of breath), the potential for further tumor enlargement which could lead to hypoxia, necrosis, or degeneration, a marginal elevation in tumor marker levels (CA-125=41.8 U/mL), and restricted pelvic space, which could compromise normal uterine expansion.

Given the potential neoplastic nature and ambiguous etiology of the masses, an MRI without contrast was conducted following the ultrasound to improve diagnostic precision. Current guidelines advise against the use of gadolinium-based contrast in MRI during pregnancy unless there are compelling indications, such as certain rheumatological, inflammatory, or infiltrative dermatologic disorders, or concerns about fetal demise. Consequently, the multidisciplinary team opted for a non-contrast MRI and ensured that the magnetic field strength did not exceed 1.5-T, a protocol deemed acceptably safe beyond the first trimester.

In this case, a multidisciplinary group including surgeons, gynecologists, and obstetricians chose to perform a longitudinal laparotomic surgery after securing formal written informed consent from the patient. During the surgical evaluation, the upper lesion demonstrated a pedicle, which posed a notable risk for a sudden, classic complication. This observation justified restricting the surgical intervention to an upper myomectomy only. Additionally, fetal viability was tracked intraoperatively by repeated transvaginal ultrasound examinations, accompanied by prophylactic intraoperative administration of progesterone.

According to a large multicentric study conducted by Zhao et al, it is mandatory to check and document the fetal heart rate before, during and after the surgery.

Typically, surgical intervention is postponed until after delivery, with a preference for medical management and watchful waiting. Nevertheless, Sakinci et al recently performed a study at a tertiary center, demonstrating that there were no statistically significant differences regarding preoperative and postoperative hemoglobin levels or rates of blood transfusion (p >0.05) between women who underwent caesarean section (CS) with concurrent myomectomy and those without fibroids who had CS alone. Despite this, the lengths of hospitalization and surgical procedures were notably longer for the CS with myomectomy group (p =0.001 for both comparisons). The average myoma size measured 8.3 ± 4.1 cm, and a statistically significant positive association was observed between fibroid size and length of hospital stay (p =0.01).

The same research study also showed a statistically significant and inverse correlation between the size of the myoma and the delivery week (p = 0.035).

Uterine leiomyomas exhibit a non-linear pattern of growth and regression throughout pregnancy and the puerperium. Specifically, leiomyoma size increases by 6.90% weekly, with growth rates of 6.12% (p<0.001) observed between the first and second trimesters and 3.10%±0.39% between the second and third trimesters (p<0.05). This indicates a notably greater rate of enlargement during the first trimester compared to subsequent gestational periods. Conversely, during the third trimester and within 6 weeks postpartum, the average size of myomas significantly decreased by 44.25%±36.05% (p<0.05).

These papers showed the importance of an accurate US examination of the size, location, number and distance of uterine myomas from the placenta before the surgical approach.

When uterine myomas are identified during pregnancy, management typically favors conservative therapy over surgery, given the associated risks such as significant hemorrhage, pregnancy loss, preterm delivery, and uterine rupture. Nonetheless, surgical removal may be warranted for complicated lesions, such as torsion of a pedunculated fibroid, or when persistent symptoms do not respond to conservative measures. Surgical intervention should be performed as efficiently as possible to support continuation of the pregnancy, and a laparoscopic technique—preferably utilizing the open Hasson method—may be suitable, particularly for cases in the first or early second trimester with smaller, non-intramural myomas. This method is associated with improved maternal outcomes and a higher likelihood of vaginal delivery. The current case, however, illustrates the frequent selection bias for choosing laparotomy over laparoscopy in scenarios involving larger, multiple, intramural, or critically located myomas. Such selection is often linked to an increased incidence of cesarean section, as demonstrated by this case featuring a large myoma attached to the posterior wall at the isthmic region of the uterus, potentially acting as a physical barrier to vaginal birth.
