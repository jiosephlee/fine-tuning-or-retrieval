Title: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

Rosalba De Nola, Emma Bassi, Cataldo Paletta, Mario Vicino

Abstract:

A woman in her 40s who had not previously given birth presented during her second trimester with abdominal discomfort and shortness of breath. Ultrasound imaging identified two sizable abdominal masses: the superior lesion was located in the left subphrenic region, extending toward the uterine fundus, while the inferior mass was situated within the pouch of Douglas. The patient underwent laparotomy, during which the lower mass was retained in the pelvis. She continued with routine antenatal care and ultimately underwent a planned caesarean section, which yielded an excellent outcome for both mother and infant. For pregnant patients presenting with large, symptomatic masses, a thorough assessment, including bimanual examination and the use of safe imaging techniques like ultrasound and non-contrast MRI, is crucial. Surgical intervention should be reserved for cases with oncologic suspicion or when clinical complications arise, with a focus on preserving the pregnancy and avoiding unnecessary procedures. The approach to delivery should be individualized, based on the size, quantity, and anatomical position of the uterine masses.

Background:

Identifying and treating unusual or symptomatic abdominal masses in pregnancy is associated with considerable clinical difficulty. Available diagnostic tools are restricted to physical examination, ultrasound (US) including colour Doppler, and MRI without contrast. Preoperative US proves especially helpful for localising symptomatic uterine leiomyomas that are situated on the anterior aspect of the pregnant uterus, regardless of whether they are submucosal or pedunculated. For multiple, large, intramural, or posterior leiomyomas, MRI provides more detailed diagnostic information, as it can accurately measure the distance from the myoma to the uterine cavity.

The utility of serum tumour markers during pregnancy continues to be controversial. CA-125, in particular, naturally increases during the first trimester and returns to baseline thereafter. Consequently, a single value lacks diagnostic value, whereas repeated measurements may offer valuable insights for differential diagnosis and ongoing monitoring. Effective management of these rare and complex cases demands thoughtful, multidisciplinary evaluation to adequately weigh obstetric risks against the unique clinical characteristics present.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 40s with obesity (BMI 33), gravida 1, para 0, abortion 1, presented during her second trimester at 21 weeks gestation with intense, sudden-onset abdominal pain in both the upper left quadrant and pelvic regions (Visual Analogue Scale score 5 for each), accompanied by mild shortness of breath. Her routine prenatal screening in both the first and second trimesters showed low risk for aneuploidy, confirmed by non-invasive prenatal testing, and normal fetal anatomical development.

She was initially evaluated in the emergency department through a comprehensive multidisciplinary approach. The patient had oxygen saturation averaging 97%, normal chest findings with a heart rate of 74 bpm, and blood pressure averaging 100/60 mm Hg. The ECG revealed no abnormalities. Subsequently, she was transferred to the Obstetrics and Gynecology department. Examination found the abdomen enlarged beyond what is typically expected for this gestational age, with a tender, taut-elastic swelling filling the entire upper left quadrant, measuring 18 cm, and lacking signs of ascites.

On bimanual vaginal examination, a fixed, rounded, firm mass approximately 9 cm in diameter was identified in the lesser pelvis, partially protruding toward the posterior fornix.

Analgesic treatment with paracetamol 1000 mg intravenously twice daily, combined with a semi-upright position thought to mitigate diaphragmatic pressure from the uterus and sizable abdominal masses, led to modest symptom relief. No supplemental oxygen or ventilation was required, and her pain reduced from a score of 5 to 1 on the Visual Analogue Scale.

During hospitalization, she developed a low-grade fever. A PCR test for SARS-CoV-2 using a nasopharyngeal swab was negative, but urine cultures identified infection with Klebsiella pneumoniae. Laboratory findings included iron deficiency anemia, evidenced by hemoglobin (Hb) at 9.9 g/L (reference range 12.0–16.6 g/L) and serum ferritin at 25 mcg/L (reference range 15–300 mcg/L), mildly elevated C-reactive protein at 1.39 mg/dL (reference range 0–0.3 mg/dL), and increased neutrophil count—total white blood cells at 11.29×10^3/uL (reference range 4–10×10^3/uL) with neutrophils at 9.27×10^3/uL (reference range 2.8–5.5×10^3/uL)—findings consistent with physiological changes during pregnancy.

Investigations:

Ultrasound revealed two sizable, unilocular cystic lesions exhibiting smooth borders and peripheral blood flow. The superior lesion measured 18×12×7 cm and extended from the left subphrenic area to the fundus of the uterus. The inferior lesion measured 9.5×8×8 cm, appearing adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma undergoing cystic degeneration (58×71 mm).

Tumor markers including CA 19–9, CA 15–3, CEA, β2-microglobulin, and LDH were all within reference ranges. However, CA-125 was elevated at 41.8 U/mL (normal <35 U/mL) and had risen from 36 U/mL noted two weeks earlier, demonstrating a subtle upward trend. Given this increase, the patient underwent MRI without contrast, which demonstrated thick cyst walls (1.4 cm), inhomogeneous fluid on T1 and T2 fat-saturated images, and focal solid portions that were hyperintense on both T1 and T2-weighted sequences (refer to figure 1; only T2 fat-sat images are included). The caudal lesion’s cleavage plane was indistinct, suggesting continuity with the previously identified posterior leiomyoma on ultrasound. The MRI did not detect any lymphadenopathy.

Differential diagnosis:

During ultrasonography, the cystic lesions closely resembled two large decidualised endometriomas, with the inferior cyst appearing to be adherent to an intramural-subserosal leiomyoma located on the posterior uterine wall measuring 58×71 mm.

MRI demonstrated two spherical, unilocular cysts in the adnexa, with prominent vessels and heterogeneous fluid inside (refer to figure 1); the inferior lesion appeared adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration, also measuring 58×71 mm.

Owing to the volumetric prominence of the gravid uterus and the considerable size of the masses, no connecting pedicle or feeding vessel was seen for the upper mass, guiding the diagnostic consideration toward ovarian origin, particularly endometriomas, given the modestly increased CA-125 during the second trimester of pregnancy (see discussion).

Sonographic and MRI findings raised possibilities of decidualised endometriomas and cystically degenerated leiomyomas: the mild CA-125 elevation supported endometrioma for the upper cyst, whereas its continuity with uterine tissue pointed toward a myomatous origin for the lower lesion. The patient's dyspnoea was attributed to mechanical effects from the substantial abdominal masses, in the absence of pulmonary or cardiac abnormalities.

Treatment:

Given the deterioration of the patient’s condition—including abdominal pain from the masses and dyspnoea attributed to restrictive lung disease caused by their size—as well as the need to establish a definitive diagnosis, the risk of complications such as rupture, torsion, or infection, and the likelihood of progressive uterine enlargement accompanying fetal growth, a laparotomy was performed at 21 weeks and 6 days’ gestation following informed consent, using a longitudinal incision from the xiphoid to pubic region.

Initially, two samples of free peritoneal fluid were collected for culture and cytology. The abdominal cavity was then explored, and fetal cardiac activity was confirmed.

Unexpectedly, the superior mass consisted of a very large, pedunculated, atypical cystic and taut-elastic uterine leiomyoma. The mass was highly vascular, appeared twisted and leaking, which may account for the patient’s fever during hospitalization (see figure 2). Consequently, the surgical team opted to carefully detach the mass from its uterine base using a double clamp technique (see figure 2). After resection of the stalk, the exposed base was meticulously closed with a single absorbable suture. The inferior mass, which arose from the uterine torus, was preserved in situ to reduce obstetric risk; it was firm, with a smooth surface and soft consistency, resembling a large cystic intramural-subserosal leiomyoma.

The operation lasted 2 hours and 25 minutes. The patient was given prophylactic progesterone at a dose of 100 mg intramuscularly, initiated intraoperatively and continued daily at 100 mg IM until discharge. Subsequently, she received intramuscular hydroxyprogesterone caproate (341 mg in one ampule every three days, per the package leaflet) until planned delivery. Perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis consisted of cefazolin 2 g IV, followed by postoperative administration of cefazolin 1 g IV three times daily plus clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily, tailored to urine culture sensitivities (Klebsiella pneumoniae highly susceptible to cephalosporins and clarithromycin) during the hospital stay.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following the operation, the patient's anaemia deteriorated (Hb 7.5 g/L, HCT 24.3%) and iron deficiency persisted, necessitating intravenous administration of ferric carboxymaltose at a dose of 1 g and two units of blood transfusion. This intervention resulted in improvement (Hb 10.0 g/L, HCT 30.4%). Ten days after surgery, the patient was discharged without adverse events and subsequently began intensive obstetric follow-up appointments every three weeks. These assessments indicated fetal growth at the 86th percentile, according to the WHO fetal growth chart calculator (available online at https://srhr.org/fetalgrowthcalculator/#/), as well as an evaluation of amniotic fluid index.

Analysis of peritoneal fluid revealed negative results for both microbial cultures and cytology. Pathological review confirmed benign uterine leiomyomas, with evidence of ischemic necrosis adjacent to the area of the twisted pedicle.

The caudal leiomyoma posed a substantial barrier to descent of the fetal head during the expulsive stage of labor. For this reason, an elective cesarean section was performed through the existing surgical incision upon the fetus reaching 37 weeks' gestation, thereby reducing the likelihood of uterine scar rupture. This approach yielded an excellent neonatal outcome.

Currently, the patient does not experience myoma-related symptoms, although the size of the remaining pelvic myoma has decreased modestly to 7.5 cm. She has been able to breastfeed without difficulty and, given the partial postpartum shrinkage of the remaining leiomyomas, lack of symptoms, and successful attainment of motherhood, she has elected to postpone any further surgical intervention. The infant is progressing well.

Discussion:

Myomas can often be misdiagnosed as adnexal masses: the incidence during pregnancy is 0.3–10.1% and 1–6%, respectively .13

In this instance, the initial assessment revealed abdominal masses resembling two large decidualised endometriomas, aside from the inferior solid portion of the pelvic mass, which appeared consistent with a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration and measuring 58×71 mm on both ultrasound and MRI examinations. Given the lack of definitive guidelines for managing pelvic masses during pregnancy, the multidisciplinary group determined that elective surgical intervention might lower the probability of preterm birth.

Throughout pregnancy, serum CA-125 typically demonstrates two peaks: one during the first trimester (median 23 U/mL, p<0.00001) and another in the third trimester (median 21 U/mL, p<0.001), which are elevated compared to second trimester levels (median 14.0 U/mL). In contrast, the patient’s CA-125 level was 41.8 U/mL, exceeding the median value expected in the second trimester.

CA-125 is predominantly utilized as a tumor marker for ovarian carcinoma (current standard cut-off ≥35 U/mL), with improved diagnostic parameters in women older than 50 (including greater area under the curve, positive predictive value, sensitivity, and specificity). However, elevated CA-125 can also be linked with numerous non-malignant conditions, such as endometriosis and adenomyosis (>19 U/mL), pelvic inflammatory disease, menstruation, early or late pregnancy (median 23 U/mL and 21 U/mL, respectively), increased risk of miscarriage, and myoma (cut-off <19 U/mL).57

The primary factors guiding clinical management included the identification of large, vascular masses containing both solid and cystic regions, which carried a significant risk for complications such as rupture, torsion, or infection. Additionally, the patient’s symptoms, which included worsening pain and increasing dyspnoea, raised further concern. The potential for mass enlargement, with the associated risks of hypoxia, necrosis, and degeneration, as well as a mild elevation in tumor marker CA-125 (41.8 U/mL), and physical constraints that might impede normal uterine growth, were also taken into account.

Given the suspicion of a neoplastic process and the indeterminate nature of the masses, a non-contrast MRI was conducted following the ultrasound to improve diagnostic precision. Current guidelines advise against the use of gadolinium-enhanced MRI during pregnancy except when absolutely necessary, such as in certain rheumatologic, inflammatory, or cutaneous infiltrative disorders, or when there is a concern for stillbirth or neonatal mortality. In keeping with this, the multidisciplinary team opted for an MRI without contrast, restricting the field strength to 1.5 Tesla or less, which is considered relatively safe after the first trimester.

In this case, the multidisciplinary team—including surgeons, gynecologists, and obstetricians—chose to perform a longitudinal laparotomic surgical intervention following formal written informed consent from the patient. During surgical evaluation, the upper lesion was found to have a pedicle, which posed a considerable risk for an acute, characteristic complication. Consequently, this finding informed the decision to restrict the operation to an upper myomectomy. Throughout the procedure, fetal viability was consistently assessed using repeated transvaginal ultrasonography, and the use of intraoperative progesterone for prophylaxis was employed.

A large multicenter investigation by Zhao et al emphasizes the necessity to monitor and record fetal heart rate before, during, and after surgery.

Typically, surgical intervention is postponed until the postpartum period, favoring medical and conservative management. Nevertheless, Sakinci et al recently reported from a tertiary center that there was no statistically significant difference between patients who underwent caesarean section (CS) combined with myomectomy and those who underwent CS alone (without myoma) regarding preoperative and postoperative hemoglobin levels or rates of blood transfusion (p >0.05). However, the group receiving CS with myomectomy experienced longer hospital stays and increased surgery durations (p = 0.001 and p = 0.001, respectively). The average size of myoma was 8.3 ± 4.1 cm, and a statistically significant, positive relationship was found between myoma size and duration of hospitalization (p = 0.01).

The same investigation found a statistically significant negative association between myoma size and the gestational week at delivery (p = 0.035).

Uterine leiomyomas exhibit a non-linear pattern of growth and regression throughout pregnancy and the postpartum period. Specifically, the size of these leiomyomas increases by 6.90% per week: 6.12% (p<0.001) between the first and second trimesters and 3.10%±0.39% during the transition from the second to the third trimester (p<0.05). Thus, their growth rate is markedly greater during the first trimester compared to subsequent weeks of gestation. Conversely, the size of myomas in the third trimester and up to 6 weeks postpartum decreases significantly, with an average reduction of 44.25%±36.05% (p<0.05).

These studies emphasize the necessity for comprehensive ultrasonographic assessment of uterine myomas, including evaluation of their dimensions, position, quantity, and proximity to the placenta prior to surgical intervention.

When uterine myomas are identified in pregnancy, the usual management strategy favors conservative therapy over surgery because of heightened risks such as severe hemorrhage, pregnancy loss, premature labor, and uterine rupture. Nonetheless, myomectomy may be indicated for complicated cases involving torsion of a pedunculated myoma or unresolved symptoms unresponsive to conservative measures. Surgical intervention should be kept brief to maintain pregnancy viability and may be accomplished laparoscopically, ideally using the open Hasson technique when the gestational age is low (within the first or early second trimester), the mass is smaller, and the myoma is located at a site other than intramural. This technique provides improved maternal outcomes and facilitates vaginal delivery. Yet, as highlighted in the current case, there is a frequent bias favoring laparotomy over laparoscopy for larger, multiple, intramural, or high-risk myomas, which may be associated with increased cesarean section rates, especially in instances where a substantial myoma adherent to the posterior aspect of the uterine isthmus may hinder vaginal delivery.
