Title: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

Rosalba De Nola, Emma Bassi, Cataldo Paletta, Mario Vicino

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her 40s with no prior pregnancies presented in her second trimester with abdominal pain and shortness of breath. Ultrasound imaging identified two substantial masses within her abdomen: the superior mass spanned the entire left subphrenic region and extended to the fundus of the uterus, while the inferior mass filled the Douglas pouch. The patient underwent laparotomy, during which the lower pelvic mass was left untouched in situ. She continued with routine antenatal care and ultimately had an elective caesarean section, achieving optimal outcomes for both mother and newborn. For pregnant patients with sizeable, symptomatic masses, thorough assessment by bimanual examination alongside safe imaging techniques, including ultrasound and non-contrast MRI, is crucial. Surgery should only be performed if there is suspicion of malignancy or clinical complications, with care taken to maintain the pregnancy and prevent unnecessary interventions. The delivery method should be based on the mass characteristics—specifically, their size, quantity, and anatomical location.

Background:

Diagnosing and treating atypical or symptomatic abdominal masses in pregnant patients poses considerable clinical difficulties. Available diagnostic modalities include physical examination, ultrasound (US) with color Doppler, and non-contrast magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Preoperative ultrasound is particularly effective for detecting symptomatic uterine leiomyomas situated on the anterior uterine wall in the pregnant uterus, whether submucosal or pedunculated. MRI demonstrates enhanced accuracy for identifying multiple, large, intramural, or posterior fibroids, allowing for precise assessment of their proximity to the uterine cavity.

The utility of serum tumor markers for diagnosis during pregnancy is still uncertain. For example, CA-125 concentrations typically increase in the first trimester and normalize thereafter, rendering single measurements inconclusive; however, serial monitoring may offer helpful trends for differential diagnosis and ongoing surveillance. Management of such cases necessitates thorough multidisciplinary evaluation of risk, weighing obstetric considerations against the uncommon and intricate nature of these presentations.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 40s, with a body mass index of 33 and obstetric history of G1P0A1, presented at 21 weeks gestation with severe, acute pain in the upper left abdomen and pelvis (both rated 5 on the Visual Analogue Scale), along with mild shortness of breath. Previous screening in the first and second trimesters had demonstrated a low probability for aneuploidies (confirmed by non-invasive prenatal testing) and normal fetal anatomy.

Upon arrival at the emergency department, a comprehensive multidisciplinary assessment was conducted. Her oxygen saturation averaged 97%. Thoracic examination revealed a heart rate of 74 bpm and blood pressure of 100/60 mm Hg, both within normal ranges. Electrocardiogram results were unremarkable. She was subsequently moved to the Obstetrical and Gynecological unit. On abdominal examination, the size appeared larger than expected for her gestational age. A tender, tense, elastic mass measuring 18 cm was detected occupying the entire upper left quadrant, with no evidence of ascites.

A bimanual pelvic exam identified a fixed, rounded, and firm mass approximately 9 cm in the lesser pelvis, partially protruding at the posterior fornix.

Symptom control included intravenous paracetamol at 1000 mg twice daily and maintaining a semi-orthostatic position, theorized to reduce diaphragmatic compression caused by the pregnant uterus and sizeable abdominal masses. These measures provided some relief, reducing her pain score from 5 to 1, without the need for supplemental oxygen or ventilation.

During her hospitalization, she developed a mild fever. Testing for severe acute respiratory syndrome Coronavirus 2 via nasopharyngeal swab returned negative, but urine cultures identified a Klebsiella pneumoniae infection. Blood investigations demonstrated iron deficiency anemia, with hemoglobin at 9.9 g/L (reference: 12.0–16.6 g/L) and serum ferritin at 25 mcg/L (reference: 15–300 mcg/L). C reactive protein was mildly elevated at 1.39 mg/dL (reference: 0–0.3 mg/dL). Leukocyte counts were raised, with white blood cells at 11.29×10^3 /uL (reference: 4–10×10^3 /uL) and neutrophils at 9.27×10^3 /uL (reference: 2.8–5.5×10^3 /uL), findings considered compatible with changes seen in pregnancy.

Investigations:

Ultrasound revealed two sizable, single-chamber cystic lesions with smooth borders and vascularization at their periphery. The superior lesion measured 18×12×7 cm and spanned from the left subphrenic area down to the uterine fundus. The inferior lesion measured 9.5×8×8 cm and appeared to be attached to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration (58×71 mm).

Tumor markers including CA 19–9, CA 15–3, CEA, β2-microglobulin, and LDH were all in the normal range, but CA-125 was raised at 41.8 U/mL (normal <35 U/mL), with an upward trend from 36 U/mL measured two weeks earlier. As a result, the patient was referred for a non-contrast MRI, which showed thick walls (1.4 cm), heterogeneous fluid signal on T1 and T2 fat-saturated sequences, and areas of hyperintense solid tissue on both T1 and T2 weighted images (refer to figure 1; only T2 fat-sat images provided). The inferior mass was difficult to delineate and appeared continuous with the posterior leiomyoma previously noted on ultrasound. MRI revealed no evidence of lymphadenopathy.

Differential diagnosis:

On ultrasonography, the cystic lesions closely resembled two large decidualised endometriomas, with the more inferior cyst seemingly attached to an intramural-subserosal leiomyoma located on the posterior wall of the uterus, measuring 58×71 mm.

Magnetic resonance imaging revealed two round, unilocular cysts in the adnexal region, showing hypertrophied blood vessels and heterogeneous internal fluid (refer to figure 1); the lower lesion appeared to be adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma with cystic degeneration, also measuring 58×71 mm.

Owing to the considerable physical size of the gravid uterus and the substantial masses, it was not possible to identify a connecting stalk or any feeding vessel for the upper lesion, leading clinicians to suspect ovarian origin, notably endometrioma, given the mild increase of CA-125 during the second trimester of gestation (see discussion).

Based on the findings from both ultrasound and MRI, the primary considerations were decidualised endometrioma and cystic degeneration of leiomyoma; the slight rise in CA-125 supported endometrioma as the diagnosis for the upper cyst, whereas the apparent continuity with the uterus pointed toward a myomatous cause for the lower mass. The patient's dyspnoea was thought to result from mechanical compression exerted by the large abdominal masses, in the absence of any pulmonary or cardiac abnormality.

Treatment:

Given the progression of her symptoms—abdominal pain from the mass, and dyspnoea secondary to restrictive lung disease caused by the substantial abdominal masses—along with the need to secure a definitive diagnosis, anticipated risk of complications (such as rupture, torsion, or infection), and potential uterine expansion as the fetus developed, the patient was taken to laparotomy via a longitudinal xiphoid-to-pubic incision at 21 weeks and 6 days following informed consent.

Initially, two samples of free peritoneal fluid were obtained for cytological analysis and culture. The abdomen was subsequently examined, confirming ongoing fetal cardiac activity.

Unexpectedly, the upper (cranial) mass consisted of an exceptionally large, pedunculated, cystic and taut-elastic uterine leiomyoma, notable for its atypical appearance and prominent vascularity; the lesion had a twisted pedicle and was leaking, which may have contributed to her fever during admission (refer to figure 2). The surgical team thus proceeded to carefully excise this mass from its uterine origin, employing a double-clamp technique (see figure 2). Following transection of the stalk, the exposed base was meticulously closed with a single absorbable suture. The lower (caudal) mass, located on the uterine torus, was left untouched to reduce obstetric risk, and was found to be immobile, smooth surfaced, and soft, consistent with a large cystic intramural-subserosal leiomyoma.

The operative time was 2 hours and 25 minutes. Importantly, prophylactic progesterone (100 mg intramuscular) was administered intraoperatively, and continued daily (100 mg intramuscular) until discharge. Postoperatively, the patient transitioned to intramuscular caproate hydroxyprogesterone (341 mg in a single vial administered intramuscularly every three days, in accordance with the package information) until the scheduled delivery. Intraoperative antibiotic prophylaxis with cefazolin 2 g intravenously was supplemented by postoperative cefazolin 1 g intravenously three times daily plus clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily during hospitalization, guided by the urine culture antibiogram (Klebsiella pneumoniae highly susceptible to cephalosporins and clarithromycin).

Outcome and follow-up:

Following the operation, the patient’s anaemia worsened (Hb 7.5 g/L, HCT 24.3%), and persistent iron deficiency necessitated administration of intravenous iron (ferric carboxymaltose 1 g) in addition to two successful blood transfusions (Hb rising to 10.0 g/L, HCT 30.4%). She was discharged after 10 days with no complications and began close obstetrical monitoring every 3 weeks, which demonstrated fetal growth (86th percentile per WHO fetal growth chart calculator, accessed at https://srhr.org/fetalgrowthcalculator/#/) and satisfactory amniotic fluid levels.

Testing of peritoneal fluid yielded negative results for both culture and cytology. Histopathological assessment identified benign uterine leiomyomas with zones of ischaemic necrosis adjacent to the twisted pedicle.

The location of the caudal myoma was anticipated to pose significant obstruction to fetal head passage during labor’s expulsive stage. Therefore, a planned caesarean section was carried out using the prior surgical incision once the fetus had reached 37 weeks gestation, thereby lowering the potential for uterine scar rupture and resulting in an excellent neonatal outcome.

Currently, the patient is asymptomatic regarding myoma, despite the residual pelvic myoma having decreased slightly in size to 7.5 cm. She has been successfully breastfeeding but prefers not to schedule further surgical intervention, citing partial postpartum regression of the remaining leiomyomas, lack of symptoms, and her completed motherhood goals. The child is developing well.

Discussion:

Myomas can often be misdiagnosed as adnexal masses: the incidence during pregnancy is 0.3–10.1% and 1–6%, respectively .13

In this case, the initial assessment revealed abdominal masses resembling two large decidualized endometriomas, with the exception of a lower solid portion of the pelvic mass that mimicked a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma with cystic degeneration measuring 58×71 mm on both ultrasonography and MRI. Due to a lack of established guidelines for the management of pelvic masses during pregnancy, the multidisciplinary team determined that elective surgery might reduce the likelihood of preterm labor.

Serum CA-125 levels typically present with two peaks during pregnancy: the first in the first trimester (median 23 U/mL, p<0.00001) and the second in the third trimester (median 21 U/mL, p<0.001), both higher in comparison to the median value of 14.0 U/mL observed in the second trimester. Conversely, this patient demonstrated a CA-125 level of 41.8 U/mL, which surpasses the median value expected in the second trimester.

CA-125 is primarily utilized as a tumor marker in ovarian carcinoma (with a commonly accepted threshold of ≥35 U/mL), particularly in women older than 50 years, where it exhibits improved diagnostic metrics (area under the curve, positive predictive value, sensitivity, and specificity). However, elevated CA-125 is also found in various benign conditions including endometriosis and adenomyosis (>19 U/mL), pelvic inflammatory disease, menstruation, early and late pregnancy (median 23 and 21 U/mL, respectively), increased miscarriage risk, as well as in cases of myoma (cut-off <19 U/mL).57

The main factors influencing the clinical approach included the presence of large, highly vascularized masses displaying both solid and cystic elements, which carried an immediate risk of complications such as rupture, torsion, or infection. The patient’s worsening symptoms, including increasing pain and dyspnoea, raised further concern, as did the potential for the masses to enlarge, raising the likelihood of hypoxia, necrosis, or degeneration. Additional considerations were the mildly elevated oncological marker (CA-125=41.8 U/mL), and limited intra-abdominal space potentially impeding normal uterine growth.

Given the possibility of a neoplastic origin and the indeterminate nature of the masses, an MRI without contrast was conducted to improve diagnostic precision following the ultrasound. Current guidelines advise against gadolinium contrast in MRI during pregnancy except when strictly indicated for rheumatologic, inflammatory, or infiltrative skin disorders, or in cases with a risk of stillbirth or neonatal death. Therefore, the multidisciplinary team opted for a non-contrast MRI, refraining from using scanners with strengths greater than 1.5-T, which is deemed sufficiently safe beyond the first trimester.

In this case, the multidisciplinary team—including surgeons, gynecologists, and obstetricians—chose a longitudinal laparotomic approach after securing specific written informed consent from the patient. Intraoperatively, the superior lesion was found to have a pedicle, posing a substantial risk for an acute, classic complication. This concern was the basis for restricting the surgical intervention to an upper myomectomy only. During the procedure, ongoing and intermittent transvaginal ultrasounds were used to continuously assess fetal viability, complemented by intraoperative administration of prophylactic progesterone.

As reported in a large multicenter study by Zhao et al, monitoring and documentation of fetal heart rate before, throughout, and after surgery is imperative.

Surgical treatment is typically postponed until after delivery, with preference given to medical or expectant management, although a recent study conducted by Sakinci et al in a tertiary hospital found no statistically significant differences in preoperative and postoperative hemoglobin levels or rates of blood transfusion (p >0.05) between women who underwent cesarean section (CS) with concurrent myomectomy and those undergoing CS alone without myomas. However, those in the CS with myomectomy group experienced notably longer hospital stays and operative times (p = 0.001 for both outcomes). The average myoma diameter was 8.3 ± 4.1 cm, and there was a statistically significant positive association between myoma size and length of hospitalization (p = 0.01).

The same study found a statistically significant inverse relationship between myoma size and the gestational age at delivery (p = 0.035).

Uterine leiomyomas demonstrate a non-linear pattern of size fluctuation throughout pregnancy and the postpartum period. Specifically, leiomyomas expand by 6.90% weekly, with a growth rate of 6.12% (p<0.001) from the first to the second trimester and 3.10%±0.39% from the second to the third trimester (p<0.05). Thus, leiomyomas exhibit more pronounced enlargement during the initial trimester compared to later stages of pregnancy. Conversely, during the third trimester and up to six weeks postpartum, myoma size significantly diminishes (mean reduction of 44.25%±36.05%, p<0.05).

These publications underline the necessity for precise ultrasonographic assessment of uterine myomas, including evaluation of their size, position, quantity, and proximity to the placenta, prior to planning surgical intervention.

When uterine myomas are identified during pregnancy, the standard approach favors conservative management over surgery due to potential risks such as severe hemorrhage, miscarriage, preterm delivery, and uterine rupture. Nevertheless, myomectomy may be warranted in scenarios involving complicated masses, such as torsion of a pedunculated myoma or ongoing symptoms that are unresponsive to conservative measures. The surgical procedure should be kept brief to safeguard the pregnancy and may be performed via laparoscopy, optimally utilizing the open Hasson technique when gestational age is lower (first and early second trimester), the mass is smaller, and the myoma’s location is not intramural. This method results in improved maternal outcomes and facilitates vaginal delivery. Still, this case illustrates a typical selection bias favoring laparotomy over laparoscopy for larger, multiple, intramural, or critically located myomas. This preference is associated with an increased likelihood of cesarean section, as in the present case involving a large myoma adherent to the posterior isthmic region of the uterus, potentially impeding vaginal birth.
