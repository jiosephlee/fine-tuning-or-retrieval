Title: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

Rosalba De Nola, Emma Bassi, Cataldo Paletta, Mario Vicino

Abstract:

A nulliparous woman in her fourth decade presented in her second trimester with abdominal discomfort and shortness of breath. Ultrasonography identified two sizable abdominal masses: the superior mass encompassed the entire left subphrenic region and extended up to the uterine fundus, while the inferior mass occupied the pouch of Douglas. The patient underwent a laparotomy, during which the lower mass was retained within the pelvic cavity. She was subsequently followed with routine obstetric care and ultimately underwent a planned caesarean section, resulting in favorable outcomes for both mother and neonate. For pregnant patients with large, symptomatic masses, a thorough evaluation utilizing bimanual examination and safe imaging methods such as ultrasonography and non-contrast MRI is critical. Surgical intervention should be reserved for situations of suspected malignancy or clinical complications, with the overarching goal to maintain pregnancy and prevent unnecessary procedures. Decisions regarding delivery method should be tailored to the mass's size, quantity, and anatomical position.

Background:

Diagnosing and treating atypical or symptomatic abdominal masses in pregnant patients pose considerable clinical difficulties. Available diagnostic tools include physical examination, ultrasound (US) with color Doppler, and non-contrast magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Preoperative US is especially helpful for localizing symptomatic uterine leiomyomas situated on the anterior surface of the enlarged uterus, whether submucosal or pedunculated. MRI is more accurate in characterizing multiple, sizeable, intramural, or posterior leiomyomas, as it can precisely establish their relationship to the uterine cavity.

The utility of serum tumor markers during pregnancy is still under investigation. For instance, CA-125 levels naturally increase in early pregnancy before returning to normal; thus, a single value lacks diagnostic significance, while repeated measurements may assist in differential diagnosis and monitoring. Management of these cases should involve thorough multidisciplinary evaluation to appropriately weigh obstetric risks against the infrequent and complex nature of such presentations.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 40s, who was obese with a body mass index of 33 and had a history of G1P0A1, presented during her second trimester (at 21 weeks of gestation) with severe, sudden abdominal pain localized to the upper left quadrant and the pelvis (Visual Analogue Scale score of 5 at both sites), along with mild shortness of breath. Routine screening in her first and second trimesters indicated a low risk for aneuploidy, which was also corroborated by non-invasive prenatal testing, and fetal morphology was normal.

She was initially evaluated in the emergency department through a multidisciplinary approach: her oxygen saturation was within normal limits (median 97%), thoracic examination found a regular heart rate (median 74 bpm) and blood pressure (median 100/60 mm Hg). The ECG did not reveal any abnormalities. Subsequently, she was referred to the Obstetrical and Gynaecological Division. Her abdomen appeared distended beyond what would be expected for her gestational age, and on examination, there was a tender swelling and a firm, elastic mass filling the upper left quadrant, measuring 18 cm, without evidence of ascites.

Upon bimanual vaginal examination, a fixed mass was noted in the lesser pelvis, approximately 9 cm in size, rounded and firm, partially protruding into the posterior fornix.

She was treated with intravenous paracetamol at 1000 mg twice daily and positioned semi-orthostatically, which was theorized to help reduce diaphragmatic compression from the uterus and large abdominal masses. These measures slightly alleviated her symptoms, and her pain decreased from 5 to 1 on the Visual Analogue Scale, without requiring additional oxygen or ventilatory support.

During her admission, she developed a low-grade fever. SARS-CoV-2 PCR from a nasopharyngeal swab was performed and returned negative, but urine cultures identified a Klebsiella pneumoniae infection. Laboratory studies showed iron deficiency anemia (haemoglobin 9.9 g/L [reference range: 12.0–16.6 g/L], serum ferritin 25 mcg/L [reference range: 15–300 mcg/L]), mild elevation of C-reactive protein at 1.39 mg/dL (reference range: 0–0.3 mg/dL), and an increased neutrophil count (white blood cells 11.29×103 /uL [normal: 4–10×103 /uL], neutrophils 9.27×103 /uL [normal: 2.8–5.5×103 /uL]), all consistent with changes related to pregnancy.

Investigations:

Ultrasound revealed two sizable, unilocular cystic lesions with smooth borders and vascularity located at their periphery. The superior lesion was 18×12×7 cm in size, stretching from the left subphrenic space to the uterine fundus. The inferior lesion measured 9.5×8×8 cm and appeared to be attached to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma undergoing cystic degeneration (58×71 mm).

Levels of tumor markers, including CA 19–9, CA 15–3, CEA, β2-microglobulin, and LDH, were within the reference range; however, CA-125 was elevated at 41.8 U/mL (normal <35 U/mL), representing a rise from 36 U/mL observed two weeks prior. Thus, there was a mild upward trend in the serum CA-125 concentration. An MRI was subsequently performed without contrast, revealing lesions with thickened walls (1.4 cm), heterogenous fluid content on T1 and T2 fat-saturated sequences, and areas of focal hyperintensity on both T1- and T2-weighted solid components (refer to figure 1; only T2 fat-sat images are provided). The separation between the lower lesion and adjacent structures could not be definitively determined, and the lesion seemed continuous with the previously assessed posterior leiomyoma on ultrasound. No lymphadenopathy was detected by MRI.

Differential diagnosis:

During the ultrasonographic assessment, the cystic lesions resembled two large endometriomas that had undergone decidualisation; additionally, the lower cyst was observed to be adherent to an intramural-subserosal leiomyoma located on the posterior wall of the uterus, which measured 58×71 mm.

Magnetic resonance imaging identified two rounded, single-chamber adnexal cysts with prominent vessels and uneven internal fluid (refer to figure 1). The lower lesion appeared attached to a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration, with dimensions of 58×71 mm.

Because of the spatial constraints imposed by the gravid uterus and the substantial size of the masses, neither a connecting stalk nor a vessel supplying the tumor could be visualized for the upper lesion. This finding supported an ovarian origin for the upper mass, likely an endometrioma, particularly given the mild rise in CA-125 observed during the second trimester of pregnancy (see discussion).

The imaging features from ultrasonography and MRI led to consideration of decidualised endometriomas and cystic degenerating leiomyomas in the differential diagnosis. The modest increase in CA-125 supported endometrioma for the upper cyst, whereas the attachment to uterine tissue indicated that the lower mass was likely myomatous. Shortness of breath was ascribed to direct compression by the sizable abdominal masses, and there was no evidence of cardiopulmonary involvement.

Treatment:

Given the progression of the patient's symptoms—including abdominal discomfort related to mass effect and shortness of breath from restrictive lung disease attributed to the growing abdominal masses—along with the need for a definitive diagnosis, increased risk for adverse events (such as rupture, torsion, and infection), and anticipated uterine expansion with fetal development, a laparotomy was performed at 21 weeks and 6 days gestation following informed consent, utilizing a vertical xiphoid-to-pubic incision.

Initially, two specimens of peritoneal fluid were obtained for both culture and cytological assessment. The surgical team then proceeded to examine the intra-abdominal cavity, confirming fetal cardiac activity.

Unexpectedly, the upper (cranial) mass proved to be a large, pedunculated, atypical cystic and taut-elastic uterine leiomyoma with marked vascularity, initially found to be twisted and leaking, potentially explaining the patient’s in-hospital fever (refer to figure 2). Consequently, the surgeons carefully resected the mass from its uterine attachment using double clamps (see figure 2). After transecting the pedicle, the exposed base was closed securely with a single absorbable suture. The lower (caudal) mass, originating from the uterine torus, was left untouched to avoid increased obstetric risk; it was firmly fixed, with a smooth surface and soft consistency reminiscent of a large cystic intramural-subserosal leiomyoma.

The procedure took 2 hours and 25 minutes. The patient was given progesterone prophylaxis with 100 mg administered intramuscularly during surgery and continued daily at the same dose until discharge. After discharge, maintenance therapy involved intramuscular hydroxyprogesterone caproate (341 mg, 1 ampule every three days according to package instructions) up to the planned delivery. Intraoperative antibiotic prophylaxis consisted of cefazolin 2 g intravenously, followed by postoperative cefazolin 1 g intravenously three times daily combined with clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily, chosen per the antibiogram of urine culture (indicating Klebsiella pneumoniae highly susceptible to cephalosporins and clarithromycin), throughout the hospitalization.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following the operation, progressive anaemia (Hb 7.5 g/L, HCT 24.3%) combined with ongoing iron deficiency necessitated intravenous administration of iron (ferric carboxymaltose 1 g) along with two successful blood transfusions (resulting in Hb 10.0 g/L, HCT 30.4%). The patient was discharged after 10 days without complications, then underwent close obstetrical surveillance every 3 weeks. Monitoring demonstrated fetal growth at the 86th percentile per the WHO fetal growth chart calculator (https://srhr.org/fetalgrowthcalculator/#/) as well as measurement of the amniotic fluid index.

Testing of the peritoneal fluid was negative for both microbiological cultures and cytological analysis. Histopathological assessment identified benign uterine leiomyomas and zones of ischemic necrosis adjacent to the twisted pedicle.

The inferior myoma would have significantly impeded fetal head descent during the second stage of labor. Therefore, an elective caesarean section was performed using the prior surgical incision upon the fetus achieving 37 weeks of gestation, thereby reducing the risk of uterine scar rupture and yielding an excellent neonatal outcome.

The patient currently does not experience any symptoms associated with the myoma, though the residual pelvic myoma has decreased slightly in size to 7.5 cm. She is breastfeeding successfully and has chosen not to schedule further surgery, given the partial postpartum reduction in the leiomyomas, the absence of symptoms, and having achieved motherhood. The child is thriving.

Discussion:

Myomas can often be misdiagnosed as adnexal masses: the incidence during pregnancy is 0.3–10.1% and 1–6%, respectively .13

In this case, initial assessment revealed large abdominal masses that resembled two substantial decidualized endometriomas, with the exception of a lower, solid portion of the pelvic mass that had imaging characteristics consistent with a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration, measuring 58×71 mm on both ultrasound and MRI. Due to the lack of specific guidelines for managing pelvic masses during pregnancy, a multidisciplinary team determined that planned surgical intervention might reduce the likelihood of preterm birth.

Typically, during gestation, serum CA-125 levels display two distinct elevations: one during the first trimester (median 23 U/mL, p<0.00001) and another in the third trimester (median 21 U/mL, p<0.001) relative to second trimester values (median 14.0 U/mL). By contrast, this patient's level was 41.8 U/mL, surpassing the median value observed during the second trimester.

While CA-125 is predominantly utilized as a tumor marker for ovarian malignancy (commonly employing a threshold of ≥35 U/mL), particularly in women over age 50 where diagnostic performance metrics are superior, elevated CA-125 concentrations may also be observed in several benign disorders. These include endometriosis and adenomyosis (commonly >19 U/mL), pelvic inflammatory disease, menstrual periods, both early and late stages of pregnancy (median 23 and 21 U/mL, respectively), increased miscarriage risk, and the presence of myomas (generally with levels <19 U/mL).57

The clinical approach was guided by several factors: the identification of large, highly vascular masses exhibiting both solid and cystic characteristics, which carried a tangible risk of complications such as rupture, torsion, or infection; the patient’s deteriorating clinical state marked by increasing pain and dyspnoea; the likelihood of mass enlargement, further raising the possibility of hypoxia, necrosis, and degeneration; mildly elevated tumor markers (CA-125=41.8 U/mL); and limited intra-abdominal space which could hinder normal uterine expansion.

Given the uncertain nature and potential neoplastic origin of the masses, after the initial ultrasound assessment, the patient underwent a non-contrast MRI to enhance diagnostic precision. Current guidelines advise against gadolinium-enhanced MRI during pregnancy except in situations of strict necessity, such as rheumatologic, inflammatory, or infiltrative skin diseases, or risk of stillbirth or neonatal death. Consequently, the multidisciplinary team opted for a non-contrast MRI, using no more than 1.5-Tesla, which is considered relatively safe in the period following the first trimester.

In this case, a multidisciplinary team consisting of surgeons, gynecologists, and obstetricians decided to pursue longitudinal laparotomic surgery after acquiring explicit written informed consent from the patient. During intraoperative evaluation, the upper lesion demonstrated a pedicle, posing a substantial risk for an acute, characteristic complication. Consequently, the surgical intervention was restricted to upper myomectomy. Fetal viability was monitored during the procedure by performing serial and intermittent transvaginal ultrasonography, and intraoperative progesterone was administered prophylactically.

A multicenter study by Zhao et al emphasizes the necessity of recording fetal heart rate before, during, and following the operation.

Typically, surgical intervention is postponed until after delivery, favoring medical and expectant management. Nonetheless, Sakinci et al recently conducted a study at a tertiary center which found no significant difference in preoperative and postoperative hemoglobin levels or rates of blood transfusion (p >0.05) between women undergoing cesarean section (CS) with myomectomy and those without myomas undergoing CS alone. However, the CS myomectomy group experienced significantly longer surgery and hospitalization durations (p = 0.001 for both). The average myoma size was 8.3 ± 4.1cm, with a statistically significant positive association between myoma size and length of hospital stay (p = 0.01).

The same study demonstrated a statistically significant negative association between myoma size and gestational week at delivery (p = 0.035).

Uterine leiomyomas exhibit a non-linear pattern in size variation during pregnancy and postpartum. Specifically, leiomyoma size increases weekly by 6.90%, with a 6.12% rise (p<0.001) between the first and second trimesters, and a 3.10%±0.39% increment from the second to third trimesters (p<0.05). Thus, the rate of growth is notably greater during the first trimester compared to subsequent gestational periods. Conversely, during the third trimester and within 6 weeks post-delivery, myomas show a significant reduction in size, with a mean decrease of 44.25%±36.05% (p<0.05).

These articles underscore the necessity for thorough ultrasonographic evaluation of uterine myomas, including size, position, quantity, and proximity to the placenta, prior to surgical intervention.

When uterine myomas are identified during pregnancy, the standard practice is conservative management rather than surgery, due to elevated risks of heavy hemorrhage, loss of pregnancy, preterm delivery, and uterine rupture. Myomectomy is considered for cases involving complicated lesions such as torsion of a pedunculated myoma or persistent symptoms unresponsive to conservative therapy. The surgical procedure should be minimized to protect the pregnancy, and can be performed laparoscopically, ideally using the open Hasson method in situations involving earlier gestational ages (first and early second trimesters), smaller masses, and myomas in specific, non-intramural locations. This technique is associated with improved maternal morbidity outcomes and higher rates of vaginal delivery. Nonetheless, the current case highlights a frequent selection bias favoring laparotomy over laparoscopy when confronting large, multiple, intramural, or critically situated myomas, which may correspond to increased caesarean section rates, particularly when a sizable myoma is attached to the posterior wall at the isthmic region of the uterus, potentially impeding vaginal birth.
