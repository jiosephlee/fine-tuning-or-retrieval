Title: Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting

Margret Ann Schneider, Hans de Groot, Jan-Willem Geerts

Abstract:

Summary: Anaphylaxis, recognized as the most serious type of allergic reaction, poses significant risks and demands immediate and effective intervention. A subset of patients experience a return of symptoms after apparent resolution, even following appropriate treatment and complete initial recovery. These biphasic or multiphasic responses present distinct difficulties for healthcare providers, as the frequency is inconsistently reported, risk factors remain uncertain, and established treatment protocols are not outlined in current recommendations. The challenge is especially pronounced in cases of multiphasic anaphylaxis, where repeated episodes occur despite effective therapy with epinephrine. Since multiphasic anaphylaxis is infrequent, individual case reports detailing management approaches serve as valuable resources for understanding this issue. Here, we describe a case involving a woman in her 60s who developed multiphasic anaphylaxis triggered by clarithromycin; further evaluation indicated a strong possibility of systemic mastocytosis, although formal diagnostic criteria were not fulfilled.

Background:

Anaphylaxis represents a severe allergic response, particularly dangerous when accompanied by anaphylactic shock, and is frequently responsible for emergency department visits. A proportion of anaphylaxis cases exhibit biphasic or even multiphasic patterns, which entail an initial reaction to the allergen, followed by symptom remission—resulting in a symptom-free interval—and subsequently recurrence of clinical manifestations without additional exposure to the inciting antigen. Typically, this recurrence occurs within 24 hours of the original allergen exposure, although instances of symptom return have been reported as late as 78 hours afterwards. Estimates of biphasic anaphylaxis frequency are variable, ranging from 0.4% to 23.3%; however, the majority of research indicates rates between 4% and 6%. Given the risk for relapse, ongoing clinical monitoring after anaphylaxis is often recommended, even once initial symptoms have subsided. Nonetheless, the definition of biphasic anaphylaxis remains inconsistent, and management recommendations—including the use of corticosteroids—have been debated recently. Multiphasic anaphylaxis, characterized by multiple recurrences of symptoms, poses unique challenges for both observation strategies and therapeutic approaches. Here, we describe the case of a female patient in her sixth decade of life who experienced multiphasic anaphylaxis triggered by clarithromycin.

Case presentation:

A female patient in her 60s, who has a documented allergy to beta-lactam antibiotics, arrived at the emergency department with an episode of anaphylaxis subsequent to ingesting oral clarithromycin 500 mg.

Her past medical history comprised type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, psoriasis, and depression. She weighed 104 kg. The medications she was taking included metformin (500 mg twice daily), gliclazide (80 mg extended-release once daily), hydrochlorothiazide (25 mg once daily), omeprazole (20 mg once daily), and sertraline (100 mg once daily). She had last taken hydrochlorothiazide in the morning, over 12 hours prior to her ED visit.

In her adolescence, she had experienced a mild allergic response to amoxicillin manifesting as facial swelling, without any associated oropharyngeal edema, rash, hypotension, or dyspnea. Additionally, she had previously exhibited a pronounced local skin reaction to a wasp sting, but had no systemic symptoms. The present episode marked her initial occurrence of anaphylaxis.

Five days prior to presenting at the ED, her general practitioner prescribed clindamycin for erysipelas. After completing 5 days of clindamycin treatment, she developed an abdominal rash (details not specified), which was presumed to be a delayed allergic reaction to clindamycin. Subsequently, her general practitioner changed her antibiotic regimen to clarithromycin, a drug she had not previously received.

She later arrived at the emergency department experiencing pruritus, flushing, excessive sweating, restlessness, and a sensation of throat swelling. These symptoms began one hour following ingestion of her first clarithromycin dose, with a progressive worsening over time. She presented to healthcare providers one hour after the onset of symptoms. On arrival, her vital signs revealed hypotension and hypoxemia (non-invasive blood pressure: 74/50 mm Hg, oxygen saturation at 88% on ambient air, respiratory rate of 22 breaths per minute). Physical assessment identified facial swelling, newly developed widespread erythema and urticaria across her body, as well as increased sweating and agitation. There was no evidence of tongue or throat swelling, and both cardiac and pulmonary auscultation findings were unremarkable. Blood glucose measured 12.7 mmol/L and electrocardiogram demonstrated sinus rhythm. Immediate management for presumed severe allergic reaction was initiated, involving intramuscular administration of 0.3 mg epinephrine via auto-injector and a slow intravenous dose of 2 mg clemastine to address cutaneous manifestations per local protocols. Additional interventions included supplemental oxygen at 15 L per minute through a non-rebreather mask and 500 mL of 0.9% sodium chloride (normal saline) for initial stabilization. Supplemental oxygen was discontinued after the patient's favorable response to epinephrine, but was required again during subsequent episodes of anaphylaxis. Clarithromycin treatment was ceased, and no further doses were administered after the initial oral 500 mg dose.

At first, the patient responded well to the initial therapy, with normalization of blood pressure and reduction in facial edema and erythema. She was admitted to the acute internal medicine ward, where enhanced observation by experienced nursing staff was available, albeit without telemetry monitoring. Despite this promising response, the patient experienced repeated episodes indicative of anaphylactic shock within the first 12 hours after admission. Following the first recurrence, she was transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) for closer observation. Each anaphylactic episode was treated with intramuscular epinephrine (see figure 1), totaling four events including the original presentation in the emergency department. The initial two doses of epinephrine were delivered via auto-injector, each containing 0.3 mg administered intramuscularly, and the subsequent two doses were provided as individually prepared 0.5 mg intramuscular injections. Although the epinephrine dose in the auto-injectors at our facility is below the recommended 0.5 mg, we prioritize auto-injector use in urgent situations, such as during initial ED stabilization of new patients, or in contexts where anaphylaxis treatment is infrequent and hesitation due to unfamiliarity might cause delays. 

Corticosteroid therapy was added after the first recurrence, reflecting the traditional use of steroids to prevent biphasic anaphylactic reactions. She was given 30 mg prednisone intravenously once daily for five days, as well as a single intravenous dose of 8 mg dexamethasone, with the selection reflecting varying recommendations between our hospital's general ward and intensive care anaphylaxis protocols. In addition, she received two intravenous doses of 2 mg clemastine for cutaneous symptoms, and 500 mL of normal saline was administered during the second anaphylactic event. Notably, symptom severity diminished with each subsequent episode, initially presenting as profound hypoxemia accompanied by cyanosis, hypotension, and severe dyspnea, and eventually progressing to episodes characterized primarily by dyspnea, recurrent rash, and pruritus.

Early consultation with the allergology department was sought for expert advice regarding management. Two possible therapeutic strategies were considered: continuous intravenous infusion of epinephrine to preemptively address anaphylaxis, as opposed to reacting to symptom recurrence; or, given the gradual reduction in symptom severity, a watchful waiting approach to assess whether symptoms would abate on their own. Both strategies posed considerable risks, especially given the scarcity of literature addressing the treatment of multiphasic anaphylactic presentations.

Outcome and follow-up:

In the end, neither intervention was required as the patient experienced no further recurrence of symptoms. After about 24 hours without additional anaphylactic episodes, she was moved back to a regular care ward. She was released from the hospital following a total of 3 days of admission. The patient was initiated on levocetirizine as her new routine antihistamine and was provided with an epinephrine auto-injector (0.3 mg), along with instruction on its proper use. There have been no subsequent instances of anaphylaxis.

Given the unusual and extended presentation, further investigations into potential underlying etiologies were undertaken. These studies indicated a strong likelihood of systemic mastocytosis; however, this finding fell short of the criteria for definitive diagnosis, as only two minor WHO criteria were fulfilled and the major criterion could not be evaluated because of insufficient bone marrow biopsy material (see figure 2). During the third anaphylactic event and at baseline, tryptase measurements were elevated, and levels remained persistently high on follow-up conducted by the allergologist, as detailed in table 1.

Regrettably, tryptase measurements were not obtained during the initial evaluation following the patient’s early anaphylactic reactions, resulting in non-compliance with established time points for tryptase assessment. Current guidelines advise collection of at least two, and preferably three, tryptase samples at particular time frames. The first sample should be drawn as soon as possible without impeding emergency management, with a subsequent sample collected 1–2 hours (and within 4 hours) after symptom onset—corresponding to the period of anticipated peak tryptase concentration—and a third sample obtained at least 24 hours following the complete resolution of clinical manifestations to determine baseline levels. For this patient, the initial tryptase specimen was acquired 1 hour following the third anaphylactic event, and the subsequent sample was drawn 27 hours after the most recent anaphylactic episode.

No confirmatory allergy testing was performed, as the potential risk of provoking another severe anaphylactic reaction via procedures such as skin testing was judged unwarranted given the relatively straightforward avoidance of further exposure. Additional findings consistent with systemic mastocytosis in this case included hepatomegaly observed on abdominal ultrasound and symptoms possibly related to neuropsychiatric involvement, such as depression and memory difficulties.

Discussion:

Anaphylaxis is recognized as a clinical entity with clear diagnostic criteria, marked by a sudden appearance and rapid escalation of symptoms that may affect the airway, respiratory or cardiovascular systems, and/or gastrointestinal tract, often associated with dermatological or mucosal signs such as urticaria, angioedema, or flushing. Although identifying an inciting allergen can reinforce the diagnosis, determining the exact trigger is not always feasible.

Presentations including biphasic or refractory anaphylaxis are less clearly characterized, though there have been recent initiatives to create standardized definitions. According to the Resuscitation Council UK, biphasic anaphylaxis describes the reappearance of symptoms hours after initial resolution, without additional exposure to the allergen. Distinguishing authentic biphasic responses from extended anaphylaxis due to a temporary benefit from epinephrine or ongoing allergen presence—such as persistent absorption from incompletely digested food or long-acting medication—remains complex. On the other hand, refractory anaphylaxis is defined as continuous symptoms that are unresponsive, or only minimally responsive, to correctly dosed and administered epinephrine.

Current guidance for biphasic anaphylaxis primarily focuses on patient observation; however, the ideal duration for post-resolution monitoring remains uncertain. Conventional practice suggests monitoring for 4 to 6 hours after symptoms abate. The guidelines from the Nederlands Huisartsen Genootschap (NHG) and the World Allergy Organisation mention that nearly 50% of biphasic events develop within 6–12 hours following the initial reaction, yet neither issues a specific observation protocol. Unlike these, the 2021 UK guideline recommends a three-level, risk-adapted observation strategy. Establishing a unified approach to monitoring is necessary to address the challenge of allocating clinical resources effectively—given the large population of single-event anaphylaxis cases—while ensuring adequate protection for the minority of patients at risk for unpredictable biphasic reactions.

Guidance regarding advanced medication approaches for biphasic and multiphasic anaphylaxis episodes is conspicuously insufficient. The management of these complex presentations generally aligns with conventional anaphylaxis therapy, relying on rapid administration of intramuscular epinephrine and suitable supportive measures. Current guidelines suggest a dosage of 0.01 mg/kg body weight for epinephrine, with a ceiling of 0.5 mg. As most adults weigh more than 50 kg, this is routinely rounded up to 0.5 mg for adult patients. Nonetheless, it is noteworthy that many commercially available auto-injectors—including those administered during the initial two anaphylactic events in the case discussed—deliver only 0.3 mg doses, even when labeled for adult use. While epinephrine auto-injectors are integral in prehospital care, their use in clinical settings can also decrease treatment delay and lower hesitancy among providers who infrequently administer vasoactive agents. However, administering a 0.3 mg auto-injector, though widespread, can result in insufficient dosing for adults exceeding 50 kg. According to a 2025 retrospective analysis, individuals who first received 0.3 mg of epinephrine required escalated intervention—including additional doses—at a significantly higher rate compared to those initially treated with 0.5 mg. Addressing obstacles such as clinician familiarity with the conventional 0.3 mg dose and the prevalence of 0.3 mg auto-injectors, as well as promoting adherence to guideline-based dosing, could substantially enhance outcomes in anaphylaxis management.

Historically, corticosteroids were utilized partly to avert biphasic reactions. Nevertheless, accumulating data indicate not only a lack of efficacy but also possible adverse outcomes, prompting a shift in practice away from administering steroids during anaphylaxis. A systematic review published in 2017 evaluated 22 studies—only one supported corticosteroid use, while 21 provided no evidence for a reduction in biphasic anaphylaxis risk. Additionally, emerging studies highlight the importance of adverse effects; a 2019 investigation found that prehospital corticosteroid therapy correlated with a greater probability of ICU or inpatient admission. As a result, current guidelines largely discourage routine steroid administration, though their use remains prevalent in clinical settings.

Biphasic anaphylaxis occurs infrequently yet is of significant clinical importance. The challenge of risk stratification and appropriate monitoring stems from the lack of dependable predictors. Proposed risk factors include delayed administration of epinephrine, severe manifestations at presentation, multiple doses of epinephrine, and previous biphasic episodes, but truly reliable markers enabling selective surveillance are still absent.

Systemic mastocytosis, which is characterized by an abnormal proliferation of mast cells, increased mediator release, and widespread organ inflammation, increases susceptibility to severe anaphylaxis.^1517 However, due to the uncommon nature of both diseases, existing literature has not specifically examined the impact of systemic mastocytosis on biphasic anaphylaxis. Further investigation into mast cell disorders and their relationship with biphasic anaphylaxis may contribute valuable information for future therapeutic strategies.

Anaphylaxis-related deaths are infrequent, particularly in areas where emergency care is readily accessible. Moreover, fatal cases of biphasic anaphylaxis are even more uncommon, despite their seemingly high risk. Nevertheless, anaphylaxis continues to represent a frequent acute condition that requires immediate intervention and proper observation. Although research and clinical guidelines predominantly emphasize the initial emergency response, post-acute management and observation remain crucial aspects of patient care. Improved insight into more complicated anaphylaxis presentations, such as biphasic anaphylaxis, may facilitate the development of more focused, individualized, and efficient management protocols.

Developing robust, evidence-based strategies is especially difficult due to the infrequent occurrence of biphasic and multiphasic anaphylaxis, as well as the obstacles associated with conducting studies in emergency contexts. In such scenarios, expert-driven consensus guidelines that include explicit recommendations are invaluable. Still, even expert consensus can be limited by insufficient data. Considering the scarcity of these multifaceted anaphylaxis cases, creating a registry to collect details on patient characteristics, proposed management strategies—as demonstrated in this case report—and clinical outcomes could serve as a foundation for forming evidence-informed expert guidelines.
