Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting

Margret Ann Schneider, Hans de Groot, Jan-Willem Geerts

Abstract:

Summary: Anaphylaxis represents the most acute manifestation of allergic responses and constitutes a potentially fatal systemic condition that demands swift and effective intervention. In certain instances, patients may experience a recurrence of symptoms even after having initially achieved complete clinical resolution and receiving appropriate treatment. Such biphasic or multiphasic patterns pose significant difficulties for clinicians, in part because reported frequencies vary, associated risk factors remain poorly defined, and existing clinical guidelines do not offer definitive management approaches. These challenges are particularly pronounced in cases of multiphasic anaphylaxis, where individuals undergo repeated episodes of anaphylaxis following an adequate therapeutic response to standard interventions, such as epinephrine. Given the rarity of multiphasic anaphylaxis, detailed case reports serve as a valuable source of practical information to guide clinical decision-making in these scenarios. In this report, we describe a case involving a woman in her sixth decade who developed multiphasic anaphylaxis in response to clarithromycin; subsequent investigation indicated a strong probability of systemic mastocytosis, although she did not fulfill the established diagnostic criteria for the condition.

Background:

Anaphylaxis constitutes a serious, and in the case of anaphylactic shock, potentially fatal hypersensitivity reaction that frequently prompts patients to seek emergency medical attention. In a proportion of cases, the clinical progression can be biphasic or even multiphasic, featuring an initial allergic response to an inciting agent, subsequent remission of clinical symptoms resulting in an interval free of manifestations, and then a resurgence of symptoms in the absence of further allergen contact. Symptom relapse typically occurs within 24 hours of the original allergen encounter, though literature also describes recurrence up to 78 hours afterwards. Published estimates for biphasic anaphylaxis demonstrate substantial variability, with incidence rates reported from 0.4\% to 23.3\%; nonetheless, the majority of evidence suggests the true incidence is likely between 4\% and 6\%. Because of the risk of symptom return, clinicians generally recommend a monitoring period after anaphylaxis, even if the initial clinical features have abated completely. Nevertheless, the precise criteria defining biphasic anaphylaxis lack uniformity across studies, and management guidelines are not universally aligned, particularly regarding the efficacy and appropriateness of corticosteroid therapy—a topic subject to recent scrutiny. Cases of multiphasic anaphylaxis, where several waves of symptom recurrence occur, pose additional complexities concerning appropriate management and observation strategies. Here, we discuss the clinical scenario of a female patient in her 60s who exhibited a multiphasic anaphylactic reaction following exposure to clarithromycin.

Case presentation:

A female patient in her sixties, with a documented hypersensitivity to beta-lactam antibiotics, arrived at the emergency department (ED) exhibiting anaphylactic symptoms after ingesting a 500 mg oral dose of clarithromycin.

Her past medical history was notable for type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, psoriasis, and depression. She weighed 104 kg. The patient’s chronic medications included metformin (500 mg twice daily), gliclazide (80 mg modified-release once daily), hydrochlorothiazide (25 mg once daily), omeprazole (20 mg once daily), and sertraline (100 mg once daily). The most recent dose of hydrochlorothiazide had been administered in the morning, over 12 hours prior to her arrival at the ED.

Previously, in adolescence, she experienced a minor allergic response to amoxicillin characterized by facial edema but not accompanied by oropharyngeal swelling, cutaneous eruption, hypotension, or respiratory distress. Additionally, she had developed a pronounced localized skin reaction to a wasp sting at another time, without any systemic involvement. Importantly, the present clinical event marked her initial encounter with anaphylaxis.

Five days prior to presenting at the ED, her general practitioner initiated treatment with clindamycin for erysipelas. After completing five days of clindamycin, the patient developed an abdominal rash, details of which were not specified. This was assessed as a delayed allergic reaction to clindamycin, prompting her general practitioner to change the antibiotic to clarithromycin, a medication she had not previously received.

She later arrived at the Emergency Department (ED) complaining of itching, facial flushing, excessive sweating, agitation, and the sensation of throat swelling. These complaints began approximately one hour following ingestion of her initial oral clarithromycin dose, with a gradual exacerbation of symptoms over time. She presented for medical attention an hour after symptom onset. Upon her arrival to the ED, her vital parameters indicated hypotension and reduced oxygen saturation (non-invasive blood pressure recorded at 74/50 mm Hg, peripheral oxygen saturation at 88% while breathing room air, and a respiratory rate of 22 breaths per minute). Physical examination revealed facial edema, new-onset widespread erythema and urticaria distributed across her body, as well as increased sweating and agitation. There was no evidence of tongue or pharyngeal swelling, and both cardiac and pulmonary auscultation produced normal findings. Capillary blood glucose measured 12.7 mmol/L, and her electrocardiogram indicated sinus rhythm. She was promptly managed for presumed severe allergic reaction, receiving an intramuscular administration of 0.3 mg epinephrine via auto-injector, together with a slow intravenous push of 2 mg clemastine, in accordance with institutional protocols for managing cutaneous allergic reactions. Furthermore, supplemental oxygen (15 L through a non-rebreather mask) and an initial 500 mL bolus of 0.9% sodium chloride solution were provided during resuscitation. Supplemental oxygen was discontinued following favorable response to epinephrine, though it required temporary reinstatement during further episodes of anaphylaxis. Treatment with clarithromycin was halted, and no further doses beyond the initial oral 500 mg were administered.

The patient initially exhibited a favorable clinical response following the commencement of therapy: blood pressure normalized and facial edema and erythema subsided. She was subsequently admitted to the acute internal medicine ward, where nursing oversight was intensified, albeit without telemetry. Nonetheless, within the initial 12-hour window post-admission, the patient experienced repeated bouts of symptoms consistent with anaphylactic shock, despite the positive initial response. Following the first recurrence, the patient was transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) for enhanced observation. Each anaphylactic event required resolution via intramuscular epinephrine administration (figure 1), culminating in a total of four episodes, including the initial presentation to the emergency department. The first two administrations consisted of 0.3 mg epinephrine via auto-injector, while the subsequent two doses were manually prepared at 0.5 mg intramuscularly. Despite the auto-injectors at our facility delivering a lower dose than the recommended 0.5 mg, they remain the preferred delivery method during critical time-sensitive scenarios—such as during acute stabilization in the emergency department—or in circumstances where staff may be unfamiliar with emergency medication protocols, potentially leading to hesitancy and delays. 

Upon the initial recurrence, adjunctive corticosteroid therapy was introduced to address the traditional prophylactic aim of preventing biphasic anaphylaxis. The patient was administered 30 mg of intravenous prednisone daily for five days, in addition to a single intravenous dose of 8 mg dexamethasone, reflecting divergent recommendations between general ward and ICU anaphylaxis protocols at our institution. Cutaneous symptoms were managed with two doses of 2 mg intravenous clemastine, and 500 mL of normal saline was administered during the second anaphylactic event. Notably, there was a progressive diminution in symptom severity with each subsequent episode, shifting from profound hypoxemia with cyanosis, hypotension, and severe respiratory distress in the initial event to primarily dyspnea, recurrent rash, and pruritus in the final occurrence.

Early consultation with the allergology department was sought for expert therapeutic guidance. Within the scope of this case, two potential management strategies were deliberated. The first involved the prophylactic use of an intravenous epinephrine infusion, aiming to preclude recurrence by maintaining consistent therapeutic levels, rather than a reactive approach. Alternatively, given the trend of symptom attenuation, an observational, expectant strategy was weighed, with the possibility that symptoms might resolve on their own. Both approaches carried substantial risks, complicated by a paucity of published guidance addressing the management of multiphasic anaphylactic reactions.

Outcome and follow-up:

In the end, neither of the considered treatments was required, since the patient experienced no additional symptom recurrence thereafter. Following approximately 24 hours without any further episodes of anaphylaxis, she was moved from intensive observation to a regular care unit. She was released from the hospital three days after her initial admission. Upon discharge, she was prescribed levocetirizine, an antihistamine, as part of her ongoing medication regimen and was provided with an epinephrine auto-injector (0.3 mg), with instructions on its use. No subsequent anaphylactic incidents have been reported since then.

Given the unusual and extended nature of the case, a more detailed investigation into potential underlying etiologies was initiated. The findings suggested a strong likelihood of systemic mastocytosis, but a definitive diagnosis could not be made according to the standard criteria: only two minor WHO criteria were fulfilled, and major criteria could not be evaluated due to the limited sample from the bone marrow biopsy (refer to figure 2). During the third episode of anaphylaxis, both acute and baseline tryptase concentrations were elevated and these increases persisted at follow-up, as documented by the allergologist (see table 1).

Regrettably, during the initial assessment of the patient’s early anaphylactic events, measurements of tryptase concentrations were omitted, resulting in deviation from the established guidelines for serial tryptase testing. Current recommendations stipulate that at least two, and preferably three, tryptase samples should be collected at designated time points: the first sample should be obtained as early as practicable without delaying therapeutic interventions, a second sample should follow 1–2 hours after symptom onset (but within a maximum of 4 hours) to coincide with peak tryptase concentration, and a third baseline sample should be collected at least 24 hours post-complete symptom resolution. In the presented case, the initial tryptase was measured one hour subsequent to the patient’s third anaphylactic event, and the second measurement was performed 27 hours following the most recent episode.

Additionally, no allergy confirmation procedures were pursued; methods such as skin testing were considered disproportionate, given the significant risk of precipitating another severe anaphylactic reaction and the relative simplicity of avoiding the presumed trigger. Other significant findings consistent with systemic mastocytosis in this patient included hepatomegaly, as detected by abdominal sonography, and possible neuropsychiatric manifestations, notably depressive symptoms and deficits in memory.

Discussion:

Anaphylaxis is a clinically distinct diagnosis, marked by a rapid onset and quick progression of symptoms affecting the airway, respiratory function, circulatory system, and/or gastrointestinal tract, and is commonly associated with skin or mucosal changes such as urticaria, angioedema, or flushing. Although the detection of a potential allergen strengthens the clinical diagnosis, identifying a precise inciting agent is not always feasible.

More intricate presentations like biphasic and refractory anaphylaxis are less clearly delineated, despite recent attempts to standardize their definitions. According to the Resuscitation Council UK, biphasic anaphylaxis involves a return of symptoms several hours after initial resolution, without renewed exposure to the allergen. Accurately distinguishing genuine biphasic reactions from prolonged anaphylactic episodes that temporarily respond to epinephrine or from ongoing absorption of the allergen (such as undigested food or sustained-release drugs) remains problematic. In contrast, refractory anaphylaxis is characterized by persistent symptoms that do not resolve despite the correct administration of epinephrine.

Clinical practice guidelines addressing biphasic anaphylaxis are primarily focused on recommendations for patient observation. Nonetheless, there is ongoing debate regarding the optimal length of observation. Standard protocols have typically advised observation for 4 to 6 hours post-symptom resolution. Both the Dutch Nederlands Huisartsen Genootschap (NHG) and the World Allergy Organisation guidelines observe that about 50% of biphasic responses manifest within 6–12 hours after the original reaction, though neither guideline specifies how long to monitor patients. The 2021 UK guideline, in contrast, suggests a stratified, three-level approach based on risk. Ultimately, reaching consensus on monitoring procedures is essential to effectively balance healthcare resource management for the majority who experience single-episode anaphylaxis with the imperative to safeguard the minority at risk for biphasic complications.

There is a conspicuous and concerning paucity of guidance regarding sophisticated pharmacological approaches for biphasic and multiphasic anaphylactic reactions. Therapeutic strategies for these recurrent forms of anaphylaxis essentially replicate the conventional management of anaphylactic episodes, primarily emphasizing rapid administration of intramuscular epinephrine together with suitable supportive interventions. According to prevailing clinical guidelines, the advised dose of epinephrine is 0.01 mg/kg, capped at a maximum of 0.5 mg per administration. In practice, this often defaults to a 0.5 mg dose for adult patients, as the majority exceed 50 kg in weight. It is noteworthy, however, that many epinephrine auto-injectors, including those utilized in the two initial events described in our case, provide only 0.3 mg of epinephrine, even when marketed for adult usage. While epinephrine auto-injectors remain indispensable, particularly in prehospital scenarios, they may also offer advantages in clinical environments by diminishing delays in intervention and alleviating uncertainties among healthcare providers with limited exposure to vasoactive medications like epinephrine. Nonetheless, administration of only 0.3 mg via auto-injector—a common practice—may result in significant underdosing in adults whose body weight exceeds 50 kg. A retrospective study conducted in 2025 demonstrated that individuals initially given 0.3 mg epinephrine were significantly more likely to require intensified management, including repeated epinephrine doses, compared to those initially administered 0.5 mg. Addressing longstanding obstacles—such as clinicians’ reliance on the customary 0.3 mg dose and the predominant availability of 0.3 mg auto-injectors—while promoting strict adherence to guideline-recommended epinephrine dosing, may meaningfully enhance the effectiveness of anaphylaxis management.

Historically, corticosteroids were advocated in the management of anaphylaxis, partly to mitigate the risk of biphasic reactions. Yet, accumulating research fails to substantiate any clear benefit and suggests possible detrimental outcomes, prompting a departure from this practice in recent years. For instance, a 2017 systematic review of 22 studies found only a single investigation in favor of corticosteroid use, while the remaining 21 did not demonstrate any reduction in biphasic anaphylaxis risk. Additional studies have brought attention to adverse effects, with one 2019 publication noting an increased incidence of ICU or hospital admissions when corticosteroids were given prior to hospital arrival. As a result, leading guidelines now generally discourage the routine use of corticosteroids, though they are still frequently administered in clinical settings.

Although uncommon, biphasic anaphylaxis has significant clinical implications. The lack of robust predictive markers presents challenges for risk stratification and decisions regarding patient observation. Proposed risk factors have included delayed initiation of epinephrine, more severe symptoms at onset, the necessity for multiple doses of epinephrine, and a history of previous biphasic episodes. Nonetheless, predictive models with sufficient reliability for guiding tailored monitoring have yet to be developed.

Systemic mastocytosis, which is characterized by an abnormal proliferation of mast cells, heightened mediator release, and systemic inflammation, is known to increase susceptibility to severe anaphylactic episodes. 1517 Nevertheless, due to the low prevalence of both systemic mastocytosis and biphasic anaphylaxis, there is currently an absence of studies directly evaluating the influence of systemic mastocytosis on biphasic reactions. Continued investigation into mast cell pathologies and their potential contribution to biphasic anaphylaxis could yield important advances in management strategies.

Anaphylaxis-related deaths are uncommon, particularly in areas where patients can quickly reach emergency healthcare services. Additionally, although biphasic anaphylaxis might seem particularly threatening, cases resulting in fatalities are exceedingly infrequent. Despite these reassuring findings, anaphylaxis persists as a frequent medical emergency that necessitates swift intervention and vigilant observation. While most research and established guidelines primarily address acute medical response, ongoing care and continued surveillance following the initial event are equally critical. Gaining deeper insights into complex presentations of anaphylaxis, such as biphasic episodes, has the potential to inform more individualized, efficient, and patient-oriented approaches to anaphylaxis management.

The infrequent occurrence of biphasic and especially multiphasic anaphylactic events, combined with the logistical and ethical challenges of conducting research in acute emergencies, makes establishing robust, evidence-based protocols particularly difficult. In these scenarios, consensus guidelines based on expert opinion, which delineate specific recommendations for management, serve as invaluable resources; nonetheless, these expert-driven directives are also restricted by a paucity of comprehensive data. Considering the uncommon nature of these complex anaphylaxis patterns, establishing a registry to systematically document relevant cases—including pertinent patient information, suggested management approaches (such as those outlined in our case report), and patient outcomes—could form a crucial foundation for generating informed, expert-based guidance.
