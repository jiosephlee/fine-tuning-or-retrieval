Title: Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting

Margret Ann Schneider, Hans de Groot, Jan-Willem Geerts

Abstract:

Summary: Anaphylaxis represents the most critical manifestation of allergic reactions and poses a significant risk to life, necessitating immediate and decisive intervention. In some patients, symptoms recur following initial clinical improvement and appropriate management. These biphasic or multiphasic patterns create significant management difficulties, particularly in view of varying prevalence rates, unclear predisposing factors, and the absence of comprehensive guidance within established protocols. This is especially pertinent in cases of multiphasic anaphylaxis where patients suffer multiple anaphylactic episodes after a satisfactory response to conventional epinephrine therapy. Because multiphasic anaphylaxis is relatively uncommon, individual case reports serve as valuable sources of clinical information regarding its management. Here, we describe a case involving a woman in her 60s who developed multiphasic anaphylaxis associated with clarithromycin. Further evaluation suggested a strong possibility of systemic mastocytosis; however, she did not fulfill the definitive diagnostic criteria.

Background:

Anaphylaxis represents a severe, and in cases of anaphylactic shock, potentially fatal hypersensitivity reaction that frequently prompts patients to seek emergency medical attention. Some instances exhibit a biphasic or even multiphasic pattern, marked by an initial hypersensitivity response to an allergen, subsequent symptom resolution resulting in an asymptomatic interval, and then the resurgence of symptoms without renewed allergen contact. Typically, this recurrence occurs within 24 hours following the initial exposure, though there are documented cases where symptoms reappear as late as 78 hours post-exposure. Estimates of the incidence for biphasic anaphylaxis differ substantially, with figures ranging from 0.4% to 23.3%, though the majority of evidence suggests rates between 4% and 6%. Given the risk of symptom return, post-anaphylaxis observation is generally recommended, even in cases where there is apparent complete resolution of clinical findings. Nonetheless, discrepancies in the definition of biphasic anaphylaxis and variation in clinical guidelines—particularly regarding the efficacy of corticosteroids—persist. Multiphasic anaphylaxis, involving several recurrences of allergic symptoms, creates further complexity in management and observation strategies. Here, we discuss the case of a woman in her sixties who developed multiphasic anaphylactic reactions subsequent to clarithromycin administration.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 60s, with a known allergy to beta-lactam antibiotics, presented to the emergency department (ED) with anaphylaxis following oral clarithromycin 500 mg.

The patient’s past medical history was notable for type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, psoriasis, and depression. She weighed 104 kg. Her current medications included metformin (500 mg twice daily), gliclazide (80 mg extended-release once daily), hydrochlorothiazide (25 mg once daily), omeprazole (20 mg once daily), and sertraline (100 mg once daily). The most recent dose of hydrochlorothiazide was taken in the morning, more than 12 hours prior to her presentation at the emergency department.

In adolescence, the patient experienced a mild allergic response to amoxicillin that manifested as swelling of the face without involvement of the oropharynx, cutaneous eruption, hypotension, or breathing difficulties. She had also had a pronounced local dermatological reaction to a wasp sting, but had not developed any systemic manifestations. The current hospitalization represented her first occurrence of anaphylaxis.

Five days prior to arriving at the emergency department, her primary care physician started her on clindamycin for treatment of erysipelas. After taking clindamycin for five days, she developed an abdominal rash that was not described in detail. This was considered a delayed hypersensitivity reaction to clindamycin, prompting her primary care provider to change her antibiotic to clarithromycin, a drug she had not previously been exposed to.

She later arrived at the emergency department experiencing itching, flushing, increased sweating, restlessness, and a sensation of throat swelling. These complaints began approximately one hour following oral administration of her initial dose of clarithromycin, progressively intensifying over time. She presented to medical services one hour after symptoms first appeared. On arrival, her vital signs included low blood pressure (non-invasive BP: 74/50 mm Hg), hypoxemia (oxygen saturation 88% on room air), and a respiratory rate of 22 breaths per minute. Physical examination revealed facial edema, recent onset of widespread erythema and urticaria across her body, alongside increased perspiration and agitation. No edema was noted in the tongue or pharyngeal region, and auscultation of the heart and lungs was unremarkable. Capillary blood glucose was 12.7 mmol/L, and her ECG demonstrated sinus rhythm. She was immediately managed for a presumed severe allergic reaction with an intramuscular dose of 0.3 mg epinephrine administered via auto-injector, and a slow intravenous injection of 2 mg clemastine was provided for cutaneous manifestations, in accordance with local protocols. Further initial stabilization included supplemental oxygen (15 L administered with a non-rebreather mask) and intravenous infusion of 500 mL sodium chloride 0.9% solution. After favorable response to the epinephrine, supplemental oxygen was discontinued but later required re-initiation during subsequent anaphylactic episodes. The clarithromycin was stopped, and no further doses beyond the single 500 mg oral dose were given.

An initial favorable response to therapy was noted, with normalization of blood pressure and reduction in both facial swelling and erythema. The patient was subsequently transferred to the acute internal medicine ward, where increased surveillance by specialized nursing personnel is offered, though no telemetry monitoring was available. Nonetheless, after this initial improvement, the patient experienced repeated episodes of symptoms consistent with anaphylactic shock within the first 12 hours post-admission. Following the first recurrence of anaphylactic shock, the patient was moved to the intensive care unit (ICU) for enhanced monitoring. Each anaphylactic episode necessitated the intramuscular administration of epinephrine for symptom resolution (figure 1), culminating in a total of four anaphylactic events, including the initial presentation to the emergency department (ED). The first two epinephrine doses were administered as auto-injectors containing 0.3 mg intramuscularly, and the subsequent two were administered as manually prepared 0.5 mg intramuscular doses. While the auto-injectors on hand provided a lower dose than the recommended 0.5 mg, their use is preferred in our facility for time-critical situations, such as initial stabilization in the ED, or in environments where emergency interventions like anaphylaxis management are infrequent, as unfamiliarity with medications may otherwise delay care. Corticosteroids were added to therapy after the first recurrent episode of anaphylaxis, following traditional practice aimed at preventing biphasic reactions. She was treated with daily intravenous prednisone 30 mg for five days and a single dose of intravenous dexamethasone 8 mg, owing to differences in the anaphylaxis management protocols between the hospital's general and intensive care wards. Additionally, she received two intravenous doses of clemastine 2 mg for management of skin symptoms, as well as an extra 500 mL of normal saline during the second anaphylactic event. Notably, the severity of symptoms diminished with each episode, progressing from profound hypoxemia with cyanosis, hypotension, and severe dyspnea in the initial phase to dyspnea, rash recurrence, and pruritus in the final event.

Early in the course, the allergology service was consulted for expert management recommendations. Two alternative strategies were contemplated in this case. One involved the continuous intravenous infusion of epinephrine to preemptively address anaphylaxis, rather than reacting to symptom recurrence. The other considered an observational approach given the gradual lessening of symptom intensity, to determine if spontaneous resolution might occur. Each approach carried significant risks, which were compounded by the absence of robust literature to guide therapy in cases exhibiting multiphasic anaphylaxis.

Outcome and follow-up:

In the end, neither intervention was required, as the patient experienced no additional recurrence of symptoms beyond this point. After remaining free of new anaphylactic events for roughly 24 hours, she was transferred to a regular medical ward. She was discharged after a total hospitalization period of 3 days. On discharge, she was prescribed levocetirizine, an antihistamine, as a new long-term medication and was provided with a 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injector, along with training on its use. She has not had any subsequent anaphylactic reactions.

Given the unusual and extended clinical presentation, further work-up for underlying etiologies was performed. This assessment indicated a significant likelihood of systemic mastocytosis, though the findings did not satisfy the complete diagnostic criteria (only two of the WHO minor criteria were present; major criteria could not be evaluated because of limited sample from the bone marrow biopsy—refer to figure 2). Regrettably, tryptase concentrations were not assessed during the initial evaluation of the first anaphylactic reactions, resulting in noncompliance with the advised timing for tryptase measurement. Current guidelines suggest at least two, preferably three, tryptase determinations at specific points: the first sample should be obtained as early as possible without postponing acute management; the second, at 1–2 hours (but no later than 4 hours) after symptom onset—coinciding with the expected peak of tryptase; and a third, at least 24 hours after full clinical recovery, to determine baseline levels. In the presented case, the initial tryptase measurement was acquired 1 hour following the third episode of anaphylaxis, with a follow-up sample taken 27 hours after the most recent event.

Allergy confirmation testing was not conducted because the risk of provoking another severe anaphylactic reaction via procedures such as skin testing was considered disproportionate, particularly since additional exposure could be readily avoided. Additional pertinent findings supportive of systemic mastocytosis encompassed hepatomegaly observed on abdominal ultrasound and possible neuropsychiatric manifestations, including depression and memory impairment.

Discussion:

Anaphylaxis is a relatively well-characterized clinical syndrome, defined by the abrupt onset and rapid escalation of symptoms affecting the airway, respiratory, cardiovascular, and/or gastrointestinal systems, usually in association with skin or mucous membrane findings such as urticaria, angioedema, or flushing. Although recognizing a probable allergen can help confirm the diagnosis, it is not always feasible to identify a specific causative agent.

More atypical forms, such as biphasic or refractory anaphylaxis, have less precise definitions, but recent initiatives have sought to develop standardized criteria for these entities as well. The Resuscitation Council UK describes biphasic anaphylaxis as the return of symptoms several hours after their initial resolution, without additional allergen exposure. Distinguishing true biphasic reactions from prolonged anaphylaxis with only short-lived improvement following epinephrine administration or from ongoing allergen absorption (for example, from undigested food or sustained-release drugs) can be difficult. In contrast, refractory anaphylaxis is defined as ongoing anaphylactic symptoms that do not resolve or resolve inadequately after appropriately delivered epinephrine.

Most recommendations regarding biphasic anaphylaxis focus on clinical observation. Nonetheless, the ideal duration of post-event monitoring is still debated. Historically, observation intervals have ranged between 4 and 6 hours after symptom resolution. Guidelines from the Nederlands Huisartsen Genootschap (NHG) and the World Allergy Organisation both note that about half of all biphasic reactions take place within 6 to 12 hours following the initial episode, but neither offers explicit monitoring guidance. Conversely, the 2021 UK guideline advocates a three-level risk-based observation protocol. Clearly, harmonized observation policies are essential to balance healthcare resource management for the larger population experiencing single-episode anaphylaxis, with the safety needs of the smaller, high-risk biphasic group that is challenging to identify.

There is a conspicuous and concerning gap in guidance regarding advanced medication regimens for managing biphasic and multiphasic anaphylactic reactions. Treatment of these cases generally follows standard anaphylaxis protocols, which involve the immediate administration of intramuscular epinephrine along with suitable supportive measures. Current guidelines advise dosing epinephrine at 0.01 mg/kg, capped at a maximum of 0.5 mg. For adults—most of whom exceed 50 kg—a dose of 0.5 mg is commonly applied for simplicity. Nonetheless, it is noteworthy that numerous auto-injectors, including those used during the initial two episodes in the case described, provide just 0.3 mg of epinephrine despite being labeled for adult administration. While epinephrine auto-injectors are crucial in prehospital scenarios, they can also be advantageous within healthcare facilities by expediting delivery and mitigating reluctance among staff members less accustomed to using vasoactive medications. Yet, administering 0.3 mg via auto-injector may result in clinically significant underdosing in adult patients weighing more than 50 kg. Data from a 2025 retrospective review demonstrated that individuals who received an initial dose of 0.3 mg more frequently required interventions such as additional epinephrine compared to those administered a starting dose of 0.5 mg. Efforts to overcome obstacles such as provider familiarity with the longstanding 0.3 mg recommendation and the widespread prevalence of 0.3 mg auto-injectors, in addition to promoting adherence to current dosing standards, could substantially enhance outcomes in anaphylaxis care.

Historically, corticosteroids were advised partly to mitigate biphasic reactions. Nonetheless, accumulating data indicate a lack of efficacy and possible adverse outcomes, which has prompted a recent movement away from administering steroids during anaphylaxis. A 2017 systematic review assessed 22 studies, with just one study supporting corticosteroid use and 21 showing no evidence of reduced risk for biphasic anaphylaxis. Moreover, current studies draw attention to notable adverse effects; for example, a 2019 study reported a higher chance of ICU or hospital ward admission following prehospital corticosteroid use. As a result, the majority of current guidelines do not recommend routine corticosteroid use, though such practice still occurs in some settings.

Although biphasic anaphylaxis remains uncommon, it is of significant clinical concern. The lack of dependable prognostic indicators complicates assessment of risk and determination of appropriate monitoring duration. Proposed risk factors have included postponed epinephrine intervention, more severe initial symptoms, requirement of multiple epinephrine doses, and a history of biphasic reactions. Nevertheless, reliable tools for focused monitoring are not yet available.

Systemic mastocytosis, characterized by increased mast cell numbers, pronounced mediator release, and multisystem inflammation, increases susceptibility to severe anaphylactic episodes. However, considering the low incidence of both conditions, no studies to date have specifically examined the impact of systemic mastocytosis on the likelihood of biphasic anaphylaxis. Additional research focused on mast cell diseases and their relationship with biphasic anaphylaxis may yield important information for future therapeutic strategies.

Anaphylaxis-related deaths are uncommon, particularly in areas where emergency care is readily accessible. Moreover, lethal cases of biphasic anaphylaxis are even more unusual, despite their seemingly heightened risk. Nonetheless, despite these encouraging figures, anaphylaxis continues to represent a frequent medical emergency that necessitates rapid intervention and appropriate surveillance. Although much research and many guidelines center on acute emergency care, ongoing monitoring and subsequent management remain critically important. Gaining a deeper insight into complex presentations such as biphasic anaphylaxis may support the development of more individualized, efficient, and patient-focused approaches to anaphylaxis management.

The infrequency of biphasic—and especially multiphasic—anaphylaxis, combined with the inherent challenges of studying emergencies, poses significant obstacles to establishing evidence-based management protocols. In such contexts, consensus guidelines from experts with explicit recommendations are highly valuable; nevertheless, crafting these expert-driven guidelines is hindered by a scarcity of robust data. Considering the unusual nature of these complex anaphylaxis cases, the establishment of a dedicated registry—documenting key patient characteristics, potential management suggestions (as outlined in our case report), and patient outcomes—may offer a critical foundation for formulating data-informed, expert-endorsed recommendations.
