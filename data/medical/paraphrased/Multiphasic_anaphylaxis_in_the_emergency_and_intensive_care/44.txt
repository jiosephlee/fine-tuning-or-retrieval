Title: Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting

Margret Ann Schneider, Hans de Groot, Jan-Willem Geerts

Abstract:

Summary: Anaphylaxis represents the most severe manifestation of allergic reactions and constitutes a potentially fatal systemic event necessitating immediate and effective intervention. In some instances, symptoms recur following initial stabilization and appropriate therapy, resulting in biphasic or multiphasic clinical trajectories. These patterns pose distinct challenges to healthcare providers, as their prevalence is inconsistently reported, contributing factors remain uncertain, and well-established management recommendations are absent from existing guidelines. This issue is particularly pertinent in cases of multiphasic anaphylaxis, where repeated episodes occur despite satisfactory response to standard epinephrine administration. Given the uncommon nature of multiphasic anaphylaxis, clinical case reports serve as valuable resources for guidance in addressing such scenarios. Here, we describe a case involving a female patient in her 60s who experienced multiphasic anaphylaxis after exposure to clarithromycin, with subsequent evaluation indicating a probable diagnosis of systemic mastocytosis, although formal diagnostic criteria were not met.

Background:

Anaphylaxis represents a significant and, in cases of anaphylactic shock, potentially fatal allergic response that frequently leads to emergency department visits. A proportion of patients exhibit a biphasic or even multiphasic pattern, defined by an initial anaphylactic episode after exposure to an allergen, a subsequent symptom-free interval, and then a recurrence of symptoms absent any further contact with the offending agent. Typically, this pattern is noted within 24 hours following the first exposure, but there have been accounts of symptoms returning up to 78 hours later. The reported rates of biphasic anaphylaxis vary substantially, from 0.4% to 23.3%, although most studies estimate the incidence to be between 4% and 6%. Owing to the risk of relapse, observation for a certain duration following the resolution of initial symptoms is generally recommended. Nevertheless, definitions for biphasic anaphylaxis are not always standardized and clinical protocols differ, particularly with ongoing debate regarding the utility of corticosteroids. Multiphasic anaphylaxis, characterized by several recurrences of symptoms, poses unique challenges in both management and monitoring. Here, we describe a case involving a female patient in her sixties who experienced a multiphasic anaphylactic response to clarithromycin.

Case presentation:

A female in her 60s, who had a documented allergy to beta-lactam antibiotics, arrived at the emergency department (ED) experiencing anaphylaxis after ingesting clarithromycin 500 mg orally.

Her past medical history was notable for type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, psoriasis, and depression. She weighed 104 kg. Her current medications included metformin 500 mg twice daily, gliclazide 80 mg retard once daily, hydrochlorothiazide 25 mg once daily, omeprazole 20 mg once daily, and sertraline 100 mg once daily. Hydrochlorothiazide had last been taken that morning, more than 12 hours prior to her arrival at the ED.

As an adolescent, she had experienced a mild allergic response to amoxicillin, presenting as facial swelling only, without involvement of the oropharynx, rash, hypotension, or dyspnoea. She had also previously reacted locally to a wasp sting with significant skin symptoms but no systemic manifestations. The current event marked her first occurrence of anaphylaxis.

Five days before coming to the ED, her general practitioner initiated clindamycin therapy for erysipelas. After five days on clindamycin, she developed a rash on her abdomen (with no additional specifics given). This was considered a delayed allergic reaction to clindamycin, leading the general practitioner to change her antibiotic regimen to clarithromycin, which she had never received previously.

She later arrived at the emergency department experiencing itching, flushing, excessive sweating, restlessness, and a sensation of throat swelling. These symptoms began one hour after she ingested the first oral dose of clarithromycin and progressively intensified. She sought medical attention approximately one hour after her symptoms began. On arrival to the ED, her initial vital signs indicated low blood pressure and reduced oxygen saturation (non-invasive blood pressure measured at 74/50 mm Hg, peripheral oxygen saturation at 88% on ambient air, and a respiratory rate of 22 breaths per minute). Physical examination revealed swelling of the face, newly developed widespread erythema and urticaria covering the body, as well as increased sweating and agitation. Examination did not show swelling of the tongue or throat, and both cardiac and pulmonary auscultation were unremarkable. Her blood glucose level was 12.7 mmol/L, and electrocardiography demonstrated sinus rhythm. Management for a presumed severe allergic reaction was immediately initiated, including intramuscular administration of 0.3 mg epinephrine using an auto-injector and a slow intravenous dose of 2 mg clemastine according to local protocols for cutaneous symptoms. She also received supplemental oxygen (15 L via a non-rebreather mask) and an infusion of 500 mL normal saline (0.9% sodium chloride solution) during initial resuscitation. The supplemental oxygen was discontinued following satisfactory clinical response to epinephrine, but had to be reinstated temporarily during subsequent recurrent episodes of anaphylaxis. Clarithromycin was stopped, with no further doses given after the single 500 mg oral administration.

An initial favorable response to therapy was noted, with normalization of blood pressure and reductions in facial swelling and erythema. The patient was then transferred to the acute internal medicine ward, which offers enhanced monitoring by specialized nursing staff, but does not utilize telemetry. Despite this early improvement, within the first 12 hours following admission, the patient experienced repeated episodes characteristic of anaphylactic shock. After the initial recurrence, she was moved to the intensive care unit (ICU) for closer surveillance. Each recurrence required intramuscular administration of epinephrine to achieve symptom resolution (see figure 1), totaling four episodes including the index presentation in the emergency department (ED). The first two doses of epinephrine were delivered using auto-injectors containing 0.3 mg, whereas the subsequent two were administered as custom-prepared intramuscular injections of 0.5 mg. Though the auto-injectors available at the clinic dispense less than the recommended 0.5 mg dose, they are preferred for time-critical situations—such as acute stabilization in the ED—or in environments where anaphylaxis treatment is not routine, which can lead to delays due to hesitancy with unfamiliar medications. After the first recurrence of anaphylactic symptoms, corticosteroid therapy was initiated, reflecting their conventional use in preventing biphasic anaphylaxis. The regimen consisted of daily intravenous prednisone 30 mg for five days, as well as a single intravenous dose of dexamethasone 8 mg, based on varying recommendations between the hospital’s general ward and intensive care anaphylaxis protocols. Additionally, she was administered intravenous clemastine 2 mg twice for cutaneous manifestations and received 500 mL of normal saline during the second anaphylactic event. Notably, symptom severity diminished with each subsequent episode, ranging initially from profound hypoxemia with cyanosis, hypotension, and severe dyspnea, to milder manifestations such as dyspnea, rash recurrence, and pruritus during the final episode.

Early consultation with the allergology department was sought for expert guidance. Two alternative strategies were considered for further management. The first involved continuous intravenous epinephrine infusion to proactively prevent anaphylaxis, rather than reactively treating recurrences. The second approach was expectant, given the progressive reduction in symptom severity, to observe if symptoms might remit spontaneously. Both options entailed significant risk due to the absence of established treatment recommendations for multiphasic anaphylactic cases in the literature.

Outcome and follow-up:

In the end, neither therapeutic strategy was required, as the patient did not experience additional recurrence of symptoms. After roughly 24 hours without another episode of anaphylaxis, she was moved to a regular care unit. Her hospital admission concluded with discharge after 3 days. Levocetirizine, an antihistamine, was initiated as a long-term medication, and she was provided with a 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injector, with instruction on its use. No further anaphylactic events have occurred since.

Given the unusual and extended nature of the clinical presentation, additional investigations into possible underlying etiologies were undertaken. These revealed a significant likelihood of systemic mastocytosis, though the formal diagnostic criteria were not met—two minor criteria of WHO were present, while the major criterion could not be evaluated due to limited sample from the bone marrow biopsy (see figure 2). Regrettably, tryptase concentrations were not measured during the initial evaluation of the patient's first anaphylactic events, resulting in non-compliance with recommended timing for tryptase assessment. Current guidelines advocate for at least two, preferably three, tryptase determinations at specific time points: the first sample should be obtained as soon as possible without delaying acute management; a second sample is advised 1–2 hours (and no later than 4 hours) after symptom onset, to capture the peak level; and a third sample should be acquired at least 24 hours following complete resolution of clinical manifestations to determine the patient’s baseline. In this case, the initial tryptase sample was drawn one hour after the third anaphylactic event, with a second sample taken 27 hours after the final anaphylactic recurrence.

An allergy confirmation assessment was not pursued, as it was judged that the potential danger of provoking a severe anaphylactic response—such as might occur with skin testing—outweighed the benefit, particularly given the straightforwardness of avoiding additional exposure. Additional findings supportive of systemic mastocytosis were also noted, including hepatomegaly detected on abdominal ultrasound and possible neuropsychiatric complaints, specifically depressive symptoms and memory disturbances.

Discussion:

Anaphylaxis is a clinically well-recognized entity, defined by an abrupt onset and quick escalation of symptoms affecting the airway, respiratory system, cardiovascular system, and/or gastrointestinal tract, and is usually associated with skin or mucosal changes like urticaria, angioedema, or flushing. Although identifying a probable allergen can help confirm the diagnosis, in many instances a specific causative agent cannot be pinpointed.

More complicated forms—such as biphasic and refractory anaphylaxis—are not as clearly delineated, though recent initiatives have worked toward creating consensus criteria for these presentations. According to the Resuscitation Council UK, biphasic anaphylaxis entails the recurrence of clinical features several hours after symptoms seem to resolve, without additional allergen exposure. It can be difficult to distinguish genuine biphasic episodes from cases where there is persistent anaphylaxis that temporarily responds to epinephrine, or ongoing allergen absorption, for example from retained food or slow-release formulations. On the other hand, refractory anaphylaxis is defined by ongoing symptoms that fail to improve or show minimal response despite appropriately administered epinephrine.

Guidelines on the management of biphasic anaphylaxis mainly focus on the need for clinical observation, though there is ongoing debate regarding the ideal duration for post-resolution monitoring. Historically, monitoring intervals of 4 to 6 hours after symptom resolution have been customary. The Dutch Nederlands Huisartsen Genootschap (NHG) and World Allergy Organisation guidelines both indicate that roughly half of biphasic reactions arise within 6–12 hours of the initial incident; however, neither specifies an exact recommendation for monitoring duration. By contrast, the 2021 UK guideline introduces a three-level, risk-based approach to observation. Development of standardized monitoring protocols remains essential in order to balance the efficient use of healthcare resources—since the majority of patients only experience a single episode—against the imperative of ensuring safety for the small, hard-to-predict subset at risk for biphasic reactions.

Guidance regarding advanced pharmacologic interventions for biphasic and multiphasic presentations is noticeably sparse. The therapeutic approach to biphasic and multiphasic anaphylaxis largely aligns with standard management protocols, primarily emphasizing the immediate administration of intramuscular epinephrine along with suitable supportive measures. Clinical guidelines advise an epinephrine dose of 0.01 mg/kg, capped at a maximum of 0.5 mg. For adults, whose body weight typically exceeds 50 kg, this dose is often generalized to 0.5 mg. Nevertheless, it is noteworthy that many commercially available auto-injectors, including those employed for the initial two episodes described in this case, deliver only 0.3 mg of epinephrine even when designated for adult use. Although epinephrine auto-injectors serve as a pivotal intervention prior to hospital arrival, their utility extends into clinical environments by minimizing therapeutic delays and overcoming reluctance among providers who rarely administer vasoactive agents. Despite their convenience, administering 0.3 mg via auto-injector in adults over 50 kg constitutes a substantial risk of underdosing. Data from a 2025 retrospective analysis revealed that patients who first received 0.3 mg of epinephrine required higher-level interventions—such as repeat dosing—significantly more often compared to those initially administered 0.5 mg. Overcoming obstacles like ingrained provider preference for 0.3 mg, the widespread use of 0.3 mg auto-injectors, and ensuring closer adherence to guideline-recommended dosing may represent critical steps toward optimizing anaphylaxis care.

Historically, corticosteroids were advocated for use, partly to mitigate biphasic reactions. Nevertheless, accumulating data suggests not only a lack of benefit but also the possibility of adverse outcomes, resulting in a recent move away from routine steroid use during anaphylaxis. A systematic review conducted in 2017 evaluated 22 studies; only one provided support for corticosteroid efficacy, while the remaining 21 found no evidence that steroids decrease the risk of biphasic anaphylaxis. Additionally, recent investigations have highlighted concerns about adverse effects, and a 2019 study demonstrated a higher risk of ICU or hospital ward admission following prehospital corticosteroid therapy. Therefore, current guidelines generally discourage the standard use of corticosteroids, though their application in actual clinical settings continues.

Although uncommon, biphasic anaphylaxis remains clinically significant. The lack of dependable predictors complicates assessment of risk and decisions regarding observation. Potential predictors include delayed administration of epinephrine, an initially severe presentation, the necessity for multiple doses of epinephrine, and a history of biphasic reactions. However, reliable predictors that would support selective monitoring strategies are presently unavailable.

Systemic mastocytosis is characterized by increased mast cell numbers, excessive release of mediators, and inflammation affecting multiple organs, which increases susceptibility to severe anaphylaxis.1517 Despite the uncommon nature of both conditions, no existing studies specifically explore the relationship between systemic mastocytosis and biphasic anaphylaxis. Additional research into mast cell disorders and biphasic anaphylaxis may yield useful guidance for management in the future.

Fortunately, mortality from anaphylaxis remains low, particularly in areas where emergency services are readily available. Additionally, deaths associated with biphasic anaphylaxis are exceedingly uncommon, in spite of their seemingly increased risk. Nevertheless, despite these reassuring trends, anaphylaxis continues to be a frequent medical emergency necessitating immediate intervention and vigilant observation. Although much research and guidelines center on the acute management phase, ongoing medical observation and care following the initial episode are also critically important. Enhanced knowledge of complex presentations, such as biphasic anaphylaxis, may facilitate more individualized, effective, and resource-conscious management approaches for anaphylaxis.

Due to the infrequency of biphasic and especially multiphasic anaphylactic events, as well as the inherent research challenges in acute care settings, establishing robust evidence-based management strategies is difficult. In these circumstances, consensus guidelines formulated by experts, featuring straightforward recommendations, are highly valuable. However, the absence of comprehensive data similarly constrains the development of such expert-driven guidance. Given the uncommon nature of these intricate cases, establishing a registry to collect data—including relevant patient characteristics, proposed management options as illustrated in our report, and clinical outcomes—may form the basis for generating expert recommendations that are more data-informed.
