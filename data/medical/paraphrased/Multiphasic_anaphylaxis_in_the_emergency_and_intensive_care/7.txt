Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting

Margret Ann Schneider, Hans de Groot, Jan-Willem Geerts

Abstract:

Summary: Anaphylaxis represents the most dangerous spectrum of allergic reactions, characterized by its capacity to induce life-threatening systemic effects that mandate rapid and decisive intervention. In a minority of patients, manifestations of anaphylaxis recur after initial symptom resolution, even with timely and appropriate therapeutic measures. Such biphasic and, more rarely, multiphasic responses pose significant clinical challenges, exacerbated by the lack of consensus on prevalence, ambiguous risk determinants, and the absence of standardized protocols in prevailing guidelines. These challenges are further compounded in cases of multiphasic anaphylaxis, wherein patients experience multiple successive episodes after apparently successful epinephrine administration. Given the infrequency of multiphasic anaphylaxis, detailed case reports remain invaluable for enhancing our understanding and shaping approaches to care. This report describes the clinical course of a woman in her 60s who developed multiphasic anaphylaxis secondary to clarithromycin exposure; subsequent evaluation strongly suggested systemic mastocytosis, despite her not meeting established diagnostic benchmarks.

Background:

Anaphylaxis constitutes a critical allergic reaction that can escalate to anaphylactic shock, posing a significant risk to life and representing a frequent reason for emergency department visits. While many cases follow a straightforward course, a proportion displays either biphasic or even multiphasic progression. Such courses involve an initial reaction to an allergen, symptom resolution with an intervening symptom-free interval, and then a spontaneous return of symptoms in the absence of further allergen exposure. Typically, this resurgence of symptoms manifests within the first 24 hours post-exposure, though instances have been documented with relapses occurring as late as 78 hours afterwards. Estimates of the incidence of biphasic anaphylaxis exhibit substantial variability, reported from 0.4% up to 23.3%, though most literature situates the rate between 4% and 6%. The risk of recurrence justifies a recommended observation period after an anaphylactic episode, even in cases where there is complete resolution of symptoms. Nonetheless, conceptual definitions of biphasic anaphylaxis are not universally standardized, and guidelines regarding management, particularly the utility of corticosteroids, have come under scrutiny. Multiphasic anaphylaxis, which involves several episodes of recurrent symptoms, adds further complexity to therapeutic and monitoring strategies. In the present report, we describe the clinical presentation of a woman in her 60s who experienced a multiphasic anaphylactic episode in response to clarithromycin.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 60s with a documented allergy to beta-lactam antibiotics arrived at the emergency department (ED) due to an episode of anaphylaxis shortly after ingesting clarithromycin 500 mg orally.

Her past medical history was notable for type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, psoriasis, and depression. She weighed 104 kg. Her ongoing medications included metformin (500 mg twice daily), gliclazide (80 mg retard once daily), hydrochlorothiazide (25 mg once daily), omeprazole (20 mg once daily), and sertraline (100 mg once daily). The most recent dose of hydrochlorothiazide had been taken in the morning, more than 12 hours before her ED admission.

In adolescence, she had experienced a minor allergic response to amoxicillin, characterized by facial swelling, but without involvement of the oropharynx, skin rash, hypotension, or difficulty breathing. She had also reacted to a wasp sting with pronounced local skin changes, although there were no systemic manifestations. Until this hospitalization, she had never suffered from anaphylaxis.

Five days prior to attending the ED, she was started on clindamycin for erysipelas by her general practitioner. After 5 days on clindamycin, she developed a rash localized to her abdomen (details of which were not documented). This was interpreted as a delayed allergic response to clindamycin, leading her general practitioner to alter her antibiotic therapy to clarithromycin, an agent she had not previously received.

The patient later arrived at the Emergency Department (ED) reporting symptoms including pruritus, flushing, excessive sweating, agitation, and a sensation of throat swelling. These manifestations began one hour following ingestion of her first dose of clarithromycin and gradually intensified. She presented for medical evaluation approximately one hour after initial symptom onset. Upon ED admission, her vital signs indicated hypotension and hypoxaemia (non-invasive blood pressure: 74/50 mm Hg, peripheral oxygen saturation at 88% on room air, and respiratory rate of 22 breaths per minute). Physical examination revealed facial swelling, newly developed diffuse erythema, urticaria affecting the entire body, as well as increased perspiration and agitation. There was no clinical evidence of tongue or pharyngeal edema, and both cardiac and pulmonary auscultation yielded normal findings. Capillary blood glucose was measured at 12.7 mmol/L, while ECG demonstrated sinus rhythm. Immediate treatment for a presumed severe allergic reaction was initiated, comprising intramuscular administration of 0.3 mg epinephrine via auto-injector and a slow intravenous dose of 2 mg clemastine in accordance with local protocols for skin involvement. Additional measures included administration of supplemental oxygen at 15 L/min via a non-rebreather mask and an infusion of 500 mL isotonic saline (sodium chloride 0.9% solution) during the initial stabilisation phase. Oxygen supplementation was successfully discontinued after a favourable response to epinephrine, although it was transiently required again during further episodes of anaphylaxis. Clarithromycin therapy was halted, and no additional doses were given beyond the initial oral 500 mg administration.

At the outset, the patient demonstrated a favorable therapeutic response, with normalization of blood pressure and a noticeable reduction in both facial edema and erythema. Admission to the acute internal medicine ward followed, providing enhanced observation by a specialized nursing team, albeit in the absence of telemetry monitoring. Nevertheless, within the first 12 hours post-admission, despite this initially positive trend, the patient experienced recurrent episodes of symptoms indicative of anaphylactic shock. Following the first recurrence, transfer to the intensive care unit (ICU) was undertaken to allow for more rigorous observation. Each anaphylactic event required administration of intramuscular epinephrine for resolution (see figure 1), with a total of four anaphylactic episodes, including the initial presentation in the emergency department. The initial two administrations of epinephrine employed a 0.3 mg auto-injector, while the subsequent two doses consisted of 0.5 mg of epinephrine delivered intramuscularly from individually prepared syringes. Although the available auto-injector dose at our facility is less than the advised 0.5 mg, its use is preferred in urgent contexts—such as the early stabilization phase in the emergency department—or in scenarios where the administration of acute treatments like anaphylaxis is infrequent, and hesitancy arising from unfamiliarity might lead to delays. Following the first recurrence of anaphylactic symptoms, corticosteroids were introduced, in alignment with their traditional utility in mitigating the risk of biphasic reactions. The regimen comprised daily intravenous prednisone at a dose of 30 mg for 5 days and a single intravenous administration of 8 mg dexamethasone, reflecting the differing recommendations between our hospital’s general ward and ICU anaphylaxis management protocols. Additionally, the patient received 2 mg clemastine intravenously on two occasions to address cutaneous manifestations, as well as an extra 500 mL infusion of normal saline during the second episode. Notably, the severity of the reactions diminished progressively across episodes, initially presenting as profound hypoxemia with cyanosis, hypotension, and marked dyspnea, evolving to milder symptoms of dyspnea, rash recurrence, and pruritus during the final occurrence.

Consultation with the department of allergology was sought early to obtain expert guidance on management. Two alternative therapeutic strategies were evaluated in this case. The first involved proactive management of anaphylaxis through continuous intravenous infusion of epinephrine, aimed at preventing further episodes rather than responding reactively. Alternatively, considering the trend toward milder symptomatology, an expectant management approach was weighed to determine whether the episodes might resolve without intervention. Both strategies posed significant risks, compounded by the paucity of evidence or established guidelines in the context of multiphasic anaphylactic cases.

Outcome and follow-up:

In the end, neither of the considered treatment strategies was required since the patient did not experience any further relapse of symptoms. After remaining free of anaphylactic episodes for about 24 hours, she was moved back to a general care unit. Her hospital stay totaled 3 days before she was discharged. At discharge, levocetirizine, an antihistamine, was prescribed for ongoing management, and she received an epinephrine auto-injector (0.3 mg), with instructions on its use. She has had no subsequent anaphylactic events.

Given the unusual and extended progression of her illness, additional investigations into potential underlying etiologies were pursued. These investigations indicated a strong suspicion of systemic mastocytosis; however, a definitive diagnosis could not be established, as only two of the WHO minor criteria were met, and assessment of the major criterion was not possible owing to insufficient material from the bone marrow biopsy (refer to figure 2). Regrettably, tryptase concentrations were not assessed at the time of the patient’s initial anaphylactic events, resulting in a deviation from the recommended schedule for tryptase measurements. Current guidelines suggest collecting at least two, and preferably three, tryptase samples at designated intervals: the first should be drawn as soon as possible without postponing therapeutic intervention; the second should be obtained 1–2 hours after symptom onset (but within a maximum of 4 hours), capturing the typical peak in tryptase levels; and a third should be taken no sooner than 24 hours following the complete resolution of symptoms to determine the baseline tryptase value. In this particular case, however, the earliest sample was collected 1 hour after the patient’s third anaphylactic event, while the second sample was drawn 27 hours following the most recent anaphylactic recurrence.

Additionally, no confirmatory allergy testing was conducted. It was determined that subjecting the patient to procedures such as skin testing, with their attendant risk of inducing severe anaphylaxis, was not warranted, particularly since ongoing avoidance of the suspected trigger could be readily achieved. Other noteworthy observations, consistent with the diagnosis of systemic mastocytosis, included the presence of hepatomegaly identified through abdominal ultrasonography, along with possible neuropsychiatric manifestations such as depression and memory impairment.

Discussion:

Anaphylaxis is recognized as a distinct clinical entity, typically presenting with abrupt onset and rapid escalation of symptoms affecting the airway, respiratory function, circulatory system, and/or gastrointestinal tract, often accompanied by cutaneous or mucosal signs such as urticaria, angioedema, or flushing. While the identification of a causative allergen can corroborate the diagnosis, pinpointing a specific trigger is not always achievable.

More atypical manifestations, like biphasic or refractory anaphylaxis, lack clear definitions, although recent initiatives have sought to develop standardized criteria in these areas as well. According to the Resuscitation Council UK, biphasic anaphylaxis is described as the reappearance of symptoms several hours after initial resolution without any renewed exposure to the allergen. Distinguishing a genuine biphasic reaction from persistent anaphylaxis characterized by a transient improvement after epinephrine administration or by ongoing allergen absorption (for example, due to residual food or slow-release pharmaceuticals) can be problematic. In contrast, refractory anaphylaxis denotes ongoing symptoms that are unresponsive or only partially responsive to appropriately administered epinephrine.

Guidelines regarding biphasic anaphylaxis are mainly concerned with recommendations for clinical observation. Nevertheless, there is ongoing debate regarding the optimal length of monitoring following an initial anaphylactic event. Conventional recommendations suggest observation for 4 to 6 hours after resolution of symptoms. The Dutch Nederlands Huisartsen Genootschap (NHG) and the World Allergy Organisation guidelines highlight that around 50% of biphasic reactions occur within 6–12 hours post initial episode, yet neither makes explicit suggestions for observation duration. The UK guideline published in 2021, on the other hand, introduces a three-level, risk-based strategy for monitoring. Achieving consensus on observation protocols is essential for managing the tension between efficient resource allocation for the majority who experience a single anaphylactic episode and ensuring safety for the minority at risk for biphasic reactions, which are infrequent and challenging to predict.

There is a concerning deficit in specific recommendations for advanced pharmacological management in biphasic and multiphasic anaphylactic presentations. Treatment protocols for these cases generally follow conventional anaphylaxis guidelines, emphasizing the rapid administration of intramuscular epinephrine alongside necessary supportive measures. Current clinical guidelines advocate for an epinephrine dose of 0.01 mg/kg body weight, with a maximum single dose not exceeding 0.5 mg. In adults—most of whom surpass the 50 kg threshold—this typically translates to a standardized dose of 0.5 mg. Nevertheless, it is notable that widely available auto-injectors, like those employed in the initial two anaphylactic episodes described in our case study, often dispense only 0.3 mg per injection, even for adult patients. Although epinephrine auto-injectors are indispensable for early intervention in community settings, their utility extends to healthcare environments as well by expediting epinephrine administration and mitigating hesitancy among staff infrequently involved with vasoactive drug delivery. Despite their prevalence, reliance on 0.3 mg auto-injectors can result in significant underdosing for adults exceeding 50 kg. Evidence from a 2025 retrospective analysis indicated that individuals initially treated with 0.3 mg doses more frequently required further interventions, such as repeat epinephrine administration, compared with those given 0.5 mg initially. Addressing obstacles—including ingrained practitioner preferences for the historically standard 0.3 mg dose and the predominant accessibility of 0.3 mg auto-injectors—and promoting consistent application of the guideline-recommended epinephrine doses, could meaningfully enhance anaphylaxis management outcomes.

Historically, corticosteroids were advocated for use, partly to mitigate the risk of biphasic reactions. Nonetheless, accumulating data have revealed a lack of efficacy and the possibility of adverse outcomes, prompting a recent movement away from steroid administration in cases of anaphylaxis. A 2017 systematic review that evaluated 22 studies found only one in favor of corticosteroid use, while the remaining 21 showed no reduction in biphasic anaphylaxis risk. Additionally, contemporary research has highlighted concerns regarding side effects; for instance, a 2019 study reported an association between prehospital corticosteroid use and higher rates of ICU or ward admission. As a result, current guidelines largely recommend against the routine use of corticosteroids, although their application in practice continues.

Biphasic anaphylaxis, while infrequent, carries considerable clinical significance. The lack of dependable predictive markers complicates the process of identifying at-risk patients and informing observation strategies. Proposed risk factors include delayed administration of epinephrine, more severe initial symptoms, requirement for multiple epinephrine doses, and a history of biphasic responses. Still, definitive predictors to guide monitoring decisions remain unavailable.

Systemic mastocytosis, characterized by increased mast cell proliferation, heightened mediator release, and systemic inflammation, elevates the risk of severe anaphylaxis. However, both systemic mastocytosis and biphasic anaphylaxis are uncommon, and, to our knowledge, there are no studies to date that specifically investigate how systemic mastocytosis influences biphasic anaphylaxis. Advancing research in mast cell pathologies and biphasic anaphylactic reactions may yield important directions for management in the future.

Anaphylaxis-related fatalities remain uncommon, particularly in areas where rapid emergency medical interventions are available. Deaths resulting from biphasic anaphylaxis are even more infrequent, despite the condition’s inherent risks. Nevertheless, anaphylaxis continues to present as a frequent emergency scenario that necessitates swift intervention and appropriate observation. Although research and guidelines often emphasize initial emergency response, the significance of ongoing medical supervision and care after the initial episode cannot be overstated. Enhanced insight into the progression of complex forms of anaphylaxis, such as biphasic reactions, holds promise for fostering more individualized, patient-focused, and resource-conscious approaches to anaphylaxis care.

The infrequency of biphasic and especially multiphasic anaphylactic reactions, combined with the inherent obstacles to conducting research during acute emergencies, complicates the establishment of robust evidence-based management protocols. In such contexts, consensus guidelines developed by experts and containing practical management advice offer significant utility, although their formulation is constrained by limited empirical data. Given how seldom these complicated forms of anaphylaxis occur, establishing a registry of relevant cases—incorporating key patient information, proposed management tactics as outlined in our report, and subsequent patient outcomes—may serve as a foundational resource for generating expert-driven, data-informed recommendations.
