Title: Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting

Margret Ann Schneider, Hans de Groot, Jan-Willem Geerts

Abstract:

Summary: Anaphylaxis represents the most critical manifestation of allergic reactions and poses a significant risk to life, necessitating immediate and effective intervention. In some instances, patients experience a return of symptoms despite initial resolution with appropriate therapy. These biphasic or even multiphasic presentations create distinct challenges for healthcare providers, as the frequency is inconsistently reported, the predisposing factors are not well defined, and current practice guidelines offer limited direction for management. This difficulty is particularly evident in cases of multiphasic anaphylaxis, where patients undergo several episodes of anaphylaxis after showing an adequate response to standard epinephrine administration. Given the infrequent nature of multiphasic anaphylaxis, individual case reports that describe management approaches are valuable resources for further understanding this condition. Here, we report on a woman in her 60s who experienced multiphasic anaphylaxis following exposure to clarithromycin, with additional findings indicating a high probability of systemic mastocytosis, although she did not strictly satisfy formal diagnostic criteria.

Background:

Anaphylaxis is a serious allergic reaction that can become life-threatening, particularly in cases of anaphylactic shock, and frequently leads patients to seek emergency care. Some individuals experience a biphasic or even multiphasic progression, marked by an initial anaphylactic event upon exposure to a specific trigger, subsequent resolution of clinical symptoms—leading to a symptom-free interval—and then a return of symptoms without further exposure to the original allergen. These recurrences typically occur within 24 hours of the first exposure, though there have been reports of delayed episodes occurring as late as 78 hours afterward. Published rates of biphasic anaphylaxis show considerable variation, from 0.4% to 23.3%, but most studies estimate an incidence between 4% and 6%. Because of the chance of delayed symptom recurrence, patients are often observed for a period following apparent resolution of anaphylaxis. Nonetheless, definitions for biphasic anaphylaxis lack uniformity, and management recommendations differ, with the efficacy of corticosteroid use recently being debated. The occurrence of multiphasic anaphylaxis, in which symptoms recur on several occasions, adds further complexity to management and observation decisions. In this report, we describe a case involving a female patient in her 60s who developed a multiphasic anaphylactic reaction after administration of clarithromycin.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 60s, with a known allergy to beta-lactam antibiotics, presented to the emergency department (ED) with anaphylaxis following oral clarithromycin 500 mg.

The patient’s past medical history was notable for type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, psoriasis, and depression. Her body weight was 104 kg. Her ongoing medications included metformin (500 mg twice daily), gliclazide (80 mg extended-release once daily), hydrochlorothiazide (25 mg once daily), omeprazole (20 mg once daily), and sertraline (100 mg once daily). The most recent dose of hydrochlorothiazide had been taken in the morning, more than 12 hours prior to arrival in the emergency department.

During adolescence, the patient had a mild allergic reaction to amoxicillin, presenting as facial swelling in the absence of oropharyngeal edema, rash, low blood pressure, or shortness of breath. She also experienced a pronounced localized cutaneous response to a wasp sting in the past, but there were no systemic manifestations. The subsequent clinical presentation represented her initial episode of anaphylaxis.

Five days prior to arriving at the emergency department, her general practitioner initiated clindamycin therapy for the treatment of erysipelas. After five days on clindamycin, she developed an abdominal rash (not further specified). This cutaneous reaction was considered to be a delayed allergy to clindamycin, and as a result, her primary care provider substituted clarithromycin—an antibiotic she had never previously received—for clindamycin.

She later arrived at the emergency department reporting itchiness, flushing, increased sweating, agitation, and a sensation of throat swelling. These complaints began one hour after ingesting her first dose of clarithromycin and progressively intensified. She presented for medical attention approximately one hour after symptoms began. On arrival, her vital signs indicated hypotension and hypoxemia (non-invasive blood pressure measured at 74/50 mm Hg, oxygen saturation of 88% on room air, respiratory rate of 22 breaths per minute). Initial physical examination revealed facial edema, newly developed generalized erythema with urticaria covering her whole body, as well as increased sweating and agitation. There was no observable swelling of her tongue or throat, and both cardiac and pulmonary auscultation were unremarkable. Her blood glucose was 12.7 mmol/L, and electrocardiogram showed sinus rhythm. She was promptly treated for suspected severe allergic reaction with an intramuscular injection of 0.3 mg epinephrine administered using an auto-injector, and a slow intravenous infusion of 2 mg clemastine to address skin symptoms according to local protocols. In addition, she received supplemental oxygen (15 L via non-rebreather mask) and 500 mL of 0.9% sodium chloride solution during the initial resuscitation. Supplemental oxygen could be discontinued following a favorable response to epinephrine, but was transiently required again during subsequent anaphylactic episodes. Clarithromycin was stopped, and she did not receive any doses other than the initial 500 mg taken orally.

At first, the therapy yielded a favorable outcome, evidenced by normalization of blood pressure and reduction in facial edema and erythema. The patient was transferred to the acute internal medicine ward for closer observation by specialized nursing personnel, though continuous cardiac monitoring was not implemented. Nonetheless, after this initial positive response, the patient experienced several recurrences of symptoms consistent with anaphylactic shock within the first 12 hours of hospitalization. Following the first recurrence, the patient was moved to the intensive care unit (ICU) for intensified observation. Each anaphylactic episode required intramuscular epinephrine administration to achieve resolution (figure 1), with a cumulative total of four episodes, including the original event in the emergency department. The initial two doses of epinephrine, each 0.3 mg intramuscularly, were given via auto-injector, while the subsequent two episodes were managed with custom-prepared intramuscular doses of 0.5 mg epinephrine. Although the auto-injectors available in our facility contain only 0.3 mg—lower than the 0.5 mg typically recommended—these devices are preferred during urgent care situations such as initial ED stabilization, or in clinical settings less accustomed to acute anaphylaxis interventions, in order to avoid delays stemming from hesitance with unfamiliar drug administration. After the first recurrence of anaphylaxis, corticosteroid therapy was introduced in line with the conventional use of steroids to help prevent biphasic anaphylactic responses. This consisted of intravenous prednisone 30 mg daily for five days, as well as a single intravenous dose of dexamethasone 8 mg, reflecting variability in our institution’s protocols between general ward and ICU management of anaphylaxis. Additionally, clemastine 2 mg intravenously was administered twice to address cutaneous manifestations, and a further 500 mL of normal saline solution was given during the second anaphylactic event. Of note, each recurrent episode presented with decreasing severity; initial episodes involved marked hypoxemia accompanied by cyanosis, hypotension, and pronounced dyspnea, while the last episode was characterized mainly by dyspnea, recurrent rash, and pruritus.

Early in the admission, the allergology department was consulted for expert input. Two alternative management strategies were weighed in this case. One involved proactive continuous intravenous infusion of epinephrine to avert recurrent anaphylaxis, rather than waiting for symptom recurrence. The second strategy, given the trend towards diminishing severity with subsequent episodes, considered an observational approach to determine if spontaneous resolution would occur. Both options were associated with substantial risk, particularly due to the limited available literature to inform management in complex, multiphasic presentations.

Outcome and follow-up:

In the end, neither intervention was required, as the patient experienced no additional recurrence of symptoms following this episode. After remaining free of new anaphylactic events for roughly 24 hours, she was moved back to a general care ward. Discharge occurred after a total hospital stay of 3 days. On discharge, levocetirizine, an antihistamine, was initiated as a routine medication, and she received both an epinephrine auto-injector (0.3 mg) and training on its use. She has not experienced any repeat episodes of anaphylaxis to date.

Given the unusual and prolonged progression of her symptoms, a more in-depth investigation into potential underlying etiologies was undertaken. This workup indicated a strong likelihood of systemic mastocytosis; however, she did not meet full diagnostic criteria (she fulfilled only two WHO minor criteria, while assessment of the major criterion was not possible due to insufficient material from the bone marrow biopsy—refer to figure 2). Regrettably, tryptase concentrations were not assessed during the initial evaluation following the first episodes of anaphylaxis, resulting in non-compliance with recommended timelines for tryptase measurement. Established guidelines advise obtaining at least two, preferably three, tryptase samples at designated times: the first collected as soon as possible without deferring therapy; a second drawn 1–2 hours after symptom onset (but not later than 4 hours), aligning with the expected peak; and a third sample secured no sooner than 24 hours after complete symptom resolution to establish a baseline value. In this case, the initial tryptase sample was drawn 1 hour subsequent to the third episode of anaphylaxis, and the follow-up sample was obtained 27 hours after the latest occurrence.

Allergy confirmation testing was not carried out, given the undue risk of provoking severe anaphylaxis through methods such as skin testing, a risk that was considered unnecessary in light of the relative ease of avoiding further exposures. Additional noteworthy findings supporting a diagnosis of systemic mastocytosis consisted of hepatomegaly detected on abdominal ultrasound and the presence of possible neuropsychiatric features such as depression and memory difficulties.

Discussion:

Anaphylaxis is recognized as a well-established clinical entity, marked by an abrupt onset and quickly escalating symptoms that affect the airway, breathing, cardiovascular system, and/or gastrointestinal tract. It is commonly associated with skin or mucosal findings, such as urticaria, angioedema, or flushing. Although identifying an offending allergen can reinforce the diagnosis, in many cases a precise trigger cannot be established.

More complicated forms like biphasic and refractory anaphylaxis lack uniform definitions, though recent initiatives have sought to create consensus criteria. According to the Resuscitation Council UK, biphasic anaphylaxis describes the recurrence of symptoms several hours after initial resolution without renewed allergen contact. Distinguishing genuine biphasic reactions from prolonged anaphylaxis that temporarily improves with epinephrine, or from ongoing allergen exposure (for instance, lingering food or a delayed-release medication), may present difficulties. Refractory anaphylaxis, on the other hand, denotes continuous anaphylactic symptoms that do not adequately respond to properly administered epinephrine.

Guidelines concerning biphasic anaphylaxis primarily focus on recommendations for clinical observation. However, the optimal length of monitoring after symptom resolution remains contentious. Conventional practice has suggested observing patients for 4 to 6 hours once symptoms have resolved. Both the Dutch Nederlands Huisartsen Genootschap (NHG) guideline and the World Allergy Organisation guideline mention that nearly half of biphasic responses occur within 6–12 hours of the initial anaphylactic episode, yet they stop short of issuing explicit recommendations regarding observation time. In contrast, the 2021 UK guideline offers a tiered, risk-based approach to patient monitoring. Establishing standardized surveillance protocols is essential to appropriately allocate resources for the majority of single-episode anaphylaxis patients while ensuring the safety of the minority at risk for biphasic complications.

There is a notable and concerning absence of detailed recommendations regarding advanced pharmacologic approaches for managing biphasic and multiphasic anaphylactic episodes. The therapeutic approach to biphasic and multiphasic anaphylaxis generally follows established protocols for initial anaphylaxis, involving immediate administration of intramuscular epinephrine in conjunction with necessary supportive interventions. Clinical guidelines specify a recommended epinephrine dose of 0.01 mg/kg, with an upper limit of 0.5 mg per dose. For practicality, this is typically administered as a 0.5 mg dose in adults, since most adults exceed 50 kg in weight. It is noteworthy, however, that a significant number of epinephrine auto-injectors—such as those administered during the initial two episodes in the described patient—deliver only 0.3 mg per dose, even in adult patients. While auto-injectors are pivotal in prehospital care, their use in clinical environments may also be advantageous, decreasing delays in administration and reducing reluctance among healthcare staff who are less familiar with vasoactive medication use. Nonetheless, employing a 0.3 mg auto-injector in adults heavier than 50 kg may result in significant underdosing. Data from a 2025 retrospective analysis revealed that individuals initially treated with a 0.3 mg dose of epinephrine required escalated therapy, including additional epinephrine doses, more frequently compared to those who received an initial 0.5 mg dose. Addressing challenges—such as the entrenched reliance on the 0.3 mg dosage and widespread availability of this formulation, as well as enhancing adherence to guideline-recommended dosing—could substantially optimize outcomes in anaphylaxis care.

Historically, corticosteroid therapy was advocated, partly to mitigate the risk of biphasic reactions. However, accumulating research indicates not only a lack of efficacy but also potential adverse outcomes, prompting a recent move away from corticosteroid use in anaphylaxis management. A 2017 systematic review assessed 22 studies, finding that only one demonstrated a benefit from corticosteroids, while the other 21 did not show any reduction in the occurrence of biphasic anaphylaxis. Additionally, newer investigations highlight the importance of adverse effects; notably, a 2019 study found a higher probability of ICU or hospital admission following prehospital administration of corticosteroids. As a result, current guidelines generally do not recommend routine corticosteroid use, despite their continued application in some clinical settings.

Although biphasic anaphylaxis occurs infrequently, it is of significant clinical concern. The challenge of forecasting risk is compounded by a lack of dependable predictors, complicating surveillance and decision-making processes. Possible risk indicators include delayed administration of epinephrine, an initially severe reaction, need for multiple epinephrine injections, and history of prior biphasic reactions. Nonetheless, consistent predictors to enable selective monitoring have yet to be identified.

Systemic mastocytosis—characterized by proliferation of mast cells, excessive mediator release, and systemic inflammation—places individuals at elevated risk for severe anaphylactic episodes. 1517 Nevertheless, both conditions are rare and, to date, no studies have specifically examined the impact of systemic mastocytosis on the occurrence of biphasic anaphylaxis. Additional investigations exploring the intersection of mast cell pathologies and biphasic anaphylactic reactions may yield important information to guide future management strategies.

Anaphylaxis-related mortality rates are generally low, particularly in areas where emergency medical services are readily available. Moreover, fatal incidents resulting from biphasic anaphylaxis are exceedingly uncommon, despite the condition’s potentially severe presentation. Nevertheless, anaphylaxis continues to represent a frequent acute condition that necessitates immediate intervention and proper surveillance. While most research and guidelines concentrate on the acute phase of emergency care, it is crucial to emphasize the significance of ongoing monitoring and management after the initial event. Enhanced comprehension of complex presentations such as biphasic anaphylaxis may facilitate the development of management approaches that are more individualized, effective, and resource-conscious.

The infrequency of biphasic and, in particular, multiphasic anaphylaxis, coupled with the methodological challenges inherent in studying emergency scenarios, poses substantial obstacles to formulating evidence-based management protocols. In such contexts, consensus guidelines created by experts are an invaluable resource, although their formulation is restricted by the paucity of robust data. Given the uncommon nature of these complicated cases, establishing a dedicated registry—including detailed patient information, potential management recommendations as described in our case, and outcomes—may serve as a critical resource to inform the creation of expert-driven, data-supported guidelines.
