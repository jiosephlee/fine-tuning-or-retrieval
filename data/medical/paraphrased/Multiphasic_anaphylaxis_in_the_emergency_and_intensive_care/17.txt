Title: Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting

Margret Ann Schneider, Hans de Groot, Jan-Willem Geerts

Abstract:

Summary: Anaphylaxis represents the most severe manifestation of allergic reactions and is a potentially fatal, systemic event that necessitates immediate and effective intervention. In some instances, symptoms may recur even after apparent clinical resolution and proper initial management. Such biphasic or multiphasic patterns of anaphylactic response complicate clinical care, especially given inconsistent reports of frequency, poorly defined risk factors, and the absence of clear recommendations in existing guidelines. This challenge is particularly pronounced in cases of multiphasic anaphylaxis, in which patients experience multiple episodes of anaphylaxis in spite of an appropriate response to standard epinephrine administration. Since multiphasic anaphylaxis is relatively uncommon, published case reports outlining clinical approaches offer valuable insights into handling these scenarios. Here, we describe the case of a female patient in her 60s who experienced multiphasic anaphylaxis following exposure to clarithromycin; further evaluation suggested a strong possibility of systemic mastocytosis, although she did not fulfill the formal diagnostic criteria for the condition.

Background:

Anaphylaxis represents a severe allergic response that can be fatal, particularly when it progresses to anaphylactic shock, and is a frequent reason for emergency department visits. In certain instances, anaphylaxis demonstrates a biphasic or even multiphasic progression, wherein an initial reaction to an allergen subsides, symptoms temporarily disappear, and then recur without any new allergen exposure. Typically, this symptom recurrence occurs within 24 hours after the initial exposure; however, episodes have been documented as late as 78 hours afterward. Estimates of the frequency of biphasic anaphylaxis are highly variable, ranging from 0.4% up to 23.3%, though most studies report an incidence of 4% to 6%. Because symptoms may recur, patients are generally monitored following an anaphylactic event, even after apparent full recovery. Nevertheless, there is inconsistency in the definitions of biphasic anaphylaxis and a lack of consensus in management recommendations, with increasing debate over the efficacy of corticosteroids. Multiphasic anaphylaxis, where symptoms resurface on several occasions, poses particular challenges in determining appropriate observation times and treatment approaches. Here, we describe the case of a woman in her sixth decade of life who developed a multiphasic anaphylactic response after receiving clarithromycin.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 60s, with a known allergy to beta-lactam antibiotics, presented to the emergency department (ED) with anaphylaxis following oral clarithromycin 500 mg.

The patient's past medical history was notable for type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, psoriasis, and depression. She weighed 104 kg. Her current medications were metformin 500 mg twice daily, gliclazide retard 80 mg once daily, hydrochlorothiazide 25 mg once daily, omeprazole 20 mg once daily, and sertraline 100 mg once daily. She had last taken hydrochlorothiazide in the morning, more than 12 hours prior to her presentation to the emergency department.

As an adolescent, she experienced a mild allergic response to amoxicillin characterized by swelling of the face without involvement of the oropharynx, rash, hypotension, or respiratory symptoms. Additionally, she had previously had a pronounced localized skin reaction to a wasp sting but had not experienced systemic manifestations. The clinical admission described below was her first occurrence of anaphylaxis.

Five days prior to her emergency department visit, her general practitioner prescribed clindamycin for erysipelas. After five days of taking clindamycin, she developed an abdominal rash (with no further details provided), which was considered to represent a delayed allergic reaction to clindamycin. Consequently, her general practitioner discontinued clindamycin and initiated clarithromycin—a medication the patient had not previously received.

She later arrived at the emergency department experiencing itching, flushing, increased sweating, agitation, and a sensation of throat swelling. These symptoms began approximately one hour after taking her first oral dose of clarithromycin, with a gradual worsening over time. She presented for medical evaluation one hour following the initial onset of symptoms. On arrival, her vital signs indicated low blood pressure and reduced oxygen saturation (non-invasive blood pressure: 74/50 mm Hg, peripheral oxygen saturation at 88% on room air, respiratory rate 22 breaths per minute). Physical examination revealed facial edema, newly developed widespread erythema, urticaria distributed across the body, along with heightened perspiration and agitation. There was no evidence of tongue or throat swelling, and both cardiac and pulmonary auscultation were unremarkable. Her blood glucose measured 12.7 mmol/L and the electrocardiogram demonstrated a sinus rhythm. Treatment for suspected severe allergic reaction was promptly initiated, which included intramuscular administration of 0.3 mg epinephrine using an auto-injector and slow intravenous infusion of 2 mg clemastine in accordance with local protocols for managing skin manifestations. She also received 15 L supplemental oxygen via a non-rebreather mask and 500 mL of 0.9% sodium chloride for initial stabilization. Oxygen supplementation was discontinued following marked clinical improvement after the epinephrine administration but needed to be resumed during subsequent episodes of anaphylaxis. Clarithromycin therapy was halted, and no further doses were taken beyond the initial single oral dose of 500 mg.

Initially, the patient exhibited a favorable reaction to therapy, with normalization of blood pressure and a reduction in facial swelling and erythema. She was transferred to the acute internal medicine ward, which offers enhanced monitoring by specialized nursing personnel but does not employ telemetry. Despite this encouraging initial course, the patient subsequently experienced multiple recurrences of symptoms characteristic of anaphylactic shock within the first 12 hours of hospitalization. Following the first episode of recurrent anaphylaxis, she was moved to the intensive care unit (ICU) for closer observation. Each anaphylactic event necessitated administration of intramuscular epinephrine for symptom control (figure 1), with a total of four episodes, including the initial one presenting in the emergency department (ED). The first two doses of epinephrine were delivered via auto-injector, each providing 0.3 mg intramuscularly, whereas the subsequent two doses consisted of individually prepared 0.5 mg intramuscular injections. Although the clinic's available auto-injectors dispense a dose lower than the recommended 0.5 mg, their use is favored in time-critical situations—such as initial ED stabilization—or in environments where emergency management of anaphylaxis is infrequent and provider inexperience might impede rapid intervention. After the first recurrence of anaphylaxis, corticosteroid therapy was introduced to reflect their conventional use in preventing biphasic anaphylactic reactions. The patient received daily intravenous prednisone 30 mg for 5 days, as well as a single dose of intravenous dexamethasone 8 mg, due to protocol differences between the general ward and ICU anaphylaxis guidelines at the institution. She was also given 2 mg intravenous clemastine on two occasions for dermatologic manifestations, and 500 mL of normal saline during the second anaphylactic event. Notably, the severity of each subsequent anaphylactic episode progressively diminished, evolving from marked hypoxemia accompanied by cyanosis, hypotension, and severe dyspnea in the initial episode to dyspnea, rash recurrence, and pruritus during the final event.

An early consultation with the allergology department was made to obtain specialized therapeutic recommendations. Two alternative management strategies were evaluated in this context. The first entailed continuous intravenous infusion of epinephrine, thereby aiming to prevent recurrence proactively rather than reacting to repeat symptoms. The second approach, prompted by the gradual reduction in symptom intensity, was to observe with expectancy and monitor for spontaneous resolution. Both management strategies entailed significant risks, especially given the absence of supporting literature or established guidelines for treating multiphasic anaphylaxis.

Outcome and follow-up:

In the end, neither of the proposed treatments was required because the patient experienced no additional recurrence of symptoms beyond this point. After remaining free of anaphylactic symptoms for about 24 hours, she was moved from intensive care to a regular medical ward. Her hospital stay lasted three days before discharge. At discharge, she began therapy with levocetirizine, a new antihistamine for her, and received both training in and prescription of a 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injector. Since then, she has not had any repeat episodes of anaphylaxis.

Given the unusual and persistent nature of her symptoms, further investigation into possible underlying etiologies was initiated. This assessment indicated a high likelihood of systemic mastocytosis, although diagnostic criteria for a definitive diagnosis were not met (she satisfied two of the WHO minor criteria, but the major criterion could not be evaluated due to limited sampling during bone marrow biopsy—refer to figure 2). Regrettably, tryptase measurements were not performed during the initial evaluation of the patient’s early anaphylactic events, resulting in non-compliance with the suggested sampling intervals for tryptase determination. Current guidelines advise collecting at least two, and preferably three, tryptase samples at defined time points: the first sample should be collected as soon as possible after symptom onset without delaying necessary therapy; a second sample should follow 1–2 hours later (but within 4 hours), aligning with the typical peak in tryptase concentration; and a third should be obtained no sooner than 24 hours after complete clinical recovery to assess baseline levels. In this instance, the first tryptase was drawn 1 hour after the third episode of anaphylaxis, and the subsequent sample was taken 27 hours following the final recurrence.

No allergy confirmation tests were conducted, as the potential risk of provoking another severe anaphylactic reaction—for example, through skin testing—was judged disproportionate, especially since avoiding further exposure is relatively straightforward. Additional pertinent findings supportive of a diagnosis of systemic mastocytosis included hepatomegaly observed on abdominal ultrasound, as well as possible neuropsychiatric manifestations such as depression and memory impairment.

Discussion:

Anaphylaxis is a clinically distinct condition, marked by the abrupt appearance and rapid escalation of symptoms affecting the airway, respiratory system, circulation, and/or gastrointestinal tract, commonly presenting with cutaneous or mucosal features such as urticaria, angioedema, or flushing. Identifying a probable allergen can assist in confirming the diagnosis, yet a specific trigger cannot always be established.

More complicated cases like biphasic or refractory anaphylaxis lack precise definitions, although recent initiatives have sought to create consensus terminology. According to the Resuscitation Council UK, biphasic anaphylaxis is described as the return of symptoms hours after their apparent resolution, without additional allergen exposure. It can be difficult to distinguish between genuine biphasic episodes and ongoing anaphylaxis resulting from only temporary symptom relief after epinephrine administration or continual allergen absorption, as might occur from residual food particles or slow-release medications. Refractory anaphylaxis, on the other hand, is characterized by ongoing symptoms despite appropriate epinephrine therapy.

Guidelines addressing biphasic anaphylaxis primarily focus on recommendations for patient observation, but the ideal monitoring timeframe remains unsettled. Conventional monitoring durations are between 4 and 6 hours after symptoms have subsided. The Dutch Nederlands Huisartsen Genootschap (NHG) and the World Allergy Organisation guidelines observe that nearly half of biphasic responses develop within 6–12 hours following the initial episode, although neither offers precise guidance on the length of observation. Conversely, the 2021 UK guideline introduces a three-tier risk-based monitoring system. There remains a significant need for unified monitoring protocols to reconcile the efficient use of healthcare resources, given the large number of patients with uncomplicated anaphylaxis, with the imperative to protect those at risk for infrequent but hard-to-predict biphasic reactions.

There remains a significant gap in guidance regarding advanced pharmacologic management for biphasic and multiphasic anaphylactic episodes. Treatment protocols for such cases largely align with those for typical anaphylaxis, focusing on rapid intramuscular administration of epinephrine along with suitable supportive measures. Clinical guidelines specify a dose of 0.01 mg/kg, up to a maximum of 0.5 mg in total. In practice, adult patients—most of whom weigh over 50 kg—are frequently administered a simplified fixed dose of 0.5 mg. Notably, numerous auto-injectors—including those utilized during the initial two anaphylactic reactions described in our case—deliver only 0.3 mg of epinephrine, even when intended for adult patients. While epinephrine auto-injectors are crucial in prehospital care, their role persists within medical facilities by allowing for swift administration and alleviating apprehension among staff with limited exposure to vasoactive agents. Nevertheless, administering a 0.3 mg auto-injector in adults over 50 kg, though commonplace, poses a significant risk of underdosing. Data from a 2025 retrospective study revealed that individuals initially treated with a 0.3 mg epinephrine dose more frequently required escalation measures, such as supplementary injections, compared with those who received the full 0.5 mg. Addressing impediments—including longstanding reliance on the 0.3 mg dose and the widespread availability of auto-injectors containing this amount—along with reinforcing adherence to recommended dosing standards, may markedly enhance the quality of anaphylaxis management.

Historically, corticosteroids have been recommended partly to reduce the incidence of biphasic reactions. More recent data, however, reveal a lack of efficacy and the possibility of adverse effects, resulting in a movement away from steroid use for anaphylaxis treatment. A 2017 systematic review analyzed 22 studies, finding only one that supported corticosteroid use, while the remaining 21 demonstrated no reduction in biphasic anaphylaxis risk. Additionally, newer research underscores the significance of steroid side effects, with a 2019 study indicating increased rates of ICU or hospital admission following prehospital steroid administration. As a result, current guidelines generally do not support routine corticosteroid use, despite persistent use in clinical settings.

Although infrequent, biphasic anaphylaxis remains a clinically significant event. The lack of dependable predictors creates challenges in evaluating risk and determining appropriate observation strategies. Potential risk factors proposed include delayed administration of epinephrine, severe symptoms at onset, need for repeated epinephrine dosing, and a prior history of biphasic reactions. Nevertheless, there are currently no reliable predictors to effectively guide selective monitoring.

Systemic mastocytosis, characterized by pathological mast cell proliferation, excessive mediator release, and widespread inflammation, confers an increased risk for severe anaphylactic reactions. 1517 Nonetheless, because both systemic mastocytosis and biphasic anaphylaxis are uncommon, there is, to our knowledge, no published research specifically investigating the influence of systemic mastocytosis on biphasic anaphylaxis. Future investigations into the association between mast cell disorders and biphasic anaphylaxis may contribute important information to guide clinical management.

Thankfully, deaths due to anaphylaxis are uncommon, particularly in areas where emergency care is readily available. Moreover, lethal cases of biphasic anaphylaxis are even more infrequent, despite their seemingly high risk. Nevertheless, despite these encouraging figures, anaphylaxis continues to present as a frequent medical emergency that requires swift intervention and vigilant observation. Although research and guidelines primarily address acute management in the emergency setting, ongoing follow-up care and surveillance remain critical. Gaining a deeper insight into more complex manifestations of anaphylaxis, such as biphasic reactions, has the potential to inform a more individualized, focused, and resource-conscious management approach.

The infrequency of biphasic and, more notably, multiphasic anaphylaxis—combined with the inherent difficulties of conducting research during emergencies—makes formulating robust, evidence-based protocols particularly challenging. In this context, consensus guidelines developed by experts, offering explicit management recommendations, represent a highly valuable approach; however, even the creation of these guidelines is hindered by the scarcity of comprehensive data. Considering the exceptional rarity of these intricate anaphylaxis presentations, establishing a dedicated registry to capture these cases—including key patient details, proposed management strategies as presented in this report, and outcome metrics—could serve as a critical foundation for developing data-driven, expert-informed recommendations.
