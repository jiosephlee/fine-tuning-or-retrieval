Title: Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting

Margret Ann Schneider, Hans de Groot, Jan-Willem Geerts

Abstract:

Summary: Anaphylaxis represents the most severe manifestation of allergic reactions, characterized as a life-threatening systemic event necessitating urgent and effective intervention. In some instances, symptoms recur even after appropriate management and resolution of the initial episode. Such biphasic or multiphasic trajectories pose particular difficulties for healthcare providers, given that reported rates vary, contributing risk factors remain uncertain, and specific management recommendations are not well established in current protocols. This is particularly relevant for cases of multiphasic anaphylaxis, where patients experience repeated anaphylactic episodes despite appropriate treatment with epinephrine. Because multiphasic anaphylaxis is infrequently encountered, case reports detailing clinical handling may offer valuable insights into its management. Here, we describe the case of a female patient in her sixties who developed multiphasic anaphylaxis in response to clarithromycin; subsequent evaluation revealed a strong suspicion for systemic mastocytosis, although formal diagnostic criteria were not fulfilled.

Background:

Anaphylaxis is a serious allergic reaction that can be life-threatening, particularly in cases of anaphylactic shock, and it frequently results in patients seeking emergency medical attention. In certain cases, the clinical course becomes biphasic or multiphasic, where an initial allergic response occurs after exposure to a trigger, symptoms then subside leading to an interval without symptoms, but subsequently recur even in the absence of repeat allergen exposure. These recurrent symptoms most often appear within 24 hours following the initial contact, but there are documented cases where they have manifested as late as 78 hours afterward. The reported prevalence of biphasic anaphylaxis varies significantly, with figures ranging from 0.4% up to 23.3%, though the majority of evidence indicates an incidence between 4% and 6%. Owing to the risk of such relapse, a period of clinical monitoring is generally recommended after apparent recovery from anaphylaxis. Nonetheless, existing definitions for biphasic anaphylaxis are sometimes ambiguous and treatment protocols are not standardized, with recent debates regarding the use of corticosteroids. Multiphasic anaphylaxis, characterized by several episodes of symptom recurrence, presents additional complexity concerning management and monitoring. Here, we describe a case involving a woman in her 60s who experienced a multiphasic anaphylactic reaction following exposure to clarithromycin.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 60s, with a documented beta-lactam allergy, arrived at the emergency department (ED) experiencing anaphylaxis after ingesting oral clarithromycin 500 mg.

Her medical history consisted of type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, psoriasis, and depression. She weighed 104 kg. The medications she was taking included metformin (500 mg twice daily), gliclazide (80 mg retard once daily), hydrochlorothiazide (25 mg once daily), omeprazole (20 mg once daily), and sertraline (100 mg once daily). Her most recent dose of hydrochlorothiazide was administered that morning, more than 12 hours prior to her arrival at the ED.

As an adolescent, she experienced a mild allergic reaction to amoxicillin, characterized solely by facial swelling and without oropharyngeal edema, rash, hypotension, or dyspnea. She had also previously developed a pronounced localized skin response to a wasp sting, which did not progress to systemic involvement. The episode leading to her current admission represented her initial occurrence of anaphylaxis.

Five days prior to presenting at the ED, she was prescribed clindamycin for erysipelas by her general practitioner. After five days of treatment with clindamycin, she developed an abdominal rash (details not specified). This reaction was considered by her general practitioner to be a delayed allergic response to clindamycin, prompting a change to clarithromycin, an antibiotic she had not received before.

She later arrived at the emergency department experiencing itching, flushing, increased sweating, restlessness, and a sensation of throat swelling. These symptoms emerged one hour following ingestion of her first clarithromycin dose and became more pronounced over time. She presented for medical assessment one hour after symptoms began. On arrival, her vital parameters revealed low blood pressure and reduced oxygen saturation (non-invasive blood pressure measured at 74/50 mm Hg, oxygen saturation at 88% while breathing room air, respiratory rate of 22 breaths per minute). The physical examination identified swelling of the face, newly developed widespread erythema, urticaria affecting her whole body, along with increased sweating and agitation. There was no evidence of tongue or throat swelling, and auscultation of the heart and lungs was unremarkable. Blood glucose was measured at 12.7 mmol/L, and her ECG demonstrated sinus rhythm. She was promptly managed for suspected severe allergic reaction with an intramuscular administration of 0.3 mg epinephrine via auto-injector and a slow intravenous dose of 2 mg clemastine to address her cutaneous symptoms, according to local protocols. She also received high-flow supplemental oxygen (15 L through a non-rebreather mask) and 500 mL of isotonic sodium chloride (0.9% NaCl) during the initial stabilization. The supplemental oxygen was discontinued after a favorable response to epinephrine but needed to be temporarily reinstated during recurrent anaphylactic episodes. Clarithromycin therapy was stopped, and she did not take any further doses after the initial 500 mg taken orally.

At the outset, the patient showed a favorable response to therapy, as normalization of blood pressure was observed and both facial edema and erythema subsided. She was subsequently transferred to the acute internal medicine ward, where she was under close observation by specialized nursing staff, though telemetry was not employed. Nevertheless, despite this initial improvement, within the first 12 hours following admission, she experienced repeated episodes with clinical features consistent with anaphylactic shock. Following the first recurrence of anaphylactic shock, she was moved to the intensive care unit (ICU) for enhanced surveillance. Each episode necessitated intramuscular epinephrine administration for resolution (see figure 1), and in total, there were four instances of anaphylaxis, including the initial presentation in the emergency department. The initial two administrations were delivered using auto-injectors containing 0.3 mg intramuscular epinephrine, while the subsequent two doses consisted of manually prepared 0.5 mg intramuscular epinephrine. While the epinephrine auto-injector dosage available at our facility is below the recommended 0.5 mg, this delivery method is preferred in situations requiring rapid intervention (such as initial stabilization in the emergency department), or in settings where urgent anaphylaxis therapy is infrequently performed and reluctance due to unfamiliarity with medication may result in delays. After the first relapse of anaphylaxis, corticosteroid therapy was initiated, adhering to the traditional practice of corticosteroid use to prevent biphasic anaphylaxis. The patient was administered 30 mg intravenous prednisone daily for 5 days, and a single dose of 8 mg intravenous dexamethasone, reflecting differences in the standard operating procedures for anaphylaxis in the hospital’s general ward and ICU protocols. Additionally, clemastine at 2 mg was given intravenously twice for dermatologic manifestations, and during the second episode of anaphylaxis, she received 500 mL of normal saline. Notably, the severity of her symptoms diminished with each successive anaphylactic event, transitioning from profound hypoxemia with cyanosis, hypotension, and intense dyspnea initially, to dyspnea, rash recurrence, and pruritus by the final episode.

The allergology department was promptly involved for expert input regarding further management. Two alternative strategies were considered. The first was continuous intravenous epinephrine infusion to proactively mitigate anaphylaxis, rather than treating only after symptomatic relapse. The second option, based on the gradual reduction in symptom intensity, was a conservative approach to observe whether symptoms would resolve on their own. Both options entailed considerable risks, compounded by the absence of published guidance for management in multiphasic anaphylactic cases.

Outcome and follow-up:

In the end, neither intervention was required since the patient experienced no additional relapse of symptoms after this time. Following approximately 24 hours without recurrence of anaphylaxis, she was moved back to a general ward. Her hospital admission lasted three days before discharge. Upon leaving, she was prescribed the antihistamine levocetirizine as a regular medication and instructed on how to use a 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injector. She has not reported any subsequent anaphylactic episodes.

Because of the unusual and extended progression of symptoms, a more in-depth evaluation of possible underlying conditions was pursued. This assessment indicated a strong likelihood of systemic mastocytosis, although the findings fell short of a definitive diagnosis; specifically, only two WHO minor criteria were fulfilled, while the major criterion could not be assessed due to insufficient bone marrow biopsy sample (refer to figure 2). Tryptase concentrations were elevated both during the third episode of anaphylaxis and at baseline, and these increased levels persisted during follow-up with the allergy specialist, as detailed in table 1.

Regrettably, tryptase measurements were not obtained during the initial assessment of the patient's early anaphylactic episodes, resulting in non-compliance with the guideline-recommended timing for tryptase evaluation. Current recommendations advise collecting at least two, and preferably three, tryptase samples at designated time points: the first as soon as possible without interfering with treatment, the second at 1–2 hours (and no later than 4 hours) after symptom onset—coinciding with peak tryptase levels—and a third specimen a minimum of 24 hours following complete clinical resolution, to determine the patient’s baseline value. For this patient, the initial tryptase draw was obtained one hour after the third episode of anaphylaxis, and the second sample was collected 27 hours after the final anaphylactic event.

No confirmatory allergy testing was conducted because provoking another severe anaphylactic reaction, such as might occur with skin testing, was considered unjustifiable in light of the relative simplicity with which additional exposures could be prevented. Additional pertinent findings consistent with systemic mastocytosis included the presence of hepatomegaly on abdominal ultrasound and possible neuropsychiatric manifestations, namely depression and memory impairment.

Discussion:

Anaphylaxis is a clinical diagnosis that is typically straightforward, distinguished by abrupt onset and swift progression of symptoms that affect the airway, respiratory, circulatory, or gastrointestinal systems, and frequently associated with skin or mucosal signs such as urticaria, angioedema, or flushing. Identifying a probable allergen can aid in confirming the diagnosis; however, pinpointing a specific cause is not always feasible.

More atypical forms, like biphasic and refractory anaphylaxis, are not as clearly characterized, although recent initiatives have attempted to reach consensus definitions in this area. According to the Resuscitation Council UK, biphasic anaphylaxis is described as a recurrence of symptoms several hours after initial resolution, without any further allergen exposure. Differentiating genuine biphasic episodes from continuous anaphylactic reactions with brief improvement due to epinephrine administration, or from ongoing allergen absorption (such as residual food or extended-release medications), can pose diagnostic difficulties. Conversely, refractory anaphylaxis describes persistent anaphylactic manifestations that do not respond, or respond insufficiently, to appropriately administered epinephrine.

Recommendations in guidelines regarding biphasic anaphylaxis predominantly concern clinical observation; nonetheless, the most effective length of monitoring remains contentious. Standard observation durations typically span 4 to 6 hours after symptom resolution. The Dutch Nederlands Huisartsen Genootschap (NHG) and the World Allergy Organisation guideline both note that around 50% of biphasic reactions arise within 6–12 hours following the initial occurrence, though they do not specify a monitoring timeframe. The UK guideline (2021) offers a risk-based, three-tiered approach to observation. Establishing consensus on monitoring strategies is necessary to address the need to optimize resource allocation for the larger population with single-episode anaphylaxis while ensuring safety for patients at risk of complications in the small and difficult-to-identify biphasic group.

There is a significant gap in guidance regarding advanced medication management for biphasic and multiphasic presentations, which is a cause for concern. Treatment of biphasic and multiphasic anaphylaxis typically aligns with established anaphylaxis protocols, involving rapid intramuscular administration of epinephrine along with suitable supportive measures. Current guidelines advise an epinephrine dose of 0.01 mg/kg, not to exceed a total of 0.5 mg. For adults, this recommendation is often distilled to a 0.5 mg dose, as most adult patients weigh more than 50 kg. Notably, many available auto-injectors—including those used to address the initial two anaphylactic events in the case described—contain only 0.3 mg of epinephrine, despite their labeling for adult use. While epinephrine auto-injectors are invaluable in prehospital environments, they may also offer advantages in clinical settings by expediting administration and decreasing hesitancy among providers who seldom utilize vasoactive agents. Nevertheless, employing a 0.3 mg auto-injector for adults exceeding 50 kg can result in a concerning level of underdosing. Retrospective data from 2025 demonstrated that recipients of a 0.3 mg epinephrine dose more frequently required escalated care, such as repeat doses of epinephrine, in comparison to those initially given 0.5 mg. Addressing obstacles—including practitioner familiarity with the standard 0.3 mg dose and the prevalent supply of 0.3 mg auto-injectors—along with promoting adherence to dosage guidelines, has the potential to substantially enhance outcomes in anaphylaxis therapy.

Historically, corticosteroids were advocated for anaphylaxis treatment, partly to reduce the incidence of biphasic reactions. Nonetheless, accumulating data indicate a lack of benefit and possible adverse outcomes, prompting a recent movement away from steroid use for anaphylaxis management. A 2017 systematic review assessed 22 studies and found only one in favor of corticosteroid administration, while the remaining 21 studies failed to demonstrate any decrease in biphasic anaphylaxis risk associated with steroids. Moreover, current research underscores the significance of adverse effects, including a 2019 study that identified increased rates of admission to ICU or hospital wards following prehospital corticosteroid therapy. As a result, most modern protocols do not endorse routine corticosteroid use, yet they remain frequently employed in practice.

Although uncommon, biphasic anaphylaxis poses substantial clinical concern. The lack of definitive predictive factors complicates both risk stratification and decisions regarding patient observation. Proposed predictors comprise late epinephrine delivery, intense symptoms at onset, administration of multiple epinephrine doses, and a history of biphasic reactions. Despite these suggestions, established predictors that facilitate selective monitoring are not currently available.

Systemic mastocytosis, characterized by proliferation of mast cells, excessive release of mediators, and widespread inflammation affecting multiple organs, increases the risk for severe anaphylaxis.1517 Nevertheless, the concurrent rarity of both conditions means there is presently no research specifically exploring systemic mastocytosis as a contributor to biphasic anaphylaxis. Ongoing investigation into mast cell disorders and biphasic reactions could enhance understanding and guide future therapeutic approaches.

Anaphylaxis-related mortality remains low, particularly in areas where rapid emergency care is accessible. Fatal biphasic anaphylaxis events are exceedingly uncommon, despite their perceived potential severity. Nonetheless, despite these encouraging figures, anaphylaxis frequently presents as a medical emergency that necessitates immediate intervention and careful observation. While most research and clinical guidelines prioritize the initial emergency management, attention to subsequent care and surveillance is also critically important. Enhanced insight into atypical anaphylaxis patterns, such as biphasic anaphylaxis, may lead to more individualized, targeted, and efficient approaches in managing this condition.

The infrequency of biphasic—and especially multiphasic—anaphylaxis, along with obstacles intrinsic to emergency research, complicates the establishment of fully evidence-based management protocols. In such contexts, consensus guidelines grounded in expert opinion with explicit recommendations are highly valuable, though their development is likewise constrained by a limited data pool. Given the rarity of these complex anaphylactic presentations, establishing a registry that captures pertinent patient characteristics, proposed management strategies like those in our report, and outcomes, could serve as a foundation for generating data-driven, expert-informed management guidelines.
