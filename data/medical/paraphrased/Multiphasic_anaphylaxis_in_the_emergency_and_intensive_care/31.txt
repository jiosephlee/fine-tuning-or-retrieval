Title: Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting

Margret Ann Schneider, Hans de Groot, Jan-Willem Geerts

Abstract:

Summary: Anaphylaxis represents the most dangerous type of allergic response and can be fatal without rapid and appropriate intervention. In certain instances, patients experience return of symptoms after initial resolution, even following proper treatment and full symptom control. These biphasic or multiphasic patterns pose significant difficulties for healthcare providers, particularly because reported frequencies differ, the risk factors remain uncertain, and established management guidelines are lacking. This issue is especially pronounced in cases of multiphasic anaphylaxis, where repeated episodes of anaphylaxis occur after appropriate therapy with epinephrine. Given the uncommon nature of multiphasic anaphylaxis, detailed case reports serve as valuable sources of clinical information for managing such scenarios. Here, we describe the case of a woman in her sixth decade who developed multiphasic anaphylaxis in response to clarithromycin; further evaluation suggested a strong probability of underlying systemic mastocytosis, though she did not fulfill the full diagnostic criteria for the condition.

Background:

Anaphylaxis is a serious, and in the case of anaphylactic shock, potentially fatal hypersensitivity reaction, representing a frequent reason for emergency department visits. While the majority of cases follow a monophasic pattern, a subset exhibits either biphasic or even multiphasic evolution. This pattern involves an initial episode of anaphylaxis triggered by an allergen, subsequent resolution of symptoms and an asymptomatic period, and then recurrence of symptoms in the absence of further exposure. Typically, these recurrences manifest within 24 hours after the primary event, although literature notes episodes reappearing as late as 78 hours post-exposure. The reported rate of biphasic anaphylaxis varies significantly, from 0.4% to 23.3%, with most sources suggesting an incidence between 4% and 6%. Because of the risk for a delayed second phase, monitoring after resolution of initial symptoms is frequently recommended. Nevertheless, definitions of biphasic anaphylaxis are not always uniform and management recommendations can differ, particularly with recent scrutiny over the role of corticosteroids. Multiphasic anaphylaxis, which involves more than two symptom relapses, introduces additional complexity in therapeutic and surveillance strategies. Here, we describe the case of a female patient in her 60s who developed a multiphasic anaphylactic response following clarithromycin exposure.

Case presentation:

A woman in her sixties with a documented beta-lactam allergy came to the emergency department (ED) after developing anaphylaxis triggered by oral clarithromycin 500 mg.

Her past medical history was notable for type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, psoriasis, and depression. She weighed 104 kg. Current medications included metformin 500 mg twice daily, gliclazide 80 mg retard once daily, hydrochlorothiazide 25 mg once daily, omeprazole 20 mg once daily, and sertraline 100 mg once daily. The most recent dose of hydrochlorothiazide had been taken earlier that morning, more than 12 hours prior to her ED visit.

As a teenager, the patient experienced a mild hypersensitivity reaction to amoxicillin, characterized solely by facial edema, without involvement of the oropharynx, cutaneous eruption, hypotension, or breathing difficulties. She also recalled a pronounced localized dermal response to a wasp sting, but denied any systemic manifestations. The anaphylactic episode described above represented her first such occurrence.

Five days prior to her ED visit, her general practitioner had initiated clindamycin for treatment of erysipelas. After five days on clindamycin, she developed a non-specified rash localized to her abdomen. This was assessed as a delayed allergic response to clindamycin, leading the general practitioner to discontinue clindamycin and commence clarithromycin, which she had not previously received.

She later arrived at the emergency department reporting itching, flushing, increased sweating, agitation, and a sensation of throat swelling. The onset of these symptoms occurred one hour after ingesting the first dose of clarithromycin, with progressive worsening noted. She presented for medical evaluation one hour after the symptoms first appeared. At her initial emergency department assessment, her vital signs revealed hypotension and hypoxaemia (non-invasive blood pressure 74/50 mm Hg, peripheral oxygen saturation 88% while breathing room air, respiratory rate 22 breaths per minute). Examination identified facial swelling, newly developed widespread erythema and urticaria covering her body, increased sweating, and agitation. There was no observed swelling of the tongue or throat, and auscultation of the heart and lungs was unremarkable. Her blood glucose measured 12.7 mmol/L, and her ECG displayed sinus rhythm. She immediately received treatment for a suspected severe allergic reaction, including 0.3 mg epinephrine intramuscularly via auto-injector and a slow intravenous injection of 2 mg clemastine to address the skin symptoms per local protocol. Additionally, she was given supplemental oxygen at 15 L via a non-rebreather mask and a 500 mL infusion of normal saline (0.9% sodium chloride) during initial stabilisation. Oxygen supplementation was discontinued following a favorable response to epinephrine but had to be temporarily resumed during subsequent episodes of anaphylaxis. Clarithromycin therapy was stopped, and no further doses were administered following the initial 500 mg oral dose.

An initial favorable response to therapy was noted, with normalization of blood pressure and reduction in facial edema and erythema. The patient was then admitted to the acute internal medicine ward, which offers enhanced observation by specialized nursing personnel but lacks telemetry monitoring. Nonetheless, after the initial improvement, the patient experienced multiple episodes resembling anaphylactic shock within the first 12 hours of hospitalization. Following the first recurrence of anaphylactic shock, the patient was transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) for closer observation. Each episode was managed with intramuscular epinephrine administration (figure 1), amounting to a total of four anaphylactic events, including the initial presentation in the emergency department (ED). The initial two epinephrine administrations were via auto-injector, delivering 0.3 mg epinephrine intramuscularly, while the subsequent two involved manually prepared doses of 0.5 mg epinephrine intramuscularly. Although the auto-injectors at our facility contain a lower dose than the recommended 0.5 mg, they are preferred during urgent scenarios such as initial stabilization in the ED or in settings where emergency treatments like anaphylaxis are infrequently performed and potential hesitation with unfamiliar drugs might delay therapy. After the first recurrence of anaphylaxis, corticosteroids were added in accordance with their traditional use in preventing biphasic anaphylactic reactions. The patient received intravenous prednisone at 30 mg daily for 5 days and a single dose of 8 mg dexamethasone intravenously, reflecting differing recommendations in our hospital's general ward and intensive care anaphylaxis protocols. Additionally, she was administered 2 mg intravenous clemastine twice to address skin manifestations, and 500 mL of normal saline during the second anaphylactic event. Notably, symptom severity decreased with each subsequent episode, initially presenting as profound hypoxemia with cyanosis, hypotension, and severe dyspnea, and ultimately manifesting as dyspnea, recurrent rash, and pruritus during the final episode.

The allergology department was consulted early for guidance on specialized management. Two alternative strategies were evaluated: first, preemptive continuous intravenous epinephrine infusion to prevent further anaphylaxis episodes, and second, a watchful waiting approach, given the gradual attenuation of symptoms, to allow for potential spontaneous resolution. Both options posed substantial risks, particularly in the context of scant literature to support treatment recommendations for multiphasic presentations.

Outcome and follow-up:

In the end, neither therapeutic option was required, as the patient experienced no recurrence of symptoms beyond this episode. After roughly 24 hours without additional episodes of anaphylaxis, she was moved to a general medical ward. Her hospital stay totaled 3 days, after which she was discharged. Levocetirizine, an antihistamine, was initiated as a routine medication, and she was provided with a 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injector, along with instructions for its use. Since then, no subsequent anaphylactic events have occurred.

Given the unusual and protracted nature of her presentation, a more extensive evaluation of possible underlying etiologies was undertaken. This workup indicated a strong likelihood of systemic mastocytosis; however, a definitive diagnosis could not be established as only two minor WHO criteria were met, and major criteria were unassessable due to insufficient bone marrow biopsy specimen (refer to figure 2). Both tryptase concentrations measured during the third anaphylactic reaction and at baseline were elevated and remained high during ongoing follow-up by her allergist, as detailed in table 1.

Regrettably, tryptase concentrations were not assessed during the initial evaluation of the first episodes of anaphylaxis, resulting in a failure to comply with the recommended schedule for tryptase sampling. Current guidelines advise collecting at least two, and preferably three, tryptase measurements at specific time points. The initial measurement should be obtained as soon as is practical, without delaying emergency therapy, followed by a second sample 1–2 hours after the onset of symptoms (but no later than 4 hours), which generally corresponds to the peak tryptase level. A third specimen should be collected no sooner than 24 hours after complete resolution of symptoms to determine the patient’s baseline value. In this particular case, the first tryptase level was measured 1 hour following the third episode of anaphylaxis, and the second was drawn 27 hours after the most recent event.

No confirmatory allergy testing was pursued, as the risk of provoking another serious anaphylactic reaction through procedures such as skin tests was deemed unnecessary, given that future contact with the suspected trigger could be readily prevented. Additional notable findings consistent with systemic mastocytosis were observed, including hepatomegaly detected on abdominal ultrasound and probable neuropsychiatric manifestations, such as depression and memory impairment.

Discussion:

Anaphylaxis is generally considered a clearly delineated clinical condition, marked by the abrupt appearance and swift advancement of symptoms affecting the airway, respiration, circulation, and/or gastrointestinal system, and is usually accompanied by skin or mucosal signs such as urticaria, angioedema, or flushing. Although identifying a probable allergen can aid in confirming the diagnosis, pinpointing an exact trigger is not always feasible.

More complicated scenarios, such as biphasic or refractory anaphylaxis, lack well-established definitions, though recent initiatives have sought to create standardised criteria for these as well. According to the Resuscitation Council UK, biphasic anaphylaxis is described as the return of symptoms hours after initial resolution, in the absence of new allergen exposure. Distinguishing genuine biphasic reactions from prolonged anaphylactic episodes that temporarily improve with epinephrine, or from reactions due to continued allergen presence (such as undigested food or extended-release medications), poses a diagnostic challenge. Refractory anaphylaxis, in contrast, is defined as ongoing symptoms that fail to resolve with appropriate administration of epinephrine.

Clinical guidelines regarding biphasic anaphylaxis tend to focus primarily on recommendations for patient observation. However, the ideal length of post-anaphylaxis monitoring is still under discussion. Conventional practices recommend observing patients for 4 to 6 hours after their symptoms have subsided. Both the Dutch Nederlands Huisartsen Genootschap (NHG) and the World Allergy Organisation note that about half of biphasic reactions develop within 6 to 12 hours of the initial episode, yet neither suggests a precise observation timeframe. Conversely, the 2021 UK guideline advocates a three-level, risk-based observation strategy. There remains a clear need for unified monitoring protocols to effectively balance resource allocation for a large number of single-phase anaphylaxis patients against the safety requirements for the relatively small, hard-to-predict group at risk for biphasic reactions.

There is a notable and concerning absence of detailed guidance regarding advanced pharmacological approaches for biphasic and multiphasic anaphylaxis. Typically, management strategies for these presentations are analogous to those used for standard anaphylactic episodes, emphasizing immediate intramuscular administration of epinephrine alongside suitable supportive measures. Clinical guidelines endorse an epinephrine dose of 0.01 mg/kg, capping at a total of 0.5 mg. For convenience, the adult dosage is often standardized to 0.5 mg, as most adults exceed 50 kg in body weight. Despite this, it is important to recognize that commonly available auto-injectors, like those administered during the initial two anaphylactic reactions in the case described, are frequently limited to a 0.3 mg epinephrine content, even for adult use. While epinephrine auto-injectors are crucial for early intervention outside the hospital and remain advantageous within healthcare settings by decreasing both treatment delays and staff reticence—particularly among personnel infrequently exposed to vasoactive medications—the 0.3 mg dose commonly delivered via auto-injector constitutes a potentially significant underdosage for adults weighing over 50 kg. A 2025 retrospective study determined that patients initially treated with 0.3 mg epinephrine required additional medical interventions, such as repeat epinephrine injections, considerably more often compared to those who received an initial dose of 0.5 mg. Addressing factors such as clinician familiarity with the traditionally recommended 0.3 mg dose and the widespread presence of 0.3 mg auto-injectors, and ensuring improved compliance with established epinephrine dosing protocols, may substantially enhance the management of anaphylaxis.

Historically, corticosteroids were advocated in anaphylaxis management, partly to avert biphasic reactions. Nonetheless, accumulating data now indicate an absence of benefit, with possible adverse effects, prompting a movement away from their routine use in treating anaphylaxis. A 2017 systematic review of 22 studies found only one supporting corticosteroid efficacy, while 21 offered no evidence that corticosteroids mitigate the risk of biphasic anaphylaxis. Additionally, emerging research highlights the importance of considering steroid-related side effects; a 2019 study reported an increased probability of ICU or hospital ward admission following prehospital corticosteroid administration. As a result, the majority of recent guidelines now recommend against standard corticosteroid use, though clinical practice has yet to universally reflect this change.

Biphasic anaphylaxis, though uncommon, carries considerable clinical significance. The lack of dependable predictors hampers effective risk assessment and decisions regarding monitoring. Proposed predictors include delayed administration of epinephrine, severe initial reaction, need for repeated epinephrine doses, and history of biphasic episodes. Despite these suggestions, predictive factors that enable tailored monitoring are still not established.

Systemic mastocytosis, characterized by abnormal mast cell proliferation, heightened mediator release, and widespread inflammation, increases susceptibility to severe anaphylaxis.15,17 Nevertheless, due to the rarity of both systemic mastocytosis and biphasic anaphylaxis, there is presently no research specifically examining the interplay between these two conditions. Further investigations into mast cell disorders and biphasic anaphylaxis could yield important information to inform future therapeutic approaches.

Anaphylaxis-related mortality is uncommon, particularly in areas where emergency care is promptly available. Moreover, deaths resulting from biphasic anaphylactic reactions are exceedingly rare, even though these episodes carry a perceived significant risk. Despite these favorable statistics, anaphylaxis continues to be a prevalent acute condition that requires immediate intervention and thorough observation. While the primary focus of research and guidelines has been on initial emergency management, follow-up care and monitoring are equally essential. Enhanced comprehension of complicated forms of anaphylaxis, such as biphasic reactions, holds potential for the development of more individualized, efficient, and resource-conscious approaches to anaphylaxis care.

Because biphasic and especially multiphasic anaphylactic reactions are infrequent, and research in acute settings faces many obstacles, forming truly evidence-based management strategies is particularly difficult. In such cases, consensus guidelines developed by experts with practical recommendations become highly valuable; yet, crafting such expert-based guidance is complicated by the limited data available. Given how unusual these complex forms of anaphylaxis are, establishing a registry for these cases—documenting relevant patient characteristics, potential management options (as proposed in this report), and outcomes—could serve as a foundation for more robust, data-informed expert recommendations.
