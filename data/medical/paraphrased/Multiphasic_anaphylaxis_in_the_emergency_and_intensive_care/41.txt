Title: Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting

Margret Ann Schneider, Hans de Groot, Jan-Willem Geerts

Abstract:

Summary: Anaphylaxis represents the most acute and dangerous variant of allergic reaction and necessitates immediate intervention. A subset of patients experiences the resurgence of symptoms following an initial resolution, even after appropriate therapy and favorable initial response. Such biphasic and, more rarely, multiphasic manifestations pose considerable difficulty for healthcare providers, given inconsistencies in reported prevalence, unidentified risk factors, and the absence of clear therapeutic guidance in contemporary protocols. This complexity is heightened in cases of multiphasic anaphylaxis, where repeated episodes of anaphylaxis occur despite satisfactory treatment with epinephrine. Given the infrequency of multiphasic anaphylaxis, individual case reports serve as valuable sources of information regarding clinical approaches. This report details a case involving a female patient in her sixth decade who developed multiphasic anaphylaxis induced by clarithromycin. Further evaluation indicated a strong possibility of systemic mastocytosis, although she failed to meet established diagnostic benchmarks.

Background:

Anaphylaxis is a serious allergic reaction that can be fatal, especially in cases of anaphylactic shock, and is a frequent reason for patients seeking emergency medical attention. Some patients experience a biphasic or even multiphasic pattern, in which an initial anaphylactic response to an allergen occurs, followed by a symptom-free interval, and then a resurgence of symptoms in the absence of additional allergen exposure. This recurrence typically occurs within the first 24 hours after the initial event, though instances of symptom return have been documented as late as 78 hours after exposure. The reported prevalence of biphasic anaphylaxis is highly variable, spanning 0.4% to 23.3%, but most evidence suggests an incidence rate between 4% and 6%. Because of the risk of symptom relapse, patients are commonly observed for a period after initial symptom resolution. Nevertheless, the exact definition of biphasic anaphylaxis lacks uniformity and recommendations for management differ, especially regarding the use of corticosteroids, which has come under scrutiny recently. Multiphasic anaphylaxis, involving several recurrences of symptoms, poses particular difficulties for clinical management and monitoring. Here, we detail the case of a woman in her 60s who experienced a multiphasic anaphylactic episode in response to clarithromycin.

Case presentation:

A woman in her sixties, known to have a beta-lactam antibiotic allergy, came to the emergency department with anaphylaxis shortly after ingesting clarithromycin 500 mg orally.

Her past medical history included type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, psoriasis, and depression. She weighed 104 kg. Current medications consisted of metformin 500 mg twice daily, gliclazide 80 mg sustained release once daily, hydrochlorothiazide 25 mg once daily, omeprazole 20 mg once daily, and sertraline 100 mg once daily. Her last intake of hydrochlorothiazide was over 12 hours prior to her arrival at the ED, taken in the morning.

During adolescence, she experienced a mild allergic response to amoxicillin, characterized only by facial swelling; there was no oropharyngeal swelling, rash, hypotension, or dyspnoea. She had also had a pronounced local skin reaction to a wasp sting without systemic manifestations. This event marked her first occurrence of anaphylaxis.

Five days prior to her ED visit, her general practitioner initiated clindamycin treatment for erysipelas. After using clindamycin for five days, she developed a rash localized to her abdomen, with no further details given. This was considered a delayed allergic reaction to clindamycin, prompting her general practitioner to change her antibiotic to clarithromycin, to which she had never previously been exposed.

She later arrived at the emergency department experiencing itching, flushing, increased sweating, agitation, and a sensation of swelling in her throat. These symptoms began one hour following ingestion of the first clarithromycin dose, progressively worsening. She presented for medical evaluation one hour after her symptoms first appeared. Upon arrival, her vital signs revealed hypotension and hypoxemia (non-invasive blood pressure 74/50 mm Hg, peripheral oxygen saturation 88% on room air, respiratory rate 22 breaths per minute). The initial physical assessment identified facial edema, newly developed generalized erythema and urticaria covering her body, increased sweating, and agitation. There was no evidence of tongue or throat swelling, and both cardiac and pulmonary auscultation were normal. Blood glucose measured 12.7 mmol/L, and ECG demonstrated sinus rhythm. She immediately received treatment for a presumed severe allergic reaction, including intramuscular epinephrine (0.3 mg administered via auto-injector) and slow intravenous clemastine (2 mg) targeting the cutaneous manifestations, in accordance with local protocols. Additional measures comprised oxygen supplementation (15 L via non-rebreather mask) and infusion of 500 mL normal saline (0.9% sodium chloride) for initial stabilization. Oxygen supplementation was discontinued after she responded well to epinephrine, though it needed to be temporarily reinitiated during later episodes of anaphylaxis. Clarithromycin was stopped; no further doses were administered beyond the initial oral 500 mg.

The patient initially exhibited a favorable clinical response to the administered therapy, as evidenced by normalization of blood pressure and reduction in both facial edema and erythema. She was transferred to the acute internal medicine ward for enhanced monitoring by specialized nursing personnel, though telemetry was not utilized. Despite this promising early response, the patient subsequently experienced multiple recurrent episodes suggestive of anaphylactic shock within the first 12 hours of hospitalization. Following the first recurrence, the patient was transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) for closer surveillance. Each episode was managed with intramuscular epinephrine (see figure 1), totaling four events including the initial presentation to the emergency department. The first two administrations were provided using auto-injectors with 0.3 mg epinephrine each, while the subsequent two were administered as manually prepared intramuscular injections containing 0.5 mg each. Although the epinephrine auto-injectors available at our institution deliver a lower dose than the guideline-recommended 0.5 mg, their use is preferred during urgent situations (such as acute stabilization in the emergency department) or in settings where the response to anaphylaxis may be less familiar and delays could occur due to uncertainty in medication handling.

Upon the first recurrence of anaphylaxis, corticosteroid therapy was added, consistent with their traditional use in reducing the risk of biphasic anaphylaxis. The regimen consisted of intravenous prednisone at 30 mg daily for 5 days, as well as a single intravenous dose of 8 mg dexamethasone, reflecting discrepancies between protocols for the general ward and those for intensive care within our hospital. Additional therapies included two intravenous doses of clemastine 2 mg each to address dermatologic manifestations, and a 500 mL bolus of normal saline during the second anaphylactic event. Notably, symptom severity appeared to diminish with each successive episode, initially presenting as profound hypoxaemia, cyanosis, hypotension, and significant dyspnoea, and evolving to milder symptoms such as dyspnoea, recurrent rash, and pruritus by the last event.

An early consultation with the allergology department was requested for expert management recommendations. Two distinct treatment strategies were considered: firstly, preemptive continuous intravenous infusion of epinephrine, aiming to prevent recurrent anaphylaxis rather than reacting to its onset; and secondly, an expectant approach to monitor for possible spontaneous symptom resolution, given the observed attenuation in clinical severity over time. Both strategies were associated with substantial risk, as management of multiphasic anaphylaxis is not well-defined in current literature.

Outcome and follow-up:

In the end, neither of the considered treatment strategies was required, since the patient experienced no additional recurrence of symptoms from that point onward. Following approximately 24 hours without further anaphylactic episodes, she was moved back to the general ward. Her hospital stay lasted a total of three days before discharge. Upon leaving the hospital, she was prescribed levocetirizine, an antihistamine, as a routine medication, and was both provided with and instructed in the use of a 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injector. Subsequently, she has not experienced any further instances of anaphylaxis.

Because of the unusual and extended progression of her case, more comprehensive investigation into possible underlying factors was undertaken. This work-up indicated a substantial likelihood of systemic mastocytosis, though it did not meet the criteria necessary for definitive diagnosis—there were two WHO minor criteria present, while major criteria could not be assessed due to insufficient bone marrow biopsy material (refer to figure 2). Tryptase measurements were not obtained during the initial assessment of the patient’s first anaphylactic reactions; therefore, the recommended sampling intervals for tryptase evaluation were not followed. Existing guidelines suggest collecting at least two, ideally three, tryptase samples at defined times: the first as soon as possible without delaying management, a second 1–2 hours (but no later than 4 hours) after symptom onset—when peak tryptase typically occurs—and a third sample at least 24 hours after complete symptom resolution to determine the patient’s baseline. In this case, the initial tryptase sample was collected 1 hour after the third episode of anaphylaxis, with the subsequent sample drawn 27 hours after the last anaphylactic recurrence.

Allergy confirmation testing was not performed, as the risk of inducing another episode of severe anaphylaxis (e.g., via skin testing) was considered unwarranted given that avoidance of the inciting agent was straightforward. Additional relevant observations consistent with systemic mastocytosis included hepatomegaly detected on abdominal ultrasound and potential neuropsychiatric features such as depression and memory difficulties.

Discussion:

Anaphylaxis is a clinically distinct diagnosis, typically marked by abrupt onset and rapid escalation of symptoms impacting the airway, respiratory function, cardiovascular status, and/or gastrointestinal system, often accompanied by skin or mucosal changes such as urticaria, angioedema, or flushing. Identification of an offending allergen can facilitate diagnosis, although pinpointing a specific cause is not always feasible.

More complicated cases, including biphasic or refractory anaphylaxis, are less precisely delineated; however, recent initiatives have attempted to develop standardized definitions. According to the Resuscitation Council UK, biphasic anaphylaxis involves a recurrence of symptoms hours after the initial resolution without renewed exposure to the allergen. Distinguishing genuine biphasic episodes from prolonged anaphylaxis with temporary epinephrine response or continued allergen absorption (for instance, residual food or slow-release pharmaceuticals) poses diagnostic difficulties. In contrast, refractory anaphylaxis describes ongoing symptoms with little or no improvement following appropriate epinephrine administration.

Guidelines relating to biphasic anaphylaxis mainly focus on clinical observation, yet the ideal duration for monitoring remains controversial. Conventional observation recommendations suggest 4 to 6 hours post-resolution of symptoms. The Nederlands Huisartsen Genootschap (NHG) and World Allergy Organisation guidelines acknowledge that roughly half of biphasic reactions manifest within 6–12 hours of the original event, although neither provides explicit instructions regarding monitoring timeframes. Differently, the UK guideline 2021 recommends a three-level, risk-based approach to observation. Establishing consensus regarding monitoring protocols is necessary to address the challenge of reconciling demands on healthcare resources (large numbers of single episode cases) with the imperative to ensure patient safety (particularly regarding the small, hard-to-identify biphasic subgroup at risk of complications).

There is a notable and concerning absence of guidance regarding advanced pharmacologic approaches in managing biphasic and multiphasic anaphylactic episodes. Therapeutic interventions for these presentations largely align with conventional anaphylaxis protocols, which prioritize rapid administration of intramuscular epinephrine and appropriate supportive measures. Consensus guidelines advocate for an epinephrine dose of 0.01 mg/kg, capped at a total maximum of 0.5 mg. In practice, this recommendation is typically simplified to 0.5 mg for adults, reflecting the fact that most adults have a body weight exceeding 50 kg. Nonetheless, it is noteworthy that numerous commercially available auto-injectors—such as those utilized during the initial two reactions in our case—are formulated with just 0.3 mg of epinephrine, despite being marketed for adult use. Although epinephrine auto-injectors are indispensable in prehospital care, their use may also offer advantages within medical environments by reducing delays in administration and mitigating provider reluctance, particularly among clinicians with limited exposure to vasoactive medications. That being said, reliance on a 0.3 mg auto-injector in adults over 50 kg may result in significant underdosing. Evidence from a 2025 retrospective analysis indicates that patients first treated with 0.3 mg epinephrine required escalated interventions, including repeat epinephrine dosing, considerably more often than those initially given 0.5 mg. Overcoming factors such as clinician familiarity with the longstanding 0.3 mg dose and widespread access to 0.3 mg devices, as well as enhancing compliance with current dosing recommendations, could meaningfully enhance the management of anaphylaxis.

Historically, corticosteroids have been advocated in part to mitigate the risk of biphasic reactions. However, increasing data reveal that steroids offer no clear benefit and may pose additional risks, prompting a recent transition away from their use in the management of anaphylaxis. A systematic review published in 2017 evaluated 22 studies, with only a single study favoring corticosteroid use, while the remaining 21 found no evidence that steroids lower the incidence of biphasic anaphylaxis. Moreover, emerging research highlights concerns about side effects; notably, a 2019 study found a higher probability of ICU or hospital ward admissions after administration of corticosteroids in the prehospital setting. As a result, most updated guidelines do not support the routine use of corticosteroids, although their utilization remains common in practice.

Although biphasic anaphylaxis is uncommon, it is clinically significant. The challenge is compounded by a lack of dependable predictive markers for its occurrence, making risk assessment and monitoring challenging. Proposed predictors include delayed administration of epinephrine, severe initial symptoms, multiple doses of epinephrine, and a history of biphasic episodes. Nonetheless, precise predictors suitable for guiding individualized monitoring have not yet been identified.

Systemic mastocytosis, characterized by proliferation of mast cells, excessive mediator release, and inflammation across multiple organs, increases susceptibility to severe anaphylactic events.15,17 Despite this association, both conditions are uncommon, and currently no studies specifically examine the impact of systemic mastocytosis on biphasic anaphylaxis. Additional investigation into mast cell disorders and biphasic anaphylaxis may inform future approaches to management.

Anaphylaxis-related mortality is generally low, particularly in areas with rapid access to emergency healthcare. Death resulting from biphasic anaphylaxis is even less common, despite its perceived severity. Nonetheless, despite these comforting data, anaphylaxis is a frequent emergency situation requiring immediate intervention and close observation. Although most studies and recommendations concentrate on acute emergency management, ongoing medical supervision and care are equally crucial. Enhanced insight into complex presentations, such as biphasic anaphylaxis, could facilitate a more individualized, efficient, and resource-conscious approach to anaphylaxis management.

Due to the infrequency of biphasic and especially multiphasic anaphylaxis, alongside the inherent obstacles to conducting research during emergencies, establishing robust, evidence-based protocols remains difficult. In these instances, consensus guidelines formulated by experts with explicit management directives are invaluable, yet their development is constrained by insufficient data. Given how uncommon these complex anaphylactic patterns are, creating a registry encompassing notable patient characteristics, potential management approaches as exemplified in our case report, and outcomes could underpin the formulation of data-supported, expert-driven recommendations.
