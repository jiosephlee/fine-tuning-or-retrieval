Title: Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting

Margret Ann Schneider, Hans de Groot, Jan-Willem Geerts

Abstract:

Summary: Anaphylaxis represents the most critical manifestation of allergic reactions, posing a significant risk of fatality unless managed swiftly and effectively. A portion of patients experience a recurrence of symptoms after initial resolution, even following appropriate treatment. These biphasic, and on occasion multiphasic, patterns complicate clinical care, particularly since incidence rates are inconsistent, risk factors remain poorly understood, and evidence-based management protocols are lacking in established guidelines. This challenge is heightened in cases of multiphasic anaphylaxis, where individuals have repeated anaphylactic episodes despite having responded as expected to standard interventions such as epinephrine administration. Given the infrequency of multiphasic anaphylaxis, published case reports are valuable for sharing clinical management strategies. Herein, we report on a female patient in her 60s who developed multiphasic anaphylaxis secondary to clarithromycin exposure; further evaluation suggested a probable diagnosis of systemic mastocytosis, although she did not fully satisfy established diagnostic criteria.

Background:

Anaphylaxis constitutes a serious and, in cases of anaphylactic shock, potentially fatal allergic event, representing a frequent reason for emergency medical visits. Notably, a proportion of patients experience biphasic or even multiphasic patterns, marked by an initial anaphylactic episode following exposure to an allergen, a subsequent symptom-free interval, and then return of symptoms without renewed contact with the allergen. While these subsequent reactions are most commonly documented within 24 hours of the first exposure, cases with symptom recurrence as late as 78 hours have been described. The frequency of biphasic anaphylaxis varies substantially in the literature, with reported rates spanning from 0.4% up to 23.3%, although the majority of studies indicate an incidence between 4% and 6%. Because of the chance for delayed symptom recurrence, clinical practice often entails a period of observation following the resolution of anaphylactic symptoms. Nonetheless, the criteria for biphasic anaphylaxis lack uniformity, and management guidelines, particularly regarding corticosteroid administration, remain under debate. Multiphasic anaphylaxis, wherein patients undergo multiple recurrences, poses particular challenges concerning appropriate management and monitoring strategies. Here, we describe a case involving a woman in her sixties who developed multiphasic anaphylaxis following exposure to clarithromycin.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 60s, with a known allergy to beta-lactam antibiotics, presented to the emergency department (ED) with anaphylaxis following oral clarithromycin 500 mg.

The patient’s past medical history was notable for type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, psoriasis, and depression. Her body weight was 104 kg. The medications she was currently taking included metformin 500 mg twice daily, gliclazide 80 mg modified-release once daily, hydrochlorothiazide 25 mg daily, omeprazole 20 mg once daily, and sertraline 100 mg daily. She had last taken hydrochlorothiazide more than 12 hours prior to presenting to the emergency department.

During adolescence, she had experienced a minor allergic reaction to amoxicillin, manifesting as facial swelling without involvement of the oropharynx, skin rash, hypotension, or difficulty breathing. Additionally, she had previously shown a pronounced local cutaneous reaction following a wasp sting, though this did not include any systemic features. The current clinical episode represented her initial experience of anaphylaxis.

Five days prior to arrival at the emergency department, her general practitioner prescribed clindamycin for the management of erysipelas. After five days on clindamycin, she developed an abdominal rash, with no further description available. This was considered a delayed allergic response to clindamycin, prompting her general practitioner to transition her antibiotic therapy to clarithromycin, which she had not taken previously.

She later arrived at the emergency department reporting pruritus, flushing, excessive sweating, restlessness, and a sensation of throat swelling. Her symptoms began one hour following ingestion of her first dose of clarithromycin, worsening progressively. She presented for medical evaluation one hour after symptom onset. Upon arrival, vital signs revealed low blood pressure and reduced oxygen saturation (non-invasive blood pressure measured at 74/50 mm Hg, peripheral oxygen saturation of 88% on room air, respiratory rate of 22 breaths per minute). Physical exam revealed facial edema, new widespread erythema, generalized urticaria, increased sweating, and agitation. There was no evidence of tongue or throat swelling, and both cardiac and pulmonary auscultation were unremarkable. Blood glucose measured 12.7 mmol/L, and electrocardiogram showed sinus rhythm. Immediate treatment for suspected severe allergic reaction was initiated, including intramuscular administration of 0.3 mg epinephrine with an auto-injector and a slow intravenous injection of 2 mg clemastine for the cutaneous manifestations, following local protocol. She also received supplemental oxygen at 15 L/min via non-rebreather mask and 500 mL of 0.9% sodium chloride during her initial stabilization. Supplemental oxygen could be discontinued after improvement with epinephrine, but required temporary re-initiation during recurrent anaphylactic events. Clarithromycin therapy was ceased, and no further doses beyond the initial 500 mg oral administration were given.

At first, the patient responded well to therapy, as blood pressure stabilized and both facial swelling and erythema subsided. She was transferred to the acute internal medicine ward, where enhanced supervision by specialized nursing personnel was available, albeit without telemetry monitoring. Despite this initial improvement, within 12 hours after admission she began experiencing repeated episodes consistent with anaphylactic shock. Following the first recurrence, she was moved to the intensive care unit (ICU) for closer monitoring. Every episode was managed successfully with intramuscular epinephrine administration (see figure 1), totaling four anaphylactic events, including the original presentation in the emergency department (ED). The initial two episodes were treated with 0.3 mg intramuscular epinephrine auto-injectors, while the subsequent two recurrences were managed with individually prepared 0.5 mg intramuscular doses of epinephrine. Although our available auto-injectors deliver 0.3 mg, which is lower than the 0.5 mg guideline recommendation, we prioritize their use in urgent scenarios such as newly presenting patients in the ED, or in settings where emergency use of epinephrine is uncommon and delays might occur due to hesitancy with less familiar preparations. After the first recurrence, corticosteroids were introduced based on their established role in preventing biphasic anaphylaxis. She was treated with 30 mg daily intravenous prednisone for 5 days, along with a single 8 mg intravenous dose of dexamethasone, reflecting discrepancies between protocols used in our hospital's general and intensive care wards. Additionally, for the management of cutaneous symptoms, she received 2 mg clemastine intravenously on two occasions and received an extra 500 mL normal saline during her second anaphylactic event. Notably, each successive episode was less severe, progressing from profound hypoxemia, cyanosis, hypotension, and marked dyspnea initially, to milder symptoms of dyspnea, urticaria, and pruritus during the final occurrence.

Early in the clinical course, the allergology department was consulted to provide specialized treatment recommendations. Two management strategies were contemplated. The first was to initiate a continuous intravenous epinephrine infusion to proactively avert further anaphylactic events, rather than intervening after symptoms reappeared. The alternative, considering the trend of diminishing symptom severity, was to monitor the patient expectantly in anticipation of spontaneous symptom resolution. Both options were associated with considerable risks, which was compounded by a lack of published evidence to guide treatment in multiphasic anaphylactic presentations.

Outcome and follow-up:

In the end, neither of the potential treatment strategies was needed, as the patient experienced no additional recurrence of symptoms after this point. Roughly 24 hours passed without any signs of recurrent anaphylaxis before she was moved back to the general medical floor. She was released from the hospital three days after her initial admission. On discharge, the antihistamine levocetirizine was prescribed for regular use, and she was provided with a 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injector and instructed in its application. No further anaphylactic events have occurred since then.

Given the unusual and protracted clinical trajectory, further evaluation to identify possible underlying etiologies was initiated. This workup indicated a high likelihood of systemic mastocytosis; however, formal diagnostic criteria were not met, as only two minor World Health Organization criteria were present and the major criterion could not be assessed due to limited bone marrow sample size (refer to figure 2). Regrettably, tryptase measurements were not obtained during the initial evaluation of the first anaphylactic episodes, resulting in noncompliance with the guideline-recommended timing for tryptase assessment. According to established protocols, at least two, and ideally three, tryptase samples should be collected at defined times: the first sample should be drawn as soon as possible without delaying acute management, the second between 1 to 2 hours (and no later than 4 hours) after symptom onset, when tryptase levels typically reach their maximum, and a third sample should be collected at least 24 hours after full symptom resolution to determine baseline levels. In this case, however, the initial tryptase sample was obtained one hour after the third episode of anaphylaxis, while the second sample was taken 27 hours following the most recent event.

Allergy confirmation tests were not conducted, as the risk of triggering a potentially severe recurrent anaphylactic reaction with procedures such as skin testing was considered unjustifiable, particularly given the straightforward nature of avoiding further exposure. Additional pertinent observations consistent with systemic mastocytosis included hepatomegaly detected via abdominal ultrasound and potential neuropsychiatric manifestations such as depression and memory impairment.

Discussion:

Anaphylaxis is a clinically distinct condition marked by an abrupt onset and swift escalation of symptoms affecting the airway, respiratory function, circulation, and/or gastrointestinal system, and is usually associated with cutaneous or mucosal signs such as urticaria, angioedema, or flushing. Although identifying a probable allergen can aid in confirming the diagnosis, pinpointing an exact cause is often not feasible.

More atypical forms, such as biphasic or refractory anaphylaxis, are less precisely characterized; however, recent initiatives have sought to develop consensus definitions. According to the Resuscitation Council UK, biphasic anaphylaxis involves the return of symptoms several hours after they have initially resolved, without additional exposure to the allergen. Distinguishing genuine biphasic events from ongoing anaphylaxis that temporarily subsides following epinephrine administration, or from situations where the allergen continues to be absorbed (such as with retained food or sustained-release medication), is often difficult. Conversely, refractory anaphylaxis is defined by ongoing symptoms that do not respond, or only minimally respond, to correctly administered epinephrine.

Guidelines relating to biphasic anaphylaxis mainly focus on recommendations for monitoring in the clinical setting. Nonetheless, there is still debate regarding the most appropriate length of observation. Traditionally, patients are monitored for 4 to 6 hours after their symptoms have resolved. Guidelines from both the Dutch Nederlands Huisartsen Genootschap (NHG) and the World Allergy Organisation acknowledge that around 50% of biphasic reactions manifest within 6 to 12 hours after the initial episode, though neither specifies an exact duration for observation. By contrast, the 2021 UK guideline recommends a tiered, risk-based system for post-anaphylaxis monitoring. There is an evident need for standardized observation protocols to properly balance the extensive resource use for patients experiencing a single anaphylactic event with the safety considerations required for the small, yet harder to identify, group at risk for biphasic reactions.

There is a notable and concerning scarcity of guidance regarding advanced medication approaches for biphasic and multiphasic anaphylactic reactions. Treatment for these presentations generally aligns with the standard management protocol for anaphylaxis: prompt administration of intramuscular epinephrine along with suitable supportive measures. Existing guidelines recommend epinephrine at a dose of 0.01 mg/kg, not to exceed a total of 0.5 mg. In clinical practice, this recommendation is often simplified to 0.5 mg for adults, as most adults have a body weight greater than 50 kg. Interestingly, many commercially available auto-injectors—including those administered during the first two anaphylactic events in the discussed case—provide only 0.3 mg of epinephrine, even when designated for adult use. While epinephrine auto-injectors are integral in prehospital care and can be advantageous in healthcare environments by reducing time to administration and easing hesitancy among staff infrequently involved in delivering vasoactive drugs, the 0.3 mg dosage commonly used may result in significant underdosing for adults above 50 kg. A retrospective analysis from 2025 found that individuals initially managed with 0.3 mg of epinephrine required escalated care, such as additional epinephrine doses, at a substantially higher rate compared to those who received the full 0.5 mg. Addressing factors such as the prevalent comfort with and ready availability of 0.3 mg auto-injectors, and ensuring adherence to recommended dosing standards, could markedly enhance outcomes in anaphylaxis treatment.

Historically, corticosteroids were advocated in part to avert biphasic reactions. Nonetheless, accumulating data now indicate a lack of benefit and even possible adverse outcomes, prompting a recent movement away from corticosteroid use in anaphylaxis management. A systematic review from 2017 evaluated 22 studies, finding only a single study in favor of corticosteroids, while the remaining 21 revealed no evidence that steroids reduce biphasic anaphylaxis risk. Additionally, recent research highlights the significance of adverse effects, with a 2019 study demonstrating increased rates of ICU or inpatient admission following prehospital corticosteroid administration. As a result, most recent guidelines do not recommend the routine use of corticosteroids, though they are still employed in some clinical settings.

Biphasic anaphylaxis remains uncommon but has important clinical implications. The challenge in identifying reliable predictors complicates both risk assessment and decisions regarding patient observation. Proposed risk factors include delayed use of epinephrine, severe initial symptoms, administration of multiple epinephrine doses, and a personal history of biphasic reactions. Nevertheless, robust predictors to inform targeted observation are still lacking.

Systemic mastocytosis, characterized by a pathological accumulation of mast cells, increased mediator secretion, and multiorgan involvement, increases the risk of severe anaphylaxis. 1517 However, due to the rarity of both disorders, there is, to our knowledge, no current research specifically exploring the relationship between systemic mastocytosis and biphasic anaphylaxis. Further investigation into mast cell pathologies and biphasic reactions could yield important information for future therapeutic strategies.

Anaphylaxis-related deaths are uncommon, particularly in areas where emergency medical services are readily accessible. Additionally, although biphasic anaphylaxis might seem particularly threatening, fatal cases are even less frequent. Despite these promising figures, anaphylaxis remains a prevalent medical emergency that necessitates immediate intervention and thorough observation. While much research and most clinical guidelines prioritize initial emergency treatment, follow-up care and continuous monitoring are equally crucial. Enhanced knowledge of complex presentations, like biphasic anaphylaxis, may support the development of more individualized, focused, and resource-conscious management strategies.

Due to the infrequent occurrence of biphasic and especially multiphasic anaphylaxis, along with the inherent challenges of conducting research during emergencies, creating truly evidence-based approaches is especially difficult. In such contexts, consensus guidelines formulated by experts, offering structured management recommendations, are of great value. Nonetheless, producing these expert-driven recommendations is complicated by limited available evidence. Given the scarcity of these intricate cases, establishing a registry that documents relevant clinical characteristics, potential management options such as those described in our report, and outcomes could serve as an essential resource for shaping expert-informed, data-supported recommendations.
