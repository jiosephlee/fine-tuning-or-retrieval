Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting

Margret Ann Schneider, Hans de Groot, Jan-Willem Geerts

Abstract:

Summary: Anaphylaxis represents the most critical manifestation of allergic responses, posing a significant risk of mortality unless treated swiftly and effectively. In some instances, individuals experience a recurrence of symptoms after apparent recovery and successful initial intervention. Such biphasic or multiphasic patterns are particularly challenging for clinicians because their reported frequency varies, associated risk factors remain poorly defined, and established guidelines offer limited direction for management. This dilemma is heightened in cases of multiphasic anaphylaxis, where patients endure repeated episodes despite a favorable reaction to standard epinephrine therapy. Given the rarity of multiphasic anaphylaxis, detailed case reports serve as valuable references for guiding clinical care. In this context, we describe a case involving a woman in her 60s who suffered from multiphasic anaphylaxis in response to clarithromycin; further evaluation indicated a strong suspicion of systemic mastocytosis, although formal diagnostic thresholds were not met.

Background:

Anaphylaxis represents a critical allergic event that can rapidly escalate to a life-threatening emergency, particularly when progressing to anaphylactic shock, and is a frequent reason for seeking urgent medical attention. Notably, in some instances, the clinical course may be biphasic or even multiphasic: an initial episode of anaphylaxis triggered by an allergen resolves, entering a symptom-free interval, only for symptoms to return absent further allergen exposure. This recurrence typically manifests within the first 24 hours post-exposure, though there are documented cases where symptoms have re-emerged as late as 78 hours following the initial event. Estimates for the frequency of biphasic anaphylaxis differ substantially, ranging from 0.4% to 23.3%, with most studies indicating a rate between 4% and 6%. The risk of recurrent symptoms prompts a recommendation for extended observation following apparent resolution of anaphylactic signs. Nevertheless, inconsistent definitions of biphasic anaphylaxis and divergent management recommendations—particularly surrounding the use of corticosteroids—create ambiguity in clinical practice. The occurrence of multiphasic anaphylaxis, which is characterized by several distinct recurrences of symptoms, poses particular difficulties in both monitoring and therapeutic approaches. Here, we describe a case involving a woman in her 60s who experienced a multiphasic anaphylactic reaction in response to clarithromycin.

Case presentation:

A woman in her sixth decade of life, with a documented hypersensitivity to beta-lactam antibiotics, arrived at the emergency department (ED) exhibiting signs of anaphylaxis after ingesting 500 mg of oral clarithromycin.

Her past medical history was notable for type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, psoriasis, and depressive disorder. She weighed 104 kg. At the time of presentation, her regular medications included metformin (500 mg twice daily), gliclazide retard (80 mg once daily), hydrochlorothiazide (25 mg once daily), omeprazole (20 mg once daily), and sertraline (100 mg once daily). It is of note that she last consumed hydrochlorothiazide more than 12 hours prior to arriving at the ED.

Previously, during adolescence, the patient had experienced a mild allergic response to amoxicillin, characterized by facial swelling without involvement of the oropharynx, cutaneous eruption, hypotension, or respiratory difficulty. Additionally, she had a history of pronounced localized cutaneous reaction to a wasp sting, although no systemic manifestations occurred. The current clinical episode represented her initial experience of anaphylaxis.

Five days preceding her ED visit, she had been prescribed clindamycin by her general practitioner to treat erysipelas. Following five days of therapy, she developed an abdominal rash (not further specified), which was considered to be a delayed allergic reaction to clindamycin. In response, her general practitioner discontinued clindamycin and initiated clarithromycin, an antibiotic she had not previously received.

She later arrived at the ED exhibiting symptoms including itching, flushing, profuse sweating, restlessness, and a sensation of throat swelling. These manifestations began approximately 1 hour following the ingestion of her first oral dose of clarithromycin and gradually intensified. She presented for medical evaluation within an hour of symptom onset. Upon arrival, vital signs revealed hypotension and hypoxemia (non-invasive blood pressure of 74/50 mm Hg, peripheral oxygen saturation at 88% while breathing room air, and a respiratory rate of 22 breaths per minute). The initial physical exam noted facial edema, newly developed widespread erythema, urticaria across her body, increased sweating, and agitation. Examination found no evidence of tongue or pharyngeal edema, and both cardiac and pulmonary auscultation were unremarkable. Her blood glucose measured 12.7 mmol/L, and her ECG demonstrated sinus rhythm. Management was initiated immediately for presumed severe allergic reaction, including intramuscular administration of 0.3 mg epinephrine via auto-injector, and slow intravenous administration of 2 mg clemastine, consistent with local treatment protocols for cutaneous symptoms. She also received supplemental oxygen (15 L through a non-rebreather mask) and 500 mL of normal saline (0.9% sodium chloride) as part of her initial stabilization. Oxygen supplementation was discontinued after favorable clinical improvement following epinephrine; nevertheless, it required brief resumption during recurrent anaphylactic events. Clarithromycin was ceased, with no further doses administered after the initial 500 mg oral intake.

At first, the patient exhibited a favorable therapeutic response, with normalization of blood pressure and reduction in facial edema and erythema. She was subsequently admitted to the acute internal ward, which facilitates enhanced surveillance by experienced nursing personnel, albeit without telemetry capabilities. Despite the promising initial improvement, within 12 hours of admission she experienced multiple recurrences of symptoms consistent with anaphylactic shock. After the initial relapse, she was transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) for closer observation. Resolution of each anaphylactic episode (see figure 1), four in total including the index episode in the emergency department, necessitated intramuscular epinephrine administration. The initial two doses—each 0.3 mg—were delivered via auto-injector, while the subsequent two episodes were managed with 0.5 mg intramuscular epinephrine, prepared manually. Although our clinic’s auto-injectors contain a lower dose than the recommended 0.5 mg, their use is prioritized during acute stabilization, particularly in situations such as the initial emergency department management or when emergency interventions like anaphylaxis treatment are infrequent, as hesitance arising from unfamiliarity with injectable medications might delay urgent care. After the first recurrence of anaphylaxis, corticosteroids were added to her regimen, in line with their conventional role in reducing the risk of biphasic reactions. Specifically, she received 30 mg of intravenous prednisone daily for five days, along with a single 8 mg intravenous dose of dexamethasone, reflecting variance between our hospital’s protocols for general ward and ICU management of anaphylaxis. She was also treated twice with 2 mg intravenous clemastine for skin manifestations, and an extra 500 mL normal saline during the second anaphylactic episode. Notably, each successive episode showed a reduction in severity: while the initial presentation included profound hypoxemia with cyanosis, hypotension, and severe respiratory distress, later episodes were marked only by dyspnea, rash, and pruritus.

Early involvement of the department of allergology was sought to guide management. Two primary management strategies were evaluated. The first option involved initiating a continuous intravenous infusion of epinephrine, aiming to pre-empt further anaphylactic reactions rather than awaiting the onset of new symptoms. Alternatively, in recognition of the gradually waning symptom severity, a watchful waiting approach was considered to determine if resolution might occur without additional intervention. Each of these strategies entailed significant risk, particularly given the absence of robust literature informing the management of multiphasic anaphylaxis.

Outcome and follow-up:

In the end, neither of the proposed therapeutic strategies was required, since the patient experienced no recurrence of symptoms thereafter. Following a 24-hour period free of additional anaphylactic episodes, she was moved to a general care ward. Her total hospital stay lasted 3 days, after which she was discharged. Upon discharge, she was prescribed the antihistamine levocetirizine as part of her ongoing medication regimen, and she received education on the use of an epinephrine auto-injector (0.3 mg). She has not experienced any further anaphylactic episodes since that time.

Given the unusual and extended progression of her clinical presentation, a more detailed investigation into possible underlying etiologies was conducted. The findings indicated a strong likelihood of systemic mastocytosis, but this condition could not be definitively diagnosed: while two minor criteria set by the WHO were met, the major criterion could not be evaluated due to the limited yield of her bone marrow biopsy (refer to figure 2). Regrettably, during the assessment of the patient’s initial anaphylactic episodes, tryptase measurements were not obtained, thereby failing to comply with the recommended temporal protocol for tryptase testing. Current guidelines advise collecting at least two—preferably three—tryptase samples at designated timepoints: the first should be acquired as soon as reasonably possible, without impeding urgent treatment; a second should follow 1–2 hours after symptom onset (but within 4 hours), coinciding with the typical peak in tryptase concentration; and a third should be collected at least 24 hours after complete clinical recovery, to determine the patient's baseline tryptase level. In the reported case, the initial sample was drawn 1 hour subsequent to the third anaphylactic reaction, with the second specimen obtained 27 hours after the most recent episode.

No confirmatory allergy testing was conducted, as the risk of provoking another severe anaphylactic event through procedures such as skin testing was considered unjustifiable, especially since avoiding re-exposure was relatively straightforward. Additional pertinent clinical features consistent with systemic mastocytosis were noted, such as hepatomegaly detected by abdominal sonography, as well as possible neuropsychiatric manifestations like depression and memory impairment.

Discussion:

Anaphylaxis is generally recognized as a distinct clinical entity, defined by its acute onset and swift escalation of symptoms that may affect the airway, respiratory function, cardiovascular system, and/or gastrointestinal tract. It is most often associated with skin or mucosal signs, including urticaria, angioedema, or flushing. Although pinpointing a causative allergen can assist in establishing the diagnosis, identifying the exact trigger is not always feasible.

Atypical forms of anaphylaxis, such as biphasic and refractory presentations, are less clearly delineated, though recent initiatives have sought to reach consensus definitions. The Resuscitation Council UK characterizes biphasic anaphylaxis as the reappearance of symptoms hours after initial resolution, with no additional allergen exposure. Discriminating true biphasic reactions from prolonged anaphylaxis that transiently improves with epinephrine or from cases involving ongoing allergen presence—such as undigested food or extended-release pharmaceuticals—poses notable diagnostic difficulties. In contrast, refractory anaphylaxis denotes cases in which anaphylactic symptoms persist despite appropriate administration of epinephrine.

Current guidelines primarily focus on monitoring strategies following biphasic anaphylactic reactions, though recommendations regarding the ideal length of observation are inconsistent. Conventional protocols suggest monitoring patients for 4 to 6 hours post symptom resolution. Both the Dutch Nederlands Huisartsen Genootschap (NHG) and the World Allergy Organisation acknowledge that around half of biphasic cases manifest within 6–12 hours after the initial reaction, yet neither issues precise guidance on monitoring duration. The 2021 UK guideline, on the other hand, advocates for a stratified, three-level observation protocol tailored to risk. The development of unified monitoring approaches is necessary to reconcile the need for prudent use of healthcare resources for the larger cohort experiencing single-phase anaphylaxis with the imperative of protecting the smaller, higher-risk group susceptible to biphasic reactions.

There is a notable and concerning deficiency of specific recommendations for advanced pharmacologic interventions in biphasic and multiphasic anaphylaxis. Current therapeutic approaches for these forms predominantly adhere to conventional anaphylaxis management protocols, centering on the rapid administration of intramuscular epinephrine alongside necessary supportive measures. Established guidelines endorse an epinephrine dose of 0.01 mg/kg body weight, with an upper limit of 0.5 mg per administration. In clinical practice, this often results in a simplified adult dose of 0.5 mg, as most adults exceed 50 kg. Yet, it is noteworthy that numerous commercially available auto-injectors, including those applied in the initial two episodes described in our case study, are formulated to deliver only 0.3 mg of epinephrine, despite being marketed for adult usage. Auto-injectors are indispensable in out-of-hospital environments and retain utility within clinical settings by mitigating treatment delays and overcoming reluctance among healthcare workers who infrequently encounter the use of agents like epinephrine. Nevertheless, reliance on a 0.3 mg auto-injector for adults surpassing 50 kg can result in clinically relevant underdosing. According to a 2025 retrospective analysis, initial treatment with 0.3 mg epinephrine was associated with a markedly higher frequency of escalation—such as the requirement for subsequent epinephrine administrations—when compared to the 0.5 mg initial dose. Addressing factors such as practitioners’ habitual preference for the traditionally accepted 0.3 mg dose, coupled with the widespread availability of 0.3 mg auto-injectors, and enhancing compliance with guideline-recommended epinephrine dosing, could substantially optimize the management of anaphylaxis.

Historically, corticosteroids were commonly administered, partly as a strategy to mitigate the risk of biphasic reactions. Nonetheless, accumulating data has failed to demonstrate efficacy and has highlighted possible adverse outcomes, prompting a reevaluation of their role in anaphylaxis management. A systematic review from 2017 encompassing 22 studies identified that only one study supported corticosteroid use, whereas 21 studies did not show evidence that corticosteroids decrease the incidence of biphasic anaphylaxis. In addition, newer research has brought attention to the potential side effects, with a notable 2019 study reporting increased ICU or hospital admission rates following prehospital corticosteroid administration. As a result, most up-to-date clinical guidelines discourage the routine use of corticosteroids for anaphylaxis, although these medications are still employed in practice.

While biphasic anaphylaxis occurs infrequently, its clinical implications are significant. Effective risk assessment and monitoring remain challenging due to the absence of robust predictive markers. Potential indicators that have been proposed include late administration of epinephrine, pronounced severity at onset, necessity for multiple epinephrine doses, and a history of previous biphasic reactions. However, these factors have yet to provide a reliable basis for selective monitoring strategies.

Systemic mastocytosis, a disorder characterized by the pathological proliferation of mast cells and resultant excessive mediator release affecting multiple organs, increases susceptibility to severe anaphylactic episodes.\({ }^{15 17}\) Nevertheless, owing to the low prevalence of both systemic mastocytosis and biphasic anaphylaxis, there is presently no research directly examining their interrelation. Further studies focused on mast cell pathologies in the context of biphasic anaphylaxis could yield important insights relevant to patient care.

Fortunately, fatalities due to anaphylaxis are uncommon, particularly in areas where emergency medical services are readily accessible. Moreover, deaths resulting from biphasic anaphylaxis are even less frequent, notwithstanding the condition’s ostensibly serious nature. Still, despite these encouraging figures, anaphylaxis continues to constitute a frequent medical emergency, necessitating swift intervention and vigilant observation. Research and clinical guidelines often prioritize acute emergency response; however, ongoing monitoring and follow-up care are equally critical. Enhancing our comprehension of intricate forms of anaphylaxis, such as biphasic reactions, may facilitate the development of more individualized, efficient, and resource-conscious approaches to anaphylaxis management.

The infrequency of biphasic and especially multiphasic anaphylactic episodes, combined with intrinsic obstacles to conducting research in urgent care settings, presents significant difficulties in formulating genuinely evidence-based management protocols. In these circumstances, consensus guidelines rooted in expert opinion and incorporating explicit recommendations serve as a highly valuable alternative. Yet, the creation of such expert-driven guidance is itself hindered by the paucity of robust data. Given the exceptional nature of these complex anaphylactic patterns, establishing a registry to document relevant cases—including pertinent patient details, proposed management approaches (as illustrated in our case study), and patient outcomes—could lay the foundation for empirically informed expert recommendations.
