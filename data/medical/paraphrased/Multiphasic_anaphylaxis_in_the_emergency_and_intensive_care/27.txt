Title: Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting

Margret Ann Schneider, Hans de Groot, Jan-Willem Geerts

Abstract:

Summary: Anaphylaxis represents the most severe type of allergic reaction and constitutes a potentially fatal systemic event that necessitates swift and effective intervention. Certain cases experience symptom recurrence even after appropriate therapy and apparent resolution, creating biphasic or multiphasic patterns that pose significant difficulties for clinicians. Incidence rates reported in the literature vary, risk factors remain poorly understood, and established management recommendations for these relapsing presentations are absent from current guidelines. This situation is particularly problematic in cases of multiphasic anaphylaxis, where patients suffer repeated episodes of anaphylaxis despite showing initial improvement following standard treatment, including epinephrine administration. Since multiphasic anaphylaxis occurs infrequently, published case reports become valuable resources for sharing approaches to clinical management. In this report, we describe a female patient in her 60s who experienced multiphasic anaphylaxis triggered by clarithromycin, with subsequent evaluation indicating a probable diagnosis of systemic mastocytosis, although she did not fulfill official diagnostic criteria.

Background:

Anaphylaxis is a serious and, particularly in cases of anaphylactic shock, potentially fatal allergic response frequently encountered in emergency settings. In some instances, patients experience biphasic or even multiphasic patterns, characterized by an initial reaction to an allergen, subsequent improvement and asymptomatic period, followed by recurrence of symptoms despite no further exposure to the allergen. Typically, this biphasic response occurs within 24 hours of the initial insult, though documented cases have noted symptom reappearance as late as 78 hours post-exposure. The prevalence of biphasic anaphylaxis is reported inconsistently across studies, ranging from 0.4% to 23.3%, but most evidence suggests it affects between 4% and 6% of anaphylactic episodes. Because relapse is possible, patients are generally observed for a period after apparent resolution of symptoms. Nevertheless, there is variability in the definitions of biphasic anaphylaxis and treatment recommendations differ, with ongoing debate regarding the use of corticosteroids in recent literature. Multiphasic anaphylaxis, in which symptoms return multiple times, poses particular difficulties for both therapeutic and observational strategies. This report details a case involving a female patient in her 60s who experienced a multiphasic anaphylactic response following clarithromycin administration.

Case presentation:

A female patient in her 60s, who is allergic to beta-lactam antibiotics, arrived at the emergency department with anaphylaxis after ingesting oral clarithromycin 500 mg.

Her past medical history includes type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, psoriasis, and depression. She weighs 104 kg. Her regular medications are metformin 500 mg twice daily, gliclazide 80 mg retard once daily, hydrochlorothiazide 25 mg once daily, omeprazole 20 mg once daily, and sertraline 100 mg once daily. She had taken her most recent dose of hydrochlorothiazide more than 12 hours prior to arrival at the ED.

As an adolescent, she experienced a minor allergic reaction to amoxicillin, presenting only as facial swelling and without involvement of oropharyngeal swelling, rash, hypotension, or dyspnoea. She had also experienced a pronounced localized skin response to a wasp sting, though without systemic manifestations. The current hospital admission marks her first encounter with anaphylaxis.

Five days prior to presenting to the ED, her general practitioner prescribed clindamycin for treatment of erysipelas. After five days of clindamycin therapy, a rash appeared on her abdomen (details not further specified), which was presumed to be a delayed allergic response to clindamycin. Consequently, her general practitioner changed the antibiotic treatment to clarithromycin, to which she had not previously been exposed.

She later arrived at the emergency department reporting pruritus, flushing, increased sweating, agitation, and a sensation of throat swelling. These symptoms developed one hour following ingestion of her initial oral dose of clarithromycin and intensified over time. She sought emergency care one hour after symptom onset. On initial assessment in the emergency department, her vital signs indicated hypotension and hypoxemia (non-invasive blood pressure measured at 74/50 mm Hg, peripheral oxygen saturation of 88% breathing ambient air, respiratory rate at 22 breaths per minute). Examination revealed facial edema, newly developed widespread erythema and urticaria affecting her entire body, as well as increased perspiration and restlessness. There was no evidence of tongue or pharyngeal swelling, and both cardiac and pulmonary auscultation were unremarkable. Blood glucose measured 12.7 mmol/L, and her electrocardiogram demonstrated sinus rhythm. Immediate management for suspected severe allergic reaction included administration of 0.3 mg epinephrine intramuscularly via auto-injector, together with slow intravenous infusion of 2 mg clemastine for cutaneous symptoms according to local protocols. Additional supportive measures consisted of high-flow oxygen supplementation (15 L via non-rebreather mask) and a 500 mL infusion of 0.9% sodium chloride during the initial stabilization period. Oxygen therapy was discontinued once her clinical condition improved in response to epinephrine, though it was temporarily reintroduced during subsequent anaphylactic events. Clarithromycin was stopped, with no further doses administered following the initial 500 mg oral dose.

At the outset, the patient responded favorably to therapy, evidenced by normalization of blood pressure and reduction in facial edema and erythema. She was transferred to the acute internal medicine ward, where enhanced observation by specialized nursing staff was implemented, although telemetry was not available. Nonetheless, after initial improvement, the patient experienced multiple recurrences of symptoms consistent with anaphylactic shock during the first 12 hours post-admission. Following the first recurrence, she was moved to the intensive care unit (ICU) for closer surveillance. Each episode was managed with intramuscular epinephrine administration (figure 1), totaling four anaphylactic episodes including the initial presentation in the emergency department (ED). The first two administrations employed a 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injector intramuscularly, while subsequent episodes were treated with individually prepared intramuscular doses of 0.5 mg epinephrine. Although the clinic’s available auto-injectors deliver a lower dose than the recommended 0.5 mg, the preference is for auto-injector use in urgent situations—such as during ED stabilization of new patients—or in environments where emergency interventions for anaphylaxis are infrequent and hesitation over unfamiliar medication could delay therapy. Following the initial recurrence, corticosteroid treatment was initiated, given their conventional role in preventing biphasic anaphylaxis. She was administered 30 mg intravenous prednisone daily for five days and a single dose of 8 mg intravenous dexamethasone, reflecting protocol differences between the hospital’s general ward and ICU anaphylaxis management guidelines. Additionally, 2 mg intravenous clemastine was given twice for cutaneous manifestations, and 500 mL of normal saline was infused during the second anaphylactic episode. Notably, the severity of each anaphylactic episode diminished progressively, beginning with profound hypoxemia accompanied by cyanosis, hypotension, and marked dyspnea, and ending with dyspnea and recurrence of rash and pruritus in the final event.

Early consultation with the allergology department was sought for specialized management guidance. Two distinct therapeutic strategies were evaluated. One involved continuous intravenous epinephrine infusion to proactively prevent further anaphylaxis, as opposed to reacting to symptomatic relapses. The other option, given the gradual decline in symptom severity, was a watchful waiting approach to allow for possible spontaneous resolution. Both strategies entailed considerable risk, compounded by the absence of supporting literature to guide treatment in multiphasic anaphylactic presentations.

Outcome and follow-up:

In the end, neither of the previously considered therapeutic interventions was required, since the patient experienced no further recurrence of symptoms. After remaining free of new anaphylactic episodes for about 24 hours, she was moved from the intensive unit to a regular care ward. Discharge from the hospital occurred after a total of three days. Upon leaving, she was prescribed levocetirizine, an antihistamine, as a new maintenance therapy, and was both provided with and trained in the use of a 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injector. There have been no subsequent instances of anaphylaxis.

Given the unusual and persistent nature of her clinical presentation, further investigation for underlying etiologies was conducted. These evaluations indicated a high likelihood of systemic mastocytosis, though a definitive diagnosis could not be established because only two minor WHO criteria were met, while the major criteria could not be evaluated due to insufficient bone marrow biopsy material (refer to figure 2). Regrettably, tryptase measurements were not obtained during the initial evaluation of the first episodes of anaphylaxis, resulting in non-compliance with the guideline-recommended schedule for tryptase assessment. Current recommendations advise collection of at least two, and preferably three, tryptase samples at designated times: the first collected as early as possible without interrupting urgent management, the second drawn 1–2 hours (but no later than 4 hours) after symptom onset—corresponding with expected peak tryptase levels—and a third sample acquired at least 24 hours following complete clinical recovery to determine the baseline value. For the patient described, the earliest tryptase level was measured one hour after the third episode of anaphylaxis, while a subsequent sample was obtained 27 hours after the latest occurrence of symptoms.

Allergy confirmation testing was not conducted, as the risk of triggering another severe anaphylactic reaction (e.g., by skin testing) was considered unjustified, particularly since future exposure could be avoided relatively easily. Additional findings consistent with systemic mastocytosis in this case included hepatomegaly identified by abdominal ultrasonography and possible neuropsychiatric manifestations such as depression and memory difficulties.

Discussion:

Anaphylaxis is recognized as a distinct clinical entity, marked by its abrupt onset and swift evolution, affecting the airway, respiratory function, circulatory system, and/or gastrointestinal tract. It is typically accompanied by skin or mucosal findings such as urticaria, angioedema, or flushing. Although pinpointing a causative allergen reinforces the diagnosis, determining the exact trigger is not always achievable.

More atypical manifestations, like biphasic or refractory anaphylaxis, lack precise definitions, though recent initiatives have sought to generate consensus in these areas. According to the Resuscitation Council UK, biphasic anaphylaxis is defined as the recurrence of symptoms several hours after initial resolution, without renewed exposure to the allergen. It can be difficult to distinguish true biphasic reactions from prolonged anaphylaxis showing only temporary improvement with epinephrine or ongoing absorption of an allergen, such as from residual food or a slow-release medication. In contrast, refractory anaphylaxis denotes continued symptoms despite appropriately administered epinephrine with little or no improvement.

Guidelines addressing biphasic anaphylaxis largely emphasize the importance of clinical observation. However, the ideal length of post-resolution monitoring is a subject of ongoing debate. Conventionally, patients are observed for 4 to 6 hours once symptoms have resolved. The Nederlands Huisartsen Genootschap (NHG) and the World Allergy Organisation guidelines highlight that about half of biphasic episodes develop within 6–12 hours after the initial event, although neither suggests a specific monitoring timeframe. The 2021 UK guideline, on the other hand, recommends a three-level risk-stratified protocol for observation. There is a clear need for unified monitoring recommendations that reconcile resource utilization for the many with single-episode anaphylaxis and the safety concerns posed by the small, hard-to-predict group susceptible to biphasic complications.

There is a notable and concerning absence of guidance regarding advanced medication management in biphasic and multiphasic presentations. Treatment for biphasic and multiphasic anaphylaxis closely parallels that of standard anaphylaxis, relying on the rapid administration of intramuscular epinephrine along with suitable supportive measures. Current guidelines specify an epinephrine dose of 0.01 mg/kg body weight, up to a ceiling of 0.5 mg. For adults, this is generally approximated to a flat dose of 0.5 mg since most adults exceed 50 kg. Despite this, it is noteworthy that many available auto-injectors—including those applied during the initial two episodes in the described case—deliver only 0.3 mg of epinephrine, even though their intended users are adults. While epinephrine auto-injectors are crucial in out-of-hospital situations, they may also confer benefits within clinical settings by facilitating timely intervention and alleviating reluctance among staff infrequently exposed to vasoactive drugs like epinephrine. Nevertheless, employing a 0.3 mg auto-injector—which is still relatively widespread—can result in substantial underdosing for adult patients weighing more than 50 kg. A retrospective analysis conducted in 2025 found that patients initially treated with 0.3 mg of epinephrine required care escalation, such as additional epinephrine doses, at a markedly higher rate compared to those who received 0.5 mg initially. Addressing factors such as prescriber familiarity with the traditionally used 0.3 mg dose and the wide distribution of 0.3 mg auto-injectors, along with strengthening adherence to recommended dosing practices, has the potential to substantially enhance outcomes in anaphylaxis management.

Historically, corticosteroids were used in part to mitigate biphasic reactions. Nonetheless, increasing data shows both a lack of efficacy and possible adverse effects, prompting a movement away from steroid use in anaphylaxis management. A systematic review published in 2017 analyzed 22 studies; only one indicated a positive effect of corticosteroids, while the remaining 21 found no reduction in biphasic anaphylaxis risk. Additional recent investigations highlight the importance of steroid-associated side effects. For example, a 2019 study identified a higher probability of ICU or hospital admissions following prehospital corticosteroid administration. As a result, current recommendations largely discourage routine corticosteroid use, though their administration remains common in practice.

Biphasic anaphylaxis is uncommon but of considerable clinical significance. The lack of reliable risk indicators complicates patient monitoring and risk assessment. Proposed risk factors include late initiation of epinephrine, severe presentation on admission, repeated epinephrine use, and a history of biphasic reactions, although no dependable markers exist for focused observation.

Systemic mastocytosis is characterized by abnormal mast cell proliferation, excessive mediator secretion, and inflammation involving multiple organs, placing affected individuals at risk for severe anaphylaxis.1517 Yet, due to the low prevalence of both systemic mastocytosis and biphasic anaphylaxis, the relationship between these entities has not been specifically investigated. Further studies into mast cell diseases and biphasic anaphylaxis are needed to inform future therapeutic approaches.

Fortunately, deaths due to anaphylaxis are uncommon, particularly where emergency medical services are readily accessible. Additionally, lethal instances of biphasic anaphylaxis are even more infrequent, despite the seemingly higher risk. Nonetheless, in spite of these encouraging figures, anaphylaxis continues to represent a frequent emergency requiring immediate intervention and close observation. Although much of the literature and existing guidelines concentrate on the initial emergency response, the subsequent phases of medical care and surveillance are equally essential. Enhanced insight into complex forms of anaphylaxis, such as biphasic reactions, has the potential to inform a more individualized, patient-focused, and efficient approach to managing this condition.

The infrequency of both biphasic and especially multiphasic anaphylaxis, combined with research obstacles presented by emergency care settings, make it highly challenging to produce truly evidence-based protocols. In these contexts, expert consensus-based guidelines offering structured recommendations hold significant value; nevertheless, developing such expert opinions is likewise complicated by the limited amount of empirical data. Given the uncommon occurrence of these complicated anaphylactic reactions, establishing a case registry—including pertinent patient information, management proposals like those presented in this report, and patient outcomes—could underpin the development of data-informed, expert-driven recommendations.
