Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting

Margret Ann Schneider, Hans de Groot, Jan-Willem Geerts

Abstract:

Summary: Anaphylaxis represents the most critical form of allergic response, characterized by its systemic involvement and potential to threaten life, thus demanding immediate and decisive intervention. Some patients, however, experience recurrence of symptoms even after seemingly successful management. Such biphasic or multiphasic manifestations complicate clinical decision-making, as their frequency is inconsistently reported, predisposing factors remain ambiguous, and existing guidelines offer insufficient direction. This challenge is particularly pronounced in multiphasic anaphylaxis, marked by repeated episodes despite an initially favorable reaction to conventional epinephrine therapy. Given the infrequency with which multiphasic anaphylaxis occurs, detailed case studies become a valuable resource, offering practical insights into management strategies. Herein, we report a case involving a female patient in her 60s who developed multiphasic anaphylaxis triggered by clarithromycin; further evaluation suggested a significant probability of underlying systemic mastocytosis, despite her not fulfilling all formal diagnostic criteria.

Background:

Anaphylaxis represents a critical, and—particularly in the case of anaphylactic shock—a potentially fatal hypersensitivity response frequently prompting visits to emergency departments. Nonetheless, a proportion of patients display a biphasic or even multiphasic progression: these courses begin with an initial anaphylactic event triggered by exposure to an allergen, proceed to a symptom-free interval, and are subsequently marked by a resurgence of symptoms in the absence of further allergen exposure. Although this secondary manifestation most commonly develops within 24 hours after the initial insult, there are documented instances in which symptom reappearance occurs as late as 78 hours post-exposure. The frequency of biphasic anaphylaxis as reported in the literature shows significant variability, ranging from 0.4\% to 23.3\%, with the majority of findings suggesting an incidence in the 4\% to 6\% range. Given the risk for delayed symptom recurrence, post-anaphylaxis monitoring is generally recommended, even following apparent clinical resolution. Nevertheless, inconsistencies exist in how biphasic anaphylaxis is defined across studies, and there remains ongoing debate regarding management approaches—most notably concerning the routine use of corticosteroids. The occurrence of multiphasic anaphylaxis, involving several recurrences of symptoms, raises particular concerns regarding optimal treatment and appropriate duration of observation. The current report describes the case of a woman in her sixties who developed a multiphasic anaphylactic response after administration of clarithromycin.

Case presentation:

A woman in her seventh decade of life, known to have a beta-lactam antibiotic allergy, arrived at the emergency department (ED) experiencing anaphylaxis subsequent to ingesting 500 mg of oral clarithromycin.

Her past medical history included type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, psoriasis, and depression. She weighed 104 kg. Her ongoing pharmacological regimen consisted of metformin (500 mg twice daily), gliclazide (80 mg retard once daily), hydrochlorothiazide (25 mg once daily), omeprazole (20 mg once daily), and sertraline (100 mg once daily). The most recent dose of hydrochlorothiazide was administered more than 12 hours prior to her ED arrival.

In adolescence, the patient had a mild hypersensitivity response to amoxicillin characterized by isolated facial swelling in the absence of oropharyngeal edema, exanthema, hypotension, or respiratory compromise. She also previously experienced a pronounced localized dermal reaction to a wasp sting, with no accompanying systemic features. Nonetheless, this clinical episode constituted her initial occurrence of anaphylaxis.

Five days prior to her ED visit, she had been prescribed clindamycin by her primary care physician for treatment of erysipelas. After five days on clindamycin, she developed an abdominal rash (details not specified), which was interpreted as a delayed allergic reaction to clindamycin. Consequently, her general practitioner changed her antibiotic to clarithromycin, which she had never previously taken.

The patient later arrived at the emergency department exhibiting symptoms including itching, flushing, excessive sweating, restlessness, and a sensation of throat swelling. These symptoms began approximately one hour following the ingestion of her first oral dose of clarithromycin and intensified progressively. She sought medical attention one hour after her initial symptom onset. Upon presentation, her vital signs indicated low blood pressure and reduced oxygen saturation (non-invasive blood pressure: 74/50 mm Hg, peripheral oxygen saturation: 88% on room air, respiratory rate: 22 breaths per minute). Physical examination revealed swelling of the face, a newly developed generalized erythema and urticaria covering her body, along with heightened sweating and agitation. There was no evidence of swelling affecting her tongue or throat, and findings from cardiac and pulmonary auscultation were unremarkable. Her blood glucose measured 12.7 mmol/L, and ECG demonstrated sinus rhythm. Management for a presumptive diagnosis of severe allergic reaction was initiated immediately, including administration of 0.3 mg epinephrine intramuscularly via auto-injector and a slow intravenous injection of 2 mg clemastine in accordance with local treatment guidelines to address the dermatologic manifestations. She also received supplemental oxygen (15 L/min through a non-rebreather mask) and an initial intravenous infusion of 500 mL 0.9% sodium chloride. Supplemental oxygen was discontinued after she demonstrated substantial improvement following epinephrine administration, but had to be resumed briefly during further episodes of anaphylaxis. Clarithromycin therapy was stopped, and no further doses were administered beyond the initial single 500 mg oral dose.

At first, the patient demonstrated a favorable response to the instituted therapy, with normalization of blood pressure and a reduction in facial edema and erythema. Upon admission to the acute internal medicine ward, where she benefited from heightened supervision by trained nursing personnel—but without telemetry—her condition initially stabilized. Nonetheless, within 12 hours, the patient experienced repeated episodes consistent with anaphylactic shock, despite the early positive trend. Following the first recurrence, she was transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) for more intensive observation. Every episode necessitated intramuscular epinephrine administration to achieve symptom control (see figure 1); altogether, four discrete anaphylactic incidents occurred, inclusive of the initial emergency department (ED) event. The first two administrations employed an epinephrine auto-injector delivering 0.3 mg intramuscularly, whereas the subsequent two relied on manually prepared 0.5 mg intramuscular doses. Although the auto-injector dose available at our facility is below the standard recommended 0.5 mg, auto-injectors are preferred in urgent or unfamiliar clinical circumstances (as during the initial ED management of new arrivals) or in areas where anaphylaxis interventions are not frequently practiced, in order to avoid treatment delays due to uncertainty with drug preparation.

Following the first anaphylaxis recurrence, corticosteroid therapy was initiated because of their conventional application in the prevention of biphasic reactions. The regimen included 30 mg intravenous prednisone daily over five days and a single intravenous dose of 8 mg dexamethasone, reflecting differing protocols between the hospital’s general and intensive care wards for managing anaphylaxis. Additionally, for cutaneous symptoms, the patient received two intravenous doses of 2 mg clemastine, and a further 500 mL infusion of normal saline during the second anaphylactic event. Notably, symptom severity diminished with each successive episode, with the clinical presentation evolving from profound hypoxemia accompanied by cyanosis, hypotension, and severe dyspnea initially, to mainly dyspnea, rash recurrence, and pruritus during the final episode.

An early consultation with the department of allergology was undertaken to obtain expert therapeutic recommendations. Two alternative management strategies were contemplated: first, to administer epinephrine continuously via an intravenous infusion, thus aiming to avert recurrence proactively; second, considering the gradual attenuation of symptoms, to adopt a watchful waiting approach in anticipation of spontaneous resolution. Both options entailed significant risk, compounded by the absence of substantive literature to guide therapy in the context of multiphasic anaphylaxis.

Outcome and follow-up:

In the end, neither of the proposed treatment strategies proved to be required, as the patient experienced no additional relapses of symptoms from that point onward. Following a symptom-free period of roughly 24 hours—during which no further episodes of anaphylaxis occurred—she was transferred out of intensive care to a general medical ward. The patient was subsequently discharged after a total hospital stay of 3 days. Upon discharge, she was prescribed the antihistamine levocetirizine as part of her regular medication regimen and was provided with, as well as trained in the use of, an epinephrine auto-injector (0.3 mg). Since her discharge, she has not experienced any recurrence of anaphylaxis.

Given the unusual and protracted nature of her symptoms, additional investigations into potential underlying etiologies were initiated. These investigations suggested a significant likelihood of systemic mastocytosis; however, the diagnostic criteria established by the WHO were not fully satisfied—only two minor criteria were present, and the major criterion could not be assessed due to the limited amount of bone marrow available for biopsy analysis (refer to figure 2). Elevated tryptase concentrations were observed both during her third episode of anaphylaxis and at baseline, and these levels remained persistently high at subsequent follow-up appointments with her allergologist, as summarized in table 1.

Regrettably, tryptase measurements were omitted during the initial assessment of the patient’s first anaphylactic episodes, and thus the standard timing protocols for tryptase analysis were not followed. Current guidelines advise obtaining at least two, preferably three, tryptase samples at well-defined intervals: the first should be collected as promptly as possible, without causing delays in acute treatment; the second between 1–2 hours (but within a maximum of 4 hours) following symptom onset, coinciding with the expected peak of tryptase levels; and a third sample no less than 24 hours after the complete resolution of clinical symptoms, serving as a baseline reference. In the presented case, the initial tryptase was sampled 1 hour after the third instance of anaphylaxis, and the subsequent sample was taken 27 hours after the final event.

No confirmatory allergy diagnostics, such as skin testing, were undertaken due to the disproportionate risk of provoking another severe anaphylactic reaction, particularly since avoidance of the suspected trigger was straightforward. Additional noteworthy observations supportive of a diagnosis of systemic mastocytosis included hepatomegaly identified on abdominal ultrasonography, along with possible neuropsychiatric manifestations such as depression and impaired memory.

Discussion:

Anaphylaxis is a clearly delineated clinical syndrome, notable for its abrupt onset and rapid escalation of symptoms impacting the airway, respiratory function, cardiovascular system, and/or gastrointestinal tract. These clinical features are often, though not invariably, accompanied by cutaneous or mucosal signs such as urticaria, angioedema, or erythema. Although identification of a probable causative allergen may reinforce the diagnosis, in some cases pinpointing a specific inciting agent is not feasible.

More atypical manifestations, including biphasic or refractory anaphylaxis, have less precise definitions, though recent consensus-building initiatives have sought to standardise these terms. According to the Resuscitation Council UK, biphasic anaphylaxis is defined by the recurrence of anaphylactic symptoms several hours after initial symptom resolution, provided that there has been no further allergen exposure. Distinguishing a true biphasic episode from a protracted anaphylactic response—possibly due to only transient effectiveness of epinephrine or continual allergen absorption (as might occur with undigested food remnants or sustained-release pharmaceuticals)—can present significant diagnostic difficulty. Refractory anaphylaxis, on the other hand, describes ongoing anaphylactic symptoms that fail to resolve or only minimally improve despite correct administration of epinephrine.

Clinical guidelines addressing biphasic anaphylaxis predominantly focus on recommendations for patient observation post-event. However, the question of the ideal monitoring duration remains unresolved. Conventional protocols have called for 4 to 6 hours of observation after symptom abatement. The Nederlands Huisartsen Genootschap (NHG) and World Allergy Organisation guidelines both observe that close to half of all biphasic reactions occur between 6 and 12 hours after the initial incident, though neither issues precise guidance regarding observation length. In contrast, the UK guideline from 2021 advocates for a risk-stratified, three-level framework for post-anaphylaxis monitoring. Ultimately, the field requires consensus-driven monitoring protocols to properly weigh resource allocation for the majority experiencing single-episode anaphylaxis against the safety needs of the smaller, but higher-risk, group prone to biphasic events.

There is a notable and concerning deficit in detailed guidance regarding advanced pharmacological approaches for treating biphasic and multiphasic anaphylactic reactions. The management of these more complex anaphylactic courses typically adheres closely to conventional protocols for standard anaphylaxis, centering on the rapid administration of intramuscular epinephrine in conjunction with suitable supportive interventions. According to existing guidelines, the advised dose of epinephrine is 0.01 mg/kg based on body weight, not exceeding a total of 0.5 mg. In clinical practice, this recommendation is often streamlined to a fixed dose of 0.5 mg for adult patients, as most adults surpass the 50 kg weight threshold. Despite this, it is noteworthy that commonly utilized auto-injectors, such as those administered in the initial two events described in our case, generally contain only 0.3 mg of epinephrine, even when marketed for adult use. While epinephrine auto-injectors are indispensable in prehospital care and retain utility in clinical environments—particularly by limiting delays and reducing hesitancy among practitioners with limited exposure to vasoactive drugs like epinephrine—the use of a 0.3 mg dose in adults over 50 kg risks significant underdosing. Evidence from a 2025 retrospective study indicated that recipients of an initial 0.3 mg dose required subsequent escalations in care, including additional epinephrine administration, at a higher rate than those who initially received 0.5 mg. Addressing persistent obstacles, such as providers' familiarity with the historically standard 0.3 mg dose and the widespread availability of 0.3 mg auto-injectors, alongside reinforcing adherence to updated dosing recommendations, could markedly enhance outcomes in anaphylaxis management.

Historically, corticosteroids have been administered, partially with the intent to reduce the likelihood of biphasic reactions. Nonetheless, accumulating research indicates that corticosteroids neither confer benefit nor are they free from risk, prompting a recent paradigm shift in their role within anaphylaxis treatment. In a 2017 systematic review of 22 studies, only a single study found a benefit associated with corticosteroid use, while the remaining 21 failed to demonstrate any reduction in biphasic anaphylaxis risk attributable to corticosteroids. In addition, newer studies draw attention to adverse effects, with a 2019 investigation noting an elevated risk of ICU or hospital admission among patients receiving corticosteroids in the prehospital setting. As a result, the majority of recent clinical guidelines no longer endorse corticosteroids as routine management in anaphylaxis, despite their continued use in some practice settings.

Although biphasic anaphylaxis remains an infrequent occurrence, its clinical significance is substantial. The difficulty in predicting which patients will experience biphasic responses complicates efforts to stratify risk and determine appropriate observation durations. While some risk factors have been proposed—such as delayed administration of epinephrine, more severe initial manifestations, the need for repeated epinephrine doses, and a personal history of biphasic reactions—these indicators lack sufficient reliability to guide targeted monitoring.

Systemic mastocytosis, which is marked by pathological mast cell proliferation, increased mediator release, and multiorgan inflammation, increases vulnerability to severe anaphylaxis. 1517 However, owing to the scarcity of both systemic mastocytosis and biphasic anaphylaxis, there is, to our knowledge, an absence of studies specifically examining how systemic mastocytosis influences biphasic anaphylaxis risk. Further investigations exploring the intersection of mast cell diseases and biphasic anaphylactic episodes could yield important information for the optimization of management strategies.

Anaphylaxis-related deaths are infrequent, particularly in locations where patients can quickly access emergency healthcare services. Moreover, fatalities due to biphasic anaphylaxis are exceedingly uncommon, even though the condition may seem particularly hazardous. Despite these encouraging trends, anaphylaxis continues to present as a prevalent emergency that requires immediate intervention and vigilant observation. While the emphasis of both clinical research and guidelines is predominantly on the initial phase of emergency intervention, the subsequent phases of care and surveillance are equally significant. Enhancing our grasp of the complexities inherent to cases like biphasic anaphylaxis may lead to more individualized, efficient, and focused approaches to managing this condition.

Research into biphasic and especially multiphasic anaphylaxis is hampered by both the scarcity of these events and the intrinsic difficulties associated with conducting studies in emergency scenarios, posing a substantial obstacle to the development of robust evidence-based protocols. In this context, consensus-based expert guidelines with explicit recommendations for management are invaluable, though the formulation of such guidelines is itself limited by a paucity of supporting data. Given the uncommon nature of these complex anaphylactic reactions, establishing a registry to systematically collect pertinent case data—including detailed patient information, possible management suggestions such as those outlined in our case report, and patient outcomes—could serve as a critical foundation for future data-informed expert recommendations.
