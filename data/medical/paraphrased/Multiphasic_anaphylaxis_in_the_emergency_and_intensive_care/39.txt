Title: Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting

Margret Ann Schneider, Hans de Groot, Jan-Willem Geerts

Abstract:

Summary: Anaphylaxis represents the most extreme form of allergic reaction and constitutes a systemic emergency that demands immediate and effective intervention. In a portion of cases, symptoms reappear after apparent resolution despite appropriate treatment, posing biphasic or even multiphasic clinical courses that can complicate management. These presentations create particular difficulties for healthcare providers given variable reported incidence, ambiguous risk factors, and the absence of well-defined approaches in current clinical guidelines. This issue is especially relevant for multiphasic anaphylaxis, which involves several recurrent episodes despite satisfactory initial response to epinephrine therapy. Owing to its infrequent occurrence, published case reports illustrating management approaches are valuable for shedding light on this rare condition. This report describes a case involving a female patient in her 60s who experienced multiphasic anaphylaxis triggered by clarithromycin; subsequent evaluation suggested a strong probability of systemic mastocytosis, although formal diagnostic criteria were not met.

Background:

Anaphylaxis constitutes a severe allergic response that can be life-threatening, particularly in cases of anaphylactic shock, and frequently leads patients to seek emergency medical care. In certain instances, the clinical course may be biphasic or multiphasic, distinguished by an initial reaction upon exposure to an allergen, subsequent resolution of symptoms and an asymptomatic interval, followed by a recurrence of symptoms in the absence of further allergen exposure. Typically, such recurrences occur within 24 hours of the first exposure, though cases have been documented in which symptoms return up to 78 hours afterward. The incidence of biphasic anaphylaxis reported in literature varies considerably, from 0.4% up to 23.3%, though most evidence suggests rates between 4% and 6%. Owing to the potential for symptom relapse, observation periods are generally recommended following an anaphylactic event, even when initial symptoms have subsided completely. Nonetheless, definitions surrounding biphasic anaphylaxis can lack consistency and recommendations for management differ, with recent debate regarding the efficacy of corticosteroids. Multiphasic anaphylaxis—characterized by multiple symptom recurrences—poses unique challenges for therapeutic strategies and monitoring. This report describes the case of a woman in her 60s who experienced a multiphasic anaphylactic reaction following administration of clarithromycin.

Case presentation:

A woman in her sixties, with a documented beta-lactam allergy, arrived at the emergency department (ED) exhibiting anaphylaxis after ingesting clarithromycin 500 mg orally.

Her medical background was notable for type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, psoriasis, and depression. She weighed 104 kg. Current medications included metformin 500 mg twice daily, gliclazide 80 mg retard once daily, hydrochlorothiazide 25 mg once daily, omeprazole 20 mg once daily, and sertraline 100 mg once daily. The most recent hydrochlorothiazide dose had been administered in the morning, more than 12 hours prior to her ED visit.

Previously, she experienced a mild allergic reaction to amoxicillin during adolescence, characterized solely by facial swelling without associated oropharyngeal swelling, rash, hypotension, or dyspnea. Additionally, she once had a significant localized skin response to a wasp sting, without systemic involvement. This episode marked her initial occurrence of anaphylaxis.

Five days prior to presenting to the ED, her general practitioner prescribed clindamycin to treat erysipelas. After five days on clindamycin, she developed an unspecified abdominal rash, which was assessed as a delayed allergic response to clindamycin. As a result, the antibiotic regimen was switched to clarithromycin, which she had not previously received.

She later arrived at the emergency department with complaints of itching, facial flushing, excessive sweating, restlessness, and a sensation of throat swelling. These symptoms began approximately one hour after she ingested her first dose of clarithromycin, and intensified as time passed. She presented for medical evaluation one hour following symptom onset. Upon her arrival at the ED, her vital parameters were notable for low blood pressure (non-invasive measurement: 74/50 mm Hg) and reduced oxygen saturation (SpO2 88% on room air), with a respiratory rate of 22 breaths per minute. Physical assessment revealed swelling of the face, newly developed widespread erythema, diffuse urticaria, excessive sweating, and agitation. There was no evidence of tongue or throat swelling, and both cardiac and pulmonary auscultation were unremarkable. Her blood glucose measured 12.7 mmol/L, and electrocardiogram demonstrated sinus rhythm. She was immediately managed for presumed severe allergic reaction, which included administration of 0.3 mg intramuscular epinephrine by auto-injector and a slow intravenous injection of 2 mg clemastine, following local protocols for dermatological manifestations. Supportive therapy was provided with 15 L supplemental oxygen delivered via non-rebreather mask and an infusion of 500 mL 0.9% sodium chloride for initial stabilization. Oxygen supplementation was discontinued after she improved with epinephrine, though it was temporarily reintroduced during subsequent episodes of anaphylaxis. Clarithromycin therapy was halted, and no further doses were given after the single initial 500 mg oral dose.

A favorable initial response to the treatment was noted, with normalization of blood pressure and reduction in both facial swelling and erythema. The patient was transferred to the acute internal medicine ward, where she received heightened supervision by dedicated nursing personnel, albeit without telemetry monitoring. Despite the promising initial outcome, the patient experienced multiple episodes consistent with anaphylactic shock within the first 12 hours following admission. After the first recurrence of anaphylactic shock, she was moved to the intensive care unit (ICU) for closer observation. Each recurrence required intramuscular epinephrine administration to achieve symptom resolution (figure 1), with a total of four episodes including the index event in the emergency department (ED). For the initial two administrations, a 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injector was used intramuscularly, while the subsequent two episodes were managed with individually prepared 0.5 mg intramuscular epinephrine doses. Although the auto-injector dosage at our facility is below the 0.5 mg recommended amount, we prefer its use in time-critical situations—such as the acute stabilization of a new ED patient—or when emergency interventions for anaphylaxis are infrequent and uncertainties regarding drug administration could delay necessary treatment. Corticosteroid therapy was initiated following the first relapse to address the traditional preventive role for biphasic anaphylaxis. The regimen included 30 mg intravenous prednisone daily over 5 days, in addition to a single intravenous dose of 8 mg dexamethasone, due to discrepancies between the general ward and ICU anaphylaxis protocols in our institution. Additionally, she was administered 2 mg intravenous clemastine on two occasions for dermatological manifestations, and received 500 mL of normal saline during her second anaphylaxis episode. Notably, the severity of symptoms progressively decreased with each event, ranging from pronounced hypoxemia with cyanosis, hypotension, and significant dyspnea initially, to dyspnea, return of rash, and pruritus in the final episode.

An early consultation with the allergology department was arranged to seek specialized management recommendations. Two potential therapeutic strategies were discussed. The first involved preemptive, continuous intravenous epinephrine infusion to forestall the development of further anaphylactic episodes. The alternative, given the gradual decline in symptom severity, was to adopt a watchful waiting approach to monitor for spontaneous resolution. Both strategies presented considerable risks, particularly given the absence of supporting literature to guide therapy in cases characterized by multiple phases.

Outcome and follow-up:

Ultimately, neither management strategy proved necessary, as the patient experienced no additional recurrence of symptoms beyond this point. Following approximately 24 hours without any further anaphylactic events, she was moved back to a general care ward. She was discharged after a 3-day hospitalization. Upon discharge, levocetirizine, an antihistamine, was introduced as her new standard medication, and she was supplied with and trained in the use of an epinephrine auto-injector (0.3 mg). She has not had any subsequent instances of anaphylaxis.

Given the unusual and extended course of illness, further investigation into possible underlying etiologies was initiated. The evaluation demonstrated a strong likelihood of systemic mastocytosis; however, it did not fulfill the criteria for a definitive diagnosis, as only two minor WHO criteria were present, and major criteria could not be evaluated due to insufficient bone marrow biopsy material (refer to figure 2). Regrettably, serum tryptase was not assessed during the initial evaluation of the patient’s first episodes of anaphylaxis, resulting in non-compliance with the recommended timing for tryptase sampling. Current guidelines advise collecting at least two, preferably three, tryptase specimens at defined intervals: one obtained as soon as reasonably possible without delaying emergency intervention, a second drawn 1–2 hours (but no later than 4 hours) following symptom onset to capture peak tryptase levels, and a third specimen collected at least 24 hours after all symptoms have resolved to document baseline tryptase. In the present case, the initial tryptase measurement occurred one hour after the third anaphylactic event, and a subsequent sample was obtained 27 hours following the final anaphylactic recurrence.

No tests to confirm allergy were conducted, as procedures such as skin testing—entailing the risk of eliciting another severe anaphylactic event—were considered unjustified, especially since subsequent exposures could be easily avoided. Additional pertinent findings, consistent with a diagnosis of systemic mastocytosis, were the detection of hepatomegaly on abdominal ultrasound and possible neuropsychiatric manifestations, such as depression and memory impairment.

Discussion:

Anaphylaxis is a clinically well-recognized syndrome, typically marked by an abrupt onset and rapid escalation of symptoms impacting the airway, respiratory, circulatory, and/or gastrointestinal systems, and often presenting with cutaneous or mucosal signs such as urticaria, angioedema, or flushing. Although establishing a likely allergen enhances diagnostic confidence, identification of a precise trigger is not always feasible.

More complicated forms of anaphylaxis, like biphasic or refractory presentations, lack clear definitions, though efforts have recently been made to reach consensus. According to the Resuscitation Council UK, biphasic anaphylaxis is characterized by recurrence of symptoms several hours after apparent resolution without further exposure to the allergen. It can be difficult to distinguish true biphasic reactions from cases of prolonged anaphylaxis with only temporary improvement following epinephrine administration, or ongoing allergen absorption from retained food or slow-release drugs. In contrast, refractory anaphylaxis is defined as ongoing symptoms that show little or no response to properly administered epinephrine.

Guidelines focusing on biphasic anaphylaxis primarily offer recommendations on clinical observation; however, the most suitable duration of monitoring is still subject to debate. Traditional recommendations suggest observing patients for 4 to 6 hours after symptoms resolve. Both the Dutch Nederlands Huisartsen Genootschap (NHG) and World Allergy Organisation guidelines state that about 50% of biphasic reactions develop within 6–12 hours after the initial incident, yet neither stipulates a precise duration for monitoring. By comparison, the 2021 UK guideline introduces a tiered risk-based protocol for observation. There is a clear need for agreed-upon strategies for monitoring to address the balance between efficient use of healthcare resources—mainly for patients with a single anaphylactic event—and the safety concerns posed by the smaller but less easily identifiable group at risk for biphasic complications.

There is a notable and concerning absence of specific recommendations regarding advanced pharmacologic interventions for biphasic and multiphasic anaphylactic reactions. Therapeutic approaches to these forms largely follow standard anaphylaxis protocols, including immediate intramuscular administration of epinephrine in conjunction with suitable supportive measures. Guidelines specify an epinephrine dose of 0.01 mg/kg body weight, up to a maximum of 0.5 mg. For adults, this is frequently approximated to 0.5 mg, given that most adults weigh more than 50 kg. Nevertheless, it is noteworthy that many commercially available auto-injectors—like those employed in the initial two episodes in our case report—dispense only 0.3 mg epinephrine, even when designated for adult use. While epinephrine auto-injectors are critical in prehospital scenarios, they remain valuable in healthcare facilities by shortening delays and lessening hesitation, especially among staff who infrequently administer vasoactive drugs. Despite their common usage, delivering 0.3 mg via auto-injector may result in significant underdosing for adults weighing over 50 kg. Data from a 2025 retrospective analysis indicated that individuals who first received 0.3 mg required additional interventions, including further doses of epinephrine, more often than those administered an initial dose of 0.5 mg. Addressing factors such as provider familiarity with the traditionally used 0.3 mg dose and the ready availability of 0.3 mg auto-injectors, as well as enhancing compliance with recommended dosing, may be crucial for optimizing anaphylaxis management.

Historically, corticosteroids were advocated in part to reduce the likelihood of biphasic reactions. Nevertheless, accumulating data now indicate a lack of efficacy and even possible adverse effects, prompting a recent movement away from steroid use in anaphylaxis management. A systematic review published in 2017 analyzed 22 studies, of which only one supported corticosteroid use while 21 provided no evidence that these agents decrease the incidence of biphasic anaphylaxis. In addition, newer studies have highlighted the significance of side effects, with a 2019 study demonstrating an increased rate of admission to ICU or hospital wards following prehospital administration of corticosteroids. As a result, the majority of current guidelines recommend against routine use of corticosteroids, despite their ongoing application in clinical practice.

Although biphasic anaphylaxis is uncommon, it remains clinically significant. The lack of dependable indicators makes it difficult to assess risk and determine monitoring strategies. Proposed risk factors include delayed use of epinephrine, severity of initial symptoms, need for multiple doses of epinephrine, and previous occurrences of biphasic reactions. However, predictive markers sufficient for focused monitoring have yet to be established.

Systemic mastocytosis is characterized by increased numbers of mast cells, elevated mediator release, and widespread inflammatory involvement of various organs, which makes patients more susceptible to severe anaphylactic episodes.1517 Despite the infrequency of both diagnoses, existing literature to date does not specifically explore the impact of systemic mastocytosis on biphasic anaphylaxis. Additional investigation into mast cell disorders and their relation to biphasic anaphylaxis may help inform future therapeutic strategies.

Anaphylaxis-related mortality remains low, particularly in areas where emergency medical services are readily accessible. Fatalities associated with biphasic anaphylactic reactions are even less common, in spite of their seemingly high-risk nature. Nonetheless, anaphylaxis continues to represent a frequently encountered emergency, necessitating immediate intervention and thorough observation. While much research and guidelines concentrate on the initial emergency response, the importance of follow-up care and appropriate surveillance is equally critical. Enhanced insight into complex presentations like biphasic anaphylaxis may facilitate the development of management strategies that are more individualized, focused, and efficient in resource utilization.

Because biphasic and especially multiphasic anaphylactic reactions occur so infrequently and conducting studies during emergent clinical situations presents significant difficulties, devising robust, evidence-based protocols is highly challenging. In these contexts, expert consensus guidelines offering structured recommendations hold significant merit; yet, formulating these guidelines is itself constrained by limited existing data. Given the uncommon nature of these complex anaphylaxis cases, establishing a case registry that incorporates key patient characteristics, proposed management approaches as demonstrated in our case report, and relevant outcome measures could establish a valuable foundation for the creation of expert-driven, data-informed recommendations.
