Title: Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting

Margret Ann Schneider, Hans de Groot, Jan-Willem Geerts

Abstract:

Summary: Anaphylaxis represents the most serious allergic reaction and can rapidly become fatal if not addressed promptly with appropriate interventions. In certain instances, patients experience a return of symptoms following initial stabilization and successful treatment, constituting biphasic or multiphasic courses that pose significant clinical difficulties. The prevalence of such recurrences, their underlying risk factors, and optimal management approaches are not well defined in the literature or existing protocols. This challenge is particularly notable in cases of multiphasic anaphylaxis, where repeated episodes occur after administration of standard therapy such as epinephrine. Given the uncommon nature of multiphasic anaphylaxis, published case reports that describe management strategies are especially valuable for guiding clinicians. This paper details the clinical course of a female patient in her sixth decade who developed multiphasic anaphylaxis following exposure to clarithromycin, with further evaluation revealing a strong probability of systemic mastocytosis, though she did not meet established diagnostic thresholds.

Background:

Anaphylaxis is an acute, severe allergic reaction which can be particularly dangerous in cases of anaphylactic shock, and frequently leads patients to seek urgent medical attention. A portion of these cases demonstrate a biphasic or even multiphasic progression, in which an initial anaphylactic response to an allergen occurs, followed by symptom resolution—a symptom-free interval—then a return of symptoms despite no further allergen exposure. Typically, this pattern emerges within 24 hours after the initial event, although some reports document recurrence as late as 78 hours afterwards. The documented frequency of biphasic anaphylaxis is variable, reported between 0.4% and 23.3%, with most studies suggesting a rate between 4% and 6%. Because of the risk of symptom recurrence, patients are often kept under observation after an anaphylactic episode, even if symptoms appear to have subsided completely. Nevertheless, there is inconsistency in defining biphasic anaphylaxis and significant variability in management recommendations, with recent debate concerning the efficacy of corticosteroids in prevention of relapse. Multiphasic anaphylaxis, which involves multiple recurrences of symptoms, presents particular challenges for clinical management and monitoring strategies. Here, we describe the case of a woman in her 60s who experienced a multiphasic anaphylactic reaction to clarithromycin.

Case presentation:

A female patient in her sixties, known to have a beta-lactam antibiotic allergy, arrived at the emergency department (ED) experiencing anaphylaxis after ingesting clarithromycin 500 mg orally.

Her past medical history was notable for type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, psoriasis, and depression. She weighed 104 kg. Her ongoing medications included metformin 500 mg twice daily, gliclazide 80 mg extended-release once daily, hydrochlorothiazide 25 mg once daily, omeprazole 20 mg once daily, and sertraline 100 mg once daily. The most recent dose of hydrochlorothiazide was taken that morning, more than 12 hours before she arrived at the ED.

During adolescence, the patient experienced a mild allergic response to amoxicillin, which manifested solely as facial swelling without involvement of the oropharynx, rash, hypotension, or shortness of breath. Additionally, she had previously developed a pronounced localized skin reaction after a wasp sting, but did not exhibit any systemic manifestations. The current clinical episode marked her initial occurrence of anaphylaxis.

Five days prior to her ED visit, her general practitioner prescribed clindamycin for the treatment of erysipelas. Following five days of clindamycin therapy, she developed a rash on her abdomen (details not provided). This rash was considered a delayed allergic reaction to clindamycin, prompting her general practitioner to change her antibiotic regimen to clarithromycin, which she had never received before.

She later arrived at the emergency department with complaints of itchiness, flushing, excessive sweating, restlessness, and a sensation of throat swelling. These symptoms began one hour following oral administration of her initial clarithromycin dose and became more severe over time. She presented for medical assessment one hour after the onset of these symptoms. Upon initial evaluation in the ED, her vital signs revealed hypotension and hypoxemia (non-invasive blood pressure at 74/50 mm Hg, peripheral oxygen saturation measured at 88% on room air, and respiratory rate of 22 breaths per minute). Physical examination demonstrated facial edema, new generalized erythema, and urticaria covering her body, accompanied by increased perspiration and agitation. There was no evidence of tongue or pharyngeal swelling, and both cardiac and pulmonary auscultation yielded normal findings. Laboratory testing showed a blood glucose of 12.7 mmol/L, and her electrocardiogram revealed a sinus rhythm. She immediately received treatment for a suspected severe hypersensitivity reaction, which included intramuscular administration of 0.3 mg epinephrine via auto-injector and a slow intravenous injection of 2 mg clemastine to address cutaneous manifestations, in accordance with local protocols. In addition, she was provided with supplemental oxygen (15 L using a non-rebreather mask) and an infusion of 500 mL normal saline (0.9% sodium chloride) during initial stabilization. Oxygen therapy could be withdrawn following an adequate response to the epinephrine, although it required temporary reinitiation during later episodes of anaphylaxis. Clarithromycin was stopped, and no further doses were administered after the single initial oral 500 mg dose.

Initially, the patient responded well to therapy, with normalization of blood pressure and reduction in facial swelling and erythema. She was transferred to the acute internal ward, which offers enhanced monitoring by specialized nursing staff, though without telemetry. Despite the initial improvement, within the first 12 hours of admission she experienced repeated episodes consistent with anaphylactic shock. Following the first recurrence, she was moved to the intensive care unit (ICU) for closer observation. Each anaphylactic episode was resolved by intramuscular epinephrine administration (figure 1); altogether, there were four episodes including the initial presentation at the emergency department. The first two episodes were managed with a 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injector administered intramuscularly, while for the subsequent two, individually prepared doses of 0.5 mg epinephrine were delivered intramuscularly. Although the auto-injectors in use at our clinic contain less than the recommended 0.5 mg, they are favored for urgent situations such as initial stabilization in the ED or in environments where anaphylaxis is rare and hesitation due to unfamiliarity with emergency medications could delay intervention. After the first anaphylactic relapse, corticosteroids were added to therapy owing to their established role in biphasic anaphylaxis prevention. She was given 30 mg of intravenous prednisone daily for 5 days and a single 8 mg dose of intravenous dexamethasone, as recommended by differing protocols for anaphylaxis management on the general ward and ICU. Additionally, for cutaneous symptoms, she received 2 mg clemastine intravenously on two occasions and was given 500 mL of normal saline during the second anaphylactic episode. Notably, symptom severity progressively decreased with each episode, starting with profound hypoxaemia accompanied by cyanosis, hypotension, and severe dyspnoea, ultimately presenting as dyspnoea and recurrence of rash with pruritus during the final episode.

An allergology consultation was obtained early for expert guidance. Two possible therapeutic approaches were discussed: first, continuous intravenous epinephrine via infusion pump to preempt further episodes of anaphylaxis, and second, observation to monitor for spontaneous resolution given the diminishing symptom intensity. Each option entailed significant risks, highlighted by the lack of published evidence to inform management in multiphasic anaphylaxis cases.

Outcome and follow-up:

In the end, neither intervention was needed as the patient did not experience any additional recurrence of symptoms. Approximately 24 hours after remaining free of new anaphylactic episodes, she was returned to a general ward. After a total hospital stay of 3 days, she was discharged. Levocetirizine, an antihistamine, was introduced as a routine medication, and she was provided with a 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injector, along with instructions for its use. There have been no subsequent anaphylactic events.

Given the unusual and extended course of her illness, further investigation into possible underlying factors was initiated. This evaluation indicated a strong likelihood of systemic mastocytosis, though a definitive diagnosis could not be established, as only two WHO minor criteria were met and the major criteria could not be assessed owing to insufficient material obtained from the bone marrow biopsy (refer to figure 2). Regrettably, tryptase measurements were not obtained during the initial assessment of the first episodes of anaphylaxis, resulting in deviation from the recommended timeframes for tryptase sampling. Current guidelines advise collecting at least two, preferably three tryptase samples at designated intervals: the first sample should be obtained as soon as possible, provided it does not delay therapy; the second should be collected 1–2 hours after symptom onset (but within a maximum of 4 hours), coinciding with the anticipated peak in tryptase concentration; and a third sample should be drawn at least 24 hours after complete clinical recovery to ascertain baseline tryptase values. In the present case, the initial tryptase measurement occurred 1 hour following the third anaphylactic event, and a subsequent sample was taken 27 hours after the final anaphylactic recurrence.

No confirmatory allergy testing was conducted, as the potential risk of inducing another severe anaphylactic reaction through modalities such as skin testing was considered disproportionate, particularly because future exposure could be readily circumvented. Additional noteworthy findings consistent with systemic mastocytosis included hepatomegaly seen on abdominal ultrasound and possible neuropsychiatric features such as depression and memory disturbances.

Discussion:

Anaphylaxis is generally recognized as a distinct clinical condition, marked by the abrupt appearance and quick escalation of symptoms that impact the airway, respiratory or cardiovascular systems, and/or the gastrointestinal tract, and it is often seen with cutaneous or mucosal signs such as urticaria, angioedema, or flushing. Although identifying a probable allergen strengthens the diagnosis, in many cases the specific trigger cannot be confirmed.

Atypical forms, including biphasic and refractory anaphylaxis, are not as clearly defined, though there have been recent attempts to standardize their definitions. According to the Resuscitation Council UK, biphasic anaphylaxis is defined as the return of symptoms several hours after they have initially resolved, without further allergen exposure. Distinguishing true biphasic reactions from instances of prolonged anaphylaxis with brief improvement following epinephrine administration or continued absorption of allergen (such as residual food particles or slow-release drugs) poses significant diagnostic challenges. Refractory anaphylaxis, on the other hand, is persistent anaphylactic activity that does not adequately resolve with properly delivered epinephrine.

Recommendations within guidelines on biphasic anaphylaxis are largely centered on the need for clinical observation, yet there is ongoing controversy regarding the ideal duration for monitoring. Historically, patients have been observed for 4 to 6 hours after symptoms resolve. The Nederlands Huisartsen Genootschap (NHG) and World Allergy Organisation guidelines both highlight that nearly fifty percent of biphasic reactions manifest within 6–12 hours following the initial episode, but neither includes a defined monitoring interval. By contrast, the UK 2021 guideline suggests a three-tier, risk-based framework for post-anaphylaxis observation. There is an evident need for unified monitoring protocols that effectively reconcile the allocation of clinical resources (particularly in the majority presenting with isolated single episodes) with the imperative of protecting patients from rare but serious biphasic complications.

There is a notable and concerning gap in recommendations for advanced pharmacologic interventions in biphasic and multiphasic anaphylaxis. Treatment protocols for these recurrent forms essentially replicate those used for standard anaphylactic reactions, prioritizing immediate intramuscular administration of epinephrine in conjunction with suitable supportive measures. Current guidelines advise an epinephrine dose of 0.01 mg/kg, up to a ceiling of 0.5 mg. Since most adults weigh more than 50 kg, this is commonly rounded to a single 0.5 mg dose in adult patients. Nonetheless, it is noteworthy that many commercial auto-injectors, including the ones utilized for the initial anaphylactic events in this case, deliver only 0.3 mg of epinephrine, despite being labeled for adult administration. While auto-injectors are indispensable in prehospital environments, they also offer advantages in clinical contexts by shortening the time to treatment and mitigating reluctance among staff who seldom administer vasoactive medications such as epinephrine. Yet, relying on a 0.3 mg auto-injector for adults exceeding 50 kg frequently results in underdosing, which may carry significant consequences. Findings from a 2025 retrospective analysis indicated that individuals initially treated with 0.3 mg of epinephrine required escalation of therapy, including further epinephrine doses, at a substantially higher rate compared to those who received the 0.5 mg dose initially. Addressing barriers—such as the entrenched preference for the traditionally used 0.3 mg dose and the predominance of 0.3 mg auto-injectors—and encouraging strict adherence to guideline-recommended dosing may be pivotal in optimizing anaphylaxis management.

Historically, corticosteroids were utilized with the aim of preventing biphasic reactions. Nonetheless, increasing evidence suggests their use confers no benefit and may actually pose risks, prompting a recent move away from corticosteroids in the treatment of anaphylaxis. A systematic review published in 2017 evaluated 22 studies; only one study favored corticosteroid use, whereas the other 21 did not demonstrate any reduction in the likelihood of biphasic anaphylaxis. Moreover, recent research has highlighted the importance of corticosteroid-associated adverse effects, with a 2019 study finding a greater probability of ICU or hospital admissions following prehospital steroid administration. As a result, most updated guidelines recommend against routine corticosteroid use, although the practice still persists in some clinical settings.

Although biphasic anaphylaxis occurs infrequently, it remains of significant clinical concern. The lack of reliable risk factors complicates the process of identifying patients requiring enhanced observation. Proposed risk factors include delayed epinephrine intervention, severe initial symptoms, administration of multiple epinephrine doses, and history of biphasic reactions. Despite these suggestions, no dependable markers exist to guide selective monitoring.

Systemic mastocytosis, characterized by abnormal mast cell proliferation, excessive mediator release, and multi-organ inflammation, increases susceptibility to severe anaphylaxis.1517 Nonetheless, given the infrequency of both systemic mastocytosis and biphasic anaphylaxis, there is currently no research specifically exploring their relationship. Additional studies on mast cell disorders and biphasic anaphylaxis may yield important information for future therapeutic strategies.

Anaphylaxis-related deaths are uncommon, particularly in areas where emergency medical services can be rapidly accessed. Fatal cases of biphasic anaphylaxis are even less frequent, despite the condition's potentially serious nature. Nonetheless, anaphylaxis continues to be a prevalent acute medical condition necessitating immediate intervention and careful observation. Although initial emergency management is often emphasized in both research and clinical guidelines, ongoing care and monitoring are also essential. Improved insights into atypical presentations of anaphylaxis, such as biphasic forms, may facilitate the development of more tailored, patient-specific, and resource-conscious approaches to management.

The infrequency of biphasic and especially multiphasic anaphylaxis, combined with methodological challenges posed by emergent care research, complicates the establishment of robust evidence-based protocols. In these circumstances, consensus-driven guidelines from experts offering specific management directions are extremely useful; however, their creation is also restricted by limited clinical data. Given the scarcity of these complex presentations, a registry compiling cases—detailing pertinent patient characteristics, potential management options (such as those described in our report), and outcomes—may provide a foundation for informed, expert-led recommendations.
