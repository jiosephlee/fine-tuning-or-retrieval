Title: Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting

Margret Ann Schneider, Hans de Groot, Jan-Willem Geerts

Abstract:

Summary: Anaphylaxis represents the most critical form of allergic response and constitutes a systemic reaction that can be fatal if not treated swiftly and appropriately. In a subset of patients, symptoms reappear even after seemingly successful therapy and initial resolution, resulting in biphasic or even multiphasic patterns. These recurrent anaphylactic episodes pose particular difficulties for healthcare providers, given the inconsistency in reported frequency, lack of well-defined risk factors, and absence of robust management recommendations in existing protocols. This concern is heightened when multiphasic anaphylaxis occurs, characterized by several recurrences of anaphylactic symptoms after an apparent full response to standard epinephrine therapy. Because multiphasic anaphylaxis remains relatively uncommon, detailed case reports describing its clinical approach serve as valuable contributions to the understanding of this challenging entity. Herein, we describe the case of a woman in her sixties who experienced multiphasic anaphylaxis attributed to clarithromycin, and subsequent evaluation indicated a strong probability of systemic mastocytosis, though she did not fulfill the full formal diagnostic criteria.

Background:

Anaphylaxis represents a critical allergic reaction that can be fatal, particularly in cases progressing to anaphylactic shock, and frequently prompts emergency medical attention. In certain patients, the clinical course can be biphasic or even multiphasic, wherein an initial anaphylactic event is followed by a symptom-free interval before the recurrence of symptoms despite the absence of further allergen exposure. Such recurrences are most often noted within 24 hours of the initial contact with the allergen, though there are documented instances of relapse occurring as late as 78 hours afterwards. Reports of the frequency of biphasic anaphylaxis display considerable variability, ranging from 0.4% to 23.3%, although the majority of studies suggest a prevalence of 4% to 6%. Given the risk of secondary reactions, standard practice recommends a period of clinical observation after apparent resolution of the initial anaphylactic episode. Nevertheless, inconsistencies exist in the definitions used for biphasic anaphylaxis, and corresponding management guidelines differ, with recent debate surrounding the use of corticosteroids. Multiphasic anaphylaxis, in which symptoms recur multiple times, poses particular difficulties in establishing optimal monitoring and therapeutic strategies. Herein, we describe the case of a female patient in her 60s who developed a multiphasic anaphylactic reaction subsequent to clarithromycin exposure.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 60s, with a known allergy to beta-lactam antibiotics, presented to the emergency department (ED) with anaphylaxis following oral clarithromycin 500 mg.

The patient's past medical history was notable for type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, psoriasis, and depression. She weighed 104 kg. Her current medications included metformin 500 mg twice daily, gliclazide 80 mg sustained release once daily, hydrochlorothiazide 25 mg once daily, omeprazole 20 mg once daily, and sertraline 100 mg once daily. She had last taken hydrochlorothiazide in the morning, over 12 hours prior to arriving at the emergency department.

During adolescence, she had experienced a mild allergic response to amoxicillin, manifested as facial edema without involvement of the oropharynx, cutaneous rash, hypotension, or breathing difficulty. Additionally, she had previously developed a marked localized skin reaction following a wasp sting, but this was unaccompanied by any systemic manifestations. The clinical episode described below was her first anaphylactic reaction.

Five days prior to coming to the emergency department, she was started on clindamycin for erysipelas by her primary care physician. After five days of taking clindamycin, she developed a rash on her abdomen, details of which were not specified. This rash was considered a delayed allergic response to clindamycin, prompting her primary care provider to change the antibiotic to clarithromycin, an agent she had never received before.

She later arrived at the emergency department experiencing itching, flushing, excessive sweating, restlessness, and a sensation of throat swelling. These symptoms began approximately 1 hour following the ingestion of her first dose of clarithromycin and intensified progressively. She sought medical attention within 1 hour after her symptoms initially appeared. On initial evaluation in the emergency department, her vital signs indicated low blood pressure and hypoxemia (non-invasive blood pressure: 74/50 mm Hg, peripheral oxygen saturation at 88% on room air, respiratory rate of 22 breaths per minute). Physical examination revealed swelling of the face, newly developed widespread redness and hives across her body, increased sweating, and agitation. There was no evidence of swelling in her tongue or throat, and both cardiac and pulmonary auscultation were unremarkable. Laboratory investigations showed a blood glucose level of 12.7 mmol/L, and her electrocardiogram demonstrated a sinus rhythm. She was immediately managed for a presumed severe allergic reaction, receiving 0.3 mg epinephrine intramuscularly via auto-injector and 2 mg clemastine administered slowly by intravenous injection for treatment of her cutaneous symptoms, in accordance with local protocols. She was also provided supplemental oxygen (15 L via non-rebreather mask) and 500 mL of 0.9% sodium chloride (normal saline) as part of her initial resuscitation. Oxygen supplementation was discontinued once she showed a favorable response to epinephrine; however, it was briefly reinstated during further episodes of anaphylaxis. Clarithromycin therapy was halted, and no additional doses were administered beyond the initial oral 500 mg.

Initially, the patient responded well to treatment, as her blood pressure normalized and there was a noticeable reduction in facial edema and erythema. She was transferred to the acute internal medicine ward, where she was closely monitored by specialized nursing staff, but without telemetry surveillance. Despite her initial improvement, within the first 12 hours of hospitalization she experienced repeated episodes consistent with anaphylactic shock. Following the first recurrence, she was moved to the intensive care unit (ICU) for more intensive observation. Each anaphylactic event was managed with intramuscular epinephrine (see figure 1), totaling four episodes including the original presentation in the emergency department. The first two doses of epinephrine were delivered via auto-injector, each containing 0.3 mg, while the subsequent two episodes were treated with manually prepared intramuscular injections of 0.5 mg epinephrine. Although the auto-injectors in our facility provide a dose lower than the recommended 0.5 mg, we prefer using auto-injectors in situations requiring rapid intervention—such as during initial emergency department stabilization—or in clinical areas where acute management of anaphylaxis is uncommon and unfamiliarity could cause delay. After the first recurrence, corticosteroid therapy was added, acknowledging their customary use in reducing the risk of biphasic anaphylactic reactions. The patient received prednisone intravenously at 30 mg daily for five days and a one-time intravenous dose of dexamethasone 8 mg, reflecting variability between our hospital’s general ward and intensive care anaphylaxis management protocols. In addition, she was given 2 mg of clemastine intravenously on two occasions to manage skin symptoms, and 500 mL of normal saline intravenously during her second anaphylactic episode. Notably, the severity of her symptoms diminished with each recurrence, ranging from profound hypoxemia with cyanosis, hypotension, and pronounced dyspnea during the initial event, to dyspnea, re-emergence of rash, and pruritus by the final episode.

An allergology consultation was requested early in the course for expert input. Two alternative management strategies were evaluated in this situation. One option involved the pre-emptive use of continuous intravenous epinephrine infusion to forestall further anaphylactic events rather than awaiting symptom recurrence. The second strategy considered was a watchful waiting approach, particularly as the patient's symptom intensity appeared to wane over time, with the possibility that her symptoms might resolve spontaneously. Both potential courses of action entailed significant risks, complicated by a lack of evidence-based recommendations for managing multiphasic anaphylaxis in the literature.

Outcome and follow-up:

In the end, both proposed treatments were unnecessary, as the patient did not experience additional recurrence of symptoms. Following about 24 hours without subsequent anaphylactic events, she was moved back to a regular medical ward. She was ultimately released from the hospital after a total of 3 days of inpatient care. Upon discharge, levocetirizine, an antihistamine, was initiated as a regular therapy, and she was prescribed a 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injector with appropriate instruction for its use. No subsequent anaphylactic events have occurred since discharge.

Given the unusual and protracted progression of her illness, further investigation into possible underlying etiologies was undertaken. This assessment indicated a strong likelihood of systemic mastocytosis, although a definitive diagnosis could not be established (only two WHO minor criteria were met, with major criteria not evaluable due to limited bone marrow biopsy sampling—refer to figure 2). Regrettably, tryptase measurements were not obtained during the initial assessment of the patient’s first anaphylactic reactions, and as such, the standard timing for tryptase testing was not followed. Current guidelines advocate for collecting at least two, and preferably three, tryptase samples at designated times: the first should be drawn as soon as possible, provided that treatment is not delayed; a second sample is to be collected 1–2 hours after symptom onset (but within 4 hours), coinciding with the anticipated peak in tryptase concentration; and a third sample should be taken at least 24 hours after complete symptom resolution to determine the baseline. In this case, the initial tryptase sample was obtained one hour following the third episode of anaphylaxis, and the second sample was drawn 27 hours after the last episode.

Allergy provocation testing was not conducted, as the risk of triggering another severe anaphylactic reaction—for example, via skin testing—was considered disproportionate, especially since avoidance of the suspected trigger is relatively straightforward. Additional findings supporting the diagnosis of systemic mastocytosis included hepatomegaly identified on abdominal ultrasound, as well as potential neuropsychiatric manifestations such as depression and memory impairment.

Discussion:

Anaphylaxis is recognized as a well-established clinical diagnosis, defined by its abrupt onset and swift evolution of symptoms impacting the airway, respiration, circulation, and/or gastrointestinal system, frequently presenting with skin or mucosal signs such as urticaria, angioedema, or flushing. Although identifying an associated allergen can corroborate the diagnosis, isolating a distinct causative agent is not always feasible.

More complicated forms, including biphasic and refractory anaphylaxis, are not as clearly outlined, though recent initiatives have sought to develop consensus definitions. According to the Resuscitation Council UK, biphasic anaphylaxis refers to the return of symptoms several hours following initial symptom resolution without subsequent exposure to the allergen. Distinguishing true biphasic responses from persistent anaphylaxis with only temporary improvement after epinephrine administration, or from ongoing allergen uptake (for example, remaining food particles or slow-release medications), presents diagnostic challenges. Conversely, refractory anaphylaxis is described as continuous anaphylaxis with inadequate or absent response to properly administered epinephrine.

Most guidelines addressing biphasic anaphylaxis focus on recommendations for clinical observation. However, the ideal observation timeframe remains contentious. Conventional guidance suggests monitoring patients for 4 to 6 hours post-symptom resolution. The Dutch Nederlands Huisartsen Genootschap (NHG) and the World Allergy Organisation state that around 50% of biphasic reactions develop within 6–12 hours of the initial episode, yet neither issues a concrete monitoring duration. By contrast, the 2021 UK guideline advocates for a three-level risk-based monitoring protocol. There is an evident need for harmonized observation strategies to adequately allocate healthcare resources in the majority of single-episode anaphylaxis cases while maintaining patient safety for the smaller, and harder to identify, group at risk for biphasic reactions.

There is a significant and concerning absence of guidance regarding advanced medication approaches for biphasic and multiphasic presentations. The approach to treating biphasic and multiphasic anaphylactic reactions is essentially the same as conventional anaphylaxis therapy, involving immediate administration of intramuscular epinephrine together with suitable supportive measures. Guidelines advocate a dose of 0.01 mg/kg body weight of epinephrine, not to exceed a total of 0.5 mg. For adults, this is often rounded up to 0.5 mg, since most adults weigh more than 50 kg. Notably, many auto-injectors—including those utilized during the initial two episodes of anaphylaxis in the case described—provide only 0.3 mg epinephrine per device, even for adult patients. While epinephrine auto-injectors are vital in the prehospital environment, they may also be advantageous in healthcare settings because they limit delays in therapy and help reduce reluctance among staff members who are infrequently exposed to vasoactive medications like epinephrine. Despite the prevalence of 0.3 mg auto-injectors, their use in adults exceeding 50 kg can result in a dangerously subtherapeutic dose. A 2025 retrospective analysis found that individuals first treated with 0.3 mg epinephrine required escalation of therapy, including further epinephrine administration, significantly more frequently than those initially administered 0.5 mg. Addressing obstacles, such as the traditional reliance on the 0.3 mg dosage and the widespread availability of this auto-injector strength, as well as enhancing adherence to recommended epinephrine dosing, has the potential to significantly optimize anaphylaxis management.

Corticosteroids were historically advocated for anaphylaxis, partly to mitigate the risk of biphasic reactions. Nonetheless, accumulating data indicate they are ineffective and may even be detrimental, prompting a recent decline in their use for anaphylaxis. In a 2017 systematic review of 22 studies, only one supported corticosteroid use, while 21 failed to demonstrate a reduction in biphasic anaphylaxis risk. In addition, newer studies highlight notable side effects; for example, a 2019 study identified an elevated chance of ICU or hospital admission following prehospital corticosteroid therapy. As a result, most current guidelines discourage routine corticosteroid use, though it still frequently occurs in clinical settings.

Biphasic anaphylaxis, while infrequent, remains of significant clinical concern. The lack of dependable predictors makes assessing risk and decisions regarding monitoring particularly challenging. Proposed predictors include late administration of epinephrine, a severe initial reaction, the requirement for multiple epinephrine doses, and a history of biphasic reactions. Nevertheless, validated predictors that could guide selective monitoring have yet to be established.

Systemic mastocytosis is characterized by abnormal mast cell proliferation, increased mediator release, and systemic inflammation, increasing susceptibility to severe anaphylaxis. 1517 However, both systemic mastocytosis and biphasic anaphylaxis are uncommon, and to date, no studies have specifically explored the impact of systemic mastocytosis on biphasic anaphylaxis. Additional investigation into mast cell disorders and their relationship with biphasic anaphylaxis could yield important information for future management strategies.

Although deaths due to anaphylaxis are uncommon, particularly where emergency medical services are readily available, the mortality associated with biphasic anaphylaxis is even less frequent, despite its perceived seriousness. Still, these encouraging figures do not diminish the fact that anaphylaxis remains a frequent emergency that necessitates immediate intervention and careful observation. Research and clinical guidelines largely center on acute management, yet ongoing assessment and follow-up care are also essential. Enhanced insight into intricate presentations, such as biphasic anaphylaxis, has the potential to facilitate more individualized, patient-focused, and resource-conscious approaches to anaphylaxis care.

The infrequent occurrence of biphasic and especially multiphasic anaphylaxis, combined with the unique obstacles to conducting studies in emergent clinical contexts, significantly complicates the formulation of evidence-based management protocols. In such scenarios, consensus recommendations formulated by experts hold significant merit; however, their development is constrained by limited data availability. Considering the low incidence of these atypical anaphylaxis patterns, creating a dedicated registry to capture key patient characteristics, proposed management options like those detailed in this case report, and patient outcomes could serve as a critical foundation for generating expert-guided, data-informed recommendations.
