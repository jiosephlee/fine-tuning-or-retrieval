Title: Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting

Margret Ann Schneider, Hans de Groot, Jan-Willem Geerts

Abstract:

Summary: Anaphylaxis represents the most acute manifestation of allergic responses, carrying a significant risk of fatality and demanding immediate, effective intervention. In a proportion of cases, symptoms can recur even after appropriate management and apparent resolution—these are termed biphasic or, in rare instances, multiphasic reactions. Such patterns pose distinct difficulties for healthcare providers, compounded by inconsistencies in reported frequencies, poorly defined risk factors, and the absence of robust, guideline-based management approaches. The clinical dilemma is particularly pronounced in instances of multiphasic anaphylaxis, characterized by repeated anaphylactic episodes despite an initial satisfactory response to epinephrine. Owing to the infrequent nature of multiphasic anaphylaxis, individual case reports serve as valuable resources for informing clinical practice in this area. This report details the case of a woman in her 60s who experienced multiphasic anaphylaxis after clarithromycin exposure; further investigation suggested a strong suspicion for systemic mastocytosis, though she did not fulfill established diagnostic criteria.

Background:

Anaphylaxis represents a serious allergic response that can become life-threatening, particularly in cases involving anaphylactic shock, and it frequently prompts emergency medical evaluation. While many cases follow a monophasic course, some display a biphasic or even multiphasic trajectory, where an individual experiences an initial anaphylactic episode after contact with a trigger, undergoes a period of complete symptom resolution, and subsequently develops a recurrence of symptoms without further allergen exposure. Typically, such symptom recurrence arises within 24 hours of the initial reaction, although there are accounts of relapses as late as 78 hours afterward. The reported rates of biphasic anaphylaxis are variable, with literature indicating an incidence from 0.4% up to 23.3%; most studies, however, estimate an incidence within the 4% to 6% range. Given the risk of subsequent reaction, it is standard practice to recommend an observation period following initial symptom resolution. Nonetheless, there remains some inconsistency in defining biphasic anaphylaxis, and clinical management guidelines differ—particularly in regard to corticosteroid use, whose efficacy has been questioned in recent investigations. Multiphasic anaphylaxis, characterized by several recurrences of symptoms, poses particular difficulties for both treatment strategies and decisions regarding patient monitoring. This paper describes the case of a woman in her sixties who experienced a multiphasic anaphylactic reaction after administration of clarithromycin.

Case presentation:

A woman in her sixth decade of life, with a documented beta-lactam antibiotic allergy, arrived at the emergency department (ED) experiencing anaphylaxis after ingesting clarithromycin 500 mg orally.

Her past medical history was significant for type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, psoriasis, and depression. She weighed 104 kg. Her current medications included metformin (500 mg twice daily), gliclazide retard (80 mg once daily), hydrochlorothiazide (25 mg once daily), omeprazole (20 mg once daily), and sertraline (100 mg once daily). The last hydrochlorothiazide dose had been administered that morning, more than 12 hours before her arrival in the ED.

As an adolescent, she had experienced a mild allergic response to amoxicillin, manifesting as facial swelling but without oropharyngeal involvement, rash, hypotension, or respiratory distress. Additionally, she had previously exhibited a pronounced localized cutaneous reaction to a wasp sting, though this did not progress to systemic symptoms. Notably, this ED visit marked her first documented episode of anaphylaxis.

Five days prior to her ED visit, her general practitioner initiated clindamycin therapy for erysipelas. After five days of clindamycin, she developed a rash on her abdomen (no further details provided), which was interpreted as a delayed hypersensitivity reaction to clindamycin. Consequently, her general practitioner discontinued clindamycin and began clarithromycin treatment, an agent she had not been exposed to previously.

She later arrived at the emergency department (ED) reporting symptoms of pruritus, flushing, excessive sweating, restlessness, and a sensation of throat swelling. These manifestations commenced approximately one hour after she ingested her first dose of clarithromycin and became progressively more severe. She presented for medical evaluation one hour following the onset of these symptoms. Upon admission to the ED, her vital signs were notable for hypotension and hypoxaemia (non-invasive blood pressure: 74/50 mm Hg, peripheral oxygen saturation 88% on room air, respiratory rate 22 breaths per minute). Initial physical examination revealed facial edema, newly developed generalized erythema and widespread urticaria, accompanied by increased sweating and agitation. There was no evidence of lingual or pharyngeal swelling, and both cardiac and pulmonary auscultation were unremarkable. Her blood glucose level measured 12.7 mmol/L, while the ECG displayed sinus rhythm. Management for a presumed severe hypersensitivity reaction was initiated, consisting of intramuscular administration of 0.3 mg epinephrine via auto-injector and a slow intravenous dose of 2 mg clemastine, targeting the dermatologic findings in accordance with local protocols. She also received supplemental oxygen (15 L delivered through a non-rebreather mask) and 500 mL of 0.9% sodium chloride solution during the initial resuscitation. After demonstrating a favorable clinical response to epinephrine, supplemental oxygen could be discontinued, though it was briefly required again during subsequent anaphylactic recurrences. Clarithromycin therapy was halted, with no further doses given beyond the single initial oral dose of 500 mg.

Initially, the patient demonstrated a favorable response to the therapy: blood pressure normalized, and both facial swelling and erythema subsided. She was admitted to the acute internal medicine ward, an environment offering enhanced surveillance by specialized nursing personnel, albeit without telemetry monitoring. Nonetheless, this positive trend was short-lived. Within the first 12 hours of hospitalization, the patient experienced repeated manifestations consistent with anaphylactic shock. Following the initial recurrence, she was transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) to enable closer observation. Each anaphylactic episode was managed with intramuscular epinephrine (see figure 1), totaling four episodes including the initial one that presented in the emergency department (ED). The initial two episodes were treated with epinephrine auto-injectors delivering 0.3 mg intramuscularly, while the subsequent two utilized manually prepared 0.5 mg intramuscular doses. While the 0.3 mg dose from auto-injectors is lower than the advised 0.5 mg, their use is prioritized in our clinic, particularly in acute or unfamiliar contexts—such as the initial ED stabilization—where the immediacy and user-friendliness of auto-injectors can circumvent hesitation or treatment delays associated with unfamiliar medication preparation.

Upon the first recurrence of anaphylaxis, corticosteroids were introduced, reflecting their traditional role in attempting to mitigate biphasic anaphylactic reactions. Specifically, the patient received 30 mg of intravenous prednisone daily for five days and a single intravenous dose of 8 mg dexamethasone, corresponding to varying recommendations in the hospital's general versus intensive care ward protocols for anaphylaxis. Additionally, intravenous clemastine (2 mg, administered twice) was used to address persistent cutaneous manifestations, and a supplementary 500 mL bolus of normal saline was provided during the second episode. Notably, the severity of clinical signs diminished with each successive event, progressing from profound hypoxaemia accompanied by cyanosis, hypotension, and significant respiratory distress at first, to comparatively milder symptoms—namely dyspnoea, recurrence of rash, and pruritus—by the final episode.

An early consultation with the department of allergology was initiated for expert guidance. Two possible management strategies were considered in this context: first, the continuous infusion of intravenous epinephrine as a proactive measure against further anaphylactic events, and second, an observational approach given the apparent spontaneous reduction in symptom intensity. Both strategies entailed considerable risks, compounded by a notable absence of literature to guide therapeutic decision-making in patients experiencing multiphasic anaphylactic reactions.

Outcome and follow-up:

In the end, neither of the previously considered treatment strategies was required, as the patient experienced no subsequent recurrence of symptoms. After remaining free of further anaphylactic events for approximately 24 hours, she was relocated to a general medical ward. She was discharged home following a 3-day hospitalization. On discharge, she was prescribed levocetirizine, an antihistamine, as a permanent medication, and received both training in and prescription for an epinephrine auto-injector (0.3 mg). Since these interventions, there have been no additional anaphylactic episodes.

Given the unusual and persistent nature of her clinical presentation, further investigations were undertaken to explore potential underlying etiologies. The findings indicated a high likelihood of systemic mastocytosis; however, the diagnostic criteria for a definitive diagnosis were not fulfilled (only two of the WHO minor criteria were present, while assessment of the major criteria was not possible due to the insufficient sample from bone marrow biopsy; see figure 2). Regrettably, tryptase concentrations were not measured during the initial evaluation of the patient’s first episodes of anaphylaxis, thus deviating from the recommended timing for tryptase assessment. Current guidelines suggest collecting at least two, and preferably three, tryptase samples at defined time points: the first sample should be drawn as soon as possible—without delaying acute management—followed by a second sample obtained 1–2 hours after the onset of symptoms (and no later than 4 hours), corresponding to the expected peak in tryptase, and a third sample collected no less than 24 hours following the complete resolution of clinical symptoms to determine the baseline value. In the case described, the first tryptase measurement was obtained 1 hour after the third episode of anaphylaxis, and the second measurement was taken 27 hours after the last documented occurrence.

No confirmatory allergy testing was conducted, given the risk of provoking another severe anaphylactic reaction—such as with skin testing—was judged to outweigh the potential benefit, especially since avoidance of further exposure was relatively straightforward. Additional notable clinical findings consistent with systemic mastocytosis included hepatomegaly observed on abdominal ultrasound, as well as possible neuropsychiatric manifestations such as depressive symptoms and memory impairment.

Discussion:

Anaphylaxis is generally recognised as a distinct clinical syndrome, marked by the abrupt appearance and swift worsening of symptoms that may involve the airway, respiratory and circulatory systems, and/or the gastrointestinal tract. These manifestations are frequently, but not invariably, accompanied by skin or mucosal changes such as urticaria, angioedema, or erythema. While pinpointing a specific allergenic trigger can reinforce the diagnosis, it is often not feasible to isolate an exact causative agent.

Presentations with greater complexity, such as biphasic or refractory anaphylaxis, have less universally accepted definitions, though ongoing research has sought to formalise these terms. The Resuscitation Council UK delineates biphasic anaphylaxis as the return of symptoms several hours following apparent resolution, without renewed exposure to the allergen. It is often difficult to distinguish true biphasic reactions from ongoing anaphylactic events with temporary improvement after epinephrine or continued allergen absorption, as might occur with retained food in the gastrointestinal tract or extended-release pharmaceuticals. Conversely, refractory anaphylaxis describes persistent clinical symptoms that do not resolve despite the correct administration of epinephrine.

Most guideline frameworks that address biphasic anaphylaxis focus on recommendations for clinical monitoring. However, consensus regarding the ideal length of observation is lacking. Conventional practices suggest monitoring for 4 to 6 hours post-symptom abatement. Both the Dutch Nederlands Huisartsen Genootschap (NHG) and the World Allergy Organisation highlight that around 50% of biphasic reactions emerge within 6–12 hours after initial symptom onset but refrain from specifying exact monitoring durations. In comparison, the UK guideline (2021) advocates a risk-stratified, three-level approach to post-anaphylaxis observation. Clearly, standardised monitoring protocols are required to ensure an appropriate equilibrium between conserving healthcare resources, especially for the majority experiencing only a single episode, and upholding safety measures for the minority at risk of complex or biphasic reactions.

There is a notable and concerning absence of clear recommendations for advanced pharmacologic interventions specifically tailored to biphasic and multiphasic anaphylactic courses. Therapeutic approaches for these presentations predominantly replicate those established for standard anaphylaxis, principally involving rapid intramuscular administration of epinephrine accompanied by suitable supportive measures. Current clinical guidelines advise a dosage of 0.01 mg/kg of body weight for epinephrine, capping the dose at a maximum of 0.5 mg. For adult patients—most of whom exceed 50 kg—this often translates to a simplified administration of 0.5 mg. Notably, a significant proportion of auto-injectors on the market, including those employed during the initial two anaphylactic episodes described in our case, are manufactured with only 0.3 mg of epinephrine per dose, despite their designation for adult use. While epinephrine auto-injectors serve a crucial function in out-of-hospital contexts, their deployment can also expedite care and mitigate hesitation among medical personnel who infrequently use vasoactive medications like epinephrine. Nevertheless, the routine use of 0.3 mg auto-injectors in adults surpassing 50 kg poses the risk of meaningful underdosing. Evidence from a retrospective analysis conducted in 2025 indicates that individuals initially treated with a 0.3 mg epinephrine dose required further medical intervention, such as subsequent epinephrine administrations, at a significantly greater rate compared to those receiving an initial 0.5 mg dose. Addressing persistent obstacles—including entrenched clinician reliance on the historically prevalent 0.3 mg dosage and the widespread availability of such auto-injectors—and fostering adherence to recommended epinephrine dosing strategies may substantially enhance outcomes in the management of anaphylaxis.

Historically, corticosteroids were advocated partly for their presumed role in preventing biphasic reactions. Nevertheless, accumulating research indicates not only a lack of benefit but also possible adverse outcomes, prompting a recent movement away from prescribing steroids in anaphylaxis cases. A systematic review conducted in 2017 that encompassed 22 studies found that only a single study supported corticosteroid use, while the remaining 21 failed to demonstrate any reduction in biphasic anaphylaxis risk attributed to these agents. Additionally, newer investigations have highlighted concerns about side effects, with a 2019 study even reporting higher rates of ICU or hospital admissions following corticosteroid use in prehospital settings. As a result, the majority of updated clinical guidelines now discourage routine administration of corticosteroids, although such practice still occurs in some settings.

Although biphasic anaphylaxis is uncommon, its clinical implications are significant. The lack of dependable prognostic indicators makes it challenging to stratify risk and decide on observation strategies. Some proposed factors include delayed epinephrine therapy, initial severity, need for multiple epinephrine doses, and a personal history of biphasic episodes. Nonetheless, effective and reliable predictive tools for individualized monitoring remain unavailable.

Systemic mastocytosis, characterized by abnormal mast cell proliferation, pronounced mediator release, and systemic inflammation, increases vulnerability to severe anaphylactic reactions.\textsuperscript{15,17} Nonetheless, the simultaneous rarity of both systemic mastocytosis and biphasic anaphylaxis means that, to date, there are no studies explicitly evaluating their intersection. Further studies focusing on mast cell disorders and biphasic anaphylaxis have the potential to enhance future clinical management.

Anaphylaxis-related deaths are uncommon, particularly in areas where emergency medical services are readily available. Moreover, although biphasic anaphylaxis poses significant clinical concerns, fatalities associated with it are exceedingly infrequent. Despite these reassuring data, anaphylaxis continues to represent a frequent and critical medical emergency that necessitates rapid intervention and appropriate follow-up. While current research and practice guidelines primarily emphasize the importance of immediate emergency intervention, the subsequent phases of patient management and observation are also crucial components. Enhanced comprehension of complex manifestations of anaphylaxis, such as biphasic forms, holds the potential to foster more individualized, efficient, and effective approaches to management.

Due to the infrequent occurrence of biphasic and especially multiphasic anaphylaxis, coupled with inherent difficulties in conducting research within acute care settings, establishing genuinely evidence-based clinical protocols is particularly challenging. In such contexts, consensus guidelines formulated by subject matter experts become highly valuable, offering structured management pathways; yet the formulation of these guidelines is itself impeded by the scarcity of robust data. Given the rarity of these more complicated presentations, establishing a comprehensive case registry—incorporating key patient details, potential management strategies such as those described in our case report, and associated outcomes—could serve as a foundational resource for the creation of data-informed, expert-driven management recommendations.
