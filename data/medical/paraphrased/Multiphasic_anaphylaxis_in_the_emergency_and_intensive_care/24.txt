Title: Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting

Margret Ann Schneider, Hans de Groot, Jan-Willem Geerts

Abstract:

Summary: Anaphylaxis represents the most extreme manifestation of allergic reactions and constitutes a medical emergency requiring immediate, effective intervention. In some instances, patients experience a return of symptoms after initial treatment and resolution, known as biphasic or multiphasic reactions, which complicate clinical management. These forms pose distinct challenges to healthcare providers, as their reported prevalence is inconsistent, associated risk factors remain elusive, and standardized treatment protocols are not well defined in current guidelines. This complexity is particularly notable in multiphasic anaphylaxis, characterized by repeated episodes even after adequate response to epinephrine. Owing to its infrequent occurrence, clinical case reports serve as a valuable resource for guiding management of multiphasic anaphylaxis. Here, we discuss the case of a woman in her 60s who experienced multiphasic anaphylaxis triggered by clarithromycin; further evaluation suggested a high probability of systemic mastocytosis, although she did not fulfill the strict diagnostic criteria.

Background:

Anaphylaxis is a serious allergic reaction that, particularly in the context of anaphylactic shock, can be life-threatening and frequently leads to emergency department visits. A subset of patients demonstrates a biphasic or even multiphasic progression, wherein an initial anaphylactic episode triggered by an allergen is followed by a symptom-free period, succeeded by a recurrence of symptoms without any further exposure to the inciting agent. Typically, this pattern manifests within 24 hours of the primary exposure, although documented cases have described recurrences up to 78 hours afterward. The prevalence of biphasic anaphylaxis has been reported to range from 0.4% to 23.3%, but the majority of studies suggest a frequency of between 4% and 6%. Because of the potential for recurrent symptoms, observation following an initial anaphylactic event is often recommended, even if clinical manifestations have subsided completely. Nevertheless, there are inconsistencies in the definition of biphasic anaphylaxis and variability in management recommendations, with recent debates regarding the use of corticosteroids. Multiphasic anaphylaxis, involving several episodes of symptom recurrence, poses unique challenges for both treatment and monitoring. In this report, we describe the case of a female patient in her sixties who developed a multiphasic anaphylactic reaction secondary to clarithromycin.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 60s, who was known to be allergic to beta-lactam antibiotics, arrived at the emergency department (ED) experiencing anaphylaxis after taking oral clarithromycin 500 mg.

Her past medical history included type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, psoriasis, and depression. She weighed 104 kg. The medications she was actively taking included metformin (500 mg twice daily), gliclazide (80 mg extended-release once daily), hydrochlorothiazide (25 mg once daily), omeprazole (20 mg once daily), and sertraline (100 mg once daily). Her last hydrochlorothiazide dose had been administered more than 12 hours prior to her presentation at the ED, during the morning.

Previously, the patient had experienced a mild allergic episode to amoxicillin during adolescence, characterized by facial edema without involvement of the oropharynx, rash, hypotension, or shortness of breath. She had also developed a pronounced localized skin response to a wasp sting, though there were no systemic manifestations. The episode leading to her ED admission was her initial occurrence of anaphylaxis.

Five days prior to her ED visit, her general practitioner had prescribed clindamycin to treat erysipelas. After five days of clindamycin therapy, she developed an abdominal rash (details not further provided). This was considered a delayed allergic reaction to clindamycin, prompting her general practitioner to change her antibiotic treatment to clarithromycin, which she had never taken previously.

She later arrived at the emergency department reporting itching, facial flushing, excessive sweating, restlessness, and a sensation of throat swelling. These symptoms began approximately one hour after taking her first oral dose of clarithromycin and progressively intensified. She presented for medical evaluation one hour after symptoms first appeared. On arrival, her vital signs revealed low blood pressure and reduced oxygenation (non-invasive blood pressure measured at 74/50 mm Hg, peripheral oxygen saturation 88% on ambient air, respiratory rate 22 breaths per minute). Initial examination identified facial swelling, newly developed generalized erythema and urticaria extending over her entire body, as well as increased sweating and agitation. There was no evidence of tongue or throat swelling, and both cardiac and pulmonary auscultation were unremarkable. Capillary blood glucose measured 12.7 mmol/L and her electrocardiogram demonstrated sinus rhythm. Immediate management for a presumed severe allergic reaction was initiated, consisting of a 0.3 mg intramuscular epinephrine auto-injector and a slow intravenous infusion of 2 mg clemastine, in accordance with local protocols for skin reactions. She was also provided with supplemental oxygen at 15 L per minute via non-rebreather mask and received 500 mL intravenous normal saline (0.9% sodium chloride solution) during the initial phase of stabilisation. Supplemental oxygen could be discontinued following a favorable response to epinephrine, but required temporary reinstatement during subsequent anaphylactic episodes. Clarithromycin was withdrawn, with no further doses taken beyond the single 500 mg oral administration.

Initially, the patient exhibited a favorable response to the therapy, with normalization of blood pressure and a reduction in facial edema and erythema. She was then transferred to the acute internal medicine ward, which offers enhanced observation by specialized nursing staff but does not include telemetry monitoring. Nonetheless, within the first 12 hours after admission, the patient experienced multiple recurrences of anaphylactic shock symptoms, despite the earlier improvement. After the first recurrence, she was moved to the intensive care unit (ICU) for closer surveillance. Each episode was resolved with intramuscular epinephrine administration (see figure 1), totaling four anaphylactic episodes including the initial presentation in the emergency department. The first two doses of epinephrine were delivered using an auto-injector with 0.3 mg of epinephrine intramuscularly; the third and fourth doses were manually prepared and contained 0.5 mg intramuscularly. While the auto-injectors at our facility dispense a lower dose than the standard recommendation of 0.5 mg, they are preferred in urgent scenarios (such as initial stabilization in the ED) or in environments where emergent treatment procedures like those for anaphylaxis are uncommon and where hesitation due to unfamiliarity could postpone intervention. After the first repeat episode of anaphylaxis, corticosteroid therapy was initiated based on their established role in preventing biphasic anaphylactic reactions. The patient received intravenous prednisone 30 mg daily for five days, and a single intravenous dose of dexamethasone 8 mg, reflecting differing protocols between the general ward and ICU anaphylaxis guidelines at our institution. Additionally, intravenous clemastine at 2 mg was administered twice for skin manifestations, and a 500 mL bolus of normal saline was given during the second anaphylactic episode. Notably, the severity of anaphylactic symptoms decreased with each recurrence, progressing from profound hypoxemia accompanied by cyanosis, hypotension, and pronounced dyspnea initially, to milder presentations such as dyspnea and return of rash and itching during the final episode.

The allergology department was consulted promptly for expert input on therapeutic options. Two alternative management strategies were contemplated: one involved proactive intravenous epinephrine infusion via pump to prevent further anaphylactic episodes, while the other was an observational approach, considering the progressively milder symptomatology, to determine if the reactions might resolve independently. Both strategies carried significant risks due to the absence of evidence in the literature guiding treatment for multiphasic cases.

Outcome and follow-up:

In the end, neither therapeutic intervention was required, since the patient experienced no additional recurrence of symptoms following this episode. After remaining free of anaphylactic events for approximately 24 hours, she was moved to a general medical ward. Hospital discharge occurred after a total of three days. Upon discharge, levocetirizine was introduced as a routine antihistaminic therapy, and she was provided with a 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injector and instructed in its administration. She has not had any subsequent anaphylactic episodes.

Given the unusual and persistent nature of her clinical presentation, further investigations into potential underlying etiologies were undertaken. These investigations suggested a significant likelihood of systemic mastocytosis. However, a definitive diagnosis could not be established, as only two minor WHO criteria were met and the major diagnostic criterion could not be assessed due to insufficient bone marrow biopsy material (see figure 2). Regrettably, tryptase measurements were not obtained during the initial evaluation of the patient’s first anaphylactic episodes, resulting in deviation from the established timeline for tryptase sampling. Current guidelines advocate obtaining at least two, preferably three tryptase samples at designated intervals. The first sample should be collected as promptly as possible without delaying necessary treatment, with a subsequent sample drawn 1–2 hours after symptom onset (and within 4 hours), coinciding with the typical tryptase peak, and a third sample acquired at least 24 hours following full resolution of clinical symptoms to determine baseline levels. In this case, the initial tryptase sample was obtained one hour after the third episode of anaphylaxis, and the follow-up sample was collected 27 hours following the most recent recurrence.

An allergy confirmation test was not carried out, as the risk of inducing another severe anaphylactic event through procedures such as skin testing was considered disproportionate, particularly since avoidance of further exposure is relatively straightforward. Additional findings supportive of a diagnosis of systemic mastocytosis in this patient included hepatomegaly on abdominal ultrasound and potential neuropsychiatric manifestations such as depression and memory impairment.

Discussion:

Anaphylaxis is a clinically distinct condition, marked by the abrupt appearance and swift worsening of symptoms affecting the airway, respiratory and cardiovascular systems, and/or gastrointestinal tract. These features are usually seen alongside skin or mucosal signs such as urticaria, angioedema, or flushing. Identifying a probable allergen can facilitate diagnosis, but it is often not possible to pinpoint the exact cause.

Cases with more atypical presentations, like biphasic or refractory anaphylaxis, are described less precisely, although ongoing efforts seek to reach agreement on their definitions. According to the Resuscitation Council UK, biphasic anaphylaxis is defined as a return of symptoms several hours after apparent resolution, in the absence of renewed exposure to the allergen. Distinguishing between genuine biphasic reactions and prolonged anaphylaxis with brief improvement after epinephrine administration, or reactions due to continued allergen uptake (such as undigested food or sustained-release pharmaceuticals), can pose difficulties. Refractory anaphylaxis, on the other hand, is classified as ongoing anaphylaxis that fails to adequately respond to appropriately given epinephrine.

Guidelines related to biphasic anaphylaxis focus mainly on protocols for patient observation. However, there is still debate regarding the ideal length of monitoring. Conventional observation periods typically last 4 to 6 hours after symptom resolution. The Dutch Nederlands Huisartsen Genootschap (NHG) guideline and the World Allergy Organisation both observe that about 50% of biphasic reactions develop within 6–12 hours of the initial incident, but neither sets a firm recommendation for monitoring duration. Conversely, the UK guideline 2021 offers a risk-based, three-tiered approach for observation. Clearly, there remains a need for standardized monitoring recommendations that both optimize use of healthcare resources—given the large number of patients experiencing a single episode of anaphylaxis—and address patient safety by reducing risk for the smaller group at risk for biphasic reactions.

There is a significant and concerning absence of specific recommendations regarding advanced pharmacological interventions in biphasic and multiphasic anaphylaxis. The therapeutic approach for these types of anaphylactic responses remains largely consistent with conventional anaphylaxis protocols, with immediate intramuscular administration of epinephrine and suitable supportive measures being the mainstay. According to current guidelines, epinephrine should be dosed at 0.01 mg/kg, not exceeding a maximum of 0.5 mg per dose. For adults—most of whom have a body weight greater than 50 kg—this is generally rounded up to a standard 0.5 mg dose. Yet, it is important to recognize that a substantial proportion of auto-injectors, including those utilized during the initial two anaphylactic events in the patient described here, are formulated to deliver only 0.3 mg of epinephrine, even when marketed for adults. While epinephrine auto-injectors are vital for immediate, out-of-hospital intervention and can also be advantageous in clinical environments by minimizing delays in treatment and reducing uncertainty among providers less familiar with vasoactive drugs, employing a 0.3 mg dose in adults weighing more than 50 kg can result in a significant risk of underdosing. Findings from a retrospective study in 2025 indicated that individuals given an initial dose of 0.3 mg epinephrine required intensified medical interventions, such as repeated epinephrine administration, at a notably higher rate compared to those who received 0.5 mg as the initial dose. Enhancing compliance with established epinephrine dosing recommendations and overcoming obstacles such as habitual reliance on the 0.3 mg dose and widespread use of 0.3 mg auto-injectors could lead to substantial improvements in the care of patients with anaphylaxis.

Historically, corticosteroids were advocated in part to reduce the risk of biphasic reactions. Nevertheless, accumulating research highlights the lack of efficacy and even possible negative outcomes associated with their use, prompting a shift in anaphylaxis treatment practices away from steroids. A systematic review from 2017 evaluated 22 studies and found that only one demonstrated a benefit from corticosteroid administration, while the remaining 21 provided no evidence that corticosteroids decrease the occurrence of biphasic anaphylaxis. Additionally, more recent investigations have brought attention to potential adverse effects, with a 2019 study reporting an increased rate of ICU or hospital admission among patients who received prehospital corticosteroids. As a result, the majority of current guidelines discourage the routine use of corticosteroids in anaphylaxis, despite their ongoing application in some clinical settings.

Biphasic anaphylaxis occurs infrequently, yet holds significant clinical importance. The lack of dependable predictors complicates both stratification of risk and decisions regarding observation periods. Proposed predictors encompass delayed administration of epinephrine, severe symptoms at onset, requirement for multiple epinephrine doses, and history of biphasic reactions. However, reliable indicators that would enable selective monitoring have yet to be identified.

Systemic mastocytosis, characterized by abnormal mast cell proliferation, heightened mediator release, and inflammation affecting multiple organs, increases vulnerability to severe anaphylaxis.1517 Nevertheless, considering the rarity of these diseases, to date, there is no published research specifically exploring the impact of systemic mastocytosis on biphasic anaphylaxis. Additional investigation into the relationship between mast cell disorders and biphasic anaphylaxis could yield important knowledge for the advancement of patient management.

Anaphylaxis is associated with a low mortality rate, particularly in areas where emergency care can be accessed promptly. Even more infrequently do biphasic anaphylactic reactions result in fatality, despite their significant potential risk. Nonetheless, despite these encouraging mortality rates, anaphylaxis frequently presents as an acute emergency requiring immediate intervention and careful observation. While the majority of studies and guidelines concentrate on the initial emergency response, the subsequent phases of care and patient surveillance are equally crucial. A deeper insight into intricate manifestations of anaphylaxis, such as biphasic variants, may facilitate the development of more individualized, efficient, and resource-conscious strategies for managing anaphylactic reactions.

Developing robust evidence-based approaches is notably challenging due to both the infrequency of biphasic and particularly multiphasic anaphylaxis, and the inherent difficulties of conducting research in emergency contexts. In such scenarios, expert consensus guidelines that offer well-defined management recommendations become especially valuable, though these are also constrained by limited available data. Given the uncommon occurrence of these complicated anaphylactic events, establishing a registry of documented cases—including key patient characteristics, proposed management approaches (as illustrated in our case report), and patient outcomes—could serve as a critical foundation for formulating data-informed, expert-driven recommendations.
