Title: Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting

Margret Ann Schneider, Hans de Groot, Jan-Willem Geerts

Abstract:

Summary: Anaphylaxis represents the most acute and dangerous manifestation of allergic reactions, demanding rapid and decisive intervention due to its potentially fatal systemic effects. In some instances, however, symptoms recur after apparent resolution and adequate therapeutic response, a phenomenon termed biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis. These relapsing patterns introduce additional clinical complexities, particularly given inconsistencies in reported prevalence, unidentified risk factors, and the absence of definitive management protocols within current clinical guidelines. This challenge is heightened in cases of multiphasic anaphylaxis, where patients experience repeated episodes of anaphylaxis following initially effective treatment with epinephrine. Given the uncommon nature of multiphasic anaphylaxis, detailed case reports serve as valuable resources for sharing management strategies and clinical observations. In this context, we report the case of a woman in her sixties who experienced multiphasic anaphylaxis triggered by clarithromycin. Further investigation indicated a strong suspicion of systemic mastocytosis, despite her not satisfying formal diagnostic criteria for the disorder.

Background:

Anaphylaxis represents a critical and often, particularly in cases of anaphylactic shock, life-endangering hypersensitivity reaction frequently leading to emergency department visits. In certain patients, the clinical course deviates from the typical single episode; rather, a biphasic or even multiphasic pattern emerges. Here, after the initial trigger-induced anaphylactic event and an interval of symptom resolution—i.e., a symptom-free period—patients may experience renewed manifestations of anaphylaxis in the absence of any subsequent allergen exposure. Most commonly, these secondary responses arise within 24 hours of initial exposure, although the literature documents recurrences occurring as late as 78 hours post-trigger. The frequency of biphasic anaphylactic episodes is reported inconsistently, with figures ranging from 0.4% up to 23.3%, though most evidence converges around a 4% to 6% prevalence. Owing to the risk of symptom relapse, extended observation following apparent clinical improvement is generally recommended. Nevertheless, there is lack of consensus regarding the precise criteria for biphasic anaphylaxis and heterogeneity in management protocols; notably, the efficacy of corticosteroid therapy has become increasingly debated. Multiphasic anaphylaxis, featuring more than two symptom recurrences, introduces particular difficulties for both monitoring and treatment approaches. The present work details the case of a woman in her sixties who experienced a multiphasic anaphylactic response after receiving clarithromycin.

Case presentation:

A woman in her sixth decade, known to have a hypersensitivity to beta-lactam antibiotics, arrived at the emergency department (ED) experiencing anaphylaxis subsequent to ingestion of oral clarithromycin 500 mg.

Her past medical history was significant for type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, psoriasis, and depression. She weighed 104 kg. Her ongoing pharmacological therapy consisted of metformin 500 mg twice daily, gliclazide 80 mg retard once daily, hydrochlorothiazide 25 mg once daily, omeprazole 20 mg once daily, and sertraline 100 mg once daily. The most recent dose of hydrochlorothiazide was administered in the morning, more than 12 hours prior to presentation at the ED.

As an adolescent, the patient had experienced a mild allergic response to amoxicillin, characterized by isolated facial edema without accompanying oropharyngeal swelling, rash, hypotension, or respiratory compromise. She had also previously developed a pronounced local cutaneous reaction to a wasp sting, though systemic manifestations were absent. The current clinical episode represented her initial occurrence of anaphylaxis.

Five days before presenting to the ED, her primary care provider prescribed clindamycin for treatment of erysipelas. After five days on clindamycin, she manifested an abdominal rash (further details not specified), which was interpreted as a delayed hypersensitivity reaction to clindamycin. Consequently, her general practitioner altered her antibiotic regimen to clarithromycin, a medication to which she had not been previously exposed.

She later arrived at the emergency department (ED) experiencing pruritus, flushing, excessive sweating, restlessness, and a sensation of throat swelling. These manifestations began approximately one hour after ingesting her initial dose of clarithromycin and intensified progressively. She presented to the ED within one hour from symptom onset. Upon arrival, her vital signs indicated hypotension and hypoxemia (non-invasive blood pressure: 74/50 mm Hg, peripheral oxygen saturation 88% on room air, respiratory rate 22 breaths per minute). Physical examination identified facial edema, newly developed generalized erythema and urticaria extending over her body, increased sweating, and agitation. There was no evidence of tongue or throat swelling, and both cardiac and pulmonary auscultation were unremarkable. Blood glucose measured 12.7 mmol/L, while her electrocardiogram (ECG) revealed a sinus rhythm. Given the suspicion of a severe allergic reaction, she was immediately administered 0.3 mg intramuscular epinephrine via auto-injector and a slow intravenous infusion of 2 mg clemastine to address the cutaneous symptoms, in line with local protocols. She also received high-flow supplemental oxygen (15 L via a non-rebreather mask) and an infusion of 500 mL normal saline (sodium chloride 0.9% solution) as part of her initial management. After demonstrating an adequate clinical response to epinephrine, supplemental oxygen was discontinued, though it required temporary reinitiation during recurrent episodes of anaphylaxis. Clarithromycin was withdrawn, and no additional doses beyond the initial 500 mg oral administration were provided.

At first, the patient responded well to the administered therapy: her blood pressure normalized, and both facial edema and erythema subsided. She was subsequently placed in the acute internal ward, which offers enhanced observation from specialized nursing staff but lacks telemetry monitoring. Despite this promising initial reaction, within the initial 12 hours following admission, the patient began experiencing recurrent manifestations consistent with anaphylactic shock. After the first recurrence, she was transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) for heightened surveillance. Each episode of anaphylaxis was resolved with intramuscular epinephrine administration (figure 1); in total, the patient experienced four such episodes, including the one that precipitated her arrival at the emergency department (ED). The first two epinephrine injections were delivered using an auto-injector containing 0.3 mg epinephrine intramuscularly, while the subsequent two episodes were managed with manually prepared intramuscular doses of 0.5 mg epinephrine each. Although our clinic’s auto-injectors dispense less than the recommended 0.5 mg dose, they remain our preferred choice in situations demanding rapid intervention (such as the initial stabilization in the ED) or in clinical environments where the infrequent use of emergency medications might otherwise cause hesitation and treatment delay. After the first recurrence, corticosteroid therapy was initiated, in alignment with their established, though unproven, function in preventing biphasic anaphylactic responses. The patient received daily intravenous prednisone at a dose of 30 mg for five days, as well as a single dose of 8 mg intravenous dexamethasone; this dual regimen reflected differences between the anaphylaxis protocols of the general and intensive care wards in our institution. Additionally, two 2 mg doses of intravenous clemastine were administered to address persistent dermatologic symptoms, and an extra 500 mL of normal saline was given during the second anaphylactic episode. Notably, each successive episode displayed reduced severity—progressing from severe hypoxaemia with cyanosis, hypotension, and marked dyspnoea initially to milder symptoms of dyspnoea, recurrent urticaria, and pruritus in the final event.

Early in the course of management, the allergology department was involved to provide specialized treatment recommendations. Two main management strategies were deliberated. The first involved continuous intravenous infusion of epinephrine, thereby seeking to preempt subsequent anaphylactic reactions rather than treating symptoms reactively. The alternative strategy, supported by the apparent trend toward symptom diminution, favored expectant management to assess if resolution would occur without further intervention. Both approaches entailed significant risks, complicated by the paucity of evidence-based guidelines for handling multiphasic anaphylactic cases.

Outcome and follow-up:

In the end, neither therapeutic strategy needed to be pursued, as the patient did not experience additional recurrence of symptoms following this period. After roughly 24 hours without new anaphylactic episodes, she was transitioned back to a regular care unit. Her hospital stay concluded with discharge 3 days after admission. She began routine treatment with the antihistamine levocetirizine, and was provided with an epinephrine auto-injector (0.3 mg) alongside appropriate training for its use. There have been no subsequent occurrences of anaphylaxis.

Given the unusual and extended progression of the case, further investigations were carried out to identify any underlying etiologies. Findings indicated a significant likelihood of systemic mastocytosis; however, the case did not fulfill all criteria necessary for a definitive diagnosis—only two WHO minor criteria were met, and assessment of the major criteria was not possible due to insufficient bone marrow biopsy material (refer to figure 2). Measurements of tryptase during the third anaphylactic event, as well as baseline levels, were found to be elevated and persisted at higher values during continued follow-up with the allergologist, as presented in table 1.

Regrettably, tryptase concentrations were not assessed during the initial assessment of the patient's early anaphylactic events, resulting in deviation from the guideline-recommended timing for tryptase measurements. Current best practice suggests obtaining at least two, and preferably three, tryptase samples at distinct intervals: the first sample should be collected as early as possible, provided this does not postpone acute management, the second should be drawn 1–2 hours (but within 4 hours) following symptom onset, corresponding to the expected peak of tryptase, and a third baseline sample at least 24 hours after complete clinical recovery. In this particular case, the initial tryptase measurement was conducted 1 hour after the third episode of anaphylaxis, while the subsequent sample was obtained 27 hours post the final anaphylactic occurrence.

Additionally, confirmatory allergy testing was not undertaken, as the potential for provoking another severe anaphylactic reaction through procedures such as skin testing was judged unwarranted, especially given the feasibility of effectively avoiding future exposure. Other noteworthy clinical findings suggestive of systemic mastocytosis included evidence of hepatomegaly observed on abdominal sonography and the presence of potential neuropsychiatric manifestations, such as depressive symptoms and impaired memory.

Discussion:

Anaphylaxis is recognized as a clinically distinct syndrome, marked by the abrupt emergence and rapid progression of symptoms that may affect the airway, breathing, circulatory system, and/or gastrointestinal tract, and is often accompanied by skin or mucosal signs such as urticaria, angioedema, or flushing. Identifying a probable allergen can lend support to the diagnosis, though pinpointing a precise inciting agent is not always feasible.

Presentations like biphasic and refractory anaphylaxis are not as clearly delineated, despite recent attempts to develop consensus definitions. According to the Resuscitation Council UK, biphasic anaphylaxis is defined by the return of symptoms hours after their apparent resolution and without further allergen exposure. Distinguishing genuine biphasic episodes from cases where anaphylaxis is ongoing—yet appears to temporarily resolve with epinephrine or persists due to continued allergen absorption (for example, from retained food or extended-release medications)—remains difficult. Refractory anaphylaxis, in comparison, describes persistent symptoms that fail to improve following proper administration of epinephrine.

Guidelines regarding biphasic anaphylaxis predominantly emphasize the importance of post-reaction clinical monitoring. However, there is ongoing debate about the most suitable duration of observation. Historically, observation times of 4 to 6 hours post-resolution have been recommended. The Dutch Nederlands Huisartsen Genootschap (NHG) and the World Allergy Organisation guideline acknowledge that about half of biphasic responses occur within 6 to 12 hours of the initial event, yet neither offers a definitive recommendation for observation. By contrast, the UK guideline from 2021 outlines a three-level, risk-based monitoring strategy. Establishing unified monitoring protocols is critical to address the dual objectives of optimizing healthcare resource allocation for the majority who experience a single anaphylactic event and ensuring adequate safety for those at risk of uncommon but serious biphasic reactions.

There is a marked and concerning gap in guidance regarding advanced pharmacological interventions for biphasic and multiphasic presentations. The approach to these complex forms of anaphylaxis closely aligns with conventional management protocols, relying on rapid administration of intramuscular epinephrine in conjunction with suitable supportive measures. Clinical guidelines specify a dose of 0.01 mg/kg of epinephrine, up to a ceiling of 0.5 mg per administration. This protocol is often streamlined in adults—most of whom exceed 50 kg—to a uniform 0.5 mg dose. Notably, many commercially available auto-injectors, including those employed in the initial two anaphylactic events detailed in this report, supply only 0.3 mg of epinephrine, despite being marketed for adult use. While epinephrine auto-injectors remain indispensable in prehospital care and retain value in clinical environments—by facilitating timely administration and overcoming reluctance among staff less accustomed to vasoactive drugs—reliance on the 0.3 mg dose for adults above 50 kg may result in substantial underdosing. A retrospective study published in 2025 indicated that patients treated with a 0.3 mg initial epinephrine dose were significantly more likely to require escalation, including subsequent doses, compared to those who received 0.5 mg from the outset. Addressing persistent issues, such as provider familiarity with the traditionally endorsed 0.3 mg amount and the prevalent stock of 0.3 mg auto-injectors, alongside stricter adherence to current dosing recommendations, has the potential to substantially enhance outcomes in anaphylaxis management.

Historically, corticosteroids were advocated, partially with the intention of preventing biphasic reactions. Nevertheless, accumulating data indicate a lack of efficacy and even possible adverse outcomes, prompting a contemporary move away from corticosteroid use in anaphylaxis management. A 2017 systematic review analyzed 22 studies, of which only one reported a benefit from corticosteroids, whereas the remaining 21 found no evidence that corticosteroids lowered the incidence of biphasic anaphylaxis. Additionally, newer investigations highlight the significance of adverse effects, such as a 2019 study reporting a higher probability of ICU or hospital admission following prehospital corticosteroid administration. As a result, most current guidelines recommend against the routine use of corticosteroids, despite their continued use in clinical settings.

Although biphasic anaphylaxis occurs infrequently, it remains of considerable clinical concern. The lack of dependable predictive factors poses challenges for risk assessment and surveillance strategies. Potential indicators, such as delayed epinephrine treatment, pronounced initial symptoms, need for multiple epinephrine doses, and a history of biphasic reactions, have been proposed. However, predictive markers that would enable focused patient monitoring have yet to be established.

Systemic mastocytosis, a disorder marked by abnormal mast cell proliferation, elevated mediator release, and systemic inflammation, places individuals at increased risk for severe anaphylaxis.1517 Despite this association, the infrequency of both systemic mastocytosis and biphasic anaphylaxis means there is, to date, no dedicated research examining the connection between these conditions. Further investigation into mast cell pathologies and their relationship to biphasic anaphylaxis is warranted to inform future management strategies.

Encouragingly, deaths resulting from anaphylaxis are uncommon, particularly in areas where rapid emergency medical services are accessible. The occurrence of fatal biphasic anaphylaxis is even more exceptional, despite its perceived risk. Still, despite these comforting numbers, anaphylaxis continues to represent a frequent acute medical condition that necessitates immediate intervention and vigilant follow-up. Although most research and current protocols emphasize initial emergency response, the importance of continued observation and care in the aftermath is significant. Enhanced insight into complicated forms of anaphylaxis, like biphasic anaphylaxis, has the potential to enable more personalized, precise, and efficient management approaches for affected patients.

The infrequency of biphasic and especially multiphasic anaphylaxis, compounded by practical obstacles to studying emergency scenarios, presents a significant barrier to developing thoroughly evidence-based treatment pathways. In such cases, consensus guidelines built upon expert opinion with well-defined recommendations become crucial; nevertheless, the absence of comprehensive data continues to impede the formulation of such guidelines. Given the exceptional rarity of these intricate presentations, establishing a dedicated registry that documents relevant patient information, management recommendations as illustrated in this case report, and related outcome measures, could lay the groundwork for data-informed, expert-driven clinical recommendations.
