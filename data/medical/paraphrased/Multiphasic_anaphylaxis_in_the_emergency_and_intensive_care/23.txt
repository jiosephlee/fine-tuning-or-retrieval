Title: Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting

Margret Ann Schneider, Hans de Groot, Jan-Willem Geerts

Abstract:

Summary: Anaphylaxis represents the most intense manifestation of allergic reactions and poses a significant threat to life, necessitating immediate and thorough intervention. Certain instances exhibit a reappearance of symptoms after initial resolution and proper management, referred to as biphasic or, in rare instances, multiphasic reactions. These patterns complicate care for clinicians, given the wide variability in reported frequency, indeterminate risk factors, and the absence of well-established treatment protocols within current recommendations. This issue is especially pronounced in cases of multiphasic anaphylaxis, where patients repeatedly experience new episodes of anaphylaxis even after successful administration of epinephrine. Given that multiphasic anaphylaxis is uncommon, published case reports serve as valuable resources to inform clinical practice regarding these presentations. Herein, we detail the case of a female patient in her 60s who developed multiphasic anaphylaxis following exposure to clarithromycin; further evaluation indicated a strong probability of underlying systemic mastocytosis, although the patient did not fulfill all diagnostic requirements for the disorder.

Background:

Anaphylaxis constitutes a serious, and in the setting of anaphylactic shock, potentially fatal allergic response, frequently resulting in emergency department visits. Nevertheless, a portion of patients experience a biphasic or even multiphasic course, characterized initially by an anaphylactic episode triggered by an allergen, subsequent resolution of clinical manifestations, an asymptomatic period, and then resurgence of symptoms without further allergen exposure. Such recurrence generally occurs within the initial 24 hours after allergen contact, although cases have documented symptom reappearance as late as 78 hours afterward. The documented frequency of biphasic anaphylaxis varies considerably, with figures reported between 0.4% and 23.3%, but most literature indicates an incidence in the range of 4% to 6%. Owing to the risk of symptom relapse, observation after resolution of anaphylactic symptoms is often recommended. Despite this, definitions for biphasic anaphylaxis lack consistency across studies, and management guidelines differ, with the therapeutic utility of corticosteroids increasingly debated. Multiphasic anaphylaxis, which involves several recurrences of symptoms, poses unique difficulties for both treatment strategies and monitoring recommendations. Here, we describe the case of a woman in her 60s who developed multiphasic anaphylaxis after clarithromycin exposure.

Case presentation:

A woman in her sixties, known to have a hypersensitivity to beta-lactam antibiotics, arrived at the emergency department (ED) experiencing anaphylaxis after ingesting oral clarithromycin 500 mg.

Her past medical history was significant for type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, psoriasis, and depression. She weighed 104 kg. The medications she was taking included metformin (500 mg twice daily), gliclazide (80 mg sustained release, once daily), hydrochlorothiazide (25 mg once daily), omeprazole (20 mg once daily), and sertraline (100 mg once daily). Her last hydrochlorothiazide dose had been consumed in the morning, more than 12 hours prior to presenting to the ED.

During adolescence, she had experienced a mild hypersensitivity reaction to amoxicillin manifesting as swelling of the face, without rash, oropharyngeal swelling, hypotension, or dyspnoea. Additionally, she had previously developed a pronounced local skin response to a wasp sting, but had not had any associated systemic manifestations. The current clinical episode represented her initial occurrence of anaphylaxis.

Five days prior to presenting at the ED, she was prescribed clindamycin for erysipelas by her general practitioner. After five days of therapy, she developed an abdominal rash (details not specified). This was interpreted by her general practitioner as a delayed allergic reaction to clindamycin, prompting a change in antibiotic treatment to clarithromycin, which she had not previously received.

She later arrived at the emergency department (ED) experiencing itching, flushing, increased sweating, restlessness, and a sensation of throat swelling. These complaints began one hour following oral administration of her first dose of clarithromycin and progressively intensified. She presented for medical evaluation one hour after onset of symptoms. Upon arrival, her vital signs revealed low blood pressure and reduced oxygen saturation (non-invasive blood pressure measured at 74/50 mm Hg, peripheral oxygen saturation was 88% on room air, respiratory rate 22 breaths per minute). Initial physical examination identified facial edema, widespread new erythema and urticaria across the body, as well as increased perspiration and agitation. No edema of the tongue or oropharynx was noted, and both cardiac and pulmonary auscultation were unremarkable. Her blood glucose level measured 12.7 mmol/L, and her electrocardiogram revealed sinus rhythm. Management for presumed severe allergic reaction was initiated without delay, including intramuscular administration of 0.3 mg epinephrine using an auto-injector and a slow intravenous injection of 2 mg clemastine to address cutaneous manifestations, in accordance with local recommendations. She also received supplemental oxygen (15 L via non-rebreather mask) and an infusion of 500 mL normal saline (0.9% sodium chloride solution) during initial resuscitation. Oxygen supplementation was discontinued after favorable response to epinephrine but was temporarily resumed during subsequent anaphylactic events. Clarithromycin therapy was halted, and no further doses beyond the initial 500 mg oral administration were given.

Initially, the patient showed a favorable response to therapy; her blood pressure normalized, and the facial swelling and erythema diminished. She was then admitted to the acute internal medicine ward, where she received heightened surveillance from specialized nursing staff, but was not placed under telemetry. Despite this initial improvement, within the first 12 hours of hospitalization, she experienced repeated episodes suggestive of anaphylactic shock. After the first recurrence, she was transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) for closer monitoring. Each instance of anaphylaxis was managed with intramuscular epinephrine (see figure 1), amounting to four episodes in total, including the initial presentation in the emergency department. For the first two episodes, 0.3 mg doses of epinephrine were administered via auto-injector, while the subsequent two relapses were treated with manually prepared 0.5 mg epinephrine intramuscular injections. Although the auto-injectors in our facility deliver a dose lower than the standard 0.5 mg recommendation, they are preferred in situations requiring rapid intervention, such as initial stabilization in the emergency department, or when immediate care may be delayed due to unfamiliarity with medication preparation in settings where anaphylactic emergencies are uncommon. Following the first recurrence of anaphylaxis, corticosteroids were introduced to the regimen, recognizing their conventional use to reduce the risk of biphasic reactions. She received intravenous prednisone at 30 mg per day for 5 days, as well as a single 8 mg intravenous dose of dexamethasone, reflecting discrepancies in dosing guidance between the hospital’s general ward and ICU anaphylaxis protocols. Additionally, clemastine was given intravenously at 2 mg on two occasions to address cutaneous manifestations, and a further 500 mL of normal saline was infused during the second anaphylactic event. Notably, the severity of her symptoms progressively decreased with each anaphylactic episode, initially presenting with profound hypoxemia, cyanosis, hypotension, and marked dyspnea, but ultimately declining to dyspnea and recurrent urticaria with pruritus by the fourth episode.

Early consultation with the allergology department was sought for expert input. Two possible strategies were contemplated: preemptive intravenous epinephrine infusion via pump to avert further anaphylactic episodes, or adopting a watchful waiting approach given the gradual reduction in symptom severity, in hopes of spontaneous resolution. Both options were associated with significant risks, compounded by the absence of published guidance on managing multiphasic anaphylaxis.

Outcome and follow-up:

In the end, neither therapeutic option was required since the patient experienced no subsequent recurrence of symptoms. Approximately 24 hours after remaining free of new anaphylactic events, she was transferred from intensive care to a general medical unit. She was released from the hospital following a total admission of 3 days. At discharge, she was prescribed levocetirizine, an antihistamine, as part of her ongoing regimen and instructed on proper use of an epinephrine auto-injector (0.3 mg). Since discharge, she has not reported any additional anaphylactic reactions.

Given the unusual and extended nature of her presentation, further investigation into potential underlying etiologies was pursued. This workup indicated a high likelihood of systemic mastocytosis, but the formal diagnostic criteria were not fully met; only two minor criteria as per WHO were fulfilled, and major criteria could not be evaluated due to insufficient bone marrow sample, as depicted in figure 2. Regrettably, tryptase measurements were not obtained during the initial evaluation of the early anaphylactic events, resulting in deviation from the guideline-recommended timing for tryptase testing. Current recommendations suggest collecting at least two, preferably three, tryptase samples at defined time points: the first sample should be drawn as soon as possible after symptom onset without delaying urgent interventions; the second sample is advised 1–2 hours (and up to 4 hours) after symptoms begin, corresponding to the expected tryptase peak; and a third should be collected at least 24 hours following complete resolution of symptoms to establish a baseline level. In this reported case, the first sample was obtained 1 hour after the third anaphylactic episode, while the subsequent sample was collected 27 hours after the final recurrence.

No allergy verification tests were conducted, as the risk of provoking another severe anaphylactic episode—such as might occur with skin testing—was considered disproportionate, especially since ongoing exposure could be readily prevented. Additional pertinent findings supportive of systemic mastocytosis included hepatomegaly detected by abdominal ultrasound and possible neuropsychiatric manifestations, such as depressive symptoms and memory impairment.

Discussion:

Anaphylaxis is recognized as a distinct clinical entity, marked by an abrupt onset and swift escalation of symptoms that may affect the airway, respiratory, circulatory systems, and/or gastrointestinal tract, usually accompanied by skin or mucosal signs such as urticaria, angioedema, or flushing. Although identifying a suspected allergen can strengthen the diagnosis, pinpointing the exact cause is not always feasible.

More atypical cases—including biphasic and refractory anaphylaxis—have less established definitions, though recent initiatives have sought to standardize their characterization. The Resuscitation Council UK describes biphasic anaphylaxis as the reappearance of symptoms several hours after they have subsided, without further exposure to the allergen. Distinguishing genuine biphasic reactions from protracted anaphylaxis, which may show only temporary improvement with epinephrine or result from ongoing allergen absorption (such as remaining food or slow-release drugs), remains complex. Refractory anaphylaxis denotes persistent symptoms that fail to respond—or respond inadequately—to appropriately administered epinephrine.

Guidelines addressing biphasic anaphylaxis chiefly focus on clinical observation, yet consensus regarding ideal monitoring duration has not been established. Traditionally, patients are observed for 4 to 6 hours after resolution of symptoms. Both the Dutch Nederlands Huisartsen Genootschap (NHG) and World Allergy Organisation guidelines indicate that nearly half of biphasic events occur between 6–12 hours following the initial episode, though neither specifies an exact monitoring timeframe. Conversely, the UK guideline released in 2021 recommends a risk-based, three-tier monitoring strategy. Clearly, harmonized approaches to monitoring are required to optimize the allocation of healthcare resources in individuals with single episode anaphylaxis, while safeguarding the small subgroup at risk for biphasic reactions, which are often difficult to predict.

There is a conspicuous and concerning absence of specific recommendations for advanced medication management in cases of biphasic and multiphasic anaphylaxis. Therapeutic approaches for biphasic and multiphasic reactions generally follow conventional anaphylaxis protocols, which entail immediate intramuscular administration of epinephrine accompanied by suitable supportive measures. According to guidelines, the advised epinephrine dose is 0.01 mg/kg, with a maximum single administration of 0.5 mg. For adults, this is typically condensed to a flat 0.5 mg dose, since most adults exceed 50 kg in body weight. Notably, however, many auto-injectors—including those used to address the initial two anaphylactic episodes in the current case—deliver only 0.3 mg per dose, even when designated for adult usage. Auto-injectors containing epinephrine serve a pivotal function in out-of-hospital settings but can also provide substantial value in healthcare environments by limiting delays in intervention and overcoming hesitancy among providers unaccustomed to handling vasoactive agents like epinephrine. Nevertheless, administering a 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injector—though widespread—can result in significant underdosing for adults weighing over 50 kg. Findings from a 2025 retrospective analysis indicated that patients initially treated with 0.3 mg of epinephrine required escalated interventions, such as additional epinephrine administration, substantially more frequently than those who began with 0.5 mg doses. Reducing obstacles such as the entrenched practice of 0.3 mg dosing and the widespread use of 0.3 mg auto-injectors, and enhancing compliance with established dosing guidelines, may substantially enhance the management of anaphylaxis.

Historically, corticosteroids were prescribed in part to mitigate the risk of biphasic reactions. Nonetheless, accumulating research indicates their lack of efficacy and potential for harm, prompting a recent decline in their use for anaphylaxis. A systematic review conducted in 2017 analyzed 22 studies; only one demonstrated a positive effect from corticosteroids, while the remaining 21 provided no evidence that corticosteroids decrease the likelihood of biphasic anaphylaxis. In addition, newer studies highlight concerns regarding adverse effects, such as a 2019 study that found increased rates of ICU or hospital admissions following prehospital corticosteroid administration. As a result, most current guidelines recommend against routine use of corticosteroids in anaphylaxis, although their application continues in some settings.

Although biphasic anaphylaxis occurs infrequently, its clinical significance is considerable. The challenge lies in the lack of dependable risk markers, making risk assessment and monitoring difficult. Factors that have been proposed as predictors include delayed initiation of epinephrine therapy, severe first-phase symptoms, administration of multiple doses of epinephrine, and prior biphasic episodes. Despite these suggestions, robust and actionable predictors for targeted monitoring remain unavailable.

Systemic mastocytosis is characterized by pathological mast cell proliferation, increased mediator release, and involvement of multiple organ systems, rendering patients susceptible to severe anaphylactic episodes. 1517 Yet, given the uncommon nature of both systemic mastocytosis and biphasic anaphylaxis, there is currently no research specifically evaluating the contribution of systemic mastocytosis to biphasic anaphylaxis. Continued investigation into mast cell disorders and their relationship with biphasic anaphylaxis may help guide future clinical strategies.

Anaphylaxis-related mortality remains low, particularly in areas with rapid access to emergency healthcare. Death resulting from biphasic anaphylaxis is even less common, though such reactions are inherently serious. Nevertheless, despite these promising statistics, anaphylaxis continues to represent a frequent emergency that necessitates immediate intervention and vigilant observation. Although research and clinical guidelines typically emphasize initial emergency management, the significance of ongoing care and surveillance following the acute episode is equally paramount. Enhanced knowledge of complex manifestations such as biphasic anaphylaxis may enable more individualized, efficient, and resource-conscious approaches to managing this condition.

Developing robust evidence-based approaches for biphasic and especially multiphasic anaphylaxis is highly challenging, given their infrequency and the practical obstacles associated with conducting research during emergencies. In these contexts, expert consensus guidelines offering explicit recommendations serve as a critical resource, yet their formulation is impeded by limited available evidence. Considering the scarcity of these complex cases, establishing a registry that captures key patient data, proposed management strategies—as discussed in this report—and clinical outcomes, may form the foundation for evidence-informed expert recommendations.
