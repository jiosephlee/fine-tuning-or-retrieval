Title: Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting

Margret Ann Schneider, Hans de Groot, Jan-Willem Geerts

Abstract:

Summary: Anaphylaxis represents the most critical type of allergic response and poses a risk to life, necessitating immediate and effective intervention. In some patients, symptoms can reappear after initial improvement and proper treatment, resulting in biphasic or even multiphasic reactions that present specific difficulties for healthcare providers. The reported frequency of these courses varies, associated risk factors remain uncertain, and established management protocols are not well-defined in current recommendations. This is particularly problematic in multiphasic anaphylaxis, where patients suffer repeated episodes despite initially responding to standard therapies such as epinephrine. Given the infrequent nature of multiphasic anaphylaxis, individual case reports that outline clinical approaches are valuable for enhancing understanding of this issue. Here, we detail the case of a woman in her 60s who experienced multiphasic anaphylaxis after taking clarithromycin, with further evaluation indicating a high probability of systemic mastocytosis, although she did not fulfill formal diagnostic criteria.

Background:

Anaphylaxis is a serious allergic response that can be life-threatening, particularly in cases of anaphylactic shock, and frequently leads to emergency department visits. A proportion of anaphylactic episodes exhibit a biphasic or even multiphasic pattern, where initial symptoms appear following exposure to the allergen, resolve with a symptom-free interval, and then recur without further contact with the original trigger. Symptom recurrence most commonly develops within 24 hours of the initial exposure, although cases of relapse as late as 78 hours have been documented. The reported frequency of biphasic anaphylaxis is highly variable, with estimates ranging from 0.4% to 23.3%; however, most studies suggest an incidence between 4% and 6%. Because symptoms may return, patients are typically observed for a period following the resolution of acute anaphylaxis. Nonetheless, criteria for biphasic anaphylaxis can lack standardization, and management recommendations differ between guidelines, especially concerning the routine use of corticosteroids. Multiphasic anaphylaxis, marked by several distinct episodes of recurring symptoms, presents particular difficulties in both treatment and monitoring strategies. Here, we describe a case involving a woman in her sixth decade who experienced a multiphasic anaphylactic response following clarithromycin administration.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 60s, with a known allergy to beta-lactam antibiotics, presented to the emergency department (ED) with anaphylaxis following oral clarithromycin 500 mg.

The patient’s past medical history was notable for type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, psoriasis, and depression. She weighed 104 kg. Her current medications included metformin 500 mg twice daily, gliclazide retard 80 mg once daily, hydrochlorothiazide 25 mg daily, omeprazole 20 mg daily, and sertraline 100 mg daily. She last took hydrochlorothiazide in the morning, more than 12 hours prior to arrival in the emergency department.

In adolescence, she had experienced a mild allergic reaction to amoxicillin characterized by swelling of the face without involvement of the oropharynx, rash, hypotension, or dyspnea. She had also developed a pronounced localized cutaneous response to a wasp sting on a previous occasion, but no systemic manifestations were noted. The current admission represented her initial occurrence of anaphylaxis.

Five days preceding her visit to the emergency department, her general practitioner prescribed clindamycin for erysipelas. After five days of taking clindamycin, she developed a rash on her abdomen, with no additional details provided. This was considered to be a delayed hypersensitivity reaction to clindamycin, prompting her general practitioner to change the antibiotic to clarithromycin, which she had never received before.

She later arrived at the emergency department reporting itching, flushing, increased sweating, agitation, and a sensation of throat swelling. These complaints began approximately one hour after she ingested her first dose of clarithromycin, and symptoms continued to worsen. She presented for medical evaluation about one hour following the onset of her symptoms. Initial assessment in the emergency department revealed hypotension and low oxygen saturation (blood pressure by non-invasive measurement: 74/50 mm Hg, oxygen saturation on room air 88%, respiratory rate 22 breaths per minute). Physical examination noted facial edema, newly developed widespread erythema with urticaria affecting her whole body, heightened sweating, and agitation. There was no evidence of tongue or oropharyngeal swelling, and cardiac and pulmonary auscultation were unremarkable. Her blood glucose measured 12.7 mmol/L, and an ECG revealed sinus rhythm. She was promptly managed for presumed severe allergic reaction, receiving 0.3 mg epinephrine intramuscularly via auto-injector and 2 mg clemastine administered as a slow intravenous injection, addressing her cutaneous symptoms as per local protocols. Additionally, she was given supplemental oxygen at 15 L/min using a non-rebreather mask and 500 mL of 0.9% sodium chloride intravenously during initial resuscitation. Oxygen therapy could be discontinued after her symptoms responded well to epinephrine, though it was necessary to resume temporarily with recurrent anaphylactic episodes. Clarithromycin was withdrawn, and no doses were administered following the initial 500 mg oral dose.

At first, the therapy yielded a favorable effect, normalizing blood pressure and reducing both facial edema and erythema. The patient was then transferred to the acute internal medicine unit for enhanced observation by specialized nursing personnel, though without telemetry surveillance. Nevertheless, despite this initially positive reaction, the patient experienced repeated episodes consistent with anaphylactic shock within the initial 12 hours after admission. Following the first recurrence, the patient was moved to the intensive care unit (ICU) for closer monitoring. Resolution of each anaphylactic event required intramuscular epinephrine administration (see figure 1), with a total of four episodes, including the initial emergency department (ED) presentation. The initial two doses of epinephrine were administered via auto-injector at a dose of 0.3 mg intramuscularly, while the subsequent two were given as individually prepared 0.5 mg intramuscular doses. Although the epinephrine auto-injectors available at our facility deliver 0.3 mg, which is below the recommended 0.5 mg dose, their rapid availability makes them preferable for urgent situations such as during a new ED presentation or in settings where anaphylaxis is not frequently managed and unfamiliarity could delay therapy. After the first recurrence of anaphylaxis, corticosteroids were added, in keeping with their conventional use in preventing biphasic anaphylactic responses. The patient received 30 mg intravenous prednisone daily for 5 days, along with a single intravenous dose of 8 mg dexamethasone, reflecting variations in protocols between the hospital’s general ward and intensive care unit anaphylaxis guidelines. Additionally, the patient was treated for cutaneous manifestations with two doses of 2 mg intravenous clemastine and was given 500 mL of normal saline during the second anaphylactic episode. Notably, the severity of each subsequent episode diminished, with the presentation evolving from initial severe hypoxemia, cyanosis, hypotension, and marked dyspnea, to milder dyspnea and recurrent rash with pruritus during the final event.

Early in the hospital course, the allergology service was engaged for expert guidance regarding treatment. Two potential management strategies were evaluated. The first involved the pre-emptive, continuous intravenous infusion of epinephrine to avert further anaphylactic episodes. The second approach, given the apparent gradual reduction in symptom severity, entailed a wait-and-see strategy to determine if spontaneous resolution would occur. Each method carried significant risks, compounded by a lack of literature offering direction for managing multiphasic anaphylaxis.

Outcome and follow-up:

In the end, neither management strategy was required because the patient experienced no additional recurrence of symptoms beyond this time. After remaining free of new anaphylactic episodes for about 24 hours, she was moved back to a regular care unit. Her hospitalization concluded after a total of 3 days, at which point she was discharged. On discharge, she was prescribed levocetirizine, an antihistamine, for ongoing use, and she was both supplied with and educated in the administration of a 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injector. There have been no subsequent anaphylactic events reported.

Given the unusual and extended progression of her illness, additional evaluation for possible underlying etiologies was pursued. This workup indicated a strong suspicion for systemic mastocytosis, though diagnostic confirmation was limited by the fact that only two minor criteria from the WHO were met, and major criteria could not be assessed due to insufficient bone marrow biopsy material (refer to figure 2). Regrettably, tryptase measurements were not obtained during the initial assessment of the first episodes of anaphylaxis, resulting in deviation from the recommended timing for tryptase sampling. Current guidelines suggest collecting at least two, preferably three, tryptase specimens at designated time points: the first sample should be drawn as early as possible without postponing therapy, followed by a second sample 1–2 hours after symptom onset (and no later than 4 hours) when tryptase concentration typically peaks, and a third sample at least 24 hours following complete clinical recovery to determine baseline tryptase. In the described case, the initial tryptase assay was performed 1 hour after the patient’s third anaphylactic event, and the subsequent sample was collected 27 hours after the most recent recurrence.

Confirmation of allergy was not pursued, since procedures such as skin testing—carrying a risk of inducing severe anaphylaxis—were considered disproportionate, especially given how easily further exposure could be avoided. Additional relevant observations compatible with systemic mastocytosis were detected, including hepatomegaly on abdominal ultrasound and possible neuropsychiatric manifestations, such as depressive symptoms and impaired memory.

Discussion:

Anaphylaxis is generally recognized as a distinct clinical entity, marked by an abrupt onset and swift escalation of symptoms affecting the airway, respiration, circulation, and/or gastrointestinal system, usually accompanied by skin or mucosal signs such as urticaria, angioedema, or flushing. Although identifying a probable allergen reinforces the diagnosis, it is not always feasible to pinpoint a specific cause.

Atypical forms, including biphasic or refractory anaphylaxis, have less clear definitions, though recent initiatives have sought to standardize terminology in these cases. According to the Resuscitation Council UK, biphasic anaphylaxis is described as a return of symptoms occurring several hours after apparent clinical recovery, without additional allergen exposure. Distinguishing genuine biphasic events from ongoing anaphylaxis showing only a brief improvement after epinephrine, or from continued allergen uptake (such as persistent food or delayed-release medications), presents a diagnostic challenge. Refractory anaphylaxis, on the other hand, is characterized by ongoing anaphylactic symptoms despite correct administration of epinephrine.

Guidelines focusing on biphasic anaphylaxis emphasize the importance of clinical observation. Nonetheless, there remains uncertainty regarding the optimal length of monitoring. Conventionally, patients are observed for 4 to 6 hours after their symptoms resolve. The Dutch Nederlands Huisartsen Genootschap (NHG) and World Allergy Organisation guidelines state that around half of biphasic responses arise within 6–12 hours post-incident, but neither makes specific recommendations for monitoring duration. Conversely, the 2021 UK guideline introduces a three-level risk-based monitoring scheme. Achieving consensus on observation protocols is essential to balance healthcare resource allocation for the majority with isolated anaphylactic events against the safety of patients at risk for rare but severe biphasic episodes, who are difficult to identify in advance.

There is a notable and concerning absence of detailed guidance regarding advanced pharmacologic approaches in managing biphasic and multiphasic reactions. Treatment protocols for biphasic and multiphasic anaphylaxis generally parallel those used for acute anaphylaxis, emphasizing immediate intramuscular administration of epinephrine in conjunction with appropriate supportive measures. Clinical guidelines specify a recommended dose of 0.01 mg/kg, not to exceed a maximum of 0.5 mg in total. In clinical practice, this frequently results in administration of 0.5 mg for most adults, as the majority have body weight greater than 50 kg. Interestingly, many commercially available auto-injectors—including those employed during the initial two anaphylactic episodes in the case described—are formulated to deliver only 0.3 mg of epinephrine, even when labeled for adult use. Although epinephrine auto-injectors are crucial in out-of-hospital settings, they remain valuable within healthcare environments by streamlining timely therapy and diminishing hesitancy among providers infrequently exposed to vasoactive medications like epinephrine. Nevertheless, the prevalent reliance on 0.3 mg auto-injectors for adults over 50 kg may result in substantial underdosing. Evidence from a retrospective study in 2025 demonstrated that individuals given an initial 0.3 mg dose of epinephrine more frequently required escalated interventions—such as repeat epinephrine administration—when compared to those started on a 0.5 mg dose. Addressing factors such as provider familiarity with the conventionally used 0.3 mg dose and the widespread use of 0.3 mg auto-injectors, as well as promoting adherence to established dosing recommendations, could meaningfully enhance the management of anaphylaxis.

Historically, corticosteroids were advocated in anaphylaxis management partly to mitigate the risk of biphasic reactions. Nonetheless, accumulating data indicate a lack of efficacy and the possibility of adverse outcomes, prompting a recent move away from routine steroid use in this context. A systematic review in 2017 analyzed 22 studies; only one suggested corticosteroids were beneficial, whereas 21 found no reduction in biphasic anaphylaxis risk. Additionally, more recent investigations have underscored concerns about adverse effects, such as a 2019 study that reported higher rates of ICU or inpatient admissions following prehospital corticosteroid administration. As a result, the majority of up-to-date guidelines now advise against regular use of corticosteroids, although they are still used in practice.

Although uncommon, biphasic anaphylaxis poses substantial clinical significance. Risk stratification and appropriate monitoring remain challenging due to the lack of consistent predictive factors. Proposed risk indicators have included delayed administration of epinephrine, a severe initial episode, requirement for multiple epinephrine doses, and a history of biphasic responses, yet reliable predictive criteria suitable for guiding observation durations remain unestablished.

Systemic mastocytosis—a disorder characterized by abnormal mast cell proliferation, excessive mediator release, and multi-organ involvement—predisposes individuals to severe anaphylaxis.1517 However, given the scarcity of both systemic mastocytosis and biphasic anaphylaxis, there appears to be no existing research that specifically examines the impact of systemic mastocytosis on the risk of biphasic anaphylactic events. Further investigation into mast cell diseases and their relationship with biphasic anaphylaxis could yield important information for future therapeutic strategies.

Thankfully, deaths due to anaphylaxis are uncommon, particularly in areas where rapid emergency medical services are accessible. Additionally, fatalities resulting from biphasic anaphylaxis, though potentially severe, are even less frequently reported. Despite these encouraging figures, anaphylaxis continues to represent a frequent acute event necessitating immediate intervention and close observation. Although research and clinical guidelines predominantly emphasize initial emergency response, ongoing care and surveillance after the initial episode are equally crucial. Enhanced insight into atypical manifestations, including biphasic anaphylaxis, may contribute to more precise, patient-oriented, and resource-conscious approaches to managing anaphylaxis.

The infrequency of biphasic and especially multiphasic anaphylactic reactions, alongside the inherent difficulties of conducting research during acute emergencies, renders the establishment of evidence-based protocols particularly difficult. In such contexts, consensus guidelines developed by experts offering specific management directions serve as a valuable alternative; nevertheless, creating such expert-driven recommendations is also impeded by a limited amount of published data. Given the scarcity of these complex cases, developing a case registry—which incorporates pertinent patient details, potential management approaches as illustrated in this report, and patient outcomes—could provide a crucial foundation for informed, expert-generated recommendations.
