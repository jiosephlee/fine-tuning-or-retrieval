Title: Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting

Margret Ann Schneider, Hans de Groot, Jan-Willem Geerts

Abstract:

Summary: Anaphylaxis represents the most acute and dangerous type of allergic response, characterized by rapid onset and requiring immediate intervention. In certain patients, symptoms recur following initial resolution, even after appropriate treatment and clinical improvement. These biphasic or multiphasic manifestations pose a distinct challenge for practitioners, as their prevalence is inconsistent, associated risk factors remain uncertain, and clear treatment protocols are often absent from established guidelines. This issue is particularly prominent in cases of multiphasic anaphylaxis, in which individuals experience repeated episodes of anaphylaxis after successful epinephrine therapy. Given the rarity of multiphasic anaphylaxis, published case reports can contribute valuable guidance regarding clinical approach. Here, we describe a case involving a woman in her sixties who experienced multiphasic anaphylactic reactions to clarithromycin, and upon evaluation, was found to have features suggestive of systemic mastocytosis, despite not fulfilling all diagnostic criteria for the condition.

Background:

Anaphylaxis represents a serious allergic response that can be fatal, particularly when it progresses to anaphylactic shock, and frequently leads individuals to seek emergency care. Nevertheless, a portion of patients experience a biphasic or even multiphasic trajectory, marked by an initial reaction to the allergen, subsequent resolution of manifestations—yielding a symptom-free interval—then the reappearance of symptoms absent further allergen exposure. Such episodes typically develop within 24 hours following the initial trigger, though there are accounts of symptom relapse up to 78 hours post-exposure. The reported frequency of biphasic anaphylaxis is highly variable, from 0.4% to 23.3%, though the majority of the literature supports a prevalence between 4% and 6%. In light of the risk of recurrence, it is generally recommended to observe patients for a period after apparent resolution of symptoms. The characterization of biphasic anaphylaxis is not always consistent in the literature, and clinical practice guidelines diverge, notably regarding the application of corticosteroids, whose efficacy has been subject to recent scrutiny. Multiphasic anaphylaxis, featuring repeated episodes of symptom re-emergence, poses particular difficulties for both therapeutic strategies and monitoring. Here, we report the case of a woman in her 60s who experienced a multiphasic anaphylactic reaction following administration of clarithromycin.

Case presentation:

A woman in her sixties, with a documented beta-lactam antibiotic allergy, arrived at the emergency department (ED) experiencing anaphylaxis after ingesting clarithromycin 500 mg orally.

Her past medical history included type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, psoriasis, and depression. She weighed 104 kg. Her current medications consisted of metformin (500 mg twice daily), gliclazide (80 mg retard once daily), hydrochlorothiazide (25 mg once daily), omeprazole (20 mg once daily), and sertraline (100 mg once daily). She had taken her most recent dose of hydrochlorothiazide that morning, more than 12 hours prior to coming to the ED.

Previously, she had experienced a mild allergic reaction to amoxicillin during adolescence, limited to swelling of the face and absent any oropharyngeal involvement, rash, hypotension, or dyspnea. She also had a history of pronounced local skin response to a wasp sting, with no accompanying systemic manifestations. The current episode was her initial instance of anaphylaxis.

Five days prior to her ED visit, her general practitioner prescribed clindamycin for erysipelas. After five days of clindamycin treatment, she developed an unspecified abdominal rash. This was regarded as a delayed allergic response to clindamycin, leading her general practitioner to change the antibiotic to clarithromycin, to which she had not previously been exposed.

She later arrived at the emergency department reporting itching, facial flushing, excessive sweating, restlessness, and a sensation of throat swelling. These symptoms began approximately one hour after ingesting her first oral dose of clarithromycin and gradually intensified. She presented for medical evaluation within an hour of symptom onset. On initial examination in the emergency department, her vital signs revealed low blood pressure (non-invasive measurement: 74/50 mm Hg) and reduced oxygen saturation (peripheral oxygen saturation 88% on room air), along with a respiratory rate of 22 breaths per minute. Physical assessment demonstrated facial edema, recent widespread erythema, urticaria covering her body, increased sweating, and agitation. There was no evidence of tongue or pharyngeal swelling, and auscultation of the heart and lungs was unremarkable. Her blood glucose measured 12.7 mmol/L, while ECG findings indicated sinus rhythm. She was promptly managed for suspected severe allergic reaction, receiving an intramuscular 0.3 mg dose of epinephrine by auto-injector and a slow intravenous administration of 2 mg clemastine to address the dermatologic manifestations, as per local protocol. Supportive therapy included administration of 15 L of supplemental oxygen by non-rebreather mask and 500 mL of intravenous normal saline (0.9% sodium chloride) for initial stabilization. Oxygen supplementation was discontinued following significant clinical improvement after epinephrine administration but required temporary reinitiation during subsequent anaphylactic events. Clarithromycin therapy was stopped, with no additional doses administered beyond the initial 500 mg oral dose.

An initial favorable response to treatment was noted, with normalization of blood pressure and reduction in facial swelling and erythema. The patient was transferred to the acute internal medicine unit, where enhanced observation by specialized nursing personnel was available, although continuous cardiac telemetry was not employed. Despite the initial improvement, the patient experienced repeated episodes of symptoms consistent with anaphylactic shock during the first 12 hours post-admission. Following the first recurrence of anaphylactic shock, she was moved to the intensive care unit (ICU) for advanced monitoring. Each recurrence was managed with intramuscular epinephrine administration (see figure 1), amounting to four total anaphylactic episodes, including the original emergency department (ED) event. The initial two epinephrine injections consisted of 0.3 mg delivered intramuscularly via auto-injector, while the subsequent two doses were 0.5 mg intramuscularly, individually prepared. Though the auto-injectors available at our center contain less than the 0.5 mg recommended dosage, their use is preferred during emergent situations—such as initial ED stabilization or in non-specialist settings—where prompt administration is critical and delay due to inexperience with medication preparation could be detrimental. After the first anaphylaxis recurrence, corticosteroids were added to her regimen, reflecting their traditional use for prevention of biphasic reactions. She was administered 30 mg of intravenous prednisone daily for five days and a single 8 mg intravenous dose of dexamethasone; this decision was guided by differing hospital protocols between general and intensive care wards. In addition, she was given two doses of 2 mg clemastine intravenously for persistent dermatologic manifestations and received 500 mL normal saline intravenously during her second episode. Notably, each consecutive anaphylactic event presented with milder clinical severity, progressing from pronounced hypoxemia accompanied by cyanosis, hypotension, and significant dyspnea during the initial event, to milder symptoms such as dyspnea, rash reappearance, and pruritus at the last occurrence.

An early consult with the allergology service was obtained for expert therapeutic recommendations. At this juncture, two alternative management strategies were contemplated: proactive continuous intravenous infusion of epinephrine to avert further anaphylactic episodes, or, in view of the decreasing symptom severity, a watchful waiting strategy to assess if episodes would subside on their own. Both approaches were considered to involve considerable risk, compounded by an absence of robust literature to guide treatment in such multiphasic presentations.

Outcome and follow-up:

In the end, neither of the considered therapeutic strategies was required because the patient experienced no additional recurrence of symptoms thereafter. After a further 24-hour period without any new episodes of anaphylaxis, she was moved back to a general ward. Her hospital stay lasted 3 days in total, after which she was discharged. She was initiated on levocetirizine, an antihistamine, as her ongoing daily medication and provided with an epinephrine auto-injector (0.3 mg) along with instructions for proper usage. There have been no subsequent anaphylactic episodes.

Given the unusual and extended nature of her clinical presentation, additional investigations were undertaken to explore possible underlying etiologies. Findings from these assessments pointed toward a strong likelihood of systemic mastocytosis, although diagnostic confirmation was unattainable since only two WHO minor criteria were met; the major criterion could not be evaluated due to insufficient bone marrow biopsy specimen (see figure 2). Regrettably, tryptase measurements were not obtained during the initial assessment of the patient’s first episodes of anaphylaxis, resulting in noncompliance with the suggested sampling schedule for tryptase determination. Current guidelines advise obtaining at least two, preferably three, tryptase specimens at recommended time points: the first sample should be drawn as soon as possible without delaying acute management; the second is advised between 1 and 2 hours (but within 4 hours) after symptom onset, which corresponds to the expected peak in tryptase concentration; and a third sample is to be collected at least 24 hours following complete symptom resolution, to determine the baseline tryptase value. In this reported case, the initial tryptase sample was collected 1 hour after the patient’s third episode of anaphylaxis, while the subsequent sample was obtained 27 hours following the final anaphylactic event.

Allergy confirmation testing was not conducted, as the risk of provoking another severe anaphylactic event, such as through skin testing, was considered disproportionate, particularly given that future exposures could be readily avoided. Additional findings consistent with systemic mastocytosis included hepatomegaly identified on abdominal ultrasound and the possible presence of neuropsychiatric symptoms such as depression and difficulties with memory.

Discussion:

Anaphylaxis is a distinct clinical syndrome, marked by abrupt onset and quickly evolving symptoms that affect the airway, respiratory system, cardiovascular system, and/or gastrointestinal tract. It is often associated with cutaneous or mucosal signs, including urticaria, angioedema, or flushing. While pinpointing a probable allergen can aid in diagnosis, identification of a precise causative agent is not always feasible.

More complicated forms such as biphasic and refractory anaphylaxis have less clear definitions, although there have been recent attempts to standardize terminology. According to the Resuscitation Council UK, biphasic anaphylaxis involves the reemergence of symptoms several hours after initial improvement, occurring without additional allergen exposure. Distinguishing genuine biphasic reactions from cases where anaphylaxis persists but only temporarily abates with epinephrine, or is due to ongoing allergen presence (for example, from retained food or extended-release drugs), is often difficult. Refractory anaphylaxis, in comparison, denotes continuous anaphylactic symptoms with minimal or absent response to appropriately given epinephrine.

Current guidelines regarding biphasic anaphylaxis mainly focus on recommendations for patient observation after resolution of initial symptoms, though the ideal duration for monitoring is a subject of ongoing debate. Conventionally, patients are observed for 4 to 6 hours after symptom improvement. Both the Nederlands Huisartsen Genootschap (NHG) and World Allergy Organisation highlight that nearly 50% of biphasic reactions develop 6–12 hours post initial episode, yet neither issues firm monitoring guidance. Unlike these, the 2021 UK guideline introduces a risk-stratified, three-tier framework for observation. Developing uniform monitoring protocols remains critical to appropriately allocate healthcare resources to patients with isolated episodes, while ensuring those at higher risk for biphasic reactions receive sufficient oversight to maintain safety.

There is a concerning absence of guidance regarding advanced medication regimens for biphasic and multiphasic presentations. Treatment for these forms of anaphylaxis typically replicates conventional approaches, which prioritize timely administration of intramuscular epinephrine along with suitable supportive interventions. Current guidelines specify an epinephrine dose of 0.01 mg/kg, up to a ceiling of 0.5 mg total, and this is often approximated to 0.5 mg in adults since most exceed 50 kg. Nevertheless, it is noteworthy that many epinephrine auto-injectors—including those utilized in the initial anaphylactic episodes described in this report—provide only 0.3 mg, even when designated for adult use. While auto-injectors are critical prior to hospital care and continue to have utility in healthcare environments by reducing both delays in administration and hesitancy among clinicians with limited exposure to vasoactive drugs like epinephrine, administration of a 0.3 mg dose in adults over 50 kg frequently results in inadequate dosing. A retrospective review from 2025 demonstrated that recipients of an initial 0.3 mg epinephrine dose required additional interventions, such as repeated epinephrine administration, considerably more often compared to those first treated with 0.5 mg. Reducing barriers—such as longstanding clinical comfort with 0.3 mg dosing and the widespread accessibility of 0.3 mg auto-injectors—and enhancing compliance with guideline-recommended dosing could substantially optimize anaphylaxis outcomes.

Historically, corticosteroids have been prescribed partly to avert biphasic reactions in anaphylaxis. However, accumulating evidence indicates a lack of benefit and even possible adverse outcomes, which has prompted a recent trend away from using steroids in these situations. A systematic review conducted in 2017 analyzed 22 studies, with only one favoring corticosteroid therapy, while the remaining 21 found no reduction in the risk of biphasic anaphylaxis attributable to steroids. Moreover, newer research highlights the significance of side effects, with a 2019 study reporting an increased probability of ICU or hospital admission following prehospital administration of corticosteroids. As a result, most updated guidelines recommend against routine corticosteroid use, yet their application continues in some clinical settings.

Biphasic anaphylaxis occurs infrequently, but it poses substantial clinical concern. The lack of definitive predictors complicates the process of assessing risk and determining appropriate observation durations. Proposed risk factors include delayed initiation of epinephrine, severe symptoms on presentation, need for repeated epinephrine dosing, and a history of biphasic reactions. Despite these suggestions, no robust predictors exist to enable selective monitoring.

Systemic mastocytosis is characterized by a pathological proliferation of mast cells, leading to excessive mediator release and widespread organ inflammation, which increases susceptibility to severe anaphylaxis.1517 Nonetheless, due to the uncommon nature of both systemic mastocytosis and biphasic anaphylaxis, there are currently no studies specifically evaluating their interrelationship. Additional investigation into mast cell disorders and biphasic anaphylaxis could yield important advances in patient care.

The mortality rate associated with anaphylaxis is low, particularly in areas where prompt access to emergency medical services is available. In addition, deaths attributed to biphasic anaphylaxis occur even less frequently, despite their potential severity. Nonetheless, anaphylaxis still constitutes a frequent medical emergency that requires rapid intervention and sufficient observation. Although research and clinical guidelines predominantly address initial emergency management, ongoing patient care and surveillance are equally significant. Enhanced knowledge of complex manifestations, such as biphasic anaphylaxis, may facilitate the development of more focused, individualized, and resource-conscious approaches to anaphylaxis management.

Owing to the infrequent occurrence of biphasic and especially multiphasic anaphylactic reactions, as well as the challenges inherent to conducting studies in acute care scenarios, establishing robust evidence-based treatment strategies is difficult. In these cases, consensus guidelines based on expert opinion that offer explicit recommendations are extremely valuable. However, the formulation of such expert-driven guidance is limited by a shortage of comprehensive data. Considering the uncommon nature of these complicated cases, the creation of a registry encompassing these events—detailing key patient characteristics, potential management recommendations as illustrated in our case report, and outcome measures—may lay the groundwork for developing expert-based, data-informed guidance.
