Title: Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting

Margret Ann Schneider, Hans de Groot, Jan-Willem Geerts

Abstract:

Summary: Anaphylaxis represents the most dangerous manifestation of allergic reactions, involving systemic involvement that necessitates urgent and effective intervention. In certain patients, symptoms recur after initial resolution, even following appropriate therapy and complete initial improvement. These biphasic and multiphasic reactions pose distinct difficulties for practitioners, as reported prevalence varies widely, associated risk factors remain unclear, and established protocols in existing guidelines are absent. The issue is particularly pronounced with multiphasic anaphylaxis, characterized by several episodes of anaphylactic recurrence following standard epinephrine therapy. Since multiphasic anaphylaxis is uncommon, individual case reports describing management strategies are valuable sources of guidance for clinicians facing this condition. Here, we describe the experience of a woman in her 60s who developed multiphasic anaphylaxis in response to clarithromycin. Subsequent evaluation suggested a probable diagnosis of systemic mastocytosis, although the formal diagnostic criteria were not fully satisfied.

Background:

Anaphylaxis is a serious allergic reaction that can be life-threatening, particularly when it progresses to anaphylactic shock, and it frequently leads patients to seek emergency medical care. A proportion of cases follow a biphasic or even multiphasic pattern, with symptoms initially triggered by an allergen, then subsiding to an asymptomatic period, before recurring without further contact with the allergen. This recurrence typically appears within the first 24 hours after the initial exposure, though instances of symptom return have been documented up to 78 hours later. Published rates for biphasic anaphylaxis are quite variable, from 0.4% up to 23.3%; nevertheless, most evidence suggests a prevalence between 4% and 6%. Because of the risk for symptom recurrence, it is standard practice to observe patients following initial symptom resolution after anaphylaxis. Still, there is inconsistency in how biphasic anaphylaxis is defined, and clinical recommendations differ, with recent debate surrounding the use of corticosteroids. Multiphasic anaphylaxis, characterized by multiple episodes of symptom return, raises particular concerns about appropriate management and monitoring. In the present study, we describe the case of a woman in her sixties who experienced a multiphasic anaphylactic response following exposure to clarithromycin.

Case presentation:

A woman in her sixties with a documented allergy to beta-lactam antibiotics arrived at the emergency department with anaphylaxis after ingesting clarithromycin 500 mg orally.

Her past medical history featured type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, psoriasis, and depression. She weighed 104 kg. Her current medication regimen included metformin (500 mg twice daily), gliclazide (80 mg extended-release, once daily), hydrochlorothiazide (25 mg once daily), omeprazole (20 mg once daily), and sertraline (100 mg once daily). She had taken her last dose of hydrochlorothiazide in the morning, more than 12 hours prior to arriving at the ED.

As a teenager, she experienced a mild allergic reaction to amoxicillin, presenting only as facial swelling—there was no oropharyngeal involvement, rash, hypotension, or dyspnoea. She also had previously reacted to a wasp sting with a pronounced localized skin response but without any systemic manifestations. The current incident marked her first experience of anaphylaxis.

Five days before her ED visit, her general practitioner had prescribed clindamycin for erysipelas. After five days on clindamycin, she developed an unspecified rash on her abdomen. This was considered a delayed allergic reaction to clindamycin, prompting her general practitioner to change her antibiotic therapy to clarithromycin, which she had never taken before.

She later arrived at the emergency department experiencing itching, flushing, increased sweating, restlessness, and a sensation of throat swelling. These manifestations began approximately one hour after ingesting her first oral dose of clarithromycin and became progressively more severe. She presented to medical services one hour following the onset of these symptoms. Upon arrival, her vital signs revealed low blood pressure (non-invasive measurement: 74/50 mm Hg), reduced oxygen saturation (88% on ambient air), and a respiratory rate of 22 breaths per minute. Initial physical examination noted facial edema, a new generalized erythematous rash with urticaria covering her body, increased sweating, and agitation. There was no observed tongue or throat swelling, and both cardiac and pulmonary examinations were unremarkable. Capillary blood glucose measured 12.7 mmol/L, and electrocardiogram demonstrated sinus rhythm. She promptly received treatment for a presumed severe hypersensitivity reaction, including 0.3 mg of intramuscular epinephrine delivered by auto-injector, as well as a slow intravenous administration of 2 mg clemastine according to local protocols for dermatologic symptoms. Further, she was provided with supplemental oxygen at 15 liters per minute through a non-rebreather mask and 500 mL of 0.9% sodium chloride solution intravenously during her initial stabilization. Oxygen supplementation was discontinued after clinical improvement was noted following epinephrine administration, although it was temporarily restarted during recurrent episodes of anaphylaxis. Clarithromycin was permanently stopped, with no further doses given after the initial 500 mg oral administration.

A favorable initial response to therapy was noted, with normalization of blood pressure and reduction of both facial edema and erythema. The patient was subsequently transferred to the acute internal ward, offering enhanced surveillance by trained nursing staff but lacking telemetry monitoring. Nonetheless, within the first 12 hours after admission, the patient experienced repeated episodes consistent with anaphylactic shock despite the initial improvement. Following the first recurrence, transfer to the intensive care unit (ICU) was arranged for closer observation. Each anaphylactic episode was managed with intramuscular epinephrine administration (figure 1); the patient had a total of four episodes, including the initial presentation in the emergency department. The first two doses were given via auto-injector, delivering 0.3 mg of epinephrine intramuscularly, and the next two were administered as manually prepared doses containing 0.5 mg epinephrine intramuscularly. While the epinephrine auto-injectors available in our facility have a lower dose than the recommended 0.5 mg, their use is favored in situations where rapid intervention is needed, such as during initial stabilization in the emergency department, or where emergent treatments may be infrequently performed and uncertainty regarding medication could otherwise delay timely administration. After the first anaphylaxis relapse, corticosteroid therapy was added, adhering to the traditional application for prevention of biphasic anaphylaxis. The patient was given intravenous prednisone 30 mg daily for 5 days, along with a single intravenous dose of dexamethasone 8 mg; this reflected differing recommendations from our institution's protocols for anaphylaxis management in general wards versus intensive care units. Additionally, for cutaneous symptoms, intravenous clemastine 2 mg was administered twice, and during the second anaphylactic episode, she received 500 mL of normal saline solution. Notably, each successive anaphylactic episode demonstrated reduced severity, progressing from profound hypoxaemia with cyanosis, hypotension, and severe dyspnoea initially, to milder manifestations such as dyspnoea, rash reappearance, and pruritus in the final episode.

Specialized input was sought from the allergology department early for guidance on management. Two alternative treatment strategies were evaluated: one involving continuous intravenous epinephrine infusion aimed at preventing anaphylactic events before symptom recurrence, and another adopting a watchful waiting approach given the gradual reduction in symptom intensity, to determine if the episodes would resolve independently. Both strategies posed significant risks, especially in view of limited literature regarding therapeutic options for multiphasic anaphylaxis.

Outcome and follow-up:

In the end, neither therapeutic intervention was required, since the patient did not experience any additional recurrence of symptoms after this stage. Approximately 24 hours without new episodes of anaphylaxis passed before she was moved back to a general care unit. She was discharged following a 3-day hospitalization. Levocetirizine, an antihistamine, was initiated as her new regular medication, and she received training to use an epinephrine auto-injector (0.3 mg). She has not had any further instances of anaphylaxis since discharge.

Given the unusual and extended progression, a deeper investigation into possible underlying conditions was undertaken. This assessment indicated a strong likelihood of systemic mastocytosis, although diagnostic criteria were not fully met; only two WHO minor criteria were identified, with the major criterion unable to be evaluated because bone marrow biopsy yielded insufficient material (refer to figure 2). Regrettably, tryptase concentrations were not measured during the initial assessment of the early anaphylactic reactions, resulting in a failure to follow the recommended timeframes for tryptase testing. Current guidelines suggest collecting at least two, preferably three tryptase measurements at defined points: the first sample should be obtained as soon as possible without postponing therapy, a second sample 1–2 hours (but within 4 hours) after symptom onset when tryptase typically reaches its maximum, and a third measurement at least 24 hours after complete symptom resolution to determine baseline levels. In this patient's case, the initial tryptase sample was acquired 1 hour following the third episode of anaphylaxis, while the subsequent sample was drawn 27 hours after the final recurrence.

Allergy confirmation testing was not pursued, as the potential for provoking another severe anaphylactic event—such as by skin testing—was considered unjustified, particularly given the ease of avoiding further antigen exposure. Additional pertinent findings consistent with systemic mastocytosis were observed, including hepatomegaly detected via abdominal ultrasound and potential neuropsychiatric manifestations, such as depression and memory impairment.

Discussion:

Anaphylaxis is generally recognized as a clinical diagnosis with distinct characteristics, including rapid onset and swift escalation of symptoms that may involve the airway, respiration, circulation, and/or the gastrointestinal system. These are typically seen alongside skin or mucosal signs such as urticaria, angioedema, or flushing. While finding a probable allergen reinforces the diagnosis, it is often not feasible to pinpoint an exact inciting factor.

Atypical manifestations like biphasic or refractory anaphylaxis are not as clearly described, though consensus definitions have been the focus of recent work. According to the Resuscitation Council UK, biphasic anaphylaxis involves a return of symptoms several hours after initial resolution, without renewed exposure to the allergen. Discerning true biphasic reactions from cases of ongoing anaphylaxis that temporarily improve with epinephrine, or from continued absorption of the offending agent (as may occur with retained food or sustained-release drugs), is not straightforward. In contrast, refractory anaphylaxis denotes an episode that persists without adequate resolution despite appropriately dosed epinephrine.

Management guidelines for biphasic anaphylaxis mainly pertain to monitoring protocols. Nevertheless, the ideal period for observation is still under discussion. Commonly, recommendations suggest monitoring for 4–6 hours after symptoms have subsided. The Nederlands Huisartsen Genootschap (NHG) and the World Allergy Organisation guideline state that nearly 50% of biphasic reactions develop within 6 to 12 hours of the original event, although they do not specify a precise observation timeframe. The 2021 UK guideline, however, outlines a three-tier, risk-based approach to post-event monitoring. The creation of standardized monitoring strategies is essential to effectively balance resource utilization for the majority experiencing a single episode of anaphylaxis with the safety considerations for the relatively smaller, higher-risk biphasic subset.

Guidance for advanced pharmacologic interventions in biphasic and multiphasic anaphylaxis episodes remains conspicuously insufficient. The therapeutic approach to these presentations is largely analogous to conventional anaphylaxis management, focusing on immediate intramuscular epinephrine administration along with necessary supportive measures. Consensus guidelines recommend an epinephrine dose of 0.01 mg/kg, with an upper limit of 0.5 mg. In practice, this is often simplified to a 0.5 mg dose in adults, as most adult patients weigh more than 50 kg. Nevertheless, it is notable that many epinephrine auto-injectors—including those administered during the first two episodes in the discussed case—provide only 0.3 mg, despite adult designation. Auto-injectors are indispensable in out-of-hospital environments and retain value even in clinical settings by mitigating delays and provider reluctance to administer vasoactive agents such as epinephrine, particularly among personnel with limited experience in their use. Despite this, reliance on the 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injector, while frequent, may result in clinically significant underdosing in adults above 50 kg. A retrospective review from 2025 demonstrated that individuals initially receiving 0.3 mg were significantly more likely to require escalation of care—including repeated epinephrine dosing—compared with those first given the full 0.5 mg. Enhancing prescriber confidence with the recommended 0.5 mg dose, overcoming preference for the more commonly available 0.3 mg preparations, and improving guideline adherence could markedly advance outcomes in anaphylaxis management.

Historically, corticosteroids were advocated partly to mitigate biphasic reactions; however, accumulating data now indicate not only an absence of therapeutic benefit, but also the possibility of adverse effects. This has prompted a recent departure from steroid administration during anaphylaxis management. A systematic review published in 2017 evaluated 22 studies: only one study favored corticosteroid use, while 21 demonstrated no reduction in biphasic anaphylaxis risk with corticosteroid therapy. Moreover, recent research has highlighted the significance of corticosteroid-associated adverse events, including a 2019 study that found prehospital corticosteroid administration correlated with higher rates of ICU and ward admission. As a result, the majority of current guidelines recommend against routine corticosteroid use, though clinical practice continues to include them in some cases.

While biphasic anaphylaxis is uncommon, it remains clinically significant. The lack of dependable predictors makes stratifying risk and selecting monitoring strategies challenging. Proposed predictors include late administration of epinephrine, severe initial manifestations, multiple epinephrine requirements, and previous biphasic reactions. Nonetheless, there is presently no robust predictive tool to enable selective monitoring.

Systemic mastocytosis, characterized by proliferation of mast cells, excessive release of mediators, and multiorgan inflammatory involvement, confers susceptibility to severe anaphylactic episodes.1517 Despite this, due to the infrequency of both systemic mastocytosis and biphasic anaphylaxis, there appears to be no current literature examining the relationship between the two. Further investigation into mast cell disorders and their connection to biphasic anaphylaxis may be crucial for advancing future therapeutic approaches.

Luckily, fatalities resulting from anaphylaxis are infrequent, particularly where there is rapid access to emergency care. In addition, deaths related to biphasic anaphylaxis are even less common, despite the potentially serious nature of these reactions. Nevertheless, despite these encouraging data, anaphylaxis remains a frequent emergency that necessitates immediate intervention and careful observation. While existing research and guidelines focus primarily on acute medical response, the importance of follow-up care and surveillance cannot be overstated. Enhanced insight into complicated forms of anaphylaxis, such as biphasic reactions, may facilitate the development of more individualized, efficient, and targeted management approaches.

Developing rigorously evidence-based protocols for biphasic and multiphasic anaphylaxis is particularly challenging due to the infrequency of these cases and obstacles to conducting research in acute emergency environments. In these settings, expert opinion-based consensus guidelines with explicit recommendations serve as an important resource; yet, the creation of such guidance remains limited by the scarcity of robust data. Given the unusual nature of these complex anaphylactic episodes, establishing a registry to gather key patient data, suggested management approaches—as illustrated in our case report—and patient outcomes could provide a vital foundation for formulating data-driven, expert consensus-based strategies.
