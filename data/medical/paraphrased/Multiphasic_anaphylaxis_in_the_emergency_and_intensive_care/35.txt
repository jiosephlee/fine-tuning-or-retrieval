Title: Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting

Margret Ann Schneider, Hans de Groot, Jan-Willem Geerts

Abstract:

Summary: Anaphylaxis represents the most severe variant of allergic reaction and constitutes a potentially fatal, systemic event that necessitates rapid and appropriate intervention. In certain patients, symptoms return after resolution despite satisfactory initial management. These biphasic or multiphasic anaphylactic episodes pose distinct difficulties for healthcare providers, particularly given that reported rates fluctuate, associated risk factors remain undefined, and definitive recommendations are absent in contemporary guidelines. This challenge is heightened in cases of multiphasic anaphylaxis, in which repeated episodes occur despite appropriate therapy with epinephrine. Owing to its rarity, case reports detailing multiphasic anaphylaxis can serve as valuable resources for clinical practice. This report describes a woman in her sixth decade of life who developed multiphasic anaphylaxis following clarithromycin exposure. Subsequent assessment revealed a strong probability of systemic mastocytosis, although she did not fulfill the established diagnostic criteria.

Background:

Anaphylaxis represents a severe allergic reaction that can be life-threatening, particularly in cases of anaphylactic shock, and it is a frequent reason for emergency medical evaluation. Nevertheless, a proportion of individuals experience biphasic or, in some instances, multiphasic reactions. These are defined by an initial anaphylactic episode triggered by an allergen, subsequent symptom resolution resulting in an asymptomatic interval, and then recurrence of symptoms in the absence of further allergen contact. Typically, this recurrence occurs within 24 hours following the initial exposure, although literature describes relapses occurring up to 78 hours afterward. Estimates of the frequency of biphasic anaphylaxis range widely from 0.4% to 23.3%, though most studies report rates between 4% and 6%. In light of potential relapse, observation following anaphylactic episodes is generally recommended, even when symptoms have subsided completely. However, diagnostic criteria for biphasic anaphylaxis are sometimes inconsistent, and there is variability among management guidelines, with recent scrutiny over the use of corticosteroids. Multiphasic anaphylaxis, which involves more than one recurrence of symptoms, raises particular difficulties regarding therapeutic strategies and monitoring duration. This report discusses a case involving a woman in her 60s who developed a multiphasic anaphylactic response after exposure to clarithromycin.

Case presentation:

A female in her sixth decade, with a documented hypersensitivity to beta-lactam antibiotics, arrived at the emergency department experiencing anaphylaxis after ingesting clarithromycin 500 mg orally.

Her past medical history was significant for type 2 diabetes mellitus, arterial hypertension, psoriasis, and depression. She weighed 104 kg. Current medications included metformin 500 mg twice daily, gliclazide 80 mg (retard formulation) once daily, hydrochlorothiazide 25 mg once daily, omeprazole 20 mg once daily, and sertraline 100 mg once daily. The most recent dose of hydrochlorothiazide had been consumed over 12 hours prior to presentation.

During her teenage years, the patient experienced a mild allergic response to amoxicillin characterized by facial edema, but without involvement of the oropharynx, absence of cutaneous rash, hypotension, or dyspnea. She had also previously reacted strongly at the skin to a wasp sting, with no accompanying systemic manifestations. Notably, this was her initial occurrence of anaphylaxis requiring hospital admission.

Erysipelas had been diagnosed by her general practitioner five days before this ED visit, leading to a prescription for clindamycin. After using clindamycin for 5 days, she noticed a rash developing on her abdomen (further details not provided), which was judged to be a delayed allergic response. Consequently, her general practitioner altered her antibiotic treatment to clarithromycin, which she had not received previously.

She later arrived at the emergency department reporting itching, facial flushing, increased sweating, agitation, and a sensation of throat swelling. Her symptoms began approximately one hour following ingestion of her first clarithromycin dose and became progressively more severe. She sought medical attention one hour after the onset of her symptoms. On initial evaluation in the emergency department, her vital signs revealed low blood pressure and decreased oxygen saturation (non-invasive blood pressure: 74/50 mm Hg, oxygen saturation 88% on room air, respiratory rate 22 breaths per minute). Physical examination found facial swelling, newly developed widespread erythema, urticaria over her entire body, increased perspiration, and agitation. There was no swelling of the tongue or throat, and both cardiac and pulmonary auscultation were unremarkable. Her blood glucose measured 12.7 mmol/L, and her ECG demonstrated sinus rhythm. She received immediate treatment for suspected severe allergic reaction, which included intramuscular administration of 0.3 mg epinephrine via an auto-injector and slow intravenous administration of 2 mg clemastine for the cutaneous symptoms according to local protocols. In addition, she was given supplemental oxygen (15 L via non-rebreather mask) and 500 mL of normal saline (0.9% sodium chloride solution) during her initial stabilisation. Oxygen supplementation could be discontinued following a positive response to epinephrine but needed to be temporarily reintroduced during subsequent anaphylactic events. Clarithromycin was withdrawn, and she did not receive any further doses beyond the single initial 500 mg oral administration.

At first, the patient demonstrated a favorable reaction to therapy, with normalization of blood pressure and reduction in facial swelling and erythema. She was transferred to the acute internal medicine ward, where she received enhanced supervision from specialized nursing staff, though continuous cardiac monitoring was not used. Despite the initial positive response, the patient experienced repeated episodes indicative of anaphylactic shock within the first 12 hours post-admission. After the first recurrence, she was moved to the intensive care unit (ICU) for closer observation. Each event necessitated intramuscular epinephrine administration (see figure 1), totaling four anaphylactic episodes including the original emergency department presentation. The initial two doses of epinephrine were delivered via auto-injector at 0.3 mg intramuscularly, while the subsequent two doses were given as custom-prepared 0.5 mg intramuscular injections. Although our clinic’s auto-injectors provide less than the standard recommended 0.5 mg dose, they are preferred in situations requiring prompt intervention—such as initial stabilization in the emergency department—or when emergency management of anaphylaxis is infrequent and unfamiliarity with dosing could cause delays. Following the first recurrence, corticosteroid therapy was added due to its conventional application in preventing biphasic anaphylactic reactions. The patient received intravenous prednisone at 30 mg daily for 5 days and a single intravenous dose of 8 mg dexamethasone; this was based on differing protocols between the general ward and ICU for anaphylaxis management at our institution. Additionally, intravenous clemastine at 2 mg was administered twice to address cutaneous symptoms, and an extra 500 mL of normal saline was given during the second anaphylactic episode. Notably, symptom severity diminished progressively with each occurrence—from profound hypoxemia, cyanosis, hypotension, and intense dyspnea at onset, to milder dyspnea, rash recurrence, and pruritus during the last episode.

The allergology department was consulted early for expert therapeutic guidance. Two possible management strategies were deliberated. One involved the proactive administration of intravenous epinephrine via infusion pump to preempt anaphylactic events rather than treating upon symptom emergence. Alternatively, considering the gradual decrease in symptom intensity, an expectant management was discussed, monitoring if symptoms would remit spontaneously. Both strategies entailed substantial risks, particularly given the absence of evidence or literature guiding treatment in cases with multiphasic presentations.

Outcome and follow-up:

In the end, neither treatment option was required since the patient experienced no recurrence of symptoms beyond this time. Approximately 24 hours after remaining free from new anaphylactic events, she was transferred to a regular care unit. After a total hospitalization period of 3 days, she was discharged. She was newly prescribed the antihistamine levocetirizine for routine use and provided with a 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injector, along with instructions for its administration. There have been no subsequent instances of anaphylaxis.

Given the unusual and extended progression of the case, a more thorough investigation into potential underlying etiologies was initiated. The assessment indicated a strong likelihood of systemic mastocytosis, though it did not satisfy the official diagnostic criteria (two WHO minor criteria present; major criteria could not be evaluated due to insufficient sample from the bone marrow biopsy, see figure 2). Tryptase measurements were not obtained during the initial evaluation of the patient’s first anaphylactic events, so the recommended protocol for timing of tryptase assessments was not followed. Current guidelines advise collecting at least two, preferably three, samples at specified times: one should be drawn as soon as possible without interrupting acute management, a second sample should be collected 1–2 hours (and no later than 4 hours) after symptom onset, coinciding with the expected tryptase peak, and a third specimen should be obtained at least 24 hours after complete symptom resolution to determine the baseline tryptase value. For the patient discussed here, the first tryptase sample was obtained one hour following the third episode of anaphylaxis, and the second sample was drawn 27 hours after the most recent occurrence.

Allergy confirmation testing was not performed since the risk of precipitating another episode of severe anaphylaxis with procedures such as skin testing was judged to be excessive, particularly given that subsequent exposures could be readily prevented. Additional findings that may support a diagnosis of systemic mastocytosis consisted of hepatomegaly detected by abdominal ultrasound and potential neuropsychiatric features such as memory impairment and depression.

Discussion:

Anaphylaxis is generally recognized as a clinical syndrome with distinct diagnostic criteria, marked by an acute and rapidly evolving onset of symptoms affecting the airway, breathing, circulation, and/or gastrointestinal system. These episodes usually present with skin or mucosal findings, such as urticaria, angioedema, or flushing. While the detection of a probable allergen can aid in diagnosis, pinpointing the exact trigger is frequently not feasible.

More atypical forms, including biphasic and refractory anaphylaxis, are less clearly delineated, though recent initiatives have sought to create consensus definitions. The Resuscitation Council UK defines biphasic anaphylaxis as the return of symptoms several hours after initial resolution, without renewed allergen exposure. Distinguishing genuine biphasic events from either ongoing anaphylaxis briefly suppressed by epinephrine, or from sustained allergen absorption (for instance, due to retained food or slow-release medications), can be complex. In contrast, refractory anaphylaxis denotes ongoing symptoms unresponsive or minimally responsive to appropriately administered epinephrine.

Clinical practice guidelines regarding biphasic anaphylaxis primarily focus on patient observation and monitoring, but the ideal length for this period is still contested. Typical observation intervals after symptom resolution fall between 4 and 6 hours. Guidelines from the Dutch Nederlands Huisartsen Genootschap (NHG) and the World Allergy Organisation indicate that up to half of biphasic reactions may manifest 6–12 hours after the initial presentation, yet neither suggests a definitive duration for monitoring. The UK’s 2021 guideline instead introduces a three-tier, risk-stratified framework for surveillance. There remains an evident need for unified monitoring protocols to effectively reconcile the allocation of healthcare resources (primarily for individuals experiencing only a single anaphylactic episode) with the imperative to safeguard those who are at risk of rare but serious biphasic reactions.

There is a notable and concerning absence of specific recommendations regarding advanced pharmacologic management for biphasic and multiphasic anaphylaxis. Treatment protocols for these forms primarily replicate standard anaphylaxis interventions, focusing on immediate administration of intramuscular epinephrine together with suitable supportive measures. Current guidelines advise an epinephrine dose of 0.01 mg/kg, with a ceiling of 0.5 mg total. In clinical practice, this recommendation is generally approximated as a 0.5 mg dose for adults, since most adults exceed 50 kg in body weight. Despite this, it is noteworthy that many available auto-injectors—including those employed during the first two events in the present case—provide only 0.3 mg of epinephrine, even for adult indications. While auto-injectors for epinephrine remain pivotal in pre-hospital contexts, they also confer benefits within healthcare settings by facilitating rapid administration and diminishing reluctance among staff unfamiliar with vasoactive therapies like epinephrine. Nevertheless, use of 0.3 mg auto-injectors in adults over 50 kg often constitutes significant underdosing. Evidence from a 2025 retrospective analysis indicates that patients initially administered 0.3 mg epinephrine required care escalation, such as repeated epinephrine dosing, significantly more frequently than those initially given 0.5 mg. Addressing challenges such as clinician preference for the traditional 0.3 mg dose and the high prevalence of 0.3 mg auto-injectors, as well as bolstering compliance with dosing recommendations, may be vital in optimizing outcomes in anaphylaxis management.

Historically, corticosteroids were administered to mitigate the risk of biphasic reactions in anaphylaxis. Nonetheless, growing data reveals not only a lack of efficacy but also potential adverse effects, prompting a recent movement away from steroid use in this context. A systematic review conducted in 2017 evaluated 22 studies: only one favored corticosteroid therapy, whereas the remaining 21 found no reduction in biphasic anaphylaxis risk. Recent investigations highlight the significance of steroid-related side effects; notably, a 2019 study identified a greater chance of ICU or hospital ward admission when corticosteroids were given in the prehospital setting. As a result, the majority of updated guidelines recommend against routine corticosteroid use for anaphylaxis, although they are still commonly administered in practice.

Although biphasic anaphylaxis occurs infrequently, its clinical importance remains substantial. The lack of dependable predictors creates challenges in both risk assessment and decisions regarding patient observation. Proposed risk factors include delayed epinephrine delivery, severity at onset, administration of multiple epinephrine doses, and history of biphasic reactions. However, prediction models that reliably guide focused monitoring are currently unavailable.

Systemic mastocytosis is characterized by increased mast cell proliferation, heightened mediator secretion, and inflammation across multiple organs, putting affected individuals at a higher risk for severe anaphylactic episodes.1517 Despite this association, given the infrequent occurrence of both conditions, there is, to date, no research directly exploring the impact of systemic mastocytosis on biphasic anaphylaxis. Ongoing studies in mast cell disorders and biphasic anaphylaxis are needed and may advance future management strategies.

Thankfully, mortality associated with anaphylaxis is low, particularly in areas where emergency medical services are readily available. Moreover, fatal cases of biphasic anaphylaxis are exceedingly uncommon, even though these episodes may seem particularly threatening. Despite this encouraging data, anaphylaxis continues to be a frequent emergency that necessitates immediate intervention and careful observation. Most research and guideline development focuses on the acute management of anaphylaxis; nevertheless, follow-up care and ongoing monitoring are also essential. Gaining more insight into the course of complex anaphylactic reactions, such as biphasic episodes, may help establish more individualized, patient-focused, and resource-effective management approaches.

The infrequency of biphasic—and especially multiphasic—anaphylaxis, combined with the inherent difficulties of conducting research in emergency contexts, presents significant obstacles to developing genuinely evidence-based protocols. In these scenarios, guidelines derived from expert consensus and offering explicit recommendations are highly valuable; however, formulating such recommendations is also restricted by the scarcity of available evidence. Given the unusual nature of these complex anaphylactic reactions, assembling a registry that records detailed patient characteristics, potential management approaches (such as those described in this case report), and outcomes may lay the foundation for developing data-informed, expert consensus guidelines.
