Title: Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting

Margret Ann Schneider, Hans de Groot, Jan-Willem Geerts

Abstract:

Summary: Anaphylaxis represents the most severe allergic reaction, constituting a potentially fatal systemic event that necessitates immediate and appropriate intervention. In certain instances, symptoms re-emerge after apparent resolution despite adequate treatment and complete initial clinical improvement. These biphasic and even multiphasic patterns of anaphylaxis create specific difficulties for healthcare providers, particularly since reported rates of occurrence are inconsistent, the contributing factors are not well-established, and existing guidelines lack detailed recommendations. This challenge is heightened in multiphasic anaphylaxis, characterized by several recurrences of anaphylaxis following an initial favorable response to standard epinephrine therapy. Because multiphasic anaphylaxis is relatively uncommon, individual case reports that detail management approaches are a valuable source of information about this issue. Here, we describe the case of a woman in her 60s who experienced multiphasic anaphylaxis triggered by clarithromycin, and subsequent evaluation suggested a strong possibility of systemic mastocytosis, although she did not satisfy the formal diagnostic criteria.

Background:

Anaphylaxis represents a serious allergic reaction that can be life-threatening, particularly when it progresses to anaphylactic shock, and frequently leads patients to seek emergency care. A proportion of cases experience biphasic or even multiphasic manifestations, distinguished by an initial allergic episode upon exposure to a trigger, subsequent resolution of symptoms with an asymptomatic interval, and later return of symptoms in the absence of additional allergen exposure. This pattern typically emerges within the first 24 hours following the original exposure, though recurrences have been documented up to 78 hours afterwards. The reported frequency of biphasic anaphylaxis varies greatly, from 0.4% to 23.3%, but most evidence indicates an incidence of 4% to 6%. Given the risk of symptom recurrence, observation for a certain duration after initial symptom resolution is routinely recommended. Nonetheless, definitions for biphasic anaphylaxis can be inconsistent, and therapeutic recommendations differ, especially regarding the effectiveness of corticosteroids, which has been debated in recent times. Multiphasic anaphylaxis, involving several episodes of symptom relapse, poses distinct challenges for management and monitoring protocols. This report discusses the case of a female patient in her sixth decade who experienced a multiphasic anaphylactic response to clarithromycin.

Case presentation:

A woman in her sixth decade, who had a documented hypersensitivity to beta-lactam antibiotics, arrived at the emergency department (ED) experiencing anaphylaxis after ingesting clarithromycin 500 mg orally.

Her medical background comprised type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, psoriasis, and depression. She weighed 104 kg. Her regular medications were metformin 500 mg twice daily, gliclazide 80 mg (retard) once daily, hydrochlorothiazide 25 mg once daily, omeprazole 20 mg once daily, and sertraline 100 mg once daily. The most recent hydrochlorothiazide dose was administered in the morning, more than 12 hours prior to her arrival in the ED.

She had experienced a mild allergic reaction to amoxicillin as an adolescent, characterized solely by facial edema, without involving oropharyngeal swelling, rash, hypotension, or dyspnoea. Additionally, she previously exhibited a pronounced localized skin response to a wasp sting, but did not develop any systemic manifestations. The current hospital admission marked her initial encounter with anaphylaxis.

Five days before her ED visit, clindamycin was prescribed by her general practitioner to treat erysipelas. After five days on clindamycin, she developed a rash on her abdomen (details not provided). This was regarded as a delayed allergic response to clindamycin, prompting her general practitioner to change her antibiotic treatment to clarithromycin, which she had not taken previously.

She later arrived at the emergency department experiencing itching, flushing, excessive sweating, restlessness, and a sensation of throat swelling. These complaints began one hour following her first oral administration of clarithromycin and intensified progressively. She presented for medical evaluation one hour after her symptoms began. Upon arrival, her initial vital signs revealed low blood pressure and decreased oxygen saturation (non-invasive blood pressure measured at 74/50 mm Hg; peripheral oxygen saturation was 88% breathing room air; respiratory rate was 22 breaths per minute). On physical examination, she had swelling of the face, newly developed widespread erythema and urticaria over her entire body, increased sweating, and agitation. There was no evidence of tongue or pharyngeal swelling; cardiac and pulmonary auscultation findings were unremarkable. Her blood glucose level measured 12.7 mmol/L, and her ECG demonstrated sinus rhythm. Given strong suspicion for a severe allergic reaction, immediate treatment included intramuscular administration of 0.3 mg epinephrine via auto-injector and slow intravenous delivery of 2 mg clemastine to address the dermatological symptoms, in alignment with local protocols. She also received high-flow supplemental oxygen (15 L by non-rebreather mask) and an infusion of 500 mL normal saline (0.9% sodium chloride solution) during initial resuscitation. Supplemental oxygen could be withdrawn after significant improvement in response to epinephrine, though it was briefly reinitiated during further anaphylactic episodes. Clarithromycin was stopped, and no additional doses beyond the initial 500 mg taken orally were administered.

Initially, the therapy yielded a favorable response, with normalization of blood pressure and reduction in facial swelling and erythema. The patient was subsequently transferred to the acute internal medicine ward, which offers enhanced observation by specialized nursing staff but does not employ telemetry monitoring. Nevertheless, although initial improvement was evident, within 12 hours of admission, the patient experienced repeated episodes consistent with anaphylactic shock. After the first recurrence, she was moved to the intensive care unit (ICU) for closer monitoring. Each event required intramuscular epinephrine administration to achieve resolution (figure 1), amounting to four episodes of anaphylaxis including the initial presentation in the emergency department. The first two doses consisted of 0.3 mg epinephrine delivered via auto-injector intramuscularly, while the third and fourth episodes were managed with individually prepared intramuscular doses of 0.5 mg epinephrine. Although the available auto-injectors at our facility dispense less than the recommended 0.5 mg, their use is favored in urgent settings—such as during initial stabilization of a patient in the ED—or in circumstances where anaphylactic emergencies are infrequent and potential hesitancy with unfamiliar medications could delay therapy. Following the initial recurrence of anaphylaxis, corticosteroid therapy was introduced, reflecting the conventional rationale for steroids in preventing biphasic reactions. The patient received 30 mg of intravenous prednisone daily for five days, and a single dose of 8 mg intravenous dexamethasone, attributable to differences in protocol between general ward and ICU anaphylaxis management at our institution. Additionally, two 2 mg intravenous doses of clemastine were administered for dermatologic symptoms, and 500 mL of normal saline was given during the second episode. Notably, the severity of anaphylactic manifestations diminished progressively, ranging from profound hypoxemia accompanied by cyanosis, hypotension, and marked dyspnea at onset, to dyspnea, recurrent rash, and pruritus during the final episode.

The allergology department was consulted early to advise on specialized management strategies. Two alternative therapeutic options were deliberated: first, prophylactic intravenous epinephrine infusion via pump to prevent symptom recurrence, and second, a wait-and-see approach considering the observed gradual decrease in symptom severity, allowing for potential spontaneous resolution. Both strategies carried considerable risks, accentuated by the absence of literature informing management of multiphasic anaphylactic cases.

Outcome and follow-up:

In the end, no additional treatment strategies were required since the patient experienced no recurrence of symptoms after this stage. Following roughly 24 hours without any new episodes of anaphylaxis, she was moved back to a general care unit. The patient was discharged after spending 3 days in the hospital. Upon discharge, she began regular administration of levocetirizine, an antihistamine, and received instruction on proper use of an epinephrine auto-injector (0.3 mg). She has not experienced any further anaphylactic incidents since then.

Given the unusual and extended nature of the patient’s presentation, a more detailed investigation into possible underlying etiologies was pursued. This evaluation indicated a strong likelihood of systemic mastocytosis; however, a definitive diagnosis could not be established due to only meeting two minor WHO criteria and inability to assess major criteria because of insufficient bone marrow biopsy sample (refer to figure 2). Regrettably, serum tryptase concentrations were not measured during the initial evaluation of the first episodes of anaphylaxis, so the recommended timing for tryptase assessment was not followed. Current guidelines advise collecting at least two, preferably three, tryptase specimens at specified time points: the first as early as possible without delaying emergency management; the second between 1 and 2 hours (and no more than 4 hours) after symptom onset, capturing the typical peak; and a third at least 24 hours post-complete symptom resolution to determine baseline tryptase. In this patient, the initial sample was obtained 1 hour following the third episode, and the subsequent sample was drawn 27 hours after the most recent episode of anaphylaxis.

No confirmatory allergy test was conducted due to the unacceptable risk of provoking another severe anaphylactic reaction, for example, via skin testing, especially considering that avoiding further exposure can be achieved with relative simplicity. Additional pertinent findings supporting a diagnosis of systemic mastocytosis included hepatomegaly identified on abdominal ultrasound, as well as possible neuropsychiatric symptoms such as depression and memory impairment.

Discussion:

Anaphylaxis is generally a well-established clinical condition, marked by acute onset and swiftly advancing symptoms that can affect the airway, breathing, cardiovascular system, and/or gastrointestinal tract, usually with associated cutaneous or mucosal signs such as urticaria, angioedema, or flushing. While the recognition of a probable allergen enhances diagnostic confidence, a definite cause may not always be identified.

More complicated cases—namely, biphasic or refractory anaphylaxis—are less precisely delineated, but recent initiatives have sought to standardize their definitions. According to the Resuscitation Council UK, biphasic anaphylaxis is defined by a resurgence of symptoms occurring hours after resolution without renewed allergen exposure. Distinguishing genuine biphasic events from ongoing anaphylaxis with only a temporary response to epinephrine, or from continuous allergen absorption (such as undigested food or extended-release drugs), can prove difficult. In contrast, refractory anaphylaxis indicates symptoms persist despite appropriately administered epinephrine having little or no effect.

Guidelines for biphasic anaphylaxis predominantly focus on recommendations for clinical monitoring, though the ideal observation period remains controversial. Traditionally, patients are observed for 4 to 6 hours once symptoms resolve. The Nederlands Huisartsen Genootschap (NHG) and World Allergy Organisation guidelines mention that about 50% of biphasic responses manifest within 6–12 hours of the initial incident, but neither specifies a distinct monitoring timeframe. In contrast, the 2021 UK guideline recommends a three-level, risk-based monitoring framework. There is a clear need for uniform monitoring approaches that appropriately weigh the demands on healthcare services (stemming from the large population with isolated anaphylaxis) against the imperative of ensuring safety for the minority at risk for complicated, less readily recognizable biphasic reactions.

There is a notable and concerning absence of guidance regarding advanced medication approaches for biphasic and multiphasic anaphylaxis. The treatment of these presentations is essentially consistent with that of typical anaphylaxis, relying on immediate intramuscular administration of epinephrine alongside suitable supportive measures. Current guidelines advise an epinephrine dose of 0.01 mg/kg, capping at a maximum of 0.5 mg. In clinical practice, this is typically simplified to a flat 0.5 mg dose for adults, as most adults weigh more than 50 kg. It is noteworthy, however, that many available auto-injectors, including those utilized during the initial two anaphylactic episodes in this report, provide only 0.3 mg of epinephrine, despite being labeled for adult use. While epinephrine auto-injectors are indispensable in prehospital settings, their utility extends to medical environments as well by reducing delays in therapy and decreasing the reluctance of staff who may be unfamiliar with vasoactive medications like epinephrine. Nevertheless, administering a 0.3 mg auto-injector in adults exceeding 50 kg frequently results in subtherapeutic dosing, which can be problematic. According to a 2025 retrospective study, patients who initially received 0.3 mg epinephrine were significantly more likely to require escalated interventions, including repeat doses of epinephrine, compared to those first treated with 0.5 mg. Addressing obstacles such as the traditional reliance on 0.3 mg doses and the widespread availability of 0.3 mg auto-injectors, alongside efforts to increase adherence to guideline-recommended dosing, could substantially enhance the management of anaphylaxis.

Historically, corticosteroids were advocated to help prevent biphasic reactions during anaphylaxis. Nonetheless, accumulating data indicates not only a lack of benefit but also potential adverse effects, resulting in a recent trend away from prescribing steroids for anaphylaxis. A systematic review conducted in 2017 assessed 22 studies, revealing that only one supported corticosteroid use, while the remaining 21 provided no evidence that steroids decrease the incidence of biphasic anaphylactic responses. Additionally, newer research has highlighted the importance of side effects; for instance, a 2019 study found an increased risk of ICU or hospital ward admission among patients treated with corticosteroids prior to hospital arrival. As a result, current guidelines generally recommend against routine corticosteroid use, although such practice remains common in clinical settings.

Biphasic anaphylaxis, although uncommon, has significant clinical implications. The lack of consistent predictors makes risk assessment and decisions regarding observation challenging. Proposed risk factors include late administration of epinephrine, a severe presentation at onset, the need for multiple doses of epinephrine, and history of biphasic episodes. Nonetheless, dependable predictors to guide targeted monitoring have yet to be established.

Systemic mastocytosis is characterized by increased mast cell numbers, excessive mediator release, and widespread inflammation, which makes affected patients particularly susceptible to severe anaphylaxis.1517 However, because both conditions are uncommon, there is currently no research specifically exploring the impact of systemic mastocytosis on biphasic anaphylactic events. Future studies addressing mast cell disorders and their relation to biphasic anaphylaxis may contribute important knowledge to enhance management strategies.

Fortunately, deaths due to anaphylaxis are uncommon, particularly in areas where emergency care can be delivered promptly. Fatal cases of biphasic anaphylaxis are even less frequent, despite the potential severity of such reactions. Nonetheless, anaphylaxis persists as a frequent medical emergency that requires immediate intervention and thorough observation. Although much research and many guidelines emphasize initial acute management, ongoing monitoring and follow-up care are equally essential. Gaining a better understanding of complicated presentations, including biphasic anaphylaxis, may enable more patient-specific, resource-conscious, and precise approaches to anaphylaxis management.

Due to the infrequency of biphasic and especially multiphasic anaphylactic episodes, combined with the inherent challenges of conducting research in emergency contexts, establishing robust evidence-based management protocols is particularly difficult. In these settings, consensus guidelines from clinical experts with explicit recommendations are an important tool; still, creating such guidelines is complicated by the limited data available. Given the scarcity of these complex cases, establishing a case registry—with detailed patient information, proposed management approaches as illustrated in our case report, and outcome metrics—may be fundamental for forming data-driven, expert consensus recommendations.
