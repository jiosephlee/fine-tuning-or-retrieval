Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting

Margret Ann Schneider, Hans de Groot, Jan-Willem Geerts

Abstract:

Summary: Anaphylaxis represents the most critical manifestation of allergic reactions, often posing an immediate risk to life and demanding swift and decisive intervention. In a proportion of patients, symptoms recur after apparent resolution and adequate therapeutic intervention, complicating clinical management. These biphasic or even multiphasic reactions are particularly problematic for healthcare providers, as their incidence rates fluctuate, risk profiles remain poorly defined, and established management protocols are insufficient or absent in prevailing guidelines. This complexity is heightened in cases of multiphasic anaphylaxis, wherein individuals experience repeated episodes of anaphylactic relapse even after an initial favorable response to epinephrine. Given the infrequency of multiphasic anaphylaxis, detailed case reports play a critical role in informing clinical practice. Here, we describe a case involving a female patient in her 60s who experienced multiphasic anaphylaxis triggered by clarithromycin, and subsequent investigations indicated a strong suspicion for systemic mastocytosis, despite not fulfilling the established diagnostic criteria.

Background:

Anaphylaxis constitutes a critical and, particularly in instances of anaphylactic shock, potentially fatal hypersensitivity response, frequently resulting in emergency medical visits. Nonetheless, a portion of these cases display a biphasic—or in rarer circumstances, multiphasic—progression, wherein an initial anaphylactic response to an offending agent is followed by a symptom-free interval and subsequent spontaneous symptom recurrence, absent further allergen contact. Such recurrent reactions predominantly arise within 24 hours post-exposure, though cases have been documented with relapses occurring as late as 78 hours afterwards. Estimates for the frequency of biphasic anaphylaxis show substantial variability, with figures spanning from 0.4% to 23.3%, although a majority of research suggests an incidence rate in the 4%–6% range. Given the risk of delayed symptom return, it is standard practice to observe patients for a period following the apparent resolution of anaphylactic symptoms. Despite this, inconsistencies exist in how biphasic anaphylaxis is defined, and approaches to management remain heterogeneous; recent debate has questioned the utility of corticosteroids in prevention of secondary reactions. Multiphasic anaphylaxis, marked by repeated episodes of recurrent symptoms, poses particular challenges concerning both therapeutic intervention and monitoring strategies. Here, we describe the case of a woman in her sixties who experienced a multiphasic anaphylactic reaction following clarithromycin administration.

Case presentation:

A woman in her sixth decade of life, who had a documented hypersensitivity to beta-lactam antibiotics, arrived at the emergency department (ED) experiencing anaphylaxis after ingesting 500 mg of oral clarithromycin.

Her medical background was notable for type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, psoriasis, and depression, and she weighed 104 kg. The medications she regularly used included metformin (500 mg twice daily), gliclazide (80 mg modified release, once daily), hydrochlorothiazide (25 mg once daily), omeprazole (20 mg once daily), and sertraline (100 mg once daily). The most recent dose of hydrochlorothiazide had been administered in the morning, more than 12 hours prior to her arrival at the ED.

In her adolescence, she had a mild allergic response to amoxicillin, characterized solely by facial swelling and without involvement of oropharyngeal tissues, skin rash, hypotension, or dyspnoea. Additionally, she had experienced a marked local reaction to a wasp sting previously, but this did not progress to systemic involvement. The current event represented her initial episode of anaphylaxis.

Five days before presenting to the ED, her general practitioner prescribed clindamycin for erysipelas. After taking clindamycin for five days, she developed a rash on her abdomen, details of which were not further specified. This rash was considered by her physician to be a delayed allergic reaction to clindamycin, prompting a change to clarithromycin—a medication to which she had not previously been exposed.

She later arrived at the ED complaining of pruritus, flushing, excessive sweating, restlessness, and a sensation of throat swelling. These symptoms began approximately 1 hour following ingestion of her first clarithromycin dose and intensified over time. She presented to the ED 1 hour after symptom onset. Upon arrival, her vital parameters indicated hypotension and hypoxaemia (non-invasive blood pressure: 74/50 mm Hg, peripheral oxygen saturation at 88% on room air, respiratory rate 22 breaths per minute). Physical examination revealed facial edema, newly developed widespread erythema and urticaria covering her whole body, as well as diaphoresis and agitation. There was no evidence of tongue or pharyngeal swelling, and both cardiac and pulmonary auscultation were unremarkable. Her blood glucose measured 12.7 mmol/L, and ECG demonstrated a sinus rhythm. Immediate treatment for a presumed severe allergic reaction was provided, which included an intramuscular dose of 0.3 mg epinephrine administered by auto-injector, as well as a slow intravenous injection of 2 mg clemastine according to institutional protocols for cutaneous reactions. She also received supplemental oxygen (15 L/min through a non-rebreather mask) and 500 mL of isotonic saline (0.9% sodium chloride) during her initial resuscitation. Supplemental oxygen was able to be discontinued after improvement with epinephrine, though it required reinitiation during later anaphylactic events. Clarithromycin therapy was stopped immediately; she received only the initial oral 500 mg dose and no further doses were administered.

Initially, the patient exhibited a favorable clinical response to the administered therapy, demonstrated by normalization of blood pressure as well as reductions in facial edema and erythema. She was subsequently transferred to the acute internal medicine ward, which allowed for intensified surveillance by specialized nursing personnel, albeit without continuous telemetry monitoring. Nevertheless, the patient's condition deteriorated despite this initial improvement, as she developed repeated manifestations of anaphylactic shock within the first 12 hours following admission. After experiencing the first recurrence of anaphylactic shock, she was moved to the intensive care unit (ICU) for enhanced observation. Each incident necessitated intramuscular epinephrine administration to achieve symptom resolution (figure 1), amounting to four total episodes of anaphylaxis, inclusive of her initial emergency department (ED) presentation. The first two doses of epinephrine were delivered using auto-injectors (0.3 mg per dose, intramuscularly), whereas the subsequent two administrations consisted of manually prepared 0.5 mg intramuscular doses. While the epinephrine auto-injectors available at this facility supply less than the 0.5 mg guideline-recommended dose, auto-injectors are nonetheless favored during urgent scenarios—such as initial ED stabilization—or when emergent interventions are rare, reducing delays from staff hesitation with less familiar medications. After the initial recurrence of anaphylaxis, corticosteroid therapy was added, acknowledging the established use of corticosteroids in attempting to prevent biphasic anaphylactic responses. The patient received 30 mg intravenous prednisone daily for 5 days, along with a one-time dose of 8 mg intravenous dexamethasone; this regimen reflects discrepancies between the anaphylaxis protocols of the general ward and ICU at this institution. Additionally, treatment included 2 mg intravenous clemastine administered twice to alleviate cutaneous manifestations, and an extra 500 mL of normal saline during the second anaphylactic episode. Notably, each subsequent episode presented with milder symptoms—progressing from initial episodes marked by profound hypoxemia, cyanosis, hypotension, and severe respiratory distress, to latter episodes characterized primarily by dyspnea, the reemergence of rash, and pruritus.

Early in the course of management, consultation with the allergology department was obtained for expert therapeutic input. Two primary alternative management strategies were evaluated. The first involved continuous intravenous epinephrine infusion as a prophylactic measure, aiming to prevent further anaphylactic episodes rather than treating symptoms once they emerged. The second considered a watchful waiting approach, predicated on the observation that symptom severity appeared to be diminishing, thereby allowing time for potential spontaneous resolution. Both options, however, were associated with significant risks, compounded by the absence of established literature to guide the management of such multiphasic presentations.

Outcome and follow-up:

In the end, neither of the proposed treatment strategies was required, as the patient experienced no additional symptom recurrence beyond this stage. Approximately 24 hours without further anaphylactic episodes elapsed before she was transferred to a regular care ward. She was released from the hospital following a total admission period of 3 days. On discharge, she was prescribed levocetirizine, an antihistamine, for ongoing management, and provided with an epinephrine auto-injector (0.3 mg), with instruction on its use. Since then, she has remained free from recurrent anaphylactic events.

Given the unusual and extended course of her illness, an investigation into possible underlying etiologies was undertaken. The findings from this assessment indicated a strong likelihood of systemic mastocytosis. However, a definitive diagnosis could not be established, as only two WHO minor criteria were met, and evaluation of major criteria was not possible owing to insufficient bone marrow biopsy material (refer to figure 2). Elevated tryptase concentrations were detected both during the third episode of anaphylaxis and at baseline; these values stayed elevated throughout subsequent monitoring with the allergologist, as documented in table 1.

Regrettably, the measurement of tryptase was omitted during the initial assessment following the first anaphylactic episodes, resulting in noncompliance with the standard protocol for tryptase testing intervals. According to current guidelines, tryptase should be measured at least twice, and preferably three times, at specific time points: an initial sample should be obtained as soon as possible—prioritizing patient care over sampling—followed by a second sample 1–2 hours (but within a maximum of 4 hours) after symptom onset, coinciding with peak tryptase concentrations, and a third sample no sooner than 24 hours after complete resolution of clinical symptoms to determine baseline values. In this reported case, the first tryptase assessment occurred 1 hour subsequent to the third anaphylactic reaction, with the second sample collected 27 hours after the most recent recurrence.

Allergy confirmation testing was not pursued, as the potential risk of triggering another severe anaphylactic event via skin testing or similar procedures was considered disproportionate, especially since avoidance of additional exposures is relatively straightforward. Additional notable observations consistent with systemic mastocytosis included hepatomegaly as detected by abdominal ultrasonography, as well as possible neuropsychiatric manifestations such as depression and memory impairment.

Discussion:

Anaphylaxis is generally recognized as a distinct clinical syndrome, marked by the abrupt emergence and swift escalation of symptoms that affect the airway, respiration, circulatory system, and/or gastrointestinal tract. Frequently, these symptoms are accompanied by skin or mucosal changes, such as urticaria, angioedema, or flushing. Although pinpointing a causative allergen can reinforce the clinical suspicion, in some instances, a definitive trigger cannot be identified.

More nuanced clinical scenarios, including biphasic or refractory anaphylaxis, lack standardized definitions, though recent initiatives have sought to clarify these through consensus statements. According to the Resuscitation Council UK, biphasic anaphylaxis is defined as the reappearance of symptoms hours after apparent resolution, without renewed allergen exposure. Distinguishing genuine biphasic events from persistent anaphylaxis showing only a temporary response to epinephrine, or resulting from protracted allergen absorption (such as with lingering food allergens or slow-release formulations), presents considerable diagnostic difficulty. In contrast, refractory anaphylaxis denotes a state where anaphylactic symptoms continue despite appropriate and correctly delivered epinephrine therapy.

Current guidelines on biphasic anaphylaxis mainly pertain to protocols for patient observation; nevertheless, there remains ongoing debate regarding the ideal observation period. Conventionally, a 4 to 6-hour window is recommended post-symptom resolution. The Dutch Nederlands Huisartsen Genootschap (NHG) and World Allergy Organisation guidelines both acknowledge that about 50% of biphasic reactions manifest within 6–12 hours after the initial episode, yet neither guideline specifies a definitive period for monitoring. Conversely, the UK guideline 2021 introduces a tiered, risk-based strategy for clinical observation. There remains a clear need for agreed-upon monitoring protocols to help reconcile the efficient use of healthcare resources—given the prevalence of single-phase anaphylaxis—with the imperative of ensuring patient safety for those at risk of complications due to biphasic reactions, who represent a smaller and less easily identified subset.

There is a conspicuous deficiency of guidance concerning advanced pharmacological approaches for managing biphasic and multiphasic anaphylactic reactions. The therapeutic approach for such cases generally aligns with standard anaphylaxis protocols, emphasizing the rapid administration of intramuscular epinephrine alongside suitable supportive interventions. Current guidelines advise a dose of 0.01 mg/kg of epinephrine, capped at a maximum of 0.5 mg. In clinical practice, this is frequently rounded up to 0.5 mg for adults, since most adults exceed 50 kg in body weight. Notably, however, many epinephrine auto-injectors, including those employed during the initial two anaphylactic events in our reported patient, provide only 0.3 mg of epinephrine, even when prescribed for adult use. While epinephrine auto-injectors are invaluable in prehospital emergencies, they can also expedite intervention and alleviate hesitancy among healthcare providers who are less accustomed to administering vasoactive medications like epinephrine, even in hospital settings. Nevertheless, the standard availability of 0.3 mg auto-injectors raises concerns about insufficient dosing in adults exceeding 50 kg. Evidence from a 2025 retrospective analysis demonstrated that individuals who received 0.3 mg as an initial epinephrine dose required more frequent escalation of therapy, such as further epinephrine administration, in contrast to those who were given an initial 0.5 mg dose. Addressing factors such as practitioner reliance on the traditional 0.3 mg dosing and the widespread prevalence of 0.3 mg auto-injectors, and encouraging adherence to the guideline-recommended dosages, may substantially enhance outcomes in anaphylaxis management.

Historically, corticosteroids were advocated for anaphylaxis management in part to mitigate the risk of biphasic reactions. Yet, growing research indicates not only a lack of benefit but also possible adverse outcomes, prompting a move away from routine corticosteroid use in treating anaphylaxis. A systematic review from 2017 surveyed 22 studies: only one demonstrated support for corticosteroid administration, while the remaining 21 did not find any significant reduction in biphasic anaphylaxis risk. Moreover, newer studies have highlighted potential side effects, with a 2019 investigation finding increased rates of ICU or hospital ward admissions following prehospital corticosteroid use. As a result, most up-to-date guidelines no longer recommend corticosteroids for standard care, despite their continued use in some clinical settings.

Although biphasic anaphylaxis is infrequent, its potential clinical impact is considerable. The lack of consistent predictors makes decisions about risk assessment and patient observation challenging. Factors such as delayed administration of epinephrine, pronounced initial symptoms, the need for multiple epinephrine doses, and history of prior biphasic episodes have been proposed as indicators, but none have proven reliable enough to facilitate individualized monitoring.

Systemic mastocytosis, characterized by excessive mast cell accumulation, heightened mediator release, and multiorgan inflammation, is recognized as a risk factor for severe anaphylaxis.\textsuperscript{1517} Nevertheless, due to the low incidence of both systemic mastocytosis and biphasic anaphylaxis, there are currently no studies specifically exploring the relationship between the two. Ongoing research into mast cell pathologies and their connection to biphasic anaphylaxis may offer meaningful advancements for future clinical management.

Anaphylaxis-related deaths are relatively uncommon, particularly in areas where emergency medical intervention is readily accessible. Fatal incidents of biphasic anaphylaxis occur even less frequently, despite the potential risks associated with this condition. Nonetheless, anaphylaxis continues to represent a frequent and urgent medical situation that necessitates swift intervention and vigilant observation. While much research and many clinical guidelines primarily concentrate on the immediate phase of emergency care, it is equally essential to emphasize ongoing medical management and surveillance post-initial treatment. Gaining deeper insights into complicated forms of anaphylaxis, such as biphasic presentations, may facilitate the development of more individualized, efficient, and resource-conscious strategies for managing anaphylaxis.

The infrequent occurrence of biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis, coupled with the challenges inherent in conducting research during emergencies, significantly impedes the establishment of thoroughly evidence-based approaches. In these contexts, consensus guidelines formulated by experts and featuring explicit clinical recommendations offer substantial value. However, producing such expert-driven guidance is limited by the scarcity of robust data. Given the uncommon nature of these multifaceted anaphylaxis cases, constructing a registry that captures key patient characteristics, proposed management protocols—like those outlined in this case report—and associated outcomes, could serve as a foundational resource for formulating empirically informed, expert-led recommendations.
