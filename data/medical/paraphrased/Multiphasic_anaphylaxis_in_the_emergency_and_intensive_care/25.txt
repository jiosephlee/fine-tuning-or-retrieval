Title: Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting

Margret Ann Schneider, Hans de Groot, Jan-Willem Geerts

Abstract:

Summary: Anaphylaxis represents the most severe type of allergic response and constitutes a life-threatening systemic emergency that demands rapid and appropriate intervention. In certain cases, symptoms reappear even after sufficient treatment and initial resolution. These biphasic or multiphasic patterns pose distinct challenges for healthcare providers, particularly given the wide range in reported incidence, ambiguous risk factors, and lack of established management protocols in existing guidelines. This issue is especially relevant for multiphasic anaphylaxis, characterized by repeated episodes of anaphylaxis following effective epinephrine administration. Given the uncommon nature of multiphasic anaphylaxis, individual case reports detailing clinical approaches are invaluable for providing guidance in these scenarios. Here, we report the case of a female patient in her 60s who experienced multiphasic anaphylaxis after clarithromycin exposure. Subsequent evaluation indicated a strong suspicion for systemic mastocytosis, though she did not fulfill definitive diagnostic criteria.

Background:

Anaphylaxis is a serious, often life-threatening allergic event—particularly when it manifests as anaphylactic shock—and frequently prompts individuals to seek emergency medical attention. Although typical cases involve a single, acute response to an allergenic stimulus, certain instances develop a biphasic or multiphasic pattern. This pattern is defined by an initial anaphylactic episode followed by a symptom-free period, after which clinical features reappear in the absence of further allergen exposure. Generally, symptom recurrence is documented within 24 hours after the original exposure, but cases have described late presentations up to 78 hours post-exposure. Estimates of biphasic anaphylaxis occurrence fluctuate considerably from 0.4% to 23.3%, although the majority of reports suggest rates between 4% and 6%. Because of the risk of secondary episodes, it is customary to observe patients after resolution of acute symptoms, even if recovery appears complete. However, the criteria for biphasic anaphylaxis are not always clearly defined, and clinical management recommendations differ; the efficacy of corticosteroids has been debated in recent years. Multiphasic anaphylaxis, involving several relapses of symptoms, poses unique challenges in relation to therapeutic strategies and monitoring periods. This report describes the case of a woman in her 60s who experienced a multiphasic anaphylactic response after exposure to clarithromycin.

Case presentation:

A woman in her sixties, with a documented beta-lactam antibiotic allergy, arrived at the emergency department experiencing anaphylaxis after taking 500 mg of oral clarithromycin.

Her medical history was notable for type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, psoriasis, and depression. She weighed 104 kg. Her current medications included metformin 500 mg twice daily, gliclazide retard 80 mg once daily, hydrochlorothiazide 25 mg once daily, omeprazole 20 mg once daily, and sertraline 100 mg once daily. Hydrochlorothiazide had last been taken more than 12 hours before her arrival at the ED.

Previously, she experienced a mild allergic reaction to amoxicillin during adolescence, characterized by facial swelling but no oropharyngeal involvement, rash, hypotension, or dyspnea. She also had a pronounced localized skin reaction to a wasp sting in the past, but did not develop systemic symptoms. This hospital admission marked her initial episode of anaphylaxis.

Five days prior to ED presentation, clindamycin was prescribed by her general practitioner for erysipelas. After taking clindamycin for five days, she developed an abdominal rash (with no further description given). The rash was interpreted as a delayed allergic response to clindamycin, prompting her physician to change her antibiotic regimen to clarithromycin, which she had not previously received.

She later arrived at the emergency department exhibiting pruritus, flushing, increased sweating, restlessness, and a sensation of throat swelling. These symptoms began one hour after ingesting her first oral dose of clarithromycin and progressively intensified. She presented for medical evaluation one hour after her symptoms started. On arrival, her vital signs were notable for hypotension and hypoxemia (non-invasive blood pressure of 74/50 mm Hg, oxygen saturation of 88% on room air, and respiratory rate of 22 breaths per minute). Physical examination revealed facial edema, new diffuse erythema, urticaria over the entire body, excessive sweating, and agitation. There was no evidence of tongue or throat swelling, and findings on cardiac and pulmonary auscultation were unremarkable. Her blood glucose measured 12.7 mmol/L, and the electrocardiogram demonstrated sinus rhythm. For presumed severe allergic reaction, treatment was initiated with intramuscular 0.3 mg epinephrine via auto-injector, along with a slow intravenous infusion of 2 mg clemastine for the cutaneous manifestations, in accordance with local protocols. She also received supplemental oxygen (15 L by non-rebreather mask) and 500 mL intravenous normal saline (0.9% sodium chloride) for initial stabilization. Oxygen supplementation was discontinued after a favorable response to epinephrine but required temporary re-administration with subsequent anaphylactic episodes. Clarithromycin therapy was halted and no further doses were administered following the initial 500 mg oral intake.

At first, the patient responded well to treatment: blood pressure normalized and both facial swelling and erythema diminished. She was admitted to the acute internal medicine ward, which offers enhanced surveillance by specialized nursing staff but does not use telemetry. Nonetheless, after this favorable initial response, the patient experienced multiple recurrences of symptoms characteristic of anaphylactic shock within the first 12 hours post-admission. Following the initial recurrence, she was transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) for closer observation. Each episode necessitated intramuscular epinephrine (see figure 1) and, in total, she experienced four episodes of anaphylaxis, including the original emergency department (ED) presentation. The first two epinephrine doses were delivered using an auto-injector (0.3 mg intramuscularly), whereas the subsequent two were administered as individually prepared 0.5 mg intramuscular doses. Although the 0.3 mg auto-injectors used at our facility provide a dose lower than the recommended 0.5 mg, they are preferred in urgent or unfamiliar settings, such as initial stabilization in the ED or in environments where prompt intervention for anaphylaxis is not routine, so as to avoid delays stemming from treatment hesitancy. Following the first recurrence of anaphylaxis, corticosteroids were introduced in line with their established role in attempting to prevent biphasic anaphylactic responses. She was given both prednisone 30 mg intravenously per day for 5 days and a single 8 mg intravenous dose of dexamethasone, owing to differing protocols between our hospital's general ward and ICU anaphylaxis guidelines. Additionally, she received two doses of intravenous clemastine (2 mg each) for dermatologic manifestations, as well as an extra 500 mL of normal saline during the second anaphylactic episode. Notably, each successive anaphylactic episode was less severe: the first involved profound hypoxaemia, cyanosis, hypotension, and severe dyspnoea, whereas the final episode was limited to dyspnoea, rash recurrence, and pruritus.

Early in the course, the allergology department was consulted for expert management recommendations. Two treatment alternatives were evaluated. One involved commencing a continuous intravenous epinephrine infusion to attempt to prevent further episodes, instead of waiting for symptoms to reappear. The other option was a watchful waiting strategy, especially since symptoms appeared to be diminishing in severity, to determine if spontaneous resolution would occur. Both strategies entailed significant risks, compounded by the absence of evidence-based guidance for managing multiphasic anaphylaxis.

Outcome and follow-up:

In the end, neither of the considered treatment options was required, as the patient experienced no recurrence of symptoms thereafter. Approximately 24 hours after her last anaphylactic episode, she was moved back to a regular care ward. She was discharged from the hospital after a total of 3 days as an inpatient. On discharge, she was prescribed levocetirizine, an antihistamine, as a regular medication, and instructed in the use of a 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injector. No subsequent anaphylactic episodes have been reported.

Given the unusual and extended nature of the patient’s presentation, further investigations into possible underlying conditions were undertaken. These investigations indicated a high likelihood of systemic mastocytosis; however, criteria for a definitive diagnosis were not met, as only two minor WHO criteria were present, and the major criterion could not be evaluated because the bone marrow biopsy provided insufficient material (refer to figure 2). Regrettably, tryptase measurements were not obtained during the evaluation of the initial anaphylactic events, resulting in noncompliance with recommended timing for tryptase assessment. Current guidelines advocate for at least two, preferably three, tryptase determinations collected at designated intervals: the first should be drawn as promptly as possible without impeding emergency management, a second sample between 1 and 2 hours after symptom onset (but within a 4-hour window), coinciding with the expected peak in tryptase, and a third sample at least 24 hours post complete symptom resolution to ascertain the baseline tryptase value. In this case, the earliest tryptase was sampled one hour following the third episode of anaphylaxis, while the subsequent sample occurred 27 hours after the last documented anaphylactic event.

No confirmatory allergy testing was conducted, as the potential risk of provoking a severe anaphylactic reaction with procedures such as skin testing was considered disproportionate, particularly given that further exposure could be readily avoided. Additional findings supporting a diagnosis of systemic mastocytosis included hepatomegaly identified on abdominal ultrasound, as well as neuropsychiatric symptoms, notably depression and reported memory disturbances.

Discussion:

Anaphylaxis is a clinically distinct condition, defined by the abrupt onset and swift escalation of symptoms affecting the airway, respiratory function, circulation, and/or gastrointestinal tract, and is usually associated with skin or mucosal signs such as urticaria, angioedema, or flushing. Identification of a probable allergen can help confirm the diagnosis, although a definitive trigger cannot always be ascertained.

Atypical cases, including biphasic and refractory anaphylaxis, are less clearly delineated, though recent work has sought to develop consensus criteria for these entities. According to the Resuscitation Council UK, biphasic anaphylaxis is characterised by the reappearance of symptoms several hours after resolution, without further exposure to the allergen. Distinguishing genuine biphasic reactions from prolonged anaphylaxis showing temporary improvement with epinephrine, or from ongoing allergen absorption (such as residual food ingestion or extended-release medications), remains a diagnostic challenge. In contrast, refractory anaphylaxis is defined as continuous anaphylactic symptoms that do not adequately respond to properly administered epinephrine.

Guidelines on biphasic anaphylaxis predominantly focus on clinical observation protocols. Nonetheless, there is continued debate regarding the ideal length of monitoring. Commonly recommended observation times range from 4 to 6 hours after symptoms abate. The Dutch Nederlands Huisartsen Genootschap (NHG) and World Allergy Organisation guidelines mention that up to half of biphasic events take place within 6–12 hours post-onset, yet neither offers a concrete recommendation for monitoring periods. Conversely, the 2021 UK guideline suggests a three-tier risk-based monitoring strategy. It is evident that harmonised approaches to observation are necessary in order to optimise resource allocation (given that most patients experience only a single anaphylactic episode) and ensure safety (addressing the risk of complications among the small and difficult-to-identify subset prone to biphasic reactions).

There is a conspicuous and concerning absence of specific guidance for advanced pharmacologic interventions in biphasic and multiphasic anaphylaxis. Management approaches for these anaphylactic patterns largely reflect established protocols for typical anaphylaxis, involving rapid intramuscular administration of epinephrine along with suitable supportive measures. Current guidelines advise a dose of 0.01 mg/kg body weight, not exceeding a total of 0.5 mg. Clinically, this is often rounded to a 0.5 mg dose for adults, given that most adults weigh more than 50 kg. Notably, many commercially available epinephrine auto-injectors—including those used in the initial two anaphylactic events described in our case—are formulated with only 0.3 mg of epinephrine, despite being designated for adult use. While auto-injectors are indispensable for prehospital care, they may also offer advantages in healthcare environments by reducing both the time to treatment and hesitation among staff less familiar with administering vasoactive substances such as epinephrine. Nevertheless, administering 0.3 mg via auto-injector often results in insufficient dosing for adults exceeding 50 kg, raising significant clinical concern. A retrospective study from 2025 found that patients who received an initial 0.3 mg dose of epinephrine required escalation of therapy—including further epinephrine dosing—significantly more frequently compared to those started on 0.5 mg. Addressing issues such as ingrained practitioner preferences for the long-recommended 0.3 mg dose and the widespread availability of 0.3 mg auto-injectors, as well as reinforcing adherence to dosing recommendations, may be pivotal to optimizing anaphylaxis management.

Historically, corticosteroids have been recommended in part to mitigate biphasic reactions. Nonetheless, accumulating data reveal a lack of efficacy and possible adverse effects, resulting in a recent trend away from routine steroid use in anaphylaxis management. A systematic review conducted in 2017 analyzed 22 studies; only one supported corticosteroid use, while the remaining 21 provided no evidence that corticosteroids decrease the likelihood of biphasic anaphylaxis. Moreover, recent investigations underscore concerns about adverse effects, with one study from 2019 reporting an increased risk of ICU or hospital ward admission following prehospital corticosteroid administration. As a result, the majority of current guidelines no longer support the routine use of corticosteroids, though they remain in practice in some settings.

Although biphasic anaphylaxis is uncommon, its clinical significance is considerable. The lack of dependable predictors creates challenges in stratifying risk and determining appropriate monitoring strategies. Proposed predictors include postponed epinephrine administration, initial severity of presentation, requirement for multiple epinephrine doses, and a history of biphasic reactions, yet none have proven reliable for tailored monitoring.

Systemic mastocytosis, characterized by abnormal mast cell proliferation, increased mediator release, and inflammation affecting multiple organs, is known to predispose individuals to severe anaphylaxis.1517 Despite this association, due to the infrequency of both systemic mastocytosis and biphasic anaphylaxis, there appears to be no published research directly evaluating the influence of systemic mastocytosis on biphasic anaphylaxis. Further studies focusing on mast cell disorders and their relationship with biphasic anaphylaxis could offer valuable guidance for future therapeutic approaches.

Anaphylaxis-related deaths are uncommon, particularly in areas where prompt emergency care is accessible. Additionally, mortality due to biphasic anaphylaxis is even less frequent, despite the condition’s perceived severity. Nevertheless, anaphylaxis continues to represent a frequent emergency situation necessitating immediate intervention and thorough observation. While much attention in research and guidelines centers around acute management, follow-up care and surveillance are also critical components. Improved knowledge of intricate presentations, such as biphasic anaphylaxis, may lead to more patient-focused, efficient approaches to anaphylaxis management.

The infrequency of biphasic—and particularly multiphasic—anaphylaxis, along with obstacles characteristic of emergency research, pose significant challenges to establishing evidence-based protocols. In these contexts, expert consensus statements offering structured management guidance remain invaluable, though generating such guidelines is complicated by limited available evidence. Given the unusual occurrence of these complex reactions, assembling a registry encompassing relevant patient characteristics, potential management recommendations, as highlighted in our report, and outcome measures, could serve as a foundation for expert-informed, data-based recommendations.
