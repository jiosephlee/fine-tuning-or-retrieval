Title: Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting

Margret Ann Schneider, Hans de Groot, Jan-Willem Geerts

Abstract:

Summary: Anaphylaxis represents the most dangerous manifestation of allergic reactions, posing a significant risk to life and demanding rapid, effective intervention. A subset of patients experience a return of symptoms after initial resolution, even following appropriate therapy, leading to what is termed biphasic or, on occasion, multiphasic anaphylaxis. Such cases present substantial challenges to healthcare providers, as rates of recurrence differ across reports, contributing risk factors are poorly defined, and standardized management protocols remain inadequate in existing guidelines. The complexity is further heightened in situations of multiphasic anaphylaxis, where individuals undergo repeated episodes of anaphylaxis despite demonstrating appropriate responses to epinephrine. Given the infrequency of multiphasic anaphylaxis, detailed case reports are valuable resources for guiding clinical practice. This report describes a female patient in her 60s who experienced multiphasic anaphylaxis following clarithromycin administration. Further evaluation raised a strong suspicion for systemic mastocytosis, although she did not fulfill established diagnostic criteria for the condition.

Background:

Anaphylaxis represents a serious, and particularly in cases involving anaphylactic shock, potentially fatal allergic response frequently encountered in emergency departments. While the majority of cases follow a monophasic course, a proportion demonstrate a biphasic or even multiphasic pattern, which involves an initial anaphylactic episode triggered by allergen exposure, subsequent symptom resolution with a symptom-free interval, and later recurrence of symptoms in the absence of additional allergen contact. Such recurrent episodes typically arise within 24 hours following the primary event, although delayed recurrences up to 78 hours have been documented. The incidence of biphasic anaphylaxis is reported to range considerably from 0.4% to 23.3%, with the majority of studies indicating a rate between 4% and 6%. Because of the risk for secondary reactions, patients are commonly observed for a period after apparent resolution of anaphylactic symptoms. Nevertheless, definitions of biphasic anaphylaxis can differ, leading to variability in recommended management strategies; recent discussions have also questioned the efficacy of corticosteroids in prevention. Multiphasic anaphylaxis, wherein several discrete recurrences of symptoms occur, poses particular difficulties regarding therapeutic approach and duration of observation. Here, we describe the case of a female patient in her 60s who developed a multiphasic anaphylactic response to clarithromycin.

Case presentation:

A female patient in her 60s with a documented beta-lactam antibiotic allergy arrived at the emergency department (ED) experiencing anaphylaxis after ingesting oral clarithromycin 500 mg.

Her medical background comprised type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, psoriasis, and depression. She weighed 104 kg. Her regular medications included metformin 500 mg twice daily, gliclazide retard 80 mg once daily, hydrochlorothiazide 25 mg daily, omeprazole 20 mg daily, and sertraline 100 mg daily. The most recent intake of hydrochlorothiazide had occurred more than 12 hours before her arrival at the ED.

During her teenage years, the patient had an episode of mild allergic reaction to amoxicillin, limited to facial edema, with no involvement of the oropharynx, rash, hypotension, or shortness of breath. Additionally, she had previously experienced a marked localized skin response following a wasp sting, which was not accompanied by systemic manifestations. The current incident represented her initial encounter with anaphylaxis.

Five days prior to presenting at the ED, she was prescribed clindamycin for erysipelas by her primary care physician. After five days on clindamycin, she developed an abdominal rash (not described further), which was considered a delayed allergic reaction to the drug. Subsequently, her general practitioner changed her antibiotic therapy to clarithromycin, which she had not taken previously.

She later arrived at the emergency department reporting itching, flushing, excessive sweating, restlessness, and a sensation of swelling in her throat. These complaints began one hour after she consumed her first dose of clarithromycin orally and progressively intensified. She sought medical attention an hour following the onset of symptoms. Upon arrival, her initial vital signs indicated low blood pressure and reduced oxygen levels (non-invasive blood pressure of 74/50 mm Hg, peripheral oxygen saturation of 88% on room air, respiratory rate of 22 breaths per minute). Physical examination at presentation revealed swelling of the face, newly developed widespread redness and hives throughout the body, increased sweating, and agitation. There was no evidence of tongue or throat swelling, and both cardiac and pulmonary auscultation were unremarkable. Her blood glucose measured 12.7 mmol/L, and ECG demonstrated sinus rhythm. She was immediately managed for a suspected severe allergic reaction with an intramuscular dose of 0.3 mg epinephrine administered via auto-injector and a slow intravenous dose of 2 mg clemastine, targeting her cutaneous manifestations in accordance with local protocols. Supplemental oxygen at 15 L via a non-rebreather mask and 500 mL of 0.9% sodium chloride solution were also provided during initial resuscitation. Oxygen therapy could be discontinued after a favorable response to epinephrine, although it needed to be temporarily resumed during additional episodes of anaphylaxis. Clarithromycin was stopped and no further doses were given beyond the single initial oral dose of 500 mg.

At the outset, the patient responded favorably to the initial therapy, as evidenced by normalization of blood pressure and a reduction in facial edema and erythema. Following this, the patient was transferred to the acute internal ward, allowing for enhanced surveillance by specialized nursing personnel, but without the use of telemetry monitoring. Despite these improvements, within the first 12 hours after admission, the patient experienced repeated bouts of symptoms consistent with anaphylactic shock. Upon the first recurrence, the patient was moved to the intensive care unit (ICU) for closer observation. Each episode necessitated intramuscular epinephrine administration to alleviate symptoms (figure 1). In total, the patient endured four episodes of anaphylaxis, counting the initial emergency department (ED) event. The first two doses of epinephrine were delivered via auto-injector, each containing 0.3 mg intramuscularly, while the subsequent two episodes were managed with individually prepared intramuscular injections of 0.5 mg epinephrine. Although the auto-injectors used at this facility contain a lower dose (0.3 mg) than the 0.5 mg commonly recommended, they are preferred in situations requiring rapid intervention—such as initial patient stabilization in the ED—or in areas where emergent treatments like anaphylaxis are infrequent and hesitation due to unfamiliarity may lead to delays. 

Following the first recurrence, adjunct therapy with corticosteroids was initiated owing to their traditional use in preventing biphasic anaphylactic reactions. The patient received a daily intravenous dose of 30 mg prednisone for five days and a single intravenous dose of 8 mg dexamethasone. These differing regimens reflect variations between the hospital’s general ward and ICU anaphylaxis protocols. Additionally, clemastine was administered intravenously at a dose of 2 mg twice to address skin manifestations, and 500 mL of normal saline was provided during the second anaphylactic episode. Notably, symptom severity declined with each successive event, ranging from pronounced hypoxemia accompanied by cyanosis, hypotension, and significant dyspnea initially, to milder manifestations such as dyspnea, rash recurrence, and pruritus in the final episode.

An early consultation with the department of allergology was sought for expert guidance regarding further management. Two alternative strategies were contemplated: the first involved continuous intravenous infusion of epinephrine as a preventive measure rather than waiting for another symptom relapse; the second option, given the diminishing symptom severity, considered observation to assess for spontaneous resolution. Both strategies carried substantial risks, complicated by the absence of existing literature to guide therapy in cases involving multiphasic anaphylaxis.

Outcome and follow-up:

In the end, no additional therapeutic interventions were required since the patient did not experience recurrence of symptoms following this period. After about 24 hours free from further anaphylactic events, she was moved back to a general ward. She was discharged after being hospitalized for 3 days. Upon discharge, the patient was prescribed levocetirizine, an antihistamine, to be taken regularly, and provided with a 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injector, along with instructions on its proper use. She has not had any subsequent episodes of anaphylaxis.

Given the unusual and extended progression, more extensive evaluation for potential underlying etiologies was pursued. This assessment indicated a strong likelihood of systemic mastocytosis; however, the findings did not satisfy the formal diagnostic criteria (with only two WHO minor criteria fulfilled, and major criterion unable to be evaluated owing to limited bone marrow biopsy specimen—see figure 2). Regrettably, serum tryptase was not measured during the initial assessment following the first anaphylactic reactions, resulting in deviation from the suggested timing for tryptase evaluation. Current guidelines advise collection of at least two, preferably three, tryptase measurements at designated time points: the first sample should be obtained promptly, provided this does not hinder urgent management, a second sample is collected 1–2 hours (but within 4 hours) after symptom onset to coincide with expected peak levels, and a third sample is taken at least 24 hours after complete clinical recovery to determine baseline levels. In this case, the initial tryptase measurement was obtained 1 hour post-third anaphylactic episode, and the subsequent sample was drawn 27 hours following the final episode.

No allergy confirmation testing, such as skin tests, was performed, since provoking another severe anaphylactic reaction was considered disproportionate given the relative simplicity of avoiding re-exposure. Additional pertinent findings consistent with systemic mastocytosis included hepatomegaly seen on abdominal ultrasound and potential neuropsychiatric manifestations such as depression and memory impairment.

Discussion:

Anaphylaxis is recognized as a clearly delineated clinical diagnosis, marked by abrupt onset and swiftly escalating symptoms that affect the airway, respiratory, circulatory, or gastrointestinal systems, commonly presenting alongside skin or mucosal signs such as urticaria, angioedema, or flushing. Although identifying an associated allergen can aid in establishing the diagnosis, pinpointing a specific trigger is not always achievable.

Less straightforward forms, including biphasic or refractory anaphylaxis, are not as precisely characterized, though recent initiatives have sought to create standardized definitions. According to the Resuscitation Council UK, biphasic anaphylaxis refers to the recurrence of symptoms several hours after initial resolution, without additional exposure to the allergen. Distinguishing genuine biphasic reactions from prolonged anaphylaxis with transient improvement from epinephrine or continuous allergen absorption (such as from remaining food particles or slowly released medications) proves to be complex. Conversely, refractory anaphylaxis pertains to ongoing anaphylactic symptoms despite proper administration of epinephrine, with insufficient or absent response.

Recommendations in guidelines about biphasic anaphylaxis mainly revolve around monitoring patients clinically. However, the ideal length of observation remains uncertain. Historically, periods of 4 to 6 hours of monitoring following resolution of symptoms have been standard. The Dutch Nederlands Huisartsen Genootschap (NHG) and the World Allergy Organisation guideline report that roughly 50% of biphasic cases manifest within 6–12 hours of the primary episode, yet neither endorses a definitive observation duration. By comparison, the UK guideline from 2021 proposes a three-tiered monitoring system based on risk stratification. Establishing universal protocols for monitoring is vital to effectively allocate healthcare resources for the majority experiencing single-episode anaphylaxis, while also safeguarding those at risk for complications from the less common and harder to identify biphasic reactions.

There is a concerning deficiency of guidance regarding advanced pharmacological approaches for biphasic and multiphasic anaphylactic reactions. The therapeutic approach to these cases typically parallels that of uncomplicated anaphylaxis, involving immediate intramuscular administration of epinephrine in conjunction with suitable supportive measures. Guidelines stipulate an epinephrine dose of 0.01 mg/kg, capped at a total of 0.5 mg. For adults—most of whom exceed 50 kg—this is routinely rounded to a flat 0.5 mg dose. Nonetheless, it is noteworthy that many auto-injectors used, such as those administered for the initial two anaphylactic events in the current report, supply only 0.3 mg of epinephrine, even when intended for adult patients. Although epinephrine auto-injectors are indispensable in prehospital care, their utility extends into clinical environments by minimizing the time to treatment and mitigating reluctance among healthcare workers who infrequently use potent vasoactive medications like epinephrine. Nevertheless, reliance on the 0.3 mg auto-injector can result in substantial underdosing for adults exceeding 50 kg. Evidence from a 2025 retrospective analysis found that those initially treated with 0.3 mg of epinephrine required escalation measures—such as repeat doses—significantly more often compared to individuals given the recommended 0.5 mg initially. Overcoming obstacles—including practitioner habituation to the long-standing 0.3 mg dose and the prevalent accessibility of 0.3 mg auto-injectors—and strengthening adherence to established dosing recommendations may substantially enhance outcomes in anaphylaxis management.

Historically, corticosteroids were advised partly to mitigate biphasic reactions. Nevertheless, growing evidence suggests not only a lack of benefit but also possible adverse outcomes, prompting a move away from routine steroid use in anaphylaxis. A 2017 systematic review analyzed 22 studies; just one indicated support for corticosteroid efficacy, while 21 failed to show they decrease the risk of biphasic anaphylaxis. Additionally, recent literature highlights the importance of potential side effects, with one 2019 study demonstrating increased rates of ICU or ward admissions following prehospital corticosteroid administration. As a result, most current guidelines recommend against regular use of corticosteroids, though they remain common in practice.

While biphasic anaphylaxis is infrequent, its clinical significance is substantial. Risk stratification and decisions regarding patient monitoring are hampered by the lack of dependable predictors. Proposed risk factors include delayed administration of epinephrine, an initially severe episode, requirement for multiple doses of epinephrine, and history of previous biphasic reactions. Yet, predictors that would reliably facilitate targeted monitoring strategies are still lacking.

Systemic mastocytosis is characterized by an abnormal proliferation of mast cells, heightened mediator release, and involvement of multiple organ systems, predisposing individuals to severe anaphylactic events. However, because both conditions are uncommon, there is currently no published research directly exploring the impact of systemic mastocytosis on biphasic anaphylaxis. Additional investigations focused on mast cell disorders and biphasic anaphylaxis are warranted and could enhance future treatment strategies.

Fortunately, the risk of death from anaphylaxis is relatively low, particularly in areas where rapid emergency medical services are accessible. In addition, fatal cases resulting from biphasic anaphylaxis are even less frequent, despite their seemingly significant risk. Nevertheless, even with these comforting numbers, anaphylaxis remains a frequently encountered emergency that requires immediate intervention and close observation. Although much research and many guidelines emphasize the initial phase of emergency care, ongoing monitoring and post-emergency management are equally critical. Improved knowledge of intricate forms of anaphylaxis, such as biphasic reactions, may allow for more precise, patient-focused, and resource-effective approaches to anaphylaxis care.

Given the infrequency of biphasic and particularly multiphasic anaphylaxis, as well as the inherent obstacles to conducting research during acute emergencies, establishing robust evidence-based management protocols is quite challenging. In these scenarios, consensus recommendations based on expert opinion, complete with explicit clinical guidelines, are extremely useful; nevertheless, generating such recommendations is hindered by insufficient available data. Considering how uncommon these complex anaphylactic events are, compiling a registry that documents pertinent case details—including patient characteristics, suggested management strategies (such as those described in our report), and outcomes—could form the foundation for evidence-informed, expert consensus recommendations.
