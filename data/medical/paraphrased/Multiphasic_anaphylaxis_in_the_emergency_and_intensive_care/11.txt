Title: Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting

Margret Ann Schneider, Hans de Groot, Jan-Willem Geerts

Abstract:

Summary: Anaphylaxis represents the most critical type of allergic reaction, capable of causing systemic symptoms that can endanger life and necessitate immediate, decisive intervention. In a proportion of patients, symptoms may recur after initial resolution, despite appropriate treatment and apparent clinical recovery. These so-called biphasic, or on occasion multiphasic, presentations pose significant challenges for healthcare providers, as incidence rates are inconsistent, contributing risk factors remain uncertain, and clear, evidence-based guidelines for management are lacking. This difficulty is particularly pronounced with multiphasic anaphylaxis, where patients experience repeated episodes of anaphylaxis even after an appropriate response to first-line therapy, such as epinephrine. Given the infrequency with which multiphasic anaphylaxis is observed, individual case reports serve as valuable resources, shedding light on potential approaches to management for this atypical clinical entity. Herein, we describe a case involving a female patient in her 60s who experienced multiphasic anaphylaxis following exposure to clarithromycin. Further evaluation indicated a high probability of underlying systemic mastocytosis, although she did not fulfill the strict diagnostic criteria for this condition.

Background:

Anaphylaxis represents a serious allergic reaction that can be fatal, particularly in cases of anaphylactic shock, and is frequently seen in emergency departments. A subset of these patients may experience a biphasic or even multiphasic pattern, which is characterized by an initial anaphylactic event following exposure to a specific trigger, subsequent symptom improvement with a symptom-free interval, and later recurrence of symptoms despite no further contact with the allergen. While this secondary response most often manifests within the first 24 hours after exposure, there are documented cases with recurrences as late as 78 hours. The published incidence rates of biphasic anaphylaxis are highly variable, from 0.4% to 23.3%, though the majority of evidence suggests it occurs in 4–6% of cases. Given the risk of symptom recurrence, observation is usually recommended following resolution of anaphylactic episodes. Nevertheless, inconsistencies exist in the definitions applied to biphasic anaphylaxis and the recommendations in treatment protocols differ, with recent questions raised concerning the efficacy of corticosteroids. Multiphasic anaphylaxis, involving more than one episode of symptom recurrence, poses particular difficulties in both management and decisions about monitoring. Here, we describe a case involving a woman in her 60s who developed a multiphasic anaphylactic response to clarithromycin.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 60s, with a known allergy to beta-lactam antibiotics, presented to the emergency department (ED) with anaphylaxis following oral clarithromycin 500 mg.

The patient’s past medical history was significant for type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, psoriasis, and depression. She weighed 104 kg. Her ongoing medications included metformin 500 mg twice daily, gliclazide retard 80 mg once daily, hydrochlorothiazide 25 mg once daily, omeprazole 20 mg once daily, and sertraline 100 mg once daily. She had taken her last dose of hydrochlorothiazide in the morning, more than 12 hours prior to arrival at the emergency department.

As an adolescent, the patient had experienced a mild allergic reaction to amoxicillin, manifesting as facial swelling without involvement of the oropharynx, skin rash, hypotension, or respiratory distress. She had also had a pronounced localized cutaneous response to a wasp sting in the past but did not exhibit systemic features. The current admission marked her initial experience with anaphylaxis.

Five days preceding her presentation to the emergency department, she was started on clindamycin by her general practitioner to treat erysipelas. On the fifth day of therapy, she noticed an abdominal rash that was not otherwise characterized. This was regarded as a delayed allergic reaction to clindamycin, leading her general practitioner to switch her antibiotic treatment to clarithromycin, a drug she had never previously received.

She later arrived at the emergency department complaining of itching, flushing, increased sweating, restlessness, and a sensation of throat swelling. Her symptoms began approximately one hour following oral ingestion of her first clarithromycin dose, with progressive worsening over time. She presented to medical services one hour after symptom onset. On arrival, her vital signs revealed low blood pressure and hypoxemia (non-invasive blood pressure measured at 74/50 mm Hg, peripheral oxygen saturation of 88% on room air, and a respiratory rate of 22 breaths per minute). Examination identified facial edema, newly developed generalized erythema and urticaria covering her body, in addition to heightened perspiration and restlessness. Swelling was not detected in the tongue or throat, and heart and lung auscultation were unremarkable. Capillary blood glucose was recorded at 12.7 mmol/L, and her ECG showed sinus rhythm. She immediately received management for suspected severe allergic reaction, including a 0.3 mg intramuscular epinephrine injection via auto-injector and a slow intravenous injection of 2 mg clemastine in accordance with local treatment guidelines for skin manifestations. Furthermore, she was administered supplemental oxygen (15 L via non-rebreather mask) and received a 500 mL infusion of normal saline (0.9% sodium chloride solution) as part of the initial stabilization process. Supplemental oxygen was discontinued following favorable response to epinephrine, though temporary reinstatement was necessary during subsequent anaphylactic recurrences. Clarithromycin was stopped, with no further doses administered beyond the initial 500 mg oral dose.

The initial response to treatment was favorable, with normalization of blood pressure and a reduction in facial swelling and erythema. The patient was transferred to the acute internal medicine ward, where more frequent observation by specialized nursing staff was available, though without continuous telemetry monitoring. Despite this early improvement, the patient experienced multiple recurrent episodes of symptoms consistent with anaphylactic shock within the first 12 hours of hospitalization. Following the first recurrence, the patient was moved to the intensive care unit (ICU) for intensified observation. Resolution of each episode required intramuscular epinephrine administration (figure 1), with four total anaphylactic events including the initial emergency department presentation. The initial two doses of epinephrine (0.3 mg each) were given via auto-injector, while subsequent episodes were managed with individually prepared intramuscular doses of 0.5 mg. Although the auto-injectors at our facility deliver a dose lower than the recommended 0.5 mg, they are preferred in urgent situations—such as acute stabilization in the emergency department or in less routine circumstances—where hesitation due to unfamiliarity might delay critical interventions. Corticosteroid therapy was initiated after the first recurrence, in keeping with the traditional role of corticosteroids to prevent biphasic anaphylactic reactions. The patient received intravenous prednisone 30 mg daily for five days as well as a single intravenous dose of dexamethasone 8 mg, in accordance with varying recommendations between the general and ICU anaphylaxis protocols at our hospital. Additionally, clemastine 2 mg IV was administered twice to address cutaneous symptoms, and a further 500 mL of normal saline was infused during the second anaphylactic episode. Notably, the severity of reactions diminished with each episode, with manifestations progressing from marked hypoxemia with cyanosis, hypotension, and severe dyspnea initially to milder symptoms such as dyspnea, recurrent rash, and pruritus during the last event.

Early in the course, consultation with the allergology department was sought for expert input. Two alternative management strategies were evaluated: first, continuous intravenous epinephrine infusion to prevent anaphylactic episodes before recurrence; and second, an observational approach to assess whether symptoms would abate on their own as the severity appeared to lessen over time. Both strategies entailed considerable risks, particularly in the absence of clear literature guidance for management of multiphasic anaphylaxis.

Outcome and follow-up:

In the end, neither therapeutic option was required, as the patient did not experience any recurrence of symptoms beyond this time. After remaining free of new anaphylactic episodes for approximately 24 hours, she was moved back to a regular care unit. She was discharged from the hospital after a 3-day admission period. The antihistamine levocetirizine was introduced as part of her regular medications, and she was provided with a 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injector along with instructions for its use. Since then, she has not had additional anaphylactic events.

Given the unusual and extended progression of her condition, further workup was undertaken to explore potential underlying causes. This evaluation indicated a strong likelihood of systemic mastocytosis; however, a definitive diagnosis was not possible because only two minor WHO criteria were met, and the major criterion could not be assessed due to limited bone marrow biopsy sampling (refer to figure 2). Regrettably, tryptase measurements were not obtained during the initial assessment of the first episodes of anaphylaxis, resulting in non-compliance with the guideline-recommended timing for tryptase evaluation. Current guidance advocates for collecting at least two, ideally three, tryptase samples at defined time points: the first should be drawn as early as possible without delaying therapy; the second between 1 and 2 hours after symptom onset (but no later than 4 hours), to capture the expected tryptase peak; and a third sample at least 24 hours after the complete resolution of symptoms to determine baseline values. In this reported case, however, the first tryptase was sampled one hour following the third episode of anaphylaxis, and the second sample was drawn 27 hours after the final recurrence.

No confirmatory allergy testing was conducted, as the potential for inducing another severe anaphylactic reaction through procedures such as skin testing was considered an undue risk, especially given the manageable avoidance of further allergen exposure. Additional notable observations supporting the diagnosis of systemic mastocytosis included hepatomegaly detected via abdominal ultrasonography and possible neuropsychiatric manifestations such as depression and memory impairment.

Discussion:

Anaphylaxis is generally recognized as a distinct clinical entity, defined by an abrupt onset and rapid escalation of symptoms affecting the airway, respiration, circulatory system, and/or gastrointestinal tract, often associated with skin or mucosal changes such as urticaria, angioedema, or flushing. Although finding a plausible allergen can help establish the diagnosis, a specific cause is not always identifiable.

More complicated scenarios like biphasic or refractory anaphylaxis lack such clear definitions, though recent initiatives have sought to create standard criteria for these presentations. According to the Resuscitation Council UK, biphasic anaphylaxis is described as the recurrence of symptoms several hours after apparent symptom resolution, without additional exposure to the allergen. It is often difficult to distinguish a true biphasic event from ongoing anaphylaxis with only temporary improvement following epinephrine administration or from continued allergen absorption (for instance, lingering food allergen or slowly absorbed medications). In contrast, refractory anaphylaxis is defined as persistent anaphylactic symptoms that do not resolve or show inadequate improvement despite correctly administered epinephrine.

Recommendations in current guidelines regarding biphasic anaphylaxis focus mainly on the need for clinical observation, though the ideal length of monitoring remains contentious. Historically, observation intervals of 4 to 6 hours after symptom resolution have been suggested. Guidelines from the Nederlands Huisartsen Genootschap (NHG) and the World Allergy Organisation note that around half of all biphasic reactions arise within 6–12 hours post-initial presentation, but neither offers explicit guidance on the duration of monitoring. The 2021 UK guideline instead introduces a three-tier risk-based model for patient monitoring. There is a clear need for standardized monitoring protocols that adequately balance the efficient use of healthcare resources (given the large cohort experiencing only single-phase anaphylaxis) with the imperative to safeguard the subset of patients at risk for rare but severe biphasic episodes.

There is a clear and concerning absence of specific recommendations regarding advanced medication protocols for biphasic and multiphasic anaphylactic episodes. The treatment of such courses generally aligns with conventional anaphylaxis management, which involves immediate administration of intramuscular epinephrine and appropriate supportive measures. Guideline-directed dosing for epinephrine is 0.01 mg/kg of body weight, with an upper limit of 0.5 mg per dose. In practice, this is typically rounded to 0.5 mg for adults, as most adults surpass the 50 kg weight threshold. Notably, a significant number of commercially available auto-injectors—including those administered during the initial two anaphylactic events in this report—deliver only 0.3 mg of epinephrine per dose, even when labeled for adult use. Auto-injectors have a pivotal function in community settings and can also expedite therapy in medical environments, helping to limit delays and reduce provider reluctance in the use of vasoactive agents such as epinephrine. Nonetheless, reliance on a 0.3 mg auto-injector, though widespread, may result in substantial underdosing in adults weighing more than 50 kg. Data from a 2025 retrospective analysis demonstrated that patients who initially received a 0.3 mg dose required subsequent escalation of care, including additional epinephrine administration, at a significantly higher rate compared to those first given 0.5 mg. Addressing obstacles such as healthcare provider familiarity with the traditional 0.3 mg dose and the prevalent availability of 0.3 mg auto-injectors, while promoting adherence to recommended epinephrine dosages, may markedly enhance anaphylaxis management.

Historically, corticosteroids were advocated in part to mitigate the risk of biphasic reactions. Nonetheless, accumulating data indicate they confer no benefit and may actually cause harm, prompting a recent trend away from prescribing steroids for anaphylaxis. A systematic review published in 2017 evaluated 22 studies: only one demonstrated any support for corticosteroid use, while 21 showed no evidence that corticosteroids reduce biphasic anaphylaxis risk. Additionally, more recent research has highlighted the significance of side effects, with a 2019 study reporting an increased probability of ICU or hospital admission following prehospital administration of corticosteroids. As a result, the majority of updated guidelines now recommend against the routine use of corticosteroids, although they are still commonly administered in real-world practice.

Biphasic anaphylaxis, though uncommon, carries notable clinical importance. The lack of consistent predictors poses challenges for risk assessment and surveillance planning. Factors suggested as potential predictors include delayed epinephrine delivery, severe manifestations at onset, necessity for multiple doses of epinephrine, and a history of biphasic reactions. Yet, no definitive predictors are available to reliably guide individualized monitoring.

Systemic mastocytosis is characterized by abnormal mast cell proliferation, heightened mediator release, and widespread organ inflammation, all of which increase susceptibility to severe anaphylactic episodes.1517 However, as both disorders are infrequent, there appears to be a lack of dedicated research evaluating the impact of systemic mastocytosis on biphasic anaphylaxis. Further investigation into mast cell pathologies and their connection with biphasic anaphylaxis may yield important information for future clinical management.

Fortunately, the risk of death from anaphylaxis is low, particularly in areas where emergency medical services are readily accessible. Moreover, fatalities resulting from biphasic anaphylactic reactions are even more uncommon, despite their ostensibly higher risk. Nonetheless, despite these positive figures, anaphylaxis continues to be a frequent medical emergency requiring immediate intervention and close observation. Although much of the research and existing guidelines center on acute emergency response, ongoing medical assessment and follow-up are equally significant. A more comprehensive grasp of atypical anaphylactic patterns, such as biphasic reactions, may allow for more individualized, efficient, and targeted management of anaphylaxis.

Due to the infrequency of biphasic and, especially, multiphasic anaphylaxis, along with the inherent challenges of studying emergency scenarios, formulating evidence-based management approaches is particularly difficult. In such instances, guidelines based on expert consensus, offering specific management directions, serve as an essential resource. Nonetheless, the formulation of these recommendations is similarly challenged by a scarcity of robust clinical data. Considering the uncommon nature of these complicated anaphylactic presentations, establishing a registry to record such cases—including key patient details, proposed management options (such as those highlighted in our case), and outcomes—could form the foundation for creating data-informed, expert-driven guidelines.
