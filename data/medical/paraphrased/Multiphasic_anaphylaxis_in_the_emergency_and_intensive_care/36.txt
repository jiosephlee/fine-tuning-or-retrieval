Title: Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting

Margret Ann Schneider, Hans de Groot, Jan-Willem Geerts

Abstract:

Anaphylaxis represents the most critical form of allergic reaction and necessitates immediate and effective intervention due to its potential to threaten life. In some patients, symptoms can re-emerge after initial stabilization and appropriate therapy, resulting in biphasic or even multiphasic episodes. These atypical clinical courses pose considerable difficulties for healthcare providers, particularly as the reported frequency of recurrence varies, associated risk factors remain uncertain, and evidence-based management recommendations in the literature are insufficient. This challenge is heightened when managing cases of multiphasic anaphylaxis, characterized by several episodes of anaphylactic relapse in spite of appropriate administration of epinephrine and initial therapeutic success. Given that multiphasic anaphylaxis is relatively uncommon, published case reports serve as valuable resources for understanding practical approaches to this issue. Here, we describe the clinical management of a female patient in her sixth decade who developed multiphasic anaphylaxis following exposure to clarithromycin; subsequent evaluation indicated a strong probability of underlying systemic mastocytosis, although formal diagnostic criteria were not met.

Background:

Anaphylaxis represents a serious, and in the case of anaphylactic shock, potentially fatal allergic response, frequently leading patients to seek emergency medical attention. Some cases, however, exhibit a biphasic or even multiphasic progression, which is characterized by an initial reaction to an allergenic stimulus, subsequent remission of symptoms—resulting in a symptom-free interval—and then the reappearance of clinical manifestations in the absence of further allergen exposure. This pattern generally occurs within 24 hours following the initial trigger, though there are documented instances of symptom relapse up to 78 hours post-exposure. Published rates of biphasic anaphylaxis are highly variable, spanning from 0.4% to 23.3%; nevertheless, the majority of evidence suggests an incidence between 4% and 6%. Because of the risk of symptom recurrence, monitoring after resolution of anaphylactic symptoms is typically recommended. Nevertheless, criteria for biphasic anaphylaxis lack uniformity and management protocols differ, with recent debate regarding the utility of corticosteroids. Multiphasic anaphylaxis, distinguished by repeated episodes of symptom return, poses unique challenges for both observation and therapeutic strategies. This report details the case of a female patient in her 60s who developed a multiphasic anaphylactic reaction to clarithromycin.

Case presentation:

A female patient in her 60s, with a documented beta-lactam antibiotic allergy, arrived at the emergency department (ED) experiencing anaphylaxis after taking 500 mg of oral clarithromycin.

Her past medical history was notable for type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, psoriasis, and depression. She weighed 104 kg. Her current medications included metformin (500 mg twice daily), gliclazide (80 mg extended release once daily), hydrochlorothiazide (25 mg once daily), omeprazole (20 mg once daily), and sertraline (100 mg once daily). The last dose of hydrochlorothiazide had been administered that morning, more than 12 hours prior to ED arrival.

In adolescence, she had a mild allergic response to amoxicillin, characterized by facial swelling but no involvement of the oropharynx, and no development of rash, hypotension, or dyspnoea. Additionally, she had previously exhibited a pronounced localized skin reaction to a wasp sting, but no systemic features were reported. The incident described here was her initial episode of anaphylaxis.

Five days prior to presenting at the ED, she was prescribed clindamycin by her general practitioner for erysipelas. After five days of clindamycin, she noticed a rash on her abdomen (not otherwise detailed). This was assessed as a delayed allergic reaction to clindamycin, prompting her general practitioner to discontinue clindamycin and initiate clarithromycin, which she had not taken before.

She later arrived at the emergency department complaining of itching, flushing, excessive sweating, restlessness, and a sensation of throat swelling. These symptoms began approximately one hour after ingesting her first oral dose of clarithromycin and progressively intensified. She presented for medical attention one hour after the symptoms first appeared. Upon arrival, her vital signs revealed low blood pressure and reduced oxygen saturation (non-invasive blood pressure measured at 74/50 mm Hg, oxygen saturation at 88% on ambient air, and a respiratory rate of 22 breaths per minute). The initial physical examination identified swelling of the face, new generalized erythema with urticaria spread across her entire body, increased perspiration, and agitation. No tongue or pharyngeal swelling was detected, and heart and lung sounds were unremarkable. Laboratory testing showed a blood glucose of 12.7 mmol/L and an electrocardiogram indicating sinus rhythm. She was immediately managed for a suspected severe allergic reaction with a 0.3 mg intramuscular injection of epinephrine administered via auto-injector, along with a slow intravenous infusion of 2 mg clemastine, in accordance with local treatment protocols for cutaneous manifestations. She was also provided with supplemental oxygen at 15 L/minute via a non-rebreather mask and received 500 mL of normal saline (0.9% sodium chloride solution) during the initial resuscitation. Supplemental oxygen was discontinued after clinical improvement was observed following epinephrine administration, but was temporarily reintroduced during recurrent anaphylactic episodes. Clarithromycin therapy was halted, and she did not receive further doses beyond the initial oral 500 mg.

Initially, the patient responded well to therapy, with normalization of blood pressure and reduction of facial swelling and erythema. She was admitted to the acute internal ward, where she was under close observation by specialized nursing staff, although telemetry was not used. Nevertheless, after the initial improvement, she experienced repeated episodes of anaphylactic shock within the first 12 hours of admission. Following the first recurrence, she was transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) for enhanced monitoring. Each episode was managed with intramuscular epinephrine administration, as depicted in figure 1, resulting in a total of four anaphylactic episodes, including the original presentation in the emergency department (ED). The first two epinephrine doses were delivered via auto-injector, each containing 0.3 mg of epinephrine intramuscularly. The subsequent two doses were prepared individually, with 0.5 mg administered intramuscularly each time. While the auto-injector dose available at the clinic is less than the recommended 0.5 mg, its use is favored in situations requiring rapid intervention (such as initial stabilization in the ED) or settings where emergency treatments for anaphylaxis are uncommon, reducing the risk of treatment delays due to unfamiliarity. After the first recurrence, corticosteroid therapy was initiated because of their traditional use in preventing biphasic anaphylaxis. She was given 30 mg of intravenous prednisone daily for 5 days and a single intravenous dose of 8 mg dexamethasone, reflecting protocol differences between the hospital’s general ward and ICU anaphylaxis guidelines. Additionally, 2 mg of intravenous clemastine was administered twice to manage skin manifestations, along with 500 mL of normal saline during the second anaphylactic episode. Notably, each subsequent episode presented with reduced severity, beginning with profound hypoxemia accompanied by cyanosis, hypotension, and pronounced dyspnea, and progressing to episodes characterized mainly by dyspnea, rash recurrence, and pruritus.

Early consultation with the department of allergology was obtained to seek expert guidance on treatment. Two alternative management strategies were evaluated in this case. The first involved the continuous infusion of epinephrine using an intravenous pump, aiming to prevent recurrence by proactive administration rather than waiting for symptomatic episodes. The second considered an observational approach, given the gradual attenuation of symptom severity, to determine if the reactions might resolve on their own. Both strategies posed significant risks, especially as there is a lack of literature to guide treatment in cases of multiphasic anaphylaxis.

Outcome and follow-up:

In the end, neither of the proposed interventions was required, since the patient experienced no additional recurrence of symptoms. After remaining free of anaphylaxis for roughly 24 hours, she was moved back to a general medical ward. She was discharged from the hospital following a 3-day inpatient stay. Levocetirizine, an antihistamine, was initiated as a regular medication, and she was provided with an epinephrine auto-injector (0.3 mg) along with instructions for its use. No subsequent anaphylactic events have occurred.

Given the unusual and extended nature of her illness, additional investigations into potential underlying etiologies were conducted. These assessments suggested a strong likelihood of systemic mastocytosis, though criteria for a definitive diagnosis were not fully met (the patient fulfilled only two WHO minor criteria, and major criteria could not be evaluated owing to the limited sample acquired during bone marrow biopsy, see figure 2). Tryptase measurements taken during the third anaphylactic event, as well as baseline values, were found to be elevated and continued to be elevated during follow-up with the allergologist, as detailed in table 1.

Regrettably, tryptase concentrations were not measured during the initial evaluation of the patient’s first episodes of anaphylaxis, resulting in deviation from the guideline-recommended timing for tryptase assessment. Current recommendations advise collecting at least two, ideally three, tryptase samples at prescribed intervals: the first should be obtained as early as possible without delaying acute management; the second should be collected 1–2 hours after symptom onset (and no later than 4 hours), a point at which tryptase generally reaches its highest level; a third sample should be drawn at least 24 hours following full clinical recovery to determine the baseline value. In this report, the initial tryptase measurement occurred 1 hour after the third anaphylactic reaction, with the subsequent sample collected 27 hours after the last episode.

Allergy confirmation testing was not pursued due to concerns that procedures such as skin testing could provoke another episode of severe anaphylaxis and considering that further exposure to the suspected allergen could be readily avoided. Additional notable findings consistent with systemic mastocytosis included hepatomegaly observed on abdominal ultrasound and possible neuropsychiatric manifestations such as depression and memory impairment.

Discussion:

Anaphylaxis is a clinically recognized diagnosis that presents with abrupt onset and swiftly evolving symptoms affecting the airway, respiration, circulatory system, and/or gastrointestinal tract, and often includes skin or mucosal features such as urticaria, angioedema, or flushing. Although identifying a probable allergen can strengthen diagnostic certainty, pinpointing an exact cause is not always achievable.

Atypical forms, including biphasic and refractory anaphylaxis, are described less precisely, though recent initiatives have sought to define these patterns with greater clarity. According to the Resuscitation Council UK, biphasic anaphylaxis is the return of symptoms hours after initial improvement, without further exposure to the causative agent. Distinguishing true biphasic phenomena from ongoing anaphylaxis with temporary epinephrine effect or prolonged allergen absorption (as with residual food or sustained-release drugs) poses significant difficulty. In contrast, refractory anaphylaxis is defined as unrelenting symptoms despite proper administration of epinephrine.

Current guidance for biphasic anaphylaxis mainly focuses on clinical surveillance, but there is ongoing debate about the ideal length of post-reaction observation. Conventional practices recommend monitoring for 4 to 6 hours following resolution of symptoms. The Dutch Nederlands Huisartsen Genootschap (NHG) and World Allergy Organisation guidelines state that around half of biphasic reactions manifest within 6–12 hours of the initial event, though they stop short of offering explicit monitoring timelines. Conversely, the UK guideline (2021) advocates for a risk-based, three-tiered surveillance strategy. Establishing unified observation protocols remains necessary to address the balance between resource allocation for the larger group of patients with single-phase anaphylaxis and safeguarding those at risk for the relatively rare, but difficult to predict, biphasic responses.

There is a notable deficiency in guidance regarding advanced medication approaches for biphasic and multiphasic anaphylactic reactions. Treatment of these complex presentations closely follows conventional protocols for acute anaphylaxis, involving immediate intramuscular administration of epinephrine along with appropriate supportive interventions. According to current guidelines, the recommended dose of epinephrine is 0.01 mg/kg, up to a maximum of 0.5 mg. This recommendation is frequently rounded up to 0.5 mg for adults, since most weigh more than 50 kg. It is worth highlighting that several auto-injectors, including those employed during the initial two anaphylactic events in the described case, supply only 0.3 mg of epinephrine per injection, even in adult settings. While epinephrine auto-injectors are invaluable for pre-hospital management, they can also facilitate timely treatment and mitigate hesitancy among medical personnel unfamiliar with administering vasoactive medications. Nonetheless, reliance on the 0.3 mg auto-injector, commonly used, poses a risk of significant underdosing in adults exceeding 50 kg. A retrospective analysis conducted in 2025 demonstrated that patients first treated with a 0.3 mg dose of epinephrine required escalation of therapy—including supplementary epinephrine administration—significantly more frequently compared to those who initially received 0.5 mg. Addressing limitations such as clinicians’ longstanding familiarity with the 0.3 mg dose and the widespread use of auto-injectors with this dosage, as well as bolstering adherence to dosing guidelines, has the potential to markedly enhance outcomes in anaphylaxis management.

Historically, corticosteroids were advocated partly to mitigate the risk of biphasic reactions, but accumulating data indicate not only a lack of efficacy but also potential harm, resulting in a recent trend away from prescribing steroids for anaphylaxis. A systematic review from 2017 assessed 22 studies; only one demonstrated benefit from corticosteroids, while the remaining 21 did not support their effectiveness in reducing biphasic anaphylaxis risk. In addition, recent investigations have underscored possible adverse effects, with a 2019 study reporting an increased chance of ICU or hospital ward admission following prehospital administration of corticosteroids. As a result, the majority of current guidelines recommend against the routine use of corticosteroids, even though they continue to be administered in some clinical settings.

Although biphasic anaphylaxis occurs infrequently, it has significant clinical implications. The challenge in identifying reliable predictors hampers effective risk stratification and monitoring protocols. Proposed indicators include late administration of epinephrine, an initially severe clinical picture, administration of multiple epinephrine doses, and a prior history of biphasic reactions. Nonetheless, no robust predictors currently exist to guide selective patient monitoring.

Systemic mastocytosis, a condition characterized by an abnormal proliferation of mast cells, heightened mediator release, and multisystem inflammatory involvement, elevates the risk for severe anaphylaxis.1517 However, because both systemic mastocytosis and biphasic anaphylaxis are uncommon, there is, to our knowledge, no published research specifically analyzing the relationship between these two entities. Ongoing studies into mast cell disorders and their role in biphasic anaphylaxis could help inform future therapeutic strategies.

Anaphylaxis-associated mortality remains low, particularly in areas where rapid emergency medical services are accessible. Moreover, despite the potentially severe nature of biphasic anaphylaxis, fatal cases are exceedingly uncommon. Nevertheless, anaphylaxis continues to be a frequent urgent medical event, necessitating immediate intervention and vigilant observation. While research and clinical guidelines primarily emphasize initial emergency responses, ongoing care and surveillance following the acute event are equally critical. Enhanced comprehension of intricate anaphylaxis presentations, such as biphasic reactions, may contribute to more individualized, efficient, and targeted management strategies.

The infrequency of biphasic and, more notably, multiphasic anaphylactic reactions, combined with the challenges of conducting research in acute settings, complicates the formulation of robust evidence-based protocols. In such circumstances, expert consensus guidelines offering well-defined management approaches hold significant merit; yet, their creation is impeded by limited available information. Given how uncommon these complex manifestations are, establishing a registry documenting these cases—including relevant patient details, proposed management approaches, such as those described in our report, and outcome data—could support the development of empirically informed, expert-driven recommendations.
