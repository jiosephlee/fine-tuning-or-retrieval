Title: Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting

Margret Ann Schneider, Hans de Groot, Jan-Willem Geerts

Abstract:

Summary: Anaphylaxis represents the most critical form of allergic reaction, often posing a risk to life and requiring immediate, effective intervention. Some patients experience symptom recurrence even after appropriate therapy and initial full resolution, known as biphasic or multiphasic reactions. These patterns create distinct challenges for healthcare providers, as the frequency in the literature varies, underlying risk factors remain ambiguous, and standardized treatment approaches are not outlined in current guidelines. This complexity is heightened in multiphasic anaphylaxis, characterized by repeated episodes following initial successful epinephrine administration. Given the infrequency of multiphasic anaphylaxis, case reports documenting clinical approaches offer valuable information regarding its management. Here, we describe a case involving a female patient in her 60s who developed multiphasic anaphylaxis following exposure to clarithromycin, with subsequent assessment indicating a strong probability of systemic mastocytosis; however, she did not fulfill all criteria necessary for a definitive diagnosis.

Background:

Anaphylaxis represents a serious, often life-threatening allergic event—particularly in cases involving anaphylactic shock—and is frequently encountered in emergency settings. A proportion of instances demonstrate biphasic or even multiphasic patterns, which involve an initial anaphylactic response to an allergen, subsequent remission of symptoms, an asymptomatic interval, and then a return of clinical manifestations absent further exposure to the precipitating agent. Although this recurrence typically arises within 24 hours following the first allergen encounter, documented cases have reported symptom reappearance up to 78 hours afterwards. The prevalence of biphasic anaphylaxis has been reported as varying from 0.4% to 23.3%, with the majority of studies indicating rates between 4% and 6%. Because of the risk for recurrence, patients are generally observed for a period after resolution of anaphylactic symptoms. Nevertheless, the criteria for biphasic anaphylaxis are not always uniform and management recommendations differ; notably, the efficacy of corticosteroid therapy has been questioned in recent literature. Multiphasic anaphylaxis, which involves multiple instances of symptom recurrence, is particularly challenging in terms of both treatment and monitoring strategies. Here, we describe the clinical course of a woman in her 60s who experienced a multiphasic anaphylactic reaction after receiving clarithromycin.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 60s with a documented beta-lactam antibiotic allergy arrived at the emergency department (ED) exhibiting anaphylaxis after ingestion of 500 mg oral clarithromycin.

Her past medical history was notable for type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, psoriasis, and depression, and she weighed 104 kg. Current medications included metformin 500 mg twice daily, gliclazide retard 80 mg once daily, hydrochlorothiazide 25 mg once daily, omeprazole 20 mg once daily, and sertraline 100 mg once daily. The most recent hydrochlorothiazide dose had been administered over 12 hours before her ED visit.

As a teenager, she had experienced a mild allergic response to amoxicillin characterized by isolated facial swelling and no involvement of the oropharynx, rash, hypotension, or dyspnoea. Additionally, she previously developed a pronounced local reaction to a wasp sting with no systemic manifestations. However, the current presentation was her first occurrence of anaphylaxis.

Five days prior to presenting to the ED, her general practitioner prescribed clindamycin for erysipelas. Following 5 days of clindamycin therapy, she noted a rash (description unspecified) on her abdomen. This was considered a delayed allergic reaction to clindamycin, leading her general practitioner to discontinue clindamycin and initiate clarithromycin, to which the patient had not been previously exposed.

She later arrived at the emergency department experiencing itching, flushing, increased sweating, restlessness, and a sensation of throat swelling. These symptoms appeared one hour following the initial oral dose of clarithromycin and gradually intensified. She sought emergency medical attention one hour after the onset of symptoms. On presentation, her vital signs demonstrated low blood pressure and reduced oxygen saturation (non-invasive blood pressure measured at 74/50 mm Hg, peripheral oxygen saturation at 88% on room air, and a respiratory rate of 22 breaths per minute). Physical examination revealed swelling of the face, newly developed widespread erythema and urticaria covering her entire body, along with increased perspiration and agitation. There was no observed edema of the tongue or throat, and cardiac and pulmonary auscultation were normal. Her blood glucose was 12.7 mmol/L, and ECG findings indicated sinus rhythm. Immediate management for a presumed severe allergic reaction included an intramuscular injection of 0.3 mg epinephrine via auto-injector and a slow intravenous dose of 2 mg clemastine, administered to address the cutaneous symptoms per local protocol. She also received supplemental oxygen at 15 L/min via a non-rebreather mask and 500 mL intravenous normal saline (0.9% sodium chloride) for initial resuscitation. Oxygen therapy could be discontinued after marked improvement following epinephrine administration, but it was required intermittently during subsequent episodes of anaphylaxis. Clarithromycin therapy was stopped, and no further doses beyond the initial 500 mg oral administration were given.

Initially, the patient exhibited a favorable response to the prescribed therapy, as her blood pressure normalized and both facial swelling and redness diminished. She was transferred to the acute internal ward, where she received increased monitoring by specialized nursing staff but without telemetry surveillance. Despite initial improvement, the patient soon experienced multiple recurrent episodes consistent with anaphylactic shock within the first 12 hours after admission. Following the first recurrence, she was admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) for enhanced observation. Each episode was managed with intramuscular epinephrine (see figure 1); overall, four anaphylactic events occurred, including her initial emergency department (ED) presentation. The first two administrations involved auto-injectors delivering 0.3 mg epinephrine intramuscularly, while the latter two episodes were treated with individually prepared 0.5 mg epinephrine doses given intramuscularly. Although our clinic’s available auto-injectors provide a lower dose than the standard 0.5 mg, we favor auto-injector use during acute situations—such as early stabilization of a new ED arrival—or in settings where emergency therapies are uncommon and hesitation or inexperience could otherwise lead to delays. After the first anaphylaxis recurrence, corticosteroid therapy was initiated based on its conventional use in preventing biphasic anaphylaxis. She was given 30 mg prednisone intravenously once daily for five days and a single 8 mg intravenous dose of dexamethasone, reflecting differing protocols between our hospital’s general ward and intensive care unit anaphylaxis guidelines. Additionally, she received two doses of 2 mg intravenous clemastine for cutaneous manifestations, and a 500 mL infusion of normal saline was provided during her second episode. Notably, the severity of her symptoms progressively declined with each episode, from initial profound hypoxemia accompanied by cyanosis, hypotension, and marked dyspnea to later presentations with dyspnea, recurrence of rash, and pruritus.

The allergology department was promptly involved for expert therapeutic recommendations. Two alternative management strategies were deliberated. One approach entailed continuous intravenous epinephrine infusion to preempt anaphylaxis, instead of awaiting symptom recurrence. The other, considering the gradual reduction in symptom severity, was to adopt an observational stance to assess whether symptoms might subside without intervention. Both strategies posed considerable risks, amplified by the absence of published guidance for managing multiphasic anaphylaxis.

Outcome and follow-up:

In the end, neither of the considered interventions was required, as the patient remained free of symptom recurrence thereafter. Following approximately 24 hours without additional episodes of anaphylaxis, she was moved to a general medical ward. She was discharged from the hospital after a total of 3 days. The patient began treatment with levocetirizine, an antihistamine, added as a regular medication, and received instruction in the use of an epinephrine auto-injector (0.3 mg), which she was supplied with. No further instances of anaphylaxis have occurred since that time.

Given the unusual and extended course of illness, an investigation into possible underlying factors was initiated. This workup identified a substantial likelihood of systemic mastocytosis; however, a definitive diagnosis could not be made, as only two of the World Health Organization (WHO) minor criteria were met and evaluation of the major criterion was not possible due to insufficient bone marrow biopsy material (see figure 2). Regrettably, tryptase measurements were not obtained during the initial assessment of the patient’s early anaphylactic events, resulting in noncompliance with the standard timing for tryptase evaluation. Current guidelines advise collecting at least two, and preferably three, tryptase samples at defined time points: the first sample should be acquired as promptly as possible without delaying emergency management, the second should occur 1–2 hours (but no later than 4 hours) following symptom onset, coinciding with expected peak tryptase levels, and a third sample should be drawn at least 24 hours after complete resolution of symptoms to establish a baseline value. In this reported case, the initial tryptase determination was obtained 1 hour subsequent to the third anaphylactic episode, with the follow-up sample drawn 27 hours after the final episode of anaphylaxis.

Allergy confirmation testing was not pursued, as the potential risk of provoking another severe anaphylactic reaction—such as through skin testing—was considered disproportionate, especially given the relative simplicity of avoiding future exposure. Additional findings supportive of a diagnosis of systemic mastocytosis included hepatomegaly on abdominal ultrasound and neuropsychiatric symptoms such as depression and memory impairment.

Discussion:

Anaphylaxis is generally recognized as a well-defined clinical condition, distinguished by abrupt onset and swift escalation of symptoms affecting the airway, respiratory function, circulation, and/or gastrointestinal system, typically presenting alongside cutaneous or mucosal signs such as urticaria, angioedema, or flushing. Identifying a potential allergenic cause can facilitate diagnosis, though pinpointing a precise trigger is often not achievable.

Atypical cases, including biphasic or refractory anaphylaxis, are less clearly characterized, although recent initiatives have sought to develop consensus definitions for these variants. According to the Resuscitation Council UK, biphasic anaphylaxis describes the return of symptoms hours after they have seemingly resolved, occurring without renewed exposure to the allergen. Distinguishing genuine biphasic responses from prolonged anaphylaxis that temporarily abates with epinephrine or continued absorption of the allergen (such as leftover food or slow-release medication) may be difficult. In contrast, refractory anaphylaxis pertains to cases where symptoms persist despite correct and adequate administration of epinephrine.

Guidelines predominantly focus on clinical observation for biphasic anaphylaxis. Nonetheless, there is ongoing disagreement about how long monitoring should last. Traditionally, patients are observed for 4 to 6 hours post-resolution of symptoms. Both the Nederlands Huisartsen Genootschap (NHG) and the World Allergy Organisation guideline highlight that around half of biphasic reactions take place within 6–12 hours of the initial episode, although neither sets forth precise recommendations regarding the duration of monitoring. The 2021 UK guideline differs by suggesting a three-level, risk-based monitoring strategy. Clearly, establishment of uniform monitoring protocols is necessary to ensure effective allocation of healthcare resources for the majority experiencing isolated anaphylaxis, while maintaining adequate safety measures for the minority at risk of complex biphasic reactions.

There is a notable and concerning absence of specific guidance regarding advanced pharmacological approaches for biphasic and multiphasic anaphylactic episodes. The approach to managing biphasic and multiphasic anaphylaxis generally aligns with standard anaphylaxis protocols, emphasizing the immediate administration of intramuscular epinephrine along with suitable supportive measures. Current guidelines advise a dosage of 0.01 mg/kg, not to exceed a total of 0.5 mg. For the majority of adult patients, who typically weigh more than 50 kg, the standard dose is often rounded to 0.5 mg. Nevertheless, it is remarkable that commonly used auto-injectors—including those applied in the initial two episodes of anaphylaxis described in this report—contain merely 0.3 mg of epinephrine, even when intended for adults. Epinephrine auto-injectors are indispensable for use outside the hospital environment and retain utility in clinical practice, helping to prevent delays in administration and to minimize hesitancy among staff who seldom handle vasoactive substances like epinephrine. Despite the frequent use of the 0.3 mg dosage, such practice may result in clinically significant underdosing for adult patients weighing more than 50 kg. A 2025 retrospective analysis indicated that individuals initially administered 0.3 mg required more frequent escalation of therapy, including repeat epinephrine injections, compared to those who received an initial 0.5 mg dose. Addressing factors such as the prevailing preference for the 0.3 mg dosage and the widespread availability of 0.3 mg auto-injectors, as well as promoting compliance with recommended epinephrine dosages, may substantially enhance outcomes in anaphylaxis management.

Historically, corticosteroid therapy was advised in anaphylaxis management, partly to reduce the risk of biphasic reactions. Recent data, however, have highlighted a lack of efficacy and possible adverse outcomes associated with steroid use, prompting a move away from their routine administration in anaphylaxis. A systematic review from 2017 assessed 22 studies, only one of which favored corticosteroid use, while the remaining 21 found no evidence that steroids lower the risk of biphasic anaphylaxis. In addition, recent investigations have underscored concerns about side effects; notably, a 2019 study reported increased ICU or hospital admissions following prehospital steroid administration. As a result, current guidelines largely discourage standard corticosteroid use, yet their administration continues in clinical practice.

Though infrequent, biphasic anaphylaxis is of considerable clinical significance. The absence of consistent predictors complicates both risk stratification and surveillance choices. Proposed risk factors encompass delayed epinephrine delivery, severe initial episodes, repeated epinephrine dosing, and a history of biphasic reactions. Nonetheless, dependable indicators for selective monitoring have not yet been established.

Systemic mastocytosis, characterized by abnormal mast cell proliferation, excessive mediator secretion, and inflammation affecting multiple organs, increases susceptibility to severe anaphylaxis. However, due to the uncommon occurrence of both systemic mastocytosis and biphasic anaphylaxis, there is, at present, no published research specifically examining their relationship. Further studies investigating mast cell pathologies and biphasic anaphylaxis may contribute to improved management strategies.

Anaphylaxis-associated mortality rates remain low, particularly in areas where prompt emergency medical intervention is available. Fatal episodes of biphasic anaphylaxis occur even less frequently, although these reactions may appear particularly hazardous. Nonetheless, despite these relatively reassuring figures, anaphylaxis continues to present as a frequent emergency that necessitates immediate therapy and vigilant observation. Although the emphasis of most research and guidelines lies with initial emergency interventions, subsequent patient monitoring and follow-up care are equally significant. Enhanced understanding of intricate anaphylaxis presentations—such as biphasic reactions—could contribute to the advancement of more individualized, efficient, and targeted management approaches for anaphylaxis.

The infrequency of biphasic and especially multiphasic anaphylactic responses, coupled with intrinsic difficulties of conducting studies in emergency settings, poses substantial obstacles to establishing evidence-based management protocols. In such scenarios, consensus guidelines based on expert opinion and featuring specific recommendations represent a crucial resource; yet, these also face limitations due to restricted data availability. Considering the uncommon nature of these complex forms of anaphylaxis, the creation of a registry compiling relevant patient details, proposed management approaches (as discussed in this report), and clinical outcomes, may serve as a foundational tool for shaping expert-driven, data-informed recommendations.
