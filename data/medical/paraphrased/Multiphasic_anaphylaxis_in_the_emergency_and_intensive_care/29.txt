Title: Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting

Margret Ann Schneider, Hans de Groot, Jan-Willem Geerts

Abstract:

Summary: Anaphylaxis represents the most extreme form of allergic reaction and carries a significant risk of mortality unless treated swiftly and appropriately. In a portion of patients, symptoms return after initial resolution, even when treatment is considered sufficient and clinical improvement is evident. Such biphasic or multiphasic patterns introduce distinct difficulties for healthcare providers, particularly given the inconsistent incidence rates in the literature, ambiguous risk factors, and absence of definitive management protocols in present guidelines. This issue is even more pronounced in multiphasic anaphylaxis, where patients repeatedly relapse into anaphylaxis after apparently successful administration of standard therapy, such as epinephrine. Given that multiphasic anaphylaxis is uncommon, individual case reports are valuable in enhancing understanding and guiding clinical approaches to this phenomenon. Here, we detail the case of a female patient in her 60s who experienced multiphasic anaphylaxis in response to clarithromycin; subsequent evaluation suggested a strong probability of underlying systemic mastocytosis, despite the patient not fulfilling the established diagnostic standards.

Background:

Anaphylaxis is a serious allergic reaction that, particularly in the form of anaphylactic shock, can be life-threatening and is a frequent reason for emergency department visits. Some patients, however, experience a biphasic or even multiphasic pattern, which involves an initial anaphylactic episode following exposure to a trigger, subsequent resolution of symptoms with a symptom-free interval, and then a return of clinical signs without additional allergen contact. While this pattern most commonly manifests within 24 hours of the initial event, documented recurrences have been seen as late as 78 hours after exposure. The prevalence of biphasic anaphylaxis is reported to be quite variable, from as low as 0.4% to as high as 23.3%, although most literature suggests an incidence in the range of 4% to 6%. Because of the risk of symptom recurrence, observation after resolution of the initial episode is typically recommended. Nonetheless, there is inconsistency in the definitions used for biphasic anaphylaxis and considerable variation in management protocols, including recent questions regarding the use of corticosteroids. Multiphasic anaphylaxis, which is characterized by more than one recurrence of symptoms, creates additional complexities for management and monitoring. Here, we describe the case of a female patient in her 60s who developed a multiphasic anaphylactic response following administration of clarithromycin.

Case presentation:

A female in her 60s with a documented beta-lactam antibiotic allergy arrived at the emergency department (ED) exhibiting anaphylaxis after ingesting clarithromycin 500 mg orally.

Her past medical history consisted of type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, psoriasis, and depression. She weighed 104 kg. Current medications included metformin 500 mg twice daily, gliclazide 80 mg (retard formulation) once daily, hydrochlorothiazide 25 mg once daily, omeprazole 20 mg once daily, and sertraline 100 mg once daily. The most recent dose of hydrochlorothiazide had been administered more than 12 hours prior to her ED visit.

Previously, during adolescence, she had experienced a mild allergic response to amoxicillin, characterized by facial swelling only, with no associated oropharyngeal edema, rash, hypotension, or dyspnea. In addition, she had reacted with significant localized skin findings after a wasp sting, without systemic effects. The current event marked her initial experience of anaphylaxis.

Five days before her ED admission, clindamycin had been initiated by her general practitioner for erysipelas. After five days on clindamycin, she developed an unspecified abdominal rash. This was considered a delayed allergic response to clindamycin, prompting her general practitioner to discontinue clindamycin and initiate clarithromycin, to which she had no prior exposure.

She later arrived at the emergency department reporting itching, flushing, excessive sweating, agitation, and a sensation of throat swelling. These symptoms emerged approximately one hour after taking her first oral dose of clarithromycin and progressively intensified. She presented to medical services one hour after the onset of symptoms. Upon arrival, initial vital signs revealed low blood pressure and reduced oxygen levels (non-invasive blood pressure: 74/50 mm Hg, peripheral oxygen saturation 88% on room air, respiratory rate 22 breaths per minute). Physical examination noted swelling of the face, new widespread erythema and urticaria, increased sweating, and agitation. There was no evidence of tongue or throat edema, and both cardiac and pulmonary examinations were unremarkable. Her blood glucose was measured at 12.7 mmol/L, and electrocardiogram showed sinus rhythm. She promptly received treatment for a suspected severe allergic reaction, which included 0.3 mg epinephrine administered intramuscularly via auto-injector and 2 mg clemastine given as a slow intravenous injection according to local protocol for skin manifestations. In addition, she was supplied with 15 L oxygen via a non-rebreather mask and 500 mL of 0.9% sodium chloride solution during initial stabilization. Supplemental oxygen was discontinued after her clinical status improved following epinephrine, but it had to be temporarily restarted during recurrent anaphylactic events. Clarithromycin was stopped, with no further doses taken after the single 500 mg oral administration.

At first, the patient showed a favorable response to therapy, with normalization of blood pressure and reductions in both facial swelling and erythema. She was admitted to the acute internal ward for heightened nursing oversight, albeit without telemetry monitoring. Nonetheless, after this initial improvement, the patient experienced several recurrent episodes compatible with anaphylactic shock during the first 12 hours following admission. After the first relapse, she was transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) for enhanced surveillance. Each recurrence necessitated intramuscular epinephrine administration (see figure 1), totaling four episodes including her initial presentation to the emergency department (ED). The first two doses were delivered with an auto-injector containing 0.3 mg epinephrine intramuscularly, while the subsequent two episodes were managed with individually prepared intramuscular doses of 0.5 mg epinephrine. Although the available auto-injectors at our facility contain less epinephrine than the recommended 0.5 mg dose, this method is favored for use in time-critical situations (such as initial stabilization in the ED) or environments where urgent interventions for anaphylaxis are not commonplace and hesitancy might delay administration. After the first recurrence, corticosteroids were introduced owing to their established role in preventing biphasic anaphylactic reactions. She received 30 mg of intravenous prednisone daily over five days as well as a single intravenous dose of 8 mg dexamethasone, reflecting differing hospital protocols for the general ward versus intensive care management of anaphylaxis. In addition, she was treated with 2 mg intravenous clemastine twice for cutaneous manifestations and received a supplemental 500 mL infusion of normal saline during the second episode. Notably, symptom severity diminished with each recurrence, initially presenting with profound hypoxemia accompanied by cyanosis, hypotension, and marked dyspnea, then subsequently with dyspnea, return of rash, and pruritus during the final episode.

Early consultation with the allergology department was sought for expert recommendations regarding management. Two alternate strategies were contemplated: one involved continuous intravenous epinephrine infusion, proactively addressing anaphylaxis before symptom recurrence; the other entailed a watchful approach, monitoring to see if symptoms would resolve independently, given their progressive reduction in severity. Both strategies posed considerable risks, underscored by an absence of literature guiding treatment in cases involving multiple anaphylactic phases.

Outcome and follow-up:

In the end, neither therapeutic option proved to be required, as the patient experienced no additional recurrence of symptoms from that point on. Following an anaphylaxis-free interval of about 24 hours, she was returned to a regular care unit. She was discharged home after a total inpatient stay of 3 days. At discharge, levocetirizine, an antihistamine, was introduced as a routine medication, and she was provided with a 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injector along with instructions for its use. There have been no recurrent anaphylactic events reported since.

Given the unusual and extended course of her illness, further investigations were pursued to uncover any contributing conditions. This assessment identified a strong suspicion for systemic mastocytosis; however, diagnostic criteria for a definitive diagnosis were not fully met, as only two minor criteria set by the WHO were observed and the major criterion could not be evaluated due to insufficient sample from the bone marrow biopsy (see figure 2). Regrettably, tryptase measurements were not obtained during the initial assessment of the early anaphylactic events, resulting in a deviation from the recommended time points for tryptase evaluation. Current guidelines advise collecting at least two, and preferably three, tryptase specimens at specific intervals: the first should be drawn as soon as possible without impeding acute treatment; the second should be obtained 1–2 hours (but no later than 4 hours) after symptom onset, corresponding to the expected tryptase peak; and the third should be taken no sooner than 24 hours after complete symptom resolution to determine baseline levels. In the case described, the initial tryptase measurement was drawn 1 hour following the third episode of anaphylaxis, with the subsequent sample collected 27 hours after the last anaphylactic event.

Allergy confirmation testing was not performed due to the disproportionate risk of inducing another severe anaphylactic reaction, as might occur with skin testing, especially since future antigen exposure could be readily avoided. Additional findings supporting a diagnosis of systemic mastocytosis included hepatomegaly observed on abdominal ultrasound and possible neuropsychiatric manifestations such as depression and memory impairment.

Discussion:

Anaphylaxis is a clinically recognized syndrome, marked by an abrupt onset and swift escalation of symptoms affecting the airway, respiratory function, cardiovascular system, and/or gastrointestinal tract, often presenting with skin or mucosal signs including urticaria, angioedema, or flushing. Although pinpointing an allergen can assist in confirming the diagnosis, it may not always be feasible to identify the causative factor.

Atypical cases, such as biphasic or refractory anaphylaxis, lack precise definitions, although recent initiatives are working towards consensus descriptions. Biphasic anaphylaxis is described by the Resuscitation Council UK as a return of symptoms several hours following initial resolution, occurring without re-exposure to the allergen. Distinguishing genuine biphasic episodes from continuous anaphylaxis interrupted temporarily by epinephrine or from continued allergen absorption (for example, from unabsorbed food or sustained-release medications) proves challenging. Refractory anaphylaxis is characterized by persistent symptoms unresponsive or only minimally responsive to properly administered epinephrine.

Guidelines for biphasic anaphylaxis primarily focus on clinical observation. However, the optimal length of monitoring after resolution of symptoms remains uncertain. Standard practice suggests observation periods from 4 to 6 hours post-symptom resolution. The Dutch Nederlands Huisartsen Genootschap (NHG) and World Allergy Organisation guidelines observe that approximately half of biphasic responses manifest within 6–12 hours after the initial reaction, yet neither specifies a recommended monitoring timeframe. Conversely, the 2021 UK guideline offers a three-tier approach based on stratified risk. Establishing agreed-upon monitoring protocols is essential in order to balance resource allocation for the majority experiencing single anaphylaxis episodes against ensuring safety for those at risk for complex, harder-to-identify biphasic reactions.

Guidance on advanced pharmacologic interventions for biphasic and multiphasic anaphylactic reactions is conspicuously limited. The management approach to these types of anaphylaxis typically follows standard protocols, emphasizing immediate intramuscular administration of epinephrine alongside suitable supportive interventions. Current guidelines recommend dosing epinephrine at 0.01 mg/kg, not exceeding a total dose of 0.5 mg. This is frequently rounded up to a 0.5 mg dose for adults, since most exceed 50 kg in weight. It is notable, however, that many epinephrine auto-injectors, including those employed during the initial two episodes in the described patient, deliver only 0.3 mg of epinephrine, even when labeled for adult use. Auto-injectors for epinephrine are a crucial tool in out-of-hospital environments but may also offer advantages in healthcare settings by facilitating timely administration and alleviating staff reluctance in contexts where epinephrine is infrequently used. Nevertheless, utilizing a 0.3 mg auto-injector for adults exceeding 50 kg, though common, may result in subtherapeutic dosing. A retrospective analysis from 2025 demonstrated that individuals who were first given a 0.3 mg epinephrine dose required additional interventions, such as further epinephrine administration, more often than those initially treated with 0.5 mg. Addressing issues like providers’ reliance on the traditionally recommended 0.3 mg dose and the widespread availability of 0.3 mg auto-injectors, as well as reinforcing compliance with guideline-specified epinephrine doses, could substantially optimize outcomes in anaphylaxis management.

Historically, corticosteroids were advocated for use partly to reduce the risk of biphasic reactions. Nonetheless, increasing data indicate not only a lack of benefit but also possible negative effects, resulting in recent reconsideration of steroid administration in anaphylaxis. A systematic review published in 2017 reviewed 22 studies, with only a single study favoring corticosteroids, while the remaining 21 offered no supporting evidence for their efficacy in preventing biphasic anaphylaxis. Moreover, new investigations highlight adverse effects, with a 2019 study reporting higher rates of ICU or inpatient admission associated with prehospital steroid administration. Accordingly, most up-to-date recommendations discourage the routine use of corticosteroids, despite continued use in clinical settings.

Although biphasic anaphylaxis occurs infrequently, it carries significant clinical importance. The inability to identify consistent predictors challenges both risk assessment and decisions regarding patient observation. Proposed factors include delayed administration of epinephrine, marked severity of initial symptoms, requirement for multiple epinephrine doses, and history of biphasic reactions; nevertheless, dependable markers for individualized surveillance have yet to be identified.

Systemic mastocytosis, characterized by a pathological proliferation of mast cells, heightened release of inflammatory mediators, and widespread organ involvement, increases susceptibility to severe anaphylaxis. 1517 Nevertheless, owing to the scarcity of both systemic mastocytosis and biphasic anaphylaxis, current literature lacks studies specifically examining their association. Additional research exploring the connection between mast cell pathologies and biphasic anaphylaxis could improve future therapeutic approaches.

Fortunately, deaths from anaphylaxis are infrequent, particularly in areas where prompt access to emergency care is available. Moreover, while biphasic anaphylaxis might seem particularly threatening, fatalities resulting from it are even more uncommon. Despite these reassuring figures, anaphylaxis continues to represent a prevalent emergency situation that requires swift intervention and thorough observation. Although research and existing guidelines predominantly concentrate on immediate emergency treatment, follow-up care and continued monitoring are equally significant. Enhanced insight into more complicated presentations of anaphylaxis, such as biphasic reactions, could support the development of more individualized, efficient, and resource-conscious approaches to managing anaphylaxis.

The infrequency of biphasic and especially multiphasic anaphylactic episodes, coupled with the practical difficulties of conducting research during emergencies, poses significant obstacles to establishing robust evidence-based protocols. In these scenarios, consensus guidelines based on expert opinion and containing specific management advice are particularly valuable; nonetheless, generating such expert recommendations is constrained by the paucity of available data. Given how uncommon these multifaceted cases are, establishing a dedicated case registry—capturing pertinent patient information, proposed management strategies like those illustrated in our report, and clinical outcomes—could serve as a foundational resource to support informed, expert-based guidance.
