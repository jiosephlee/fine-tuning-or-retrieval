Title: Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting

Margret Ann Schneider, Hans de Groot, Jan-Willem Geerts

Abstract:

Summary: Anaphylaxis represents the most extreme manifestation of allergic reactions and poses a significant risk to life, necessitating immediate and effective intervention. In some instances, symptoms recur even after initial management and apparent resolution, termed biphasic or, on occasion, multiphasic anaphylaxis. These patterns complicate clinical care, as their frequency is inconsistently reported, contributing factors remain poorly defined, and standardized approaches are not well established within current recommendations. This issue is particularly evident in cases of multiphasic anaphylaxis, where patients experience several episodes despite appropriate administration of epinephrine and initial clinical improvement. Because multiphasic anaphylaxis is relatively uncommon, individual case reports can be a valuable resource for guiding clinical practice. Here, we describe a case involving a woman in her sixties who experienced multiphasic anaphylaxis triggered by clarithromycin, with further assessment indicating a strong probability of underlying systemic mastocytosis, although she fell short of fulfilling official diagnostic standards.

Background:

Anaphylaxis represents a serious and potentially fatal hypersensitivity reaction, particularly when presenting as anaphylactic shock, and is a frequent reason for patients seeking emergent medical attention. In certain instances, patients may experience biphasic or even multiphasic patterns, wherein an initial allergic event is succeeded by a symptom-free interval, after which symptoms return absent any new exposure to the allergen. Typically, these recurrent manifestations occur within 24 hours of the initial incident, although instances of recurrence have been documented as late as 78 hours post-exposure. The reported frequency of biphasic anaphylaxis exhibits substantial variability, ranging from 0.4% to 23.3%, but most literature indicates an incidence between 4% and 6%. As symptom recurrence is possible, patients are routinely observed for a period of time after apparent resolution of anaphylaxis. Nevertheless, discrepancies exist in the exact criteria for biphasic anaphylaxis and treatment strategies are not uniform; notably, there has been increasing debate over the utility of corticosteroids. Multiphasic anaphylaxis, involving several separate episodes of returning symptoms, creates additional complexity in terms of management and monitoring. This report details a case involving a female patient in her 60s who developed a multiphasic anaphylactic response to clarithromycin.

Case presentation:

A woman in her sixties with a documented beta-lactam antibiotic allergy arrived at the emergency department (ED) experiencing anaphylaxis after ingesting 500 mg of oral clarithromycin.

Her past medical history comprised type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, psoriasis, and depression. She weighed 104 kg. Her ongoing medications included metformin (500 mg twice daily), gliclazide (80 mg retard once daily), hydrochlorothiazide (25 mg once daily), omeprazole (20 mg once daily), and sertraline (100 mg once daily). She had last taken hydrochlorothiazide that morning, more than 12 hours prior to arriving at the ED.

In adolescence, the patient had a mild allergic reaction to amoxicillin, presenting as facial swelling without involvement of the oropharynx, cutaneous rash, hypotension, or respiratory distress. Additionally, she had exhibited a pronounced localized reaction to a wasp sting previously, though systemic symptoms were absent. The current event marked her initial presentation with anaphylaxis.

Five days before coming to the ED, she had been prescribed clindamycin for erysipelas by her general practitioner. Following five days of clindamycin therapy, she developed an abdominal rash (not further specified). This was regarded as a delayed allergic reaction to clindamycin, prompting her general practitioner to replace clindamycin with clarithromycin—a medication she had not taken before.

She later arrived at the emergency department reporting itching, flushing, excessive sweating, restlessness, and a sensation of throat swelling. These manifestations began one hour following ingestion of her first clarithromycin dose, intensifying gradually. She sought medical assistance one hour after symptom onset. Upon arrival, her vital signs indicated low blood pressure and reduced oxygen saturation (non-invasive measurement: 74/50 mm Hg; peripheral oxygen saturation at 88% on room air; respiratory rate of 22 breaths per minute). Initial examination revealed facial swelling, a recent generalized erythema, urticaria covering her whole body, increased sweating, and agitation. There was no evidence of tongue or throat swelling; heart and lung auscultation yielded normal findings. Blood glucose measured 12.7 mmol/L, and ECG showed sinus rhythm. She was immediately managed for presumed severe allergic reaction, including intramuscular administration of 0.3 mg epinephrine via auto-injector and slow intravenous injection of 2 mg clemastine to address dermatologic symptoms per local protocols. Furthermore, she received supplemental oxygen (15 L delivered with a non-rebreather mask) and 500 mL normal saline (sodium chloride 0.9% solution) for initial stabilization. Oxygen therapy was discontinued after she responded well to epinephrine, but had to be temporarily reinstated during later episodes of anaphylaxis. Clarithromycin therapy was stopped, with no further doses given beyond the single initial oral dose of 500 mg.

At first, the patient exhibited a favorable reaction to therapy, as her blood pressure normalized and her facial edema and erythema subsided. She was placed in the acute internal medicine unit, where she received heightened observation from specialized nursing personnel, but without continuous telemetry. Nonetheless, despite this positive early course, the patient experienced multiple recurrent episodes consistent with anaphylactic shock within 12 hours of hospitalization. After the initial recurrence, she was transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) for more intensive surveillance. Every relapse necessitated intramuscular epinephrine administration (see figure 1), resulting in a total of four anaphylactic episodes, including the initial presentation in the emergency department. For the first two episodes, epinephrine was delivered by auto-injector at 0.3 mg intramuscularly, while the subsequent two events were treated with manually prepared doses of 0.5 mg epinephrine intramuscularly. While our facility's auto-injectors deliver a lower dose than the standard 0.5 mg, we prefer their use in urgent situations—such as initial stabilization in the emergency department or in environments where acute interventions for anaphylaxis are infrequent and staff may hesitate due to unfamiliarity with the medication, potentially causing treatment delay. After the first recurrence, corticosteroids were initiated as an adjunct owing to their traditional use in reducing the likelihood of biphasic anaphylaxis. The patient received daily intravenous prednisone 30 mg for five days, and a single 8 mg intravenous dose of dexamethasone, to align with variations between the general ward and intensive care anaphylaxis protocols at our institution. Additionally, she was administered clemastine 2 mg intravenously on two occasions for dermatologic symptoms, and 500 mL of normal saline during her second anaphylactic episode. Notably, the severity of anaphylactic manifestations diminished with subsequent episodes, ranging initially from profound hypoxemia with cyanosis, hypotension, and marked respiratory distress, to only dyspnea, rash recurrence, and pruritus during the final episode.

The allergology service was promptly involved for expert consultation on management. Two distinct treatment strategies were contemplated. One option was to commence intravenous epinephrine infusion, providing continuous prophylactic management rather than waiting for recurrent symptoms. Alternatively, given the gradual reduction in symptom severity, a watchful waiting approach was considered to monitor for spontaneous resolution. Each strategy entailed significant risks, complicated by the scarcity of evidence-based guidelines for multiphasic anaphylactic cases.

Outcome and follow-up:

In the end, neither intervention was required because the patient did not experience additional recurrence of symptoms beyond this point. After about 24 hours with no new anaphylactic events, she was moved back to a general medical ward. She was discharged three days following admission. On discharge, she was prescribed levocetirizine as an ongoing antihistamine therapy and was provided with a 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injector, including instructions for use. She has not had any recurrent anaphylactic episodes since discharge.

Given the unusual and extended progression of symptoms, further investigations into possible underlying etiologies were performed. Findings suggested a high likelihood of systemic mastocytosis; however, diagnostic criteria were not fully met (two WHO minor criteria were present, but the major criterion could not be evaluated due to insufficient sample from the bone marrow biopsy, refer to figure 2). Regrettably, tryptase measurements were not obtained during the initial assessment of the first episodes of anaphylaxis, and thus the recommended timing for sampling was not observed. Current guidelines suggest collecting at least two, and preferably three, tryptase specimens at designated times: the first as promptly as possible without delaying emergency management, a second sample within 1–2 hours (not exceeding 4 hours) from symptom onset when tryptase peaks, and a third drawn at least 24 hours after full symptom resolution to determine the patient’s baseline level. In this report, the initial tryptase was measured 1 hour following the third anaphylactic event, and the subsequent sample was drawn 27 hours after the most recent episode.

Allergy confirmation testing was not pursued, as the potential for provoking another severe anaphylactic event through procedures such as skin testing was considered unwarranted, especially since avoidance of future exposures was judged straightforward. Additional findings supporting the diagnosis of systemic mastocytosis comprised hepatomegaly observed on abdominal ultrasound, as well as possible neuropsychiatric manifestations such as depression and memory impairment.

Discussion:

Anaphylaxis is recognized as a distinct clinical condition, defined by an abrupt onset and rapid escalation of symptoms affecting the airway, respiration, circulatory system, and/or gastrointestinal tract, frequently accompanied by skin or mucosal findings such as hives, swelling, or redness. While identifying a probable allergen can support the diagnosis, it may not always be feasible to pinpoint a specific cause.

Complex manifestations, including biphasic and refractory anaphylaxis, lack as clear definitions, though there have been recent initiatives to establish consensus descriptions. According to the Resuscitation Council UK, biphasic anaphylaxis refers to the reappearance of symptoms several hours after their resolution, without additional exposure to the offending agent. Distinguishing genuine biphasic episodes from prolonged anaphylaxis due to temporary response to epinephrine or sustained allergen uptake (for example, residual ingestion or slow-release pharmacologic agents) presents diagnostic challenges. Refractory anaphylaxis is characterized by ongoing symptoms unresponsive, or insufficiently responsive, to correctly administered epinephrine.

Guidelines relating to biphasic anaphylaxis focus largely on recommendations for clinical surveillance. However, there remains ongoing debate regarding ideal observation periods. Traditionally, observation times have varied from 4 to 6 hours post symptom improvement. The Dutch Nederlands Huisartsen Genootschap (NHG) and World Allergy Organisation guidelines highlight that around half of biphasic cases develop within 6–12 hours after initial presentation, yet neither guideline specifies exact monitoring durations. By contrast, the UK guideline 2021 introduces a three-level, risk-based monitoring strategy. Harmonized protocols for monitoring are needed to effectively balance the utilization of healthcare resources for the large cohort of patients with single episodes and the safety concerns in the small, hard-to-identify group at risk for biphasic complications.

There is a notable and concerning absence of detailed recommendations regarding advanced pharmacologic management in biphasic and multiphasic anaphylactic reactions. Treatment strategies for these presentations are largely aligned with conventional anaphylaxis protocols, prioritizing immediate intramuscular administration of epinephrine alongside supportive interventions as needed. Current guidelines stipulate an epinephrine dose of 0.01 mg/kg, not to exceed a single dose of 0.5 mg. For adults, this is typically rounded to 0.5 mg, since most adults weigh more than 50 kg. Nonetheless, it is noteworthy that many auto-injectors—such as those employed in the initial two anaphylactic episodes in the case under discussion—contain only 0.3 mg of epinephrine, even when designated for adult use. While auto-injectors are invaluable outside hospital environments, they also serve a useful purpose in clinical settings by expediting delivery of therapy and lessening reluctance among practitioners less accustomed to using vasoactive medications like epinephrine. The administration of a 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injector, though common, can result in significant underdosing for adult patients exceeding 50 kg. A retrospective analysis conducted in 2025 indicated that recipients of an initial 0.3 mg epinephrine dose required further escalation, including subsequent epinephrine doses, at a much higher rate than those who received the full 0.5 mg dosage. Reducing barriers such as comfort with the traditionally used 0.3 mg dosage and the broad availability of 0.3 mg auto-injectors, as well as promoting stricter adherence to dosing guidelines, could substantially enhance outcomes in the treatment of anaphylaxis.

Historically, corticosteroids were advised partly to minimize the risk of biphasic reactions in anaphylaxis. Recent data, however, indicate a lack of efficacy and potential for adverse effects, prompting a move away from steroids in anaphylaxis management. A 2017 systematic review encompassed 22 studies; only one supported corticosteroid use, while 21 failed to demonstrate a reduction in biphasic anaphylaxis risk. Additionally, newer studies highlight the significance of steroid-related adverse effects, including a 2019 study that reported a greater chance of ICU or hospital admission after prehospital corticosteroid administration. As a result, most modern guidelines discourage routine use of corticosteroids, though their application continues in practice.

Biphasic anaphylaxis, while uncommon, carries considerable clinical significance. Risk assessment and monitoring are made challenging by the lack of consistent predictive factors. Proposed predictors include late epinephrine administration, severity at onset, the requirement for multiple epinephrine doses, and prior biphasic events, but no reliable predictors for directed monitoring have been established.

Systemic mastocytosis, characterized by increased mast cell populations, heightened mediator release, and multiorgan inflammation, increases susceptibility to severe anaphylaxis.1517 Nevertheless, due to the infrequency of both diagnoses, there is currently no literature specifically examining systemic mastocytosis in the context of biphasic anaphylaxis. Further investigations into mast cell disorders and biphasic anaphylaxis could yield important information for future clinical strategies.

Fortunately, deaths from anaphylaxis are uncommon, particularly in areas where rapid emergency medical intervention is accessible. In addition, fatal cases of biphasic anaphylaxis are exceedingly rare, despite their seemingly high risk. Nevertheless, even with these encouraging statistics, anaphylaxis continues to be a frequently encountered emergency that requires immediate therapy and careful observation. Although much research and guidelines concentrate on the initial response, follow-up care and surveillance are also crucial. Enhanced insight into intricate presentations such as biphasic anaphylaxis could facilitate more individualized, focused, and efficient management of anaphylaxis.

The infrequent nature of biphasic and especially multiphasic anaphylaxis, combined with the challenges of conducting studies in acute settings, makes it particularly difficult to establish solid evidence-based management approaches. In these circumstances, consensus guidelines derived from expert opinion and featuring explicit recommendations are highly valuable, yet their formulation is also limited by insufficient available evidence. Considering how rare these complex cases are, establishing a registry that collects detailed patient information, proposes potential management options such as those outlined in our report, and records outcomes could serve as a foundational resource for forming expert-led, data-driven guidance.
