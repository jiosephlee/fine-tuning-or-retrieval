Title: Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting

Margret Ann Schneider, Hans de Groot, Jan-Willem Geerts

Abstract:

Summary: Anaphylaxis represents the most acute manifestation of allergic reactions and poses a serious threat to life, necessitating rapid and effective intervention. In certain instances, symptoms reappear following initial resolution despite appropriate management and complete response, resulting in biphasic or occasionally multiphasic patterns that present a distinct clinical dilemma. These cases are particularly difficult for healthcare providers because reported rates vary, contributing risk factors remain uncertain, and definitive treatment protocols are not established in the current guidelines. This complexity is accentuated in multiphasic anaphylaxis, characterized by repeated episodes of anaphylaxis after successful treatment with epinephrine. Given the rarity of multiphasic anaphylaxis, individual case reports documenting clinical management offer valuable perspectives on this issue. Here, we describe the case of a female patient in her 60s who experienced multiphasic anaphylaxis following clarithromycin administration, with further evaluation indicating a strong possibility of systemic mastocytosis, although formal diagnostic criteria were not fulfilled.

Background:

Anaphylaxis represents a severe allergic response, often leading to anaphylactic shock and posing significant risks to life, commonly prompting visits to emergency services. A proportion of patients may experience biphasic or even multiphasic patterns, where an initial episode triggered by an allergen resolves, followed by a symptom-free interval and subsequent recurrence of symptoms in the absence of further allergen exposure. Typically, such recurrences arise within 24 hours of the initial event, though some cases document symptom resurgence up to 78 hours later. The reported frequency of biphasic anaphylaxis spans broadly, with figures ranging from 0.4% to 23.3%; however, prevailing evidence indicates an incidence in the range of 4% to 6%. Because of the potential for symptom relapse, observation following the resolution of anaphylactic symptoms is generally recommended. Despite this, there remains inconsistency in defining biphasic anaphylaxis, and variation exists in clinical management protocols, including recent scrutiny regarding corticosteroid use. Multiphasic anaphylaxis, characterized by repeated episodes of symptom recurrence, complicates decisions regarding treatment and monitoring duration. Here, we describe the case of a woman in her sixties who exhibited a multiphasic anaphylactic response to clarithromycin.

Case presentation:

A female patient in her sixties with a documented beta-lactam antibiotic allergy arrived at the emergency department (ED) exhibiting signs of anaphylaxis after ingesting clarithromycin 500 mg orally.

Her medical background included type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, psoriasis, and depression. She weighed 104 kg. The current pharmacological regimen consisted of metformin 500 mg twice daily, gliclazide 80 mg retard once daily, hydrochlorothiazide 25 mg once daily, omeprazole 20 mg once daily, and sertraline 100 mg once daily. The most recent dose of hydrochlorothiazide had been administered in the morning, more than 12 hours prior to her arrival at the ED.

During adolescence, the patient experienced a mild allergic response to amoxicillin, presenting as facial swelling but without oropharyngeal involvement, rash, hypotension, or dyspnoea. She had also previously exhibited a pronounced localized cutaneous reaction to a wasp sting, though no systemic manifestations occurred. The current episode marked the patient’s first instance of anaphylaxis.

Five days prior to presenting at the ED, she was prescribed clindamycin by her general practitioner for erysipelas. After five days of treatment with clindamycin, she developed a rash on her abdomen (details not further specified). This rash was regarded as a delayed allergic response to clindamycin, prompting her physician to change the antibiotic to clarithromycin, for which she had no prior history of exposure.

She later arrived at the emergency department reporting itching, flushing, increased sweating, agitation, and a sensation of throat swelling. These symptoms appeared one hour after she ingested her first dose of clarithromycin and gradually intensified. She presented to medical services one hour after her symptoms began. Upon arrival, her vital signs revealed low blood pressure and low oxygen saturation (non-invasive blood pressure 74/50 mm Hg; peripheral oxygen saturation 88% breathing room air; respiratory rate 22 breaths per minute). Physical assessment demonstrated facial edema, newly developed diffuse erythema and urticaria covering her entire body, along with increased sweating and agitation. There was no observed swelling of the tongue or throat, and auscultation of the heart and lungs was unremarkable. Blood glucose was measured at 12.7 mmol/L, and ECG showed a sinus rhythm. She immediately received treatment for a presumed severe allergic reaction, which included a 0.3 mg intramuscular epinephrine auto-injector and a slow intravenous injection of 2 mg clemastine for cutaneous symptoms, per local protocol. Additional supportive care consisted of supplemental oxygen (15 L via non-rebreather mask) and an infusion of 500 mL normal saline (0.9% sodium chloride solution) during the initial stabilisation. Oxygen therapy was discontinued after she responded well to epinephrine, but had to be resumed temporarily during further anaphylactic episodes. Clarithromycin therapy was stopped, and she did not receive any additional doses following the initial 500 mg orally.

Initially, the patient responded well to therapy, with normalization of blood pressure and reduction in facial swelling and erythema. She was admitted to the acute internal medicine ward, which offers enhanced monitoring by specialized nurses but does not utilize telemetry. Despite the favorable initial response, within the first 12 hours after admission, she experienced multiple episodes consistent with anaphylactic shock. Following the first recurrence, she was transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) for closer observation. Each anaphylactic episode was treated successfully with intramuscular epinephrine (figure 1); in total, there were four anaphylactic events, including the initial presentation to the emergency department. The initial two epinephrine administrations were delivered via auto-injector containing 0.3 mg intramuscular epinephrine, while the subsequent two doses were manually prepared, each consisting of 0.5 mg epinephrine intramuscularly. Although the auto-injectors available at the facility contain a lower dose than the recommended 0.5 mg, their rapid accessibility makes them preferable for urgent situations, such as initial patient stabilization in the emergency department, or in contexts where anaphylaxis treatment is not common and delays may occur due to unfamiliarity with dosing. After the first recurrence, corticosteroids were added to the treatment regimen, given their conventional use in biphasic anaphylaxis prevention. The patient was administered intravenous prednisone at 30 mg daily for five days, as well as a single intravenous dose of 8 mg dexamethasone, following protocol variations between the hospital’s general ward and ICU anaphylaxis protocols. She also received 2 mg of clemastine intravenously on two occasions for skin-related symptoms and an additional infusion of 500 mL normal saline during the second anaphylactic event. Notably, each subsequent anaphylactic episode was less severe, starting with profound hypoxaemia accompanied by cyanosis, hypotension, and severe dyspnoea, progressing to dyspnoea and recurrent rash and itching by the final event.

The allergology department was consulted early for expert therapeutic recommendations. Two alternative management strategies were discussed: first, continuous intravenous infusion of epinephrine to preempt further anaphylactic reactions, and second, an expectant strategy in light of the progressively diminishing symptom severity, observing for spontaneous symptom resolution. Each strategy entailed considerable risks, compounded by a scarcity of evidence in the medical literature regarding optimal treatment for multiphasic anaphylaxis.

Outcome and follow-up:

In the end, neither intervention was required, as the patient did not experience any additional relapses of symptoms after this period. Following roughly 24 hours free from further anaphylactic events, she was moved to a general care unit. Her hospital stay lasted 3 days before discharge. She was initiated on levocetirizine, an antihistamine, as a routine prescription, and she received instruction in the use of an epinephrine auto-injector (0.3 mg). No subsequent anaphylactic events have been reported.

Given the unusual and extended nature of her presentation, additional evaluation of possible underlying etiologies was undertaken. The investigation indicated a strong likelihood of systemic mastocytosis, though criteria for a definitive diagnosis were not fulfilled—only two WHO minor criteria were present, and major criteria could not be evaluated because insufficient material was acquired with the bone marrow biopsy (refer to figure 2). Tryptase concentrations were elevated both during the third episode of anaphylaxis and at baseline, and remained persistently high during follow-up with the allergologist, as presented in table 1.

Regrettably, serum tryptase concentrations were not measured during the initial assessment of the early anaphylactic events, resulting in noncompliance with recommended timings for tryptase assessment. Current guidelines advise obtaining at least two, and preferably three, tryptase measurements at specific time points: the first as promptly as possible without delaying intervention; a second 1–2 hours after symptom onset (but within a 4-hour window), corresponding to the expected peak in tryptase; and a third sample at least 24 hours following complete resolution of symptoms, to determine the baseline value. In this particular case, the initial tryptase measurement was acquired 1 hour after the patient’s third episode of anaphylaxis, with a subsequent sample drawn 27 hours after the final recurrence.

Allergy confirmation testing was not pursued, as the potential for provoking another severe anaphylactic response, such as through skin testing, was considered an undue risk given the straightforward possibility of avoiding re-exposure. Additional findings supportive of systemic mastocytosis included hepatomegaly observed on abdominal ultrasound and potential neuropsychiatric manifestations, namely depression and memory impairment.

Discussion:

Anaphylaxis is generally recognized as a well-defined clinical condition, marked by an abrupt onset and swift development of symptoms impacting the airway, respiratory system, circulation, and/or gastrointestinal system, often presenting with skin or mucosal findings such as urticaria, angioedema, or flushing. Although identifying a probable allergen can support the diagnosis, pinpointing the exact trigger is not always feasible.

More complicated forms, including biphasic and refractory anaphylaxis, are not as clearly delineated, but recent initiatives have attempted to create standardized definitions for these scenarios. According to the Resuscitation Council UK, biphasic anaphylaxis involves the return of symptoms hours after their apparent resolution without any further exposure to the allergen. Distinguishing genuine biphasic episodes from protracted anaphylaxis with only a short-lived response to epinephrine or from continued absorption of the allergen (such as remains from ingested food or sustained-release medications) can be difficult. In contrast, refractory anaphylaxis is defined by continuous anaphylactic symptoms that fail to improve or show insufficient improvement despite correctly administered epinephrine.

Guidelines related to biphasic anaphylaxis are primarily focused on recommendations for clinical observation. Nonetheless, there is ongoing debate regarding the optimal length of monitoring. Traditionally, observation has been advised for 4 to 6 hours after symptoms resolve. The Dutch Nederlands Huisartsen Genootschap (NHG) and World Allergy Organisation guidelines indicate that around half of biphasic reactions manifest within 6–12 hours after the initial episode; however, these guidelines do not specify exact observation periods. Unlike these, the UK guideline from 2021 suggests a risk-based three-tier framework for patient monitoring. Establishing agreed monitoring protocols is essential for efficiently allocating healthcare resources for the majority who experience a single episode, while ensuring safety for the smaller, harder-to-predict biphasic group.

Guidance regarding advanced medication regimens for biphasic and multiphasic presentations is notably insufficient. The therapeutic approach to biphasic and multiphasic anaphylaxis generally aligns with the conventional management of anaphylactic reactions, emphasizing rapid administration of intramuscular epinephrine together with appropriate supportive interventions. Guidelines suggest a dosing of epinephrine at 0.01 mg/kg based on body weight, with a ceiling dose of 0.5 mg. For adults, this is typically approximated as 0.5 mg, considering that most adults surpass 50 kg. Nevertheless, it is noteworthy that the majority of epinephrine auto-injectors, including those administered during the initial two episodes in the present case, are limited to 0.3 mg doses, despite being indicated for adults. While epinephrine auto-injectors are crucial tools in prehospital settings, they can also be advantageous in healthcare facilities by facilitating timely administration and reducing reluctance among providers who infrequently use vasoactive medications like epinephrine. Despite their common use, administering a 0.3 mg auto-injector to adults over 50 kg may result in meaningful underdosing. According to a 2025 retrospective analysis, initial administration of 0.3 mg of epinephrine was associated with a significantly increased requirement for escalation measures, including repeat epinephrine dosing, compared to patients receiving an initial 0.5 mg dose. Addressing factors such as prescriber familiarity with the traditional 0.3 mg dosage and the widespread accessibility of 0.3 mg auto-injectors, in addition to reinforcing the use of guideline-recommended epinephrine doses, may be pivotal for optimizing anaphylaxis management.

Historically, corticosteroids were utilized partially to avert biphasic reactions. Nonetheless, growing data now indicates a lack of efficacy and possible adverse effects, resulting in a recent movement away from corticosteroid use in anaphylaxis management. A systematic review conducted in 2017 evaluated 22 studies; only one study found benefit from corticosteroids, while the remaining 21 studies demonstrated no evidence that corticosteroids decrease the incidence of biphasic anaphylaxis. Additionally, more recent publications highlight the significance of side effects, with one 2019 investigation reporting higher rates of ICU or hospital admissions after prehospital corticosteroid administration. As a result, the majority of current guidelines discourage the routine use of corticosteroids, even though their application persists in practice.

While biphasic anaphylaxis occurs infrequently, its clinical importance is substantial. The challenge lies in the lack of consistent predictors, making risk stratification and surveillance difficult. Potential predictive factors include delayed epinephrine treatment, severe manifestations at presentation, administration of multiple epinephrine doses, and a history of biphasic reactions. Despite these suggestions, robust predictors that enable precise monitoring are not presently established.

Systemic mastocytosis is characterized by abnormal mast cell proliferation, excessive mediator release, and widespread inflammation across multiple organs, which increases susceptibility to severe anaphylaxis.1517 Nonetheless, due to the uncommon nature of both entities, no studies to date specifically explore the connection between systemic mastocytosis and biphasic anaphylaxis. Additional research on mast cell disorders and their relationship with biphasic anaphylaxis may inform future therapeutic strategies.

Luckily, deaths from anaphylaxis occur infrequently, particularly in areas where rapid access to emergency services is available. Moreover, although biphasic anaphylaxis carries significant perceived risk, fatalities associated with it are exceedingly uncommon. Still, despite these encouraging figures, anaphylaxis constitutes a frequent medical emergency that necessitates immediate intervention and thorough observation. While much of the literature and current guidelines concentrate on acute emergency response, the need for comprehensive follow-up care and monitoring is equally significant. Enhanced insight into the more complicated presentations of anaphylaxis, such as biphasic reactions, has the potential to facilitate more individualized, patient-focused, and resource-conscious approaches to management.

The infrequent occurrence of biphasic—and particularly multiphasic—anaphylaxis, combined with inherent obstacles to conducting studies during emergencies, presents significant difficulties for the formulation of robust, evidence-based approaches. In these circumstances, consensus guidelines developed by experts offer significant value by providing concrete management recommendations, yet these efforts are similarly constrained by limited available evidence. Given the uncommon nature of these intricate anaphylactic patterns, establishing a database to document cases—along with relevant patient details, proposed management strategies such as those outlined in this report, and resulting outcomes—could lay the groundwork for forming expert-driven, data-informed guidelines.
