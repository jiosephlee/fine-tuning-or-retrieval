Title: Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting

Margret Ann Schneider, Hans de Groot, Jan-Willem Geerts

Abstract:

Summary: Anaphylaxis represents the most critical manifestation of allergic reactions, characterized by a potentially fatal systemic response that necessitates immediate and effective intervention. In some instances, patients experience a recurrence of symptoms after initial resolution and adequate treatment; these biphasic or even multiphasic presentations introduce particular difficulties for healthcare providers, as their frequency is inconsistently reported, risk factors remain ill-defined, and established management protocols are absent in existing guidelines. This complexity is particularly pronounced in multiphasic anaphylaxis, where individuals suffer repeated episodes of anaphylaxis despite initially responding appropriately to standard therapy with epinephrine. Given the infrequency of multiphasic anaphylaxis, individual case reports play a valuable role in illustrating clinical approaches to this condition. Here, we discuss the case of a female patient in her 60s who developed multiphasic anaphylaxis in response to clarithromycin; subsequent evaluation indicated a strong suspicion for systemic mastocytosis, although formal diagnostic benchmarks were not satisfied.

Background:

Anaphylaxis is a serious allergic response that can be life-threatening, particularly in cases progressing to anaphylactic shock, and is frequently encountered in emergency settings. Some patients, however, exhibit a biphasic or even multiphasic progression, in which an initial anaphylactic episode triggered by an allergen resolves, followed by a symptom-free period, and then a return of symptoms without additional exposure to the initial trigger. Recurrence of symptoms in such reactions most commonly occurs within 24 hours of initial exposure, though instances of delayed recurrence as late as 78 hours have been documented. Published rates of biphasic anaphylaxis are highly variable, reported from 0.4% up to 23.3%, but the majority of literature suggests an incidence between 4% and 6%. Because of the risk of symptom recurrence, post-anaphylaxis observation is generally recommended even after apparent complete resolution. Nevertheless, there is heterogeneity in how biphasic anaphylaxis is defined, inconsistencies among management guidelines, and the effectiveness of corticosteroids has recently been questioned. Multiphasic anaphylaxis—defined by more than two episodes of symptom return—presents additional complexity regarding both management and observation strategies. Here, we describe the case of a woman in her sixties who developed multiphasic anaphylaxis in response to clarithromycin.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 60s, with a known allergy to beta-lactam antibiotics, presented to the emergency department (ED) with anaphylaxis following oral clarithromycin 500 mg.

The patient’s past medical history consisted of type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, psoriasis, and depression. She weighed 104 kg. Her ongoing medications were metformin 500 mg twice daily, gliclazide 80 mg retard once daily, hydrochlorothiazide 25 mg once daily, omeprazole 20 mg once daily, and sertraline 100 mg once daily. The most recent dose of hydrochlorothiazide was consumed in the morning, more than 12 hours prior to her emergency department arrival.

In adolescence, she had experienced a mild allergic reaction to amoxicillin, manifested by facial edema without involvement of the oropharynx, absence of rash, hypotension, or dyspnea. She had also developed a pronounced localized cutaneous response following a wasp sting, but without any systemic features. This current hospitalization represented her initial encounter with an episode of anaphylaxis.

Five days prior to her arrival at the emergency department, her general practitioner prescribed clindamycin to treat erysipelas. After five days on clindamycin, she developed an abdominal rash (not described in further detail). This was considered to be a delayed allergic response to clindamycin, resulting in a change of antibiotic therapy by her general practitioner to clarithromycin, an agent to which she had not previously been exposed.

She later arrived at the emergency department reporting itching, flushing, increased sweating, restlessness, and a sensation of throat swelling. These symptoms appeared one hour after she took her first oral dose of clarithromycin and progressively intensified. She presented for medical evaluation an hour after symptom onset. On initial assessment in the emergency department, her vital signs indicated low blood pressure and reduced oxygen saturation (non-invasive BP: 74/50 mm Hg, SpO2: 88% on room air, respiratory rate: 22 breaths per minute). Physical examination revealed facial swelling, new diffuse erythema and urticaria across the entire body, as well as increased perspiration and agitation. There was no evidence of tongue or throat swelling, and both cardiac and pulmonary auscultation were unremarkable. Her blood glucose measured 12.7 mmol/L, and ECG demonstrated sinus rhythm. Immediate management for a presumed severe allergic reaction was initiated, including intramuscular administration of 0.3 mg epinephrine via auto-injector and a slow intravenous dose of 2 mg clemastine to address the cutaneous manifestations, following institutional protocols. She also received supplemental oxygen (15 L/min by non-rebreather mask) and a 500 mL bolus of 0.9% sodium chloride solution for initial stabilization. Oxygen supplementation was successfully discontinued following a favorable response to epinephrine but had to be temporarily reinstated during later anaphylactic recurrences. Clarithromycin therapy was stopped, with no doses taken beyond the single 500 mg oral administration.

Initially, the patient responded favorably to therapy, demonstrated by normalization of blood pressure and reduction in facial swelling and erythema. She was admitted to the acute internal medicine ward, where enhanced nursing surveillance is standard, though cardiac telemetry is not used. Despite the early positive response, she experienced repeated manifestations consistent with anaphylactic shock within the first 12 hours after admission. After the first recurrence, the patient was transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) for more advanced monitoring. Each recurrence necessitated intramuscular epinephrine, as illustrated in figure 1; in total, she suffered four anaphylactic episodes including the index emergency department (ED) event. The initial two epinephrine administrations utilized 0.3 mg intramuscular auto-injectors, while the subsequent two involved individually prepared doses of 0.5 mg intramuscular epinephrine. Although the 0.3 mg auto-injectors stocked in our facility deliver a lower dose than the 0.5 mg typically recommended, auto-injectors are preferred in scenarios where rapid administration is required—such as in the initial management phase in the ED—or when staff familiarity with emergency medications may be limited, thus averting delays due to hesitance. After the first recurrence, corticosteroid therapy was added in accordance with established practice to help prevent biphasic anaphylactic reactions. The patient was administered intravenous prednisone at 30 mg daily for 5 days as well as a single dose of intravenous dexamethasone 8 mg, reflecting divergent recommendations between our hospital's general and ICU anaphylaxis protocols. Additionally, she received clemastine 2 mg IV on two occasions for management of dermatologic symptoms, and 500 mL of normal saline IV during the second anaphylactic episode. Notably, symptom severity decreased with each anaphylactic event, initially presenting as profound hypoxemia, cyanosis, hypotension, and marked dyspnea, and later manifesting as dyspnea with return of rash and pruritus in the final episode.

Consultation with the allergology department was obtained early for expert guidance. Two alternative management strategies were contemplated. One option involved continuous intravenous epinephrine infusion, to pre-emptively suppress anaphylactic events rather than intervening after recurrence. The other considered a watchful waiting approach, given the observed decline in symptom intensity, to determine if symptoms would abate spontaneously. Both strategies carried considerable risk, complicated by a lack of existing literature offering guidance for management of multiphasic cases.

Outcome and follow-up:

Ultimately, neither of the proposed interventions was required, as the patient experienced no additional recurrence of symptoms thereafter. Following an observation period of about 24 hours without any further anaphylactic events, she was moved back to a general care unit. She was discharged after a total hospital stay of three days. Upon discharge, she was initiated on levocetirizine, an antihistamine, as a regular medication, and she was provided with an epinephrine auto-injector (0.3 mg) along with instructions on its use. She has not suffered from any more episodes of anaphylaxis since that time.

Given the unusual and extended progression of her illness, further evaluation into possible underlying conditions was undertaken. This investigation indicated a strong likelihood of systemic mastocytosis, though formal diagnostic criteria were not met: only two World Health Organization (WHO) minor criteria were fulfilled, and assessment of the major criterion was not possible because the bone marrow biopsy sample was insufficient (see figure 2). During her third anaphylactic event, as well as at baseline, tryptase concentrations were elevated, and these remained persistently raised during follow-up as monitored by her allergist (see table 1).

Regrettably, tryptase measurements were omitted during the initial assessment of the patient's first anaphylactic reactions, resulting in a failure to observe the recommended time points for tryptase testing. Current guidelines advise obtaining at least two, and preferably three, tryptase samples at designated intervals: the first sample should be collected as promptly as possible without postponing therapy, a second should follow 1–2 hours (no later than 4 hours) after symptom onset—when tryptase typically peaks—and a third sample should be acquired at least 24 hours post-complete resolution of symptoms to determine baseline values. In this reported case, the initial tryptase specimen was drawn one hour after the third episode of anaphylaxis, with the subsequent sample obtained 27 hours after the most recent anaphylactic event.

Allergy confirmation testing was not pursued, as the potential for precipitating another severe anaphylactic event—such as by skin testing—was considered disproportionate given that further exposure could readily be prevented. Additional pertinent findings consistent with systemic mastocytosis included the detection of hepatomegaly via abdominal ultrasound and possibly neuropsychiatric manifestations such as depression and memory impairment.

Discussion:

Anaphylaxis is generally recognized as a clinical syndrome defined by abrupt onset and swift escalation of signs affecting the airway, respiration, circulatory system, and/or gastrointestinal tract, and is commonly associated with skin or mucosal symptoms such as urticaria, angioedema, or flushing. Identifying a possible inciting allergen can help confirm the diagnosis, though a definite causative agent is not always established.

Atypical forms, including biphasic or refractory anaphylaxis, are less precisely delineated; however, recent initiatives have attempted to formulate agreed-upon definitions. According to the Resuscitation Council UK, biphasic anaphylaxis is characterized by reemergence of symptoms several hours after initial resolution, without additional exposure to the triggering allergen. Distinguishing true biphasic events from protracted anaphylaxis with only a temporary response to epinephrine, or from ongoing allergen absorption—such as might occur with lingering food antigen or sustained-release medication—can be difficult. Conversely, refractory anaphylaxis refers to sustained symptoms that do not resolve, or resolve inadequately, despite correctly administered epinephrine.

Recommendations in clinical guidelines regarding biphasic anaphylaxis mainly concern observation of affected individuals. Nonetheless, there is ongoing debate about the most appropriate duration of observation. Conventional practice generally advises monitoring for 4 to 6 hours after clinical recovery. The Dutch Nederlands Huisartsen Genootschap (NHG) and World Allergy Organisation guidance highlight that around 50% of biphasic events develop within 6–12 hours of the original reaction, but these sources stop short of specifying a precise period for observation. In contrast, the UK guideline issued in 2021 suggests a three-level, risk-based protocol for post-anaphylaxis monitoring. There remains a clear need for consensus on observation protocols that reconcile efficient use of medical resources—given the majority present as single-episode cases—with the need for patient safety, as those susceptible to biphasic complications form a smaller and harder-to-identify group.

There is a conspicuous and concerning absence of guidance regarding advanced pharmacologic approaches for biphasic and multiphasic presentations. Treatment for biphasic and multiphasic anaphylaxis largely aligns with conventional anaphylaxis protocols, incorporating immediate intramuscular administration of epinephrine alongside appropriate supportive measures. Current guideline recommendations specify an epinephrine dose of 0.01 mg/kg of body weight, with an upper limit of 0.5 mg. In practice, a 0.5 mg dose is typically utilized for adults, as most exceed 50 kg in weight. Notably, many epinephrine auto-injector devices—such as those administered during the first two reactions in the case described—deliver only 0.3 mg per dose, even though these are designated for adult use. While auto-injectors serve a vital function in out-of-hospital contexts, they may also offer benefits within medical environments by diminishing delays in administration and mitigating hesitancy among providers infrequently exposed to vasoactive drugs like epinephrine. Nevertheless, administering only 0.3 mg via auto-injector may result in substantial underdosing for adults weighing more than 50 kg. In a 2025 retrospective review, patients who received an initial 0.3 mg epinephrine dose required escalation of care, including additional epinephrine doses, at a significantly higher frequency compared to those given a 0.5 mg initial dose. Addressing obstacles such as healthcare provider familiarity with the long-standing 0.3 mg dosage and the prevalent availability of 0.3 mg auto-injectors, along with enhancing adherence to recommended dosing protocols, has the potential to markedly improve outcomes in anaphylaxis management.

Historically, corticosteroids were advocated, partially with the intention of minimizing biphasic reactions. Recent accumulating data, however, indicate not only a lack of benefit but also suggest possible adverse effects, prompting a contemporary move away from steroid administration in anaphylaxis. A 2017 systematic review encompassing 22 studies found only one study in favor of corticosteroid use, with the remaining 21 failing to demonstrate a reduction in biphasic anaphylaxis risk with these agents. Additionally, more recent investigations have highlighted concerns regarding adverse outcomes, including a 2019 study that reported higher rates of ICU or hospital admission associated with prehospital corticosteroid administration. In line with these findings, the majority of up-to-date guidelines now discourage the standard use of corticosteroids, although routine use still occurs in some clinical settings.

Biphasic anaphylaxis, while uncommon, carries significant clinical implications. The lack of consistent predictive markers makes determining risk and appropriate monitoring challenging. Factors that have been proposed to increase risk include delayed administration of epinephrine, severe initial symptoms, requirement for multiple epinephrine doses, and a history of biphasic episodes. Nonetheless, no definitive or universally reliable predictors have been identified to guide selective monitoring.

Systemic mastocytosis—characterized by an abnormal proliferation of mast cells, heightened mediator release, and multiorgan inflammation—places patients at heightened risk for severe anaphylaxis.1517 However, both systemic mastocytosis and biphasic anaphylaxis are rare events, and to date, specific studies investigating the association between systemic mastocytosis and biphasic anaphylactic reactions are lacking. Further investigation into mast cell pathologies and biphasic anaphylaxis could enhance the future approach to management.

Fortunately, mortality associated with anaphylaxis remains low, particularly in areas where rapid emergency care is accessible. Moreover, deaths resulting from biphasic anaphylaxis are even less frequent, in spite of the condition's seemingly serious risk. Nonetheless, despite these encouraging numbers, anaphylaxis continues to represent a frequent medical emergency that necessitates swift intervention and proper observation. Although research and guidelines primarily emphasize acute management, ongoing care and post-emergency monitoring are equally critical. Improved insight into complicated forms such as biphasic anaphylaxis could contribute to more individualized, effective, and resource-conscious approaches to anaphylaxis management.

Because biphasic and especially multiphasic anaphylaxis cases are uncommon, and emergency research faces intrinsic limitations, establishing genuinely evidence-based management protocols is especially difficult. Consensus guidelines formulated by experts, offering clear directives for handling such situations, are extremely beneficial under these circumstances; nevertheless, formulating such recommendations is complicated by scarce available evidence. Given the infrequency of these intricate cases, developing a registry that documents key patient information, potential management approaches like those described in this case report, and outcome measures could serve as a foundation for generating data-supported, expert-driven recommendations.
