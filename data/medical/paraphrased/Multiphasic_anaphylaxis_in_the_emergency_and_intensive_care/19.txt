Title: Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting

Margret Ann Schneider, Hans de Groot, Jan-Willem Geerts

Abstract:

Summary: Anaphylaxis, recognized as the most serious type of allergic reaction, represents a systemic event that can be fatal without swift and appropriate intervention. In certain cases, symptoms recur after initial resolution and treatment, despite a complete response, resulting in biphasic or even multiphasic episodes. Such clinical patterns pose particular difficulties for practitioners, as reported rates vary widely, risk factors remain ambiguous, and definitive therapeutic approaches are not outlined in existing recommendations. This is particularly relevant for multiphasic anaphylaxis, where repeated episodes occur following satisfactory management with epinephrine. Given the uncommon nature of multiphasic anaphylaxis, individual case reports are valuable for informing clinical practice regarding management strategies. Here, we report the case of a female in her 60s who experienced multiphasic anaphylaxis following exposure to clarithromycin, and subsequent evaluation indicated a high probability of systemic mastocytosis, although she failed to meet strict diagnostic standards.

Background:

Anaphylaxis is a serious and potentially fatal hypersensitivity reaction, particularly in its shock form, and frequently leads to emergency department visits. In some patients, a biphasic or even multiphasic pattern occurs, involving an initial response to an allergen, subsequent symptom remission (asymptomatic interval), and then return of symptoms without any further exposure to the original trigger. This type of reaction typically arises within 24 hours of the initial allergen encounter, though episodes have been reported as late as 78 hours afterward. The documented frequency of biphasic anaphylaxis is highly variable, from 0.4% to 23.3%, but most estimates fall within a 4% to 6% range. Because of the risk of symptom recurrence, observation after an anaphylactic event is generally recommended, even if there is full initial resolution. However, there is a lack of consistency in how biphasic anaphylaxis is defined, and clinical management guidelines diverge—particularly regarding corticosteroid use, which has been debated in recent literature. Multiphasic anaphylaxis, in which there are several relapsing episodes, introduces added complexities for both treatment decisions and monitoring approaches. Here, we describe the case of a woman in her 60s who experienced a multiphasic anaphylactic response to clarithromycin.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 60s, with a documented beta-lactam antibiotic allergy, arrived at the emergency department (ED) experiencing anaphylaxis after ingesting 500 mg of oral clarithromycin.

Her past medical history included type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, psoriasis, and depression. She weighed 104 kg. Her ongoing medications were metformin 500 mg twice daily, gliclazide retard 80 mg once daily, hydrochlorothiazide 25 mg once daily, omeprazole 20 mg daily, and sertraline 100 mg daily. The final dose of hydrochlorothiazide had been consumed that morning, more than 12 hours prior to arrival at the ED.

During adolescence, she had a minor allergic reaction to amoxicillin, characterized by isolated facial edema without oropharyngeal involvement, cutaneous rash, hypotension, or respiratory distress. She also once experienced a pronounced local cutaneous reaction following a wasp sting, but did not exhibit systemic manifestations. The present hospital admission marked her first occurrence of anaphylaxis.

Her general practitioner had prescribed clindamycin for erysipelas five days before her ED visit. After completing five days of clindamycin, she noticed an abdominal rash (details unspecified), which was assumed to represent a delayed hypersensitivity reaction to clindamycin. Consequently, her general practitioner substituted clarithromycin, a drug she had not previously received, as the new antibiotic therapy.

She later arrived at the emergency department reporting itching, flushing, increased sweating, restlessness, and a sensation of throat swelling. These manifestations began one hour following her first oral dose of clarithromycin and progressively intensified. She presented for medical evaluation one hour after symptom onset. Upon arrival, her vital signs indicated low blood pressure and reduced oxygen saturation (non-invasive blood pressure measured at 74/50 mm Hg, peripheral oxygen saturation at 88% on room air, and respiratory rate of 22 breaths per minute). Physical assessment noted facial edema, newly developed generalized erythema and urticaria affecting her entire body, with increased perspiration and agitation. There was no evidence of tongue or pharyngeal swelling, and heart and lung auscultation produced normal findings. Her blood glucose was 12.7 mmol/L, and ECG demonstrated sinus rhythm. Management for suspected severe allergic reaction was initiated immediately, including intramuscular administration of 0.3 mg epinephrine via auto-injector and slow intravenous delivery of 2 mg clemastine in accordance with local protocol for cutaneous symptoms. She was also given supplemental oxygen at 15 L/min via a non-rebreather mask and 500 mL of normal saline (0.9% sodium chloride) during initial resuscitation. Oxygen supplementation was discontinued after a favorable response to epinephrine but was briefly reintroduced during later episodes of anaphylaxis. No further doses of clarithromycin were given after the initial 500 mg oral dose.

A positive initial response to the therapy was observed, with normalization of blood pressure and reduction in facial erythema and swelling. The patient was subsequently admitted to the acute internal medicine ward, where she received enhanced observation from specialized nursing personnel, although telemetry monitoring was not utilized. Despite early improvement, within the first 12 hours of hospitalization the patient experienced several recurrences of symptoms consistent with anaphylactic shock. Following the first recurrence, she was transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) for closer surveillance. Resolution of each anaphylactic episode was achieved through intramuscular epinephrine administration (figure 1), with a total of four episodes, including the initial presentation in the emergency department. The initial two doses were delivered via auto-injector containing 0.3 mg of epinephrine intramuscularly; the subsequent two doses were manually prepared at 0.5 mg epinephrine IM. While the auto-injectors in our facility contain less epinephrine than the standard recommended dose of 0.5 mg, their use is preferred in emergent situations (such as the early stabilization in the ED), or in contexts where emergency interventions for anaphylaxis are infrequent and unfamiliarity might lead to delay. After the first recurrent episode, corticosteroid therapy was initiated, based on the conventional use of corticosteroids to prevent biphasic anaphylaxis. The patient received intravenous prednisone at 30 mg daily for 5 days and a single dose of 8 mg intravenous dexamethasone, reflecting differing protocols between our hospital’s general ward and intensive care settings for anaphylaxis management. Additionally, 2 mg intravenous clemastine was administered twice for cutaneous manifestations, along with 500 mL normal saline infusion during the second episode. Notably, the severity of symptoms diminished with successive anaphylactic events, ranging from profound hypoxemia with cyanosis, hypotension, and marked dyspnea at onset, to milder manifestations including dyspnea, rash recurrence, and pruritus during the final episode.

The allergology department was consulted promptly to provide expert treatment guidance. In this context, two alternative therapeutic strategies were evaluated. The first considered pre-emptive, continuous intravenous infusion of epinephrine, aiming to proactively manage anaphylaxis rather than reacting to symptom recurrence. The second option, given the gradual decline in symptom severity, involved an observational approach to determine whether symptoms might resolve without further intervention. Both strategies carried substantial risks, as highlighted by the limited evidence base available to inform treatment decisions in cases of multiphasic anaphylaxis.

Outcome and follow-up:

In the end, neither therapeutic option was required, as the patient experienced no additional recurrence of symptoms beyond this point. After remaining free of new anaphylactic episodes for about 24 hours, she was transferred from the intensive setting to a general medical ward. She was discharged home following a total hospital stay of 3 days. Upon discharge, levocetirizine, an antihistamine, was initiated as her regular medication, and she was supplied with a 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injector and educated on its use. There have been no subsequent anaphylactic episodes.

Given the unusual and prolonged progression of her illness, further investigations were undertaken to determine underlying etiologies. This evaluation indicated a strong likelihood of systemic mastocytosis; however, the criteria for a definite diagnosis were not satisfied (two World Health Organization minor criteria were met, but the major criterion could not be evaluated due to insufficient bone marrow sample—refer to figure 2). Regrettably, serum tryptase concentrations were not assessed during the initial evaluation of the first episodes of anaphylaxis; thus, the standard timelines for obtaining tryptase measurements were not observed. Current guidelines advise that at least two, and preferably three, tryptase samples should be collected at defined time points: the first as soon as possible without postponing acute management, a second sample 1–2 hours after the onset of symptoms (but within 4 hours), which generally corresponds to the peak tryptase concentration, and a third sample at least 24 hours following complete clinical resolution to determine baseline levels. In this patient’s case, the initial tryptase was drawn 1 hour after the third episode of anaphylaxis, while the subsequent sample was collected 27 hours after the last occurrence.

Allergy provocation testing was not performed, as the potential risk of triggering another severe anaphylactic event—such as during skin testing—was considered excessive, especially given the relative simplicity of avoiding subsequent exposure. Additional noteworthy findings supporting a diagnosis of systemic mastocytosis were hepatomegaly observed on abdominal ultrasonography and the possible presence of neuropsychiatric symptoms such as depression and memory impairment.

Discussion:

Anaphylaxis is generally recognized as a clearly defined clinical entity, marked by a sudden appearance and swift progression of symptoms affecting the airway, respiratory system, cardiovascular system, and/or gastrointestinal tract. This is often associated with skin or mucosal findings such as urticaria, angioedema, or flushing. While confirming an allergen exposure can aid in diagnosis, the exact trigger is not always identifiable.

More atypical presentations, like biphasic or refractory anaphylaxis, lack precise definitions, though consensus statements have been recently pursued. According to the Resuscitation Council UK, biphasic anaphylaxis is characterized by the reemergence of symptoms several hours after apparent resolution, without additional allergen contact. Distinguishing true biphasic reactions from persistent anaphylaxis—where there is only a brief improvement after epinephrine or ongoing allergen absorption (for example, due to residual food or sustained-release medication)—can be difficult. In contrast, refractory anaphylaxis is described as ongoing anaphylaxis that does not resolve, or only minimally improves, despite proper epinephrine administration.

Guidelines pertaining to biphasic anaphylaxis mainly focus on recommendations for patient monitoring, yet there is ongoing debate regarding the optimal observation period. Historically, observation durations of 4 to 6 hours following symptom improvement have been standard. The Dutch Nederlands Huisartsen Genootschap (NHG) and the World Allergy Organisation guidelines indicate that about half of biphasic reactions manifest within 6–12 hours after the initial event; however, neither offers explicit recommendations for monitoring intervals. The 2021 UK guideline, on the other hand, suggests a three-level risk-based observation strategy. There remains a clear need for consensus on monitoring protocols to appropriately balance the allocation of healthcare resources (given the large numbers experiencing a single episode) against ensuring adequate safety (in the smaller, harder-to-predict group at risk for biphasic reactions).

There is a concerning absence of guidance regarding advanced pharmacological interventions for biphasic and multiphasic anaphylactic reactions. Treatment protocols for biphasic and multiphasic anaphylaxis primarily adhere to the established management of standard anaphylaxis, focusing on rapid administration of intramuscular epinephrine along with suitable supportive measures. Current guidelines specify an epinephrine dose of 0.01 mg/kg (maximum 0.5 mg). For adults—who generally exceed 50 kg—this is typically simplified to a fixed 0.5 mg dose. Nevertheless, it is notable that many commercially available auto-injectors, including those utilized for the initial two anaphylactic episodes in the current report, deliver only 0.3 mg of epinephrine even when designated for adult use. While epinephrine auto-injectors serve a critical function in out-of-hospital care, they retain utility in clinical environments by decreasing treatment delays and mitigating reluctance among providers who may be less familiar with vasoactive medication administration. Yet, the common use of 0.3 mg auto-injectors can result in substantial underdosing for adults over 50 kg. In a retrospective analysis published in 2025, recipients of an initial 0.3 mg epinephrine dose required escalation of interventions—including subsequent epinephrine doses—at significantly higher rates compared to those who were administered 0.5 mg initially. Facilitating provider transition away from the traditionally favored 0.3 mg dose, coupled with increased accessibility to the recommended dosing regimen, may play a pivotal role in enhancing outcomes in anaphylaxis management.

Historically, corticosteroids have been recommended, partly to mitigate the risk of biphasic reactions. Nonetheless, increasing research reveals a lack of efficacy and possible adverse outcomes, resulting in a trend away from steroid use in the management of anaphylaxis. A 2017 systematic review assessed 22 studies, with only one demonstrating support for corticosteroids, while the remaining 21 provided no evidence that these agents lower the incidence of biphasic anaphylaxis. Additionally, newer studies highlight the significance of adverse events, including a 2019 study that reported a higher rate of ICU or hospital admissions following the use of prehospital corticosteroids. For these reasons, current guidelines largely recommend against the routine administration of corticosteroids; despite this, their use in practice remains common.

Biphasic anaphylaxis, though uncommon, holds substantial clinical importance. The lack of robust prognostic indicators makes risk assessment and monitoring challenging. Proposed risk factors include delayed administration of epinephrine, severe presentation at onset, the necessity for multiple epinephrine injections, and a history of previous biphasic episodes. Nevertheless, dependable markers to guide individualized monitoring have not yet been identified.

Systemic mastocytosis, characterized by pathologic mast cell proliferation, excessive mediator release, and widespread inflammation, increases susceptibility to severe anaphylactic reactions. 1517 Despite this association, no research to date specifically addresses the influence of systemic mastocytosis on biphasic anaphylaxis, likely due to the infrequency of both conditions. Additional studies focused on mast cell diseases and biphasic anaphylaxis could enhance future therapeutic strategies.

Fortunately, the fatality rate from anaphylaxis is low, particularly in areas with prompt emergency medical services. Even more uncommon are deaths resulting from biphasic anaphylaxis, despite its seemingly increased risk. Nevertheless, despite these positive figures, anaphylaxis continues to be a frequently encountered emergency that requires immediate intervention and vigilant observation. While research and clinical guidelines mainly emphasize initial emergency management, ongoing care and surveillance are equally essential. Gaining a deeper insight into complex presentations of anaphylaxis, including biphasic forms, may enable the adoption of more individualized, patient-focused, and resource-conscious approaches to management.

The infrequent occurrence of biphasic, and especially multiphasic, anaphylaxis, along with the inherent difficulties in conducting research during emergency episodes, renders the creation of robust, evidence-based management strategies particularly challenging. In such scenarios, guidelines based on expert consensus offer significant value by providing structured recommendations, yet the formation of these guidelines is restricted by the limited data available. Given the scarcity of these complex anaphylactic events, establishing a registry to collect pertinent patient information, proposed management approaches—as illustrated in our case report—and outcome metrics, could serve as a crucial foundation for generating data-informed, expert-driven recommendations.
