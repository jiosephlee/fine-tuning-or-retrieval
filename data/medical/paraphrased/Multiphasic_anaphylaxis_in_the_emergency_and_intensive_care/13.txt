Title: Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting

Margret Ann Schneider, Hans de Groot, Jan-Willem Geerts

Abstract:

Summary: Anaphylaxis represents the most critical manifestation of allergic reactions and constitutes a systemic emergency that demands immediate and decisive intervention. In certain instances, patients may experience a return of symptoms after initially appearing to recover, even with appropriate treatment. Such biphasic or multiphasic patterns are especially challenging for healthcare providers, as published rates differ, risk factors remain poorly defined, and established management protocols are lacking within current recommendations. This is particularly relevant for multiphasic anaphylaxis, in which individuals have repeated episodes despite adequate response to epinephrine. Given the infrequency of multiphasic anaphylaxis, individual case reports detailing approaches to care offer valuable clinical guidance. This report describes the case of a female in her 60s who exhibited multiphasic anaphylaxis following exposure to clarithromycin; subsequent work-up suggested a strong possibility of systemic mastocytosis, although she did not fulfill all formal diagnostic criteria.

Background:

Anaphylaxis is a serious allergic reaction that can be life-threatening, particularly in cases progressing to anaphylactic shock, and is a frequent reason for emergency department visits. Some patients, however, exhibit a biphasic or even multiphasic pattern, involving an initial reaction to an allergen, subsequent resolution with a symptom-free interval, and then recurrence of symptoms without renewed exposure to the trigger. Such recurrences typically occur within the first 24 hours after initial contact with the allergen, although episodes have been described as late as 78 hours post-exposure. The frequency of biphasic anaphylaxis has been reported from 0.4% to 23.3%, but the majority of studies indicate an incidence between 4% and 6%. Because of the potential for symptom relapse, observation after the apparent resolution of anaphylaxis is commonly recommended. Nonetheless, there are inconsistencies in how biphasic anaphylaxis is defined and variations exist in management guidelines, with recent debate regarding the efficacy of corticosteroids. Multiphasic anaphylaxis, defined by several episodes of symptom recurrence, poses particular difficulties for both management and monitoring strategies. Here, we describe the case of a woman in her sixties who experienced a multiphasic anaphylactic reaction following clarithromycin administration.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 60s, with a known allergy to beta-lactam antibiotics, presented to the emergency department (ED) with anaphylaxis following oral clarithromycin 500 mg.

The patient’s past medical history was notable for type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, psoriasis, and depression. She weighed 104 kg. Her ongoing medications consisted of metformin (500 mg twice daily), gliclazide (80 mg extended release once daily), hydrochlorothiazide (25 mg once daily), omeprazole (20 mg once daily), and sertraline (100 mg once daily). She had last ingested hydrochlorothiazide in the morning, more than 12 hours prior to her presentation to the emergency department.

In adolescence, the patient experienced a mild allergic response to amoxicillin, manifested as facial swelling without oropharyngeal involvement, cutaneous eruption, hypotension, or dyspnea. Additionally, she had a pronounced localized skin reaction to a wasp sting in the past, though there were no accompanying systemic features. The clinical encounter described here represented her initial episode of anaphylaxis.

Five days prior to arriving at the emergency department, she was given a prescription for clindamycin by her primary care physician for treatment of erysipelas. After completing 5 days of clindamycin, the patient developed an unspecified rash on her abdomen. This reaction was considered to be a delayed allergic response to clindamycin, prompting her general practitioner to switch her antibiotic therapy to clarithromycin, a drug she had not previously received.

She later arrived at the emergency department experiencing itching, flushing, increased sweating, agitation, and a sensation of throat swelling. These complaints began one hour after she took her first oral dose of clarithromycin and became progressively more severe. She presented for medical attention one hour after the onset of these symptoms. On initial assessment in the emergency department, vital signs revealed low blood pressure and reduced oxygen saturation (non-invasive blood pressure: 74/50 mm Hg, peripheral oxygen saturation 88% on room air, respiratory rate 22 breaths per minute). Physical examination noted facial swelling, newly developed widespread erythema and urticaria covering her entire body, along with increased sweating and agitation. No enlargement of the tongue or throat was detected, and auscultation of the heart and lungs was unremarkable. Blood glucose measured 12.7 mmol/L, and her electrocardiogram showed a sinus rhythm. She was immediately managed for a presumed severe allergic reaction, receiving a 0.3 mg intramuscular epinephrine injection via auto-injector and a slow intravenous dose of 2 mg clemastine according to local protocols to address her skin symptoms. In addition, she was given oxygen supplementation (15 L via non-rebreather mask) and 500 mL of sodium chloride 0.9% solution for fluid resuscitation during the initial stabilization phase. Oxygen therapy could be discontinued after she responded favorably to the epinephrine, but it needed to be reinitiated during later episodes of anaphylaxis. Clarithromycin was stopped, and she did not receive further doses after the initial oral 500 mg administration.

Initially, the patient exhibited a favorable response to treatment, with normalization of blood pressure and reduction in facial swelling and erythema. She was subsequently transferred to the acute internal medicine unit, where she was subject to enhanced surveillance by specialized nursing staff, although continuous telemetry monitoring was not in place. Despite the initial improvement, within the first 12 hours following admission, the patient experienced repeated occurrences of symptoms indicative of anaphylactic shock. Following the first recurrence, she was moved to the intensive care unit (ICU) for more intensive observation. Each event necessitated intramuscular epinephrine administration for resolution (see figure 1), totaling four episodes of anaphylaxis, including the initial presentation in the emergency department (ED). For the first two episodes, epinephrine auto-injectors delivering 0.3 mg intramuscularly were used, while for the subsequent episodes, manually prepared 0.5 mg intramuscular epinephrine doses were administered. Although the auto-injectors available at our facility deliver a lower dose (0.3 mg) than the standard 0.5 mg recommended, their use is preferred in urgent scenarios—such as initial stabilization in the ED or in areas where anaphylaxis management is infrequent and unfamiliarity may delay timely treatment. After the first anaphylaxis recurrence, corticosteroids were introduced—reflecting their traditional application to prevent biphasic anaphylaxis. She received both intravenous prednisone at 30 mg daily for 5 days and a single intravenous dose of 8 mg dexamethasone, adhering to the differing recommendations outlined in the hospital’s ward and ICU anaphylaxis protocols. Additionally, she was administered intravenous clemastine at 2 mg twice to address cutaneous symptoms, and was provided with an extra 500 mL of normal saline during the second anaphylactic event. Notably, symptom severity diminished with each successive anaphylactic episode, ranging from severe initial presentation involving marked hypoxemia with cyanosis, hypotension, and profound dyspnea, to later episodes marked by dyspnea, rash recurrence, and pruritus.

Early in the course of treatment, the allergology department was consulted to provide expertise and guidance. Two alternative management strategies were evaluated. The first involved continuous intravenous epinephrine infusion as a proactive measure to avert further episodes rather than responding to symptoms as they recurred. The second approach, given the trend of declining symptom severity, was to observe and potentially allow the process to resolve on its own. Each strategy carried significant risks, further complicated by the absence of established literature regarding optimal management in cases of multiphasic anaphylaxis.

Outcome and follow-up:

In the end, neither proposed therapy was required, as the patient experienced no additional episodes of symptom recurrence. After remaining free of recurrent anaphylactic events for around 24 hours, she was moved back to a regular ward. Her hospital stay lasted a total of 3 days, after which she was discharged. At discharge, she began daily levocetirizine antihistamine therapy and was provided with, and instructed in the use of, an epinephrine auto-injector (0.3 mg). She has not reported any further instances of anaphylaxis since then.

Given the unusual and extended course of her illness, further investigation into possible underlying etiologies was pursued. The subsequent workup indicated a high likelihood of systemic mastocytosis; however, the diagnostic criteria for a definitive diagnosis were not fulfilled (only two WHO minor criteria met, with major criteria unable to be evaluated due to inadequate bone marrow sample, see figure 2). Tryptase concentrations measured during the third anaphylactic event, as well as baseline levels, were both elevated and remained persistently high at follow-up with the allergologist, as documented in table 1.

Regrettably, tryptase measurements were omitted during the initial assessment of the patient's first episodes of anaphylaxis, and the suggested time frames for tryptase sampling were therefore not followed. Current guidelines advocate for at least two, and preferably three, tryptase assays to be collected at defined intervals: the first as soon as possible after symptom onset—without delaying management—the second between 1 and 2 hours (but no later than 4 hours) post-symptom onset, corresponding to the expected peak in tryptase concentration, and a third sample a minimum of 24 hours after all symptoms have resolved to determine the baseline tryptase. In this patient's case, the earliest tryptase specimen was obtained one hour following the third anaphylactic reaction, and the subsequent sample was collected 27 hours after the most recent episode of anaphylaxis.

No confirmatory allergy testing was performed, as the risk of provoking another severe anaphylactic event with procedures such as skin testing was considered unjustifiable, especially given the feasibility of completely avoiding additional exposures. Additional findings supportive of a diagnosis of systemic mastocytosis included detection of hepatomegaly on abdominal ultrasound and the presence of possible neuropsychiatric symptoms such as depression and memory deficits.

Discussion:

Anaphylaxis is generally recognized as a distinct clinical entity, marked by the acute onset and swift progression of symptoms affecting the airway, respiratory function, cardiovascular system, and/or gastrointestinal tract, and is often accompanied by cutaneous or mucosal signs such as urticaria, angioedema, or flushing. The identification of a probable allergen can assist with diagnosis; however, determining a definite trigger is not always achievable.

Atypical forms, such as biphasic or refractory anaphylaxis, lack precise definitions, though recent initiatives have sought to create consensus criteria for these scenarios. According to the Resuscitation Council UK, biphasic anaphylaxis is described as a recurrence of symptoms several hours after apparent resolution, without any re-exposure to the offending allergen. Distinguishing between a genuine biphasic event and persistent anaphylaxis with only temporary improvement following epinephrine, or ongoing allergen uptake (for example, due to remaining food particles or slow-release drugs), can present significant diagnostic difficulties. In contrast, refractory anaphylaxis is characterized by ongoing symptoms despite the correct administration of epinephrine and little or no clinical response.

Existing guidelines for biphasic anaphylaxis are primarily focused on recommendations for clinical surveillance. However, there remains ongoing debate regarding the ideal length of monitoring. Traditionally, observation periods after resolution of symptoms have varied from 4 to 6 hours. Both the Dutch Nederlands Huisartsen Genootschap (NHG) and the World Allergy Organisation acknowledge that nearly half of biphasic reactions manifest within 6–12 hours of the initial presentation, but neither specifies an exact duration for patient monitoring. Conversely, the 2021 UK guideline introduces a three-level risk-stratified protocol for monitoring. There is a pressing need for unified observation recommendations to navigate the challenge of efficiently allocating medical resources for the majority of single anaphylactic cases while ensuring adequate safety measures for the smaller subset at risk for biphasic reactions.

Guidance for advanced pharmacologic approaches in cases with biphasic or multiphasic progression is notably insufficient. The therapeutic approach to biphasic and multiphasic anaphylaxis is essentially the same as that for standard anaphylactic reactions, involving immediate intramuscular administration of epinephrine alongside necessary supportive interventions. Current guidelines suggest an epinephrine dose of 0.01 mg/kg, not exceeding a maximum of 0.5 mg in total. For adults, this is often rounded up to 0.5 mg, as most adults surpass a body weight of 50 kg. Nevertheless, it is worth noting that many epinephrine auto-injectors, including those utilized for the initial two episodes in the case under discussion, are limited to 0.3 mg of epinephrine, even for adult patients. While auto-injectors are crucial tools in community settings, their use can also be advantageous in healthcare facilities—by decreasing the time to first dose and reducing hesitation among providers infrequently managing vasoactive medications like epinephrine. Despite being frequently used, administering a 0.3 mg auto-injector to adults over 50 kg can result in clinically significant underdosing. Data from a retrospective review in 2025 indicate that patients who initially received 0.3 mg epinephrine required escalation measures, including further doses, at a much higher rate compared to those initially given 0.5 mg. Addressing obstacles such as practitioners’ reliance on the traditionally recommended 0.3 mg dose and the widespread availability of 0.3 mg auto-injectors, and increasing compliance with recommended dosing regimens, has the potential to meaningfully enhance outcomes in anaphylaxis management.

Historically, corticosteroids were advised in part to mitigate the risk of biphasic anaphylactic reactions. However, accumulating data now indicate a lack of efficacy and the possibility of harm, resulting in a contemporary move away from steroid administration in anaphylaxis management. A 2017 systematic review assessed 22 studies, with only one demonstrating support for corticosteroid use and 21 studies failing to find evidence that corticosteroids decrease the incidence of biphasic anaphylaxis. Additionally, recent publications highlight concerns about adverse effects, including a 2019 study that reported a higher rate of ICU or hospital admissions following prehospital corticosteroid use. As a result, most current guidelines do not support routine use of corticosteroids, though their application remains common in clinical settings.

Biphasic anaphylaxis, while uncommon, remains clinically significant. Reliable risk factors are lacking, making it challenging to determine appropriate observation periods and identify patients at increased risk. Proposed risk factors include delayed administration of epinephrine, severe initial symptoms, repeated epinephrine dosing, and a history of biphasic anaphylaxis, but no consistently reliable predictors have been established for selective monitoring.

Systemic mastocytosis is characterized by a pathological accumulation of mast cells, leading to excessive mediator release and inflammation in multiple organs, and increases susceptibility to severe anaphylactic episodes.1517 However, due to the rarity of both systemic mastocytosis and biphasic anaphylaxis, existing literature does not specifically investigate the relationship between them. Additional studies focusing on mast cell diseases and biphasic anaphylaxis are warranted to improve future management strategies.

Thankfully, deaths from anaphylaxis are uncommon, particularly in areas where emergency healthcare services are readily accessible. Moreover, deaths resulting from biphasic anaphylaxis are even less frequent, despite their seemingly significant risk. Nevertheless, despite these encouraging figures, anaphylaxis continues to be a prevalent acute condition requiring immediate intervention and close observation. While research and guidelines predominantly concentrate on the provision of initial emergency treatment, ongoing medical care and patient monitoring are also crucial elements. Improved insight into intricate presentations, such as biphasic anaphylaxis, may support the development of more personalized, patient-focused, and resource-conscious approaches to managing anaphylaxis.

The infrequency of biphasic and especially multiphasic forms of anaphylaxis, along with the obstacles faced in conducting research within emergency settings, renders the creation of genuinely evidence-based protocols particularly difficult. In these circumstances, expert consensus guidelines offering explicit management pathways are extremely valuable, yet the formulation of such recommendations is also restricted by the limited available evidence. Given the scarcity of these complex cases, establishing a registry that documents key patient characteristics, proposed management strategies—such as those outlined in this case report—and outcomes could serve as an essential resource for forming data-supported expert guidance.
