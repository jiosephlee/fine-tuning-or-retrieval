Title: Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting

Margret Ann Schneider, Hans de Groot, Jan-Willem Geerts

Abstract:

Summary: Anaphylaxis represents the most acute and dangerous manifestation of allergic reactions, characterized by widespread and potentially fatal consequences necessitating immediate intervention. Some patients experience a recurrence of symptoms after initially responding well to treatment, termed biphasic or even multiphasic reactions. These patterns pose significant challenges for healthcare providers, particularly given inconsistent reporting of incidence, uncertain risk factors, and an absence of well-defined therapeutic recommendations in existing guidelines. Multiphasic anaphylaxis, marked by repeated episodes following successful administration of epinephrine, presents particular complexity. Because multiphasic anaphylaxis is seldom seen, published case reports are valuable in informing approaches to clinical care. In this report, we describe a case involving a female patient in her 60s who developed multiphasic anaphylaxis after clarithromycin administration. Subsequent assessment suggested probable systemic mastocytosis, although formal diagnostic thresholds were not satisfied.

Background:

Anaphylaxis is a serious allergic reaction that can be fatal, particularly in instances of anaphylactic shock, and frequently leads to visits in emergency settings. Certain cases display a biphasic or multiphasic pattern, wherein an initial reaction to an allergen resolves, followed by a symptom-free interval, and then a return of symptoms without subsequent exposure to the triggering agent. These recurrences are typically seen within the first 24 hours post-exposure, though documentation exists of episodes reemerging as late as 78 hours afterwards. The reported frequency of biphasic anaphylaxis ranges significantly, from 0.4% up to 23.3%, but the majority of literature estimates this rate to fall between 4% and 6%. Given the potential for symptom relapse, post-anaphylaxis observation is standard, even when symptoms have disappeared. Nonetheless, there is variability in the definitions and management recommendations for biphasic anaphylaxis, with recent scrutiny regarding the effectiveness of corticosteroids. Multiphasic anaphylaxis, involving several successive symptom relapses, poses unique complexities in terms of management and monitoring strategies. This report details the case of a female patient in her 60s who developed a multiphasic anaphylactic reaction in response to clarithromycin.

Case presentation:

A woman in her sixties, with a documented allergy to beta-lactam antibiotics, arrived at the emergency department (ED) experiencing anaphylaxis after ingesting clarithromycin 500 mg orally.

Her past medical history included type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, psoriasis, and depression. She weighed 104 kg. The medications she was taking were metformin (500 mg twice daily), gliclazide (80 mg extended release once daily), hydrochlorothiazide (25 mg once daily), omeprazole (20 mg once daily), and sertraline (100 mg once daily). The last dose of hydrochlorothiazide had been taken in the morning, more than 12 hours prior to her ED visit.

Previously, the patient experienced a mild allergic reaction to amoxicillin during her teenage years, which was limited to swelling of the face only, without oropharyngeal swelling, rash, hypotension, or shortness of breath. She also had a pronounced localized skin reaction to a wasp sting in the past, which was not accompanied by systemic manifestations. The current episode represented her first occurrence of anaphylaxis.

Five days before presenting to the ED, clindamycin was prescribed by her primary care provider for erysipelas. After five days of clindamycin therapy, she developed an abdominal rash, with no further details documented. This was deemed a delayed allergic reaction to clindamycin, leading her general practitioner to change her antibiotic treatment to clarithromycin, which she had not previously received.

She later arrived at the emergency department experiencing pruritus, flushing, excessive sweating, restlessness, and a sensation of throat swelling. These symptoms began approximately one hour after she ingested her first clarithromycin dose and progressively intensified. She presented to medical staff one hour following symptom onset. On arrival, her vital signs revealed hypotension and hypoxaemia (non-invasive blood pressure: 74/50 mm Hg; oxygen saturation: 88% on room air; respiratory rate: 22 breaths per minute). Physical examination identified facial edema, widespread new-onset erythema, generalized urticaria, increased sweating, and agitation. There was no evidence of tongue or throat swelling, and both cardiac and pulmonary auscultations were normal. Her blood glucose measured 12.7 mmol/L, and electrocardiogram demonstrated sinus rhythm. Immediate therapy was administered for a suspected severe allergic reaction, including 0.3 mg epinephrine intramuscularly via auto-injector and a slow intravenous dose of 2 mg clemastine in accordance with local protocols to address the cutaneous manifestations. Additional treatment included high-flow oxygen (15 L via a non-rebreather mask) and a 500 mL bolus of normal saline (0.9% sodium chloride solution) for initial stabilization. Oxygen supplementation was discontinued after she responded well to epinephrine, though it needed to be reinstated briefly during later episodes of anaphylaxis. Clarithromycin was stopped, and she received no further doses following the initial 500 mg oral administration.

There was an initial favorable reaction to therapy, marked by normalization of blood pressure and reduction in facial edema and erythema. The patient was transferred to the acute internal medicine ward, which affords enhanced observation from specialized nursing personnel, but lacks telemetry monitoring. Nonetheless, within the first 12 hours post-admission, and in spite of the early improvement, the patient experienced repeated episodes consistent with anaphylactic shock. Following the first recurrence, the patient was moved to the intensive care unit (ICU) for advanced observation. Every episode necessitated the administration of intramuscular epinephrine (figure 1), amounting to a total of four anaphylactic events, including the initial presentation in the emergency department. The first two doses—0.3 mg each—were given via an auto-injector intramuscularly, while the subsequent two doses—0.5 mg each—were administered as individually prepared intramuscular injections. Although our clinic’s auto-injectors contain a lower-than-recommended epinephrine dose (0.3 mg instead of 0.5 mg), we favor this approach in urgent scenarios, such as patient stabilization in the emergency department, or in contexts where emergency medication use may not be routine, as familiarity with auto-injectors may reduce treatment delays. After the initial recurrence, corticosteroid therapy was introduced, in accordance with their established use in attempting to prevent biphasic anaphylaxis. She received daily intravenous prednisone 30 mg for 5 days, and a single 8 mg intravenous dose of dexamethasone, reflecting discrepancies between the general ward and ICU anaphylaxis protocols at our institution. In addition, the patient was given 2 mg of clemastine intravenously on two occasions for dermatologic symptoms, as well as an extra 500 mL of normal saline during the second anaphylactic event. Notably, the severity of clinical features diminished with successive episodes, progressing from profound hypoxaemia with cyanosis, hypotension, and pronounced dyspnea initially, to primarily dyspnea and return of rash with pruritus during the last occurrence.

Specialist input from the allergology department was sought early for expert management recommendations. Two principal alternative therapeutic strategies were contemplated in this case. The first was the preemptive continuous intravenous infusion of epinephrine, intended to forestall further episodes of anaphylaxis rather than awaiting symptom re-emergence. Alternatively, given the apparent decline in symptom intensity, a conservative approach was also considered to monitor for possible spontaneous resolution. Each option entailed significant risk, compounded by the lack of literature-guided protocols in the context of multiphasic anaphylaxis.

Outcome and follow-up:

In the end, both treatment strategies were deemed unnecessary since the patient did not experience any recurrence of symptoms. After remaining free of anaphylactic episodes for roughly 24 hours, she was moved to a regular care ward. Her hospital stay lasted a total of 3 days, after which she was discharged. Levocetirizine, an antihistamine, was prescribed as a routine medication, and she received an epinephrine auto-injector (0.3 mg) along with instruction on its use. She has not had any additional anaphylactic events since.

Given the unusual and extended course of her illness, further investigations into potential underlying etiologies were initiated. This workup indicated a significant likelihood of systemic mastocytosis, but the criteria for definitive diagnosis were not met—only two of the WHO minor criteria were fulfilled, with major criteria unable to be evaluated owing to insufficient sample from the bone marrow biopsy (refer to figure 2). Regrettably, tryptase measurements were not performed during the initial assessment of the patient’s early anaphylactic episodes, resulting in non-compliance with established time frames for obtaining tryptase levels. Current guidelines suggest collecting at least two, and preferably three, tryptase samples at designated intervals: the first should be acquired as soon as practically possible without postponing emergency care; the second should follow 1–2 hours after symptom onset, but not later than 4 hours, capturing peak tryptase concentrations; and the third should be obtained no less than 24 hours after complete clinical resolution, to serve as a baseline. In the present case, the first tryptase was drawn 1 hour subsequent to the third anaphylactic event, while the second sample was collected 27 hours after the final recurrence.

No allergy provocation or confirmation test was conducted due to concerns that such testing, including cutaneous testing, would pose a disproportionate risk of inducing another severe anaphylactic reaction—especially given the relative simplicity of avoiding further exposure. Additional findings consistent with systemic mastocytosis comprised hepatomegaly observed on abdominal ultrasonography and potential neuropsychiatric manifestations, such as depressive symptoms and memory difficulties.

Discussion:

Anaphylaxis is generally a well-recognized clinical condition, marked by an abrupt onset and swift worsening of symptoms impacting the airway, breathing, circulatory system, and/or gastrointestinal tract, and frequently associated with skin or mucous membrane signs such as urticaria, angioedema, or flushing. Though pinpointing a probable allergen can aid in diagnosis, identifying the exact precipitating factor is not always feasible.

More complicated presentations, such as biphasic or refractory anaphylaxis, are less clearly characterized, but recent initiatives have sought to create standardized definitions for these cases as well. According to the Resuscitation Council UK, biphasic anaphylaxis is described as the return of symptoms hours after the initial resolution, without renewed allergen exposure. It can be difficult to distinguish authentic biphasic reactions from ongoing anaphylaxis presenting as only temporary improvement after epinephrine or from sustained allergen presence due to residual food or prolonged-release drugs. In contrast, refractory anaphylaxis refers to a persistent reaction that does not adequately respond to appropriately administered epinephrine.

Guidelines regarding biphasic anaphylaxis chiefly focus on recommendations for clinical observation; however, the optimal length of monitoring is still debated. Conventionally, observation periods have ranged from 4 to 6 hours after symptoms have settled. The Dutch Nederlands Huisartsen Genootschap (NHG) and the World Allergy Organisation guideline observe that close to half of biphasic episodes occur within 6–12 hours of the initial incident, yet neither details explicit monitoring intervals. Differently, the UK guideline published in 2021 suggests a risk-stratified, three-level system for observation. There is a clear need for standardized observation protocols to address the challenge of aligning resource allocation—especially with the large number of patients experiencing a single episode—with safeguarding against the rare but severe risks faced by those who develop biphasic reactions.

There is a notable and concerning absence of guidance regarding advanced pharmacological interventions for biphasic and multiphasic anaphylaxis. The therapeutic approach to these presentations generally parallels conventional anaphylaxis management, involving immediate administration of intramuscular epinephrine alongside suitable supportive measures. Guidelines advise a dose of 0.01 mg/kg of epinephrine, capping at a total maximum of 0.5 mg. For adults, this is commonly rounded up to 0.5 mg, since most adult patients weigh more than 50 kg. Of interest, many auto-injector devices—including those utilized for the initial two anaphylactic occurrences in the case described here—provide only 0.3 mg of epinephrine per dose, even though they are marketed for adult use. While epinephrine auto-injectors are crucial for prehospital management, their value persists within medical facilities, where their use can shorten time to treatment and mitigate reluctance among staff infrequently administering vasoactive medications like epinephrine. Nonetheless, administering a 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injector, though frequent, may result in a potentially significant underdose for adult patients weighing more than 50 kg. A retrospective study published in 2025 found that individuals initially treated with 0.3 mg epinephrine required advanced interventions, such as subsequent epinephrine doses, significantly more often than those who were given an initial 0.5 mg dose. Addressing obstacles—including clinician preference for the historically recommended 0.3 mg, and the widespread use of 0.3 mg auto-injectors—and ensuring greater conformity to guideline-based epinephrine dosing may substantially enhance outcomes for anaphylaxis patients.

Historically, corticosteroid therapy was advised partially to decrease the risk of biphasic reactions. Nonetheless, increasing research indicates not only an absence of benefit but also possible negative effects, prompting a recent move away from steroid use in anaphylaxis management. A systematic review from 2017 included 22 studies; only one supported corticosteroid usage, while the remaining 21 did not demonstrate any reduction in biphasic anaphylaxis risk. Moreover, recent investigations highlight the significance of adverse effects, with a 2019 study reporting higher rates of ICU or ward admission following prehospital corticosteroid treatment. Therefore, most recent guidelines recommend against standard corticosteroid administration for anaphylaxis, though their use persists in practice.

Biphasic anaphylaxis is uncommon but of substantial clinical importance. The lack of reliable indicators makes risk assessment and monitoring challenging. Proposed risk factors are delayed epinephrine administration, a severe initial reaction, receipt of several epinephrine doses, and previous biphasic events. Despite these suggestions, dependable predictors to guide targeted observation are not currently established.

Systemic mastocytosis is characterized by excessive mast cell accumulation, heightened mediator release, and widespread inflammatory involvement, making affected patients more susceptible to severe anaphylaxis. 1517 Nevertheless, both conditions are infrequent, and at present, there is no research explicitly evaluating the impact of systemic mastocytosis on biphasic anaphylaxis. Further studies focusing on mast cell disorders and biphasic reactions may be instrumental in shaping future management strategies.

Anaphylaxis-related mortality rates are generally low, particularly in areas where emergency medical services are readily accessible. Deaths resulting from biphasic anaphylactic reactions are even less frequent, despite the condition’s potentially severe nature. Nevertheless, anaphylaxis continues to be a prevalent acute medical emergency that requires immediate intervention and vigilant observation. While research and established guidelines mainly emphasize initial acute management, ongoing post-acute care and surveillance are equally critical. Enhanced comprehension of the nuanced presentations of complex anaphylactic reactions, such as biphasic anaphylaxis, may enable more individualized, efficient, and effective management approaches for patients.

Due to the infrequent occurrence of biphasic and especially multiphasic anaphylaxis, along with intrinsic research challenges in emergency settings, formulating thoroughly evidence-based management protocols remains difficult. In these scenarios, expert consensus guidelines with specific recommendations are invaluable; however, their formulation is also limited by insufficient data. Considering the scarcity of these complex anaphylactic events, the establishment of a registry cataloging such cases—including relevant patient information, proposed management strategies, as demonstrated in our case report, and outcome measures—may lay the foundation for more robust, data-informed expert consensus recommendations.
