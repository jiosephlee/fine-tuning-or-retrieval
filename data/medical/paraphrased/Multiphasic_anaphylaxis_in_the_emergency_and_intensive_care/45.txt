Title: Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting

Margret Ann Schneider, Hans de Groot, Jan-Willem Geerts

Abstract:

Summary: Anaphylaxis represents the most serious allergic reaction and poses a risk for mortality unless managed swiftly and appropriately. In some instances, symptoms recur after initial resolution despite appropriate treatment, resulting in biphasic or even multiphasic courses that complicate clinical management. These patterns are particularly challenging due to inconsistent reports of frequency, uncertain risk factors, and a lack of definitive recommendations in current protocols. Multiphasic anaphylaxis, characterized by repeated episodes following satisfactory response to standard interventions such as epinephrine, is particularly difficult to address. Because multiphasic anaphylaxis is uncommon, individual case reports are valuable sources of information regarding management approaches. Here, we describe a case involving a female patient in her 60s who experienced multiphasic anaphylaxis triggered by clarithromycin. Further evaluation suggested a high probability of systemic mastocytosis, although the patient did not fulfill established diagnostic requirements.

Background:

Anaphylaxis represents a severe allergic response that can be life-threatening, particularly when manifesting as anaphylactic shock, and frequently prompts emergency medical attention. Nonetheless, some instances display a biphasic or even multiphasic progression, which consists of an initial allergic reaction triggered by exposure, followed by a symptom-free interval, and then subsequent reappearance of symptoms in the absence of further allergen contact. These recurrent episodes typically occur within 24 hours post-exposure, but documented cases have reported relapse as late as 78 hours afterwards. The prevalence of biphasic anaphylaxis is reported with considerable variability, from 0.4% to 23.3%, though the majority of literature estimates an incidence between 4% and 6%. Because relapse remains a possibility, patients are often recommended for observation following resolution of anaphylactic symptoms. However, definitions surrounding biphasic anaphylaxis lack uniformity and management recommendations differ, with recent discourse questioning the efficacy of corticosteroids. Cases of multiphasic anaphylaxis, involving numerous symptom recurrences, pose distinct challenges for treatment approaches and observation protocols. Here, we describe the case of a woman in her 60s who experienced a multiphasic anaphylactic reaction following exposure to clarithromycin.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 60s with a documented beta-lactam antibiotic allergy arrived at the emergency department with anaphylaxis after ingesting 500 mg oral clarithromycin.

Her past medical history included type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, psoriasis, and depression. She weighed 104 kg. Her current medications were metformin 500 mg twice daily, gliclazide 80 mg sustained release once daily, hydrochlorothiazide 25 mg once daily, omeprazole 20 mg once daily, and sertraline 100 mg once daily. Her last dose of hydrochlorothiazide had been taken in the morning, more than 12 hours prior to presenting to the ED.

During adolescence, the patient had experienced a mild allergic reaction to amoxicillin, characterized by facial swelling alone, without oropharyngeal involvement, rash, hypotension, or shortness of breath. She had also previously exhibited a pronounced localized skin reaction to a wasp sting but did not develop any systemic symptoms. The current event represented her first occurrence of anaphylaxis.

Five days before her ED visit, the patient’s general practitioner prescribed clindamycin to treat erysipelas. After 5 days of clindamycin, she developed a rash on her abdomen (not further specified), believed to be a delayed allergic reaction to clindamycin. In response, her general practitioner changed her antibiotic to clarithromycin, to which she had not previously been exposed.

She later arrived at the emergency department experiencing itching, flushing, excessive sweating, restlessness, and a sensation of throat swelling. These manifestations began one hour following ingestion of her first clarithromycin dose, and gradually intensified. She presented for emergency care an hour after symptoms first appeared. Upon arrival, her vital signs indicated low blood pressure and hypoxemia (non-invasive blood pressure at 74/50 mm Hg, peripheral oxygen saturation at 88% while breathing room air, and respiratory rate at 22 breaths per minute). Physical examination revealed swelling of the face, newly developed widespread erythema, generalized urticaria, increased perspiration, and agitation. There was no observed swelling of the tongue or throat, and cardiac as well as pulmonary auscultation yielded normal findings. Her blood glucose was measured at 12.7 mmol/L, and electrocardiogram demonstrated sinus rhythm. Immediate management for suspected severe allergic reaction included intramuscular administration of 0.3 mg epinephrine via auto-injector and slow intravenous administration of 2 mg clemastine to address the dermatologic symptoms according to local protocols. In addition, she was provided with supplemental oxygen (15 L/min via non-rebreather mask) and 500 mL of isotonic saline (0.9% sodium chloride) during initial stabilization. Oxygen supplementation could be discontinued after a favorable response to epinephrine, but needed to be temporarily restarted during subsequent episodes of anaphylaxis. Clarithromycin was stopped, and no further doses were administered following the initial oral 500 mg.

At first, the patient demonstrated a favorable reaction to therapy, with normalization of blood pressure and reduction in both facial edema and erythema. She was admitted to the acute internal ward for enhanced observation by specialized nursing staff, although without telemetry monitoring. Despite the initial improvement, she subsequently experienced multiple episodes consistent with anaphylactic shock within the first twelve hours of hospital admission. Following the initial recurrence of anaphylactic shock, the patient was transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) for closer surveillance. Each occurrence necessitated intramuscular epinephrine administration for resolution (see figure 1), amounting to four total anaphylactic events including the initial presentation in the emergency department (ED). The first two epinephrine treatments were administered via auto-injector, each containing 0.3 mg of epinephrine intramuscularly, while the third and fourth episodes were treated with individually drawn 0.5 mg epinephrine intramuscular doses. Although our clinic's auto-injectors deliver a lower dose (0.3 mg) than the recommended 0.5 mg, we prefer their use in time-critical contexts—such as initial stabilization in the ED—or situations where anaphylaxis treatment is not commonplace and unfamiliarity with medications may otherwise postpone intervention. After the first anaphylaxis recurrence, corticosteroid therapy was introduced as a supplementary measure due to their established role in preventing biphasic anaphylaxis. The patient received both intravenous prednisone at a dosage of 30 mg daily for 5 days and a single intravenous dose of dexamethasone 8 mg, reflecting differences between our hospital’s general ward and ICU anaphylaxis protocols. Additionally, she was administered 2 mg of clemastine intravenously on two occasions for skin manifestations and an extra 500 mL of normal saline during the second anaphylactic event. Notably, the severity of her anaphylaxis episodes progressively diminished, beginning with significant hypoxaemia, cyanosis, hypotension, and severe dyspnea, and later presenting as dyspnea, rash recurrence, and pruritus in the final episode.

The allergology department was consulted promptly to provide expert management recommendations. Two potential therapeutic strategies were explored in this case. The first involved continuous epinephrine infusion via intravenous pump to proactively manage anaphylaxis rather than reacting to repeated symptom onset. Alternatively, given the gradually declining symptom severity, a watchful waiting strategy was considered to observe whether the episodes would resolve without intervention. Both strategies were associated with substantial risks, compounded by the absence of evidence-based guidelines in multiphasic anaphylaxis.

Outcome and follow-up:

In the end, neither therapeutic option was needed because the patient experienced no recurrence of symptoms beyond this time. After remaining free of new anaphylactic events for approximately 24 hours, she was moved to a regular care unit. Her hospitalization concluded after a total of three days. Upon discharge, levocetirizine was prescribed as a maintenance antihistamine, and she was both supplied with and instructed in the proper use of a 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injector. She has not reported any additional anaphylactic episodes since.

Given the unusual and extended nature of the clinical course, an investigation into possible underlying etiologies was initiated. This evaluation suggested a significant likelihood of systemic mastocytosis; however, the criteria for a definitive diagnosis were not met (two WHO minor criteria present, while major criteria could not be assessed due to inadequate bone marrow sample, as depicted in figure 2). During her third bout of anaphylaxis, tryptase levels were elevated; both the baseline and subsequent follow-up measurements via allergy specialist remained high, as detailed in table 1.

Tryptase concentrations were not assessed during the initial evaluation following the first anaphylactic events, resulting in deviation from the recommended time points for obtaining tryptase levels. According to current guidelines, at least two, and preferably three, tryptase measurements should be collected at designated intervals: the first sample should be obtained as soon as possible without delaying urgent care, the second within 1–2 hours (and up to 4 hours) after symptom onset—coinciding with peak tryptase—and a third specimen at least 24 hours post-symptom resolution to determine the patient’s baseline level. In the reported case, the initial tryptase sample was drawn one hour after the third anaphylactic event, while the subsequent measurement was taken 27 hours following the final anaphylactic recurrence.

Allergy confirmation testing was not conducted, as the risk of provoking another severe anaphylactic reaction—such as via skin testing—was judged to be unjustified, particularly given that additional exposure could be readily prevented. Additional pertinent clinical features consistent with systemic mastocytosis were observed, including hepatomegaly detected on abdominal ultrasound and possible neuropsychiatric manifestations such as depression and memory impairment.

Discussion:

Anaphylaxis is generally recognized as a clinical syndrome with distinctive diagnostic features, marked by the abrupt development and quick escalation of symptoms affecting the airway, respiratory and cardiovascular systems, or the gastrointestinal tract, often with accompanying skin or mucosal findings such as urticaria, angioedema, or erythema. Although identifying a plausible allergen can reinforce the diagnosis, it is not always feasible to ascertain an exact precipitant.

Presentations involving biphasic or refractory anaphylaxis are less thoroughly described; however, recent initiatives have sought to create standard definitions for these forms as well. According to the Resuscitation Council UK, biphasic anaphylaxis is defined as symptom recurrence several hours after apparent resolution and without re-exposure to the allergen. It can be difficult to distinguish true biphasic events from prolonged anaphylaxis that temporarily responds to epinephrine or from ongoing allergen absorption, such as that from residual ingested material or slow-release drugs. By contrast, refractory anaphylaxis describes a persistent condition not resolving or inadequately improving despite proper epinephrine administration.

Guidelines pertaining to biphasic anaphylaxis focus largely on post-event observation. Nonetheless, the ideal length of monitoring remains unsettled. Conventional advice is to observe patients for 4 to 6 hours after symptoms resolve. The Dutch Nederlands Huisartsen Genootschap (NHG) and World Allergy Organisation guidelines state that roughly half of biphasic episodes arise 6 to 12 hours following the original reaction, yet neither specifies an exact observation timeframe. Conversely, the UK guideline 2021 introduces a risk-based, tiered framework for patient monitoring. There is a recognized need for consensus protocols to reconcile the requirements of patient safety for those at risk of complications from biphasic reactions, against the broader need to manage healthcare resources for patients presenting with a single episode of anaphylaxis.

There is a notable and concerning deficiency in guidance regarding advanced pharmacologic approaches for biphasic and multiphasic reactions. Treatment of these anaphylaxis courses generally follows the same protocols as standard anaphylaxis, prioritizing immediate administration of intramuscular epinephrine alongside adequate supportive measures. Clinical guidelines suggest an epinephrine dose of 0.01 mg/kg body weight, not to exceed a total of 0.5 mg, which is routinely rounded up to 0.5 mg for adults given that most surpass 50 kg in weight. Nevertheless, a noteworthy issue is that many commercially available auto-injectors, including those utilized for the initial two episodes in the present case, deliver only 0.3 mg of epinephrine, even for adult patients. While auto-injectors serve a pivotal function prior to hospital care, they also have utility within medical facilities, as they may reduce initiation delays and help circumvent hesitation among staff who infrequently use vasoactive medications like epinephrine. Still, administering 0.3 mg by auto-injector, although prevalent, can result in substantial underdosing for adults over 50 kg. A 2025 retrospective analysis found that individuals initially receiving 0.3 mg were significantly more likely to require further interventions, such as additional epinephrine doses, compared to those given an initial 0.5 mg dose. Addressing obstacles, such as clinicians’ comfort with the traditional 0.3 mg amount and the widespread presence of 0.3 mg auto-injectors, in tandem with enhancing compliance to the recommended epinephrine doses, has the potential to meaningfully advance the management of anaphylaxis.

Historically, corticosteroid therapy was recommended partly to mitigate biphasic reactions in anaphylaxis. Nevertheless, accumulating data now indicate not only a lack of benefit, but also potential risks associated with their use, resulting in recent changes in practice away from steroid administration in this context. A systematic review published in 2017 included 22 studies: only one found support for corticosteroids, whereas 21 showed no evidence that these agents decrease the incidence of biphasic anaphylaxis. In addition, newer studies highlight the importance of adverse effects; notably, a 2019 investigation found an increased probability of ICU or hospital admission following prehospital corticosteroid treatment. Therefore, most current guidelines advise against routine corticosteroid use, although these medications are still often administered in practice.

Although biphasic anaphylaxis occurs infrequently, it is of significant clinical importance. The lack of dependable predictors complicates both risk assessment and decisions regarding patient monitoring. Proposed risk factors comprise delayed administration of epinephrine, severe initial symptoms, multiple epinephrine doses, and a history of biphasic reactions. Nonetheless, no robust predictors exist to guide selective monitoring.

Systemic mastocytosis is characterized by abnormal mast cell proliferation, excessive mediator secretion, and inflammation affecting multiple organs, increasing vulnerability to severe anaphylactic events. 1517 Despite the association, both conditions are rare, and, to date, no research has specifically examined the influence of systemic mastocytosis on biphasic anaphylaxis. Additional investigations into mast cell pathologies and biphasic anaphylaxis may contribute important information for optimizing care.

Anaphylaxis-related mortality remains low, particularly in areas where emergency care is readily accessible. Additionally, deaths due to biphasic anaphylaxis are exceedingly uncommon, even though the condition is potentially life-threatening. Despite these reassuring figures, anaphylaxis is a frequently encountered emergency that necessitates immediate intervention and appropriate surveillance. Although research and guidelines tend to emphasize acute emergency care, the significance of ongoing management and observation should not be underestimated. Improved comprehension of complicated anaphylaxis presentations, such as biphasic reactions, may contribute to developing more precise, patient-focused, and resource-conscious management approaches.

The infrequency of biphasic and especially multiphasic anaphylaxis, along with the practical challenges associated with conducting research during emergent scenarios, renders the creation of robust evidence-based management strategies particularly difficult. In these settings, expert consensus guidelines with explicit recommendations serve as an important alternative, though their formulation is restricted by limited data availability. Given the unusual nature of these complex cases, compiling a registry that captures key patient characteristics, proposed management approaches like those outlined in this report, and outcomes could form a foundation for generating data-driven, expert-led clinical recommendations.
