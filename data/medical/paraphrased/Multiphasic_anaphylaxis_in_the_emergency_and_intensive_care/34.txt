Title: Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting

Margret Ann Schneider, Hans de Groot, Jan-Willem Geerts

Abstract:

Summary: Anaphylaxis represents the most acute manifestation of allergic reactions and constitutes a systemic event that may be fatal without swift and appropriate intervention. In certain instances, patients experience a resurgence of symptoms after initial resolution, despite receiving optimal care and demonstrating a complete therapeutic response. Such biphasic or multiphasic progressions pose particular difficulties for medical practitioners, owing to inconsistent reporting of their occurrence, ambiguous predisposing factors, and an absence of defined treatment recommendations in existing guidelines. This challenge is particularly pronounced in multiphasic anaphylaxis, which involves repeated episodes of anaphylaxis in the face of appropriate epinephrine therapy. Given the relative rarity of multiphasic anaphylaxis, documented case reports serve as valuable resources for clinical guidance. The present report describes a woman in her sixth decade of life who developed multiphasic anaphylaxis attributed to clarithromycin. Subsequent evaluation suggested a strong probability of systemic mastocytosis, although she failed to satisfy formal diagnostic criteria.

Background:

Anaphylaxis is a serious allergic reaction that may become life-threatening, especially in the form of anaphylactic shock, and frequently leads individuals to seek emergency medical attention. In certain patients, the clinical course is biphasic or even multiphasic, beginning with an initial anaphylactic episode triggered by exposure to an allergen, followed by a period of symptom resolution during which the patient is asymptomatic, and subsequently symptoms recur despite no additional exposure to the causative agent. Typically, these recurrences occur within the first 24 hours post-exposure, although some cases have documented the return of symptoms as late as 78 hours after the original event. The documented rate of biphasic anaphylaxis ranges substantially, from 0.4% to 23.3%, though most evidence indicates an incidence of 4% to 6%. Because of the potential for symptom relapse, patients are generally monitored for a period after apparent resolution of anaphylactic symptoms. Nevertheless, inconsistent definitions of biphasic anaphylaxis and varying therapeutic guidelines exist, with recent years seeing debate around the use of corticosteroids. Multiphasic anaphylaxis, involving multiple episodes of recurring symptoms, poses additional challenges for management and observation strategies. This report describes the case of a woman in her sixties who developed a multiphasic anaphylactic reaction following administration of clarithromycin.

Case presentation:

A female patient in her 60s, with a documented beta-lactam allergy, arrived at the emergency department (ED) experiencing anaphylaxis after ingesting clarithromycin 500 mg orally.

Her past medical history included type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, psoriasis, and depression. She weighed 104 kg. Current medications consisted of metformin (500 mg twice daily), gliclazide (80 mg retard once daily), hydrochlorothiazide (25 mg once daily), omeprazole (20 mg once daily), and sertraline (100 mg once daily). The last dose of hydrochlorothiazide had been taken in the morning, over 12 hours prior to her ED arrival.

During her teenage years, she had experienced a minor allergic reaction to amoxicillin, which manifested only as facial swelling without involvement of the oropharynx, rash, hypotension, or difficulty breathing. She had also previously developed a pronounced local cutaneous response to a wasp sting but did not exhibit systemic symptoms. The present event marked her initial episode of anaphylaxis.

Five days prior to this ED visit, her general practitioner had prescribed clindamycin to treat erysipelas. After five days on clindamycin, she developed an abdominal rash (details unspecified), interpreted as a delayed allergic response to the medication. Subsequently, her general practitioner changed her antibiotic to clarithromycin, which she had never received before.

She later arrived at the emergency department reporting itching, flushing, increased sweating, agitation, and a sensation of throat swelling. These symptoms began approximately one hour after she took her initial oral dose of clarithromycin and intensified progressively. She presented for medical assessment one hour after symptom onset. At initial evaluation, her vital signs revealed low blood pressure and reduced oxygen saturation (non-invasive blood pressure of 74/50 mm Hg, peripheral oxygen saturation 88% on room air, respiratory rate of 22 breaths per minute). Physical examination demonstrated facial edema, a newly developed generalized erythema with widespread urticaria, enhanced sweating, and agitation. There was no evidence of tongue or pharyngeal swelling, and heart and lung sounds were normal on auscultation. Capillary blood glucose measured 12.7 mmol/L, and her ECG indicated a sinus rhythm. She promptly received treatment for a presumed severe allergic reaction, including an intramuscular 0.3 mg epinephrine injection via auto-injector and a slow intravenous infusion of 2 mg clemastine, administered to address the cutaneous findings as per institutional protocols. She was also provided supplemental oxygen at 15 L/min via a non-rebreather mask and infused with 500 mL normal saline (0.9% sodium chloride solution) during initial resuscitation. After showing a favorable response to epinephrine, supplemental oxygen was discontinued but later had to be briefly restarted during further episodes of anaphylaxis. Clarithromycin was stopped, and she did not receive any additional doses after the single 500 mg oral administration.

The initial response to therapy was favorable, with normalization of blood pressure and reduction of facial swelling and erythema. The patient was transferred to the acute internal medicine ward, which offers enhanced monitoring by specialized nurses, but does not include telemetry. Despite these early improvements, the patient experienced repeated episodes resembling anaphylactic shock within the first 12 hours of admission. Following the first recurrence, she was moved to the intensive care unit (ICU) for closer observation. Each episode necessitated intramuscular epinephrine to achieve resolution (see figure 1), with a total of four anaphylactic events including the initial emergency department (ED) presentation. For the first two events, epinephrine was administered via auto-injector, delivering 0.3 mg intramuscularly. The subsequent two episodes were treated with manually prepared doses of 0.5 mg epinephrine intramuscularly. Although the auto-injectors available in our facility deliver less than the recommended dose of 0.5 mg, their use is preferred in urgent situations, such as initial stabilization in the ED, or in scenarios where emergency interventions for anaphylaxis are infrequent, and unfamiliarity with medication might cause delays. Corticosteroid therapy was added following the first recurrence, due to the conventional belief in their efficacy to prevent biphasic anaphylactic reactions. The patient received 30 mg of intravenous prednisone daily for 5 days, as well as a single intravenous dose of 8 mg dexamethasone, aligning with different recommendations between the hospital’s general ward and ICU anaphylaxis protocols. Additionally, clemastine was administered intravenously at 2 mg twice for dermatological manifestations, and she received 500 mL of normal saline during the second anaphylactic event. Notably, the severity of symptoms diminished with each episode, progressing from profound hypoxemia with cyanosis, hypotension, and severe dyspnea at onset to only dyspnea, rash recurrence, and pruritus by the final episode.

Early in the course, the allergology department was engaged to provide expert guidance. Two alternate management strategies were evaluated: continuous intravenous epinephrine infusion as a prophylactic measure against further anaphylaxis, or an observation-based approach due to the gradually waning symptom severity, to determine if symptoms would subside without intervention. Both options carried substantial risks, compounded by an absence of published evidence to inform treatment in multiphasic anaphylactic cases.

Outcome and follow-up:

In the end, neither therapeutic option proved necessary since the patient experienced no further recurrence of symptoms thereafter. After roughly 24 hours without any new episodes of anaphylaxis, she was moved to a general ward. She was discharged home following a 3-day hospitalization. Upon discharge, levocetirizine, an antihistamine, was initiated as her regular medication, and she was instructed on the use of a 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injector. No additional anaphylactic incidents have occurred since.

Given the unusual and extended clinical presentation, additional investigation into possible underlying etiologies was undertaken. This evaluation suggested a strong likelihood of systemic mastocytosis, although the criteria for a definitive diagnosis were not met (only two minor criteria from the WHO classification were fulfilled, with the major criterion unable to be assessed because of insufficient bone marrow sample, as illustrated in figure 2). Tryptase concentrations were elevated during the third anaphylactic episode, as well as at baseline, and these levels remained high on subsequent follow-up with the allergist, detailed in table 1.

Regrettably, tryptase measurements were not obtained during the initial evaluation of the patient’s early anaphylactic episodes, resulting in noncompliance with the guideline-recommended timing for tryptase sampling. The established recommendations suggest collecting at least two, and preferably three, tryptase measurements at distinct time points: the first as soon as is practical without impeding emergency therapy, the second between 1 and 2 hours (but no later than 4 hours) after symptom onset to capture the peak level, and the third at least 24 hours after complete symptom resolution to determine the baseline value. In this patient, the initial tryptase sample was collected 1 hour following the third episode of anaphylaxis, while the subsequent sample was drawn 27 hours after the final anaphylactic event.

No confirmatory allergy test was conducted, as the potential for triggering another severe anaphylactic reaction—such as could occur with skin testing—was considered disproportionate given that subsequent exposure could easily be avoided. Additional notable findings consistent with systemic mastocytosis were identified, including hepatomegaly on abdominal ultrasound and possible neuropsychiatric manifestations such as depression and memory impairment.

Discussion:

Anaphylaxis is recognized as a distinct clinical syndrome, marked by its abrupt onset and swift escalation of symptoms affecting the airway, respiration, cardiovascular system, and/or gastrointestinal tract. It is usually associated with skin or mucosal findings like urticaria, angioedema, or flushing. Although identifying a probable allergen can support the diagnosis, pinpointing an exact cause is not always achievable.

Atypical cases such as biphasic or refractory anaphylaxis lack uniformly accepted definitions, though recent initiatives have sought to standardize terminology. According to the Resuscitation Council UK, biphasic anaphylaxis is defined as the return of symptoms hours after initial resolution without additional exposure to the allergen. Distinguishing genuine biphasic episodes from prolonged anaphylaxis with temporary improvement after epinephrine, or from symptoms due to ongoing allergen absorption (such as undigested food or slow-release preparations), presents diagnostic challenges. In comparison, refractory anaphylaxis describes a persistent clinical picture with inadequate or no response to appropriate epinephrine administration.

Most guidance on biphasic anaphylaxis focuses on post-event monitoring. However, the ideal period of observation is still subject to debate. Conventional recommendations suggest monitoring patients for 4 to 6 hours following resolution of symptoms. Both the Nederlands Huisartsen Genootschap (NHG) and the World Allergy Organisation have noted that about half of all biphasic reactions develop within 6–12 hours of the initial presentation, but neither organization specifies an exact observation protocol. In contrast, the UK guideline published in 2021 introduces a three-tiered, risk-based system for patient monitoring. Clearly, further consensus is needed to optimize monitoring strategies so that health system resources are used efficiently for most anaphylaxis cases while adequately protecting those few patients at risk for biphasic reactions.

There is a significant and concerning absence of guidance regarding advanced pharmacologic management for biphasic and multiphasic anaphylactic episodes. Treatment for these patterns primarily replicates that for typical anaphylaxis, consisting of rapid administration of intramuscular epinephrine alongside suitable supportive therapies. Existing guidelines advise dosing epinephrine at 0.01 mg/kg up to a maximum of 0.5 mg, with practical application frequently defaulting to a 0.5 mg dose in adults, given that most adults exceed 50 kg in weight. Notably, many epinephrine auto-injectors—including those employed in the initial two episodes detailed in this case report—are supplied with only 0.3 mg, even when indicated for adults. While auto-injectors are indispensable in prehospital contexts, they also offer advantages in medical environments by decreasing delays and easing hesitancy among staff who may be infrequently exposed to potent vasopressors such as epinephrine. Nonetheless, the standard use of 0.3 mg auto-injectors in adults above 50 kg may result in marked underdosing. In a 2025 retrospective analysis, patients initially given 0.3 mg required further interventions, including additional epinephrine dosing, at a higher rate compared to those who first received 0.5 mg. Addressing factors such as the longstanding familiarity with the 0.3 mg dose among practitioners and the predominance of 0.3 mg auto-injectors, while enhancing adherence to guideline-recommended dosing, holds the potential to substantially improve outcomes in anaphylaxis care.

Historically, corticosteroids were advocated in part to reduce the risk of biphasic reactions during anaphylaxis. Nonetheless, accumulating data now indicate a lack of efficacy and possible adverse effects, prompting a move away from corticosteroid use in this context. A 2017 systematic review of 22 studies found that only one study favored corticosteroid therapy, whereas 21 studies failed to demonstrate that corticosteroids decrease the risk of biphasic anaphylaxis. Additionally, recent research highlights concerns regarding side effects; a 2019 study, for example, identified higher rates of ICU or hospital ward admission following prehospital corticosteroid administration. As a result, most modern guidelines now discourage routine corticosteroid use, although their application in practice continues.

While biphasic anaphylaxis is infrequent, it remains clinically significant. The lack of dependable predictive factors challenges effective risk stratification and appropriate monitoring. Proposed predictors encompass delayed administration of epinephrine, initial severe presentation, use of multiple epinephrine doses, and a history of biphasic episodes. Nevertheless, definitive predictors that would permit selective monitoring have yet to be established.

Systemic mastocytosis, which is characterized by an abnormal proliferation of mast cells, excessive release of mediators, and inflammation across multiple organs, increases susceptibility to severe anaphylactic events.1517 However, owing to the uncommon nature of both systemic mastocytosis and biphasic anaphylaxis, there is currently no research directly examining the relationship between these two conditions. Investigating mast cell disorders in the context of biphasic anaphylaxis may enhance understanding and inform future therapeutic strategies.

Anaphylaxis is associated with a low mortality rate, particularly in areas where emergency medical services can be rapidly accessed. Even less common are deaths resulting from biphasic anaphylaxis, despite its seemingly high risk. Nevertheless, these comforting figures do not diminish the fact that anaphylaxis frequently occurs in emergency settings and demands swift intervention and careful observation. Although the emphasis in research and clinical guidelines is primarily on the acute emergency management, ongoing care and surveillance following the initial episode are equally significant. Gaining a more thorough understanding of complex presentations such as biphasic anaphylaxis may lead to more individualized, efficient, and effective approaches to anaphylaxis management.

The infrequency of biphasic and especially multiphasic anaphylaxis, coupled with the inherent difficulties in conducting research during emergencies, creates significant obstacles to establishing evidence-based management strategies. In these circumstances, consensus guidelines developed by clinical experts provide valuable guidance; still, the creation of such recommendations is constrained by the limited data available. Given the scarcity of these intricate anaphylaxis cases, maintaining a dedicated registry that captures relevant patient details, potential management recommendations (such as those discussed in this case report), and outcome measures may serve as a crucial resource for forming data-driven, expert-informed guidelines.
