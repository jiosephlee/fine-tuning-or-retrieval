Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting

Margret Ann Schneider, Hans de Groot, Jan-Willem Geerts

Abstract:

Summary: Anaphylaxis represents the most critical manifestation of allergic reactions, carrying a substantial risk of fatality and necessitating immediate, decisive intervention. A fraction of cases, however, exhibit symptom recurrence after initial resolution, even when management has been appropriate and effective. These biphasic or, in rare circumstances, multiphasic courses complicate clinical care, particularly since their frequency is inconsistently reported, contributory risk factors remain poorly defined, and current guidelines do not provide definitive approaches for management. This complexity is heightened in cases of multiphasic anaphylaxis, which are characterized by multiple episodes of anaphylactic symptoms despite satisfactory responses to standard epinephrine therapy. Given the infrequency of multiphasic anaphylaxis, detailed case reports contribute valuable perspectives to inform clinical decision-making. Here, we report the clinical course of a woman in her 60s who experienced multiphasic anaphylaxis in response to clarithromycin; subsequent investigation suggested a strong possibility of underlying systemic mastocytosis, although her clinical profile did not fulfill formal diagnostic requirements.

Background:

Anaphylaxis constitutes a severe allergic reaction which, in the case of anaphylactic shock, may threaten life and frequently necessitates emergency medical attention. A proportion of cases, however, exhibits a biphasic or even multiphasic evolution, in which the patient experiences an initial anaphylactic event after encountering a trigger, subsequently becomes asymptomatic as symptoms subside, and later undergoes a recurrence of symptoms in the absence of further exposure to the original allergen. While such recurrences most often manifest within 24 hours of the initial allergen exposure, cases have been documented with recurrence as late as 78 hours afterward. Estimates of the frequency of biphasic anaphylaxis vary considerably, with reported rates spanning 0.4% to 23.3%, although most studies suggest an incidence of 4%–6%. Given the risk of symptom recurrence, observation periods are typically recommended for patients after apparent resolution of anaphylaxis. Despite this, varying definitions of biphasic anaphylaxis and differing treatment protocols persist, and the use of corticosteroids has become increasingly controversial. Multiphasic anaphylaxis, involving several distinct episodes of symptom recurrence, introduces further complexity in determining optimal monitoring and therapeutic strategies. This report details the clinical course of a woman in her sixties who experienced multiphasic anaphylactic reactions in response to clarithromycin.

Case presentation:

A woman in her sixties, with a documented beta-lactam antibiotic allergy, arrived at the emergency department (ED) exhibiting symptoms of anaphylaxis after ingesting 500 mg of oral clarithromycin.

Her past medical history included type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, psoriasis, and depression. She weighed 104 kg. Her current medications were metformin (500 mg twice daily), gliclazide (80 mg sustained-release once daily), hydrochlorothiazide (25 mg once daily), omeprazole (20 mg once daily), and sertraline (100 mg once daily). The last hydrochlorothiazide dose had been taken that morning, more than 12 hours before her ED admission.

During her teenage years, she experienced a mild allergic response to amoxicillin, which involved only swelling of the face, and did not include oropharyngeal swelling, skin rash, hypotension, or breathing difficulties. She also had a marked local reaction to a wasp sting previously, characterized by skin involvement only and without systemic features. The present clinical event marked her initial occurrence of anaphylaxis.

Five days prior to arriving at the ED, her general practitioner prescribed clindamycin to treat erysipelas. After using clindamycin for five days, she noticed a rash developing on her abdomen, though no further details were provided. This was thought to be a delayed allergic reaction to clindamycin, and consequently, her general practitioner replaced clindamycin with clarithromycin, an antibiotic she had not previously received.

The patient later arrived at the emergency department reporting symptoms including itching, flushing, excessive sweating, restlessness, and a sensation of throat swelling. These manifestations emerged approximately 1 hour after ingesting her first dose of clarithromycin and intensified steadily. She presented for medical evaluation within 1 hour of symptom onset. On arrival, her vital signs indicated low blood pressure and reduced oxygen saturation (non-invasive blood pressure: 74/50 mm Hg, peripheral oxygen saturation 88% in ambient air, respiratory rate 22 breaths per minute). Physical examination revealed facial edema, newly developed widespread erythema and urticaria across the body, along with increased sweating and agitation. There was no observed edema of the tongue or throat, and both cardiac and pulmonary auscultation were unremarkable. Her blood glucose measured 12.7 mmol/L, and the electrocardiogram demonstrated sinus rhythm. Immediate management for presumed severe allergic reaction included intramuscular administration of 0.3 mg epinephrine via auto-injector and a slow intravenous infusion of 2 mg clemastine to address the cutaneous symptoms, in accordance with local protocol. She was additionally given supplemental oxygen at 15 L through a non-rebreather mask and received 500 mL of normal saline (0.9% sodium chloride) during the initial phase of stabilization. Oxygen supplementation was able to be withdrawn following clinical improvement post-epinephrine; however, it was reintroduced temporarily during subsequent episodes of anaphylaxis. Clarithromycin therapy was halted, and no further doses beyond the initial 500 mg oral dose were administered.

The patient initially responded favorably to the administered therapy, exhibiting normalization of blood pressure and a reduction in facial erythema and edema. She was subsequently transferred to the acute internal medicine unit, where enhanced nursing observation is available, although this monitoring does not include continuous telemetry. Nonetheless, despite this promising initial course, the patient experienced multiple recurrences of symptoms indicative of anaphylactic shock within the first 12 hours post-admission. After the first recurrence, she was transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) for more intensive observation. Each anaphylactic event necessitated the intramuscular injection of epinephrine (see figure 1); across her stay, she experienced four total anaphylactic episodes, including the initial emergency department presentation. The initial two doses of epinephrine (0.3 mg each) were delivered via auto-injector, while the subsequent two doses (0.5 mg each) were administered as individually prepared intramuscular injections. At our institution, the epinephrine auto-injectors on hand deliver a lower dose (0.3 mg) than the generally advised 0.5 mg; nevertheless, auto-injectors are preferentially utilized during emergent situations—such as stabilizing a patient in the emergency department or in settings where anaphylaxis treatment is infrequent and staff hesitancy regarding unfamiliar medication could delay intervention. Following the initial recurrence of anaphylactic symptoms, corticosteroids were introduced as adjunct therapy, consistent with their conventional use to reduce the likelihood of biphasic anaphylactic reactions. The patient received a daily dose of 30 mg intravenous prednisone for five days and a single 8 mg intravenous dose of dexamethasone, reflecting divergent recommendations in the general ward and ICU anaphylaxis protocols at our institution. In addition, clemastine (2 mg IV, twice) was administered to address cutaneous manifestations, and 500 mL of normal saline solution was given during the second anaphylactic episode. Notably, symptom severity appeared to decline with each subsequent episode, transitioning from profound hypoxemia with cyanosis, hypotension, and severe respiratory distress in the initial episode, to milder manifestations—principally dyspnea, rash reoccurrence, and pruritus—by the final episode.

The allergology department was promptly engaged to provide expert management recommendations. Within this context, two potential therapeutic strategies were considered. One option involved the preemptive, continuous infusion of epinephrine by intravenous pump to avert further anaphylactic events, rather than administering treatment reactively following symptom recurrence. The alternative approach, supported by the observed gradual diminution of symptom severity, was to adopt a watchful waiting strategy to determine whether the episodes would spontaneously resolve. Both treatment pathways were associated with significant risks, compounded by the paucity of literature guiding management in cases of multiphasic anaphylaxis.

Outcome and follow-up:

In the end, neither of the proposed treatment strategies was required since the patient did not experience any further recurrence of symptoms. After remaining free from additional anaphylactic events for roughly 24 hours, she was moved to a general ward. Her hospitalization lasted three days in total, after which she was released. Upon discharge, she was prescribed levocetirizine, an antihistamine, as her ongoing medication. In addition, she was provided with an epinephrine auto-injector (0.3 mg) and instructed in its proper use. No subsequent anaphylactic episodes have been reported.

Given the unusual duration and presentation of her clinical course, additional investigations were initiated to identify potential underlying factors. These studies suggested a strong likelihood of systemic mastocytosis; however, the diagnostic criteria were not fully met (she met two minor criteria as per WHO guidelines, but the major criterion could not be evaluated because of insufficient bone marrow sample material, refer to figure 2). Tryptase concentrations, both at the time of her third anaphylactic episode and at baseline, were found to be elevated and continued to be high during follow-up appointments with her allergologist, as illustrated in table 1.

Regrettably, tryptase concentrations were not assessed during the initial management of the first anaphylactic reactions, resulting in noncompliance with recommended timing for tryptase measurements. Current guidelines advocate collecting at least two, preferably three, tryptase samples at designated times: the first should be obtained as soon as possible after the event, without postponing necessary interventions; the second sample is to be drawn 1–2 hours (and within 4 hours) following symptom onset, coinciding with the expected tryptase peak; and the third sample should be collected at least 24 hours after complete symptom resolution to determine the baseline tryptase level. In this particular case, the initial tryptase assessment was performed one hour subsequent to the third anaphylactic event, and the follow-up sample was collected 27 hours after the final anaphylactic episode.

No allergy confirmation testing, such as skin testing, was pursued, as the potential for provoking another severe anaphylactic response was considered disproportionate—particularly since further antigen exposure could be readily circumvented. Additional noteworthy clinical observations consistent with systemic mastocytosis included hepatomegaly identified on abdominal ultrasound and neuropsychiatric manifestations such as depressive symptoms and memory disturbances.

Discussion:

Anaphylaxis is generally recognised as a clearly delineated clinical condition, presenting acutely and evolving swiftly with symptoms affecting the airway, respiratory function, circulatory status, and/or gastrointestinal system, often in conjunction with skin or mucosal findings like urticaria, angioedema, or flushing. While the diagnosis is strengthened by identifying a plausible allergen, it is frequently not feasible to specify an exact causative agent.

More atypical presentations, including biphasic or refractory anaphylaxis, remain less clearly described, though recent initiatives have sought to establish standardized definitions in these areas as well. According to the Resuscitation Council UK, biphasic anaphylaxis entails a return of symptoms several hours after apparent initial recovery, without further exposure to the triggering allergen. Distinguishing bona fide biphasic anaphylaxis from cases involving prolonged anaphylactic episodes responding only temporarily to epinephrine, or from ongoing absorption of the allergen (such as undigested food or slowly releasing medication), is frequently complex. In contrast, refractory anaphylaxis is characterised by the persistence of symptoms that fail to resolve despite appropriate administration of epinephrine.

Guideline statements concerning biphasic anaphylaxis generally focus on recommendations for patient monitoring, yet there remains no clear consensus regarding the ideal observation timeframe. Conventional protocols advocate monitoring for 4–6 hours after symptoms have abated. Both the Dutch Nederlands Huisartsen Genootschap (NHG) and the World Allergy Organisation guidelines observe that roughly half of all biphasic reactions emerge within 6–12 hours following the original event, though neither provides a concrete recommendation for observation period. The 2021 UK guideline, on the other hand, introduces a three-level, risk-based approach to monitoring. The need for harmonised strategies is apparent, as they must reconcile the allocation of healthcare resources (given the high volume of straightforward anaphylaxis cases) with the imperative to safeguard against adverse outcomes in the relatively rare and often unpredictable group at risk for biphasic reactions.

There is a notable and concerning absence of specific guidance regarding advanced pharmacological interventions for biphasic and multiphasic anaphylactic reactions. The therapeutic approach for these complex courses generally aligns with established protocols for acute anaphylaxis, which emphasize rapid intramuscular administration of epinephrine alongside supportive measures. Clinical guidelines stipulate an epinephrine dose of 0.01 mg/kg, with a ceiling dose of 0.5 mg. In practice, this is frequently simplified to a flat 0.5 mg dose for adults, given that most adults surpass 50 kg in body weight. Nevertheless, it is worth highlighting that many commercially available auto-injectors—including those administered during the first two episodes of anaphylaxis described in our case—contain just 0.3 mg of epinephrine, even though these devices are designated for adult use. While auto-injectors are invaluable for initial treatment outside of healthcare settings, they retain utility within medical environments by facilitating immediate intervention and diminishing reluctance among providers less accustomed to handling vasoactive medications such as epinephrine. Despite their widespread use, the provision of only 0.3 mg via auto-injector may result in significant underdosing in adults over 50 kg. Evidence from a retrospective study conducted in 2025 indicates that patients who were first treated with a 0.3 mg dose more frequently required further medical escalation, including repeated doses of epinephrine, compared to those who received the recommended 0.5 mg initially. Addressing factors such as entrenched provider reliance on the traditional 0.3 mg dose and the broad availability of 0.3 mg auto-injectors, as well as ensuring adherence to guideline-recommended dosing, has the potential to markedly enhance outcomes in anaphylaxis management.

Historically, corticosteroids have been utilized with the rationale of preventing biphasic reactions during anaphylaxis. Nonetheless, growing research indicates a lack of efficacy and points toward potential risks associated with their administration, resulting in a recent trend to discontinue their use for anaphylaxis. A systematic review conducted in 2017 analyzed 22 studies; only one supported corticosteroid usage, whereas 21 found no evidence that corticosteroids decrease the incidence of biphasic anaphylaxis. In addition, more recent investigations highlight the adverse effects associated with corticosteroids. For example, a 2019 study reported a higher probability of ICU or hospital admissions in patients who received corticosteroids prior to hospital arrival. In light of this evidence, most up-to-date guidelines recommend against the standard administration of corticosteroids, though they remain commonly used in clinical contexts.

Although biphasic anaphylaxis occurs infrequently, its clinical significance is considerable. The inability to reliably predict its onset complicates the process of identifying at-risk individuals and informing observation strategies. Potential predictors such as delayed epinephrine delivery, more severe initial symptoms, requirement for repeated epinephrine doses, and a personal history of biphasic responses have been proposed. Nevertheless, dependable predictive criteria for risk-based monitoring have yet to be established.

Systemic mastocytosis, which is characterized by the abnormal accumulation of mast cells leading to increased mediator release and multisystem inflammation, increases susceptibility to severe anaphylaxis.1517 Despite this association, both disorders remain rare, and currently, there is an absence of studies focusing specifically on the influence of systemic mastocytosis in the context of biphasic anaphylaxis. Future research that explores mast cell diseases and their relationship to biphasic anaphylaxis could enhance our understanding and inform clinical management.

Anaphylaxis-related mortality remains uncommon, particularly in settings where patients can promptly access emergency medical services. Additionally, deaths resulting from biphasic anaphylaxis are exceedingly infrequent, despite the potentially serious nature of such reactions. Nevertheless, anaphylaxis consistently represents a frequent acute medical issue that necessitates immediate intervention and careful observation. While the majority of research and clinical protocols emphasizes the initial emergency response, subsequent care and vigilant monitoring are also crucial for optimal patient outcomes. Advancing our knowledge of complex forms of anaphylaxis, such as biphasic anaphylaxis, may enable the development of management strategies that are more precisely tailored to individual needs and are more efficient in terms of resource utilization.

The infrequency of biphasic and especially multiphasic anaphylactic episodes, coupled with the intrinsic difficulties of conducting research in emergency contexts, renders the formulation of robust evidence-based protocols particularly difficult. In these circumstances, consensus recommendations from experts—offering explicit management pathways—play an essential role, although the paucity of empirical data complicates their creation. Considering the scarcity of complex anaphylaxis presentations, establishing a registry to systematically collect details on relevant patient characteristics, proposed management approaches (such as those illustrated in our case report), and clinical outcomes could serve as a foundational resource for generating data-informed, expert-driven guidelines.
