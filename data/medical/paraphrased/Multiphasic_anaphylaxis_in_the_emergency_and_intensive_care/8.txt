Title: Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting

Margret Ann Schneider, Hans de Groot, Jan-Willem Geerts

Abstract:

Summary: Anaphylaxis represents the most acute and dangerous manifestation of allergic responses, posing a considerable risk of mortality unless managed swiftly and appropriately. In certain instances, patients may experience a return of symptoms even after initial effective treatment, known as biphasic or, more rarely, multiphasic anaphylaxis. Such patterns are particularly challenging for healthcare providers, given the inconsistent prevalence data, ambiguous risk factors, and the absence of specific therapeutic recommendations in established protocols. Multiphasic anaphylaxis, characterized by several recurrences following an initial favorable reaction to standard interventions like epinephrine, exemplifies these complexities. Given its infrequency, individual case reports serve as valuable resources for sharing approaches to management. This paper details a case involving a female patient in her 60s who exhibited multiphasic anaphylactic episodes triggered by clarithromycin; further evaluation suggested probable systemic mastocytosis, though she fell short of fulfilling formal diagnostic thresholds.

Background:

Anaphylaxis constitutes a critical allergic response that, particularly when progressing to anaphylactic shock, may endanger life and frequently necessitates urgent medical attention. Within this spectrum, a subset of patients experience a biphasic or even multiphasic evolution, wherein an initial reaction to an allergen is succeeded by temporary resolution of symptoms (an asymptomatic interval), and subsequently, a spontaneous return of clinical manifestations occurs despite no additional exposure to the offending agent. These recurrences predominantly take place within 24 hours after the initial incident, though literature documents instances of delayed relapses occurring as late as 78 hours post-exposure. Estimates of biphasic anaphylaxis incidence show significant variability, spanning from 0.4% to 23.3%; nonetheless, most studies converge on a figure between 4% and 6%. In recognition of the potential for recurrent symptoms, medical observation is generally recommended for some time after resolution of anaphylactic events. Notably, variability exists in the precise definitions applied to biphasic anaphylaxis as well as in clinical management strategies, and the efficacy of corticosteroids in this context has been increasingly questioned. The occurrence of multiphasic anaphylaxis—with multiple recurrent symptomatic episodes—introduces added complexity for management and monitoring protocols. In this report, we discuss the case of a woman in her sixth decade who developed a multiphasic anaphylactic response following administration of clarithromycin.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 60s, with a documented beta-lactam antibiotic allergy, arrived at the emergency department (ED) exhibiting anaphylaxis subsequent to ingesting 500 mg of oral clarithromycin.

Her medical background was significant for type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, psoriasis, and depression. She weighed 104 kg. At the time, her ongoing pharmacotherapy consisted of metformin (500 mg twice daily), gliclazide (80 mg retard, once daily), hydrochlorothiazide (25 mg once daily), omeprazole (20 mg once daily), and sertraline (100 mg once daily). The most recent intake of hydrochlorothiazide had occurred more than 12 hours before her ED visit, having been administered that morning.

As an adolescent, the patient had experienced a mild allergic response to amoxicillin, which manifested as localized facial swelling without associated oropharyngeal edema, rash, hypotension, or dyspnea. Additionally, she had previously had a pronounced localized dermal reaction to a wasp sting but had never developed systemic manifestations. The current event represented her initial episode of anaphylaxis.

Five days prior to her ED attendance, her general practitioner initiated clindamycin therapy for erysipelas. After five days of treatment, she developed an abdominal rash (not otherwise characterized), which was presumed to be a delayed allergic reaction to clindamycin. As a result, her general practitioner replaced clindamycin with clarithromycin, an antibiotic to which she had not previously been exposed.

She later arrived at the emergency department (ED) reporting symptoms including pruritus, flushing, excessive sweating, agitation, and a sensation of throat swelling. These complaints began approximately one hour following ingestion of her first clarithromycin dose and continued to intensify. Medical attention was sought within an hour of symptom onset. Upon arrival at the ED, initial assessment revealed hypotension and hypoxemia (non-invasive blood pressure measured at 74/50 mm Hg, peripheral oxygen saturation of 88% in room air, respiratory rate of 22 breaths per minute). Examination detected facial edema, newly emerged widespread erythema and urticaria affecting the entire body, as well as increased sweating and agitation. There was no evidence of lingual or pharyngeal swelling, and both cardiac and pulmonary auscultation were unremarkable. Her blood glucose was 12.7 mmol/L, and ECG indicated sinus rhythm. She was promptly managed for a presumed severe allergic reaction with intramuscular administration of 0.3 mg epinephrine via auto-injector and a slow intravenous injection of 2 mg clemastine, addressing the cutaneous manifestations as per local protocol. Additional interventions included administration of supplemental oxygen at 15 L via a non-rebreather mask and an initial infusion of 500 mL normal saline (0.9% sodium chloride) for stabilization. Oxygen supplementation was discontinued after improvement in response to epinephrine, though it had to be intermittently resumed during subsequent anaphylactic recurrences. No further doses of clarithromycin were given, as therapy was ceased after the first 500 mg oral dose.

At first, the patient exhibited a favorable response to the initiated therapy, as her blood pressure stabilized and both facial erythema and swelling subsided. She was transferred to the acute internal medicine unit, where care from specialized nursing personnel was available with heightened observation, although telemetry monitoring was not employed. Nevertheless, within the initial 12 hours following admission, the patient experienced repeated episodes of symptoms characteristic of anaphylactic shock, despite initial improvement. Upon the first recurrence of anaphylaxis, the patient was moved to the intensive care unit (ICU) for enhanced surveillance. Every episode necessitated intramuscular epinephrine for symptom resolution (see figure 1), with four anaphylactic events documented including the initial emergency department (ED) presentation. The first two administrations consisted of 0.3 mg intramuscular epinephrine delivered via auto-injector, while the subsequent two were individually prepared 0.5 mg intramuscular doses. Although the auto-injectors stocked in our facility provide a dose lower than the typically recommended 0.5 mg, we prioritize their use for immediate interventions (such as stabilizing new arrivals in the ED), especially in settings where emergency interventions for anaphylaxis are rare and concerns about unfamiliar medication could hinder rapid response. After the first anaphylactic relapse, corticosteroids were introduced owing to their traditional application in the prevention of biphasic reactions. The regimen included a daily intravenous dose of 30 mg prednisone over 5 days and a single dose of 8 mg intravenous dexamethasone, reflecting discrepancies in guidance between our hospital's general ward and ICU protocols for anaphylaxis management. The patient also received 2 mg intravenous clemastine administered twice for cutaneous manifestations, alongside an additional 500 mL infusion of normal saline during the second anaphylactic episode. Notably, there was a progressive reduction in symptom severity with successive episodes, starting with profound hypoxemia, cyanosis, hypotension, and pronounced dyspnea, and culminating in milder symptoms including dyspnea, a rash recurrence, and pruritus in the final episode.

Early in the course, the allergology department was involved for expert input regarding therapeutic options. Two principal alternative management strategies were contemplated. The first involved continuous intravenous infusion of epinephrine as a proactive measure to forestall anaphylactic recurrences. The second, motivated by the diminishing intensity of clinical manifestations, involved watchful waiting to determine if the symptoms would spontaneously abate. Both proposed strategies carried substantial risks, particularly given the scarcity of published guidance for managing multiphasic anaphylaxis.

Outcome and follow-up:

In the end, neither of the initially considered treatment strategies was required, as the patient experienced no additional symptom recurrence. Approximately 24 hours after her last anaphylactic event, she was moved from intensive care to a general ward, and was discharged from the hospital three days post-admission. The patient was prescribed levocetirizine, an antihistamine, as a permanent medication, and was instructed in the use of a 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injector. There have been no subsequent anaphylactic events.

Given the unusual and persistent nature of her symptoms, further investigations were initiated to explore potential underlying etiologies. These assessments indicated a significant likelihood of systemic mastocytosis. However, a definitive diagnosis could not be established: only two World Health Organization (WHO) minor criteria were met, and the major criterion could not be evaluated due to insufficient bone marrow biopsy material (refer to figure 2). Regrettably, during the initial assessment of the patient's early anaphylactic episodes, tryptase levels were not measured, resulting in non-compliance with the standard timing recommendations for tryptase assessment. According to established guidelines, at least two—preferably three—tryptase samples should be collected at designated time points: the first as promptly as possible (without delaying therapeutic intervention), the second between 1 and 2 hours (but no later than 4 hours) after symptom onset to capture the anticipated peak, and a third sample at least 24 hours following complete symptom resolution to determine the baseline value. In the scenario described, the first tryptase measurement was conducted 1 hour after the third episode of anaphylaxis, with the second collected 27 hours subsequent to the most recent occurrence.

Allergy confirmation testing was omitted; the risk of triggering another severe anaphylactic reaction—such as through skin testing—was considered disproportionate given that further contact with the suspected trigger could be easily avoided. Additional findings consistent with systemic mastocytosis included hepatomegaly identified via abdominal ultrasound, as well as potential neuropsychiatric manifestations, such as depressive symptoms and memory impairment.

Discussion:

Anaphylaxis is recognised as a distinct clinical syndrome, marked by an abrupt onset and rapid escalation of symptoms that affect the airway, breathing, circulation, and/or gastrointestinal tract. This clinical picture is usually accompanied by skin or mucosal findings, such as urticaria, angioedema, or flushing. Although identifying a probable allergen can aid in confirming the diagnosis, pinpointing a definitive trigger is not always achievable.

More complicated manifestations, including biphasic and refractory anaphylaxis, lack clear definitions, though recent initiatives have attempted to establish consensus criteria in these areas. The Resuscitation Council UK characterises biphasic anaphylaxis as the reappearance of symptoms several hours after an initial resolution, without renewed allergen exposure. Accurately distinguishing genuine biphasic reactions from prolonged anaphylaxis—with only a brief response to epinephrine—or ongoing absorption of allergen (such as remnants of ingested food or slow-release drugs) is often difficult. In contrast, refractory anaphylaxis denotes persistent symptoms that do not sufficiently improve with appropriately administered epinephrine.

Current guidelines primarily focus on clinical monitoring in the context of biphasic anaphylaxis. Nonetheless, the optimal duration for observation is still contested. Conventional recommendations advocate for monitoring patients for 4 to 6 hours after symptoms have subsided. Guidelines such as those from the Dutch Nederlands Huisartsen Genootschap (NHG) and the World Allergy Organisation acknowledge that about 50% of biphasic episodes manifest within 6–12 hours of the initial event, but neither guideline specifies exact monitoring periods. Conversely, the UK 2021 guideline introduces a stratified three-level approach to observation. There is a clear need for harmonised monitoring protocols that can reconcile the efficient use of healthcare resources (given the large number of patients with single, uncomplicated anaphylactic episodes) with safeguarding patients at increased risk of infrequent, but harder to predict, biphasic reactions.

Guidance on advanced pharmacological interventions for biphasic and multiphasic presentations is conspicuously insufficient and raises concerns. The therapeutic approach to biphasic and multiphasic anaphylaxis generally replicates that of typical anaphylaxis, prioritizing immediate intramuscular epinephrine alongside suitable supportive interventions. Current guidelines advise administering epinephrine at a dose of 0.01 mg/kg body weight, not exceeding a total dose of 0.5 mg. In clinical practice, this recommendation is commonly simplified to a flat 0.5 mg for adults, as the majority surpass 50 kg in weight. Of note, many epinephrine auto-injectors—including those used in the initial two anaphylactic incidents described in our case—provide just 0.3 mg of epinephrine per dose, even when designated for adult use. While epinephrine auto-injectors are indispensable in out-of-hospital emergencies, their utility extends to clinical environments by facilitating rapid administration and diminishing reluctance among healthcare staff who infrequently encounter vasoactive drugs like epinephrine. Nevertheless, administering only 0.3 mg via auto-injector to adults exceeding 50 kg may result in substantial underdosing. Data from a 2025 retrospective analysis indicated that recipients of an initial 0.3 mg epinephrine dose required advanced interventions—including repeated epinephrine administration—at a significantly higher frequency compared to those initially receiving 0.5 mg. Addressing obstacles such as clinicians’ reliance on the traditional 0.3 mg dose and the predominance of 0.3 mg auto-injector devices, alongside promoting consistent application of the guideline-recommended epinephrine dosages, may substantially optimize the management of anaphylaxis.

Historically, corticosteroids have been advocated, partly due to the belief that they might avert biphasic anaphylactic reactions. Nevertheless, accumulating data now indicate that steroids not only lack benefit but may actually be detrimental, prompting a recent decline in their application for anaphylaxis. A 2017 systematic review spanning 22 studies revealed that just one investigation favored corticosteroid use, while 21 provided no indication that they diminish biphasic anaphylaxis risk. Additional contemporary research highlights the potential for adverse outcomes; notably, a 2019 study identified an elevated risk of ICU or hospital ward admission following prehospital corticosteroid administration. As a result, current clinical guidelines largely discourage routine use of corticosteroids, despite ongoing use in some healthcare settings.

Although uncommon, biphasic anaphylaxis is of considerable clinical significance. Reliable means of predicting which patients are at risk remain elusive, complicating appropriate monitoring and risk assessment. Proposed risk factors include delayed administration of epinephrine, severe clinical presentation at onset, need for multiple epinephrine doses, and a history of biphasic reactions. Nonetheless, these factors have yet to yield consistently reliable risk stratification for clinical decision-making.

Systemic mastocytosis—characterized by abnormal mast cell proliferation, elevated mediator release, and widespread inflammation—confers heightened vulnerability to severe anaphylaxis. $^{15-17}$ However, due to the infrequency of both systemic mastocytosis and biphasic anaphylaxis, specific studies evaluating their relationship are lacking. Ongoing research into mast cell-related disorders and their intersection with biphasic anaphylaxis could enrich future therapeutic approaches.

Anaphylaxis-related deaths are uncommon, particularly in areas where emergency medical services can be accessed quickly. Although biphasic anaphylaxis poses theoretical risks due to its recurrent nature, fatalities from such cases are even less frequent. Despite these positive figures, anaphylaxis continues to represent a frequent emergency, necessitating rapid intervention and careful observation. Although current research and clinical guidelines predominantly emphasize the importance of immediate emergency treatment, ongoing follow-up care and surveillance are equally crucial. Advancing the understanding of atypical or severe presentations—such as biphasic anaphylaxis—could facilitate the creation of more precise, individualized, and resource-conscious approaches to managing anaphylaxis.

Developing rigorously evidence-based protocols for biphasic and particularly multiphasic anaphylaxis remains challenging, given their infrequent occurrence and the inherent difficulties in conducting research in acute emergency contexts. In such scenarios, expert consensus guidelines that offer clear recommendations can serve as an indispensable resource; nonetheless, the scarcity of comprehensive data complicates their formulation. Given how seldom these intricate forms of anaphylaxis occur, establishing a registry that systematically collects detailed case information, proposed management approaches (such as those described in our case study), and outcome data could form the basis for robust, data-informed expert consensus guidance.
