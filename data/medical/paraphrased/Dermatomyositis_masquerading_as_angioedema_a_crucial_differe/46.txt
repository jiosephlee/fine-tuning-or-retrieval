Title: Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook

Kevin Chan, Gemma Kwan, Hwei Choo Soh, Louise Evans

Abstract:

Summary: Dermatomyositis is an uncommon autoimmune disorder that most often impacts the skin, musculature, and respiratory system. Patients diagnosed with dermatomyositis generally need urgent therapy involving aggressive immunosuppression to mitigate disease advancement and protect against multiorgan injury. A previously healthy male in his 40s presented to the emergency department with pronounced neck swelling. Initially, ear, nose, and throat specialists evaluated him for a suspected deep neck space infection. After this etiology was excluded, he was referred to clinical immunology for assessment and management of presumed idiopathic angioedema. Due to the unusual characteristics of the neck oedema—which did not fit angioedema—and in consideration of accompanying skin and nail abnormalities, a creatine kinase level was promptly ordered. The patient was provisionally diagnosed with dermatomyositis, and later confirmed to have a positive small ubiquitin-like modifier-1 activating enzyme (SAE1) autoantibody along with characteristic findings on muscle biopsy. This case demonstrates that neck swelling not caused by histamine or bradykinin can be a rare manifestation of dermatomyositis, presenting with dysphagia and substantial muscular involvement necessitating rapid intervention.

Background:

Dermatomyositis is an inflammatory myopathy of unknown cause, primarily involving the skin and muscles, though it may also impact the gastrointestinal system, joints, lungs, and heart. Its incidence is estimated at 10 cases per million people. The disorder shows a bimodal age distribution, with onset most frequently occurring in children aged 5–14 years and in adults between 45–64 years. Typical skin manifestations include Gottron’s papules, the shawl sign, heliotrope rash, and periungual telangiectasias. Additional clinical features comprise proximal muscle weakness, interstitial lung disease, and difficulty swallowing. Pseudoangioedema is a rare presentation within dermatomyositis, as illustrated by a study that identified this finding in only 8 out of 121 patients with the diagnosis. When pseudoangioedema affects the neck, it can mimic a primary otolaryngologic condition or be confused with histaminergic or bradykinin-induced angioedema.

Case presentation:

A man in his 40s arrived at the emergency department reporting three weeks of gradually worsening neck swelling and proximal muscle weakness affecting his hip flexors, as well as mild muscle aches. He had no relevant past medical conditions and had never smoked. He was not using a 3-hydroxy-3-methyl-glutaryl-coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase inhibitor. Ludwig’s angina was suspected initially, so a CT scan of the neck was performed, revealing widespread oedema in the strap muscles and inflammatory changes reaching the retropharyngeal space (figure 1). On evaluation, he was afebrile, with oxygen saturation at 99% on room air, and was not tachypneic. Nailfold capillaroscopy identified proximal microhaemorrhages and avascular zones. Laboratory results included a white blood cell count of 9.1×10^9/L and a C-reactive protein (CRP) of 55.6 mg/L at admission. With continued neck swelling and no identifiable cause, he was referred to a quaternary care center specializing in ENT surgery for further assessment. ENT specialists performed a flexible nasendoscopy, which showed no notable structural abnormalities in the soft palate, vallecula, larynx, or post-cricoid space, but the exam was notable for increased secretions and a cough during the procedure.

The on-call immunologist was consulted regarding suspected angioedema, as there was no evident structural abnormality or infectious etiology. Upon evaluation, the patient exhibited widespread facial erythema, including the nasolabial folds, along with an erythematous, swollen, but non-tender neck. He stated he was not taking any medications, including angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and reported no personal or family history of angioedema. His overall presentation did not match typical features of either acquired or hereditary angioedema. Detailed examination revealed discrete nailfold abnormalities indicative of periungual erythema, a cutaneous eruption on the right lateral thigh, and reduced hip flexion strength graded at 4/5. Gottron’s papules and heliotrope rash were not observed. Serum creatine kinase testing was performed, yielding a markedly increased value of 16,184 U/L the following day. Renal function remained within normal limits and urine testing for myoglobin was negative. The patient also demonstrated raised liver transaminases, with an ALT of 143 U/L, AST of 396 U/L, and a high lactate dehydrogenase level of 692 U/L.

Investigations:

Due to concerns regarding possible angioedema, a range of laboratory tests were conducted to investigate potential etiologies for spontaneous angioedema. Complement components C3 and C4, as well as C1 esterase function and concentration, were found to be within normal limits.

The patient's anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) test was positive, displaying a titre of 1:1280 and a speckled pattern. Testing for anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA) yielded negative results. A myositis panel targeting 16 antigens associated with autoimmune inflammatory myopathies was performed using the Euroimmun Euroline immunoassay. The assay demonstrated a strongly positive result for antibodies against small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1), with a signal intensity of 173 (reference range <35). Other myositis antibodies, including anti-Mi-2α, anti-TIF1-γ, anti-Jo-1, and anti-OJ, were negative. Immunoglobulin G (IgG) concentrations, including its subclasses, were within the normal range. Screening for infections revealed negative results for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), hepatitis B core antibody (HBcAb), hepatitis B e antibody (HBeAb), hepatitis C virus (HCV), HIV, varicella zoster virus (VZV) IgG, and cytomegalovirus (CMV) IgG and IgM. The QuantiFERON-TB Gold test returned an indeterminate result; however, testing for acid-fast bacilli in three sputum specimens was negative for Mycobacterium tuberculosis.

Pulmonary function testing demonstrated a restrictive pattern localized to the extrathoracic region, without a notable response to bronchodilator administration. Chest CT imaging identified several non-cavitating nodules dispersed primarily across the mid to lower lung zones, with the largest measuring 15 mm within the right lower lobe. There were irregular areas of peribronchial consolidation consistent with possible aspiration pneumonitis; however, there was no radiographic indication of pulmonary fibrosis. Abdominal CT found no abdomino-pelvic lymphadenopathy and showed neither primary nor metastatic malignancy within the abdomen or pelvis. On video fluoroscopic (barium) swallow assessment, the patient exhibited marked pharyngeal dysphagia, resulting in significant pyriform sinus residue and post-swallow penetration of thin and slightly thickened fluids to the level of the vocal cords.

MRI of the thighs demonstrated findings indicative of active myositis affecting the bilateral obturator internus and externus muscles, with less pronounced involvement seen in the hip adductors, bilateral gracilis, and both distal iliopsoas muscles (see figure 2). Evidence of ongoing muscular inflammation was corroborated by a fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) positron emission tomography (PET) scan, which revealed widespread and abnormal FDG uptake across numerous muscle groups, most notably within the head and neck region, bilateral deltoids, forearms, chest wall, abdominal, and pelvic muscles (refer to figure 3). No sites of metabolically active disease were identified to indicate underlying malignancy. Measurements of tumour markers, such as carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), cancer antigen 19-9 (CA19-9), cancer antigen 125 (CA-125), and prostate-specific antigen (PSA) were all within normal limits.

Histopathological examination of a thigh muscle biopsy identified localized regions with moderate numbers of necrotic, vacuolated degenerating, myophagocytosed, and regenerating myofibers in a perifascicular pattern, along with scattered inflammatory infiltrates containing dispersed histiocytes in the perimysium (figure 4). Immunohistochemical staining for membrane attack complex indicated complement component 5b-9 (C5b-9) deposits in perifascicular endomysial capillaries, a finding characteristic of dermatomyositis. The patient was ultimately diagnosed with moderate to severe dermatomyositis, positive for SAE1 antibody, presenting with inflammatory edema, peribronchial consolidation due to micro-aspiration, dysphagia, and myositis. Skin findings included a positive Holster sign and periungual erythema.

Differential diagnosis:

Due to his subacute onset featuring cervical swelling and increased CRP levels, there was an early suspicion for Ludwig angina or idiopathic angioedema. Notably, muscle weakness and difficulty swallowing were not significant findings during his initial evaluation. The differential diagnoses also encompassed other inflammatory myopathies such as immune-mediated necrotising myopathy and inclusion body myositis, infectious etiologies like cervical cellulitis or erysipelas, deep neck space infections, and bacterial sialadenitis. Additionally, neuromuscular junction disorders including myasthenia gravis, delayed drug hypersensitivity reactions such as drug rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms, acute contact dermatitis, orofacial granulomatosis, hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis, and superior vena cava syndrome were considered.

Treatment:

Owing to considerable involvement of airway musculature, the patient received intravenous methylprednisolone for 3 days at a daily dose of 1000 mg. Subsequently, his regimen was transitioned to a prednisone taper, beginning at 60 mg per day and reduced by 5 mg every 2 to 3 weeks. An initial IVIG (Gamunex 10%) loading dose of 2 g/kg (totaling 165 g) was delivered over 5 days (35 g daily), and he proceeded to receive 5 additional cycles at 4-week intervals, using the same total dose divided among these sessions. Mycophenolate mofetil was initiated at 1 g twice daily, and he was administered rituximab intravenously at 1000 mg per dose, with two doses spaced 2 weeks apart. Methotrexate was avoided because of its link to interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis, given the patient’s abnormal pulmonary function and after discussions regarding adverse effects. Azathioprine and tacrolimus were not employed as mycophenolate mofetil was well tolerated. For Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia prevention, he started on sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim; however, this was discontinued due to the onset of haemolytic anaemia in the absence of G6PD deficiency, after which nebulised pentamidine 300 mg every 4 weeks was tolerated without issue.

The patient was discharged following a 21-day hospitalization. Due to ongoing dysphagia, oral medications were given via nasogastric tube upon discharge.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following three weeks of inpatient care and therapy, the patient showed a moderate recovery in hip flexor strength and significant reduction in cervical soft tissue swelling, although the swelling was not completely resolved at discharge. Dysphagia persisted, requiring continued nasogastric tube feeding for an additional two months.

At the three-week post-discharge follow-up, proximal muscle strength was fully restored, and serum creatine kinase had decreased to 37 U/L. During the immunology assessment two months after discharge, cervical swelling was further diminished, with only some remaining violaceous facial erythema and periungual abnormalities noted on physical examination. Swallowing had improved enough to discontinue the nasogastric tube and initiate soft solid foods. The patient’s regimen included mycophenolate mofetil, monthly intravenous immunoglobulin, and a gradually reducing dose of prednisone.

At three months following discharge, proximal muscle strength remained unchanged, the chest exam did not reveal any abnormalities, and cervical soft tissue swelling had fully resolved. Prednisone was successfully weaned off at seven months post-discharge, at which point dysphagia had resolved completely. Pulmonary function tests repeated at that time demonstrated modest improvement but continued to show a residual restrictive pattern, believed to represent the patient’s baseline physiology (see figure 5a, b). A chest CT performed at eleven months showed near-total resolution of previously identified basal ground-glass changes and airspace consolidation, findings that were initially ascribed to aspiration. At twelve months after diagnosis, his respiratory status continued to be stable and exercise tolerance remained good.

In terms of malignancy surveillance, the patient did not report any constitutional symptoms at follow-up and is to be monitored based on ongoing symptoms, physical assessments, routine laboratory evaluations, and appropriate imaging—including serial chest CT scans. He is also scheduled for gastroscopy and colonoscopy to rule out hidden gastrointestinal or colorectal malignancy.

The management plan included ongoing IVIG therapy and gradual tapering of mycophenolate over the next six months, depending on the clinical trajectory. Close follow-up will be maintained to assess whether adjustments to his medication regimen are warranted.

Discussion:

We describe a patient with dermatomyositis associated with anti-SAE1 antibodies, who initially presented with significant swelling of the cervical soft tissue, which was first interpreted as idiopathic angioedema. Identifying dermatomyositis as a possible cause, together with a detailed physical examination that uncovered subtle skin findings, facilitated prompt diagnosis and management.

Anti-SAE1 antibodies target the small-ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) activating enzyme, a protein resembling ubiquitin in structure, which participates in the sumoylation process—a form of post-translational modification affecting numerous substrates. Dermatomyositis with SAE1 antibody positivity typically manifests with skin signs including heliotrope rash, Gottron’s papules, V-neck sign, and shawl sign. According to a comprehensive review including 215 cases, dysphagia and interstitial lung disease were observed in 46% and 43% of SAE1 dermatomyositis cases, respectively, with malignancy identified in 18%.

Edema of the subcutaneous tissue, particularly presenting as swelling of the face or neck, is rarely observed in dermatomyositis. Among a sizeable cohort of 121 patients diagnosed with dermatomyositis, only 8 individuals (6.61%) experienced angioedema. A review of literature spanning from 1985 to 2013 identified just 19 reported cases where dermatomyositis was accompanied by edema. Autoantibodies like anti-melanoma differentiation-associated gene (MDA)-5 and anti-transcriptional intermediary factor 1γ (TIF1-γ) were more frequently linked to angioedema in comparison to other antibodies. While facial swelling was noted in multiple reports, involvement of the neck was documented less often. Upon reviewing the literature for 10 instances of SAE1-positive dermatomyositis, only three cases included edema, and only one referenced swelling in the throat area, illustrating the rarity of neck region involvement.79

This case underscores the importance for emergency physicians, otolaryngologists, immunologists, and rheumatologists to recognize dermatomyositis as a potential cause of neck swelling, particularly when localized ‘angioedema’ appears without an obvious etiology or is classified as idiopathic. Since symptoms such as dysphagia or other manifestations may be minor, careful assessment for physical signs of dermatomyositis and measurement of creatine kinase are essential. In this instance, the patient’s subcutaneous oedema was attributable to muscular inflammation related to myositis, rather than angioedema resulting from histamine or bradykinin pathways.

Following the recommendations of the International Guideline for Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathy-Associated Cancer Screening developed by the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS), this patient exhibited two high-risk characteristics (dermatomyositis, dysphagia) and two intermediate-risk features (SAE1 antibody positivity, male sex), thereby classifying him as high risk for malignancy. He underwent cancer screening that included computed tomography, tumor marker analysis, and positron emission tomography, with further plans for both upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopic evaluation. Extensive initial and ongoing surveillance has continued to show no evidence of malignancy thus far.

Available data regarding the best pharmacological management for anti-SAE1–positive dermatomyositis are sparse. The largest reported cohort demonstrated varied treatment regimens; most individuals received glucocorticoids alongside multiple steroid-sparing agents, predominantly methotrexate, with others administered mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, or azathioprine, and intravenous immunoglobulin was employed in selected instances. In this case, the patient was managed with steroids, mycophenolate mofetil, intravenous immunoglobulin, and rituximab, resulting in remission. Mycophenolate mofetil was chosen, taking into account the patient's preference for oral daily therapy as well as its tolerability profile and established use in dermatomyositis. Notably, the patient has experienced a favorable prognosis relative to other cases presenting with pronounced tissue oedema in dermatomyositis.
