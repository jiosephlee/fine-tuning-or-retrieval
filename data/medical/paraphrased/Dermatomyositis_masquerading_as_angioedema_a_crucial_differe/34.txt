Title: Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook

Kevin Chan, Gemma Kwan, Hwei Choo Soh, Louise Evans

Abstract:

Summary: Dermatomyositis is an uncommon autoimmune disorder primarily involving the skin, skeletal muscles, and respiratory tract. Rapid initiation of high-dose immunosuppressive therapy is typically necessary in affected individuals to mitigate disease advancement and limit organ involvement. This report describes a previously healthy man in his 40s who presented to the emergency department with pronounced cervical swelling. Initial evaluation by otolaryngology considered a deep neck space infection, but this diagnosis was excluded. The patient was subsequently referred to clinical immunology for assessment and management of presumed idiopathic angioedema. Owing to the atypical characteristics of the neck oedema—unlike classic angioedema—and the presence of cutaneous and periungual changes, creatine kinase was urgently measured. He received a provisional diagnosis of dermatomyositis and was later confirmed to have a positive small ubiquitin-like modifier-1 activating enzyme (SAE1) autoantibody along with characteristic findings on muscle biopsy. This case underscores the rarity of neck swelling not attributable to histamine or bradykinin mechanisms as an initial sign of dermatomyositis, presenting with both dysphagia and significant myopathy that necessitated immediate therapy.

Background:

Dermatomyositis is a myopathy of unknown etiology that is characterized by inflammation and commonly involves the skin and muscles, though other organs such as the gastrointestinal tract, joints, lungs, and heart can also be implicated. The condition's estimated incidence is 10 per million individuals. Onset demonstrates a bimodal pattern, predominantly occurring in pediatric patients aged 5–14 years and adults between 45–64 years. Typical skin manifestations consist of Gottron’s papules, the shawl sign, heliotrope rash, and periungual telangiectasias. Additional clinical features include proximal muscle weakness, interstitial lung involvement, and difficulty swallowing. Pseudoangioedema is a rare finding in dermatomyositis; one study reported its presence in just 8 of 121 individuals diagnosed with the disease. When pseudoangioedema involves the neck, it may present similarly to primary disorders seen in otolaryngology or be misinterpreted as either histaminergic or bradykinin-driven angioedema.

Case presentation:

A man in his 40s came to the emergency department reporting a three-week progression of neck swelling and weakness in the hip flexor muscles, with mild muscle pain. He had no prior significant medical issues and had never smoked. He was not using any HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors. The primary diagnosis was Ludwig’s angina, and a CT scan of the neck was ordered, revealing widespread oedema of the strap muscles and inflammation extending into the retropharyngeal space (see figure 1). His temperature was normal, oxygen saturation was 99% on room air, and he did not have rapid breathing. Nailfold capillaroscopy revealed proximal microhaemorrhages and areas lacking vascularity. Upon admission, his white blood cell count measured 9.1×10^9/L, and C-reactive protein was 55.6 mg/L. Because the neck swelling persisted without an identified cause, he was sent to a quaternary care center with ENT surgical capabilities for further assessment. ENT specialists performed flexible nasendoscopy, finding no substantial structural abnormalities in the soft palate, vallecula, larynx, or post-cricoid region, but observed increased secretions and the patient coughed during the procedure.

The on-call immunologist was consulted regarding a possible diagnosis of angioedema, given the absence of evident structural or infectious etiologies. Upon assessment, the patient demonstrated widespread facial erythema, which extended across the nasolabial folds, as well as a swollen, erythematous, but non-tender neck. He reported no use of medications such as angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and stated there was no history of angioedema in himself or his family. His presentation was not consistent with either acquired or hereditary angioedema. Detailed examination detected mild nailfold abnormalities indicative of periungual erythema, in addition to a rash over the right lateral thigh and diminished hip flexor strength rated at 4/5. There were no Gottron’s papules or heliotrope eruption. Creatine kinase was measured the following day and found to be markedly elevated at 16 184 U/L. Renal function remained within normal limits and urine myoglobin tested negative. Liver enzymes were also raised, with alanine aminotransferase (ALT) at 143 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) at 396 U/L, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) at 692 U/L.

Investigations:

Due to the initial suspicion for angioedema, a series of diagnostic evaluations were undertaken to identify possible sources of spontaneous angioedema. Complement assays for C3 and C4 were within reference limits, and both the functional activity and concentration of C1 esterase inhibitor were normal.

Testing revealed a positive anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) at a titre of 1:1280 with a speckled pattern. Anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA) results were negative. A myositis-specific antibody panel covering 16 autoantigens associated with autoimmune inflammatory myopathies was analyzed using the Euroimmun Euroline immunoassay. This demonstrated a strong positivity for anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibodies, with a signal intensity of 173 (reference value <35). The panel was negative for other myositis-specific antibodies, including anti-Mi-2 alpha (anti-Mi-2α), anti-Transcription Intermediary Factor-1 gamma (anti-TIF1-γ), anti–Histidyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-Jo-1), and anti–Isoleucyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-OJ). Serum immunoglobulin G (IgG) levels and subclasses were unremarkable.

For immunosuppression screening, serologies were negative for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), hepatitis C core antibody (HBcAb), hepatitis B e antibody (HBeAb), hepatitis C virus (HCV), HIV, varicella zoster virus (VZV) IgG, and cytomegalovirus (CMV) IgG/immunoglobulin M (IgM). QuantiFERON-TB Gold testing yielded an indeterminate result, yet three sputum samples tested negative for acid-fast bacilli consistent with Mycobacterium tuberculosis.

Pulmonary function testing demonstrated a restrictive pattern originating outside the thoracic cavity, with no notable improvement following bronchodilator administration. Chest CT identified several non-cavitating pulmonary nodules predominantly located in the mid to lower lung zones, the largest measuring 15 mm in diameter within the right lower lobe. Additionally, there were irregular areas of peribronchial consolidation, likely attributable to aspiration pneumonitis, though pulmonary fibrosis was not evident radiologically. Abdominal CT revealed that abdomino-pelvic lymph nodes were not enlarged, and there were no signs of primary or metastatic cancer within the abdomen or pelvis. On video fluoroscopic barium swallow examination, marked pharyngeal dysphagia was observed, resulting in substantial pyriform fossa residue and post-swallow penetration of thin and slightly thickened liquids reaching the vocal cords.

Magnetic resonance imaging of the thighs revealed findings indicative of active myositis involving the bilateral obturator internus and externus muscles, with less severe involvement observed in the hip adductor muscles, bilateral gracilis, and both distal iliopsoas muscles (figure 2). Evidence of ongoing muscle inflammation was corroborated by a fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) positron emission tomography (PET) scan, which demonstrated widespread abnormal FDG uptake within various muscle groups, most notably in the head and neck, both deltoids, forearms, the chest wall, abdomen, and pelvis (figure 3). There was no evidence of metabolically active lesions elsewhere to suggest an underlying malignancy. Tumor markers, including carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), cancer antigen 19-9 (CA19-9), cancer antigen 125 (CA-125), and prostate-specific antigen (PSA), were within normal limits.

Histopathological examination of a thigh muscle biopsy identified regions with moderate numbers of necrotic, vacuolated degenerating, myophagocytosed, and regenerating myofibers arranged in a perifascicular pattern, along with scattered perimysial infiltration by dispersed histiocytes (figure 4). Immunohistochemical analysis for the membrane attack complex detected deposition of complement component 5b-9 (C5b-9) in perifascicular endomysial capillaries, consistent with findings typically associated with dermatomyositis. The patient was ultimately diagnosed with moderate to severe dermatomyositis characterized by SAE1 antibody positivity, inflammatory edema, peribronchial consolidation due to micro-aspiration, dysphagia, and myositis. Skin findings included a positive Holster sign and periungual erythema.

Differential diagnosis:

Due to the gradual onset of neck swelling combined with increased CRP levels, initial suspicion centered on Ludwig angina or idiopathic angioedema. Early in the disease course, significant muscle weakness or difficulties with swallowing were not prevalent. The list of differential diagnoses encompassed other inflammatory myopathies such as immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy and inclusion body myositis; infectious etiologies like cellulitis or erysipelas of the neck, deep neck space abscesses, and bacterial sialadenitis; neuromuscular junction disorders including myasthenia gravis; delayed hypersensitivity reactions to medications, such as drug rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms; acute contact dermatitis; orofacial granulomatosis; hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis; and superior vena cava syndrome.

Treatment:

Given extensive involvement of airway musculature, the patient received intravenous methylprednisolone at 1000 mg daily for three days. This was followed by a transition to an oral prednisone taper starting at 60 mg per day, decreasing by 5 mg every two to three weeks. A loading course of intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) (Gamunex 10%) was provided at 2 g/kg (total of 165 g) over five days (35 g per day), then continued with five additional cycles, each spaced four weeks apart, delivering a cumulative dose of 2 g/kg (=165 g) divided among these cycles. Mycophenolate mofetil was initiated at 1 g twice daily, and he was administered two doses of rituximab at 1000 mg intravenously, separated by two weeks. Methotrexate was not chosen due to its known association with interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis, particularly given the patient’s abnormal pulmonary function tests and after discussing potential adverse effects with him. Azathioprine and tacrolimus were also not utilized as mycophenolate was well-tolerated. Sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim was prescribed initially for Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia prophylaxis, but was discontinued following onset of haemolytic anaemia (in the absence of glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency), and he subsequently tolerated inhaled pentamidine 300 mg every four weeks.

After a 21-day hospitalization, the patient was discharged. Due to persistent dysphagia, his oral medications were given via nasogastric tube upon discharge.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following three weeks of hospitalization and therapy, the patient exhibited moderate improvement in weakness of the hip flexors and significant reduction in cervical soft tissue swelling, though the swelling had not completely subsided by the time of discharge. Swallowing difficulties remained, making continued use of a nasogastric tube for nutrition necessary for an additional two months.

At a follow-up three weeks post-discharge, strength in proximal muscles had returned to normal and serum creatine kinase levels had decreased to 37 U/L. During an immunology assessment two months after discharge, there was ongoing improvement in the cervical swelling, with only minimal persistent violaceous erythema on the face and periungual changes noted. Swallowing had improved enough to permit removal of the nasogastric tube and transition to a soft-solid diet. The patient’s medications included mycophenolate mofetil, monthly intravenous immunoglobulin, and a gradually decreasing dose of prednisone.

At three months after discharge, the patient's proximal muscle strength remained consistent, chest auscultation revealed no abnormalities, and the cervical soft tissue swelling had completely subsided. Prednisone was discontinued successfully seven months post-discharge, at which point his dysphagia had resolved entirely. Subsequent pulmonary function testing indicated slight improvement, with a persistent restrictive pattern considered consistent with his baseline (see figure 5a, b). Follow-up chest CT at eleven months showed near-total resolution of previously detected basal ground-glass opacities and airspace consolidation, which had originally been ascribed to aspiration. One year after the initial diagnosis, respiratory status was stable, and exercise tolerance was good.

Regarding malignancy surveillance, he reported no constitutional symptoms at follow-up. Ongoing assessment will include monitoring for symptoms, thorough clinical examination, routine blood tests, and repeated imaging such as serial chest CTs. He is scheduled for gastroscopy and colonoscopy to rule out any hidden gastrointestinal or colorectal malignancies.

The treatment plan involves continuing intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) and gradually reducing the dose of mycophenolate over the next six months, depending on his clinical progress. He will be closely followed to assess if modifications to his medication regimen become necessary.

Discussion:

We describe a patient with dermatomyositis positive for anti-SAE1 antibodies who developed marked swelling of the cervical soft tissue, which was initially presumed to be idiopathic angioedema. Awareness of dermatomyositis as a possible cause, along with meticulous examination that detected subtle dermatological findings, permitted prompt identification and management.

Anti-SAE1 antibodies recognize the small-ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) activating enzyme, a molecule with structural similarity to ubiquitin that participates in sumoylation—a post-translational modification affecting numerous substrate proteins. The clinical phenotype of SAE1 antibody-associated dermatomyositis frequently includes skin manifestations like the heliotrope rash, Gottron’s papules, the V-neck sign, and the shawl sign. Analysis of a sizable literature cohort involving 215 cases demonstrated rates of dysphagia and interstitial lung disease of 46% and 43%, respectively, among those with SAE1 dermatomyositis; malignancy was observed in 18% of cases.

Edema of the subcutaneous tissue, notably presenting as swelling in the face or neck, occurs infrequently in cases of dermatomyositis. Among 121 patients enrolled in a large cohort study of dermatomyositis, just 8 (6.61%) were reported to have angioedema. A review of published reports from 1985 to 2013 identified only 19 instances where dermatomyositis presented with oedema. Certain autoantibodies, specifically anti-melanoma differentiation-associated gene (MDA)-5 and anti-transcriptional intermediary factor 1γ (TIF1-γ), demonstrated a higher association with angioedema compared to other antibody types. Although facial edema has been reported in several case descriptions, swelling localized to the neck is documented less often. In our review of 10 published cases of SAE1-positive dermatomyositis, oedema was noted in only three, and only one referenced swelling involving the throat, indicating that neck involvement is a rare manifestation.79

This case highlights to emergency physicians, otolaryngologists, immunologists, and rheumatologists the importance of considering dermatomyositis in the differential diagnosis for neck swelling, particularly when faced with localized 'angioedema' that lacks a clear cause or is deemed idiopathic. Because symptoms such as dysphagia and additional systemic manifestations may be mild or not readily apparent, a careful examination for physical indicators of dermatomyositis and the measurement of creatine kinase levels is essential. The patient's subcutaneous edema resulted from muscle inflammation attributable to myositis rather than from histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated pathways that typically characterize angioedema.

Following the International Guideline for Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathy-Associated Cancer Screening established by the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS), this patient exhibited two high risk features (dermatomyositis, dysphagia) along with two intermediate risk factors (SAE1 antibody positivity and male sex), categorizing him as high risk for malignancy. He underwent cancer screening that encompassed computed tomography scans, tumor marker evaluation, and positron emission tomography (PET) imaging, with planned upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopy. Both initial and follow-up surveillance have been reassuring thus far.

Current data regarding the most effective pharmacologic management of anti-SAE1–positive dermatomyositis remains sparse. In the largest cohort reported thus far, therapeutic strategies varied; the majority of patients were treated with glucocorticoids alongside various steroid-sparing agents, most frequently methotrexate, as well as mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, and azathioprine. Select individuals also received intravenous immunoglobulin. The patient described herein was administered steroids, mycophenolate mofetil, intravenous immunoglobulin, and rituximab, subsequently achieving remission. The choice of mycophenolate mofetil was influenced by the patient's preference for a daily oral medication, its favorable tolerability profile, and established utility in dermatomyositis. Notably, the patient's outlook is positive relative to others who develop prominent tissue oedema associated with dermatomyositis.
