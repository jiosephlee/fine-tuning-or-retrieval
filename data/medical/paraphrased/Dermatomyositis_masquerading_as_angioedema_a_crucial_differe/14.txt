Title: Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook

Kevin Chan, Gemma Kwan, Hwei Choo Soh, Louise Evans

Abstract:

Summary: Dermatomyositis is an uncommon autoimmune condition primarily involving the skin, muscles, and respiratory system. Individuals diagnosed with dermatomyositis often need urgent, aggressive immunosuppressive therapy to mitigate disease progression and prevent multiorgan complications. A previously healthy man in his 40s presented acutely to the emergency department with pronounced swelling of the neck. He was initially assessed by ear, nose and throat specialists, who suspected a deep neck space infection. After infection was excluded, clinical immunology was consulted to evaluate and manage presumed idiopathic angioedema. However, due to atypical characteristics of the neck swelling and concurrent skin and nail abnormalities, a creatine kinase measurement was promptly performed. A preliminary diagnosis of dermatomyositis was made, later confirmed by detection of small ubiquitin-like modifier-1 activating enzyme (SAE1) autoantibody and characteristic findings on muscle biopsy. This case demonstrates that neck swelling unrelated to histamine or bradykinin pathways can be a rare presenting feature of dermatomyositis, manifesting alongside dysphagia and substantial muscular involvement that necessitated urgent intervention.

Background:

Dermatomyositis is an inflammatory myopathy of unknown cause, most commonly involving the skin and skeletal muscles, but it may also impact the gastrointestinal tract, joints, pulmonary system, and cardiac tissue. Its estimated annual incidence is approximately 10 per million individuals. The disease demonstrates a bimodal age distribution, most frequently presenting in children between 5 and 14 years old and adults between 45 and 64 years of age. Notable skin manifestations include Gottron’s papules, heliotrope rash, the shawl sign, and periungual telangiectasias. Additional clinical features comprise proximal muscle weakness, interstitial lung disease, and difficulty swallowing. Pseudoangioedema is a rare presentation in dermatomyositis; according to one study, it occurred in just 8 out of 121 patients with the diagnosis. When it involves the neck, this finding may mimic a primary otolaryngological disorder or be misidentified as angioedema due to histamine or bradykinin.

Case presentation:

A man in his 40s presented to the emergency department after experiencing progressive neck swelling over three weeks, along with proximal muscle weakness affecting his hip flexors and mild muscle pain. He did not have any notable prior medical conditions and had never smoked. There was no history of 3-hydroxy-3-methyl-glutaryl-coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase inhibitor usage. The working diagnosis at presentation was Ludwig’s angina, prompting a neck CT scan which revealed widespread edema involving the strap muscles, with inflammatory changes extending into the retropharyngeal space (figure 1). On examination, he was afebrile, had oxygen saturation of 99% on room air, and showed no signs of tachypnea. Nailfold capillaroscopy demonstrated proximal nailfold microhemorrhages and areas lacking vascularity. Laboratory assessment showed a white blood cell count of 9.1×10^9/L and a C-reactive protein (CRP) level of 55.6 mg/L at admission. Since his neck swelling persisted without an apparent cause, he was referred to a quaternary care facility with ear, nose, and throat (ENT) surgical capabilities for further workup. Flexible nasendoscopy by ENT revealed no major anatomical anomalies in the soft palate, vallecula, larynx, or post-cricoid area, but the procedure was notable for increased secretions and induction of cough.

The case was reviewed with the on-call immunologist due to suspicion of angioedema, since no definite infectious or structural etiology had been identified. Examination revealed diffuse erythema involving the entire face, including the nasolabial folds, and the neck appeared red, edematous, yet not tender. The patient reported not taking any medications, including angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and had neither a personal nor family history of angioedema. Overall, the patient’s presentation was not typical for either acquired or hereditary forms of angioedema. Detailed physical assessment showed subtle periungual erythema at the nailfolds, a cutaneous eruption over the right lateral thigh, and diminished power of 4/5 in the hip flexors. There were no Gottron’s papules or heliotrope rash observed. Creatine kinase testing was ordered and, the following day, was noted to be markedly elevated at 16 184 U/L. Renal parameters remained within normal limits and urine myoglobin screening was negative. Liver enzymes were increased as evidenced by alanine aminotransferase (ALT) at 143 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) at 396 U/L, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) at 692 U/L.

Investigations:

With the preliminary suspicion of angioedema, multiple investigations were conducted to identify possible etiologies for spontaneous angioedema. Complement components C3 and C4 were found to be within normal limits, as were the function and quantitative measurement of C1 esterase.

Testing revealed a positive anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) with a titer of 1:1280 and a speckled immunofluorescence pattern. Anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA) screening returned negative. A myositis-specific antibody panel analyzing 16 antigens implicated in autoimmune inflammatory myopathies was performed using the Euroimmun Euroline immunoassay. Results showed a markedly elevated anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibody level, with a signal intensity of 173 (normal range <35), while antibodies to other myositis-related antigens—including anti-Mi-2 alpha (anti-Mi-2α), anti-Transcription Intermediary Factor-1 gamma (anti-TIF1-γ), anti–Histidyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-Jo-1), and anti–Isoleucyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-OJ)—were all negative. Immunoglobulin G (IgG) levels and its subclasses were measured and found to be within normal limits.

For his immunosuppression assessment, the patient tested negative for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), hepatitis C core antibody (HBcAb), hepatitis B e antibody (HBeAb), hepatitis C virus (HCV), human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), varicella zoster virus (VZV) IgG, and both immunoglobulin G (IgG) and immunoglobulin M (IgM) for cytomegalovirus (CMV). QuantiFERON-TB Gold testing was indeterminate, yet three sputum specimens were all negative for acid-fast bacilli indicating Mycobacterium tuberculosis.

Pulmonary function testing demonstrated an extrathoracic restrictive pattern without a notable bronchodilator effect. Chest CT revealed several non-cavitating nodules within the lungs, predominantly located from the mid to lower zones, with the largest nodule (15 mm) identified in the right lower lobe. There were also irregular areas of peribronchial consolidation consistent with aspiration pneumonitis, although no radiological signs of pulmonary fibrosis were noted. Abdominal CT showed normal abdomino-pelvic lymph node size and no indications of either primary or secondary malignancy in the abdominal or pelvic regions. A video fluoroscopic barium swallow examination identified profound pharyngeal dysphagia, resulting in substantial residue within the pyriform fossae and post-deglutition penetration of both thin and mildly thick liquids reaching the vocal cords.

Magnetic resonance imaging of the thighs revealed findings indicative of active myositis involving the bilateral obturator internus and externus muscles, with less pronounced involvement seen in the hip adductors, gracilis muscles bilaterally, and distal portions of the iliopsoas muscles (see figure 2). Evidence of ongoing muscle inflammation was corroborated by a fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) positron emission tomography (PET) scan, which demonstrated widespread and abnormal FDG uptake across multiple muscle groups, most notably in the head and neck, bilateral deltoids, forearms, chest wall, abdomen, and pelvis (figure 3). There was no FDG uptake in other regions to suggest an underlying malignancy. Assessment of tumor markers, including carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), cancer antigen 19-9 (CA19-9), cancer antigen 125 (CA-125), and prostate-specific antigen (PSA), yielded results within normal limits.

Histopathological analysis of a muscle biopsy from the thigh revealed areas containing a moderate number of necrotic, vacuolated degenerating, myophagocytosed, and regenerating myofibres arranged in a perifascicular pattern, along with scattered inflammation in the perimysial region composed of individual histiocytes (figure 4). Immunohistochemical staining for the membrane attack complex identified deposition of complement component 5b-9 (C5b-9) within the perifascicular endomysial capillaries, a hallmark finding in dermatomyositis. Consequently, a diagnosis was established of moderate to severe SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis, manifesting with inflammatory edema, peribronchial consolidation attributed to micro-aspiration, dysphagia, and myositis. Dermatologic features included a positive Holster sign and erythema around the nail folds.

Differential diagnosis:

Given the subacute onset of neck swelling accompanied by increased CRP, initial considerations included Ludwig angina and idiopathic angioedema. Early in his course, muscle weakness—including dysphagia—was not particularly evident. The differential diagnosis encompassed various possibilities, such as other inflammatory myopathies (including immune-mediated necrotising myopathy and inclusion body myositis), infections (such as cellulitis or erysipelas affecting the neck, deep neck space infections, and bacterial sialadenitis), neuromuscular junction conditions like myasthenia gravis, delayed drug hypersensitivity reactions (for example, drug rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms), acute contact dermatitis, orofacial granulomatosis, hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis, and superior vena cava syndrome.

Treatment:

Given the pronounced involvement of airway musculature, the patient received intravenous methylprednisolone at 1000 mg per day for 3 days. This was followed by a prednisone taper beginning at 60 mg daily, with gradual reduction by 5 mg every 2 to 3 weeks. The initial course of intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) (Gamunex 10%) was administered as a loading dose of 2 g/kg (totaling 165 g) over 5 days (35 g per day), succeeded by five additional cycles spaced 4 weeks apart, each with a cumulative dose of 2 g/kg (165 g) divided across these infusions. Mycophenolate mofetil was started at 1 g twice daily. Additionally, the patient received two intravenous rituximab infusions at 1000 mg each, spaced two weeks apart. Methotrexate was avoided due to its known association with interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis, particularly given the patient's abnormal lung function results; this decision was also informed by patient discussion regarding possible side effects. Mycophenolate was continued, as it was well tolerated, without the need for azathioprine or tacrolimus. For prevention of Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, prophylaxis initially consisted of sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim, which was discontinued due to the onset of haemolytic anaemia (not attributable to glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency). The patient subsequently tolerated inhaled pentamidine 300 mg administered every four weeks.

He was discharged after a 21-day inpatient stay. His oral medications were administered through nasogastric tube as he had persistent dysphagia at the time of discharge.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following a 3-week period of inpatient care, the patient showed moderate gains in hip flexor strength and significant reduction in cervical soft tissue edema, though the swelling was still present at discharge. However, dysphagia persisted, requiring ongoing nasogastric tube feeding for an additional 2 months.

At a follow-up 3 weeks post-discharge, proximal muscle strength had returned to normal and serum creatine kinase had decreased to 37 U/L. During an immunology assessment 2 months after discharge, further improvement in cervical swelling was observed, with examination revealing only persistent violaceous erythema on the face and periungual alterations. The ability to swallow had improved sufficiently to permit discontinuation of nasogastric feeding and introduction of a soft solid diet. The treatment regimen consisted of continued mycophenolate mofetil, monthly intravenous immunoglobulin, and a tapering dose of prednisone.

Three months after discharge, the patient’s proximal muscle strength remained unchanged, his chest exam was normal, and the cervical soft tissue swelling had completely subsided. Prednisone was successfully tapered off seven months post-discharge, at which point the dysphagia had completely resolved. Subsequent pulmonary function tests revealed slight improvement, though a persistent restrictive pattern was present, believed to be consistent with his baseline status (see figure 5a, b). An 11-month follow-up chest CT showed almost complete resolution of the earlier basal ground-glass opacities and airspace consolidation, findings that were initially ascribed to aspiration. At one year post-diagnosis, his respiratory status remained stable and he exhibited satisfactory exercise capacity.

Regarding cancer surveillance, he reported no constitutional symptoms at follow-up, and ongoing monitoring will be based on reported symptoms, physical exams, routine blood tests, and appropriate imaging, including serial chest CT scans. He is scheduled for gastroscopy and colonoscopy to rule out hidden gastrointestinal or colorectal malignancy.

The management plan includes continuing intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) therapy, with a gradual reduction in mycophenolate dosage over the next six months as long as his clinical status allows. He will be regularly reassessed to determine whether modifications to his medication regimen are necessary.

Discussion:

We present a case involving dermatomyositis with anti-SAE1 antibodies, manifesting as marked swelling of the cervical soft tissue, which was initially presumed to be idiopathic angioedema. Dermatomyositis was considered in the differential diagnosis, and a thorough physical assessment revealing faint dermatologic findings facilitated an accurate and prompt diagnosis, enabling initiation of appropriate therapy.

The anti-SAE1 antibody is directed against the small-ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) activating enzyme, a protein that resembles ubiquitin and mediates post-translational sumoylation of multiple target proteins. Patients with SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis typically display skin findings, including heliotrope rash, Gottron's papules, V-neck sign, and shawl sign. According to an extensive literature review of 215 patients, dysphagia and interstitial lung disease occurred in 46% and 43% of SAE1 dermatomyositis cases, respectively, and malignancy was observed in 18%.

Subcutaneous edema, particularly involving the face or neck, is a rare manifestation in dermatomyositis. Among a cohort of 121 individuals with dermatomyositis, only 8 (6.61%) exhibited angioedema. A review of the literature covering publications from 1985 to 2013 identified just 19 documented instances of dermatomyositis presenting with edema. Antibodies such as anti–melanoma differentiation-associated gene (MDA)-5 and anti–transcriptional intermediary factor 1γ (TIF1-γ) were observed to be more frequently linked to angioedema than other serologic markers. Although several reports noted facial edema, descriptions of neck swelling were comparatively rare. Our review of 10 cases of SAE1-positive dermatomyositis revealed that edema was reported in only three instances, with just one noting swelling in the throat area, highlighting the rarity of neck involvement.79

This report highlights the importance for emergency medicine physicians, ENT specialists, immunologists, and rheumatologists to consider dermatomyositis as a potential cause of cervical swelling, particularly when faced with localized ‘angioedema’ of unknown or idiopathic origin. Since dysphagia and additional systemic symptoms may be mild or overlooked, careful examination for signs indicative of dermatomyositis and obtaining a creatine kinase measurement are essential steps. In this patient, the subcutaneous oedema was attributable to muscle inflammation associated with myositis, rather than to angioedema mediated by histamine or bradykinin pathways.

Following the recommendations set forth in the International Guideline for Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathy-Associated Cancer Screening by the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS), the patient qualified as high risk for malignancy based on two major risk factors (presence of dermatomyositis and dysphagia) as well as two intermediate risk factors (positivity for SAE1 antibody and male gender). Accordingly, comprehensive malignancy evaluation was initiated, encompassing CT imaging, tumor marker assessments, and PET scanning, with planned upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopic procedures. Both baseline and follow-up surveillance thus far have provided reassuring results.

There is a scarcity of robust data regarding the most effective medication regimen for anti-SAE1–positive dermatomyositis. The largest available cohort demonstrates considerable variability in management, although the majority of individuals were administered glucocorticoids along with diverse steroid-sparing therapies, most frequently methotrexate, but also including mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, and azathioprine; intravenous immunoglobulin was reserved for certain patients. In this case, the patient received steroids, mycophenolate mofetil, intravenous immunoglobulin, and rituximab, resulting in disease remission. Mycophenolate mofetil was chosen, taking into account the patient’s wish for an oral daily medication, its favorable tolerability profile, and its recognized application in dermatomyositis. Comparatively, this patient’s outlook is favorable relative to others who experience substantial tissue edema associated with dermatomyositis.
