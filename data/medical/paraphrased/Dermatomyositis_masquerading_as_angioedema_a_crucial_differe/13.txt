Title: Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook

Kevin Chan, Gemma Kwan, Hwei Choo Soh, Louise Evans

Abstract:

Summary: Dermatomyositis is an uncommon autoimmune disorder that most often involves the skin, musculature, and the respiratory tract. Rapid initiation of high-dose immunosuppression is generally necessary for patients with dermatomyositis to limit disease advancement and minimize damage to multiple organs. A previously healthy man in his 40s presented to the emergency department with marked cervical swelling. Initially, he was assessed by otolaryngology for a likely deep neck space infection. After this etiology was excluded, he was referred to clinical immunology for evaluation and management of presumed idiopathic angioedema. Given that the pattern of neck edema was atypical for angioedema and accompanied by dermatologic and nail findings, a creatine kinase measurement was promptly performed. The working diagnosis of dermatomyositis was made, later supported by a positive SAE1 (small ubiquitin-like modifier-1 activating enzyme) autoantibody and characteristic findings on muscle biopsy. This report illustrates neck swelling independent of histamine or bradykinin mechanisms as an uncommon manifestation of dermatomyositis, which in this patient was associated with dysphagia and significant myopathy necessitating urgent therapy.

Background:

Dermatomyositis is a form of idiopathic inflammatory myopathy primarily impacting the skin and skeletal musculature, though it may also involve organs such as the gastrointestinal tract, joints, pulmonary system, and cardiac tissue. Its prevalence is approximately 10 cases per million individuals. The disease demonstrates a bimodal age distribution, with onset most frequently observed in children between 5 and 14 years of age and adults between 45 and 64 years. Classic dermatologic manifestations comprise Gottron’s papules, the shawl sign, heliotrope rash, and periungual telangiectasias. Additional clinical features can include proximal muscle weakness, interstitial lung involvement, and difficulty swallowing. Pseudoangioedema occurs rarely in dermatomyositis; in one study, only 8 out of 121 patients with the disease exhibited this finding. When present on the neck, it can mimic an otolaryngologic disorder or be erroneously identified as histaminergic or bradykinin-driven angioedema.

Case presentation:

A man in his 40s arrived at the emergency department after experiencing three weeks of gradually worsening neck swelling and weakness in his hip flexor muscles, along with mild muscle aches. He had no notable past medical conditions and had never smoked. He was not on any 3-hydroxy-3-methyl-glutaryl-coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase inhibitors. The preliminary diagnosis was Ludwig’s angina, and a CT scan of the neck revealed generalized swelling of the neck with involvement of the strap muscles and inflammatory changes extending into the retropharyngeal space (see figure 1). On examination, he was not febrile, had an oxygen saturation of 99% on room air, and was not experiencing rapid breathing. Nailfold capillaroscopy identified proximal microhaemorrhages and avascular regions at the nailfolds. Laboratory results on arrival showed a white blood cell count of 9.1×10^9/L and a C-reactive protein (CRP) level of 55.6 mg/L. As the cause of the persistent neck swelling remained unclear, he was referred to a quaternary care center with an ear, nose, and throat (ENT) surgical team for additional investigation. The ENT team performed a flexible nasendoscopy, which did not reveal major structural abnormalities in the soft palate, vallecula, larynx, or post-cricoid region; however, the procedure was notable for increased secretions and patient coughing.

The on-call immunologist was consulted regarding the possibility of angioedema, given the absence of an identifiable structural or infectious etiology. Upon assessment, the patient exhibited widespread facial erythema, including the nasolabial folds, as well as an erythematous, swollen, yet non-tender neck. He reported not taking any medications such as angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and denied any personal or family history of angioedema. Collectively, his clinical presentation did not conform to typical patterns of either acquired or hereditary angioedema. Detailed examination uncovered mild periungual erythema in the nailfolds, a rash on the right lateral thigh, and decreased hip flexor strength at 4/5. Neither Gottron’s papules nor heliotrope rash were observed. Serum creatine kinase, obtained the following day, was markedly increased at 16,184 U/L. Kidney function was unremarkable and urine myoglobin testing was negative. The patient also demonstrated elevated liver enzymes, with an alanine aminotransferase (ALT) of 143 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) of 396 U/L, and a lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) of 692 U/L.

Investigations:

Given the initial suspicion of angioedema, multiple investigations were conducted to identify potential etiologies for spontaneous angioedema. Testing revealed that his C3 and C4 complement components were within normal limits, as were both the functional activity and quantitative measurement of C1 esterase.

The antinuclear antibody (ANA) assay returned positive with a titre of 1:1280 and displayed a speckled pattern. Anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA) testing was negative. A myositis-specific antibody panel comprising 16 antigens associated with autoimmune inflammatory myopathies was analyzed using the Euroimmun Euroline immunoassay. The results demonstrated a strong positivity for anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibodies, yielding a signal intensity of 173 (reference <35). All other myositis antibodies tested, including anti-Mi-2 alpha (anti-Mi-2α), anti–Transcription Intermediary Factor-1 gamma (anti–TIF1-γ), anti–Histidyl-tRNA synthetase (anti–Jo-1), and anti–Isoleucyl-tRNA synthetase (anti–OJ), were negative. His total immunoglobulin G (IgG), as well as IgG subclasses, were within normal ranges. For evaluation of infectious risks prior to immunosuppression, screening showed negative results for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), hepatitis C core antibody (HBcAb), hepatitis B e antibody (HBeAb), hepatitis C virus (HCV), HIV, varicella zoster virus (VZV) IgG, and cytomegalovirus (CMV) IgG/IgM. QuantiFERON-TB Gold test was indeterminate; nonetheless, three sputum samples tested negative for acid-fast bacilli for Mycobacterium tuberculosis.

Pulmonary function testing demonstrated a restrictive pattern suggestive of extrathoracic involvement, and did not indicate any appreciable response to bronchodilator therapy. Chest CT identified several non-cavitating nodules located predominantly in the mid to lower lung zones, with the largest nodule measuring 15 mm in the right lower lobe. There were also patchy zones of peribronchial consolidation, considered likely due to aspiration pneumonitis, while no signs consistent with pulmonary fibrosis were observed on imaging. Abdominal CT revealed no abdomino-pelvic lymphadenopathy and showed neither primary nor metastatic malignancy within the abdomen or pelvis. A video fluoroscopic barium swallow assessment demonstrated significant pharyngeal dysphagia, resulting in marked pyriform sinus residue and penetration of thin and mildly thick liquids to the level of the vocal cords after swallowing.

MRI imaging of the thighs revealed findings indicative of active myositis affecting both obturator internus and externus muscles, with less pronounced involvement noted in the hip adductors, bilateral gracilis muscles, and both distal iliopsoas muscles (see figure 2). Evidence of ongoing muscle inflammation was corroborated by a fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) positron emission tomography (PET) scan, which demonstrated widespread and abnormal uptake of FDG in various muscle groups, with the most intense signals identified in the head and neck regions, both deltoids, forearm muscles, chest wall, abdomen, and pelvis (refer to figure 3). No other sites displayed metabolically active lesions that would suggest underlying malignancy. Tumor markers, including carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), cancer antigen 19-9 (CA19-9), cancer antigen 125 (CA-125), and prostate-specific antigen (PSA), were all within normal limits.

Histopathological examination of a muscle biopsy from the thigh identified regions containing moderate numbers of necrotic, vacuolated, degenerating, myophagocytosed, and regenerating myofibres, particularly in a perifascicular pattern, with scattered perimysial inflammatory infiltrates composed predominantly of dispersed histiocytes (see figure 4). Immunohistochemical staining targeting the membrane attack complex revealed the presence of complement component 5b-9 (C5b-9) in perifascicular endomysial capillaries, a finding characteristic of dermatomyositis. The final diagnosis established was moderate to severe SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis, accompanied by inflammatory edema, peribronchial consolidation attributable to micro-aspiration, dysphagia, and muscle inflammation. Skin findings consisted of a positive Holster sign and erythema around the nailfolds.

Differential diagnosis:

Due to the subacute onset of neck swelling accompanied by an elevated CRP, initial diagnostic considerations included Ludwig angina and idiopathic angioedema. Early in the disease course, significant muscle weakness and dysphagia were not observed. The differential diagnosis encompassed various inflammatory myopathies such as immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy and inclusion body myositis, infectious etiologies like cellulitis or erysipelas involving the neck, deep neck space abscesses, and bacterial sialadenitis, neuromuscular junction disorders including myasthenia gravis, delayed-type drug hypersensitivity reactions such as drug rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms (DRESS), acute contact dermatitis, orofacial granulomatosis, hypocomplementemic urticarial vasculitis, as well as superior vena cava syndrome.

Treatment:

Given extensive involvement of the airway musculature, the patient received intravenous methylprednisolone at 1000 mg daily for 3 days. This was followed by initiation of a prednisone taper, beginning at 60 mg daily and reduced by 5 mg increments every 2 to 3 weeks. A loading course of intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) (Gamunex 10%) was administered at 2 g/kg (totaling 165 g) over 5 days (35 g per day), which was followed by an additional 5 cycles spaced 4 weeks apart, each cycle comprising a split total dose of 2 g/kg (165 g). The patient was started on mycophenolate mofetil 1 g twice daily and received two infusions of rituximab at 1000 mg intravenously, administered 2 weeks apart. Methotrexate was avoided because of its known association with interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis, especially considering the patient’s abnormal pulmonary function tests; this decision was also made after discussion with the patient regarding potential adverse effects. As mycophenolate was tolerated, neither azathioprine nor tacrolimus was initiated. Prophylaxis for Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia began with sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim, which was discontinued after the patient developed haemolytic anaemia (despite absence of glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency), after which nebulised pentamidine 300 mg every 4 weeks was well tolerated.

He was discharged after a 21-day inpatient stay. His oral medications were administered through nasogastric tube as he had persistent dysphagia at the time of discharge.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following a three-week period of inpatient care, the patient exhibited partial recovery in hip flexor strength and significant reduction of cervical soft tissue edema, though this swelling had not entirely subsided at discharge. The patient continued to experience dysphagia, requiring ongoing nasogastric feeding for an additional two months.

At a follow-up visit three weeks post-discharge, the patient’s proximal muscle strength was fully restored and serum creatine kinase measured 37 U/L, within normal limits. During an immunology assessment two months after leaving the hospital, cervical swelling had further diminished; examination revealed only residual violaceous erythema on the face and periungual abnormalities. The patient’s swallowing capabilities had improved enough to discontinue nasogastric feeds and to introduce a diet of soft solids. Therapy was maintained with mycophenolate mofetil, monthly intravenous immunoglobulin, and a prednisone taper.

At three months after discharge, the patient's proximal muscle strength remained unchanged, chest examination yielded normal results, and the cervical soft tissue edema had fully subsided. Prednisone was discontinued without incident seven months after discharge, by which point dysphagia had completely resolved. Follow-up pulmonary function testing indicated slight improvement, with a persistent restrictive pattern considered to represent baseline physiology (figure 5a, b). A chest CT performed at eleven months showed almost total resolution of previously noted basal ground-glass opacities and airspace consolidation, changes initially linked to aspiration. One year after diagnosis, respiratory function was stable and exercise tolerance was good.

In terms of cancer surveillance, he reported no constitutional symptoms during follow-up visits and will be monitored with symptom assessment, physical exams, routine blood work, and appropriate imaging including serial CT scans of the chest. Plans were made to perform gastroscopy and colonoscopy to rule out undetected gastrointestinal or colorectal malignancy.

It was decided to maintain IVIG therapy while gradually tapering mycophenolate over the next six months depending on his clinical status. The patient will continue under close surveillance to assess if adjustments to his therapeutic regimen are warranted.

Discussion:

We describe a patient with dermatomyositis positive for anti-SAE1 antibodies who presented with marked swelling of the cervical soft tissues, a finding that was initially attributed to idiopathic angioedema. Identification of dermatomyositis in the differential diagnosis, aided by meticulous physical examination that uncovered faint cutaneous manifestations, facilitated prompt recognition and management of the condition.

Anti-SAE1 antibodies are directed against the small ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) activating enzyme, a protein closely related to ubiquitin, which participates in the post-translational modification of numerous substrates through sumoylation. Dermatomyositis with SAE1 antibody positivity commonly exhibits characteristic skin findings such as heliotrope rash, Gottron’s papules, and the V-neck and shawl signs. According to a comprehensive review including 215 reported cases, dysphagia and interstitial lung disease were observed in 46% and 43% of individuals, respectively, while malignancy developed in 18% of cases.

Subcutaneous oedema, particularly involving the face or neck, is a rare manifestation of dermatomyositis. In a substantial cohort of 121 individuals diagnosed with dermatomyositis, only 8 patients (6.61%) exhibited angioedema. Furthermore, a literature survey spanning publications from 1985 to 2013 identified just 19 documented instances where dermatomyositis presented with oedema. It has been observed that the presence of antibodies such as anti-melanoma differentiation-associated gene (MDA)-5 and anti-transcriptional intermediary factor 1γ (TIF1-γ) correlates with a higher incidence of angioedema compared to other antibodies. While facial swelling has been reported in several cases, oedema involving the neck is less commonly noted. In our review of 10 reported cases of SAE1-positive dermatomyositis, only three referenced oedema, with a single case specifying swelling localized to the throat, suggesting that neck involvement is infrequent.79

This case highlights the importance for emergency physicians, ENT specialists, immunologists, and rheumatologists to consider dermatomyositis as a potential differential diagnosis in patients presenting with neck swelling, particularly when facing unexplained or idiopathic localized ‘angioedema.’ Dysphagia and additional manifestations can be mild or overlooked; therefore, it is essential to assess for physical features of dermatomyositis and obtain a creatine kinase measurement. In this patient, the observed subcutaneous edema is attributed to muscle inflammation associated with myositis, rather than to angioedema caused by histamine or bradykinin pathways.

Following the International Guideline for Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathy-Associated Cancer Screening from the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS), the patient was identified as high risk for malignancy based on two high-risk factors (dermatomyositis, dysphagia) and two intermediate-risk factors (positive SAE1 antibody, male gender). A malignancy workup was carried out, including computed tomography scans, tumour marker assessments, and a PET scan, along with planned upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopies. So far, detailed initial evaluation and ongoing monitoring have been reassuring.

Currently, there is a paucity of data regarding the optimal medical management of anti-SAE1–positive dermatomyositis. The most extensive cohort analysis reported thus far demonstrated diverse treatment regimens, with the majority of individuals receiving glucocorticoids alongside various steroid-sparing therapies—most frequently methotrexate, but also including mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, and azathioprine; intravenous immunoglobulin was administered in particular circumstances. Our patient received a combination of corticosteroids, mycophenolate mofetil, intravenous immunoglobulin, and rituximab, achieving disease remission. The decision to initiate mycophenolate mofetil was guided by the patient’s preference for an orally administered daily therapy, as well as its tolerability and established role in dermatomyositis treatment. Notably, this patient demonstrates a favorable prognosis compared to others presenting with pronounced tissue edema secondary to dermatomyositis.
