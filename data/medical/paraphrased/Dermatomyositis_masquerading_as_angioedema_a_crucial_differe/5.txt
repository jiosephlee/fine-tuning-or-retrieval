Title: Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook

Kevin Chan, Gemma Kwan, Hwei Choo Soh, Louise Evans

Abstract:

Summary: Dermatomyositis is an uncommon autoimmune condition that primarily targets the skin, musculature, and the respiratory tract. Rapid initiation of aggressive immunosuppressive therapy is often necessary to halt disease advancement and minimize the risk of damage to multiple organs. In this report, a previously healthy man in his 40s presented to the emergency department with pronounced cervical swelling. Initial assessment by the ear, nose, and throat team centered on a suspected deep neck space infection. However, when this diagnosis was excluded, he was subsequently referred to the clinical immunology service to evaluate possible idiopathic angioedema. Due to the unusual presentation of neck oedema—alongside changes noted in his skin and nails that did not conform to angioedema—a creatine kinase test was swiftly ordered. Based on clinical findings, a preliminary diagnosis of dermatomyositis was made, which was later confirmed by the detection of small ubiquitin-like modifier-1 activating enzyme (SAE1) autoantibody positivity and classic features on muscle biopsy. This case emphasizes an atypical manifestation of dermatomyositis, presenting as non-histamine and non-bradykinin-mediated neck swelling, accompanied by dysphagia and considerable muscle involvement that necessitated urgent therapeutic intervention.

Background:

Dermatomyositis is a form of idiopathic inflammatory myopathy that primarily affects the skin and musculature, but may also involve organs such as the gastrointestinal tract, joints, lungs, and heart. Its occurrence is estimated at 10 cases per million individuals. The disease exhibits a bimodal age distribution, most commonly presenting in children between 5 and 14 years of age, as well as in adults between 45 and 64 years. Hallmark dermatological manifestations include Gottron’s papules, the shawl sign, heliotrope rash, and periungual telangiectasias. Additional clinical features can encompass proximal muscle weakness, interstitial lung disease, and dysphagia. Pseudoangioedema is a rare manifestation in dermatomyositis, with one study reporting its presence in just 8 out of 121 cases. When the neck is involved, this symptom can mimic primary otolaryngological conditions, or be confused with histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema.

Case presentation:

A man in his 40s presented to the emergency department after experiencing three weeks of progressively worsening neck swelling and proximal muscle weakness, particularly affecting his hip flexors, along with mild muscle pain. His past medical history was unremarkable, and he had never smoked. He was also not taking any 3-hydroxy-3-methyl-glutaryl-coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase inhibitors. Ludwig’s angina was initially suspected, prompting a CT scan of his neck, which revealed generalized neck oedema affecting the strap muscles as well as inflammation extending into the retropharyngeal space (see figure 1). The patient was not febrile, maintained an oxygen saturation of 99% on room air, and did not display tachypnoea. Nailfold capillaroscopy identified proximal nailfold microhaemorrhages and avascular zones. Laboratory investigations at presentation revealed a white cell count of 9.1×10^9/L and a C-reactive protein (CRP) of 55.6 mg/L. As the neck swelling persisted without an identifiable cause, he was referred to a quaternary hospital with ear, nose and throat (ENT) surgical expertise for further assessment. ENT evaluation via flexible nasendoscopy found no noteworthy anatomical irregularities in the soft palate, vallecula, larynx, or post-cricoid region, but did observe increased secretions and episodes of coughing during the procedure.

The case was evaluated in consultation with the on-call immunologist due to a presumptive diagnosis of angioedema, as investigations failed to reveal any definitive anatomical or infectious etiology. Upon assessment, the patient demonstrated widespread facial erythema that extended into the nasolabial folds, and his neck appeared erythematous, edematous, and non-tender. He reported no usage of medications, including angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and denied any personal or familial history of angioedema. His presentation was not consistent with either acquired or hereditary forms of angioedema. Comprehensive examination identified discrete nailfold abnormalities indicative of periungual erythema, a cutaneous rash over the right lateral thigh, and diminished hip flexor strength (graded at 4/5). Neither Gottron’s papules nor a heliotrope rash were present. Creatine kinase measurement, ordered for further evaluation, revealed a markedly elevated level of 16 184 U/L the following day. Renal function testing yielded normal results, and urine myoglobin was negative. Further laboratory analysis showed transaminase elevations, with alanine aminotransferase (ALT) at 143 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) at 396 U/L, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) increased to 692 U/L.

Investigations:

To investigate potential etiologies for his spontaneous angioedema, an array of diagnostic evaluations was undertaken. Complement assays revealed that both C3 and C4 concentrations fell within standard parameters, as did C1 esterase activity and levels.

Autoimmune screening showed a positive anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) at a high titre of 1:1280 exhibiting a speckled fluorescence, while anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA) testing returned negative results. For further assessment, a myositis antibody profile, comprising 16 targets related to autoimmune inflammatory myopathies, was analyzed using the Euroimmun Euroline immunoassay. This revealed a pronounced positivity for antibodies against small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1), with a signal intensity measured at 173 (reference value <35), while all other myositis-associated antibodies—including anti-Mi-2 alpha (anti-Mi-2α), anti-Transcription Intermediary Factor-1 gamma (anti-TIF1-γ), anti–Histidyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-Jo-1), and anti–Isoleucyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-OJ)—were not detected. Serum immunoglobulin G (IgG), along with its subclasses, was found to be within normal limits.

Infectious serology as part of his immunosuppression evaluation demonstrated negativity for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), hepatitis B e antibody (HBeAb), hepatitis C core antibody (HBcAb), hepatitis C virus (HCV), HIV, varicella zoster virus (VZV) IgG, and cytomegalovirus (CMV) IgG/immunoglobulin M (IgM). Although QuantiFERON-TB Gold testing yielded an indeterminate result, three sputum specimens submitted for acid-fast bacilli microscopy to exclude Mycobacterium tuberculosis were negative.

Pulmonary function assessment indicated a restrictive pattern of extrathoracic origin, without a notable response to bronchodilator therapy. Chest CT imaging identified several non-cavitating nodules within the lungs, predominantly situated in the mid and lower zones (with the largest measuring 15 mm in the right lower lobe), along with irregular peribronchial consolidation suggestive of aspiration pneumonitis. There was no radiological indication of pulmonary fibrosis. Abdominal CT scanning did not reveal any abdomino-pelvic lymphadenopathy or signs of primary or metastatic malignancy within the abdomen or pelvis. A video fluoroscopic (barium) swallow examination demonstrated marked pharyngeal dysphagia, which resulted in significant residue in the pyriform fossae and post-swallow penetration of both thin and mildly thick liquids to the level of the vocal cords.

MRI imaging of the thighs demonstrated findings in line with ongoing myositis, particularly involving the bilateral obturator internus and externus muscles, with less pronounced inflammation detected in the hip adductors, bilateral gracilis, and the distal portions of both iliopsoas muscles (see figure 2). Additional evidence for active muscular inflammation came from fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)-positron emission tomography (PET) scanning, which revealed diffuse, abnormal FDG uptake across multiple muscle groups, with the most notable accumulation observed in the head and neck region, bilateral deltoids, forearms, chest wall, abdominal area, and pelvis (refer to figure 3). There was an absence of FDG-avid lesions elsewhere to raise suspicion for malignancy. Serum tumour markers, including carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), cancer antigen 19-9 (CA19-9), cancer antigen 125 (CA-125), and prostate-specific antigen (PSA), all returned within normal limits.

Histopathological examination of a thigh muscle biopsy identified clusters of necrotic, vacuolated, degenerated, myophagocytosed, and regenerating myofibers arranged in a perifascicular pattern, as well as patchy perimysial inflammation characterized by dispersed histiocytes (see figure 4). Immunohistochemical staining for the membrane attack complex revealed deposition of complement component 5b-9 (C5b-9) within perifascicular endomysial capillaries—a finding characteristic of dermatomyositis. The collective findings led to the diagnosis of moderate to severe dermatomyositis associated with SAE1 antibodies, accompanied by inflammatory edema, peribronchial consolidation due to micro-aspiration, dysphagia, and myositis. Cutaneous findings included a positive Holster sign and periungual erythema.

Differential diagnosis:

Due to the patient’s subacute onset of neck swelling and raised CRP, initial diagnostic considerations focused on Ludwig angina and idiopathic angioedema. At presentation, muscle weakness—dysphagia included—was not a significant feature. The differential diagnosis was broad, encompassing other forms of inflammatory myopathy (such as immune-mediated necrotising myopathy and inclusion body myositis), infectious etiologies (including cellulitis/erysipelas affecting the neck, deep neck space abscesses, and bacterial sialadenitis), disorders of the neuromuscular junction like myasthenia gravis, hypersensitivity reactions to drugs (notably, drug rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms), acute contact dermatitis, orofacial granulomatosis, hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis, as well as superior vena cava syndrome.

Treatment:

Given the substantial involvement of the airway musculature, the patient received intravenous methylprednisolone for 3 days at a daily dosage of 1000 mg. This was transitioned to a tapering course of prednisone beginning at 60 mg daily, with a gradual reduction of 5 mg every 2 to 3 weeks. A loading regimen of intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) (Gamunex 10%) was administered at 2 g/kg (a total of 165 g) over a 5-day period (35 g per day), followed by five additional cycles at 4-week intervals, each cycle delivering a cumulative dose of 2 g/kg (=165 g) divided across the treatments. Mycophenolate mofetil was started at 1 g twice daily, and the patient received two doses of rituximab (1000 mg intravenously, given two weeks apart). Methotrexate was avoided, given its established link to interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis, especially pertinent in light of the patient’s abnormal pulmonary function tests and following shared decision-making regarding its adverse effect profile. Azathioprine and tacrolimus were not selected, as mycophenolate mofetil was well-tolerated. For prophylaxis against Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, the patient initially received sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim; however, this was discontinued due to onset of haemolytic anaemia (in the absence of glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency). He subsequently tolerated monthly nebulised pentamidine (300 mg every 4 weeks).

After 21 days of hospitalization, the patient was discharged. Owing to persistent dysphagia, oral medications were administered via a nasogastric tube upon discharge.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following a 3-week period of inpatient care and therapeutic intervention, the patient exhibited moderate recovery in hip flexor strength and substantial reduction in cervical soft tissue swelling, though complete resolution of the swelling was not achieved by discharge. Ongoing dysphagia required continuation of nasogastric tube feeding for an additional two months.

At a follow-up visit three weeks post-discharge, full restoration of proximal muscle strength was noted, and serum creatine kinase levels had returned to 37 U/L. During an immunology assessment conducted two months after discharge, gradual resolution of cervical swelling was observed, with only minor residual violaceous erythema on the face and periungual changes remaining. Improvement in swallowing permitted removal of the nasogastric tube and transition to a diet of soft solids. The patient continued on mycophenolate mofetil, monthly intravenous immunoglobulin, and a tapering regimen of prednisone.

At 3 months after hospital discharge, the patient's proximal muscle strength had stabilized, his chest examination revealed no significant findings, and the cervical soft tissue swelling had fully subsided. Prednisone therapy was discontinued without complications 7 months after discharge, at which point his dysphagia had completely resolved. Pulmonary function testing at follow-up demonstrated slight improvement but still showed a residual restrictive pattern, likely representing his baseline status (figure 5a, b). At the 11-month mark, repeat CT imaging of the chest showed almost total resolution of the previously noted basal ground-glass opacities and airspace consolidation, which were initially thought to be related to aspiration. By 12 months after diagnosis, his respiratory function remained stable, and he maintained satisfactory exercise tolerance.

Regarding surveillance for malignancy, the patient reported no constitutional symptoms at follow-up appointments. Ongoing monitoring is planned, including clinical assessments, regular laboratory evaluations, and relevant imaging such as serial CT scans of the chest. Additional workup with gastroscopy and colonoscopy is scheduled to rule out any occult gastrointestinal or colorectal malignancy.

The management plan includes continued administration of IVIG and a gradual tapering of mycophenolate dosage over the next 6 months, contingent on his clinical response. He will remain under close observation to assess the need for potential adjustments to his medication regimen.

Discussion:

We describe a case involving dermatomyositis with anti-SAE1 antibodies that manifested as significant cervical soft tissue swelling, which was initially misdiagnosed as idiopathic angioedema. The inclusion of dermatomyositis in the differential diagnosis, along with a thorough physical assessment that identified discrete cutaneous manifestations, facilitated prompt identification and intervention.

Anti-SAE1 antibodies target the small-ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) activating enzyme, a protein that shares structural features with ubiquitin and plays a key role in the post-translational modification process known as sumoylation. Patients with SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis frequently exhibit distinct skin findings, including the heliotrope rash, Gottron’s papules, V-neck sign, and shawl sign. According to a comprehensive review of 215 reported cases, dysphagia and interstitial lung disease were observed in 46% and 43% of individuals, respectively, while 18% developed associated malignancies.

Oedema of the subcutaneous tissue—particularly presenting as swelling of the face or neck—is a rare clinical sign in dermatomyositis. In a sizable observational study comprising 121 individuals diagnosed with dermatomyositis, angioedema was documented in just 8 cases (6.61%). A comprehensive review of published reports spanning from 1985 to 2013 revealed a mere 19 cases where oedema was a feature of dermatomyositis. The presence of certain antibodies, specifically anti-melanoma differentiation-associated gene (MDA)-5 antibody and anti-transcriptional intermediary factor 1γ (TIF1-γ) antibody, was observed to correlate more frequently with angioedema compared to other autoantibodies. While several reports mention facial oedema, the occurrence of neck swelling appears considerably less common. Upon reviewing the literature concerning 10 documented cases of SAE1-positive dermatomyositis, oedema was noted in only three, with just a single case referring to swelling in the throat region, highlighting the rarity of neck involvement in this disease.79

This case underscores the importance for emergency physicians, ENT specialists, immunologists, and rheumatologists to consider dermatomyositis as a potential cause of neck swelling, particularly in instances of localized ‘angioedema’ without an identifiable etiology. As manifestations such as dysphagia and other systemic features can be understated, careful evaluation for clinical indicators of dermatomyositis and assessment of creatine kinase levels are essential steps. In this patient, the observed subcutaneous oedema was attributed to underlying muscle inflammation characteristic of myositis, distinct from the mechanisms typically seen in histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema.

Following the recommendations set forth by the International Guideline for Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathy-Associated Cancer Screening from the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS), the patient was categorized as high risk for malignancy due to the presence of two major risk factors (dermatomyositis, dysphagia) and two intermediate risk factors (SAE1 antibody positivity, male gender). Consequently, a thorough malignancy workup was conducted, including CT imaging, tumour marker evaluation, and a PET scan, with further plans for both upper and lower GI endoscopic examinations. So far, comprehensive baseline and follow-up surveillance has been consistently reassuring.

The evidence base for determining the most effective pharmacological treatment in anti-SAE1–positive dermatomyositis remains sparse. Within the largest reported cohort, therapeutic strategies varied considerably; the majority of individuals received glucocorticoids together with diverse steroid-sparing agents, most frequently methotrexate, though mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, and azathioprine were also utilized. Intravenous immunoglobulin was administered in select instances. In the present case, management included steroids, mycophenolate mofetil, intravenous immunoglobulin, and rituximab, resulting in disease remission. Mycophenolate mofetil was chosen in alignment with the patient's preference for an oral, daily regimen and due to its favorable tolerability profile and established efficacy in dermatomyositis. Compared to other individuals with dermatomyositis and substantial tissue oedema, the prognosis for our patient appears favorable.
