Title: Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook

Kevin Chan, Gemma Kwan, Hwei Choo Soh, Louise Evans

Abstract:

Summary: Dermatomyositis is an uncommon autoimmune condition primarily involving the skin, muscles, and respiratory tract. Management often necessitates early administration of potent immunosuppressive therapy to limit progression and avoid damage to multiple organs. A previously healthy man in his 40s presented to the emergency department with marked cervical swelling. Initially, ear, nose, and throat specialists evaluated him for a suspected deep neck space infection; however, this etiology was subsequently excluded. The patient was then referred to clinical immunology for further assessment and management of presumed idiopathic angioedema. Given the unusual presentation of neck oedema that did not align with typical angioedema, together with cutaneous and nail abnormalities, serum creatine kinase was measured without delay. A provisional diagnosis of dermatomyositis was made, which was later confirmed by detection of anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier-1 activating enzyme (SAE1) autoantibodies and characteristic findings on muscle biopsy. This report underscores an atypical manifestation of dermatomyositis involving neck swelling unrelated to histamine or bradykinin, presenting with dysphagia and pronounced myopathy, and requiring urgent intervention.

Background:

Dermatomyositis is a form of idiopathic inflammatory myopathy that primarily involves the skin and muscles, but may also impact organs such as the gastrointestinal tract, joints, lungs, and heart. Its incidence is approximately 10 per million individuals. The disorder exhibits a bimodal age distribution, occurring most frequently in children between 5 and 14 years old, and in adults from 45 to 64 years old. Classic dermatologic manifestations include Gottron’s papules, the shawl sign, heliotrope rash, and periungual telangiectasias. Additional clinical features encompass proximal muscle weakness, interstitial lung disease, and dysphagia. Pseudoangioedema is rare in patients with dermatomyositis; in one report, it was observed in only 8 out of 121 individuals diagnosed with the condition. When pseudoangioedema involves the cervical region, it may imitate an otolaryngologic disorder or be confused with angioedema mediated by histamine or bradykinin.

Case presentation:

A man in his 40s presented to the emergency department reporting a three-week history of gradually worsening neck swelling and weakness in the hip flexors, accompanied by mild muscle aches. His medical background was unremarkable and he had never smoked. He had not used any 3-hydroxy-3-methyl-glutaryl-coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase inhibitors. Ludwig’s angina was considered as the initial diagnosis, and a CT scan of his neck revealed widespread oedema affecting the strap muscles as well as inflammation that reached the retropharyngeal space (figure 1). He had no fever, was breathing normally with an oxygen saturation of 99% on room air, and was not tachypnoeic. Nailfold capillaroscopy demonstrated proximal microhaemorrhages and areas lacking vascularity. His white cell count measured 9.1×10^9/L and his C-reactive protein (CRP) was 55.6 mg/L upon arrival. Because the neck swelling persisted without an identified cause, he was moved to a quaternary care hospital offering ENT surgical services for further assessment. The ENT team performed a flexible nasendoscopy, which showed no substantial abnormalities in the soft palate, vallecula, larynx, or post-cricoid region; however, the procedure was notable for increased secretions and coughing.

The case was referred to the on-call immunologist due to concern for angioedema, since no definitive anatomical or infectious etiology was identified. On examination, the patient exhibited widespread facial erythema, including involvement of the nasolabial folds, as well as an erythematous, swollen, yet non-tender neck. He reported no use of medications such as angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and had neither personal nor family history of angioedema. Collectively, his presentation did not align with typical features of either acquired or hereditary angioedema. Comprehensive physical assessment uncovered subtle periungual erythema at the nailfolds, a rash over the right lateral thigh, and mildly reduced strength in hip flexion at 4/5. There was no evidence of Gottron’s papules or a heliotrope rash. Measurement of creatine kinase was undertaken, revealing a markedly increased value of 16,184 U/L the following day. Renal parameters remained within normal limits, and screening for urinary myoglobin was negative. Liver enzymes were also elevated, with alanine aminotransferase (ALT) at 143 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) at 396 U/L, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) at 692 U/L.

Investigations:

Due to the suspicion of angioedema, multiple investigations were conducted to identify potential etiologies of spontaneous angioedema. Both C3 and C4 complement levels were within normal limits, as were the C1 esterase functional assay and quantitative measurements.

The patient’s anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) test returned positive, with a titre of 1:1280 showing a speckled pattern. Anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA) testing was negative. A myositis antibody panel targeting 16 antigens associated with autoimmune inflammatory myopathies was carried out using the Euroimmun Euroline immunoassay. This panel revealed a strong positive result for anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibodies, with a signal intensity of 173 (normal range <35), while all other tested myositis antibodies were negative, including anti-Mi-2 alpha (anti-Mi-2α), anti–Transcription Intermediary Factor-1 gamma (anti-TIF1-γ), anti–Histidyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-Jo-1), and anti–Isoleucyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-OJ) antibodies. His total immunoglobulin G (IgG) and subclass levels were within normal parameters. Regarding immunosuppression screening, he tested negative for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), hepatitis C core antibody (HBcAb), hepatitis B e antibody (HBeAb), hepatitis C virus (HCV), HIV, varicella zoster virus (VZV) IgG, and cytomegalovirus (CMV) IgG and immunoglobulin M (IgM). The QuantiFERON-TB Gold test was indeterminate; nevertheless, three sputum samples were negative for acid-fast bacilli indicative of Mycobacterium tuberculosis.

Pulmonary function testing demonstrated an extrathoracic restrictive pattern, with no appreciable response to bronchodilators. Chest CT revealed numerous non-cavitating pulmonary nodules predominately located in the mid to lower zones, the largest measuring 15 mm within the right lower lobe, as well as patchy peribronchial consolidation indicative of aspiration pneumonitis. There was no radiographic evidence for pulmonary fibrosis. Abdominal CT did not detect any enlargement of abdomino-pelvic lymph nodes and showed no signs of primary or metastatic cancer in the abdominal or pelvic regions. A video fluoroscopic (barium) swallow evaluation identified marked pharyngeal dysphagia, resulting in substantial residue within the pyriform fossae and post-swallow penetration of thin and mildly thick fluids reaching the vocal cords.

MRI scans of the thighs demonstrated findings indicative of active myositis affecting both obturator internus and externus muscles, with less pronounced involvement seen in the hip adductors, bilateral gracilis muscles, and the distal segments of both iliopsoas muscles (see figure 2). Additional confirmation of muscle inflammation was obtained from a fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) positron emission tomography (PET) scan, which revealed widespread abnormal FDG uptake across numerous muscle groups, with the highest intensity in the head and neck regions, bilateral deltoids, forearms, chest wall, abdomen, and pelvis (see figure 3). No evidence of metabolically active lesions was identified to indicate malignancy. Laboratory evaluation of tumour markers—including carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), cancer antigen 19-9 (CA19-9), cancer antigen 125 (CA-125), and prostate-specific antigen (PSA)—returned within normal limits.

Histological examination of a thigh muscle biopsy identified clusters of necrotic, vacuolated, degenerative, myophagocytosed, and regenerating myofibres with a perifascicular distribution, along with scattered perimysial inflammation mainly composed of individual histiocytes (refer to figure 4). Immunohistochemical staining for membrane attack complex revealed deposition of complement component 5b-9 (C5b-9) within the perifascicular endomysial capillaries, which is characteristic of dermatomyositis. The overall findings led to a diagnosis of moderate to severe SAE1 antibody–positive dermatomyositis, complicated by inflammatory muscle edema, peribronchial consolidation due to micro-aspiration, dysphagia, and myositis. Dermatological signs observed included a positive Holster sign and periungual erythema.

Differential diagnosis:

Due to his relatively gradual onset of neck swelling accompanied by increased CRP, initial diagnostic considerations included Ludwig angina and idiopathic angioedema. Early symptoms lacked significant muscle weakness or dysphagia. The differential diagnosis further encompassed other types of inflammatory myopathies, such as immune-mediated necrotising myopathy and inclusion body myositis, infectious etiologies including cellulitis or erysipelas involving the neck, deep neck space infections, and bacterial sialadenitis. Additional possibilities considered were disorders affecting the neuromuscular junction like myasthenia gravis, delayed hypersensitivity reactions to medications such as drug rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms, acute contact dermatitis, orofacial granulomatosis, hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis, as well as superior vena cava syndrome.

Treatment:

Given the substantial involvement of airway musculature, the patient received intravenous methylprednisolone at 1000 mg daily for 3 days. This was followed by initiation of an oral prednisone taper, beginning at 60 mg daily and reduced by 5 mg every 2 to 3 weeks. An intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG, Gamunex 10%) loading dose of 2 g/kg (total 165 g) was administered over 5 days (35 g daily), and this was succeeded by five additional cycles given 4 weeks apart, totaling another 2 g/kg (165 g) distributed across these infusions. Mycophenolate mofetil was started at 1 g twice daily, and two doses of rituximab 1000 mg IV were administered 2 weeks apart. Methotrexate was not selected because of its known risk for interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis, particularly in the context of the patient’s abnormal lung function tests and following patient-provider discussion regarding adverse effects. Since mycophenolate was well-tolerated, neither azathioprine nor tacrolimus were initiated. Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia prophylaxis initially consisted of sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim, which was discontinued following the onset of haemolytic anaemia (in the absence of G6PD deficiency); he subsequently tolerated nebulized pentamidine at 300 mg every 4 weeks.

The patient was discharged following a 21-day hospitalization. Ongoing oral medications were provided via nasogastric tube due to continued dysphagia upon discharge.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following a three-week hospitalization period and ongoing therapy, the patient exhibited moderate improvement in hip flexor strength and substantial reduction of cervical soft tissue edema, although complete resolution of the swelling was not achieved before discharge. Dysphagia persisted, requiring continued use of a nasogastric tube for feeding over the subsequent two months.

At three weeks post-discharge, proximal muscle strength was fully restored and serum creatine kinase had normalized to 37 U/L. During immunology evaluation two months after discharge, further improvement of cervical swelling was noted, with residual violaceous facial erythema and periungual changes identified during physical examination. Swallowing ability had advanced to the point that the nasogastric tube could be withdrawn and the patient could transition to consuming soft solid foods. Maintenance therapy included mycophenolate mofetil, monthly intravenous immunoglobulin infusions, and a gradually decreasing regimen of prednisone.

Three months after hospital discharge, the patient's proximal muscle strength remained consistent, physical examination of the chest revealed no abnormalities, and the cervical soft tissue swelling had resolved entirely. Prednisone therapy was stopped successfully at seven months post-discharge, with complete resolution of dysphagia by that time. Pulmonary function testing performed at follow-up revealed slight improvement, though a residual restrictive pattern persisted and was believed to reflect his baseline condition (see figure 5a, b). A chest CT scan conducted eleven months later showed almost total resolution of prior basal ground-glass opacities and airspace consolidations, which were initially thought to be due to aspiration. At the twelve-month mark since diagnosis, respiratory function was stable, and he exhibited good tolerance to exercise.

In regard to malignancy surveillance, the patient reported no constitutional symptoms during follow-up and will continue ongoing observation with clinical assessment, regular laboratory investigations, and periodic imaging, including serial chest CT scans. He is scheduled for gastroscopy and colonoscopy to rule out hidden gastrointestinal or colorectal cancers.

The management plan includes ongoing IVIG therapy and a gradual reduction in mycophenolate dosage over the next six months, contingent upon clinical outcomes. He will be carefully followed to determine whether adjustments in his medication regimen are needed.

Discussion:

We present a case involving dermatomyositis with anti-SAE1 antibodies, manifesting primarily as significant cervical soft tissue edema, which was initially misdiagnosed as idiopathic angioedema. The diagnosis was facilitated by maintaining dermatomyositis in the differential and performing a meticulous physical examination that revealed subtle cutaneous indicators, enabling prompt identification and management.

Anti-SAE1 antibodies are directed against the small-ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) activating enzyme, a molecule homologous to ubiquitin that plays a role in the post-translational modification of various proteins via ‘sumoylation’. Patients with SAE1 antibody-associated dermatomyositis commonly exhibit skin findings such as a heliotrope rash, Gottron’s papules, the V-neck sign, and the shawl sign. A comprehensive review of 215 reported cases noted that dysphagia and interstitial lung disease were present in 46% and 43% of patients, respectively, whereas malignancy was found in 18%.

Edema of the subcutaneous tissue, notably affecting the face or neck, is rarely observed in patients with dermatomyositis. In a substantial cohort comprising 121 individuals diagnosed with dermatomyositis, angioedema was noted in just 8 cases (6.61%). Examination of the literature spanning 1985 to 2013 identified only 19 reported instances of dermatomyositis presenting with edema. Angioedema has been reported more frequently in association with antibodies such as anti-melanoma differentiation-associated gene (MDA)-5 and anti-transcriptional intermediary factor 1γ (TIF1-γ) compared to other antibodies. While facial edema has been documented in several cases, edema of the neck has been described less often. Our review of 10 published cases involving SAE1-positive dermatomyositis revealed that edema was reported in only three, with a single case referencing swelling in the throat area, underscoring the rarity of neck region involvement.79

This case highlights the importance for emergency physicians, ENT specialists, immunologists, and rheumatologists to consider dermatomyositis among the differential diagnoses for neck swelling, particularly when confronted with unexplained or idiopathic cases resembling localized angioedema. Given that symptoms such as dysphagia and other features can be subtle, it is essential to assess for physical indicators of dermatomyositis and to order a creatine kinase test. In this patient, subcutaneous edema was attributed to muscle inflammation associated with myositis, rather than to angioedema mediated by histamine or bradykinin.

Following the recommendations from the International Guideline for Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathy-Associated Cancer Screening developed by the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS), the patient was found to have two high risk factors (dermatomyositis, dysphagia) and two intermediate risk factors (SAE1 antibody positivity, male gender), categorizing him as high risk for malignancy. Accordingly, he underwent a malignancy workup that included computed tomography, tumor marker assays, and positron emission tomography, with plans for both upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopic evaluation. Thorough initial and follow-up surveillance have so far provided reassuring results.

The available data on the best pharmacological management of anti-SAE1–positive dermatomyositis remain sparse. The largest cohort reported so far demonstrated considerable variability in treatment strategies, with the majority of individuals receiving glucocorticoids alongside various steroid-sparing agents, predominantly methotrexate, but also including mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, and azathioprine; intravenous immunoglobulin was administered selectively. The patient described herein was managed with steroids, mycophenolate mofetil, intravenous immunoglobulin, and rituximab, resulting in remission. Mycophenolate mofetil was chosen because the patient preferred a daily oral medication and the drug’s tolerability and established role in dermatomyositis were considered. Compared to others experiencing pronounced tissue edema associated with dermatomyositis, this patient enjoys a favorable prognosis.
