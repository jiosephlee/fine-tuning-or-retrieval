Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook

Kevin Chan, Gemma Kwan, Hwei Choo Soh, Louise Evans

Abstract:

Summary: Dermatomyositis is an uncommon autoimmune disorder that predominantly involves the skin, musculature, and pulmonary systems. Early and aggressive immunosuppressive therapy is generally necessary to slow disease advancement and prevent involvement of multiple organs. This report describes a previously healthy man in his 40s who arrived at the emergency department with marked cervical swelling. Initially, ear, nose and throat specialists evaluated him, suspecting a deep neck space infection. When this etiology was excluded, the case was referred to clinical immunology for further assessment and management of potential idiopathic angioedema. Due to the atypical presentation of the cervical oedema, which did not align with classic angioedema, as well as concurrent dermatologic and nail abnormalities, a creatine kinase assay was immediately ordered. The patient received a tentative diagnosis of dermatomyositis, which was subsequently confirmed by the detection of small ubiquitin-like modifier-1 activating enzyme (SAE1) autoantibodies and a characteristic muscle biopsy. This case exemplifies an uncommon manifestation of dermatomyositis—cervical swelling unrelated to histamine or bradykinin mechanisms—accompanied by dysphagia and notable myopathy, all of which necessitated urgent immunosuppressive intervention.

Background:

Dermatomyositis is an inflammatory muscle disease of unknown cause that primarily targets both the skin and muscles, but may also extend to the gastrointestinal tract, joints, lungs, and heart. Its incidence is approximately 10 cases per million people. The disorder exhibits a bimodal age distribution, with incidence peaks observed in children between 5 and 14 years and adults aged 45 to 64 years. Hallmark dermatological manifestations include Gottron’s papules, shawl sign, heliotrope rash, and periungual telangiectasias. Additional clinical features may consist of proximal muscle weakness, interstitial lung disease, and swallowing difficulties. Pseudoangioedema is a rare manifestation in dermatomyositis, with one study reporting its presence in only 8 out of 121 cases. When pseudoangioedema affects the neck, it may imitate primary otolaryngological conditions, or be confused with angioedema related to histamine or bradykinin.

Case presentation:

A man in his 40s presented to the emergency department reporting a three-week progression of neck swelling and weakness in his hip flexors, also noting mild muscle pain. He had no notable past medical conditions and had never smoked. Additionally, he was not on any 3-hydroxy-3-methyl-glutaryl-coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase inhibitors. The working diagnosis upon presentation was Ludwig’s angina, prompting a CT scan of the neck, which revealed generalized edema of the neck affecting the strap muscles as well as inflammation extending into the retropharyngeal space (see figure 1). At presentation, he was afebrile, maintained 99% oxygen saturation on room air, and exhibited no tachypnea. Nailfold capillaroscopy revealed both proximal nailfold microhaemorrhages and areas devoid of vasculature. Laboratory investigations demonstrated a white blood cell count of 9.1×10$^9$/L and a C-reactive protein (CRP) level of 55.6 mg/L. Persistent unexplained neck swelling led to his referral to a quaternary hospital with specialized ear, nose and throat (ENT) surgical expertise for further workup. Examination via flexible nasendoscopy conducted by ENT identified no significant structural anomalies in the soft palate, vallecula, larynx, or post-cricoid region, though there was a notable presence of increased secretions and a tendency to cough during the procedure.

The case was referred to the on-call immunologist for evaluation of possible angioedema, as no definitive structural abnormality or infection was identified. Upon reassessment, widespread erythema was observed across the face, extending into the nasolabial folds, along with a swollen, red, but non-tender neck. The patient reported no current use of medications, including angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and there was no history of angioedema in himself or his family. Collectively, these features were uncharacteristic of both hereditary and acquired forms of angioedema. A comprehensive physical assessment revealed mild changes in the nailfolds consistent with periungual erythema, a localized rash on the right lateral thigh, and a reduction in hip flexor strength (4/5). Neither Gottron’s papules nor a heliotrope rash were detected. Creatine kinase testing was performed, yielding a markedly raised value of 16 184 U/L the following day. Renal function tests were within normal parameters, and a urine screen for myoglobin was negative. Additional findings included raised liver enzymes, with an alanine aminotransferase (ALT) of 143 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) of 396 U/L, and an increased lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) of 692 U/L.

Investigations:

Due to the initial suspicion of angioedema, an array of investigations was conducted to identify potential causes of spontaneous angioedema. Complement component C3 and C4 measurements were within normal parameters, and both the function and quantitative assessment of C1 esterase fell into the reference range.

Testing revealed a positive anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) at a titre of 1:1280, displaying a speckled immunofluorescence pattern. Testing for anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA) returned negative results. A comprehensive myositis antibody panel, targeting 16 specific antigens associated with autoimmune inflammatory myopathies, was administered using the Euroimmun Euroline immunoassay. This panel detected a pronounced positivity for anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibodies, with a signal intensity of 173 (with the reference cut-off being <35). No reactivity was observed for other myositis-related autoantibodies, including anti-Mi-2 alpha (anti-Mi-2α), anti-Transcription Intermediary Factor-1 gamma (anti-TIF1-γ), anti–Histidyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-Jo-1), or anti–Isoleucyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-OJ). Total immunoglobulin G (IgG) concentrations, along with its subclasses, were unremarkable.

Regarding evaluation for possible underlying immunosuppression, serological tests demonstrated negativity for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), hepatitis C core antibody (HBcAb), hepatitis B e antibody (HBeAb), hepatitis C virus (HCV), and HIV. Varicella zoster virus (VZV) IgG and cytomegalovirus (CMV) IgG/immunoglobulin M (IgM) were also negative. The QuantiFERON-TB Gold test yielded an indeterminate result, but three sputum samples for acid-fast bacilli targeting Mycobacterium tuberculosis all tested negative.

Pulmonary function testing demonstrated a restrictive pattern indicative of extrathoracic involvement, without appreciable improvement following bronchodilator administration. Chest CT imaging identified numerous non-cavitating pulmonary nodules predominantly located in the mid and lower lung fields, the largest of which was 15 mm in diameter within the right lower lobe. Additionally, there were irregular regions of peribronchial consolidation, suggestive of aspiration pneumonitis, while there were no CT signs of pulmonary fibrosis. Abdominal CT imaging showed no abdomino-pelvic lymphadenopathy and did not reveal any primary or metastatic malignancy within the abdominal or pelvic regions. The video fluoroscopic (barium) swallow assessment revealed pronounced pharyngeal dysphagia, resulting in significant pyriform sinus residue and post-deglutitive penetration of thin and mildly thick liquids reaching the vocal cords.

MRI scans of the thighs revealed imaging findings indicative of active myositis involving both the obturator internus and externus muscles, with less pronounced inflammatory changes seen in the hip adductor muscles, gracilis muscles bilaterally, and the distal portions of the iliopsoas muscles (figure 2). Additional evidence of active myositis was provided by fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)-positron emission tomography (PET), which demonstrated widespread abnormal FDG uptake across various muscle groups, with maximal accumulation observed in the head and neck regions, both deltoids, forearms, chest wall, abdomen, and pelvic muscles (figure 3). No other areas of pathological metabolic activity were present to indicate malignancy. Serum levels of tumour markers, including carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), cancer antigen 19-9 (CA19-9), cancer antigen 125 (CA-125), and prostate-specific antigen (PSA), fell within normal ranges.

Histopathological analysis of a thigh muscle biopsy identified clusters of moderately numerous necrotic, vacuolated, degenerating, myophagocytosed, and regenerating muscle fibers arranged in a perifascicular pattern, accompanied by dispersed perimysial inflammatory infiltrates composed predominantly of histiocytes (figure 4). Immunohistochemical staining for the membrane attack complex revealed deposition of complement component 5b-9 (C5b-9) within the perifascicular endomysial capillaries, consistent with characteristic features of dermatomyositis. On the basis of these findings, a diagnosis of moderate to severe dermatomyositis associated with SAE1 antibodies, along with inflammatory edema, peribronchial consolidation attributed to micro-aspiration, dysphagia, and myositis, was established. Dermatologic assessment demonstrated cutaneous involvement, evidenced by a positive Holster sign and periungual erythema.

Differential diagnosis:

Due to the patient's subacute onset of neck swelling and raised CRP levels, initial diagnostic considerations focused on Ludwig angina and idiopathic angioedema. Notably, muscle weakness—particularly dysphagia—did not figure prominently in his early symptoms. The differential diagnosis also encompassed various inflammatory myopathies (such as immune-mediated necrotising myopathy and inclusion body myositis), infectious etiologies (including cellulitis/erysipelas affecting the neck, deep neck space infections, and bacterial sialadenitis), as well as neuromuscular junction conditions like myasthenia gravis. Additional possibilities included a delayed hypersensitivity reaction to medication (such as drug rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms), acute contact dermatitis, orofacial granulomatosis, hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis, and superior vena cava syndrome.

Treatment:

Given the pronounced involvement of airway musculature, the patient received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dosage of 1000 mg per day for a duration of 3 days. Subsequently, a tapering course of prednisone was initiated, beginning at 60 mg daily and gradually decreased by 5 mg every 2 to 3 weeks. For immunomodulation, an initial intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) (Gamunex 10%) loading dose of 2 g/kg (totaling 165 g) was infused over 5 days (35 g per day), followed by 5 additional IVIG cycles spaced 4 weeks apart; these subsequent doses summed to 2 g/kg (165 g), divided over the five administrations. Mycophenolate mofetil was started at a dose of 1 g twice daily, and two intravenous rituximab infusions of 1000 mg each were administered 2 weeks apart. Methotrexate was omitted from the regimen due to its known risk for interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis—an important consideration given the patient’s abnormal lung function—and following discussion with the patient regarding potential adverse effects. Azathioprine and tacrolimus were not pursued because mycophenolate was effective and well-tolerated. For prophylaxis against Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim was initially prescribed but discontinued when the patient developed haemolytic anaemia (without evidence of glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency); subsequently, nebulised pentamidine at 300 mg every 4 weeks was tolerated.

Following a hospital admission lasting 21 days, the patient was discharged. Due to ongoing dysphagia, all oral medications were delivered via nasogastric tube at the time of discharge.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following a 3-week period of hospitalization and therapy, the patient exhibited noticeable improvement in cervical soft tissue swelling—though not yet entirely resolved—and moderate recovery in hip flexor strength. Despite these gains, dysphagia continued, making prolonged nasogastric tube feeding necessary for an additional 2 months.

By three weeks post-discharge, the patient’s proximal muscle strength had returned to normal, and serum creatine kinase measured at 37 U/L. At a follow-up immunology assessment two months after discharge, further resolution of cervical swelling was noted, with examination revealing only lingering violaceous erythema on the face and periungual abnormalities. The patient's ability to swallow improved to the extent that the nasogastric tube could be discontinued, and intake advanced to soft solids. Ongoing treatment included mycophenolate mofetil, monthly intravenous immunoglobulin, and a gradually decreasing dose of prednisone.

Three months after hospital discharge, the patient's proximal muscle strength remained unchanged, chest auscultation was normal, and cervical soft tissue edema had fully subsided. Prednisone therapy was tapered off entirely by seven months post-discharge, coinciding with complete resolution of dysphagia. Follow-up pulmonary function testing indicated slight improvement; however, a residual restrictive defect persisted, considered consistent with the patient’s baseline status (figure 5a, b). Chest CT obtained at eleven months revealed near-complete clearance of basal ground-glass opacities and airspace consolidation previously linked to aspiration. At the twelve-month mark after diagnosis, the patient’s respiratory status remained steady, accompanied by satisfactory exercise tolerance.

Regarding malignancy surveillance, the patient reported no constitutional symptoms at subsequent assessments and will remain under observation with periodic evaluations encompassing symptom review, physical examination, routine laboratory testing, and imaging—specifically, serial CT scans of the chest. In addition, a gastroscopy and colonoscopy are planned to rule out any occult gastrointestinal or colorectal neoplasia.

The therapeutic approach includes ongoing IVIG administration and a planned reduction of the mycophenolate dose over the next six months, conditional on clinical response. The patient will continue to undergo close follow-up to assess for potential adjustments to his pharmacological management.

Discussion:

We describe a case involving dermatomyositis with anti-SAE1 antibodies that manifested predominantly as marked cervical soft tissue edema, which was at first erroneously diagnosed as idiopathic angioedema. The inclusion of dermatomyositis in the differential diagnosis, coupled with a thorough physical examination that detected faint dermatological indicators, enabled prompt identification and initiation of therapy.

Anti-SAE1 antibodies are directed against the small-ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) activating enzyme, a molecule akin to ubiquitin that participates in the sumoylation process, a post-translational modification affecting numerous substrate proteins. Dermatomyositis associated with SAE1 antibodies is generally associated with skin manifestations, such as heliotrope rash, Gottron’s papules, V-neck sign, and shawl sign. According to an extensive review of 215 reported cases, dysphagia and interstitial lung disease were present in 46% and 43% of patients with SAE1-related dermatomyositis, respectively, and malignancy was identified in 18%.

Edema of the subcutaneous tissue, particularly presenting as facial or cervical swelling, is a rare clinical manifestation in dermatomyositis. For instance, among 121 individuals diagnosed with dermatomyositis in a substantial cohort study, only 8 (6.61%) exhibited angioedema. Furthermore, a comprehensive review of published literature spanning from 1985 to 2013 identified just 19 documented cases in which dermatomyositis was accompanied by oedema. It has been observed that antibodies like anti-melanoma differentiation-associated gene (MDA)-5 and anti-transcriptional intermediary factor 1γ (TIF1-γ) are more frequently linked with angioedema compared to other antibody types. While multiple reports have noted facial swelling, descriptions specifically of neck swelling are less common. In our review encompassing 10 reported cases of SAE1-positive dermatomyositis, oedema was noted in only three instances, with only one referencing swelling around the neck area, underscoring the rarity of cervical involvement.79

This case highlights for emergency physicians, ENT specialists, immunologists, and rheumatologists the importance of considering dermatomyositis in the differential diagnosis of neck swelling, particularly when confronted with unexplained or idiopathic localized ‘angioedema’. Given that manifestations such as dysphagia and other systemic involvement can be subtle, clinicians should be vigilant in searching for clinical indications of dermatomyositis and measuring creatine kinase levels as part of the initial evaluation. In this patient, the observed subcutaneous oedema resulted from muscle inflammation associated with myositis, rather than from angioedema triggered by histaminergic or bradykinin pathways.

Following the recommendations set forth in the International Guideline for Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathy-Associated Cancer Screening by the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS), the patient was deemed at high risk for malignancy due to the presence of two major risk factors (dermatomyositis and dysphagia) in addition to two intermediate risk factors (positivity for SAE1 antibody and male gender). Accordingly, an extensive malignancy workup was initiated, which included computed tomography imaging, tumour marker evaluation, and a PET scan, with plans for both upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopy. To date, thorough initial assessment and ongoing follow-up have provided continued reassurance.

There is a paucity of data to inform the most effective pharmacological management for anti-SAE1–positive dermatomyositis. The largest cohort study available reveals considerable variation in therapeutic strategies; most individuals were administered glucocorticoids in conjunction with various steroid-sparing medications, predominantly methotrexate, though mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, and azathioprine were also utilized. Select patients received intravenous immunoglobulin as part of their regimen. Our case involved administration of steroids, mycophenolate mofetil, intravenous immunoglobulin, and rituximab, leading to disease remission. The decision to use mycophenolate was influenced by the patient’s preference for an oral daily medication, along with its favorable side-effect profile and well-documented application in dermatomyositis. Compared to others experiencing pronounced tissue oedema due to dermatomyositis, our patient demonstrates a relatively favorable prognosis.
