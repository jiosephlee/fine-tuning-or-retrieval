Title: Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook

Kevin Chan, Gemma Kwan, Hwei Choo Soh, Louise Evans

Abstract:

Summary: Dermatomyositis is an uncommon autoimmune condition predominantly involving the skin, muscles, and the respiratory tract. Management generally necessitates early and aggressive immunosuppressive therapy to limit disease progression and prevent damage to multiple organs. A previously healthy man in his 40s presented to the emergency department with marked cervical swelling. Initially evaluated by ear, nose, and throat specialists for suspected deep neck space infection, he was subsequently found not to have such an infection. Clinical immunology was then consulted for assessment and management of presumed idiopathic angioedema. Given the unusual features of the neck edema—which did not align with classic angioedema—and concurrent skin and nail abnormalities, a creatine kinase test was rapidly obtained. Dermatomyositis was provisionally diagnosed, later supported by the presence of small ubiquitin-like modifier-1 activating enzyme (SAE1) autoantibodies and a characteristic muscle biopsy. This case underscores the atypical occurrence of neck swelling unrelated to histamine or bradykinin pathways, representing an uncommon presentation of dermatomyositis with dysphagia and marked muscle involvement that necessitated urgent intervention.

Background:

Dermatomyositis is an idiopathic inflammatory myopathy that predominantly involves the skin and musculature, although it may also impact the gastrointestinal system, joints, lungs, and heart. The disorder has an estimated incidence of 10 cases per million individuals. The disease exhibits a bimodal distribution, most frequently presenting in children between 5 and 14 years old and in adults from 45 to 64 years of age. Classic dermatological manifestations include Gottron’s papules, the shawl sign, heliotrope rash, and periungual telangiectasias. Additional clinical signs are proximal muscle weakness, interstitial lung disease, and dysphagia. Pseudoangioedema is a rare finding in dermatomyositis; in one study, only 8 of 121 patients with dermatomyositis exhibited this feature. When localized to the neck, pseudoangioedema may mimic a primary otolaryngologic condition or be misinterpreted as angioedema associated with either histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated mechanisms.

Case presentation:

A man in his 40s presented to the emergency department after three weeks of progressively worsening neck swelling and proximal muscle weakness affecting his hip flexors, along with mild muscle pain. He reported no noteworthy past medical conditions and had never smoked. There was no history of 3-hydroxy-3-methyl-glutaryl-coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase inhibitor use. Ludwig’s angina was initially suspected, prompting a neck CT scan, which revealed widespread neck edema involving the strap muscles, as well as inflammatory changes reaching into the retropharyngeal space (figure 1). On examination, he was afebrile, maintained an oxygen saturation of 99% on ambient air, and did not exhibit tachypnea. Nailfold capillaroscopy identified proximal nailfold microhemorrhages and areas lacking blood vessels. His laboratory results at presentation included a white blood cell count of 9.1×10^9/L and a C-reactive protein (CRP) level of 55.6 mg/L. Since the neck swelling persisted without an identifiable origin, he was referred to a quaternary care facility equipped with otolaryngology services for additional assessment. Flexible nasendoscopy by the ENT team showed no major anatomical abnormalities of the soft palate, vallecula, larynx, or post-cricoid region; however, there was a notable increase in secretions and he coughed during the procedure.

Consultation with the on-call immunologist was sought due to a suspicion of angioedema, as no definitive structural or infectious etiology was identified. Upon assessment, the patient exhibited diffuse erythema across the face, including the nasolabial folds, as well as an erythematous, swollen, non-tender neck. He reported not taking any medications, such as angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and there was no history of angioedema in either himself or his family. Taken together, his presentation did not align with typical features of either acquired or hereditary angioedema. Comprehensive examination detected mild periungual erythema, a rash localized to the right lateral thigh, and diminished strength in hip flexion (power graded at 4/5). There were no findings of Gottron’s papules or heliotrope rash. Serum creatine kinase was measured and returned markedly elevated at 16,184 U/L the following day. Renal parameters remained within normal limits, and urine myoglobin testing was negative. Additionally, laboratory studies revealed raised transaminase levels, with an alanine aminotransferase (ALT) of 143 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) of 396 U/L, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) elevated at 692 U/L.

Investigations:

Due to the suspicion of angioedema, multiple investigations were conducted to identify possible underlying causes of spontaneous angioedema. Levels of C3 and C4 complement were found to be within the normal range, as were C1 esterase inhibitor quantitative and functional measurements.

Testing revealed a positive anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) at a titre of 1:1280, displaying a speckled pattern, while anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA) was negative. A comprehensive myositis antibody panel, covering 16 antigens associated with autoimmune inflammatory myopathies, was administered using the Euroimmun Euroline immunoassay. This panel demonstrated a strong positivity for anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibodies, with a signal intensity of 173 (reference interval <35). The patient did not exhibit other myositis-specific antibodies, including anti-Mi-2 alpha, anti-TIF1-γ, anti-Jo-1, or anti-OJ antibodies. Total immunoglobulin G (IgG) and its subclasses were unremarkable.

Regarding screening for immunosuppression, the results showed negativity for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), hepatitis C core antibody (HBcAb), hepatitis B e antibody (HBeAb), hepatitis C virus (HCV), HIV, varicella zoster virus (VZV) IgG, and cytomegalovirus (CMV) IgG and IgM. The QuantiFERON-TB Gold result was indeterminate. However, three sputum specimens were negative for acid-fast bacilli, ruling out Mycobacterium tuberculosis.

Pulmonary function testing demonstrated an extrathoracic restrictive pattern without appreciable improvement following bronchodilator administration. Chest CT identified several non-cavitating nodules predominantly in the mid to lower lung zones, with the largest nodule (15 mm) in the right lower lobe, along with patchy peribronchial consolidation consistent with aspiration pneumonitis; there was no evidence of pulmonary fibrosis on imaging. Abdominal CT did not reveal any abdominopelvic lymphadenopathy or signs suggestive of primary or metastatic malignancy within the abdomen or pelvis. A video fluoroscopic (barium) swallow assessment detected profound pharyngeal dysphagia, resulting in significant residue within the pyriform fossae and post-swallow penetration of both thin and mildly thick liquids into the area of the vocal cords.

MRI of the thighs revealed findings indicative of active myositis affecting the bilateral obturator internus and externus muscles, with less pronounced involvement in the hip adductors, gracilis muscles bilaterally, and the distal portions of the iliopsoas muscles on both sides (figure 2). Evidence of ongoing muscle inflammation was also demonstrated by a fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)-positron emission tomography (PET) scan, which showed widespread, abnormal uptake of FDG within multiple muscle groups, most prominently involving the head and neck, bilateral deltoids, forearms, chest wall, abdomen, and pelvis (figure 3). There was no FDG-avid lesion detected elsewhere that would suggest the presence of a malignancy. Tumor markers, including carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), cancer antigen 19-9 (CA19-9), cancer antigen 125 (CA-125), and prostate-specific antigen (PSA), were all within normal reference ranges.

A biopsy taken from thigh muscle demonstrated areas with a moderate number of necrotic, vacuolated, degenerate, myophagocytosed, and regenerating myofibers arranged in a perifascicular pattern, along with scattered perimysial inflammation comprising dispersed histiocytes (figure 4). Immunohistochemical analysis for membrane attack complex revealed deposition of complement component 5b-9 (C5b-9) within perifascicular endomysial capillaries, which is characteristic of dermatomyositis. The patient was diagnosed with moderate to severe SAE1 antibody positive dermatomyositis, presenting with inflammatory edema, peribronchial consolidation attributed to micro-aspiration, dysphagia, and myositis. Cutaneous findings included a positive Holster sign and periungual erythema.

Differential diagnosis:

Due to the gradual onset of neck swelling accompanied by an elevated CRP, there was initial suspicion for Ludwig angina or idiopathic angioedema. Early in the course, muscle weakness and dysphagia were not significant findings. The differential diagnosis included other inflammatory myopathies such as immune-mediated necrotising myopathy and inclusion body myositis, infectious etiologies like cellulitis or erysipelas of the neck, deep neck space infections, and bacterial sialadenitis. Additional considerations were neuromuscular junction conditions including myasthenia gravis, drug reactions such as delayed hypersensitivity (for example, drug rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms), acute contact dermatitis, orofacial granulomatosis, hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis, and superior vena cava syndrome.

Treatment:

Because of extensive involvement of airway musculature, the patient received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for three days. Following this, he transitioned to a prednisone regimen beginning at 60 mg daily, with gradual reduction by 5 mg every 2 to 3 weeks. An initial dose of intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) (Gamunex 10%) at 2 g/kg (equivalent to a total of 165 g) was administered over a five-day period (35 g daily). This was followed by five additional IVIG cycles, administered at four-week intervals, totaling 2 g/kg (165 g) split among those infusions. The patient began therapy with mycophenolate mofetil at a dose of 1 g twice daily and received two doses of intravenous rituximab at 1000 mg, spaced two weeks apart. Methotrexate was avoided given its known association with interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis—considering the patient’s abnormal pulmonary function tests—and after thorough discussion with the patient about possible adverse effects. Since mycophenolate was well-tolerated, neither azathioprine nor tacrolimus was added. For prevention of Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim was started but subsequently discontinued when the patient developed haemolytic anaemia, despite no evidence of glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency. He later tolerated nebulized pentamidine at 300 mg every four weeks.

He was discharged after a 21-day inpatient stay. His oral medications were administered through nasogastric tube as he had persistent dysphagia at the time of discharge.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following three weeks of inpatient care and management, the patient showed moderate resolution of hip flexor weakness and significant reduction in cervical soft tissue edema, though this swelling had not yet completely subsided at discharge. Dysphagia was ongoing, requiring continued feeding via nasogastric tube for an additional two months.

At a follow-up appointment three weeks after discharge, proximal muscle strength had completely normalized and serum creatine kinase was measured at 37 U/L. At an immunology evaluation two months post-discharge, cervical swelling exhibited continued improvement, with examination revealing only lingering violaceous erythema on the face and periungual changes. The patient's swallowing ability had progressed enough to discontinue nasogastric tube feeds and initiate soft solid foods. The ongoing treatment regimen included mycophenolate mofetil, monthly intravenous immunoglobulin infusions, and a gradually tapering dose of prednisone.

At three months after discharge, proximal muscle strength remained stable, chest exam findings were normal, and the cervical soft tissue swelling had entirely subsided. Prednisone was weaned off successfully seven months after discharge, at which time his dysphagia had completely resolved. Follow-up pulmonary function testing demonstrated a slight improvement, with ongoing restrictive changes believed to represent his baseline physiology (see figure 5a, b). A repeat CT scan of the chest at 11 months revealed near-total resolution of the previously seen basal ground-glass opacities and areas of airspace consolidation, which were originally considered secondary to aspiration. At the one-year mark post-diagnosis, his respiratory status was stable and he maintained good tolerance for exercise.

Regarding malignancy surveillance, he reported no constitutional symptoms at follow-up visits, and surveillance will continue using symptom assessment, physical examination, periodic blood work, and serial CT imaging of the chest as appropriate. He is also scheduled for both gastroscopy and colonoscopy to rule out hidden gastrointestinal or colorectal cancers.

The management plan includes ongoing IVIG treatment, with a gradual reduction of the mycophenolate dose over the next six months depending on his clinical status. He will be closely followed to evaluate whether any adjustment to his medication regimen becomes necessary.

Discussion:

We present a case involving dermatomyositis with anti-SAE1 antibodies that manifested as pronounced soft tissue swelling of the neck, which was initially misdiagnosed as idiopathic angioedema. Considering dermatomyositis in the differential diagnosis, along with meticulous physical assessment that uncovered faint dermatological findings, enabled accurate and prompt diagnosis and initiation of therapy.

Anti-SAE1 antibodies are directed against the small-ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) activating enzyme, a protein closely related in structure to ubiquitin and essential for ‘sumoylation’—a post-translational modification process involving various target proteins. Dermatomyositis positive for SAE1 antibodies commonly presents with dermatologic features such as heliotrope rash, Gottron’s papules, and the V-neck and shawl signs. According to a comprehensive literature review including 215 reported cases, dysphagia and interstitial lung disease were noted in 46% and 43% of SAE1-associated dermatomyositis patients, respectively, and malignancy was documented in 18% of cases.

Oedema of the subcutaneous tissue, notably swelling of the face or neck, occurs infrequently in dermatomyositis. Within a substantial cohort study including 121 individuals diagnosed with dermatomyositis, angioedema was documented in just 8 cases (6.61%). A review of literature covering publications from 1985 to 2013 identified merely 19 reported instances in which dermatomyositis presented with oedema. Certain autoantibodies, namely anti-melanoma differentiation-associated gene (MDA)-5 antibody and anti-transcriptional intermediary factor 1γ (TIF1-γ) antibody, were observed to have a higher association with angioedema relative to other antibodies. While facial oedema has been described in several reported cases, specific mention of neck oedema is more rare. In our review of 10 documented cases of SAE1-positive dermatomyositis, oedema was noted in only three, with just one case detailing swelling involving the throat area, indicating that neck involvement is seldom seen.79

This case highlights for emergency physicians, ENT specialists, immunologists, and rheumatologists that dermatomyositis should be considered as a differential diagnosis in patients presenting with neck swelling, particularly in cases of localized ‘angioedema’ without an evident cause. Clinical features such as dysphagia and other manifestations may be minimal, underscoring the importance of a thorough physical examination for signs indicative of dermatomyositis and measurement of creatine kinase levels. In this patient, subcutaneous edema resulted from inflammatory myopathy rather than from angioedema due to histamine or bradykinin pathways.

Following the International Guideline for Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathy-Associated Cancer Screening from the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS), the patient possessed two high-risk features (dermatomyositis, dysphagia) as well as two intermediate risk characteristics (positive SAE1 antibody, male gender), categorizing him as high risk for malignancy. The malignancy workup included CT imaging, tumor marker assays, and a PET scan, with additional upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopies planned. Both comprehensive initial and follow-up surveillance have, so far, provided reassuring findings.

Current evidence on the optimal pharmacological management of anti-SAE1–positive dermatomyositis is sparse. In the most extensive series available, therapeutic strategies varied, though the majority of individuals received glucocorticoids alongside various steroid-sparing agents, most frequently methotrexate, as well as mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, and azathioprine; intravenous immunoglobulin was administered in selected instances. Our patient’s regimen included corticosteroids, mycophenolate mofetil, intravenous immunoglobulin, and rituximab, leading to disease remission. Mycophenolate was chosen due to the patient's preference for an oral daily medication, its favorable tolerability profile, and established role in treating dermatomyositis. Compared to other cases marked by marked tissue edema associated with dermatomyositis, our patient has fortunately demonstrated a favorable prognosis.
