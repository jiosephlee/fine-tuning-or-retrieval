Title: Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook

Kevin Chan, Gemma Kwan, Hwei Choo Soh, Louise Evans

Abstract:

Summary: Dermatomyositis is an uncommon autoimmune disorder that most often involves the skin, skeletal muscle, and respiratory tract. Management of dermatomyositis generally necessitates early initiation of potent immunosuppressive therapy to minimize progression and prevent damage across multiple organ systems. A male patient in his 40s with no previous medical issues initially attended the emergency department complaining of pronounced cervical swelling. Ear, nose, and throat specialists initially evaluated him for a potential deep neck space infection. After this etiology was excluded, he was referred to clinical immunology for further evaluation and management of presumed idiopathic angioedema. Due to the unusual characteristics of his neck edema—which did not fit with angioedema—and in light of concurrent dermatologic and nail findings, a serum creatine kinase test was immediately ordered. The patient was provisionally diagnosed with dermatomyositis; subsequent investigations revealed positivity for small ubiquitin-like modifier-1 activating enzyme (SAE1) autoantibody and confirmed myopathy on muscle biopsy. This case underscores an atypical presentation of dermatomyositis—neck swelling not mediated by histamine or bradykinin—associated with both dysphagia and significant muscular involvement that necessitated rapid initiation of therapy.

Background:

Dermatomyositis is an idiopathic inflammatory condition primarily targeting the skin and skeletal muscles, but it may also involve the gastrointestinal system, joints, pulmonary system, and cardiac tissue. Its estimated occurrence is 10 cases per million individuals. The disease exhibits a bimodal age distribution, commonly presenting in children between 5 and 14 years and in adults from 45 to 64 years of age. Notable skin manifestations include Gottron’s papules, heliotrope rash, shawl sign, and periungual telangiectasias. Additional clinical features may include weakness of proximal muscles, interstitial lung involvement, and difficulties with swallowing. Pseudoangioedema is a rare presentation in dermatomyositis, as reported in one study where only 8 out of 121 individuals with the disease exhibited this finding. When pseudoangioedema involves the neck, it may be misidentified as a primary disorder of the otolaryngologic system or confused with angioedema related to histamine or bradykinin.

Case presentation:

A man in his 40s came to the emergency department reporting a three-week progression of neck swelling and weakness in his hip flexor muscles, also experiencing mild muscle aches. He had no relevant prior medical conditions and had never smoked. He had not used any 3-hydroxy-3-methyl-glutaryl-coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase inhibitors. Ludwig’s angina was initially suspected. A neck CT scan revealed widespread edema of the neck, particularly affecting the strap muscles, with inflammatory changes spreading to the retropharyngeal space (figure 1). On assessment, he had no fever, maintained 99% oxygen saturation on room air, and was not breathing rapidly. Nailfold capillaroscopy identified proximal nailfold microhemorrhages and avascular areas. Laboratory investigations showed a white cell count of 9.1×10^9/L and a C-reactive protein (CRP) of 55.6 mg/L upon presentation. With unresolved swelling and no clear etiology, the patient was referred to a quaternary care center with ear, nose, and throat (ENT) surgical facilities for further assessment. ENT specialists performed a flexible nasendoscopy that found no significant structural abnormalities in the soft palate, vallecula, larynx, or post-cricoid region; however, the procedure did elicit increased secretions and coughing.

The on-call immunologist was consulted regarding suspected angioedema, as neither structural nor infectious etiologies were identified. Upon assessment, the patient exhibited widespread facial erythema, which extended across the nasolabial folds, as well as a neck that appeared erythematous, swollen, and non-tender. He reported not taking any medications, including angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and there was no history of angioedema personally or within his family. Collectively, his presentation did not align with typical acquired or hereditary angioedema. Detailed examination uncovered subtle nailfold abnormalities indicating periungual erythema, a rash localized to the right lateral thigh, and diminished hip flexor strength rated at 4/5. There were no Gottron’s papules or heliotrope rash. Measurement of creatine kinase was ordered and revealed a markedly elevated value of 16,184 U/L the following day. Renal parameters were within normal limits, and a urine myoglobin test was negative. Additionally, he exhibited increased transaminase levels, with ALT measured at 143 U/L, AST at 396 U/L, and LDH elevated to 692 U/L.

Investigations:

Due to the initial suspicion of angioedema, a series of investigations was undertaken to identify possible etiologies for spontaneous angioedema. Both C3 and C4 complement levels were found to be within normal limits, as were C1 esterase functional activity and quantitative values.

Testing revealed a positive anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) with a titre of 1:1280 exhibiting a speckled pattern. The anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA) test was negative. A myositis antibody panel, targeting 16 antigens associated with autoimmune inflammatory myopathies, was performed using the Euroimmun Euroline immunoassay. This assay demonstrated a strong positive result for anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibodies, with a signal intensity of 173 (reference range <35). Other myositis antibodies, such as anti-Mi-2 alpha (anti-Mi-2α), anti-Transcription Intermediary Factor-1 gamma (anti-TIF1-γ), anti–Histidyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-Jo-1), and anti–Isoleucyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-OJ), were not detected. Immunoglobulin G (IgG) levels, including subclass assessment, were also within normal parameters. 

For screening related to immunosuppression, results included negative hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), hepatitis C core antibody (HBcAb), hepatitis B e antibody (HBeAb), hepatitis C virus (HCV), HIV, varicella zoster virus (VZV) IgG, and cytomegalovirus (CMV) IgG/immunoglobulin M (IgM). The QuantiFERON-TB Gold test yielded an indeterminate result; however, testing for acid-fast bacilli for Mycobacterium tuberculosis on three sputum specimens was negative.

Pulmonary function testing demonstrated a restrictive defect of extrathoracic origin, without notable improvement following bronchodilator administration. Chest CT imaging identified numerous non-cavitating nodules within the lungs, predominantly localized to the mid and lower zones, with the largest nodule measuring 15 mm in diameter within the right lower lobe. Additionally, the scan revealed patchy peribronchial consolidation, likely attributable to aspiration pneumonitis, and there was no radiologic indication of pulmonary fibrosis. Abdominal CT found no abdomino-pelvic lymphadenopathy and did not show signs of primary or metastatic malignancy within the abdomen or pelvis. A video fluoroscopic barium swallow examination demonstrated marked pharyngeal dysphagia, resulting in significant pyriform fossa residue and post-swallow penetration of thin and mildly thick liquids reaching the vocal cords.

Thigh MRI revealed findings indicative of active myositis within the bilateral obturator internus and externus muscles, with less pronounced involvement observed in the hip adductor muscles, bilateral gracilis, and distal portions of the iliopsoas (see figure 2). Evidence for ongoing muscle inflammation was corroborated by fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)-positron emission tomography (PET), which demonstrated widespread and abnormal FDG uptake across numerous muscle groups, most notably in the head and neck region, both deltoids, forearms, chest wall, abdomen, and pelvic areas (figure 3). No areas of metabolically active tissue were identified outside these regions to suggest an underlying malignancy. Evaluation of tumour markers, including carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), cancer antigen 19-9 (CA19-9), cancer antigen 125 (CA-125), and prostate-specific antigen (PSA), yielded results within normal limits.

Biopsy specimens taken from the thigh muscle identified focal regions containing a moderate quantity of necrotic, vacuolated, degenerative, myophagocytosed, and regenerating myofibres localized in a perifascicular pattern, along with scattered perimysial infiltration composed predominantly of dispersed histiocytes (figure 4). Immunohistochemical staining for membrane attack complex demonstrated the presence of complement component 5b-9 (C5b-9) within perifascicular endomysial capillaries, which is characteristic of dermatomyositis. Based on these findings, a diagnosis of moderate to severe dermatomyositis with SAE1 antibody positivity was established, accompanied by inflammatory swelling, peribronchial consolidation attributed to micro-aspiration, dysphagia, and myositis. Cutaneous findings included a positive Holster sign and periungual erythema.

Differential diagnosis:

Due to the gradual onset of neck swelling accompanied by a raised CRP, Ludwig angina and idiopathic angioedema were initially considered. The patient's initial symptoms did not prominently feature muscle weakness or dysphagia. The differential diagnosis included inflammatory myopathies such as immune-mediated necrotising myopathy and inclusion body myositis; infectious etiologies like cellulitis or erysipelas of the neck, deep neck space infection, and bacterial sialadenitis; neuromuscular junction diseases, notably myasthenia gravis; delayed drug hypersensitivity reactions (such as drug rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms); acute contact dermatitis; orofacial granulomatosis; hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis; and superior vena cava syndrome.

Treatment:

Given the patient's marked airway muscle involvement, intravenous methylprednisolone was administered for 3 days at a daily dose of 1000 mg. Following this, he transitioned to an oral prednisone taper beginning at 60 mg per day, decreasing by 5 mg every 2 to 3 weeks. Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG; Gamunex 10%) was initiated with a loading dose of 2 g/kg (totaling 165 g) spread over 5 days (35 g per day), with five subsequent cycles administered at 4-week intervals at a cumulative dose of 2 g/kg (165 g), divided among these infusions. Mycophenolate mofetil was started at 1 g twice daily, and the patient received two 1000 mg intravenous doses of rituximab, spaced two weeks apart. Methotrexate was avoided owing to its association with interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis, given the patient’s abnormal pulmonary function tests and after discussion regarding adverse effects. Azathioprine and tacrolimus were not prescribed, as mycophenolate was well tolerated. For prophylaxis against Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim was initiated but discontinued when haemolytic anaemia developed (despite normal G6PD levels); subsequently, nebulized pentamidine 300 mg every 4 weeks was well tolerated.

The patient was discharged after a 21-day hospital admission. Due to persistent dysphagia at discharge, oral medications were provided via a nasogastric tube.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following three weeks of inpatient care, the patient showed moderate recovery in hip flexion strength and significant reduction in cervical soft tissue edema, although complete resolution of the swelling had not occurred by the time of discharge. Dysphagia remained problematic, requiring ongoing nutritional support via nasogastric tube for an additional two months.

At a follow-up three weeks post-discharge, the patient’s proximal muscle power was fully restored, and serum creatine kinase levels had returned to 37 U/L. During immunology assessment two months after discharge, there was continued regression of cervical swelling; examination revealed only persistent violaceous facial rash and periungual findings. Improvement in swallowing permitted withdrawal of the nasogastric tube and advancement to a diet of soft foods. The therapeutic regimen consisted of continued mycophenolate mofetil, monthly intravenous immunoglobulin, and a gradually reducing dose of prednisone.

At three months after discharge, the patient's proximal muscle strength remained unchanged, chest examination was normal, and cervical soft tissue swelling had fully subsided. Prednisone was discontinued successfully seven months after discharge, at which point dysphagia had completely resolved. Subsequent pulmonary function testing showed mild improvement, though a persistent restrictive pattern was thought to represent his baseline physiology (see figure 5a, b). Follow-up chest CT at eleven months revealed almost complete resolution of the previously noted basal ground-glass opacities and airspace consolidation, which were initially believed to be due to aspiration. Twelve months after diagnosis, the patient's respiratory function continued to be stable, and he maintained good exercise tolerance.

In terms of cancer surveillance, the patient reported no constitutional symptoms during follow-up and will be observed with ongoing symptom assessment, physical examinations, regular blood work, and appropriate imaging, including serial CT scans of the chest. He is scheduled to undergo gastroscopy and colonoscopy to rule out hidden gastrointestinal or colorectal malignancies.

The management plan includes ongoing IVIG therapy and gradual reduction of the mycophenolate dose over the next six months, contingent upon his clinical course. Close monitoring will be maintained to evaluate the necessity for further modifications to his medication regimen.

Discussion:

We present the case of dermatomyositis with anti-SAE1 antibody positivity, which manifested as significant cervical soft tissue edema and was initially diagnosed as idiopathic angioedema. Identifying dermatomyositis as a possible alternative diagnosis, together with thorough examination that detected subtle skin findings, enabled prompt identification and management.

Anti-SAE1 antibodies are directed against the small-ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) activating enzyme, a molecule that bears structural resemblance to ubiquitin and is responsible for the ‘sumoylation’—post-translational modification—of multiple substrate proteins. Dermatomyositis associated with SAE1 antibodies commonly displays cutaneous manifestations including heliotrope rash, Gottron’s papules, V-neck sign, and shawl sign. According to a comprehensive literature review of 215 patients, 46% had dysphagia and 43% exhibited interstitial lung disease, while malignancy was observed in 18% of cases of SAE1 dermatomyositis.

Swelling of the subcutaneous tissue, particularly involving the face or neck, is rarely seen in dermatomyositis. Among a group of 121 dermatomyositis patients, only 8 individuals (6.61%) experienced angioedema. Additionally, an analysis of published reports from 1985 through 2013 identified just 19 cases where dermatomyositis presented with oedema. Certain antibodies, including anti-melanoma differentiation-associated gene (MDA)-5 and anti-transcriptional intermediary factor 1γ (TIF1-γ), appeared to correlate more frequently with angioedema compared to other autoantibodies. While facial swelling has been noted in multiple cases, swelling specifically affecting the neck was reported less often. In reviewing 10 cases of SAE1-positive dermatomyositis, only three referenced oedema, with a single case documenting swelling in the throat area, suggesting that neck involvement remains uncommon.79

This case highlights the importance for emergency physicians, ENT specialists, immunologists, and rheumatologists to consider dermatomyositis as a potential differential diagnosis in patients presenting with neck swelling, particularly when faced with unexplained or idiopathic localized "angioedema." Because manifestations such as dysphagia or other systemic features may be subtle, it is essential to assess for physical findings associated with dermatomyositis and to obtain a creatine kinase measurement. The subcutaneous oedema observed in our patient was due to muscle inflammation from myositis, not from mechanisms involving histaminergic or bradykinin-induced angioedema.

Following the International Guideline for Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathy-Associated Cancer Screening provided by the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS), the patient was identified as having two high-risk features (dermatomyositis and dysphagia) and two intermediate-risk factors (positive for SAE1 antibody and male gender), thus categorizing him as high risk for malignancy. He underwent an evaluation for malignancy that included CT imaging, tumor marker assays, and a PET scan, with plans for both upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopy. Initial and subsequent follow-up surveillance has so far yielded reassuring results.

Evidence regarding the best pharmacologic management for anti-SAE1–positive dermatomyositis remains scarce. The largest existing cohort demonstrates a variety of therapeutic strategies, with most individuals receiving glucocorticoids along with several steroid-sparing medications, most frequently methotrexate, but also mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, and azathioprine; intravenous immunoglobulin was administered in selected circumstances. In this case, the patient was given steroids, mycophenolate mofetil, intravenous immunoglobulin, and rituximab, resulting in remission. The choice of mycophenolate mofetil reflected the patient’s preference for a daily oral therapy and its recognized tolerability and use in dermatomyositis. Encouragingly, the patient's prognosis is favorable compared to others with notable tissue oedema associated with dermatomyositis.
