Title: Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook

Kevin Chan, Gemma Kwan, Hwei Choo Soh, Louise Evans

Abstract:

Summary: Dermatomyositis is an uncommon autoimmune disorder that primarily involves the skin, skeletal musculature, and respiratory tract. Rapid initiation of intensive immunosuppressive therapy is generally necessary to prevent further progression and damage to multiple organ systems. A man in his 40s, previously in good health, presented to the emergency department with pronounced cervical swelling. Ear, nose, and throat specialists first evaluated him for a suspected deep neck space infection. After this diagnosis was excluded, he was referred to the clinical immunology team to assess and treat presumed idiopathic angioedema. Given the unusual characteristics of the cervical edema—which did not correlate with angioedema—and associated skin and nail alterations, a creatine kinase assay was promptly obtained. Dermatomyositis was provisionally diagnosed, and subsequent testing identified a positive small ubiquitin-like modifier-1 activating enzyme (SAE1) autoantibody as well as findings consistent with dermatomyositis on muscle biopsy. This report draws attention to neck swelling not mediated by histamine or bradykinin as an atypical manifestation of dermatomyositis, which in this case presented with dysphagia and notable muscle involvement that required urgent therapy.

Background:

Dermatomyositis is a form of idiopathic inflammatory myopathy that primarily targets the skin and skeletal muscles, but may also involve organs such as the gastrointestinal tract, joints, lungs, and heart. Its incidence is estimated at 10 per million individuals. The condition displays a bimodal age distribution, commonly presenting in children between 5 and 14 years of age and adults from 45 to 64 years old. Classic skin manifestations are Gottron’s papules, heliotrope rash, shawl sign, and periungual telangiectasias. Additional clinical features can include weakness of proximal muscles, interstitial lung disease, and swallowing difficulties. Pseudoangioedema is a rare manifestation in dermatomyositis; one study identified this feature in just 8 out of 121 individuals with the disease. When pseudoangioedema affects the neck, it can mimic primary otolaryngologic conditions or be confused with histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema.

Case presentation:

A man in his 40s presented to the emergency department with three weeks of gradually worsening neck swelling and proximal muscle weakness affecting his hip flexors, along with mild muscle aches. He had no notable past medical issues and had never smoked. He denied any history of using 3-hydroxy-3-methyl-glutaryl-coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase inhibitors. Ludwig’s angina was considered as an initial diagnosis, prompting a neck CT, which revealed widespread neck edema involving the strap muscles and inflammation that reached the retropharyngeal space (figure 1). He was afebrile, had an oxygen saturation of 99% on room air, and exhibited no tachypnea. Nailfold capillaroscopy demonstrated proximal nailfold microhemorrhages and avascular zones. Laboratory findings included a white blood cell count of 9.1×10^9/L and a C-reactive protein (CRP) level of 55.6 mg/L at presentation. Given the unresolved swelling without an apparent etiology, he was referred to a quaternary care hospital with ear, nose, and throat (ENT) surgical support for further assessment. ENT performed a flexible nasendoscopy, which revealed no major structural abnormalities in the soft palate, vallecula, larynx, or post-cricoid region; however, the examination was notable for increased secretions and the patient experienced coughing.

The patient’s case was reviewed with the on-call immunologist due to concerns for angioedema, given the absence of an identifiable structural or infectious etiology. Assessment revealed diffuse facial erythema, including over the nasolabial folds, and a neck that was erythematous, edematous, yet non-tender. The patient reported not taking any medications, specifically denying use of angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs. There was no personal or family history of angioedema. Taken together, his overall presentation was uncharacteristic of both acquired and hereditary forms of angioedema. Detailed examination identified mild nailfold abnormalities consistent with periungual erythema, a cutaneous eruption over the right lateral thigh, and a reduction in hip flexor strength to 4/5. Neither Gottron’s papules nor a heliotrope rash were present. Creatine kinase was subsequently measured and found markedly elevated at 16,184 U/L the following day. Renal function remained within normal parameters, and urine myoglobin testing was negative. Additionally, laboratory studies showed increased transaminases, with alanine aminotransferase (ALT) at 143 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) at 396 U/L, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) at 692 U/L.

Investigations:

Due to the initial suspicion of angioedema, a range of tests were conducted to identify possible causes of spontaneous angioedema. Results showed that C3 and C4 complement components were within normal limits, as were C1 esterase inhibitor functional activity and quantitative measurements.

Autoimmune screening revealed a positive anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) at a titre of 1:1280 displaying a speckled pattern. Testing for anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA) was negative. Evaluation with the Euroimmun Euroline immunoassay myositis panel, which assesses 16 antigens related to autoimmune inflammatory myopathies, demonstrated a strong positive result for anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibodies, with a signal intensity of 173 (reference value <35). Other tested myositis antibodies, including anti-Mi-2 alpha (anti-Mi-2α), anti-Transcription Intermediary Factor-1 gamma (anti-TIF1-γ), anti–Histidyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-Jo-1), and anti–Isoleucyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-OJ), were negative. Serum immunoglobulin G (IgG) and its subclasses were reported as normal.

In his immunosuppression workup, screening for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), hepatitis C core antibody (HBcAb), hepatitis B e antibody (HBeAb), and hepatitis C virus (HCV) returned negative, as did serology for HIV, varicella zoster virus (VZV) IgG, and both IgG and IgM for cytomegalovirus (CMV). The QuantiFERON-TB Gold test was indeterminate, but three sputum specimens tested negative for Mycobacterium tuberculosis by acid-fast bacilli staining.

Pulmonary function testing demonstrated a restrictive deficit of extrathoracic origin and lacked a notable response to bronchodilators. Chest CT imaging identified numerous non-cavitating nodules within the lungs, predominantly located in the mid to lower lung zones; the largest nodule was 15 mm and situated in the right lower lobe. Additionally, patchy peribronchial consolidations—likely attributable to aspiration pneumonitis—were present, though there was no evidence of pulmonary fibrosis on imaging. Abdominal CT revealed no abdominopelvic lymphadenopathy and showed neither a primary tumor nor metastatic disease in the abdominal or pelvic regions. A video fluoroscopic (barium) swallow study demonstrated marked pharyngeal dysphagia, with significant pyriform sinus residue and, after swallowing, penetration of thin and slightly thick fluids to the level of the vocal cords.

MRI of the thighs revealed findings indicative of active myositis involving both the obturator internus and externus muscles, along with less pronounced myositis in the hip adductors, gracilis muscles bilaterally, and distal portions of the iliopsoas muscles on both sides (figure 2). Additional evidence of ongoing muscle inflammation was demonstrated by fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)-positron emission tomography (PET), which displayed diffuse, abnormal FDG uptake in several muscle groups, most notably in the head and neck, both deltoids, forearms, chest wall, abdomen, and pelvis (figure 3). No regions of metabolically active tissue were observed elsewhere to indicate malignancy. Tumor marker assays, including carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), cancer antigen 19-9 (CA19-9), cancer antigen 125 (CA-125), and prostate-specific antigen (PSA), yielded results within the normal reference range.

Thigh muscle biopsy demonstrated areas featuring moderate numbers of necrotic, vacuolated degenerating, myophagocytosed, and regenerating myofibers in a perifascicular distribution, with scattered perimysial inflammatory infiltrates composed predominantly of dispersed histiocytes (figure 4). Immunohistochemical analysis targeting the membrane attack complex showed deposition of complement component 5b-9 (C5b-9) in endomysial capillaries of the perifascicular region, which is characteristic of dermatomyositis. The patient was subsequently diagnosed with moderate to severe SAE1 antibody positive dermatomyositis, accompanied by inflammatory edema, peribronchial consolidation attributed to micro-aspiration, dysphagia, and myositis. Dermatologic findings included a positive Holster sign and periungual erythema.

Differential diagnosis:

Due to the gradual onset of his symptoms, including cervical swelling and raised CRP levels, initial diagnostic considerations focused on Ludwig angina and idiopathic angioedema. Early in his illness, significant muscle weakness and dysphagia were not evident. The differential diagnosis list also encompassed additional inflammatory myopathies (such as immune-mediated necrotising myopathy and inclusion body myositis), infectious etiologies (including cervical cellulitis/erysipelas, deep neck space abscesses, and bacterial sialadenitis), disorders affecting the neuromuscular junction like myasthenia gravis, delayed drug hypersensitivity reactions (for example, drug rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms), acute contact dermatitis, orofacial granulomatosis, hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis, and superior vena cava syndrome.

Treatment:

Owing to pronounced involvement of the airway musculature, the patient received intravenous methylprednisolone at 1000 mg daily for a duration of 3 days. This was followed by a gradual reduction in corticosteroid therapy using prednisone, initiated at 60 mg per day and tapered by 5 mg every 2 to 3 weeks. An initial loading dose of intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) (Gamunex 10%) amounting to 2 g/kg (a total of 165 g) was administered over 5 days (equivalent to 35 g per day), succeeded by 5 additional cycles spaced 4 weeks apart, with a cumulative dose of 2 g/kg (165 g) divided across these sessions. Mycophenolate mofetil was introduced at a dosage of 1 g twice daily, and the patient also received two infusions of rituximab at 1000 mg intravenously, spaced 2 weeks apart. Methotrexate was not considered owing to its association with interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis, given the patient’s abnormal pulmonary function tests; this decision was also made in partnership with the patient after discussing the medication’s adverse effect profile. Azathioprine and tacrolimus were not initiated, as mycophenolate was well-tolerated. Sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim was started for Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia prophylaxis but was discontinued after the development of haemolytic anaemia (despite absence of glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency), and the patient subsequently tolerated nebulised pentamidine 300 mg every 4 weeks.

He was discharged after a 21-day inpatient stay. His oral medications were administered through nasogastric tube as he had persistent dysphagia at the time of discharge.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following a 3-week hospitalization with ongoing therapy, the patient exhibited partial recovery in hip flexor strength and significant reduction in cervical soft tissue edema; however, the swelling had not entirely subsided by the time of discharge. The patient continued to experience dysphagia, requiring ongoing nasogastric tube feeding for an additional 2 months.

At a follow-up visit 3 weeks post-discharge, proximal muscle strength was completely restored, and the serum creatine kinase level had decreased to 37 U/L. During an immunology assessment 2 months after discharge, further improvement in cervical swelling was noted, with only persistent violaceous discoloration of the face and periungual changes observed on examination. Swallowing ability had advanced sufficiently to permit discontinuation of nasogastric tube feeding and transition to a diet of soft solids. The patient continued treatment with mycophenolate mofetil, monthly intravenous immunoglobulin infusions, and a gradually reducing dose of prednisone.

At three months after discharge, the patient’s proximal muscle strength remained unchanged, chest assessment revealed no abnormal findings, and the swelling of the cervical soft tissues had fully subsided. Prednisone was successfully stopped at seven months post-discharge, with resolution of dysphagia by that time. Follow-up pulmonary function testing indicated slight improvement, though a persistent restrictive pattern was noted, thought to represent the patient’s baseline state (see figure 5a, b). CT imaging of the chest conducted at 11 months showed almost complete disappearance of previously noted basal ground-glass opacities and areas of airspace consolidation, which were originally considered secondary to aspiration. Twelve months after diagnosis, respiratory performance remained stable, with preserved exercise tolerance.

Regarding malignancy surveillance, he reported no systemic symptoms during follow-up. Ongoing monitoring is planned, involving assessment of symptoms, physical examination, periodic blood work, and relevant imaging studies, including repeated CT scans of the chest. He is also scheduled for gastroscopy and colonoscopy to rule out hidden gastrointestinal or colorectal cancers.

The treatment strategy includes ongoing IVIG administration, with plans to reduce the dose of mycophenolate gradually over the next six months in accordance with clinical progress. Close follow-up will be maintained to assess if modifications in therapy are warranted.

Discussion:

We describe a patient with dermatomyositis positive for anti-SAE1 antibodies who presented with marked swelling of the cervical soft tissue, initially believed to be idiopathic angioedema. The diagnosis was revised after dermatologic signs suggestive of dermatomyositis were identified on detailed clinical examination, allowing for prompt initiation of appropriate therapy.

Anti-SAE1 antibodies are directed against the small ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) activating enzyme, which shares structural similarities with ubiquitin and functions in the sumoylation, or post-translational modification, of a variety of proteins. Clinically, dermatomyositis associated with SAE1 antibodies often displays characteristic skin findings, including heliotrope rash, Gottron's papules, the V-neck sign, and the shawl sign. According to an extensive review of 215 reported cases, dysphagia and interstitial lung disease were present in 46% and 43% of patients, respectively, and malignancy was observed in 18% of cases.

Swelling of subcutaneous tissue, particularly involving the face or neck, is rarely seen in cases of dermatomyositis. In a study comprising 121 individuals diagnosed with dermatomyositis, angioedema was noted in just 8 patients (6.61%). A review of published reports between 1985 and 2013 identified only 19 cases in which dermatomyositis presented with oedema. The presence of antibodies such as anti-melanoma differentiation-associated gene (MDA)-5 and anti-transcriptional intermediary factor 1γ (TIF1-γ) appears to have a stronger correlation with angioedema compared to other antibodies. Several case reports documented facial oedema, while descriptions of neck swelling were much less common. Our own literature review focused on 10 patients with SAE1-positive dermatomyositis revealed that oedema was noted in only three cases; of these, just one referred to swelling in the throat region, highlighting the rarity of neck involvement.79

This case underscores the importance for emergency physicians, ENT specialists, immunologists, and rheumatologists to consider dermatomyositis as a potential differential diagnosis when encountering neck swelling, particularly when presented as localized 'angioedema' of unclear or idiopathic origin. Dysphagia and other systemic features may be mild or easily overlooked; therefore, a careful physical examination for dermatological signs of dermatomyositis and measurement of creatine kinase is essential. In this patient, subcutaneous edema resulted from muscle inflammation linked to myositis, as opposed to an angioedema caused by histamine or bradykinin pathways.

Following the International Guideline for Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathy-Associated Cancer Screening from the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS), the individual displayed two major risk factors—dermatomyositis and dysphagia—alongside two intermediate risk factors—presence of the SAE1 antibody and male sex—placing him at high malignancy risk. Accordingly, an oncologic assessment was performed, including CT imaging, tumor marker analysis, and a PET scan, with further plans for upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopies. Thorough baseline and follow-up surveillance have so far been consistently reassuring.

Available data to inform the best pharmacologic management of anti-SAE1–positive dermatomyositis remain scarce. The most extensive cohort described to date demonstrates varied therapeutic strategies, with the majority of individuals receiving glucocorticoids alongside different steroid-sparing medications—most frequently methotrexate, but also including mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, and azathioprine; in certain instances, intravenous immunoglobulin was administered. In this case, the patient was managed with steroids, mycophenolate mofetil, intravenous immunoglobulin, and rituximab, resulting in remission. Mycophenolate was chosen based on the patient’s preference for a once-daily oral therapy, as well as its favorable tolerability profile and established efficacy in dermatomyositis. Notably, in contrast to other individuals with pronounced tissue oedema related to dermatomyositis, our patient appears to have a relatively favorable outlook.
