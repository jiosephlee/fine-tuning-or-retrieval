Title: Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook

Kevin Chan, Gemma Kwan, Hwei Choo Soh, Louise Evans

Abstract:

Summary: Dermatomyositis is an uncommon autoimmune condition that mainly involves the skin, skeletal muscle, and respiratory tract. Management typically necessitates early and aggressive immunosuppressive therapy to minimize progression and prevent injury to multiple organs. A man in his 40s with no significant past medical history first presented to the emergency department with pronounced cervical swelling. Ear, nose, and throat specialists initially assessed him for suspected deep neck space infection. After ruling this out, he was referred to the clinical immunology team to evaluate for idiopathic angioedema. Due to the atypical features of the cervical edema—which did not fit with angioedema—and concomitant abnormalities in the skin and nails, a creatine kinase assay was urgently obtained. Dermatomyositis was considered as a working diagnosis, which was subsequently confirmed by identification of the small ubiquitin-like modifier-1 activating enzyme (SAE1) autoantibody and characteristic findings on muscle biopsy. This report underscores that neck swelling not mediated by histamine or bradykinin can be a rare initial manifestation of dermatomyositis, presenting with dysphagia and marked muscle involvement, necessitating rapid initiation of therapy.

Background:

Dermatomyositis is an idiopathic inflammatory myopathy that predominantly affects the skin and muscles, although involvement of the gastrointestinal tract, joints, lungs, and heart may also occur. The condition’s estimated incidence is approximately 10 cases per million people. Its age distribution is bimodal, with peak onset observed in children between 5–14 years of age and adults between 45–64 years. Distinctive dermatological manifestations include Gottron’s papules, shawl sign, heliotrope rash, and periungual telangiectasias. Additional clinical features consist of proximal muscle weakness, interstitial lung disease, and dysphagia. Pseudoangioedema is a rare manifestation, as demonstrated by one study reporting its occurrence in only 8 out of 121 dermatomyositis patients. When pseudoangioedema involves the neck, it can mimic an otolaryngologic primary disorder or be confused with angioedema mediated by histamine or bradykinin.

Case presentation:

A man in his 40s arrived at the emergency department with a three-week progression of neck swelling and weakness affecting his hip flexors, associated with mild muscle aches. He had no notable past medical history and had never smoked. He was not taking any HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors. Ludwig’s angina was initially suspected, prompting a neck CT scan that revealed widespread edema of the strap muscles and inflammatory changes extending into the retropharyngeal space (see figure 1). He was afebrile, with oxygen saturation at 99% on room air, and did not display tachypnea. Examination of his nailfolds revealed proximal microhemorrhages and avascular zones, as observed on nailfold capillaroscopy. His initial laboratory studies showed a white blood cell count of 9.1×10^9/L and a C-reactive protein level of 55.6 mg/L. Because his neck swelling persisted without an identifiable etiology, he was moved to a quaternary hospital equipped with ENT surgery for further assessment. ENT evaluation using flexible nasendoscopy found no significant structural anomalies in the soft palate, vallecula, larynx, or post-cricoid area, but did note an increase in secretions and coughing during the procedure.

The case was referred to the on-call immunologist due to concern for angioedema, as evaluation had not identified any clear structural abnormality or infectious source. On assessment, the patient exhibited widespread erythema on the face, including across the nasolabial folds, along with a swollen, erythematous, but non-tender neck. The patient reported not taking any medications, such as angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and had no personal or family history of angioedema. Based on these findings, his clinical presentation was not consistent with typical acquired or hereditary angioedema. Detailed examination identified discrete periungual changes indicating erythema around the nailfolds, a rash on the right lateral thigh, and diminished strength (4/5) in hip flexion. Gottron’s papules and heliotrope eruption were not detected. Measurement of creatine kinase, which was subsequently performed the following day, showed a marked elevation to 16,184 U/L. Renal function remained within normal limits, and urine myoglobin screening was negative. Additional investigations revealed increased transaminases, with alanine aminotransferase (ALT) measured at 143 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) at 396 U/L, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) at 692 U/L.

Investigations:

Due to the initial suspicion of angioedema, a number of investigations were conducted to identify potential etiologies of spontaneous angioedema. The results showed that complement components C3 and C4 were within the normal range, as were both functional activity and quantitative levels of C1 esterase.

Serological testing revealed a positive anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) at a titre of 1:1280 displaying a speckled pattern. Anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA) testing returned negative. A myositis antibody panel targeting 16 autoantigens associated with autoimmune inflammatory myopathies was carried out using the Euroimmun Euroline immunoassay, demonstrating a robustly positive result for anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibodies with a signal intensity of 173 (reference range <35). The panel was negative for other myositis-specific antibodies including anti-Mi-2 alpha (anti-Mi-2α), anti-Transcription Intermediary Factor-1 gamma (anti-TIF1-γ), anti–Histidyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-Jo-1), and anti–Isoleucyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-OJ). Quantitative immunoglobulin G (IgG) and its subclasses were within normal parameters.

Regarding infectious screening prior to immunosuppression, the patient tested negative for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), hepatitis B core antibody (HBcAb), hepatitis B e antibody (HBeAb), hepatitis C virus (HCV), HIV, varicella zoster virus (VZV) IgG, and both cytomegalovirus (CMV) IgG and immunoglobulin M (IgM). Although the QuantiFERON-TB Gold result was indeterminate, acid-fast bacilli smears from three separate sputum specimens were negative for Mycobacterium tuberculosis.

Pulmonary function testing demonstrated a restrictive pattern localized outside the thoracic cavity, without a notable bronchodilator effect. Chest CT imaging identified multiple non-cavitating nodules distributed primarily in the mid and lower lung zones, with the largest lesion measuring 15 mm in the right lower lobe. Additionally, there were irregular regions of peribronchial consolidation considered consistent with aspiration pneumonitis, and there was no imaging evidence of pulmonary fibrosis. Abdominal CT scans showed no abdomino-pelvic lymphadenopathy and revealed no signs of either primary or secondary malignancy within the abdomen or pelvis. A video fluoroscopic swallow assessment (barium swallow) revealed marked pharyngeal dysphagia, causing substantial retention in the pyriform fossae and post-swallow penetration of thin and slightly thick fluids reaching the vocal cords.

MRI imaging of the thighs demonstrated findings indicative of active myositis within the bilateral obturator internus and externus muscles, along with less pronounced involvement in the hip adductors, gracilis muscles bilaterally, and bilateral distal portions of the iliopsoas muscles (figure 2). Additional confirmation of ongoing muscle inflammation was provided by fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) positron emission tomography (PET), which revealed widespread and abnormal FDG uptake across various muscle groups, with greatest intensity observed in the head and neck, both deltoid muscles, forearms, chest wall, abdomen, and pelvis (figure 3). No sites of metabolically active disease were identified elsewhere to indicate malignancy. Tumor markers, specifically carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), cancer antigen 19-9 (CA19-9), cancer antigen 125 (CA-125), and prostate-specific antigen (PSA), all returned within normal limits.

Thigh muscle biopsy displayed areas containing a moderate quantity of necrotic, vacuolated, degenerating, myophagocytosed, and regenerating muscle fibers predominantly in a perifascicular distribution, in addition to scattered perimysial infiltrates comprised chiefly of dispersed histiocytes (figure 4). Immunohistochemical analysis for membrane attack complex identified complement component 5b-9 (C5b-9) deposits within the perifascicular endomysial capillaries, which is characteristic of dermatomyositis. The patient was diagnosed with moderate to severe SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis manifesting as inflammatory edema, peribronchial consolidation resulting from micro-aspiration, dysphagia, and myositis. Cutaneous signs included a positive Holster sign and periungual erythema.

Differential diagnosis:

Due to his subacute onset of neck swelling accompanied by an elevated CRP, initial differential considerations included Ludwig angina and idiopathic angioedema. Early symptoms did not prominently feature muscle weakness or dysphagia. Additional differential diagnoses encompassed other inflammatory myopathies such as immune-mediated necrotising myopathy and inclusion body myositis; infectious etiologies including cellulitis or erysipelas involving the neck, deep neck space infections, and bacterial sialadenitis; disorders affecting the neuromuscular junction like myasthenia gravis; delayed hypersensitivity reactions to medications, such as drug rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms; acute contact dermatitis; orofacial granulomatosis; hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis; and superior vena cava syndrome.

Treatment:

Because of pronounced involvement of his airway musculature, the patient received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for three days. This was transitioned to an oral prednisone taper, starting at 60 mg per day, with the dose decreased by 5 mg every 2 to 3 weeks. An initial intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) loading regimen (Gamunex 10%) totaling 2 g/kg (165 g) was administered over 5 days (35 g per day), which was followed by five additional cycles, each spaced four weeks apart, totaling another 2 g/kg (165 g) distributed across these cycles. Mycophenolate mofetil was initiated at 1 g twice daily, and the patient received two rituximab infusions of 1000 mg IV, separated by two weeks. Methotrexate therapy was avoided due to its link with interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis—of particular concern in this patient given abnormal pulmonary function test results—and was also discussed with the patient considering its adverse effect profile. Azathioprine and tacrolimus were not pursued since mycophenolate was well tolerated. For prophylaxis against Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim was started but subsequently discontinued after haemolytic anaemia developed, despite the absence of glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency. The patient was able to tolerate nebulized pentamidine 300 mg every four weeks instead.

He was discharged following a 21-day hospitalization. Due to ongoing dysphagia at discharge, all oral medications were administered via nasogastric tube.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following a 3-week hospital admission and therapy, the patient showed moderate gains in hip flexor strength and significant reduction in cervical soft tissue edema; however, the swelling was not completely resolved by discharge. Ongoing dysphagia required continued nasogastric tube feeding for an additional two months.

At a follow-up three weeks post-discharge, strength in the proximal muscles was fully restored and the creatine kinase level had returned to 37 U/L. Two months after discharge, during an immunology clinic review, the cervical swelling was further diminished, with only minor residual violaceous erythema of the face and periungual abnormalities noted on physical examination. The patient's swallowing had sufficiently recovered to discontinue the nasogastric tube and advance to a soft solid diet. The patient was maintained on mycophenolate mofetil, monthly intravenous immunoglobulin, and a tapering regimen of prednisone.

Three months after discharge, the patient's proximal muscle strength remained unchanged, chest auscultation was normal, and cervical soft tissue edema had resolved entirely. Prednisone therapy was successfully ceased at seven months post-discharge, by which point dysphagia was no longer present. Follow-up pulmonary function testing indicated slight improvement, though a residual restrictive pattern persisted, believed to be consistent with baseline physiology (see figure 5a, b). An 11-month follow-up chest CT showed almost complete clearance of earlier basal ground-glass opacities and airspace consolidations, findings initially considered secondary to aspiration. Twelve months after initial diagnosis, respiratory status was stable with preserved exercise tolerance.

Regarding malignancy surveillance, the patient did not report constitutional symptoms at subsequent visits and will continue ongoing surveillance through symptom assessment, clinical exams, periodic blood work, and repeat CT imaging of the chest. Plans include upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopies to rule out concealed GI or colorectal cancer.

Management includes ongoing IVIG with an intention to decrease mycophenolate dosage over the next six months contingent upon his clinical course. Continued close monitoring is planned to assess whether modifications to the medication regimen are necessary.

Discussion:

This case involves a patient with dermatomyositis associated with anti-SAE1 antibodies who developed pronounced swelling of the cervical soft tissues, which was initially misdiagnosed as idiopathic angioedema. Prompt identification of dermatomyositis as a possible alternative diagnosis, together with meticulous clinical assessment that uncovered faint skin manifestations, facilitated early diagnosis and intervention.

Anti-SAE1 antibodies are directed against the small ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) activating enzyme, a molecule bearing structural resemblance to ubiquitin and participating in the post-translational alteration of various target proteins through a process known as sumoylation. Dermatomyositis with SAE1 antibody positivity is commonly marked by distinctive skin findings such as heliotrope rash, Gottron’s papules, the V-neck sign, and the shawl sign. According to an extensive literature review involving 215 individuals, dysphagia and interstitial lung disease were observed in 46% and 43% of cases, respectively, while malignancy was noted in 18% of SAE1 dermatomyositis patients.

Swelling of the subcutaneous tissues, particularly involving the face or neck, is a rare presentation in dermatomyositis. Among 121 dermatomyositis patients included in a large cohort analysis, angioedema was observed in only 8 individuals (6.61%). Review of published reports from 1985 through 2013 revealed just 19 cases of dermatomyositis with oedematous manifestations. Certain autoantibodies, notably anti-melanoma differentiation-associated gene (MDA)-5 and anti-transcriptional intermediary factor 1γ (TIF1-γ) antibodies, have been linked to angioedema more frequently than other antibodies. Although facial oedema has been noted in several reports, neck swelling has been less commonly documented. In a review of 10 cases of SAE1-positive dermatomyositis, only three exhibited oedema, with one case reporting swelling in the throat region, underscoring the rarity of neck involvement.79

This case underscores the importance for emergency physicians, ENT specialists, immunologists, and rheumatologists to consider dermatomyositis as a potential cause of neck swelling, particularly when faced with localized 'angioedema' of unknown or idiopathic origin. Because manifestations such as dysphagia and other systemic involvement can be subtle, careful assessment for dermatologic signs of dermatomyositis and ordering creatine kinase levels are essential steps. In this instance, the patient's subcutaneous edema was attributable to muscular inflammation due to myositis, rather than angioedema mediated by histamine or bradykinin.

According to the International Guideline for Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathy-Associated Cancer Screening from the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS), the patient possessed two factors conferring high risk (dermatomyositis, dysphagia) and two intermediate risk factors (positivity for SAE1 antibody, male sex), thereby classifying him as high risk for malignancy. He underwent cancer screening with CT imaging, tumor marker assays, and a PET scan, and upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopy were also planned. Ongoing comprehensive baseline assessments and interval follow-up evaluations have remained encouraging up to this point.

There is a scarcity of data regarding the best medication strategies for anti-SAE1–positive dermatomyositis. The largest cohort study available describes varied management, with the majority of individuals treated with glucocorticoids alongside different steroid-sparing drugs, most frequently methotrexate, and additionally agents such as mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, and azathioprine; intravenous immunoglobulin was administered in select instances. In this case, the patient received steroids, mycophenolate mofetil, intravenous immunoglobulin, and rituximab, resulting in remission. The choice of mycophenolate mofetil was based on the patient’s preference for an oral daily therapy, its favorable tolerability, and established efficacy in dermatomyositis. Compared to patients presenting with substantial tissue edema in dermatomyositis, this individual fortunately exhibits a positive prognosis.
