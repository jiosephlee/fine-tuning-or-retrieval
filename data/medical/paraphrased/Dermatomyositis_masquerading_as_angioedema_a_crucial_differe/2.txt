Title: Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook

Kevin Chan, Gemma Kwan, Hwei Choo Soh, Louise Evans

Abstract:

Summary: Dermatomyositis is an uncommon autoimmune disorder primarily involving the skin, musculature, and the respiratory tract. Management of dermatomyositis commonly necessitates immediate and vigorous immunosuppressive therapy to curb disease advancement and avert damage to multiple organ systems. In this case, a previously healthy male in his 40s presented to the emergency department with marked cervical swelling. His initial evaluation by ear, nose, and throat specialists suggested a potential deep neck space infection. After this possibility was excluded, he was referred to clinical immunology for assessment and management of suspected idiopathic angioedema. Due to the unusual presentation of cervical oedema, which did not fit the typical profile of angioedema, and the observation of accompanying dermatological and nail abnormalities, a creatine kinase test was promptly ordered. Dermatomyositis was provisionally diagnosed, later confirmed by detection of a positive small ubiquitin-like modifier-1 activating enzyme (SAE1) autoantibody and classic findings on muscle biopsy. This report underscores that neck swelling not mediated by histamine or bradykinin may represent an atypical manifestation of dermatomyositis, presenting with symptoms such as dysphagia and considerable muscle involvement that necessitate early intervention.

Background:

Dermatomyositis is an inflammatory myopathy of unknown origin that primarily targets the skin and musculature, but may also involve organs such as the gastrointestinal tract, joints, lungs, and heart. The condition occurs at a rate of approximately 10 cases per million individuals. Its onset displays a bimodal age distribution, most commonly emerging in children between 5 and 14 years old and in adults between 45 and 64 years old. Hallmark dermatological manifestations include Gottron’s papules, the shawl sign, heliotrope eruption, and periungual telangiectasias. Additional clinical features often comprise proximal muscle weakness, interstitial lung disease, and difficulty swallowing (dysphagia). Pseudoangioedema is a relatively rare presentation in dermatomyositis; one investigation documented its presence in only 8 out of 121 patients with this diagnosis. When pseudoangioedema is localized to the neck, it can be misinterpreted as a primary otolaryngological condition or be confused with angioedema mediated by histamine or bradykinin.

Case presentation:

A man in his forties came to the emergency department reporting three weeks of gradually worsening neck swelling and proximal muscle weakness, particularly in his hip flexors, along with mild muscle pain. He had no noteworthy prior medical conditions and had never smoked. Furthermore, he had not used any 3-hydroxy-3-methyl-glutaryl-coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase inhibitors. The provisional diagnosis was Ludwig’s angina, prompting a neck CT scan, which revealed extensive oedema of the neck affecting the strap muscles and inflammatory changes reaching into the retropharyngeal space (see figure 1). On admission, he had no fever, maintained an oxygen saturation of 99% on ambient air, and did not exhibit tachypnoea. Capillaroscopy of his nailfolds revealed proximal microhaemorrhages and areas devoid of blood vessels. Laboratory studies showed a white cell count of 9.1×10^9/L and a C-reactive protein (CRP) level of 55.6 mg/L. Since the swelling persisted without a clear etiology, he was referred to a quaternary care centre with specialized ear, nose, and throat (ENT) surgical services for more comprehensive assessment. ENT specialists performed a flexible nasendoscopy, which demonstrated no notable anatomical abnormalities in the soft palate, vallecula, larynx, or post-cricoid region, though the procedure did reveal increased secretions and provoked coughing.

Consultation with the on-call immunologist was sought due to concerns regarding possible angioedema, given the absence of definitive structural or infectious etiologies. Upon assessment, the patient displayed diffuse facial erythema extending into the nasolabial folds and presented with a swollen, erythematous neck that was non-tender to palpation. He reported not taking any medications, including angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and there was neither a personal nor a family history of angioedema. Collectively, his symptoms did not align with either acquired or hereditary forms of angioedema. Comprehensive physical examination identified subtle erythema at the periungual regions, a rash localized to the right lateral thigh, and decreased strength in the hip flexors measured at 4/5. There was no evidence of Gottron’s papules or a heliotrope rash. Laboratory investigations revealed a markedly increased creatine kinase level of 16 184 U/L the following day. His renal parameters remained within normal limits, and the myoglobin screen in his urine was negative. Liver biochemistry showed raised transaminases with an alanine aminotransferase (ALT) of 143 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) of 396 U/L, and an elevated lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) of 692 U/L.

Investigations:

Due to the initial suspicion of angioedema, an array of investigations was undertaken to identify potential etiologies of spontaneous angioedema. The results revealed that both C3 and C4 complement levels were within reference limits, as were the values and activity of C1 esterase.

Testing for anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) yielded a positive result with a titre of 1:1280 and a speckled immunofluorescence pattern. Anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA) were not detected. Assessment of myositis-specific antibodies was carried out utilizing the Euroimmun Euroline immunoassay, which screened for 16 autoantigens implicated in autoimmune inflammatory myopathies. Notably, there was a markedly elevated signal for anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibodies (intensity 173; reference value <35). No reactivity was observed for the other myositis-associated antibodies evaluated, such as anti-Mi-2 alpha (anti-Mi-2α), anti-Transcription Intermediary Factor-1 gamma (anti-TIF1-γ), anti–Histidyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-Jo-1), and anti–Isoleucyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-OJ). Immunoglobulin G (IgG) concentrations, along with its subclasses, fell within normal parameters. 

With respect to screening for underlying immunosuppression, all relevant viral serologies were negative: hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), hepatitis C core antibody (HBcAb), hepatitis B e antibody (HBeAb), hepatitis C virus (HCV), human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), varicella zoster virus (VZV) IgG, and cytomegalovirus (CMV) IgG and IgM. The QuantiFERON-TB Gold assay yielded an indeterminate result, but examination of three sputum samples for acid-fast bacilli did not demonstrate Mycobacterium tuberculosis.

Pulmonary function assessment demonstrated an extrathoracic restrictive pattern without notable responsiveness to bronchodilators. Chest CT imaging identified numerous non-cavitating pulmonary nodules predominantly situated in the mid to lower lung zones, the largest measuring 15 mm within the right lower lobe. Additionally, there were patchy peribronchial consolidations, presumed secondary to aspiration pneumonitis; importantly, there were no CT signs suggestive of pulmonary fibrosis. Abdominal CT did not reveal any abdomino-pelvic lymphadenopathy, nor was there evidence for either a primary or metastatic neoplasm within the abdomen or pelvis. A video fluoroscopic (barium) swallow examination disclosed marked pharyngeal dysphagia, resulting in substantial residue within the pyriform fossae and post-swallow penetration of thin and slightly thickened fluids to the level of the vocal cords.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the thighs demonstrated findings indicative of active myositis within the bilateral obturator internus and externus muscles, as well as milder inflammatory involvement of the hip adductors, gracilis muscles on both sides, and the distal segments of the iliopsoas bilaterally (see figure 2). Additional confirmation of ongoing muscle inflammation was provided by fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)-positron emission tomography (PET) imaging, which revealed widespread, abnormal FDG uptake affecting various muscle groups, with the greatest intensity observed in the head and neck, both deltoids, forearm muscles, chest wall, abdominal region, and pelvic muscles (see figure 3). There was no evidence of metabolically active lesions elsewhere that would be indicative of malignancy. Furthermore, laboratory evaluation of tumor markers—including carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), cancer antigen 19-9 (CA19-9), cancer antigen 125 (CA-125), and prostate-specific antigen (PSA)—yielded values within normal limits.

Histopathological examination of a muscle biopsy from the thigh revealed clusters of necrotic, vacuolated degenerating, myophagocytosed, and regenerating myofibres with a perifascicular pattern, accompanied by sporadic perimysial inflammatory infiltrates comprised mainly of scattered histiocytes (figure 4). Immunohistochemical staining for the membrane attack complex identified deposition of complement component 5b-9 (C5b-9) in the perifascicular endomysial capillaries, a characteristic hallmark of dermatomyositis. The patient was therefore diagnosed with moderate to severe dermatomyositis characterized by SAE1 antibody positivity, displaying features of inflammatory edema, peribronchial consolidation attributable to micro-aspiration, dysphagia, and myositis. Cutaneous findings consisted of a positive Holster sign and periungual erythema.

Differential diagnosis:

Due to his subacute onset characterized by neck swelling and an increased CRP level, initial diagnostic considerations focused on Ludwig angina and idiopathic angioedema. Notably, muscle weakness—including dysphagia—was not significant in the early clinical course. The differential diagnosis also encompassed other inflammatory myopathies (such as immune-mediated necrotising myopathy and inclusion body myositis), infectious etiologies (like cellulitis/erysipelas involving the neck, deep neck space infections, and bacterial sialadenitis), neuromuscular junction disorders such as myasthenia gravis, delayed-type drug hypersensitivity reactions (for example, drug rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms), acute contact dermatitis, orofacial granulomatosis, hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis, and superior vena cava syndrome.

Treatment:

Owing to marked involvement of the airway musculature, the patient received intravenous methylprednisolone at 1000 mg daily for 3 days. This was followed by a tapering course of prednisone, initiated at 60 mg per day and reduced by 5 mg every 2 to 3 weeks. An initial dose of intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) (Gamunex 10%) was administered at 2 g/kg (totalling 165 g) spread across 5 days (35 g per day), after which an additional 5 cycles were given at 4-week intervals, with the overall dose of 2 g/kg (=165 g) distributed across these treatments. Mycophenolate mofetil therapy was started at 1 g twice daily, and two infusions of rituximab, each 1000 mg intravenously, were given two weeks apart. Methotrexate was not considered, given its known link to interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis, especially in light of the patient’s abnormal pulmonary function; the decision was also discussed with the patient due to potential adverse effects. Azathioprine and tacrolimus were likewise not prescribed because the patient tolerated mycophenolate without issue. For prophylaxis against Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim was initially started but discontinued due to the onset of haemolytic anaemia (despite no evidence of glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency); the patient subsequently received nebulised pentamidine at 300 mg every 4 weeks without complications.

Following a 21-day hospitalisation, he was discharged, with all oral medications administered via nasogastric tube, as he continued to experience significant dysphagia upon leaving the hospital.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following three weeks of inpatient care and therapeutic intervention, the patient experienced a partial recovery in hip flexor strength and a substantial reduction in cervical soft tissue edema; however, the swelling had not completely subsided by the time of discharge. Despite these improvements, dysphagia was still present, making prolonged nasogastric feeding necessary for an additional two months.

Upon re-evaluation three weeks after leaving the hospital, the patient’s proximal muscle strength had returned to normal, and serum creatine kinase had decreased to 37 U/L. At an immunology follow-up two months post-discharge, there was ongoing resolution of the cervical swelling, though minor violaceous erythema on the face and periungual alterations persisted on physical assessment. The patient’s ability to swallow had improved enough to discontinue nasogastric feeding and transition to a diet of soft solids. The therapeutic regimen included continued use of mycophenolate mofetil, monthly intravenous immunoglobulin infusions, and a gradually decreasing dose of prednisone.

At three months following hospital discharge, the patient's proximal muscle strength remained unchanged, chest auscultation revealed no abnormalities, and the cervical soft tissue swelling had fully subsided. Prednisone was tapered and discontinued by the seventh month post-discharge, at which point the dysphagia had completely resolved. Pulmonary function reassessment demonstrated a slight improvement but retained a restrictive pattern, considered consistent with the patient's baseline (figure 5a, b). An 11-month follow-up CT scan of the chest showed almost total resolution of the ground-glass opacities and airspace consolidation at the lung bases, changes initially linked to aspiration. At the one-year mark post-diagnosis, respiratory status remained steady and exercise capacity was satisfactory.

Regarding oncological surveillance, the patient did not report any constitutional symptoms during follow-up visits and will continue to be assessed through symptom review, physical examination, regular laboratory testing, and pertinent imaging studies, including serial CT chest scans. Plans include gastroscopy and colonoscopy to rule out the presence of occult gastrointestinal or colorectal cancer.

It was decided to maintain IVIG therapy and to initiate a gradual reduction of mycophenolate dosage over the subsequent six months, contingent upon his clinical trajectory. Ongoing close follow-up is planned to assess for any need to adjust his therapeutic regimen.

Discussion:

We present a case involving dermatomyositis with anti-SAE1 antibody positivity, characterized by marked swelling of the cervical soft tissues. This clinical manifestation was initially misdiagnosed as idiopathic angioedema. Accurate identification was achieved when dermatomyositis was included in the differential diagnosis and a thorough physical assessment disclosed subtle dermatological manifestations, facilitating prompt initiation of appropriate therapy.

Anti-SAE1 antibodies are directed against the small-ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) activating enzyme, a molecule bearing structural resemblance to ubiquitin and mediating post-translational modifications—specifically, sumoylation—of numerous substrate proteins. Clinical presentation of SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis often includes distinct cutaneous signs, such as the heliotrope rash, Gottron’s papules, the V-neck sign, and the shawl sign. According to an extensive literature review covering 215 documented cases, dysphagia and interstitial lung disease were noted in 46% and 43% of patients, respectively, with 18% demonstrating evidence of malignancy.

Edema of the subcutaneous tissue, notably in the form of facial or cervical swelling, occurs rarely in cases of dermatomyositis. Within a sizeable cohort of 121 individuals diagnosed with dermatomyositis, only 8 patients (6.61%) exhibited angioedema. Furthermore, a review of published studies spanning from 1985 to 2013 identified merely 19 reports of dermatomyositis presenting with oedema. The presence of antibodies such as anti-melanoma differentiation-associated gene (MDA)-5 and anti-transcriptional intermediary factor 1γ (TIF1-γ) has been linked to a greater likelihood of angioedema compared to other autoantibodies. Although facial oedema was documented in several reports, swelling specifically localized to the neck was infrequently reported. In our review of 10 documented instances of SAE1-positive dermatomyositis, oedema was noted in only three, with just one referencing swelling in the throat region. This underscores the rarity of neck involvement in such cases.79

This case highlights the importance for emergency physicians, ENT specialists, immunologists, and rheumatologists to consider dermatomyositis as a differential diagnosis for neck swelling, particularly in instances of localized ‘angioedema’ without a clear etiology. Since symptoms such as dysphagia and other systemic features may be mild or not immediately apparent, careful assessment for the clinical hallmarks of dermatomyositis, along with measurement of creatine kinase, is essential. In this patient, the subcutaneous oedema was attributed to underlying muscle inflammation secondary to myositis, rather than classic histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema.

Following the International Guideline for Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathy-Associated Cancer Screening established by the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS), the patient presented with two major risk factors (dermatomyositis and dysphagia) and two moderate risk factors (SAE1 antibody positivity and male sex), thereby placing him in a high malignancy risk category. Consequently, malignancy workup was undertaken, including CT imaging, tumour marker assays, and a PET scan, with additional endoscopic evaluation of the upper and lower gastrointestinal tract planned. Ongoing comprehensive surveillance, both at baseline and during follow-up, has so far been unremarkable.

Currently, evidence supporting the best pharmacological management for anti-SAE1–positive dermatomyositis remains sparse. In the largest cohort study available, therapeutic strategies varied widely; the majority of individuals were managed with glucocorticoids in combination with different steroid-sparing agents. Methotrexate was most frequently prescribed, although other immunosuppressives such as mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, and azathioprine were also utilized. Intravenous immunoglobulin was reserved for specific cases. In the present case, the patient received a regimen consisting of steroids, mycophenolate mofetil, intravenous immunoglobulin, and rituximab, resulting in remission. The decision to use mycophenolate mofetil was influenced by the patient’s preference for an oral daily medication as well as its favorable tolerability profile and recognized application in dermatomyositis. Notably, compared to other patients experiencing marked tissue oedema with dermatomyositis, our patient has demonstrated a more favorable outcome.
