Title: Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook

Kevin Chan, Gemma Kwan, Hwei Choo Soh, Louise Evans

Abstract:

Summary: Dermatomyositis is an uncommon autoimmune condition that primarily involves the skin, muscles, and respiratory tract. Immediate initiation of aggressive immunosuppressive therapy is usually necessary in dermatomyositis to limit disease advancement and prevent multiorgan involvement. A previously healthy man in his 40s first arrived at the emergency department with marked cervical swelling. Initially, he was evaluated by otolaryngology specialists, who considered but ruled out a deep neck space infection. Subsequently, he was referred to clinical immunology to assess and manage possible idiopathic angioedema. Given the unusual presentation of neck oedema—which did not match classic angioedema—and associated dermatologic and nail abnormalities, a creatine kinase measurement was promptly ordered. A working diagnosis of dermatomyositis was made; this was later supported by detection of a small ubiquitin-like modifier-1 activating enzyme (SAE1) autoantibody and findings on muscle biopsy consistent with the disease. This report underscores that neck swelling not mediated by histamine or bradykinin, manifesting with dysphagia and considerable myopathic involvement, can represent an atypical presentation of dermatomyositis necessitating rapid intervention.

Background:

Dermatomyositis is an idiopathic inflammatory myopathy that commonly affects the skin and skeletal muscles, though it may also involve the gastrointestinal system, joints, lungs, and heart. Its incidence is approximately 10 cases per million individuals. The disease displays a bimodal age distribution, occurring most frequently in children between 5 and 14 years old and in adults aged 45 to 64 years. Typical dermatological findings include Gottron’s papules, heliotrope rash, shawl sign, and periungual telangiectasias. Additional manifestations include proximal muscle weakness, interstitial lung disease, and difficulty swallowing. Pseudoangioedema is a rare feature in dermatomyositis, as demonstrated by a study in which it was observed in only 8 out of 121 patients with the condition. When pseudoangioedema involves the neck, it can be misdiagnosed as a primary otolaryngologic disorder, or confused with angioedema of either histaminergic or bradykinin origin.

Case presentation:

A man in his 40s presented to the emergency department reporting three weeks of worsening neck swelling and proximal muscle weakness affecting his hip flexors, along with mild muscle pain. He had no notable past medical conditions and had never smoked. He denied any use of 3-hydroxy-3-methyl-glutaryl-coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase inhibitors. The working diagnosis at first was Ludwig’s angina. A CT scan of the neck revealed generalized oedema of the strap muscles as well as inflammatory changes extending into the retropharyngeal space (see figure 1). On assessment, he was not febrile, maintained an oxygen saturation of 99% on room air, and was not breathing rapidly. Nailfold capillaroscopy detected both proximal nailfold microhaemorrhages and areas of avascularity. Laboratory findings on arrival showed a white blood cell count of 9.1×10^9/L and a C-reactive protein (CRP) of 55.6 mg/L. Since the neck swelling persisted without a clear etiology, he was referred to a quaternary care center with ear, nose, and throat (ENT) surgical capabilities for further assessment. An ENT evaluation, including flexible nasendoscopy, revealed no significant structural abnormalities in the soft palate, vallecula, larynx, or post-cricoid region, but the procedure was notable for increased secretions and provoked coughing.

The on-call immunologist was consulted due to suspected angioedema, given the absence of definitive structural or infectious etiologies. Upon further evaluation, the patient exhibited widespread facial erythema, including over the nasolabial folds, as well as a red, swollen, but non-tender neck. He reported not taking any medications, such as angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and stated no personal or family history of angioedema. Collectively, his presentation was not typical of either acquired or hereditary forms of angioedema. Detailed physical assessment identified subtle nailfold abnormalities indicative of periungual erythema, a rash present on the lateral aspect of the right thigh, and diminished hip flexor strength rated at 4/5. There were no signs of Gottron’s papules or heliotrope rash. Creatine kinase was ordered and, on the following day, was markedly increased at 16,184 U/L. Renal function testing returned within normal limits, and urine myoglobin screening was negative. Liver enzymes were raised as well, with ALT at 143 U/L, AST at 396 U/L, and LDH measuring 692 U/L.

Investigations:

Due to initial suspicion for angioedema, multiple investigations were undertaken to evaluate possible underlying causes of spontaneous angioedema. Both C3 and C4 complement components were within reference intervals, as were levels and function of C1 esterase.

Testing for autoantibodies revealed a positive anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) at a titre of 1:1280, displaying a speckled staining pattern. Anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA) testing was negative. A comprehensive myositis antibody profile, encompassing 16 antigens related to autoimmune inflammatory myopathies and assessed by the Euroimmun Euroline immunoassay, showed a marked positive result for anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibodies, with a signal intensity of 173 (reference <35). There were no antibodies detected against other myositis-related antigens, such as anti-Mi-2 alpha (anti-Mi-2α), anti–Transcription Intermediary Factor-1 gamma (anti-TIF1-γ), anti–Histidyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-Jo-1), or anti–Isoleucyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-OJ). Serum immunoglobulin G (IgG) and its subclasses were within normal limits. Regarding his infectious disease screening prior to immunosuppressive therapy, he tested negative for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), hepatitis C core antibody (HBcAb), hepatitis B e antibody (HBeAb), hepatitis C virus (HCV), human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), varicella zoster virus (VZV) IgG, as well as cytomegalovirus (CMV) IgG and IgM. Although his QuantiFERON-TB Gold test returned indeterminate, three sputum samples examined for Mycobacterium tuberculosis using acid-fast staining did not reveal any organisms.

Pulmonary function testing demonstrated an extrathoracic restrictive defect, without notable response to bronchodilator administration. Chest CT revealed several non-cavitating nodules predominantly located in the mid and lower lung zones, with the largest lesion measuring 15 mm in the right lower lobe, as well as patchy peribronchial consolidation consistent with possible aspiration pneumonitis; there were no imaging features suggestive of pulmonary fibrosis. Abdominal CT showed no abdomino-pelvic lymphadenopathy and no signs of a primary neoplasm or metastatic disease in the abdomen or pelvis. A video fluoroscopic barium swallow demonstrated marked pharyngeal dysphagia, resulting in significant residue within the pyriform fossae and post-swallow penetration of thin and mildly thick liquids to the level of the vocal cords.

Thigh MRI demonstrated findings indicative of active myositis involving the bilateral obturator internus and externus muscles, with less pronounced myositis seen in the hip adductors, both gracilis muscles, and bilateral distal iliopsoas muscles (figure 2). Additional evidence for ongoing muscle inflammation was provided by a fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)-PET scan, which revealed widespread abnormal FDG uptake across several muscle groups, most markedly in the head and neck regions, both deltoids, forearms, chest wall, abdomen, and pelvis (figure 3). There were no other regions with increased metabolic activity suggestive of malignancy. Serum tumour markers including carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), cancer antigen 19-9 (CA19-9), cancer antigen 125 (CA-125), and prostate-specific antigen (PSA) were within normal limits.

Histological examination of a thigh muscle biopsy identified areas with moderate numbers of necrotic, vacuolated degenerative, myophagocytosed, and regenerating muscle fibers displaying a perifascicular pattern, along with scattered perimysial inflammatory infiltrates composed of dispersed histiocytes (figure 4). Immunohistochemical staining for membrane attack complex demonstrated complement component 5b-9 (C5b-9) deposits within perifascicular endomysial capillaries, a characteristic finding of dermatomyositis. The patient was diagnosed with moderate to severe SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis accompanied by inflammatory edema, peribronchial consolidation due to micro-aspiration, dysphagia, and myositis. Cutaneous features included a positive Holster sign and periungual erythema.

Differential diagnosis:

Owing to the patient’s subacute onset of neck swelling accompanied by a raised CRP, Ludwig angina and idiopathic angioedema were considered as initial diagnostic possibilities. Muscle weakness, including difficulties in swallowing, was not significant in the early clinical course. The differential diagnosis extended to other inflammatory myopathies, such as immune-mediated necrotising myopathy and inclusion body myositis; infectious etiologies, including cellulitis or erysipelas of the neck, deep neck space infections, and bacterial sialadenitis; disorders of the neuromuscular junction like myasthenia gravis; delayed hypersensitivity reactions to drugs—such as drug rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms; acute contact dermatitis; orofacial granulomatosis; hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis; and superior vena cava syndrome.

Treatment:

Owing to marked involvement of his airway musculature, the patient received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dosage of 1000 mg per day for 3 days. This was followed by a prednisone taper, starting at 60 mg daily and reduced by 5 mg every 2 to 3 weeks. He was administered an initial intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG, Gamunex 10%) dose of 2 g/kg (totaling 165 g), distributed over 5 days (35 g daily), then received 5 additional cycles spaced 4 weeks apart, with the total 2 g/kg (165 g) dose divided across these cycles. Mycophenolate mofetil was initiated at 1 g twice daily, and two intravenous rituximab doses of 1000 mg each were given 2 weeks apart. Methotrexate was avoided because of its link to interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis—especially given the patient's abnormal pulmonary function—and after discussing its adverse effects with the patient. Mycophenolate was well-tolerated, so azathioprine and tacrolimus were not used. For prevention of Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim was started but later discontinued due to the onset of hemolytic anemia (without evidence of glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency); nebulized pentamidine, 300 mg every 4 weeks, was then used successfully.

He was discharged after a 21-day inpatient stay. His oral medications were administered through nasogastric tube as he had persistent dysphagia at the time of discharge.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following three weeks of inpatient care and therapy, the patient exhibited moderate recovery in hip flexor strength and significant reduction in cervical soft tissue swelling, although some swelling persisted upon discharge. Dysphagia remained, requiring continued nasogastric feeding for an additional two months.

At a follow-up visit three weeks post-discharge, proximal muscle strength was fully restored and serum creatine kinase levels had decreased to 37 U/L. During an immunology assessment two months after leaving the hospital, there was further improvement in cervical swelling, with examination revealing only lingering violaceous erythema of the face and periungual changes. Swallowing ability had advanced to the point that the nasogastric tube was discontinued and a transition to soft solids was possible. The patient continued to receive mycophenolate mofetil, monthly intravenous immunoglobulin, and a tapering regimen of prednisone.

At three months after discharge, the patient’s proximal muscle strength remained unchanged, the chest exam was within normal limits, and swelling of the cervical soft tissues had fully subsided. Prednisone was tapered off successfully seven months post-discharge, with full resolution of dysphagia noted at that point. Pulmonary function tests repeated at that time indicated slight improvement, though a persistent restrictive pattern was present, thought to correspond to the patient’s baseline physiology (see figure 5a, b). Chest CT obtained at eleven months revealed near-total resolution of the basal ground-glass opacities and airspace consolidations that had previously been linked to aspiration. At twelve months after the initial diagnosis, the patient’s respiratory function remained steady, with preserved exercise tolerance.

Regarding malignancy surveillance, the patient continued to deny any constitutional symptoms at follow-up, and monitoring will proceed based on reported symptoms, ongoing physical examination, serial blood analyses, and imaging, including repeat chest CT. Both gastroscopy and colonoscopy have been planned to rule out underlying gastrointestinal or colorectal cancer.

The treatment plan included ongoing IVIG therapy, with a scheduled tapering of mycophenolate dosage over the next six months depending on his clinical response. He will be monitored closely for any indication to modify his current treatment regimen.

Discussion:

We present a case involving dermatomyositis associated with anti-SAE1 antibodies that manifested with significant cervical soft tissue edema, which was initially diagnosed as idiopathic angioedema. The diagnosis of dermatomyositis was considered after a thorough physical examination uncovered subtle dermatological findings, enabling prompt initiation of appropriate therapy.

Anti-SAE1 antibodies target the small ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) activating enzyme, a protein with structural similarities to ubiquitin and plays a role in the sumoylation process—a type of post-translational modification affecting multiple protein substrates. Patients with dermatomyositis testing positive for SAE1 antibodies frequently display distinctive skin findings, including heliotrope rash, Gottron’s papules, the V-neck sign, and the shawl sign. According to a comprehensive review of 215 cases reported in the literature, dysphagia was observed in 46% and interstitial lung disease in 43% of patients, while malignancies were found in 18% of cases with SAE1-positive dermatomyositis.

Oedema of the subcutaneous tissue, particularly presenting as swelling of the face or neck, is rarely observed in dermatomyositis. In a comprehensive study involving 121 dermatomyositis patients, angioedema was identified in only 8 individuals (6.61%). A review of literature spanning 1985 to 2013 identified just 19 reported cases where oedema accompanied dermatomyositis. Certain antibodies, notably anti-melanoma differentiation-associated gene (MDA)-5 and anti-transcriptional intermediary factor 1γ (TIF1-γ), have been found to have a higher association with angioedema compared to other antibodies. While facial oedema has been noted in several reports, descriptions specifically citing neck oedema are less prevalent. In our review encompassing 10 cases of SAE1-antibody positive dermatomyositis, only three referenced oedema, with merely one case mentioning swelling localized to the throat, highlighting the rarity of neck involvement.79

This case highlights for emergency physicians, ENT specialists, immunologists, and rheumatologists that dermatomyositis should be considered among the differential diagnoses for neck swelling, particularly when localized ‘angioedema’ has no apparent cause or is idiopathic. Because symptoms such as dysphagia or involvement of other areas can be mild, careful assessment for cutaneous and muscular manifestations of dermatomyositis and measurement of creatine kinase are essential steps. In this patient, subcutaneous edema was attributable to muscle inflammation associated with myositis, rather than to histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema.

Following the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS) International Guideline on Cancer Screening in Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathies, the patient was found to have two high-risk features (presence of dermatomyositis, dysphagia) and two intermediate-risk factors (positive SAE1 antibody, male gender), categorizing him as high risk for underlying malignancy. Malignancy screening was undertaken with CT imaging, tumor marker assays, and a PET scan, along with planned upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopy. Thorough initial evaluations and ongoing surveillance have continued to provide reassuring results.

Current data regarding the best pharmacological management for anti-SAE1–positive dermatomyositis remains sparse. In the most extensive cohort reported so far, therapeutic strategies varied; the majority of individuals received glucocorticoids together with diverse steroid-sparing agents, most frequently methotrexate, but also mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, and azathioprine. Intravenous immunoglobulin was administered in certain cases. Our patient was managed with a combination of steroids, mycophenolate mofetil, intravenous immunoglobulin, and rituximab, achieving remission. Mycophenolate was chosen owing to the patient's preference for a daily oral medication, its favorable tolerance profile, and its established role in treating dermatomyositis. Compared to other cases featuring marked tissue oedema associated with dermatomyositis, our patient's prognosis is favorable.
