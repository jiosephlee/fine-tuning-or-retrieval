Title: Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook

Kevin Chan, Gemma Kwan, Hwei Choo Soh, Louise Evans

Abstract:

Summary: Dermatomyositis is an uncommon autoimmune condition primarily involving the skin, skeletal muscle, and respiratory tract. Rapid initiation of potent immunosuppressive therapy is usually necessary in dermatomyositis to prevent further disease advancement and injury to multiple organ systems. A male in his 40s with no significant prior medical history presented to the emergency department experiencing pronounced neck swelling. Initially, otolaryngology was consulted for assessment of a possible deep neck space infection. After infectious causes were excluded, the patient was referred to clinical immunology to evaluate possible idiopathic angioedema. Owing to the atypical features of his cervical edema—which did not conform to classic angioedema—and with concurrent dermatologic and nail findings, a creatine kinase measurement was rapidly performed. The provisional diagnosis of dermatomyositis was made, later confirmed by detection of a small ubiquitin-like modifier-1 activating enzyme (SAE1) autoantibody and characteristic findings on muscle biopsy. This report underscores an atypical, non-histamine and non-bradykinin-driven cervical swelling as a presenting symptom of dermatomyositis, associated with dysphagia and extensive myopathic involvement requiring urgent intervention.

Background:

Dermatomyositis is an idiopathic inflammatory myopathy most commonly impacting the skin and muscles, though involvement of the gastrointestinal system, joints, lungs, and heart may occur. The incidence is reported to be 10 per million individuals. The disease exhibits a bimodal age distribution, predominantly manifesting in children between 5–14 years of age and adults from 45–64 years. Classic dermatologic signs include Gottron’s papules, shawl sign, heliotrope rash, and periungual telangiectasias. Additional clinical features are proximal muscle weakness, interstitial lung disease, and difficulty swallowing. Pseudoangioedema represents a rare manifestation in dermatomyositis, as evidenced by a study that identified this finding in only 8 of 121 dermatomyositis patients. When pseudoangioedema affects the neck region, it may present similarly to primary otolaryngology conditions, or be erroneously attributed to histamine- or bradykinin-induced angioedema.

Case presentation:

A man in his 40s presented to the emergency department with a three-week history of worsening neck swelling and proximal muscle weakness, particularly affecting his hip flexors, as well as mild muscle pain. He did not have any notable past medical history and had never smoked. He was not receiving any 3-hydroxy-3-methyl-glutaryl-coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase inhibitor therapy. Ludwig’s angina was initially suspected. A CT scan of the neck revealed generalized swelling of the neck with involvement of the strap muscles and inflammation that extended into the retropharyngeal space (see figure 1). The patient was afebrile, had an oxygen saturation of 99% on ambient air, and was not tachypneic. Nailfold capillaroscopy identified proximal nailfold microhaemorrhages and avascular regions. On presentation, his white blood cell count was 9.1×10^9/L and his C-reactive protein (CRP) measured 55.6 mg/L. As the cause of the persistent neck swelling remained undetermined, he was referred to a quaternary center with ear, nose, and throat (ENT) surgical expertise for additional assessment. ENT evaluation using flexible nasendoscopy demonstrated no noteworthy anatomical anomalies of the soft palate, vallecula, larynx, or post-cricoid area; however, there was an increase in secretions and the patient coughed during the procedure.

The on-call immunologist was consulted regarding possible angioedema, as no definite structural or infectious etiology was identified. Upon assessment, the patient displayed diffuse facial erythema extending to the nasolabial folds, alongside a swollen, erythematous, but non-tender neck. He reported not taking any medications, specifically denying use of angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and lacked both personal and family history of angioedema. His presentation was not consistent with typical patterns of acquired or hereditary angioedema. Detailed examination identified subtle periungual erythema at the nailfolds, a rash on the right lateral thigh, and diminished hip flexor strength measured at 4/5. There was no evidence of Gottron’s papules or a heliotrope rash. Creatine kinase testing was ordered and, on the following day, revealed a markedly elevated result of 16,184 U/L. Renal parameters were within normal limits, and urine screening for myoglobin was negative. The patient also demonstrated increased transaminase levels, including alanine aminotransferase (ALT) at 143 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) at 396 U/L, and an elevated lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) of 692 U/L.

Investigations:

With suspicion for angioedema, a series of investigations were undertaken to identify possible etiologies of spontaneous angioedema. Complement components C3 and C4 were within normal limits, as were both functional and quantitative measurements of C1 esterase.

Antinuclear antibody (ANA) testing returned positive, with a titer of 1:1280 and a speckled pattern. Anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA) results were negative. A myositis antibody panel targeting 16 antigens associated with autoimmune inflammatory myopathy was performed using the Euroimmun Euroline immunoassay. This revealed a strong positivity for anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibodies, with a signal intensity of 173 (reference range <35). All other myositis antibodies, including anti-Mi-2 alpha (anti-Mi-2α), anti–Transcription Intermediary Factor-1 gamma (anti-TIF1-γ), anti–Histidyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-Jo-1), and anti–Isoleucyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-OJ), were negative. Immunoglobulin G (IgG) and its subclasses measured in the normal range.

For immunosuppression screening, the patient tested negative for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), hepatitis B core antibody (HBcAb), hepatitis B e antibody (HBeAb), hepatitis C virus (HCV), HIV, varicella zoster virus (VZV) IgG, and cytomegalovirus (CMV) IgG/IgM. The QuantiFERON-TB Gold result was indeterminate, but three sputum samples for acid-fast bacilli, specifically Mycobacterium tuberculosis, were all negative.

Pulmonary function testing demonstrated an extrathoracic restrictive pattern without a notable bronchodilator effect. Chest CT revealed several non-cavitating pulmonary nodules predominately located in the mid to lower lung zones, the largest measuring 15 mm in the right lower lobe. There were also patchy regions of peribronchial consolidation, suggestive of aspiration pneumonitis, while imaging did not indicate pulmonary fibrosis. Abdominal CT showed no abdomino-pelvic lymphadenopathy or any signs of primary or metastatic cancer within the abdomen or pelvis. A video fluoroscopic barium swallow examination identified severe pharyngeal dysphagia, resulting in substantial residue within the pyriform fossae and post-swallow penetration of both thin and mildly thick fluids onto the vocal cords.

MRI scans of the thighs revealed findings indicative of active myositis affecting the bilateral obturator internus and externus muscles, with less pronounced inflammation seen in the hip adductors, both gracilis muscles, and bilateral distal iliopsoas muscles (figure 2). Evidence of ongoing muscle inflammation was further corroborated by a fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)-positron emission tomography (PET) scan, which demonstrated diffuse, abnormal FDG uptake throughout numerous muscle groups, with the highest concentration observed in the head and neck regions, bilateral deltoids, forearms, chest wall, abdominal area, and pelvis (figure 3). No metabolically active lesions were detected elsewhere to suggest a malignancy. Levels of tumor markers, including carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), cancer antigen 19-9 (CA19-9), cancer antigen 125 (CA-125), and prostate-specific antigen (PSA), were found to be within normal limits.

Thigh muscle biopsy revealed areas containing moderate numbers of necrotic, vacuolated, degenerative, myophagocytosed, and regenerating muscle fibers, arranged in a perifascicular pattern, as well as scattered perimysial inflammation made up of dispersed histiocytes (figure 4). Immunohistochemical staining for membrane attack complex indicated complement component 5b-9 (C5b-9) deposits in perifascicular endomysial capillaries, a characteristic feature of dermatomyositis. The diagnosis reached was moderate to severe SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis, associated with inflammatory edema, peribronchial consolidation secondary to micro-aspiration, dysphagia, and myositis. Skin findings included a positive Holster sign and periungual erythema.

Differential diagnosis:

Due to his gradually progressing symptoms with cervical swelling and increased CRP levels, Ludwig angina and idiopathic angioedema were initially considered as potential diagnoses. At the outset, muscle weakness—including difficulty swallowing—was not notably observed. The differential diagnosis was broad and encompassed other inflammatory muscle diseases (such as immune-mediated necrotising myopathy and inclusion body myositis), infectious etiologies (including cellulitis or erysipelas localized to the neck, infections within deep neck spaces, and bacterial sialadenitis), neuromuscular junction pathologies like myasthenia gravis, delayed hypersensitivity reactions to medications (for example, drug rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms), acute contact dermatitis, orofacial granulomatosis, hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis, and superior vena cava syndrome.

Treatment:

Because of pronounced involvement of the airway musculature, the patient received intravenous methylprednisolone at 1000 mg daily for 3 days. Subsequently, he was transitioned to a tapering course of oral prednisone, beginning at 60 mg each day and reduced by 5 mg every 2 to 3 weeks. A loading dose of intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG, Gamunex 10%) was administered at 2 g/kg, totaling 165 g, divided over 5 days (35 g per day), and followed by an additional 5 cycles spaced 4 weeks apart, again at a cumulative dose of 2 g/kg (165 g) divided between these sessions. Mycophenolate mofetil was started at 1 g twice daily, alongside two intravenous doses of rituximab at 1000 mg each, separated by 2 weeks. Methotrexate was avoided due to its known association with interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis, particularly since the patient demonstrated abnormal pulmonary function; this decision was made collaboratively with the patient, considering the risk profile. Azathioprine and tacrolimus were not employed given good tolerance to mycophenolate. For prophylaxis against Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim was initially used, but discontinued due to haemolytic anaemia unrelated to glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency. He subsequently tolerated inhaled pentamidine at 300 mg every 4 weeks.

After 21 days as an inpatient, he was discharged. At the time of discharge, oral medications continued to be given via nasogastric tube, owing to ongoing dysphagia.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following three weeks of inpatient care and therapy, the patient exhibited partial recovery of hip flexor strength and significant reduction in cervical soft tissue edema, though complete resolution had not been achieved by the time of discharge. Dysphagia continued, requiring nasogastric tube nutrition, which was maintained for an additional two months.

At a follow-up visit three weeks post-discharge, proximal muscle strength was fully restored and serum creatine kinase had decreased to 37 U/L. During an immunology assessment two months after discharge, cervical swelling was noted to be further subsiding, with only lingering violaceous facial erythema and periungual abnormalities visible on examination. Swallowing had improved enough to discontinue the nasogastric tube and transition to a diet of soft foods. The patient remained on mycophenolate mofetil, monthly intravenous immunoglobulin, and a gradually reduced regimen of prednisone.

Three months after discharge, the patient’s proximal muscle strength remained stable, the chest examination was unremarkable, and the cervical soft tissue swelling had resolved entirely. Seven months following discharge, prednisone was discontinued without issues, and by this time, the dysphagia had resolved. Subsequent pulmonary function tests revealed mild improvement, though a persistent restrictive pattern was interpreted as likely representing his baseline physiology (figure 5a, b). A CT scan of the chest at 11 months showed near-complete resolution of the previously seen basal ground-glass opacities and airspace consolidation, which were originally thought to result from aspiration. At 12 months post-diagnosis, his respiratory function was stable and he maintained good exercise tolerance.

With regard to malignancy, the patient reported no constitutional symptoms during follow-up. Ongoing surveillance will involve symptom assessment, clinical examination, routine blood tests, and appropriate imaging, including serial chest CT scans. He is scheduled for a gastroscopy and colonoscopy to evaluate for occult gastrointestinal or colorectal malignancies.

The management plan includes continuing IVIG therapy, with a gradual reduction of the mycophenolate dose over the next six months based on clinical response. The patient will receive close follow-up to assess whether adjustments to his medication regimen are necessary.

Discussion:

We present a patient with dermatomyositis testing positive for anti-SAE1 antibodies who manifested with marked cervical soft tissue edema, which was initially misdiagnosed as idiopathic angioedema. The diagnosis was established upon considering dermatomyositis among the differential diagnoses and conducting a thorough physical examination that revealed subtle dermatological findings, enabling prompt initiation of therapy.

Anti-SAE1 antibodies are directed toward the small-ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) activating enzyme—a molecule analogous in structure to ubiquitin and implicated in the ‘sumoylation’ post-translational modification of various proteins. Dermatomyositis associated with SAE1 antibodies typically displays skin manifestations including heliotrope rash, Gottron’s papules, the V-neck sign, and the shawl sign. A comprehensive review including 215 individuals with this condition found that dysphagia was noted in 46% of cases and interstitial lung disease in 43%, whereas malignancy was identified in 18% of patients.

Swelling of subcutaneous tissues, particularly in the face or neck, is rarely observed in patients with dermatomyositis. In a large observational study involving 121 individuals diagnosed with dermatomyositis, just 8 cases (6.61%) exhibited angioedema. A review of published reports from 1985 through 2013 identified only 19 instances in which dermatomyositis presented with oedema. Certain autoantibodies, including anti-melanoma differentiation-associated gene (MDA)-5 and anti-transcriptional intermediary factor 1γ (TIF1-γ), were noted to have a greater association with angioedema than other antibodies. While facial oedema has been reported in several cases, swelling specifically affecting the neck has been less frequently documented. Our review of 10 cases featuring SAE1-positive dermatomyositis revealed that oedema was mentioned in only three, and just one described swelling located near the throat, underscoring the rarity of neck involvement.79

This case highlights the importance for emergency physicians, ENT specialists, immunologists, and rheumatologists to consider dermatomyositis as a differential diagnosis in cases of neck swelling, particularly when the swelling presents as localized, idiopathic ‘angioedema’. Because symptoms such as dysphagia and involvement of other systems can be mild or easily overlooked, thorough evaluation for clinical features of dermatomyositis and assessment of creatine kinase levels are essential. In this patient, the presence of subcutaneous oedema was attributed to inflammatory changes from myositis rather than from mechanisms related to histamine or bradykinin in angioedema.

Following the recommendations of the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS) for screening for malignancy in idiopathic inflammatory myopathies, the patient was classified as high risk, possessing two major risk factors (dermatomyositis and dysphagia) and two intermediate risk factors (positive SAE1 antibody and male gender). As a result, he was evaluated with malignancy workup, including CT imaging, tumour marker testing, and PET scanning, with planned upper and lower GI endoscopies. Initial and ongoing surveillance assessments have so far been reassuring.

The available data on the best pharmacological management for anti-SAE1–positive dermatomyositis remain sparse. The largest documented patient group demonstrated varied treatment strategies, with the majority administered glucocorticoids alongside a selection of steroid-sparing medications, predominantly methotrexate, as well as mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, and azathioprine; intravenous immunoglobulin was reserved for particular individuals. In this case, the patient received steroids, mycophenolate mofetil, intravenous immunoglobulin, and rituximab, resulting in remission. The decision to use mycophenolate mofetil was influenced by the patient’s preference for a daily oral therapy, along with its favorable tolerability profile and its established application in dermatomyositis. Compared with others exhibiting notable tissue edema associated with dermatomyositis, our patient has fortunately demonstrated a relatively favorable prognosis.
