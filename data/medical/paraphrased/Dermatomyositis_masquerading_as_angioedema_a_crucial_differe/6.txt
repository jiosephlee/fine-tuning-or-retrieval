Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook

Kevin Chan, Gemma Kwan, Hwei Choo Soh, Louise Evans

Abstract:

Summary: Dermatomyositis is an uncommon autoimmune disorder primarily involving the skin, musculature, and the respiratory tract. Rapid initiation of high-dose immunosuppressive therapy is often necessary to halt disease progression and prevent damage to multiple organ systems. This report describes a previously healthy man in his 40s who presented to the emergency department with marked cervical swelling. Initially, ear, nose, and throat specialists evaluated him for a likely deep neck space infection. After infectious causes were excluded, he was referred to clinical immunology for assessment and management of presumed idiopathic angioedema. Given the atypical presentation of neck oedema—at odds with standard angioedema—accompanied by cutaneous and nail abnormalities, a creatine kinase assay was expedited. The patient received a preliminary diagnosis of dermatomyositis, further corroborated by the identification of the small ubiquitin-like modifier-1 activating enzyme (SAE1) autoantibody and characteristic findings on muscle biopsy. This case underscores neck swelling not mediated by histamine or bradykinin as a rare clinical manifestation of dermatomyositis, which in this instance was associated with dysphagia and extensive muscle involvement, necessitating urgent intervention.

Background:

Dermatomyositis is an inflammatory myopathy of unknown cause that primarily targets the skin and musculature, although involvement of organs such as the gastrointestinal tract, joints, lungs, and heart has also been reported. The condition is estimated to occur at a rate of 10 new cases per million individuals. Incidence rates reveal a bimodal age distribution, with most cases arising in children between 5–14 years and adults from 45–64 years. Dermatological hallmarks encompass Gottron’s papules, the shawl sign, heliotrope rash, and periungual telangiectasias. Additional clinical features comprise proximal muscle weakness, interstitial lung pathology, and difficulty swallowing. Pseudoangioedema is a rare manifestation, documented in only 8 of 121 individuals with dermatomyositis in a particular study. When present in the cervical region, it may be misinterpreted as an isolated otolaryngologic pathology or confused with angioedema induced by histaminergic or bradykinin mechanisms.

Case presentation:

A man in his forties presented to the emergency department reporting a three-week progression of neck swelling and proximal muscle weakness, particularly affecting the hip flexors, as well as mild myalgia. His past medical history was unremarkable and he had never smoked. He was not using any 3-hydroxy-3-methyl-glutaryl-coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase inhibitors. The initial working diagnosis was Ludwig’s angina, prompting a CT scan of the neck, which revealed widespread oedema of the neck, particularly involving the strap muscles, and inflammatory changes extending into the retropharyngeal space (see figure 1). Upon examination, he was afebrile, maintained oxygen saturations at 99% on ambient air, and did not exhibit tachypnoea. Nailfold capillaroscopy identified proximal nailfold microhaemorrhages and areas lacking vascularisation. Laboratory evaluation demonstrated a white blood cell count of 9.1×10\textsuperscript{9}/L and an elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) level of 55.6 mg/L at the time of admission. Persistent neck swelling of unknown etiology led to his transfer to a quaternary care hospital with specialized ear, nose, and throat (ENT) surgical services for additional assessment. Subsequent flexible nasendoscopy performed by ENT specialists revealed no notable structural abnormalities in the soft palate, vallecula, larynx, or post-cricoid region, though examination was notable for increased secretions and episodic coughing.

The clinical team consulted the on-call immunologist regarding suspected angioedema, given the absence of definitive structural or infectious etiologies. Upon further evaluation, the patient exhibited widespread facial erythema—including over the nasolabial folds—and a neck that appeared erythematous, swollen, but non-tender. He reported not using any medications such as angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs. There was also no reported personal or family history of angioedema. Based on his clinical presentation, neither acquired nor hereditary forms of angioedema appeared likely. Detailed examination revealed discrete periungual erythema at the nailfolds, a rash over the right lateral thigh, and reduced strength in hip flexion measured at 4/5. Classical features such as Gottron’s papules and heliotrope rash were not observed. A serum creatine kinase assay was ordered, returning markedly increased the following day at 16 184 U/L. Assessment of renal function revealed normal results, and urine screening for myoglobin was negative. Additionally, liver enzymes were elevated with alanine aminotransferase (ALT) at 143 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) at 396 U/L, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) at 692 U/L.

Investigations:

Due to the initial suspicion of angioedema, a series of investigations were conducted to identify possible etiologies of spontaneous angioedema. The patient's C3 and C4 complement concentrations were found to be within the reference ranges, and both the quantitative assessment and functional activity of C1 esterase were also normal.

Serological testing revealed a positive anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) at a titre of 1:1280, displaying a speckled immunofluorescence pattern. Tests for anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA) were negative. A myositis antibody panel comprising 16 antigens associated with autoimmune inflammatory myopathies, analyzed via the Euroimmun Euroline immunoassay, demonstrated a strong positive result for anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibodies, with a signal intensity measured at 173 (normal <35). The panel yielded negative results for additional myositis-associated autoantibodies, including anti-Mi-2 alpha (anti-Mi-2α), anti-Transcription Intermediary Factor-1 gamma (anti-TIF1-γ), anti–Histidyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-Jo-1), and anti–Isoleucyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-OJ). Immunoglobulin G (IgG) and its subclasses were within the expected ranges.

Regarding immunosuppressive screening, results for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), hepatitis C core antibody (HBcAb), hepatitis B e antibody (HBeAb), hepatitis C virus (HCV), human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), varicella zoster virus (VZV) IgG, and cytomegalovirus (CMV) IgG and immunoglobulin M (IgM) were all negative. The QuantiFERON-TB Gold test was indeterminate, but three separate sputum samples tested negative for acid-fast bacilli consistent with Mycobacterium tuberculosis.

Pulmonary function assessments indicated a restrictive defect of extrathoracic origin, without notable improvement following bronchodilator administration. Chest CT imaging identified several non-cavitating nodules in the lungs, predominantly located in the mid to lower zones (with the largest nodule measuring 15 mm in the right lower lobe), as well as patchy peribronchial consolidation, probably attributable to aspiration pneumonitis; there was no radiological indication of pulmonary fibrosis. Abdominal CT scanning did not demonstrate any enlargement of abdomino-pelvic lymph nodes nor evidence of either primary or metastatic cancer in the abdomen or pelvis. The video fluoroscopic barium swallow examination revealed marked pharyngeal dysphagia, resulting in considerable residue in the pyriform fossae and post-swallow penetration of thin and mildly thickened liquids reaching the level of the vocal cords.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the thighs revealed findings compatible with active myositis, particularly affecting the bilateral obturator internus and externus muscles, alongside less severe involvement of the hip adductors, bilateral gracilis, and distal iliopsoas muscles (see figure 2). Additional evidence of active muscle inflammation was obtained via fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)-positron emission tomography (PET) scan, which demonstrated diffuse, abnormal FDG uptake across numerous muscle groups, with the most pronounced activity observed in the head and neck, both deltoids, forearms, chest wall, abdomen, and pelvic regions (figure 3). No FDG-avid lesions were identified elsewhere that would indicate a neoplastic process. Screening for tumor markers—carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), cancer antigen 19-9 (CA19-9), cancer antigen 125 (CA-125), and prostate-specific antigen (PSA)—returned results within normal parameters.

Histopathological examination of a thigh muscle biopsy showed perifascicular areas containing moderate numbers of necrotic, vacuolated degenerated, myophagocytosed, and regenerating myofibers, accompanied by patchy perimysial inflammation featuring dispersed histiocytes (see figure 4). Immunohistochemical staining for membrane attack complex identified deposition of complement component 5b-9 (C5b-9) in perifascicular endomysial capillaries, a histological hallmark of dermatomyositis. These findings led to a diagnosis of moderate to severe SAE1 antibody–positive dermatomyositis, characterized by inflammatory edema, peribronchial consolidation as a result of micro-aspiration, dysphagia, and myositis. Skin involvement was evidenced by the presence of a positive Holster sign and periungual erythema.

Differential diagnosis:

Because the patient presented subacutely with cervical swelling and an increased CRP level, initial diagnostic considerations focused on Ludwig angina and idiopathic angioedema. Notably, muscle weakness and dysphagia were not marked during the initial evaluation. The differential diagnosis also encompassed other inflammatory myopathies (such as immune-mediated necrotising myopathy and inclusion body myositis), infectious etiologies (including cellulitis or erysipelas of the neck, deep neck space infections, and bacterial sialadenitis), as well as neuromuscular junction disorders like myasthenia gravis. Additional possibilities included delayed drug hypersensitivity reactions (for example, drug rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms), acute contact dermatitis, orofacial granulomatosis, hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis, and superior vena cava syndrome.

Treatment:

Owing to extensive involvement of the airway musculature, the patient received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for 3 days. This was subsequently transitioned to a prednisone regimen beginning at 60 mg per day, with a taper by 5 mg every 2 to 3 weeks. Initial loading of intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) (Gamunex 10%) was administered at 2 g/kg (equivalent to a total of 165 g) over 5 days (35 g per day), and this was followed by 5 additional cycles at 4-week intervals, each totaling 2 g/kg (=165 g) distributed across the subsequent doses. Mycophenolate mofetil therapy was initiated at 1 g twice daily, and two intravenous rituximab infusions (1000 mg each) were provided 2 weeks apart. Methotrexate was avoided given its established link with interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis—considering the patient’s abnormal pulmonary function results and the shared decision-making process regarding the risk profile. Mycophenolate was well-tolerated, thus azathioprine and tacrolimus were not pursued. For prophylaxis against Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim was started, but discontinued owing to development of haemolytic anaemia (without evidence of glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency); the patient later successfully received nebulised pentamidine at 300 mg every 4 weeks.

After a 21-day hospitalization, he was discharged. Due to ongoing dysphagia, all oral medications were administered via nasogastric tube at the time of discharge.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following a three-week period of hospitalisation and therapy, the patient showed partial recovery of hip flexor strength and significant reduction in cervical soft tissue edema, although the swelling had not completely subsided by discharge. Dysphagia persisted, making ongoing nasogastric feeding necessary for an additional two months.

Upon reassessment three weeks after leaving the hospital, the patient’s proximal muscle strength had returned to normal and serum creatine kinase levels had decreased to 37 U/L. At an immunology evaluation two months post-discharge, there was further regression of cervical swelling, with only mild residual violaceous erythema on the face and periungual changes observed during examination. The ability to swallow had improved, allowing removal of the nasogastric tube and advancement to a diet of soft solids. The patient continued treatment with mycophenolate mofetil, monthly intravenous immunoglobulin, and a tapering regimen of prednisone.

Three months after hospital discharge, the patient’s proximal muscle strength remained unchanged, chest examination findings were within normal limits, and the cervical soft tissue swelling had fully subsided. Prednisone therapy was able to be stopped 7 months after discharge, at which point dysphagia had also completely resolved. Subsequent pulmonary function testing indicated a slight improvement, though a persistent restrictive pattern remained that was considered likely to represent his baseline physiology (figure 5a, b). An 11-month follow-up CT scan of the chest revealed almost complete resolution of the basal ground-glass opacities and airspace consolidation previously observed, which had initially been linked to aspiration. At the 12-month mark post-diagnosis, his respiratory status was stable and he exhibited satisfactory exercise tolerance.

With regard to oncological surveillance, the patient did not report any constitutional symptoms during follow-up and will undergo ongoing monitoring with symptom assessment, physical examination, routine blood work, and appropriate imaging, including serial CT scans of the chest. Additionally, a gastroscopy and colonoscopy are planned to rule out occult malignancies of the gastrointestinal or colorectal tract.

Management moving forward involves the continuation of IVIG therapy, while a gradual reduction in the dose of mycophenolate will be considered over the next 6 months, guided by clinical response. He will remain under close observation to assess whether modifications in his medication regimen are necessary.

Discussion:

We describe a case of dermatomyositis associated with anti-SAE1 antibodies that manifested primarily as significant cervical soft tissue swelling, initially misdiagnosed as idiopathic angioedema. Prompt consideration of dermatomyositis within the differential diagnosis, along with meticulous physical examination that identified faint cutaneous findings, enabled accurate and timely diagnosis as well as appropriate therapeutic intervention.

Anti-SAE1 antibodies are directed against the small-ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) activating enzyme, a protein closely related in structure to ubiquitin, and participate in the post-translational modification of various substrate proteins via ‘sumoylation’. The clinical phenotype of SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis frequently includes skin manifestations such as heliotrope rash, Gottron’s papules, V-neck sign, and shawl sign. According to an extensive review of the literature comprising 215 individuals, dysphagia and interstitial lung disease were noted in 46% and 43% of SAE1 dermatomyositis cases, respectively, and malignancies were identified in 18%.

Oedema of the subcutaneous tissue, notably presenting as swelling in the face or neck, occurs infrequently in patients with dermatomyositis. In a substantial cohort analysis involving 121 dermatomyositis cases, just 8 individuals (6.61%) experienced angioedema. Furthermore, a review of the literature spanning from 1985 to 2013 identified merely 19 documented instances where dermatomyositis was associated with oedema. Certain antibodies, particularly anti-melanoma differentiation-associated gene (MDA)-5 antibody and anti-transcriptional intermediary factor 1γ (TIF1-γ) antibody, have been observed to have a stronger correlation with angioedema compared to other autoantibodies. Reports of facial oedema were relatively more prevalent, whereas descriptions specifically referring to neck swelling were notably less common. In our analysis of 10 published cases of SAE1-positive dermatomyositis, only three detailed oedema, and just one involved swelling localized to the throat area, highlighting the rarity of neck region involvement.79

This case highlights the importance for emergency physicians, ENT specialists, immunologists, and rheumatologists to consider dermatomyositis as a potential differential diagnosis when confronted with neck swelling, particularly when faced with localized 'angioedema' that lacks an apparent cause or is considered idiopathic. Since manifestations such as dysphagia and involvement of other organ systems can present subtly, it is essential to assess for the clinical hallmarks of dermatomyositis and to measure creatine kinase levels early in the diagnostic process. In this instance, the subcutaneous oedema observed in the patient was secondary to inflammatory myositis within the musculature, distinguishing it from angioedema etiologies linked to histaminergic or bradykinin pathways.

Following the International Guideline for Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathy-Associated Cancer Screening, as set forth by the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS), the patient’s profile included two high risk features (diagnosed dermatomyositis and dysphagia) along with two intermediate risk factors (SAE1 antibody positivity and male gender), categorizing him as high risk for associated malignancy. Consequently, comprehensive malignancy screening was initiated, including CT imaging, tumour marker assays, and a PET scan, alongside scheduled upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopies. Both the initial and follow-up surveillance have so far not indicated the presence of malignancy.

Current data on the most effective pharmacological strategies for anti-SAE1–positive dermatomyositis remain sparse. In the most extensive cohort reported thus far, therapeutic regimens were variable; the majority of individuals were managed with glucocorticoids alongside various steroid-sparing medications—most frequently methotrexate, though agents such as mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, and azathioprine were also employed. Intravenous immunoglobulin was reserved for specific situations. In our case, the patient received a combination of steroids, mycophenolate mofetil, intravenous immunoglobulin, and rituximab, leading to remission. The decision to initiate mycophenolate mofetil was influenced by the patient’s preference for a once-daily oral therapy, as well as its favorable tolerability profile and recognized effectiveness in dermatomyositis management. Notably, this patient’s outlook appears more favorable compared to others who experience considerable tissue oedema associated with dermatomyositis.
