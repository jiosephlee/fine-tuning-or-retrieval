Title: Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook

Kevin Chan, Gemma Kwan, Hwei Choo Soh, Louise Evans

Abstract:

Summary: Dermatomyositis is an uncommon autoimmune disorder that most frequently involves the skin, skeletal muscle, and respiratory tract. Individuals affected by dermatomyositis generally necessitate rapid initiation of high-intensity immunosuppressive therapy to halt disease progression and prevent damage to multiple organs. A male patient in his 40s with no significant previous medical history presented to the emergency department with marked swelling of the neck. He was initially assessed by otolaryngology specialists for a suspected deep neck space infection. After infectious causes were ruled out, he was subsequently referred to clinical immunology for workup and management of possible idiopathic angioedema. Due to the unusual presentation of neck edema, which did not fit the clinical profile of angioedema and was accompanied by cutaneous and periungual abnormalities, creatine kinase testing was performed early in the course. The working diagnosis of dermatomyositis was made and later confirmed by detection of the small ubiquitin-like modifier-1 activating enzyme (SAE1) autoantibody and characteristic muscle biopsy findings. This case underscores a rare manifestation of dermatomyositis presenting as neck edema unrelated to histamine or bradykinin pathways, which led to dysphagia and substantial muscle involvement necessitating urgent therapeutic intervention.

Background:

Dermatomyositis is a myopathy of unknown cause characterized by inflammation, most often impacting the skin and skeletal muscles, but may also involve the gastrointestinal system, joints, pulmonary structures, and the heart. The condition occurs at an estimated rate of 10 cases per million individuals. Its incidence displays two prominent peaks, primarily affecting children between 5 and 14 years of age, as well as adults between the ages of 45 and 64 years. Typical dermatologic manifestations include Gottron’s papules, the shawl sign, heliotrope rash, and periungual telangiectasias. Additional clinical signs are proximal muscle weakness, interstitial lung involvement, and difficulty swallowing. Pseudoangioedema represents a rare manifestation in dermatomyositis; a particular study identified this feature in just 8 out of 121 affected patients. When localized to the neck, it may mimic disorders typically managed by otolaryngology, or be confused with angioedema resulting from histamine or bradykinin pathways.

Case presentation:

A man in his 40s presented to the emergency department reporting three weeks of gradually worsening neck swelling and proximal muscle weakness affecting his hip flexors, accompanied by mild muscle aches. He had no notable past medical history and had never smoked. He was not on any 3-hydroxy-3-methyl-glutaryl-coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase inhibitor therapy. Ludwig’s angina was initially suspected, so a CT scan of the neck was performed, revealing widespread neck edema involving the strap muscles and inflammatory changes reaching the retropharyngeal space (figure 1). He did not have a fever, maintained oxygen saturation at 99% on ambient air, and was not experiencing tachypnea. On nailfold capillaroscopy, proximal nailfold microhaemorrhages with avascular areas were observed. Laboratory assessment showed a white cell count of 9.1×10⁹/L and a C-reactive protein (CRP) level of 55.6 mg/L upon arrival. Because the neck swelling persisted without an identifiable cause, he was referred to a quaternary hospital with ear, nose and throat (ENT) surgical capabilities for further investigation. ENT performed a flexible nasendoscopy, which did not demonstrate significant anatomical abnormalities in the soft palate, vallecula, larynx, or post-cricoid area; however, the procedure elicited increased secretions and episodes of coughing.

The case was referred to the on-call immunologist due to concerns about angioedema after structural and infectious etiologies were excluded. Examination revealed diffuse erythema on the face, including involvement of the nasolabial folds, as well as a non-tender, swollen, erythematous neck. The patient reported not taking any medications, specifically denying use of angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and did not have a history—personal or familial—of angioedema. His overall presentation was not consistent with either hereditary or acquired angioedema. Comprehensive physical assessment showed mild nailfold abnormalities suggesting periungual erythema, a rash on the lateral aspect of the right thigh, and reduced strength in the hip flexors (4/5). There were no Gottron’s papules or heliotrope rash. Serum creatine kinase was measured and found to be markedly increased at 16 184 U/L the following day. Renal function tests were normal and screening for myoglobinuria was negative. In addition, the patient’s transaminases were raised, with alanine aminotransferase (ALT) at 143 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) at 396 U/L, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) also elevated at 692 U/L.

Investigations:

In light of the preliminary suspicion for angioedema, a variety of investigations were conducted to identify potential causes of spontaneous angioedema. Measurements of complement components C3 and C4 fell within normal limits, and both the function and quantification of C1 esterase inhibitor were also normal.

The patient’s anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) test returned positive at a titer of 1:1280, displaying a speckled pattern. Testing for anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA) was negative. A myositis antibody profile screening for 16 autoantigens associated with autoimmune inflammatory myopathies was undertaken using the Euroimmun Euroline immunoassay, revealing a strong positive result for antibodies directed against small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1), with a signal intensity of 173 (normal <35). The panel was negative for additional myositis-associated antibodies such as anti-Mi-2 alpha (anti-Mi-2α), anti–Transcription Intermediary Factor-1 gamma (anti-TIF1-γ), anti–Histidyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-Jo-1), and anti–Isoleucyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-OJ). The immunoglobulin G (IgG) concentration, including all subclasses, was within normal parameters. Regarding infectious screening prior to immunosuppression, results were negative for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), hepatitis C core antibody (HBcAb), hepatitis B e antibody (HBeAb), hepatitis C virus (HCV), HIV, varicella zoster virus (VZV) IgG, and cytomegalovirus (CMV) IgG and immunoglobulin M (IgM). QuantiFERON-TB Gold yielded an indeterminate outcome, but smear studies for acid-fast bacilli for Mycobacterium tuberculosis, performed on three separate sputum samples, were negative.

Pulmonary function testing demonstrated an extrathoracic restrictive defect without notable improvement following bronchodilator administration. Chest CT identified several non-cavitating pulmonary nodules predominantly located in the mid to lower lung fields, the largest measuring 15 mm within the right lower lobe, as well as irregular regions of peribronchial consolidation, considered most consistent with aspiration pneumonitis; there was no radiologic suggestion of pulmonary fibrosis. CT imaging of the abdomen found neither abdominopelvic lymphadenopathy nor any indications of a primary or metastatic malignancy within the abdomen or pelvis. A video fluoroscopic (barium) swallow assessment revealed marked pharyngeal dysphagia, resulting in significant retention in the pyriform sinuses and penetration of thin and mildly thick liquids to the level of the vocal cords following swallowing.

MRI scans of the thighs demonstrated findings indicative of active myositis within the bilateral obturator internus and externus muscles, along with less pronounced involvement of the hip adductor muscles, bilateral gracilis, and both distal iliopsoas muscles (figure 2). Additional evidence of active muscular inflammation was provided by fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)-positron emission tomography (PET) imaging, which revealed widespread and abnormal FDG uptake across multiple muscle groups, with the most intense accumulation observed in the head and neck regions, both deltoids, forearms, chest wall, abdomen, and pelvis (figure 3). There was no evidence of hypermetabolic activity in other locations to indicate an underlying malignancy. Tumor markers, including carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), cancer antigen 19-9 (CA19-9), cancer antigen 125 (CA-125), and prostate-specific antigen (PSA), all returned within normal limits.

Histological examination of a thigh muscle biopsy identified areas containing moderate quantities of necrotic, vacuolated, degenerating, myophagocytosed, and regenerating myofibres primarily in a perifascicular pattern, along with scattered perimysial inflammation mainly composed of dispersed histiocytes (figure 4). Immunohistochemical staining for the membrane attack complex revealed deposition of complement component 5b-9 (C5b-9) within perifascicular endomysial capillaries, which is characteristic of dermatomyositis. The patient was diagnosed with moderate to severe SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis presenting with inflammatory edema, peribronchial consolidation due to micro-aspiration, dysphagia, and myositis. Dermatological features noted included a positive Holster sign and periungual erythema.

Differential diagnosis:

Due to his gradually developing neck swelling accompanied by an elevated CRP, initial diagnostic considerations included Ludwig angina and idiopathic angioedema. Early in the course, muscle weakness and symptoms such as dysphagia were not pronounced. The differential diagnosis was broad, encompassing other inflammatory myopathies like immune-mediated necrotising myopathy and inclusion body myositis, infectious etiologies such as neck cellulitis or erysipelas, deep neck space infections, and bacterial sialadenitis, in addition to neuromuscular junction conditions like myasthenia gravis. Other possibilities considered were delayed-type drug hypersensitivity reactions (such as drug rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms), acute contact dermatitis, orofacial granulomatosis, hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis, and superior vena cava syndrome.

Treatment:

Owing to pronounced involvement of the airway musculature, the patient received intravenous methylprednisolone at 1000 mg daily for 3 days. This was followed by a prednisone regimen beginning at 60 mg daily, with gradual dose reductions of 5 mg every 2 to 3 weeks. An initial intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) (Gamunex 10%) loading dose of 2 g/kg (totaling 165 g) was administered over 5 days (35 g daily), and subsequently, five additional cycles were given at 4-week intervals, also at a cumulative dose of 2 g/kg (165 g) divided among those infusions. The patient was initiated on mycophenolate mofetil at a dosage of 1 g twice daily and was also administered rituximab intravenously at 1000 mg for two doses, 2 weeks apart. Methotrexate was excluded from therapy because of its known links to interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis, particularly given the patient’s abnormal pulmonary function tests and following shared decision-making with the patient considering potential adverse effects. Azathioprine and tacrolimus were not employed since mycophenolate was well-tolerated. For prevention of Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim was initially started, but was discontinued due to the onset of haemolytic anaemia (in the absence of glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency), after which the patient successfully tolerated nebulised pentamidine 300 mg every 4 weeks.

He was discharged after a 21-day inpatient stay. His oral medications were administered through nasogastric tube as he had persistent dysphagia at the time of discharge.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following a three-week hospital admission and ongoing therapy, the patient experienced some recovery in hip flexor strength and substantial reduction in cervical soft tissue edema, although the swelling had not entirely subsided by the time of discharge. Dysphagia continued, requiring ongoing nasogastric tube nutrition for an additional two months.

Upon review three weeks post-discharge, proximal muscle strength had returned to baseline and serum creatine kinase was restored to 37 U/L. At an immunology follow-up two months after discharge, further resolution of the cervical swelling was observed, with only residual violaceous discoloration of the face and periungual abnormalities noted on physical examination. Swallowing had improved to the extent that the nasogastric tube was removed and the patient advanced to a diet of soft solids. The therapeutic regimen at that time consisted of mycophenolate mofetil, monthly intravenous immunoglobulin, and a reducing dose of prednisone.

Three months after hospital discharge, the patient’s proximal muscle strength remained stable, his chest exam revealed no abnormalities, and the previously noted cervical soft tissue swelling had fully subsided. Prednisone therapy was discontinued without issues at seven months post-discharge, by which point his dysphagia had completely improved. Follow-up pulmonary function testing indicated slight improvement, with a persistent restrictive defect believed to correspond to his baseline physiology (figure 5a, b). An 11-month follow-up chest CT showed near-total resolution of the previously documented basal ground-glass opacities and airspace consolidations that were initially considered secondary to aspiration. At twelve months after diagnosis, he continued to have stable respiratory status and maintained good exercise capacity.

Regarding malignancy surveillance, he reported no constitutional symptoms at follow-up visits and will remain under surveillance through symptom assessment, clinical examination, routine bloodwork, and periodic imaging studies, including serial CT chest scans. Plans were also made for gastroscopy and colonoscopy to rule out hidden gastrointestinal or colorectal cancer.

It was decided to maintain IVIG therapy and initiate a gradual reduction of the mycophenolate dose over the following six months, contingent upon his clinical trajectory. Ongoing monitoring will be conducted to evaluate whether adjustments in medication are warranted.

Discussion:

We describe a patient with dermatomyositis associated with anti-SAE1 antibodies who presented with marked swelling of the cervical soft tissues, initially misdiagnosed as idiopathic angioedema. Dermatomyositis was considered in the differential after detailed physical assessment uncovered minor skin findings, allowing for prompt identification and management of the condition.

Anti-SAE1 antibodies specifically target the small ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) activating enzyme, a molecule structurally related to ubiquitin that plays a role in sumoylation, a type of post-translational protein modification. Dermatomyositis cases positive for SAE1 antibodies frequently present with skin manifestations including heliotrope rash, Gottron’s papules, V-neck sign, and shawl sign. According to a comprehensive review of 215 patients in the literature, dysphagia and interstitial lung disease were observed in 46% and 43% of SAE1-positive dermatomyositis cases, respectively, and malignancy was identified in 18%.

Subcutaneous tissue edema, including swelling of the face or neck, occurs infrequently in dermatomyositis. In a sizable series comprising 121 dermatomyositis patients, angioedema was identified in only 8 individuals (6.61%). A review of the literature from 1985 through 2013 uncovered just 19 documented cases where dermatomyositis presented with edema. The presence of antibodies such as anti-melanoma differentiation-associated gene (MDA)-5 antibody and anti-transcriptional intermediary factor 1γ (TIF1-γ) antibody has been noted to correlate more often with angioedema than other autoantibodies. Several reports described swelling of the face; however, isolated neck edema was less commonly noted. In our review of the literature focusing on 10 cases of SAE1-positive dermatomyositis, edema was reported in only three cases, with a single case specifying swelling near the throat, underscoring the rarity of neck involvement.79

This case highlights for emergency physicians, ENT specialists, immunologists, and rheumatologists the importance of considering dermatomyositis as a potential differential diagnosis in patients presenting with neck swelling, particularly when there is unexplained or idiopathic localized ‘angioedema’. Because manifestations such as dysphagia or additional system involvement may be mild, thorough evaluation for clinical signs of dermatomyositis and assessment of creatine kinase levels are essential. In the present patient, the subcutaneous oedema is attributed to muscle inflammation associated with myositis, rather than to angioedema driven by histamine or bradykinin pathways.

Following recommendations from the International Guideline for Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathy-Associated Cancer Screening developed by the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS), the patient qualified as high risk for malignancy, possessing two major risk factors (dermatomyositis and dysphagia) and two intermediate factors (SAE1 antibody positivity and male sex). Consequently, the malignancy workup included CT imaging, tumour marker assessment, a PET scan, and planned upper and lower GI endoscopies. Comprehensive initial and follow-up surveillance investigations have so far been reassuring.

Currently, there is a paucity of data regarding the most effective pharmacological treatment for dermatomyositis associated with anti-SAE1 antibodies. In the largest case series reported thus far, management strategies varied, but the majority of individuals received glucocorticoids along with different steroid-sparing immunosuppressive therapies—most frequently methotrexate, though mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, and azathioprine were also utilized; in certain instances, intravenous immunoglobulin was administered. The patient described here was managed with corticosteroids, mycophenolate mofetil, intravenous immunoglobulin, and rituximab, resulting in disease remission. The choice of mycophenolate mofetil was influenced by the patient's preference for once-daily oral medication, as well as the agent's favorable tolerance profile and well-established application in dermatomyositis. Compared with other patients presenting with pronounced tissue oedema in dermatomyositis, our patient’s prognosis appears favorable.
