Title: Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook

Kevin Chan, Gemma Kwan, Hwei Choo Soh, Louise Evans

Abstract:

Summary: Dermatomyositis is an uncommon autoimmune disorder that predominantly involves the skin, muscles, and respiratory tract. Individuals diagnosed with dermatomyositis generally require urgent initiation of strong immunosuppressive therapy to halt disease progression and prevent injury to multiple organ systems. A man in his 40s with no prior health issues presented to the emergency department with marked swelling of the neck. Ear, nose, and throat surgeons initially assessed him, suspecting a deep neck space infection, but this diagnosis was excluded. Subsequently, he was referred to clinical immunology for further evaluation and management of presumed idiopathic angioedema. A creatine kinase measurement was promptly performed because the pattern of neck swelling was atypical for angioedema and was accompanied by cutaneous and periungual abnormalities. The working diagnosis of dermatomyositis was made, which was later confirmed by the detection of a small ubiquitin-like modifier-1 activating enzyme (SAE1) autoantibody and characteristic muscle biopsy findings. This case demonstrates that non-histamine and non-bradykinin-mediated neck edema is a rare clinical feature of dermatomyositis, presenting here with dysphagia and pronounced muscle involvement that necessitated urgent intervention.

Background:

Dermatomyositis is a type of idiopathic inflammatory myopathy that most often affects the skin and skeletal muscles, though it may also involve the gastrointestinal system, joints, pulmonary structures, and the heart. The condition has an estimated incidence of 10 cases per million individuals. There is a bimodal age distribution, with incidence peaks seen in children between 5 and 14 years old and in adults between 45 and 64 years. Cutaneous manifestations typically include Gottron’s papules, the shawl sign, heliotrope rash, and periungual telangiectasia. Additional clinical features may consist of proximal muscle weakness, interstitial lung disease, and difficulty swallowing. Pseudoangioedema is a rare presentation, documented in only 8 out of 121 individuals in one reported series of dermatomyositis. This finding can imitate primary otolaryngologic conditions when located on the neck or be misidentified as angioedema related to histamine or bradykinin.

Case presentation:

A man in his 40s presented to the emergency department after three weeks of progressively worsening neck swelling and proximal weakness affecting his hip flexors, along with mild muscle aches. He had no notable past medical conditions and had never smoked. He was not using any HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors. The preliminary diagnosis was Ludwig’s angina, prompting a neck CT scan that revealed widespread edema of the neck involving the strap muscles and inflammatory changes extending into the retropharyngeal space (figure 1). On examination, he was afebrile, had oxygen saturation of 99% on room air, and did not exhibit tachypnoea. Nailfold capillaroscopy identified proximal microhaemorrhages and avascular zones in the nailfolds. Laboratory evaluation showed a white blood cell count of 9.1×10^9/L and a C-reactive protein level of 55.6 mg/L at presentation. Given the ongoing swelling without an identifiable etiology, he was referred to a quaternary facility with ENT surgical expertise for further assessment. ENT performed flexible nasendoscopy, which found no substantial structural abnormalities in the soft palate, vallecula, larynx, or post-cricoid region, though increased secretions and coughing were observed during the procedure.

Consultation with the immunologist on call was initiated due to suspicion of angioedema, as no definitive structural or infectious etiology was identified. On examination, the patient exhibited diffuse facial erythema extending into the nasolabial folds, as well as an erythematous, edematous but non-tender neck. He reported not using any medications, specifically angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and had neither a personal nor a family history of angioedema. The clinical picture did not align with typical presentations of either acquired or hereditary angioedema. Comprehensive physical assessment demonstrated subtle changes in the nailfold suggestive of periungual erythema, a rash present on the right lateral thigh, and hip flexion strength decreased to 4/5. Gottron’s papules and heliotrope rash were not observed. A creatine kinase test was ordered and, on the following day, returned markedly elevated at 16,184 U/L. Renal parameters remained within normal limits, and urine myoglobin screening yielded a negative result. Additionally, the patient’s transaminase levels were increased, with alanine aminotransferase (ALT) measured at 143 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) at 396 U/L, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) at 692 U/L.

Investigations:

Due to the preliminary suspicion of angioedema, a series of investigations were conducted to identify possible etiologies of spontaneous angioedema. Complement levels C3 and C4 were within normal limits, as were the C1 esterase activity and quantitative values.

The patient demonstrated a positive anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) at a titre of 1:1280 with a speckled pattern, while anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA) testing was negative. A myositis antibody panel targeting 16 antigens associated with autoimmune inflammatory myopathies was analyzed using the Euroimmun Euroline immunoassay, revealing a strong positivity for anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibodies with a signal intensity of 173 (reference range <35). All other myositis antibodies assessed—namely anti-Mi-2α, anti-TIF1-γ, anti-Jo-1, and anti-OJ—were negative. Immunoglobulin G (IgG), including its subclasses, was found to be within the normal range. Screening for immunosuppressive-related infections returned negative results for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), hepatitis C core antibody (HBcAb), hepatitis B e antibody (HBeAb), hepatitis C virus (HCV), HIV, varicella zoster virus (VZV) IgG, and cytomegalovirus (CMV) IgG/IgM. Although QuantiFERON-TB Gold results were indeterminate, three sputum samples tested negative for acid-fast bacilli indicative of Mycobacterium tuberculosis.

Pulmonary function testing indicated an extrathoracic restrictive defect without a notable response to bronchodilators. Chest CT demonstrated several non-cavitating nodules primarily distributed in the mid to lower lung zones, with the largest being 15 mm within the right lower lobe, as well as scattered peribronchial consolidation consistent with aspiration pneumonitis; however, there was no radiographic evidence of pulmonary fibrosis. Abdominal CT did not reveal enlarged abdominopelvic lymph nodes or any findings suggestive of primary or metastatic malignancy in the abdominal or pelvic regions. A video fluoroscopic barium swallow examination revealed marked pharyngeal dysphagia, resulting in significant residue within the pyriform fossae and penetration of both thin and mildly thick fluids to the level of the vocal cords after swallowing.

MRI of the thighs revealed findings indicative of ongoing myositis in both obturator internus and externus muscles, as well as less pronounced inflammation affecting the hip adductors, gracilis muscles bilaterally, and distal portions of the iliopsoas muscles (figure 2). The presence of active muscle inflammation was corroborated by fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)-positron emission tomography (PET) imaging, which demonstrated widespread and atypical FDG uptake across several muscle groups, with the greatest intensity noted in the head and neck, both deltoid muscles, forearms, chest wall, abdomen, and pelvic regions (figure 3). No areas of metabolically active disease were identified elsewhere to suggest malignancy. Levels of tumor markers, including carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), cancer antigen 19-9 (CA19-9), cancer antigen 125 (CA-125), and prostate-specific antigen (PSA), were within normal ranges.

A muscle biopsy from the thigh identified moderate foci composed of necrotic, vacuolated, degenerating, myophagocytosed, and regenerating myofibers arranged in a perifascicular pattern, along with scattered perimysial infiltration by dispersed histiocytes (figure 4). Immunohistochemical staining for membrane attack complex revealed complement component 5b-9 (C5b-9) deposits in perifascicular endomysial capillaries, a finding characteristic of dermatomyositis. The patient was diagnosed with moderate to severe SAE1 antibody positive dermatomyositis, presenting with inflammatory swelling, peribronchial consolidation related to micro-aspiration, dysphagia, and myositis. Skin findings included a positive Holster sign and periungual erythema.

Differential diagnosis:

Considering his subacute onset characterized by cervical swelling and raised CRP, initial suspicion was directed toward Ludwig angina or idiopathic angioedema. Notably, muscular weakness—including difficulty swallowing—was not a salient aspect early in the clinical course. The differential diagnosis encompassed additional inflammatory myopathies (such as immune-mediated necrotising myopathy and inclusion body myositis), infectious etiologies (like neck cellulitis/erysipelas, deep neck space infections, and bacterial sialadenitis), disorders of the neuromuscular junction (for instance, myasthenia gravis), delayed hypersensitivity reactions to drugs (e.g., drug rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms), acute contact dermatitis, orofacial granulomatosis, hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis, and superior vena cava syndrome.

Treatment:

Owing to marked involvement of the airway musculature, the patient received intravenous methylprednisolone for 3 days at a dose of 1000 mg per day. This was followed by initiation of an oral prednisone taper, beginning at 60 mg daily and gradually decreased by 5 mg every 2 to 3 weeks. An intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG, Gamunex 10%) loading dose of 2 g/kg (totaling 165 g) was administered over 5 days (equivalent to 35 g per day), and thereafter, five additional cycles were given at 4-week intervals, each totaling 2 g/kg (165 g) divided across these sessions. Mycophenolate mofetil was started at 1 g twice daily, and two intravenous rituximab infusions of 1000 mg each were given 2 weeks apart. Methotrexate was not included in the regimen due to its established link with interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis, especially considering the patient’s abnormal pulmonary function, and this decision was made collaboratively with the patient while considering the side effect profile. Mycophenolate was selected over azathioprine and tacrolimus as it was well-tolerated. For prophylaxis against Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim was initially prescribed but discontinued after onset of haemolytic anaemia (with no glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase [G6PD] deficiency identified); thereafter, nebulised pentamidine 300 mg every 4 weeks was administered without issue.

Following a 21-day hospital admission, the patient was discharged. At discharge, persistent dysphagia necessitated administration of oral medications via nasogastric tube.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following a three-week hospitalization and therapeutic intervention, the patient experienced partial restoration of hip flexor strength and substantial reduction in cervical soft tissue swelling, though some swelling remained unresolved by the time of discharge. Persistent dysphagia required ongoing nasogastric tube feeding, which continued for an additional two months.

Upon evaluation three weeks post-discharge, proximal muscle strength had completely normalized and the serum creatine kinase measured 37 U/L. At the immunology follow-up two months after discharge, cervical swelling showed continued improvement, with only minor residual violaceous facial erythema and periungual abnormalities observed on physical exam. Swallowing abilities had improved sufficiently to discontinue nasogastric tube feeding and advance to a diet consisting of soft solids. The patient was maintained on mycophenolate mofetil, monthly intravenous immunoglobulin, and a tapering regimen of prednisone.

At three months following discharge, the patient's proximal muscle strength remained unchanged, chest auscultation revealed no abnormalities, and cervical soft tissue swelling had completely subsided. Prednisone was tapered off successfully seven months after discharge, at which point his dysphagia had fully resolved. Subsequent pulmonary function testing indicated slight improvement; however, a persistent restrictive defect was observed, likely representing his baseline status (figure 5a, b). Follow-up CT of the chest at eleven months revealed almost total resolution of the basal ground-glass opacities and consolidation previously detected, findings that were initially ascribed to aspiration. One year post-diagnosis, his respiratory function remained steady with satisfactory tolerance to physical activity.

With respect to malignancy surveillance, the patient reported no constitutional symptoms at follow-up and will undergo ongoing assessment via clinical symptoms, physical examination, routine laboratory investigations, and serial chest CT imaging as indicated. Additionally, scheduled gastroscopy and colonoscopy are planned to investigate for possible hidden gastrointestinal or colorectal neoplasms.

The therapeutic plan included continuation of IVIG and a gradual reduction of mycophenolate dosage over the ensuing six months, contingent upon clinical status. He will be monitored closely to evaluate whether further modifications to the treatment regimen are required.

Discussion:

We describe a patient with dermatomyositis positive for anti-SAE1 antibodies who presented with significant swelling of cervical soft tissues, which was initially interpreted as idiopathic angioedema. Awareness of dermatomyositis as a possible differential, together with meticulous clinical assessment uncovering subtle skin manifestations, resulted in prompt identification and management.

Anti-SAE1 antibodies target the small-ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) activating enzyme, a protein closely related to ubiquitin that participates in post-translational modification processes known as ‘sumoylation’. Dermatomyositis associated with SAE1 antibodies often displays distinct dermatologic findings, including heliotrope rash, Gottron’s papules, V-neck sign, and shawl sign. A comprehensive literature review comprising 215 cases revealed dysphagia in 46% and interstitial lung disease in 43% of patients with SAE1 dermatomyositis, whereas malignancies were observed in 18% of individuals.

Edema of the subcutaneous tissues, particularly swelling of the face or neck, rarely occurs in dermatomyositis. Within a sizable cohort of 121 individuals diagnosed with dermatomyositis, angioedema was observed in just 8 cases (6.61%). Reviewing publications between 1985 and 2013 identified only 19 reports of dermatomyositis presenting with edema. Antibodies such as anti-melanoma differentiation-associated gene (MDA)-5 and anti-transcriptional intermediary factor 1γ (TIF1-γ) showed a greater association with angioedema compared to other antibody types. Although several reports noted facial swelling, involvement of the neck was much less common. Our review of 10 documented cases of SAE1-positive dermatomyositis revealed edema in only three cases, with one specifically reporting swelling of the throat area, underscoring the rarity of neck involvement.79

This case highlights the importance for emergency physicians, ENT specialists, immunologists, and rheumatologists to consider dermatomyositis as a differential diagnosis for neck swelling, particularly when encountering localized ‘angioedema’ that lacks a clear cause or is idiopathic. Because dysphagia and other manifestations may present subtly, it is essential to assess for dermatomyositis-specific physical signs and to obtain a creatine kinase measurement. In this patient, the observed subcutaneous oedema was attributable to muscle inflammation associated with myositis, not to angioedema mediated by histamine or bradykinin.

Following the International Guideline for Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathy-Associated Cancer Screening developed by the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS), the patient met criteria for two high risk factors (dermatomyositis and dysphagia) as well as two intermediate risk factors (positive SAE1 antibody and male gender), which classified him as high risk for malignancy. A malignancy evaluation was performed, including CT imaging, tumor marker assays, and PET scanning, with further plans to conduct upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopy. Thorough baseline assessments and ongoing surveillance thus far have remained reassuring.

The evidence base for determining the most effective pharmacological management in anti-SAE1–positive dermatomyositis remains sparse. The largest cohort reported thus far demonstrates a variety of therapeutic regimens; the majority received glucocorticoids together with multiple steroid-sparing agents, predominantly methotrexate, but also including mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, and azathioprine. Intravenous immunoglobulin was administered in selected instances. In this case, the patient underwent treatment with steroids, mycophenolate mofetil, intravenous immunoglobulin, and rituximab, resulting in remission. The choice of mycophenolate was influenced by the patient's preference for a daily oral medication and consideration of its tolerability and established role in dermatomyositis management. This patient’s outcome is favourable relative to others presenting with substantial tissue edema associated with dermatomyositis.
