Title: Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook

Kevin Chan, Gemma Kwan, Hwei Choo Soh, Louise Evans

Abstract:

Dermatomyositis is an uncommon autoimmune condition that primarily targets the skin, skeletal muscle, and the respiratory tract. Management generally necessitates urgent, high-dose immunosuppression to curb disease progression and prevent damage to multiple organs. A man in his 40s with no prior health issues presented to the emergency department with pronounced swelling of the neck. Initially assessed by the ear, nose, and throat surgical team for a possible deep neck space infection, he was subsequently ruled out for this diagnosis. Clinical immunology then became involved to evaluate and manage presumed idiopathic angioedema. Due to the unusual presentation of neck edema not aligning with typical angioedema, and the concomitant presence of cutaneous and nail abnormalities, a creatine kinase measurement was promptly performed. Dermatomyositis was considered as a preliminary diagnosis; this was later confirmed through detection of small ubiquitin-like modifier-1 activating enzyme (SAE1) autoantibody and classic muscle biopsy findings. This report underscores neck swelling unrelated to histamine or bradykinin mechanisms as a rare clinical manifestation of dermatomyositis, presenting with dysphagia and significant muscular involvement that necessitated urgent therapeutic intervention.

Background:

Dermatomyositis is an inflammatory muscle disorder of unknown origin that most frequently impacts the skin and musculature, but may also involve organs such as the lungs, heart, gastrointestinal tract, and joints. Its incidence is estimated at 10 per million individuals. The condition presents two age-related peaks, most commonly manifesting in children between 5 and 14 years old and in adults aged 45 to 64. Typical skin manifestations comprise Gottron’s papules, a shawl sign, heliotrope rash, and periungual telangiectasias. Additional clinical features include weakness predominantly in proximal muscle groups, interstitial lung disease, and difficulty swallowing. Pseudoangioedema is a rare occurrence in dermatomyositis; for example, one study observed this finding in only 8 of 121 cases. When pseudoangioedema involves the neck, it can resemble primary disorders of the otolaryngology spectrum or may be misidentified as angioedema caused by either histamine or bradykinin pathways.

Case presentation:

A man in his 40s presented to the emergency department with a three-week progression of neck swelling and proximal muscle weakness, particularly affecting his hip flexors, along with mild muscle aches. He did not have any notable past medical conditions and had never smoked. He also had not used any 3-hydroxy-3-methyl-glutaryl-coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase inhibitors. Ludwig’s angina was initially suspected, prompting a CT scan of the neck, which revealed diffuse edema involving the strap muscles and inflammatory changes reaching the retropharyngeal space (figure 1). On examination, he was afebrile, maintained an oxygen saturation of 99% on room air, and showed no signs of tachypnoea. Nailfold capillaroscopy detected proximal nailfold microhaemorrhages and areas without blood vessels. His white cell count measured 9.1×10^9/L, and the C-reactive protein (CRP) was 55.6 mg/L at the time of admission. Since the neck swelling persisted without a clear etiology, he was referred to a quaternary care center with ear, nose, and throat (ENT) surgical services for further assessment. An ENT evaluation, including flexible nasendoscopy, did not reveal any substantial anatomical defects in the soft palate, vallecula, larynx, or post-cricoid area, but the procedure was remarkable for increased secretions and elicited coughing.

The case was referred to the on-call immunologist due to suspected angioedema, as there was no evident structural abnormality or infectious etiology. Upon assessment, the patient exhibited diffuse facial erythema, including involvement of the nasolabial folds, along with a swollen, erythematous, and non-tender neck. He reported not taking any medications such as angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and denied both personal and familial histories of angioedema. Clinically, his presentation did not align with typical acquired or hereditary angioedema. Comprehensive examination identified subtle nailfold findings consistent with periungual erythema, a rash on the right lateral thigh, and a decreased hip flexor strength measured at 4/5. No Gottron’s papules or heliotrope rash were observed. The following day, his creatine kinase (CK) level was markedly increased at 16,184 U/L. Renal function remained normal and urinary myoglobin screening was negative. Laboratory investigations also showed raised transaminases: alanine aminotransferase (ALT) at 143 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) at 396 U/L, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) at 692 U/L.

Investigations:

Due to the preliminary suspicion of angioedema, a series of investigations were conducted to identify potential causes of spontaneous angioedema. Results showed that his C3 and C4 complement levels were within normal limits, as were both the functional and quantitative measures of C1 esterase.

The patient’s anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) was positive with a titre of 1:1280 and exhibited a speckled pattern. Testing for anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA) yielded negative results. A myositis antibody panel, which assessed for 16 antigens associated with autoimmune inflammatory myopathies via the Euroimmun Euroline immunoassay, revealed a strong positive for anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibodies, with a signal intensity of 173 (reference range <35). No reactivity was observed for other myositis-specific antibodies, including anti-Mi-2 alpha (anti-Mi-2α), anti–Transcription Intermediary Factor-1 gamma (anti-TIF1-γ), anti–Histidyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-Jo-1), and anti–Isoleucyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-OJ). His total immunoglobulin G (IgG) and subclass levels were unremarkable. For infectious disease screening pertinent to immunosuppression, he tested negative for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), hepatitis C core antibody (HBcAb), hepatitis B e antibody (HBeAb), hepatitis C virus (HCV), HIV, varicella zoster virus (VZV) IgG, and both cytomegalovirus (CMV) IgG and IgM. QuantiFERON-TB Gold test produced an indeterminate result, but three sputum samples for acid-fast bacilli specific to Mycobacterium tuberculosis were negative.

Pulmonary function testing demonstrated an extrathoracic restrictive pattern, with no notable response to bronchodilator therapy. Chest CT identified multiple non-cavitating nodules in the lungs, predominantly located in the mid and lower zones, with the largest measuring 15 mm in the right lower lobe. There were also patchy peribronchial consolidations, which were suggestive of aspiration pneumonitis, but there was no imaging evidence of pulmonary fibrosis. Abdominal CT revealed no enlargement of abdomino-pelvic lymph nodes, nor any signs of primary or metastatic malignancy within the abdomen or pelvis. A video fluoroscopic (barium) swallow assessment demonstrated severe dysphagia affecting the pharyngeal phase, resulting in considerable pyriform fossae residue and post-swallow penetration of both thin and mildly thick fluids to the level of the vocal cords.

MRI scans of the thighs revealed findings indicative of active myositis affecting the bilateral obturator internus and externus muscles, with less pronounced involvement of the hip adductor muscles, bilateral gracilis muscles, and both distal iliopsoas muscles (figure 2). Additional evidence for ongoing muscle inflammation was obtained from a fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)-positron emission tomography (PET) scan, which demonstrated widespread and abnormal FDG uptake across multiple muscle groups, most markedly in the head and neck, both deltoids, forearms, chest wall, abdomen, and pelvic regions (figure 3). No metabolically active disease was detected elsewhere to raise concern for malignancy. Tumor markers, such as carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), cancer antigen 19-9 (CA19-9), cancer antigen 125 (CA-125), and prostate-specific antigen (PSA), were all within normal limits.

Thigh muscle biopsy displayed areas containing a moderate number of necrotic, vacuolated, degenerated, myophagocytosed, and regenerating myofibers arranged in a perifascicular pattern, alongside scattered inflammation in the perimysium composed primarily of dispersed histiocytes (figure 4). Immunohistochemical staining for the membrane attack complex identified complement component 5b-9 (C5b-9) deposits in perifascicular endomysial capillaries, which is characteristic for dermatomyositis. The patient was subsequently diagnosed with moderate to severe SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis, accompanied by inflammatory swelling, peribronchial consolidation attributed to micro-aspiration, dysphagia, and myositis. Cutaneous symptoms included a positive Holster sign and periungual erythema.

Differential diagnosis:

Due to the patient's subacute onset of neck swelling accompanied by an elevated CRP, Ludwig angina and idiopathic angioedema were initially considered. Significant muscle weakness, including difficulties with swallowing, was not evident in the early stages. The differential diagnosis encompassed other inflammatory myopathies, such as immune-mediated necrotising myopathy and inclusion body myositis; infectious etiologies like cellulitis or erysipelas affecting the neck, deep neck space infections, and bacterial sialadenitis; neuromuscular junction disorders including myasthenia gravis; delayed drug hypersensitivity reactions such as drug rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms; acute contact dermatitis; orofacial granulomatosis; hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis; as well as superior vena cava syndrome.

Treatment:

Owing to notable involvement of the airway musculature, the patient received intravenous methylprednisolone at 1000 mg per day for 3 days. Subsequently, he transitioned to a prednisone taper beginning at 60 mg daily, which was gradually reduced by 5 mg every 2 to 3 weeks. A loading dose of intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG, Gamunex 10%) was administered at 2 g/kg (totaling 165 g) spread over 5 days (35 g daily), after which he completed 5 additional cycles spaced 4 weeks apart, with the same total dosage of 2 g/kg (165 g) divided among the treatments. Mycophenolate mofetil was initiated at 1 g twice daily, and rituximab was given intravenously at 1000 mg for two doses separated by a 2-week interval. Methotrexate was avoided, given its known link with interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis, alongside the patient’s abnormal pulmonary function and in consultation regarding potential side effects. Azathioprine and tacrolimus were not selected as mycophenolate was tolerated without issue. To prevent Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, the patient initially received sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim, but this was discontinued due to the onset of haemolytic anaemia (without evidence of G6PD deficiency); he then tolerated nebulized pentamidine at 300 mg every 4 weeks.

The patient was discharged following a 21-day hospital admission. Due to ongoing dysphagia, his oral therapies were delivered via nasogastric tube at the time of discharge.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following a three-week inpatient stay and management, the patient exhibited notable resolution of cervical soft tissue swelling—though not complete by discharge—and moderate gains in hip flexor strength. However, dysphagia was ongoing, requiring the continued use of a nasogastric tube for nutrition for an additional two months.

At a follow-up appointment three weeks post-discharge, proximal muscle power was fully restored, and serum creatine kinase had returned to a normal level of 37 U/L. When reassessed by immunology two months after leaving the hospital, further reduction in cervical swelling was observed, with the physical exam showing only remaining violaceous erythema of the face and changes around the fingernails. Swallowing had improved sufficiently to discontinue the nasogastric tube and begin soft solid foods. The patient continued therapy with mycophenolate mofetil, ongoing monthly intravenous immunoglobulin, and prednisone in a tapering regimen.

At three months after discharge, the patient maintained stable proximal muscle strength, had a normal chest examination, and the cervical soft tissue swelling was entirely resolved. Prednisone therapy was successfully stopped seven months after discharge, at which point dysphagia had completely subsided. Repeat pulmonary function testing revealed modest improvement, though a persistent restrictive pattern was present, considered to represent the patient’s baseline physiology (see figure 5a, b). Follow-up chest CT at eleven months showed near-total resolution of the previously seen basal ground-glass changes and airspace consolidation, which had initially been thought to result from aspiration. Twelve months post-diagnosis, his respiratory status was unchanged and he exhibited satisfactory exercise capacity.

Regarding malignancy, he reported no constitutional symptoms during follow-up and will undergo ongoing surveillance through symptom review, clinical evaluation, routine bloodwork, and imaging, including serial CT scans of the chest. Additionally, gastroscopy and colonoscopy are planned to rule out hidden gastrointestinal or colorectal cancers.

The decision was made to continue IVIG therapy and gradually reduce the mycophenolate dose over the next six months, guided by his clinical status. He will be closely observed to assess whether any alterations in his medication regimen become necessary.

Discussion:

We describe a patient with dermatomyositis associated with anti-SAE1 antibodies who presented with marked cervical soft tissue edema, initially diagnosed as idiopathic angioedema. Identifying dermatomyositis among the differential diagnoses and conducting a thorough physical exam that uncovered subtle skin manifestations enabled prompt diagnosis and management.

Anti-SAE1 antibodies recognize the small-ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) activating enzyme, a protein with structural resemblance to ubiquitin that is responsible for post-translational ‘sumoylation’ of various substrates. Dermatomyositis with SAE1 antibodies commonly exhibits skin findings such as heliotrope rash, Gottron’s papules, V-neck sign, and shawl sign. According to an extensive literature review including 215 cases, dysphagia and interstitial lung disease were observed in 46% and 43% of patients with SAE1 dermatomyositis, respectively, while 18% developed malignancy.

Oedema of the subcutaneous tissues, particularly involving the face or neck, is rarely observed in cases of dermatomyositis. In a sizeable cohort analysis including 121 dermatomyositis patients, only 8 individuals (6.61%) experienced angioedema. Review of the literature covering the years 1985 through 2013 identified just 19 reported instances where dermatomyositis presented with oedema. Among autoantibodies, anti-melanoma differentiation-associated gene (MDA)-5 and anti-transcriptional intermediary factor 1γ (TIF1-γ) antibodies appeared more frequently in patients exhibiting angioedema compared to other antibodies. While facial oedema was noted in several accounts, descriptions of neck swelling were considerably rarer. Our literature review covering 10 cases of SAE1 antibody–positive dermatomyositis indicated oedema in only three, with one report noting throat region swelling, underscoring the infrequency of neck involvement.79

This case highlights the importance for emergency physicians, ENT specialists, immunologists, and rheumatologists to consider dermatomyositis as a potential differential diagnosis when evaluating neck swelling, particularly when encountering localized ‘angioedema’ of unclear or idiopathic origin. Because symptoms such as dysphagia and additional manifestations can be subtle, performing a thorough physical examination for signs consistent with dermatomyositis and ordering a creatine kinase test are essential steps. In this patient, subcutaneous oedema was attributable to inflammation of the muscle associated with myositis, rather than due to angioedema mechanisms mediated by histamine or bradykinin.

According to the International Guideline for Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathy-Associated Cancer Screening from the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS), this patient met criteria for high malignancy risk, having two major risk factors (dermatomyositis, dysphagia) and two moderate risk factors (positive SAE1 antibody, male gender). As a result, he underwent malignancy evaluation including computed tomography imaging, tumor marker assays, and a PET scan, with plans to also perform upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopies. Both initial and follow-up surveillance have thus far yielded reassuring results.

There is a scarcity of data regarding the most effective pharmacological management for anti-SAE1–positive dermatomyositis. The largest cohort reported thus far demonstrated varied therapeutic strategies; the majority of patients received glucocorticoids along with diverse steroid-sparing agents, predominantly methotrexate, but also included mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, and azathioprine. Selected individuals were administered intravenous immunoglobulin. In this case, the patient was given steroids, mycophenolate mofetil, intravenous immunoglobulin, and rituximab, resulting in remission. Mycophenolate mofetil was chosen due to the patient’s preference for a daily oral medication, as well as its favorable tolerability and well-established application in dermatomyositis. Notably, this patient’s outlook is relatively favorable in comparison with other patients experiencing pronounced tissue edema secondary to dermatomyositis.
