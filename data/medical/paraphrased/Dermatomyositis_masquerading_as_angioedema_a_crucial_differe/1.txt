Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook

Kevin Chan, Gemma Kwan, Hwei Choo Soh, Louise Evans

Abstract:

Summary: Dermatomyositis is an uncommon autoimmune condition primarily involving the skin, muscles, and respiratory tract. Effective management often demands urgent administration of potent immunosuppressive therapies to curtail disease progression and prevent damage to multiple organ systems. In this case, a previously healthy man in his 40s arrived at the emergency department with pronounced swelling of the neck. Initial evaluation by ear, nose and throat specialists suggested a potential deep neck space infection. After this etiology was excluded, the patient was subsequently referred to clinical immunology to assess for suspected idiopathic angioedema. However, given the unusual nature of the neck edema—atypical for angioedema—and the concurrent findings of changes in skin and nails, clinicians rapidly ordered a creatine kinase assay. The working diagnosis became dermatomyositis, later supported by detection of a positive small ubiquitin-like modifier-1 activating enzyme (SAE1) autoantibody and characteristic findings on muscle biopsy. This report underscores that neck swelling, independent of histamine or bradykinin pathways, can represent an atypical manifestation of dermatomyositis, especially when accompanied by dysphagia and marked muscular involvement, thus necessitating swift therapeutic intervention.

Background:

Dermatomyositis is an inflammatory muscle disease of unknown cause that primarily affects the skin and skeletal muscles but can also involve organs such as the gastrointestinal system, joints, lungs, and heart. The condition is estimated to occur in approximately 10 individuals per million annually. There are two notable age peaks for disease onset: one in childhood between 5 and 14 years, and another in adulthood between 45 and 64 years. Classic dermatologic manifestations include Gottron’s papules, the shawl sign, heliotrope eruption, and periungual telangiectasias. Additional clinical presentations may involve proximal muscle weakness, interstitial lung disease, and difficulty swallowing. Pseudoangioedema is a rare manifestation in dermatomyositis; one study reported its occurrence in just 8 of 121 diagnosed cases. When it affects the neck, pseudoangioedema can mimic primary otolaryngologic conditions or be confused with histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated forms of angioedema.

Case presentation:

A man in his 40s presented to the emergency department reporting three weeks of gradually worsening neck swelling and proximal muscle weakness, particularly affecting his hip flexors, alongside mild muscle aches. His medical background was unremarkable, and he had never smoked. He had not used any 3-hydroxy-3-methyl-glutaryl-coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase inhibitors. Initially, Ludwig’s angina was suspected, prompting a CT scan of the neck, which revealed widespread oedema in the strap muscles as well as inflammatory changes extending into the retropharyngeal space (see figure 1). Upon examination, he was not febrile, maintained an oxygen saturation of 99% on room air, and exhibited no tachypnoea. Nailfold capillaroscopy detected proximal nailfold microhaemorrhages and regions without vascularization. Laboratory results showed a white blood cell count of 9.1×10^9/L and a C-reactive protein (CRP) level of 55.6 mg/L at the time of admission. Persistent swelling with no clear etiology led to his referral to a quaternary hospital equipped with ear, nose, and throat (ENT) surgical capabilities for more comprehensive assessment. An ENT specialist performed flexible nasendoscopy, which revealed no remarkable structural abnormalities in the soft palate, vallecula, larynx, or post-cricoid area, though there were increased secretions and the patient coughed during the procedure.

The case was referred to the on-call immunologist due to a suspicion of angioedema, given the absence of identifiable structural or infectious etiologies. Upon further assessment, the patient displayed diffuse facial erythema, involving the nasolabial folds, alongside a swollen, erythematous, and non-tender neck. He reported not taking any medications, specifically ruling out angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and there was no personal or familial angioedema history. Consequently, the clinical presentation did not fit classic acquired or hereditary angioedema patterns. Detailed examination uncovered mild periungual erythema in the nailfolds, a rash on the right lateral thigh, and diminished hip flexor strength graded at 4/5. Neither Gottron’s papules nor a heliotrope rash was present. Laboratory tests revealed a markedly increased creatine kinase concentration of 16 184 U/L the following day. Kidney function remained within normal limits, and urine myoglobin was not detected. The patient also demonstrated raised transaminases, with alanine aminotransferase (ALT) at 143 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) at 396 U/L, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) at 692 U/L.

Investigations:

In light of the initial suspicion of angioedema, a series of diagnostic investigations were undertaken to identify potential etiologies for spontaneous angioedema. Both C3 and C4 complement concentrations fell within normal parameters, as did measurements of C1 esterase activity and quantity.

Testing revealed that the patient exhibited a positive anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) at a titre of 1:1280, displaying a speckled staining pattern. Assessment for anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA) returned a negative result. To further elucidate underlying autoimmune pathology, a myositis antibody panel targeting 16 antigens associated with autoimmune inflammatory myopathies was conducted using the Euroimmun Euroline immunoassay. Results showed a markedly elevated response to anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibodies, with a signal intensity of 173 (reference range <35). All other myositis-specific antibodies, including anti-Mi-2 alpha (anti-Mi-2α), anti–Transcription Intermediary Factor-1 gamma (anti-TIF1-γ), anti–Histidyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-Jo-1), and anti–Isoleucyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-OJ) antibodies, tested negative. The patient’s immunoglobulin G (IgG) levels and subclasses were found to be unremarkable.

For evaluation of immunosuppression, serological tests demonstrated negative results for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), hepatitis C core antibody (HBcAb), hepatitis B e antibody (HBeAb), hepatitis C virus (HCV), HIV, varicella zoster virus (VZV) IgG, and cytomegalovirus (CMV) IgG and immunoglobulin M (IgM). The QuantiFERON-TB Gold assay yielded an indeterminate result; nevertheless, examination of three sputum samples for acid-fast bacilli indicative of Mycobacterium tuberculosis showed negative findings.

Pulmonary function assessment demonstrated an extrathoracic restrictive pattern without notable response to bronchodilators. Chest CT imaging identified several non-cavitating nodules predominantly distributed in the mid to lower lung zones, with the largest lesion measuring 15 mm in the right lower lobe. Additionally, patchy peribronchial consolidation was observed, most consistent with aspiration pneumonitis, while there was no radiological indication of pulmonary fibrosis. Abdominal CT scans did not reveal any enlargement of abdomino-pelvic lymph nodes, nor was there evidence of primary or secondary malignancies within the abdomen or pelvis. The video fluoroscopic (barium) swallow examination demonstrated severe pharyngeal dysphagia, resulting in substantial pyriform fossae residue and post-deglutition penetration of both thin and mildly thick liquids to the level of the vocal cords.

MRI of the thighs demonstrated imaging findings compatible with active myositis involving the bilateral obturator internus and externus muscles, as well as less pronounced involvement of the hip adductors, bilateral gracilis, and bilateral distal iliopsoas muscles (figure 2). The presence of ongoing muscle inflammation was corroborated by fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)-positron emission tomography (PET), which revealed widespread abnormal FDG uptake across several muscle groups, most notably in the head and neck, both deltoids, forearms, chest wall, abdominal region, and pelvis (figure 3). No evidence of metabolically active lesions was identified to raise suspicion for malignancy. Routine tumour markers, including carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), cancer antigen 19-9 (CA19-9), cancer antigen 125 (CA-125), and prostate-specific antigen (PSA), all returned within reference limits.

Histopathological examination of a muscle biopsy from the thigh revealed moderate focal areas containing necrotic, vacuolated, degenerated, myophagocytosed, and regenerating myofibres exhibiting perifascicular distribution, along with scattered perimysial infiltration by dispersed histiocytes (figure 4). Immunohistochemical staining for membrane attack complex highlighted the deposition of complement component 5b-9 (C5b-9) within perifascicular endomysial capillaries, which is characteristic of dermatomyositis. Collectively, these findings established a diagnosis of moderate to severe SAE1 antibody positive dermatomyositis, associated with inflammatory edema, peribronchial consolidation due to micro-aspiration, dysphagia, and myositis. Dermatological signs were observed, including a positive Holster sign and periungual erythema.

Differential diagnosis:

Given the patient's subacute onset of neck swelling accompanied by an elevated CRP, initial diagnostic considerations included Ludwig angina and idiopathic angioedema. Notably, muscle weakness—such as dysphagia—was not a significant aspect at the outset. The differential diagnosis encompassed a range of possibilities: additional inflammatory myopathies (for instance, immune-mediated necrotising myopathy, inclusion body myositis), infectious etiologies (including cellulitis/erysipelas localized to the neck, deep neck space infections, or bacterial sialadenitis), disorders affecting the neuromuscular junction like myasthenia gravis, late-onset drug hypersensitivity reactions (such as drug rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms), acute contact dermatitis, orofacial granulomatosis, hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis, as well as superior vena cava syndrome.

Treatment:

Given the pronounced involvement of airway musculature, the patient received intravenous methylprednisolone at a daily dose of 1000 mg for 3 days. This was transitioned to a prednisone taper, initiated at 60 mg per day and reduced by 5 mg every 2 to 3 weeks. An initial intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) (Gamunex 10%) loading dose of 2 g/kg (totalling 165 g) was administered over 5 days (35 g per day), and this was followed by 5 additional IVIG cycles spaced 4 weeks apart, collectively providing 2 g/kg (165 g) divided among these infusions. The patient also began treatment with mycophenolate mofetil at 1 g twice daily, and received rituximab intravenously at a dose of 1000 mg, administered twice with a 2-week interval. Methotrexate was omitted, given its correlation with interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis, especially as the patient already demonstrated abnormal pulmonary function and after discussion regarding potential adverse effects. Azathioprine and tacrolimus were not considered necessary, as mycophenolate was well-tolerated. For prophylaxis against Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim was prescribed initially, but discontinued when haemolytic anaemia developed (with no evidence of glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency). Nebulised pentamidine at 300 mg every 4 weeks was subsequently well tolerated.

After a 21-day hospital admission, the patient was discharged. Due to ongoing dysphagia, oral medications were administered via a nasogastric tube at the time of discharge.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following a 3-week period of hospitalisation and therapeutic intervention, the patient experienced a moderate reduction in hip flexor weakness and a significant decrease in cervical soft tissue swelling, though the swelling had not entirely subsided by the time of discharge. Despite these improvements, dysphagia persisted, requiring continued support with nasogastric tube feeding for an additional 2 months.

At 3 weeks post-discharge, the patient’s proximal muscle strength had completely returned to normal, and serum creatine kinase levels had decreased to 37 U/L. During the immunology follow-up visit 2 months after discharge, ongoing improvement in cervical swelling was noted, with only minimal residual violaceous facial erythema and periungual abnormalities observed on clinical examination. Swallowing had improved to the extent that the nasogastric tube could be discontinued and the patient could advance to soft solid foods. The therapeutic regimen included ongoing mycophenolate mofetil, monthly intravenous immunoglobulin, and a gradually tapering dose of prednisone.

At the three-month follow-up after discharge, the patient’s proximal muscle strength remained unchanged, chest assessment revealed no abnormalities, and the previously noted cervical soft tissue edema had entirely subsided. Prednisone was discontinued without complication seven months after discharge, coinciding with complete resolution of dysphagia. Subsequent pulmonary function testing indicated a slight improvement, yet continued to show a residual restrictive defect, which was considered consistent with his pre-existing physiology (figure 5a, b). A CT scan of the chest performed at eleven months revealed almost total clearance of prior basal ground-glass opacities and airspace consolidation, changes that had initially been ascribed to aspiration. At the twelve-month mark post-diagnosis, his respiratory status remained steady and he exhibited good exercise tolerance.

With regard to malignancy surveillance, he reported no constitutional symptoms at follow-up, and surveillance will proceed based on symptomatology, ongoing clinical assessment, periodic laboratory investigations, and appropriate imaging modalities such as serial CT scans of the chest. Planned evaluations also include gastroscopy and colonoscopy to rule out hidden gastrointestinal or colorectal cancer.

The therapeutic approach includes ongoing IVIG administration, with plans to gradually decrease the mycophenolate dosage over the next six months contingent on clinical response. He will remain under vigilant observation to assess for potential modifications in his pharmacologic management.

Discussion:

We describe a patient diagnosed with dermatomyositis positive for anti-SAE1 antibodies, who initially exhibited significant swelling of the cervical soft tissues, a presentation first interpreted as idiopathic angioedema. The eventual identification of dermatomyositis was made possible by considering it as a differential diagnosis and performing a thorough physical assessment that detected mild but specific cutaneous manifestations, which in turn enabled prompt diagnosis and management.

Anti-SAE1 antibodies are directed against the small-ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) activating enzyme, a molecule that closely resembles ubiquitin in structure and is responsible for the post-translational modification of various proteins via the process of ‘sumoylation’. The presence of SAE1 antibodies in dermatomyositis is most commonly associated with distinctive skin findings, such as heliotrope rash, Gottron’s papules, V-neck sign, and shawl sign. According to a comprehensive review of the literature comprising 215 cases, dysphagia and interstitial lung disease were present in 46% and 43% of patients, respectively, while malignancy was observed in 18% of cases.

Oedema of the subcutaneous tissues, including swelling of the face or neck, is rarely observed in patients with dermatomyositis. For instance, in a substantial cohort comprising 121 individuals diagnosed with dermatomyositis, angioedema was reported in only 8 cases (6.61%). An analysis of published reports between 1985 and 2013 identified just 19 documented instances of dermatomyositis presenting with oedema. The presence of autoantibodies such as anti-melanoma differentiation-associated gene (MDA)-5 antibody and anti-transcriptional intermediary factor 1γ (TIF1-γ) antibody has been linked to a higher prevalence of angioedema when compared with other antibodies. While facial oedema has been documented across various reports, swelling involving the neck appears to be less common. A review we conducted of 10 SAE1-positive dermatomyositis cases revealed oedema in only three, with just a single report noting swelling in the throat region, underscoring the infrequency of neck involvement.79

This case serves to alert emergency medicine physicians, ENT specialists, immunologists, and rheumatologists to consider dermatomyositis as a potential cause of neck swelling, particularly in cases of localized ‘angioedema’ where no clear etiology is identified. Given that manifestations such as dysphagia and additional systemic involvement can be subtle, it is essential to perform a thorough physical examination for the characteristic signs of dermatomyositis and to obtain creatine kinase measurements. In this patient, the observed subcutaneous oedema was attributable to muscle inflammation related to myositis, as opposed to angioedema mediated by histamine or bradykinin pathways.

Based on recommendations from the International Guideline for Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathy-Associated Cancer Screening by the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS), this patient demonstrated two high-risk factors (the presence of dermatomyositis and dysphagia) as well as two intermediate risk factors (positive for SAE1 antibody and male gender), categorizing him as high risk for malignancy. Consequently, malignancy workup was initiated, involving computed tomography imaging, tumour marker assays, and a PET scan, along with plans for upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopy. Thus far, both the comprehensive initial screening and ongoing interval assessments have continued to yield reassuring results.

The evidence base for determining the most effective pharmacological treatment in anti-SAE1–positive dermatomyositis remains sparse. The largest cohort study available demonstrates considerable variability in management strategies, with the majority of individuals receiving glucocorticoids alongside various steroid-sparing therapies. Methotrexate appeared most frequently, but mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, and azathioprine were also employed; intravenous immunoglobulin was reserved for certain situations. The therapeutic regimen for our patient comprised steroids, mycophenolate mofetil, intravenous immunoglobulin, and rituximab, ultimately leading to disease remission. The choice of mycophenolate mofetil was influenced by the patient's preference for an oral medication, its tolerability profile, and its recognized efficacy in dermatomyositis. Compared to others experiencing pronounced tissue oedema as part of dermatomyositis, our patient’s outlook is relatively favorable.
