Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook

Kevin Chan, Gemma Kwan, Hwei Choo Soh, Louise Evans

Abstract:

Summary: Dermatomyositis, an uncommon autoimmune disorder, primarily targets the skin, musculature, and respiratory tract. Effective management frequently necessitates early and aggressive immunosuppressive therapy to prevent rapid progression and damage to multiple organs. In this report, a previously healthy man in his 40s presented to the emergency department exhibiting pronounced swelling of the neck. Initial assessment by ear, nose, and throat specialists suggested a potential deep neck space infection, which was subsequently excluded. He was then referred to clinical immunology for further evaluation of presumed idiopathic angioedema. Due to the atypical characteristics of the neck edema—uncharacteristic of angioedema—and concurrent dermatologic and nail abnormalities, creatine kinase measurement was expedited. A working diagnosis of dermatomyositis was made; further workup revealed the presence of the small ubiquitin-like modifier-1 activating enzyme (SAE1) autoantibody and a muscle biopsy consistent with dermatomyositis. This case underscores an atypical manifestation of dermatomyositis, presenting as neck swelling independent of histamine or bradykinin mediation, associated with dysphagia and significant myopathy, and necessitating urgent immunosuppressive intervention.

Background:

Dermatomyositis is a form of idiopathic inflammatory myopathy that predominantly impacts the skin and musculature, though it may also involve the gastrointestinal system, joints, pulmonary structures, and cardiac tissue. The condition has an estimated occurrence rate of 10 cases per million individuals. The age of onset typically follows a bimodal pattern, manifesting most frequently in children between 5 and 14 years and adults between 45 and 64 years. Classic dermatological manifestations encompass Gottron’s papules, the shawl sign, heliotrope rash, and periungual telangiectasias. Additional clinical features include proximal muscle weakness, interstitial lung disease, and swallowing difficulties. Pseudoangioedema represents a rare manifestation in dermatomyositis; one particular study identified its presence in just 8 of 121 patients with the disease. When present in the neck, pseudoangioedema can mimic primary disorders of otolaryngology or be confused with angioedema induced by histaminergic or bradykinin pathways.

Case presentation:

A man in his 40s presented to the emergency department reporting a three-week progression of neck swelling alongside proximal muscle weakness in the hip flexors and mild muscle pain. His medical background was unremarkable, and he had never smoked. He was not on any 3-hydroxy-3-methyl-glutaryl-coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase inhibitors. Ludwig’s angina was initially suspected, prompting a CT scan of the neck, which demonstrated widespread edema affecting the strap muscles, with associated inflammation extending to the retropharyngeal space (see figure 1). The patient was afebrile, maintained an oxygen saturation of 99% on ambient air, and exhibited no tachypnea. Nailfold capillaroscopy revealed both proximal nailfold microhaemorrhages and areas lacking vascularity. Laboratory results included a white cell count of $9.1\times10^9$/L and a C-reactive protein (CRP) level of $55.6$ mg/L on admission. Persistent unexplained swelling prompted transfer to a quaternary hospital specializing in ear, nose and throat (ENT) surgery for further assessment. Flexible nasendoscopy conducted by the ENT team did not reveal structural abnormalities of the soft palate, vallecula, larynx, or post-cricoid region; however, there were notably increased secretions and coughing observed during the procedure.

The patient’s presentation prompted consultation with the on-call immunologist owing to suspected angioedema, given the absence of definitive structural or infectious etiologies. Upon reassessment, widespread facial erythema, including the nasolabial folds, was documented, alongside a diffusely erythematous, edematous, yet non-tender neck. The patient specifically reported not using any medications such as angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and had neither a personal nor familial history indicative of angioedema. Collectively, his clinical manifestations were not consistent with either hereditary or acquired forms of angioedema. A detailed clinical evaluation detected subtle periungual erythema and nailfold abnormalities, in addition to a rash localized on the lateral aspect of the right thigh and diminished hip flexion strength scored at 4/5. Classic findings such as Gottron’s papules and heliotrope rash were not observed. Laboratory investigations revealed a marked increase in creatine kinase, measuring 16 184 U/L on the subsequent day. Renal function testing yielded normal results and myoglobin was not detected in the urine. The patient also exhibited raised hepatic enzymes, with alanine aminotransferase (ALT) at 143 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) at 396 U/L, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) at 692 U/L.

Investigations:

Due to the suspicion of angioedema, a series of investigations were undertaken to identify potential causes of spontaneous angioedema. Complement components C3 and C4 were found to be within normal limits, as were both the function and quantity of C1 esterase.

The patient demonstrated a positive anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) test, with a titre of 1:1280 and a speckled immunofluorescence pattern. Anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA) testing was negative. A myositis antibody profile targeting 16 autoantigens associated with inflammatory myopathies was conducted using the Euroimmun Euroline immunoassay. This yielded a strong positive result for anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibodies, with a signal intensity of 173 (reference range <35). No reactivity was observed for other myositis-specific antibodies, including anti-Mi-2 alpha (anti-Mi-2α), anti-Transcription Intermediary Factor-1 gamma (anti-TIF1-γ), anti–Histidyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-Jo-1), and anti–Isoleucyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-OJ) antibodies. The patient’s immunoglobulin G (IgG) levels and subclasses were also found to be within the normal range. 

For immunosuppression screening, the results were negative for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), hepatitis C core antibody (HBcAb), hepatitis B e antibody (HBeAb), hepatitis C virus (HCV), HIV, varicella zoster virus (VZV) IgG, and cytomegalovirus (CMV) IgG and immunoglobulin M (IgM). The QuantiFERON-TB Gold test produced an indeterminate result; nevertheless, all three sputum samples tested negative for acid-fast bacilli consistent with Mycobacterium tuberculosis.

Pulmonary function testing demonstrated a restrictive pattern originating outside the thoracic cavity, without any notable improvement following bronchodilator administration. Chest CT imaging identified several non-cavitating nodules within the lungs, predominantly situated in the mid and lower regions (the largest being 15 mm in diameter in the right lower lobe), as well as irregular peribronchial consolidation consistent with aspiration pneumonitis; no radiological indicators of pulmonary fibrosis were observed. Abdominal CT did not reveal any lymphadenopathy within the abdomino-pelvic region, nor were there any signs of either primary or metastatic malignancy in the abdominal or pelvic organs. A video fluoroscopic (barium) swallow assessment detected marked pharyngeal dysphagia, resulting in substantial residue within the pyriform fossae and post-deglutitive penetration of both thin and mildly thick liquids to the level of the vocal cords.

MRI scans of the thighs revealed evidence of active myositis predominantly affecting the bilateral obturator internus and externus muscles, with less severe involvement observed in the hip adductors, bilateral gracilis muscles, and bilateral distal iliopsoas muscles (see figure 2). Further confirmation of ongoing muscle inflammation was provided by fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)-positron emission tomography (PET), which demonstrated diffuse, abnormal uptake of FDG across multiple muscle groups. The most pronounced tracer accumulation was detected in the head and neck regions, bilateral deltoids, forearms, chest wall, abdomen, and pelvis (refer to figure 3). There was no indication of metabolically active pathology elsewhere to suggest malignancy. Levels of tumour markers, including carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), cancer antigen 19-9 (CA19-9), cancer antigen 125 (CA-125), and prostate-specific antigen (PSA), were within the normal range.

Histopathological examination of a thigh muscle biopsy showed areas containing moderate numbers of necrotic, vacuolated degenerating, myophagocytosed, and regenerating myofibres exhibiting a perifascicular pattern, with scattered perimysial inflammatory infiltrates comprising dispersed histiocytes (illustrated in figure 4). Immunohistochemical staining for membrane attack complex revealed the presence of complement component 5b-9 (C5b-9) deposited in perifascicular endomysial capillaries, which is a characteristic finding of dermatomyositis. The collective clinical, radiological, and pathological features led to the diagnosis of moderate to severe SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis, associated with inflammatory edema, peribronchial consolidation attributable to micro-aspiration, dysphagia, and myositis. Notable cutaneous findings included a positive Holster sign and periungual erythema.

Differential diagnosis:

Upon his subacute onset marked by cervical swelling and elevated CRP levels, initial diagnostic considerations focused on Ludwig angina and idiopathic angioedema. Notably, significant muscle weakness and dysphagia were absent in the early clinical course. The differential diagnosis was broad, encompassing various inflammatory myopathies (such as immune-mediated necrotising myopathy and inclusion body myositis), infectious etiologies (including neck cellulitis/erysipelas, deep neck space infections, and bacterial sialadenitis), neuromuscular junction disorders like myasthenia gravis, as well as hypersensitivity reactions (for example, drug rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms), acute contact dermatitis, orofacial granulomatosis, hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis, and superior vena cava syndrome.

Treatment:

Given the extensive involvement of airway musculature, the patient received intravenous methylprednisolone at a daily dose of 1000 mg for three consecutive days. This was followed by a tapering course of prednisone, initiated at 60 mg per day and decreased by 5 mg every 2 to 3 weeks. The patient was administered an initial intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG, Gamunex 10%) loading dose of 2 g/kg (totaling 165 g), distributed over five days (35 g per day), followed by an additional five cycles at four-week intervals, each comprising a total cumulative dose of 2 g/kg (=165 g) divided across the treatments. Mycophenolate mofetil was introduced at 1 g twice daily, and two intravenous infusions of rituximab at 1000 mg were administered two weeks apart. Methotrexate was deliberately withheld due to its association with interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis, especially given the patient’s abnormal pulmonary function tests and after patient consultation regarding possible adverse effects. Mycophenolate mofetil was chosen over azathioprine and tacrolimus as it was well-tolerated. For the prevention of Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim was initially prescribed but subsequently discontinued because of haemolytic anaemia, which occurred in the absence of glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency. The patient was then able to tolerate nebulized pentamidine at 300 mg every four weeks.

After a hospitalization lasting 21 days, the patient was discharged. Due to ongoing dysphagia, all oral medications were administered via nasogastric tube at the time of discharge.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following three weeks of inpatient care and intervention, the patient exhibited partial resolution of hip flexor weakness and significant reduction in cervical soft tissue swelling, although some swelling persisted at discharge. Dysphagia was ongoing, requiring continuation of nutrition via a nasogastric tube for an additional two months.

By three weeks post-discharge, proximal muscle strength had completely returned and serum creatine kinase had decreased to 37 U/L, indicating normalization. At a follow-up immunology assessment two months after discharge, cervical swelling had further diminished, and physical examination revealed only minor residual violaceous erythema of the face and periungual alterations. The patient’s swallowing ability had sufficiently improved to permit withdrawal of the nasogastric tube and transition to a diet of soft solids. The prescribed regimen included ongoing mycophenolate mofetil, monthly intravenous immunoglobulin, and a gradually reduced dose of prednisone.

At 3 months after discharge, proximal muscle strength remained unchanged, chest assessment was normal, and the cervical soft tissue swelling had fully subsided. Prednisone therapy was successfully tapered off 7 months after discharge, coinciding with complete resolution of dysphagia. Follow-up pulmonary function testing indicated mild improvement; however, a persistent restrictive defect was noted, likely representing his underlying physiological baseline (figure 5a, b). An 11-month follow-up CT scan of the chest revealed almost total resolution of the basal ground-glass opacities and airspace consolidation that were previously observed and initially attributed to aspiration. Twelve months after the initial diagnosis, his respiratory status was stable and he maintained satisfactory exercise tolerance.

In terms of malignancy surveillance, he reported no constitutional symptoms at follow-up visits and will continue routine monitoring, which will include clinical evaluations, serial blood work, and appropriate imaging modalities such as periodic CT scans of the chest. Planned diagnostic procedures include gastroscopy and colonoscopy to rule out occult gastrointestinal or colorectal malignancy.

The management strategy includes ongoing administration of IVIG and a gradual reduction of the mycophenolate dose over the next 6 months, contingent on clinical response. He will remain under close observation to assess the necessity for any alterations in his treatment regimen.

Discussion:

We describe a case involving dermatomyositis positive for anti-SAE1 antibodies that presented predominantly as significant cervical soft tissue swelling, an initial clinical picture that led to its misdiagnosis as idiopathic angioedema. Identification of dermatomyositis within the spectrum of differential diagnoses, in conjunction with thorough physical assessment that identified understated dermatological manifestations, enabled prompt initiation of appropriate management.

Anti-SAE1 antibodies are directed against the small-ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) activating enzyme, a protein closely related in structure to ubiquitin, and essential for the post-translational modification process termed ‘sumoylation’ affecting a variety of substrate proteins. Dermatomyositis cases with SAE1 antibody positivity are most commonly noted for distinctive skin manifestations such as heliotrope rash, Gottron’s papules, V-neck sign, and shawl sign. A comprehensive literature survey involving 215 individuals reported dysphagia and interstitial lung disease in 46% and 43% of SAE1-associated dermatomyositis cases, respectively, while malignancies were identified in 18% of these patients.

Oedema of the subcutaneous tissues, notably manifesting as facial or neck swelling, occurs infrequently in patients with dermatomyositis. Analysis of a substantial cohort comprising 121 individuals diagnosed with dermatomyositis revealed that angioedema was observed in only 8 cases (6.61%). Furthermore, a review of literature spanning publications from 1985 to 2013 identified merely 19 reported instances in which dermatomyositis presented with oedema. Certain antibodies, including anti-melanoma differentiation-associated gene (MDA)-5 antibody and anti-transcriptional intermediary factor 1γ (TIF1-γ) antibody, exhibited a higher association with angioedema in comparison to other antibody profiles. While facial oedema has been documented in several reported cases, swelling specifically involving the neck region is rarely noted. Upon reviewing 10 published cases of SAE1-positive dermatomyositis, only three mentioned the occurrence of oedema, and just one referred explicitly to swelling in the area surrounding the throat, underscoring the rarity of neck involvement.79

This case serves to inform emergency physicians, ENT surgeons, immunologists, and rheumatologists that dermatomyositis should be considered as a potential differential diagnosis for neck swelling, particularly in instances of localized ‘angioedema’ without an identifiable cause. Given that manifestations such as dysphagia and other systemic involvement can be mild or easily overlooked, careful evaluation for physical indications of dermatomyositis and measurement of creatine kinase are imperative. In our patient's situation, the observed subcutaneous oedema was attributable to underlying muscle inflammation associated with myositis, rather than being the result of histaminergic or bradykinin-driven angioedema.

Following the recommendations outlined in the International Guideline for Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathy-Associated Cancer Screening by the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS), the patient was found to have two high-risk features (dermatomyositis, dysphagia) and two intermediate-risk characteristics (SAE1 antibody positivity, male gender), thereby categorizing him as high risk for malignancy. A thorough malignancy workup was conducted, including CT imaging, tumour marker analysis, and PET scan, with subsequent plans for both upper and lower GI endoscopic evaluation. To date, comprehensive baseline assessments and periodic follow-up have provided ongoing reassurance.

Current evidence regarding the optimal pharmacological management of anti-SAE1–positive dermatomyositis remains sparse. The most comprehensive cohort reported so far demonstrated considerable variability in therapeutic strategies: while glucocorticoids were a mainstay, they were frequently combined with various steroid-sparing agents—including methotrexate, mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, and azathioprine. In some instances, intravenous immunoglobulin was also administered. In the present case, the patient was managed with a combination of steroids, mycophenolate mofetil, intravenous immunoglobulin, and rituximab, ultimately resulting in disease remission. The choice of mycophenolate mofetil was guided by the patient's desire for a daily oral therapy, as well as considerations of its favorable tolerability and its established efficacy in dermatomyositis. Notably, compared to individuals experiencing pronounced tissue oedema as a complication of dermatomyositis, our patient’s outlook is relatively favorable.
