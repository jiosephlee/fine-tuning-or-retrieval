Title: Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook

Kevin Chan, Gemma Kwan, Hwei Choo Soh, Louise Evans

Abstract:

Summary: Dermatomyositis is an uncommon autoimmune disorder primarily involving the skin, muscles, and respiratory tract. Management generally necessitates rapid initiation of potent immunosuppressive therapy to prevent disease advancement and damage to multiple organs. A previously healthy man in his 40s presented acutely to the emergency department with pronounced neck swelling. Initial evaluation by ear, nose, and throat specialists suggested a possible deep neck space infection; however, this etiology was excluded, after which he was referred to clinical immunology for assessment and management of presumed idiopathic angioedema. Given the atypical presentation of neck oedema inconsistent with angioedema, along with dermatologic and nail abnormalities, a creatine kinase measurement was swiftly ordered. The patient was provisionally diagnosed with dermatomyositis and subsequently found to be positive for small ubiquitin-like modifier-1 activating enzyme (SAE1) autoantibody, with confirmatory muscle biopsy findings. This report underscores the unusual manifestation of neck swelling unrelated to histamine or bradykinin mechanisms in dermatomyositis, presenting with dysphagia and substantial muscle involvement necessitating urgent intervention.

Background:

Dermatomyositis is an idiopathic inflammatory myopathy that primarily affects the skin and skeletal muscles, but may also involve the gastrointestinal tract, joints, lungs, and cardiac tissue. The condition has an estimated frequency of 10 cases per million individuals. The disease shows a bimodal age distribution, with one incidence peak among children between 5 and 14 years and another among adults aged 45 to 64 years. Distinctive skin manifestations include Gottron’s papules, the shawl sign, heliotrope rash, and periungual telangiectasias. Additional clinical features are proximal muscle weakness, interstitial lung disease, and difficulty swallowing. Pseudoangioedema is a rare presentation in dermatomyositis; a single study identified this feature in only 8 out of 121 patients with the disease. When pseudoangioedema involves the neck, it can mimic primary disorders managed by otolaryngologists or be confused with angioedema of either histaminergic or bradykinin origin.

Case presentation:

A man in his 40s presented to the emergency department with a 3-week progression of neck swelling and proximal muscle weakness affecting his hip flexors, also reporting mild muscle pain. His past medical history was unremarkable, and he had never smoked. He denied using any 3-hydroxy-3-methyl-glutaryl-coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase inhibitors. Ludwig’s angina was initially suspected, leading to a CT scan of the neck, which demonstrated diffuse swelling involving the strap muscles and inflammatory changes extending into the retropharyngeal space (figure 1). On assessment, he was afebrile, maintained oxygen saturations of 99% on room air, and did not exhibit tachypnea. Nailfold capillaroscopy revealed microhaemorrhages at the proximal nailfolds and areas of avascularity. Laboratory analysis showed a white blood cell count of 9.1×10^9/L and C-reactive protein (CRP) of 55.6 mg/L upon admission. As neck swelling persisted without a clear etiology, he was referred to a quaternary care center with otolaryngology (ENT) surgical support for further assessment. Flexible nasendoscopy conducted by the ENT service found no major structural abnormalities of the soft palate, vallecula, larynx, or post-cricoid region; however, increased secretions and coughing were observed during the procedure.

The case was reviewed by the on-call immunologist due to concerns for angioedema, as no apparent structural or infectious etiology was identified. Examination revealed widespread erythema over the face, extending across the nasolabial folds, as well as an erythematous, edematous, and non-tender neck. The patient reported not taking any medications, including angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs. He had no personal or familial history of angioedema. Taken together, his presentation did not fit typical features of either acquired or hereditary angioedema. Detailed physical assessment identified mild nailfold abnormalities consistent with periungual erythema, a rash localized to the right lateral thigh, and decreased strength in hip flexors measured at 4/5. Both Gottron’s papules and heliotrope rash were not observed. Serum creatine kinase was ordered, and on the following day was markedly raised at 16,184 U/L. Renal parameters were normal and urine testing for myoglobin returned negative. Liver function tests showed elevated transaminase levels: ALT measured at 143 U/L, AST at 396 U/L, and LDH at 692 U/L.

Investigations:

Due to initial suspicion of angioedema, a series of investigations were undertaken to identify potential etiologies of spontaneous angioedema. The patient's C3 and C4 complement levels were within normal parameters, and both functional and quantitative assessments of C1 esterase yielded results inside the reference range.

Testing revealed a positive anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) at a titre of 1:1280 with a speckled pattern. Anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA) testing was negative. A comprehensive myositis antibody panel targeting 16 antigens associated with autoimmune inflammatory myopathies was conducted using the Euroimmun Euroline immunoassay; this demonstrated a markedly positive result for anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibodies with a signal intensity of 173 (reference range <35). Other myositis antibodies, including anti-Mi-2 alpha (anti-Mi-2α), anti-Transcription Intermediary Factor-1 gamma (anti-TIF1-γ), anti–Histidyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-Jo-1), and anti–Isoleucyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-OJ), were all negative. The patient's total immunoglobulin G (IgG) and subclass levels were found to be normal. Regarding evaluation for immunosuppression, testing was negative for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), hepatitis C core antibody (HBcAb), hepatitis B e antibody (HBeAb), hepatitis C virus (HCV), HIV, varicella zoster virus (VZV) IgG, and cytomegalovirus (CMV) IgG and immunoglobulin M (IgM). QuantiFERON-TB Gold results were indeterminate, but three sputum samples for acid-fast bacilli detecting Mycobacterium tuberculosis yielded negative findings.

Pulmonary function testing demonstrated an extrathoracic restrictive defect, without a notable response to bronchodilators. Chest CT identified several non-cavitating nodules predominantly located in the mid and lower lung zones, with the largest nodule (15 mm) in the right lower lobe, and scattered peribronchial consolidations suggestive of aspiration pneumonitis; pulmonary fibrosis was not observed radiographically. Abdominal CT revealed normal-appearing abdomino-pelvic lymph nodes and did not show any signs of primary or secondary malignancy in the abdomen or pelvis. A video fluoroscopic (barium) swallow examination detected profound pharyngeal dysphagia, resulting in substantial residue within the pyriform fossae as well as penetration of thin and mildly thick liquids to the level of the vocal cords following swallowing.

MRI of the thighs demonstrated findings indicative of ongoing myositis affecting both obturator internus and externus muscles, with less pronounced involvement seen in the hip adductors, bilateral gracilis muscles, and the distal portions of the iliopsoas muscles on both sides (see figure 2). Further confirmation of active muscular inflammation was obtained from fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) positron emission tomography (PET), revealing widespread and abnormal FDG uptake across multiple muscle groups, most notably in the head and neck, bilateral deltoids, forearms, chest wall, abdomen, and pelvis (refer to figure 3). No areas of metabolically active disease suggestive of malignancy were detected. Testing for tumour markers—carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), cancer antigen 19-9 (CA19-9), cancer antigen 125 (CA-125), and prostate-specific antigen (PSA)—yielded results within normal limits.

Thigh muscle biopsy revealed areas with a moderate population of necrotic, vacuolated, degenerating, myophagocytosed, and regenerating muscle fibers showing perifascicular distribution, alongside scattered inflammation in the perimysium characterized by dispersed histiocytes (see figure 4). Immunohistochemical staining for the membrane attack complex identified deposition of complement component 5b-9 (C5b-9) in perifascicular endomysial capillaries, a finding characteristic of dermatomyositis. The patient was diagnosed with moderate to severe dermatomyositis positive for SAE1 antibody, accompanied by inflammatory edema, peribronchial consolidation attributed to micro-aspiration, dysphagia, and myositis. Cutaneous features included a positive Holster sign and periungual erythema.

Differential diagnosis:

Due to his subacute onset of neck swelling accompanied by increased CRP, initial diagnostic considerations included Ludwig angina and idiopathic angioedema. Early in the course, muscle weakness and dysphagia were not significant findings. The differential diagnosis extended to other inflammatory muscle diseases, such as immune-mediated necrotising myopathy and inclusion body myositis; infectious etiologies like cellulitis/erysipelas involving the neck, deep neck space infections, and bacterial sialadenitis; disorders affecting the neuromuscular junction including myasthenia gravis; delayed hypersensitivity reactions to medications such as drug rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms; acute contact dermatitis; orofacial granulomatosis; hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis; and superior vena cava syndrome.

Treatment:

Given substantial involvement of airway musculature, the patient received intravenous methylprednisolone for 3 days at a daily dosage of 1000 mg. This was followed by transitioning to a tapering regimen of prednisone, initiated at 60 mg per day and reduced by 5 mg every 2 to 3 weeks. Loading dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG, Gamunex 10%) was administered at 2 g/kg, totaling 165 g, delivered over 5 days (35 g daily), succeeded by an additional 5 cycles, spaced 4 weeks apart, each with a total dose of 2 g/kg (165 g), divided among these infusions. The patient began therapy with mycophenolate mofetil 1 g twice daily and received two intravenous rituximab infusions of 1000 mg each, 2 weeks apart. Methotrexate was withheld due to its association with interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis—especially relevant given the patient’s abnormal lung function tests—and after discussion with the patient concerning potential side effects. Azathioprine and tacrolimus were omitted as mycophenolate was well tolerated. For prophylaxis against Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim was initiated but discontinued following the onset of haemolytic anaemia (without evidence of glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency), after which nebulized pentamidine at 300 mg every 4 weeks was tolerated.

Following a 21-day hospital admission, he was discharged. Due to persistent dysphagia at discharge, his oral medications were administered via nasogastric tube.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following three weeks of inpatient care and therapy, the patient exhibited noticeable gains in hip flexor strength and significant reduction in cervical soft tissue edema; however, swelling was still present upon discharge. Dysphagia persisted, which made continued nasogastric tube nutrition necessary for an additional two months.

At a follow-up three weeks post-discharge, proximal muscle power was completely restored and serum creatine kinase had decreased to 37 U/L. During an immunology assessment conducted two months after leaving the hospital, further reduction in cervical swelling was observed, with only lingering violaceous facial erythema and periungual alterations noted on physical examination. The patient’s swallowing ability had improved enough to discontinue nasogastric feeds and introduce soft solid foods. Mycophenolate mofetil, monthly intravenous immunoglobulin, and a gradually decreasing dose of prednisone were maintained as part of the treatment regimen.

At 3 months after hospital discharge, the patient’s proximal muscle strength remained unchanged, chest examination findings were within normal limits, and the cervical soft tissue swelling had completely subsided. Prednisone therapy was tapered off successfully by 7 months post-discharge, at which point the patient’s dysphagia had entirely resolved. Follow-up pulmonary function testing indicated slight improvement, with persistent restrictive abnormalities considered to reflect his baseline status (figure 5a, b). An 11-month chest CT scan showed near-complete disappearance of the basal ground-glass opacities and consolidations that were previously ascribed to aspiration. One year after diagnosis, respiratory function was stable, and the patient maintained good exercise capacity.

With respect to malignancy surveillance, the patient continued to deny any constitutional symptoms during follow-up and is scheduled for ongoing monitoring involving clinical assessment, laboratory investigations, and interval imaging, including serial chest CT scans. Additionally, gastroscopy and colonoscopy are planned to rule out occult gastrointestinal or colorectal cancers.

The management plan includes continuation of IVIG and a gradual reduction of mycophenolate dosage over the next 6 months, guided by the patient’s clinical course. He will be monitored closely to assess the need for further modifications to his medication regimen.

Discussion:

We present a case involving dermatomyositis with anti-SAE1 antibody positivity, which manifested as significant swelling of the cervical soft tissue. This was initially misdiagnosed as idiopathic angioedema. Considering dermatomyositis among the differential diagnoses and conducting a detailed physical examination that identified subtle skin findings facilitated a prompt and accurate diagnosis, enabling early initiation of therapy.

Anti-SAE1 antibodies are directed against the small ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) activating enzyme, a molecule closely related to ubiquitin that is responsible for post-translational sumoylation of multiple target proteins. Dermatomyositis with SAE1 antibody positivity frequently exhibits distinctive skin manifestations including the heliotrope rash, Gottron’s papules, the V-neck sign, and the shawl sign. A comprehensive literature review of 215 patients demonstrated that 46% had dysphagia, 43% had interstitial lung disease, and malignancy was identified in 18% of those with SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis.

Edema of the subcutaneous tissues, particularly presenting as facial or neck swelling, is rarely seen in dermatomyositis. Among 121 individuals with dermatomyositis in a cohort analysis, angioedema was documented in just 8 cases (6.61%). A review of published reports spanning 1985 to 2013 identified only 19 instances where dermatomyositis was accompanied by edema. Autoantibodies, including anti-melanoma differentiation-associated gene (MDA)-5 and anti-transcriptional intermediary factor 1γ (TIF1-γ), have shown a higher correlation with angioedema than other autoantibodies. While facial edema was noted in multiple cases, swelling localized to the neck has been reported less frequently. In our literature survey of 10 dermatomyositis cases with SAE1-positivity, edema was observed in only three, with a single case referencing swelling in the throat area, underscoring the rarity of neck region involvement.79

This case highlights for emergency physicians, otolaryngologists, immunologists, and rheumatologists that dermatomyositis should be considered in the differential diagnosis of neck swelling, particularly localized 'angioedema' that lacks a clear etiology or is deemed idiopathic. Dysphagia and other manifestations may be mild, making it important to look for physical features of dermatomyositis and to check creatine kinase levels. In this patient, the subcutaneous edema was the result of muscle inflammation from myositis, not from histamine- or bradykinin-mediated angioedema.

Following the International Guideline for Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathy-Associated Cancer Screening by the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS), the patient had two high-risk criteria (dermatomyositis, dysphagia) and two intermediate-risk criteria (positive SAE1 antibody, male gender), indicating a high risk for cancer. He underwent a comprehensive malignancy evaluation, including CT imaging, tumour marker assays, and PET scanning, with plans to complete both upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopies. Extensive initial and follow-up monitoring have, so far, shown no evidence of malignancy.

Evidence supporting best pharmacological management for anti-SAE1–positive dermatomyositis is sparse. The largest reported series thus far demonstrated diverse treatment regimens, with the majority of individuals administered glucocorticoids alongside various steroid-sparing therapies, most frequently methotrexate, but also including mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, and azathioprine; selected cases received intravenous immunoglobulin. In this instance, the patient was managed with steroids, mycophenolate mofetil, intravenous immunoglobulin, and rituximab and achieved remission. The choice of mycophenolate was informed by the patient’s preference for daily oral therapy as well as its favorable tolerability profile and recognized role in dermatomyositis. Compared to others with pronounced tissue edema associated with dermatomyositis, this patient fortunately exhibits a favorable outlook.
