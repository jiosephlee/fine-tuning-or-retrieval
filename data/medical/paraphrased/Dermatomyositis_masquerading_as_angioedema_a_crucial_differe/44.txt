Title: Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook

Kevin Chan, Gemma Kwan, Hwei Choo Soh, Louise Evans

Abstract:

Summary: Dermatomyositis is an uncommon autoimmune disorder that most often involves the skin, muscles, and respiratory tract. Individuals with dermatomyositis generally necessitate urgent initiation of aggressive immunosuppressive therapy in order to slow disease progression and limit multiorgan involvement. A male in his 40s, previously in good health, presented to the emergency department with marked cervical swelling. Initially, ear, nose, and throat surgeons evaluated him for a likely deep neck space infection, but this was excluded as a cause. He was then referred to clinical immunology for assessment and management of presumed idiopathic angioedema. Due to the unusual character of the neck edema—which did not align with typical angioedema—and the simultaneous presence of skin and nail findings, a creatine kinase test was ordered without delay. Dermatomyositis was tentatively diagnosed, and this was later confirmed with the identification of a positive small ubiquitin-like modifier-1 activating enzyme (SAE1) autoantibody and characteristic muscle biopsy features. This report underscores the presentation of neck edema not mediated by histamine or bradykinin as a rare manifestation of dermatomyositis, appearing with dysphagia and prominent muscular involvement that warranted immediate therapy.

Background:

Dermatomyositis is an idiopathic inflammatory myopathy that primarily targets the skin and skeletal muscles, but may also involve organs such as the gastrointestinal tract, joints, lungs, and heart. Its incidence is estimated at 10 cases per million individuals. The disease displays a bimodal age distribution, presenting most frequently in children between 5 and 14 years old and adults aged 45 to 64. Notable dermatologic manifestations include Gottron’s papules, the shawl sign, heliotrope rash, and periungual telangiectasias. Additional symptoms can encompass proximal muscle weakness, interstitial lung involvement, and difficulty swallowing. Pseudoangioedema occurs rarely in dermatomyositis; for instance, one study identified this feature in only 8 of 121 patients with the diagnosis. When it affects the neck, pseudoangioedema may be initially interpreted as a primary otolaryngological issue or confused with angioedema due to histamine or bradykinin.

Case presentation:

A man in his 40s presented to the emergency department with three weeks of progressively worsening neck swelling and weakness in his hip flexors, along with mild muscle aches. He had no relevant medical history and had never smoked. He was not taking any 3-hydroxy-3-methyl-glutaryl-coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase inhibitors. Initially, Ludwig’s angina was suspected. A computed tomography (CT) scan of the neck revealed widespread oedema affecting the strap muscles, with inflammatory changes spreading into the retropharyngeal space (figure 1). He had no fever, oxygen saturation was 99% on room air, and he was not experiencing tachypnoea. Nailfold capillaroscopy detected microhaemorrhages and avascular regions in the proximal nailfolds. On admission, his white blood cell count measured 9.1×10^9/L and C-reactive protein (CRP) was 55.6 mg/L. Because the neck swelling persisted without a clear cause, he was transferred to a quaternary care hospital with ear, nose, and throat (ENT) surgical capabilities for additional assessment. ENT conducted a flexible nasendoscopy, which revealed no noteworthy structural abnormalities in the soft palate, vallecula, larynx, or post-cricoid area. However, the procedure was marked by increased secretions and episodes of coughing.

The on-call immunologist was consulted due to concerns about angioedema, as no obvious structural or infectious etiology had been identified. Upon assessment, the patient exhibited diffuse facial erythema, including involvement of the nasolabial folds, and his neck appeared swollen, erythematous, and was not tender to palpation. He reported not taking any medications, such as angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and denied both personal and family history of angioedema. Collectively, his presentation did not fit with typical features of either acquired or hereditary angioedema. Detailed physical examination uncovered subtle nailfold abnormalities consistent with periungual erythema, a rash on the right lateral thigh, and decreased hip flexion strength rated at 4/5. Gottron’s papules and a heliotrope rash were not observed. The following day, his creatine kinase was markedly raised at 16 184 U/L. Renal function testing was within normal limits, and urine myoglobin screening yielded a negative result. Liver function tests revealed elevated transaminases, with alanine aminotransferase (ALT) at 143 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) at 396 U/L, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) at 692 U/L.

Investigations:

Due to the preliminary suspicion of angioedema, multiple investigations were undertaken to identify potential etiologies for spontaneous angioedema. Levels of complement C3 and C4 were found to be within normal limits, and assessment of C1 esterase activity and quantity also yielded normal results.

Testing revealed that the patient’s anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) was positive, exhibiting a titre of 1:1280 and a speckled staining pattern. Anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA) was negative. A myositis antibody panel, targeting 16 antigens associated with autoimmune inflammatory myopathies and performed using the Euroimmun Euroline immunoassay, demonstrated a strong positivity for anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibodies with a signal intensity of 173 (reference range <35). All other myositis-specific antibodies were negative, including anti-Mi-2α, anti-TIF1-γ, anti-Jo-1, and anti-OJ. Measurement of immunoglobulin G (IgG), including all subclasses, revealed normal levels.

Regarding his immunosuppression screening, results for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), hepatitis C core antibody (HBcAb), hepatitis B e antibody (HBeAb), hepatitis C virus (HCV), HIV, varicella zoster virus (VZV) IgG, and cytomegalovirus (CMV) IgG/IgM were all negative. The QuantiFERON-TB Gold assay was indeterminate, but examination of three sputum samples for acid-fast bacilli (Mycobacterium tuberculosis) showed negative findings.

Pulmonary function testing demonstrated an extrathoracic restrictive defect, with no notable response to bronchodilator therapy. Chest CT identified several non-cavitating nodules within the lungs, predominantly located in the mid to lower zones—the largest measuring 15 mm in diameter in the right lower lobe—as well as patchy peribronchial consolidation areas suggestive of aspiration pneumonitis; there was no imaging evidence indicating pulmonary fibrosis. Abdominal CT revealed no abdomino-pelvic lymphadenopathy and did not demonstrate any primary or metastatic malignancy within the abdominal or pelvic regions. Video fluoroscopic (barium) swallow assessment uncovered marked pharyngeal dysphagia, resulting in significant residue within the pyriform fossae and post-swallow penetration of thin and mildly thick liquids to the level of the vocal cords.

MRI of the thighs revealed findings indicative of ongoing myositis involving both the obturator internus and externus muscles, with less pronounced inflammation affecting the hip adductor group, both gracilis muscles, and the distal portions of the bilateral iliopsoas muscles (see figure 2). Further evidence of active muscle inflammation was provided by fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)-positron emission tomography (PET) imaging, which demonstrated diffuse and abnormal FDG uptake across multiple muscle groups, most notably in the head and neck, both deltoids, forearm muscles, the chest wall, abdomen, and pelvis (refer to figure 3). No FDG-avid lesions were identified elsewhere to raise concern for malignancy. Tumor markers, including carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), cancer antigen 19-9 (CA19-9), cancer antigen 125 (CA-125), and prostate-specific antigen (PSA), were within normal limits.

A biopsy sample taken from the thigh muscle demonstrated localized areas containing moderate numbers of necrotic, vacuolated, degenerating, myophagocytosed, and regenerating muscle fibers predominantly arranged in a perifascicular pattern, as well as scattered perimysial inflammatory infiltrates composed of dispersed histiocytes (figure 4). Immunohistochemical staining for membrane attack complex identified deposition of complement component 5b-9 (C5b-9) in the capillaries of the perifascicular endomysium, which is characteristic of dermatomyositis. The diagnosis was established as moderate to severe SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis, accompanied by inflammatory edema, peribronchial consolidation attributable to micro-aspiration, dysphagia, and muscle inflammation. Skin findings included a positive Holster sign and erythema around the nailfolds.

Differential diagnosis:

Due to the patient’s subacute onset with cervical swelling and raised CRP, initial suspicion focused on Ludwig angina and idiopathic angioedema. Notably, muscle weakness and dysphagia were not prominent at the onset. The differential diagnosis encompassed other inflammatory myopathies, including immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy and inclusion body myositis; infectious etiologies such as neck cellulitis/erysipelas, deep neck space infection, and bacterial sialadenitis; neuromuscular junction disorders like myasthenia gravis; delayed drug hypersensitivity reactions, for example drug rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms; acute contact dermatitis; orofacial granulomatosis; hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis; and superior vena cava syndrome.

Treatment:

Given the marked involvement of airway musculature, the patient received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg per day for 3 days. This was transitioned to an oral prednisone taper, initiated at 60 mg daily and reduced by 5 mg every 2 to 3 weeks. Initial therapy included intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG, Gamunex 10%) administered at 2 g/kg (total dose 165 g) over a 5-day period (35 g per day). This was followed by an additional 5 cycles, each 4 weeks apart, with a cumulative dose of 2 g/kg (165 g) distributed among these cycles. Mycophenolate mofetil was started at 1 g twice daily. He also received two intravenous doses of rituximab, each 1000 mg, separated by two weeks. Methotrexate was withheld because of its known association with interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis, particularly relevant as the patient had abnormal pulmonary function testing; this decision was also made after discussing side effects with the patient. Neither azathioprine nor tacrolimus was selected, as mycophenolate was adequately tolerated. For prophylaxis against Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, the patient was initially given sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim, but this was discontinued following development of haemolytic anaemia (in the context of normal G6PD activity); nebulised pentamidine at a dose of 300 mg every 4 weeks was used instead, which was well-tolerated.

Following a 21-day hospitalization, the patient was discharged. At discharge, oral therapies were provided via nasogastric tube, as he continued to experience dysphagia.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following a three-week inpatient course, the patient showed partial recovery of hip flexor strength and significant reduction in cervical soft tissue edema, though the latter was still present at discharge. Dysphagia persisted, requiring continued use of a nasogastric tube for feeding over the subsequent two months.

Upon reevaluation three weeks post-discharge, the patient's proximal muscle power had normalized, and creatine kinase levels had decreased to 37 U/L. At the two-month immunology assessment after discharge, cervical swelling continued to subside; examination revealed only mild residual violaceous facial erythema and periungual abnormalities. Improved swallowing ability permitted withdrawal of the nasogastric tube and transition to a soft solid diet. The treatment regimen included ongoing mycophenolate mofetil, monthly intravenous immunoglobulin, and a gradually decreasing dose of prednisone.

Three months after discharge, the patient's proximal muscle strength remained unchanged, chest auscultation yielded normal findings, and cervical soft tissue swelling had resolved entirely. Seven months post-discharge, prednisone was discontinued without issue, coinciding with complete resolution of dysphagia. Follow-up pulmonary function tests indicated slight improvement; however, a residual restrictive pattern persisted and was considered consistent with his baseline physiology (figure 5a, b). At 11 months, a repeat chest CT revealed almost total resolution of basal ground-glass opacities and airspace consolidation, which had been previously linked to aspiration. At one year post-diagnosis, his respiratory status was stable and he exhibited good exercise tolerance.

Regarding malignancy surveillance, he continued to report no constitutional symptoms during follow-up visits and will remain under observation based on symptomatology, clinical examination, regular laboratory investigations, and relevant imaging studies, including serial CT scans of the chest. Plans include gastroscopy and colonoscopy to investigate for hidden gastrointestinal or colorectal malignancies.

The proposed management strategy is to maintain intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) therapy and gradually reduce the dose of mycophenolate over the next six months, with adjustments dictated by clinical response. He will be closely monitored to assess whether changes to his medication regimen are warranted.

Discussion:

We describe a patient with dermatomyositis positive for anti-SAE1 antibodies who developed significant cervical soft tissue edema, which was initially misdiagnosed as idiopathic angioedema. The accurate identification of dermatomyositis as a possible cause, together with a thorough physical examination that disclosed faint dermatologic findings, permitted early diagnosis and intervention.

Anti-SAE1 antibodies target the small ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) activating enzyme, a protein that shares structural features with ubiquitin and mediates the sumoylation process, affecting post-translational modification of a variety of substrates. Dermatomyositis associated with SAE1 antibodies commonly presents with skin manifestations such as heliotrope rash, Gottron’s papules, the V-neck sign, and the shawl sign. According to a comprehensive review of 215 individuals, dysphagia was noted in 46% and interstitial lung disease in 43% of those with SAE1 dermatomyositis, whereas malignancies were observed in 18% of cases.

Edema of the subcutaneous tissue, particularly involving the face or neck, is rarely observed in dermatomyositis. In a large cohort comprising 121 individuals diagnosed with dermatomyositis, angioedema was noted in only 8 patients (6.61%). A review of the literature spanning 1985 to 2013 identified just 19 reported cases in which dermatomyositis presented with edema. Antibodies such as anti-melanoma differentiation-associated gene (MDA)-5 and anti-transcriptional intermediary factor 1γ (TIF1-γ) were found to be linked to angioedema more often than other antibody profiles. Although facial edema has been reported in several cases, involvement of the neck is less commonly described. In our review of the literature concerning 10 cases of SAE1-positive dermatomyositis, edema was mentioned in only three, and just one case reported swelling in the region of the throat, indicating that neck involvement is indeed a rare occurrence.79

This case highlights the importance for emergency physicians, ENT specialists, immunologists, and rheumatologists to consider dermatomyositis as a potential cause of neck swelling, particularly when encountering unexplained or idiopathic localized ‘angioedema’. Dysphagia and other associated manifestations may be minimal, making it essential to check for dermatomyositis-specific physical findings and to obtain a creatine kinase measurement. The patient’s subcutaneous oedema was attributable to muscular inflammation resulting from myositis, as opposed to angioedema mediated by histamine or bradykinin.

Following the International Guideline for Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathy-Associated Cancer Screening from the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS), the patient met criteria for high malignancy risk, having two major risk factors (dermatomyositis, dysphagia) and two intermediate risk factors (SAE1 antibody positivity, male gender). He underwent thorough cancer evaluation, which included CT imaging, tumour marker assessment, a PET scan, and planned upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopy. Comprehensive baseline and ongoing surveillance have thus far yielded reassuring results.

The optimal medication regimen for anti-SAE1–positive dermatomyositis remains unclear due to scarce data. The largest reported case series described a variety of treatment strategies, with the majority of patients being administered glucocorticoids together with several steroid-sparing drugs, primarily methotrexate but also including mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, and azathioprine; intravenous immunoglobulin was selectively given in certain instances. In the case of our patient, therapy consisted of steroids, mycophenolate mofetil, intravenous immunoglobulin, and rituximab, resulting in remission. The choice of mycophenolate mofetil was influenced by the patient’s preference for daily oral dosing, as well as its tolerability profile and established efficacy in treating dermatomyositis. Our patient’s outlook appears favorable, especially relative to other individuals with substantial tissue edema associated with dermatomyositis.
