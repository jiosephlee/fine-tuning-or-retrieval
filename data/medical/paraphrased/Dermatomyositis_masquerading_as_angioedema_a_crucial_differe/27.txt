Title: Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook

Kevin Chan, Gemma Kwan, Hwei Choo Soh, Louise Evans

Abstract:

Summary: Dermatomyositis is an uncommon autoimmune disorder primarily involving the skin, musculature, and respiratory tract. Immediate initiation of potent immunosuppressive therapy is often necessary in patients with dermatomyositis to limit disease progression and prevent involvement of multiple organ systems. A previously healthy man in his 40s presented to the emergency department with marked swelling of the neck. Otolaryngology initially assessed him for a likely deep neck space infection, but this diagnosis was excluded. He was then referred to clinical immunology for further assessment and management of suspected idiopathic angioedema. Due to the unusual characteristics of the neck swelling—which did not align with typical angioedema—and additional findings of skin and nail abnormalities, a creatine kinase measurement was promptly obtained. Dermatomyositis was considered as the working diagnosis; this was later confirmed by detection of a positive small ubiquitin-like modifier-1 activating enzyme (SAE1) autoantibody and characteristic findings on muscle biopsy. This case illustrates that neck swelling unrelated to histamine or bradykinin pathways can be an atypical manifestation of dermatomyositis, presenting with dysphagia and pronounced muscle involvement that necessitates urgent therapeutic intervention.

Background:

Dermatomyositis is an idiopathic inflammatory myopathy primarily affecting the skin and muscles, though involvement may also extend to the gastrointestinal tract, joints, lungs, and heart. The incidence is estimated at 10 cases per million individuals. The condition exhibits a bimodal age distribution, most commonly presenting in children between 5 and 14 years of age and in adults between 45 and 64 years. Distinctive skin manifestations include Gottron’s papules, the shawl sign, heliotrope rash, and periungual telangiectasias. Additional clinical features may encompass proximal muscle weakness, interstitial lung disease, and dysphagia. Pseudoangioedema is rarely observed in dermatomyositis; one study reported its presence in only 8 of 121 patients with the disease. When the neck is involved, it can mimic primary otolaryngologic conditions or be confused with angioedema mediated by histamine or bradykinin.

Case presentation:

A man in his 40s presented to the emergency department with a three-week progression of neck swelling and proximal weakness affecting his hip flexors, accompanied by mild muscle aches. He had no notable past medical history and had never smoked. He was not using any 3-hydroxy-3-methyl-glutaryl-coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase inhibitors. Ludwig’s angina was suspected initially, and a cervical CT scan was performed, revealing widespread neck edema involving the strap muscles and inflammatory changes extending into the retropharyngeal space (figure 1). He was afebrile, had an oxygen saturation of 99% on room air, and showed no tachypnea. Nailfold capillaroscopy detected proximal microhaemorrhages and regions of avascularity. Upon arrival, his white blood cell count measured 9.1×10^9/L and C-reactive protein (CRP) was 55.6 mg/L. As his neck swelling persisted with no apparent cause, he was transferred to a quaternary hospital with specialized ear, nose and throat (ENT) surgical services for additional assessment. ENT conducted a flexible nasendoscopy that did not identify major structural irregularities of the soft palate, vallecula, larynx, or post-cricoid space, although the procedure did elicit increased secretions and coughing.

The on-call immunologist was consulted due to concerns for angioedema, since neither structural nor infectious etiologies could be identified. Upon assessment, the patient displayed diffuse facial erythema, involving the nasolabial folds, as well as an erythematous, swollen, and non-tender neck. He reported not using any medications, including angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and had no known personal or familial history of angioedema. His clinical characteristics did not fit with either acquired or hereditary angioedema. Comprehensive physical examination identified subtle periungual erythema at the nailfolds, a rash localized to the right lateral thigh, and decreased hip flexor strength, rated at 4/5. There were no Gottron’s papules or heliotrope rash present. Creatine kinase testing was performed and returned the next day with a marked elevation to 16,184 U/L. Renal function testing was within normal limits, and urine myoglobin was negative. Liver enzymes were also increased, with alanine aminotransferase (ALT) at 143 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) at 396 U/L, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) at 692 U/L.

Investigations:

Considering the initial suspicion for angioedema, various evaluations were conducted to identify potential causes of spontaneous angioedema. Both C3 and C4 complement levels were found to be within normal limits, and C1 esterase activity and quantitative levels were also normal.

The patient demonstrated a positive anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) with a titer of 1:1280 exhibiting a speckled pattern. Testing for anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA) returned negative results. A myositis antibody panel comprising 16 antigens associated with autoimmune inflammatory myopathy was analyzed using the Euroimmun Euroline immunoassay, which revealed a strong positivity for anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibodies, with a signal intensity of 173 (normal value <35). Other myositis antibodies, including anti-Mi-2 alpha (anti-Mi-2α), anti-Transcription Intermediary Factor-1 gamma (anti-TIF1-γ), anti–Histidyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-Jo-1), and anti–Isoleucyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-OJ) antibodies, were not detected. Immunoglobulin G (IgG) levels and its subclasses remained within the standard range. As part of the immunosuppression screening, the patient tested negative for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), hepatitis C core antibody (HBcAb), hepatitis B e antibody (HBeAb), hepatitis C virus (HCV), HIV, varicella zoster virus (VZV) IgG, and cytomegalovirus (CMV) IgG and immunoglobulin M (IgM). Although the QuantiFERON-TB Gold result was indeterminate, all three sputum samples tested negative for acid-fast bacilli, excluding Mycobacterium tuberculosis infection.

Pulmonary function testing demonstrated a restrictive pattern of extrathoracic origin, without a notable response to bronchodilators. Chest CT identified several non-cavitating nodules predominantly distributed from the middle to lower lung zones, the largest of which was 15 mm in the right lower lobe, along with scattered peribronchial consolidations suggestive of aspiration pneumonitis; no radiographic signs of pulmonary fibrosis were detected. Abdominal CT did not reveal any abdomino-pelvic lymphadenopathy or any indication of primary or secondary malignancy within the abdomen or pelvis. A video fluoroscopic (barium) swallowing assessment uncovered marked pharyngeal dysphagia, resulting in substantial residue within the pyriform fossae and post-swallow entry of both thin and mildly thick fluids into the level of the vocal cords.

MRI of the thighs demonstrated findings indicative of active myositis within both obturator internus and externus muscles, alongside less pronounced myositic involvement of the hip adductors, gracilis muscles bilaterally, and both distal iliopsoas muscles (figure 2). Evidence for ongoing muscle inflammation was also provided by fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)-positron emission tomography (PET), which revealed widespread, abnormal FDG uptake across various muscle groups, most notably involving the head and neck, both deltoid muscles, forearms, chest wall, abdomen, and pelvis (figure 3). There were no areas of metabolically active disease to suggest malignancy. Tumor marker evaluation, including carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), cancer antigen 19-9 (CA19-9), cancer antigen 125 (CA-125), and prostate-specific antigen (PSA), returned values within the normal range.

Histopathological examination of a thigh muscle biopsy revealed localized areas with moderate numbers of necrotic, vacuolated, degenerating, myophagocytosed, and regenerating myofibers arranged in a perifascicular pattern, along with scattered perimysial inflammatory infiltrates composed of dispersed histiocytes (figure 4). Immunohistochemical staining targeting membrane attack complex demonstrated complement component 5b-9 (C5b-9) deposition within perifascicular endomysial capillaries, consistent with features of dermatomyositis. Based on these findings, the diagnosis was established as moderate to severe SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis characterized by inflammatory muscle edema, peribronchial consolidation likely due to micro-aspiration, dysphagia, and myositis. Skin findings included a positive Holster sign and periungual erythema.

Differential diagnosis:

Given his subacute onset marked by neck swelling and increased CRP levels, initial diagnostic consideration included Ludwig angina and idiopathic angioedema. Early on, muscle weakness—particularly dysphagia—was not a significant aspect of his clinical picture. The differential diagnosis encompassed other inflammatory myopathies such as immune-mediated necrotising myopathy and inclusion body myositis; infectious etiologies like cellulitis or erysipelas involving the neck, deep neck space abscesses, and bacterial sialadenitis; neuromuscular junction disorders including myasthenia gravis; delayed hypersensitivity reactions to medications such as drug rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms; acute contact dermatitis; orofacial granulomatosis; hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis; as well as superior vena cava syndrome.

Treatment:

Given marked involvement of airway musculature, the patient received intravenous methylprednisolone at 1000 mg per day for 3 days. This was followed by a prednisone taper beginning with 60 mg daily, reduced by 5 mg every 2 to 3 weeks. An initial dose of intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG, Gamunex 10%) at 2 g/kg (total 165 g) was infused over 5 days (35 g daily), after which 5 additional cycles were administered 4 weeks apart, for a cumulative dose of 2 g/kg (165 g) distributed over these sessions. Mycophenolate mofetil was started at 1 g twice daily. The patient also received two doses of intravenous rituximab, 1000 mg each, separated by 2 weeks. Methotrexate was omitted because of its association with interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis, especially given the patient’s abnormal pulmonary function, and after discussion regarding its adverse effect profile. Neither azathioprine nor tacrolimus was prescribed due to satisfactory tolerance of mycophenolate. For prevention of Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim was initially used, but was discontinued after development of haemolytic anaemia (without evidence of G6PD deficiency); nebulized pentamidine at 300 mg every 4 weeks was tolerated thereafter.

The patient was discharged following a 21-day hospital stay. At discharge, persistent dysphagia necessitated that oral medications be given via nasogastric tube.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following a 3-week period of hospital admission and intervention, the patient exhibited partial recovery of hip flexor strength and significant reduction in cervical soft tissue edema, though the swelling was still not completely resolved upon discharge. The patient continued to experience dysphagia, necessitating ongoing use of a nasogastric tube for feeding, which remained in place for an additional 2 months.

At a follow-up visit 3 weeks post-discharge, strength in proximal muscles was fully restored and serum creatine kinase had decreased to 37 U/L, within normal limits. During an immunology assessment conducted 2 months after leaving the hospital, cervical swelling showed continued improvement, with clinical examination revealing only persistent violaceous erythema of the face and periungual alterations. Swallowing ability had advanced, permitting discontinuation of the nasogastric tube and transition to a diet of soft solids. The patient was maintained on mycophenolate mofetil, monthly intravenous immunoglobulin therapy, and a gradually decreasing dosage of prednisone.

At three months following discharge, the patient's proximal muscle strength remained stable, physical examination of the chest was normal, and swelling of the cervical soft tissues had fully subsided. Prednisone was weaned off successfully at seven months post-discharge, at which point dysphagia had completely resolved. Repeat pulmonary function testing indicated mild improvement but continued to show a persistent restrictive pattern, consistent with his baseline status (see figure 5a, b). An 11-month follow-up chest CT revealed almost complete resolution of the basal ground-glass opacities and airspace consolidations previously attributed to aspiration. At one year post-diagnosis, his respiratory function was stable and he demonstrated good exercise capacity.

Regarding cancer surveillance, the patient reported no constitutional symptoms at follow-up and will be monitored based on symptomatology, physical examination findings, periodic laboratory work-up, and relevant imaging studies, including serial chest CT scans. Plans are in place to perform gastroscopy and colonoscopy to rule out hidden gastrointestinal or colorectal malignancy.

It was decided to continue intravenous immunoglobulin therapy and gradually reduce the mycophenolate dosage over the next six months, contingent upon his clinical trajectory. Ongoing close monitoring will determine whether adjustments to his pharmacologic regimen are required.

Discussion:

We present a case involving dermatomyositis with anti-SAE1 antibody positivity, which manifested as significant swelling of the cervical soft tissue and was initially misdiagnosed as idiopathic angioedema. Identification of dermatomyositis within the differential diagnosis, along with a detailed physical examination revealing subtle dermatological indicators, facilitated an accurate and prompt diagnosis and initiation of therapy.

Anti-SAE1 antibodies are directed against the SUMO (small-ubiquitin-like modifier) activating enzyme, a protein related in structure to ubiquitin and responsible for catalyzing sumoylation, a type of post-translational protein modification. Patients with dermatomyositis positive for SAE1 antibodies usually display characteristic skin findings, including heliotrope rash, Gottron’s papules, and the V-neck and shawl signs. A comprehensive review of the literature examining 215 individuals with this condition found that dysphagia and interstitial lung disease were present in 46% and 43% of cases, respectively, and malignancies developed in 18% of affected patients.

Edema of the subcutaneous tissue, particularly manifesting as facial or cervical swelling, is rarely observed in dermatomyositis. In a cohort of 121 individuals diagnosed with dermatomyositis, only 8 (6.61%) presented with angioedema. A review of literature spanning from 1985 to 2013 identified just 19 reported instances in which dermatomyositis was accompanied by edema. Certain autoantibodies, including anti-melanoma differentiation-associated gene (MDA)-5 and anti-transcriptional intermediary factor 1γ (TIF1-γ), were noted to have a stronger correlation with angioedema compared to others. Several reports documented facial edema, whereas descriptions of neck swelling were comparatively rarer. Upon reviewing 10 cases of SAE1-positive dermatomyositis, edema was reported in only three, with a single case referencing swelling localized to the throat area, indicating that cervical involvement is not commonly seen.79

This case highlights the importance for emergency medicine clinicians, otolaryngologists, immunologists, and rheumatologists to consider dermatomyositis as a potential differential diagnosis for neck swelling, particularly when presenting as localized "angioedema" without a clear etiology. Because manifestations such as dysphagia and other systemic features may be mild, it is essential to evaluate for clinical signs of dermatomyositis and obtain a creatine kinase measurement. In this instance, the patient’s subcutaneous oedema was attributable to muscle inflammation related to myositis rather than an angioedema mediated by histamine or bradykinin pathways.

Following the International Guideline for Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathy-Associated Cancer Screening from the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS), the patient was classified as high risk for malignancy based on the presence of two high-risk factors (dermatomyositis, dysphagia) and two intermediate-risk factors (positive SAE1 antibody, male gender). He received malignancy evaluation including computed tomography scans, assessment of tumour markers, and a PET scan, with further plans for both upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopy. Ongoing and baseline surveillance investigations have, thus far, remained reassuring.

Available data regarding ideal drug therapy for anti-SAE1–positive dermatomyositis remain sparse. The largest cohort report thus far demonstrates diverse management strategies; the majority of individuals received glucocorticoids paired with various steroid-sparing medications, most frequently methotrexate, along with agents such as mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, and azathioprine. Intravenous immunoglobulin was administered in specific instances. The patient described was managed with steroids, mycophenolate mofetil, intravenous immunoglobulin, and rituximab, resulting in disease remission. Mycophenolate mofetil was chosen in line with the patient’s preference for a daily oral regimen and consideration of its favorable tolerability profile and documented efficacy in dermatomyositis. In comparison to individuals experiencing pronounced tissue edema in the context of dermatomyositis, this patient’s prognosis appears to be favorable.
