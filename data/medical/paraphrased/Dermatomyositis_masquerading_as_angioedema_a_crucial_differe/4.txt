Title: Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook

Kevin Chan, Gemma Kwan, Hwei Choo Soh, Louise Evans

Abstract:

Summary: Dermatomyositis is an uncommon autoimmune disorder that predominantly targets the skin, musculature, and respiratory tract. Management of this condition generally necessitates rapid initiation of potent immunosuppressive therapy to limit disease advancement and minimize the risk of multiorgan impairment. In this report, a previously healthy male in his 40s arrived at the emergency department with marked cervical swelling. Initial assessment by the ear, nose, and throat team raised concerns for a potential deep neck space infection. However, after exclusion of infectious etiologies, he was referred to clinical immunology for further evaluation of suspected idiopathic angioedema. Given the unusual presentation of neck oedema that did not align with typical angioedema and in light of concurrent cutaneous and nail abnormalities, creatine kinase testing was urgently performed. The patient received a preliminary diagnosis of dermatomyositis, later substantiated by the detection of small ubiquitin-like modifier-1 activating enzyme (SAE1) autoantibody and characteristic findings on muscle biopsy. This case underscores that neck swelling, unrelated to histamine or bradykinin pathways, can represent an atypical clinical manifestation of dermatomyositis, accompanied by dysphagia and prominent muscular involvement necessitating swift intervention.

Background:

Dermatomyositis is a form of idiopathic inflammatory myopathy that most frequently impacts both the skin and musculature, though it may also involve organs such as the gastrointestinal tract, joints, lungs, and heart. The disease’s incidence has been approximated at 10 cases per million individuals. Dermatomyositis demonstrates a bimodal age distribution, predominantly presenting in children between 5 and 14 years of age, as well as in adults aged 45 to 64 years. Distinctive dermatological manifestations encompass Gottron’s papules, the shawl sign, heliotrope eruption, and periungual telangiectasias. Additional clinical features may include proximal muscle weakness, interstitial lung involvement, and difficulty swallowing. Pseudoangioedema represents a rare manifestation within dermatomyositis; in one investigation, only 8 out of 121 dermatomyositis patients displayed this feature. When the neck is affected, pseudoangioedema can be misinterpreted as a primary otolaryngological pathology or confused with angioedema driven by histaminergic or bradykinin mechanisms.

Case presentation:

A man in his 40s arrived at the emergency department reporting a three-week progression of neck swelling and proximal muscle weakness primarily affecting his hip flexors, along with mild myalgia. His past medical history was unremarkable, and he had never smoked. Additionally, he had not used any 3-hydroxy-3-methyl-glutaryl-coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase inhibitors. On initial assessment, Ludwig’s angina was suspected, prompting a CT scan of the neck. Imaging revealed extensive oedema involving the strap muscles and inflammatory changes that extended into the retropharyngeal space (figure 1). The patient was afebrile, maintained an oxygen saturation of 99% on room air, and exhibited no signs of tachypnoea. Capillaroscopy of the nailfolds demonstrated proximal microhaemorrhages and zones of avascularity. Laboratory testing showed a white blood cell count of 9.1×10^9/L and a C-reactive protein (CRP) concentration of 55.6 mg/L at presentation. Owing to continued neck swelling of uncertain etiology, he was referred to a quaternary care facility offering ear, nose, and throat (ENT) surgical expertise for advanced diagnostic workup. Flexible nasendoscopy performed by the ENT team did not reveal any notable anatomical irregularities of the soft palate, vallecula, larynx, or post-cricoid area, but the exam was complicated by increased secretions and a frequent cough.

Consultation with the on-call immunologist was sought due to suspicion of angioedema after structural or infectious etiologies were ruled out. Examination revealed widespread erythema involving the facial area, including the nasolabial folds, as well as a diffusely erythematous, swollen, and non-tender neck. The patient reported no use of medications such as angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and denied any personal or family history indicative of angioedema. His overall presentation was not typical of either hereditary or acquired forms of angioedema. Comprehensive physical assessment identified subtle changes at the nailfolds pointing towards periungual erythema, the presence of a cutaneous eruption on the right lateral thigh, and mildly reduced strength in hip flexors (4/5 on examination). Notably, Gottron’s papules and heliotrope eruption were not observed. Laboratory evaluation showed a markedly elevated creatine kinase at 16 184 U/L the following day. Renal function remained within normal limits and urine testing for myoglobin was negative. Additionally, liver enzymes were found to be raised: alanine aminotransferase (ALT) measured at 143 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) at 396 U/L, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) at 692 U/L.

Investigations:

Given the preliminary suspicion of angioedema, multiple investigations were undertaken to identify potential causes of idiopathic angioedema. The patient's C3 and C4 complement concentrations were within the normal limits, as were both the functional activity and quantitative levels of C1 esterase inhibitor.

Testing for anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA) returned a positive result with a titre of 1:1280, exhibiting a speckled pattern. Assessment for anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA) was negative. A myositis-specific antibody panel covering 16 autoantigens commonly associated with autoimmune inflammatory myopathies was conducted using the Euroimmun Euroline immunoassay. This revealed a markedly elevated anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibody signal (intensity 173, with normal <35). Results for other myositis antibodies—including anti-Mi-2 alpha (anti-Mi-2α), anti-Transcription Intermediary Factor-1 gamma (anti-TIF1-γ), anti–Histidyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-Jo-1), and anti–Isoleucyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-OJ)—were negative. Measurement of immunoglobulin G (IgG) and its subclasses showed no abnormalities.

With respect to screening for infectious contraindications to immunosuppression, he tested negative for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), hepatitis C core antibody (HBcAb), hepatitis B e antibody (HBeAb), hepatitis C virus (HCV), HIV, varicella zoster virus (VZV) IgG, and cytomegalovirus (CMV) IgG/IgM. QuantiFERON-TB Gold assay yielded an indeterminate result; nevertheless, all three sputum samples tested for acid-fast bacilli, including Mycobacterium tuberculosis, were negative.

Pulmonary function testing demonstrated a restrictive defect localized to the extrathoracic airways and did not indicate any substantial improvement following bronchodilator administration. Chest CT imaging identified multiple non-cavitating nodules predominantly situated in the mid and lower lung fields, with the largest lesion (15 mm) observed in the right lower lobe. Additionally, there were scattered peribronchial consolidations, likely attributed to aspiration pneumonitis, while features consistent with pulmonary fibrosis were absent radiologically. Abdominal CT did not detect any abdomino-pelvic lymphadenopathy, nor was there evidence of either a primary neoplasm or metastatic disease within the abdomen or pelvis. A video fluoroscopic (barium) swallow examination demonstrated marked pharyngeal dysphagia, resulting in substantial residue in the pyriform fossae and subsequent post-swallow entry of thin and mildly thickened liquids to the level of the vocal cords.

Magnetic resonance imaging of the thighs demonstrated findings indicative of active myositis involving the bilateral obturator internus and externus muscles, with less severe inflammation identified in the hip adductors, bilateral gracilis muscles, and distal segments of the bilateral iliopsoas muscles (see figure 2). The presence of active muscle inflammation was corroborated by fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) positron emission tomography (PET), which revealed widespread, abnormal FDG uptake in multiple muscle groups, particularly concentrated in the head and neck, bilateral deltoids, forearms, chest wall, abdomen, and pelvic regions (see figure 3). There was an absence of metabolically active lesions in other locations to indicate malignancy. Serum analysis for tumour markers—including carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), cancer antigen 19-9 (CA19-9), cancer antigen 125 (CA-125), and prostate-specific antigen (PSA)—returned values within the normal range.

Histopathological examination of a thigh muscle biopsy displayed moderate focal necrosis, vacuolization, degeneration, myophagocytosis, and regeneration of myofibres, with a perifascicular distribution. Scattered perimysial inflammation consisting mainly of dispersed histiocytes was also observed (figure 4). Immunohistochemical analysis for the membrane attack complex showed deposition of complement component 5b-9 (C5b-9) within perifascicular endomysial capillaries, a characteristic commonly observed in dermatomyositis. Based on these findings, a diagnosis was made of moderate to severe dermatomyositis with SAE1 antibody positivity, featuring inflammatory edema, peribronchial consolidation likely secondary to micro-aspiration, dysphagia, and myositis. Dermatologic features included a positive Holster sign and erythema around the periungual regions.

Differential diagnosis:

Given his gradual symptom onset characterized by neck swelling and an elevated CRP, initial diagnostic considerations included Ludwig angina and idiopathic angioedema. Early in his clinical course, muscle weakness—particularly dysphagia—was not a predominant symptom. The differential diagnosis encompassed other inflammatory myopathies (such as immune-mediated necrotising myopathy and inclusion body myositis), infections (including cellulitis/erysipelas involving the neck, deep neck space infections, and bacterial sialadenitis), disorders affecting the neuromuscular junction like myasthenia gravis, drug-induced delayed hypersensitivity reactions (such as drug rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms), acute contact dermatitis, orofacial granulomatosis, hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis, and superior vena cava syndrome.

Treatment:

Given the pronounced involvement of airway musculature, the patient received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dosage of 1000 mg daily for 3 days. This was subsequently transitioned to a tapering course of oral prednisone, beginning at 60 mg per day and decreasing by 5 mg every 2 to 3 weeks. Induction intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) therapy (Gamunex 10%) was initiated at 2 g/kg (totaling 165 g), administered over 5 days (35 g daily). This was followed by 5 additional IVIG cycles, each 4 weeks apart, at an aggregate dose of 2 g/kg (=165 g), distributed across these sessions. Mycophenolate mofetil was started at 1 g twice daily, and the patient also received two 1000 mg intravenous doses of rituximab, administered 2 weeks apart. Methotrexate was not considered, given its potential association with interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis—risks highlighted by the patient’s abnormal pulmonary function tests—as well as after discussing potential adverse effects with the patient. Both azathioprine and tacrolimus were not employed, as mycophenolate proved to be well-tolerated. For prophylaxis against Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, the patient initially received sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim; however, this was discontinued due to the onset of haemolytic anaemia (in the absence of glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency). He subsequently tolerated nebulized pentamidine at a dose of 300 mg every 4 weeks.

Following a 21-day hospital admission, he was discharged. At discharge, due to ongoing dysphagia, oral medications were delivered via a nasogastric tube.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following a three-week period of hospitalisation and medical intervention, the patient exhibited moderate gains in hip flexor strength and significant reduction in cervical soft tissue oedema, though complete resolution of the swelling had not yet occurred by the time of discharge. Dysphagia persisted and required continued nutritional support via a nasogastric tube, which remained necessary for an additional two months.

At the three-week follow-up after discharge, the patient’s proximal muscle power had fully returned, and serum creatine kinase levels had decreased to 37 U/L, indicating biochemical normalisation. During the immunology assessment two months post-discharge, ongoing improvement of cervical swelling was noted, with only faint violaceous facial erythema and periungual abnormalities observed on clinical examination. Swallowing capabilities had advanced to a degree that allowed for removal of the nasogastric tube and a transition to soft solid foods. The patient’s regimen was maintained with mycophenolate mofetil, monthly intravenous immunoglobulin, and a gradually reducing dose of prednisone.

Three months after hospital discharge, the patient’s proximal muscle strength remained unchanged, chest assessment was normal, and cervical soft tissue swelling had entirely subsided. Prednisone was discontinued successfully at 7 months post-discharge, at which point the patient’s dysphagia had also completely resolved. Subsequent pulmonary function testing indicated slight improvement; however, a residual restrictive pattern persisted, considered consistent with the patient’s baseline status (figure 5a, b). A follow-up chest CT at 11 months revealed that the earlier basal ground-glass opacities and airspace consolidation, initially presumed to be secondary to aspiration, had nearly completely resolved. At the 12-month mark after diagnosis, respiratory capacity was maintained and exercise tolerance was good.

From an oncological standpoint, the patient reported no systemic symptoms at follow-up. Ongoing surveillance will include symptom monitoring, clinical evaluations, routine laboratory investigations, and pertinent imaging such as serial CT scans of the chest. Additionally, gastroscopy and colonoscopy are planned to rule out hidden gastrointestinal or colorectal malignancy.

The management strategy involved ongoing IVIG therapy and a gradual reduction of mycophenolate dosage over the following 6 months, subject to clinical status. The patient will continue to be closely observed to assess if adjustments to the medication regimen are necessary.

Discussion:

We present a case involving dermatomyositis with anti-SAE1 antibody positivity, characterized by marked cervical soft tissue edema, which was at first misdiagnosed as idiopathic angioedema. The identification of dermatomyositis as a plausible differential, together with a detailed physical assessment that uncovered faint dermatological manifestations, permitted an accurate and prompt diagnosis, facilitating early intervention.

Anti-SAE1 antibodies are directed against the small-ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) activating enzyme, a protein closely related to ubiquitin, playing a role in the sumoylation process that modifies numerous substrate proteins post-translationally. The clinical phenotype of SAE1 antibody–associated dermatomyositis most commonly includes dermatologic findings such as the heliotrope rash, Gottron’s papules, V-neck sign, and shawl sign. According to an extensive literature review involving 215 cases, dysphagia was present in 46%, interstitial lung disease in 43%, and malignancy was identified in 18% of individuals with SAE1 dermatomyositis.

Subcutaneous oedema, particularly affecting the face or neck, is a rare manifestation in dermatomyositis. In a substantial cohort comprising 121 individuals diagnosed with dermatomyositis, angioedema was present in just 8 patients (6.61%). Additionally, a review of literature covering the years 1985 to 2013 identified only 19 instances where oedema was a presenting feature of dermatomyositis. Among identified autoantibodies, anti-melanoma differentiation-associated gene (MDA)-5 and anti-transcriptional intermediary factor 1γ (TIF1-γ) antibodies demonstrated a stronger association with angioedema than others. While facial oedema was reported in several documented cases, swelling of the neck was less often observed. In our analysis of 10 reported cases of SAE1-positive dermatomyositis, only three referenced oedema, with just one specifically noting swelling localized to the throat, underscoring the infrequent involvement of the neck area.79

This case highlights the importance for emergency physicians, ENT specialists, immunologists, and rheumatologists to include dermatomyositis among the differential diagnoses for patients presenting with neck swelling, particularly when facing localized 'angioedema' that lacks a clear etiology. Because manifestations such as dysphagia or other systemic features may be mild, it is essential to perform a thorough search for the clinical hallmarks of dermatomyositis and to check creatine kinase levels. In this patient, the subcutaneous oedema was a result of inflammatory changes in the muscles characteristic of myositis, rather than stemming from angioedema mechanisms mediated by histamine or bradykinin.

Following the International Guideline for Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathy-Associated Cancer Screening issued by the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS), this individual met two high-risk criteria (the presence of dermatomyositis and dysphagia) and two intermediate-risk criteria (SAE1 antibody positivity and male gender), classifying him as high risk for malignancy. Accordingly, the malignancy workup comprised CT imaging, evaluation of tumor markers, and a PET scan, in addition to planned upper and lower GI endoscopy. To date, comprehensive initial and periodic cancer surveillance has yielded reassuring results.

Currently, there is a paucity of data to inform best practices for pharmacologic management in patients with anti-SAE1–positive dermatomyositis. The most extensive cohort study reported thus far describes a diverse array of therapeutic regimens, with the majority of individuals receiving glucocorticoids along with various steroid-sparing medications. Methotrexate was most frequently used, though other agents such as mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, and azathioprine were also employed; in selected situations, intravenous immunoglobulin was administered. In the present case, the patient underwent treatment with steroids, mycophenolate mofetil, intravenous immunoglobulin, and rituximab, ultimately achieving disease remission. The choice of mycophenolate mofetil was influenced by the patient's preference for a once-daily oral therapy, as well as the drug’s favorable tolerability profile and established role in dermatomyositis management. Compared to others with dermatomyositis who present with substantial tissue oedema, our patient’s prognosis appears more favorable.
