Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook

Kevin Chan, Gemma Kwan, Hwei Choo Soh, Louise Evans

Abstract:

Summary: Dermatomyositis is an uncommon autoimmune disorder primarily involving the skin, musculature, and respiratory tract. Immediate and aggressive immunosuppressive therapy is typically necessary for affected individuals to curb disease advancement and prevent damage to multiple organs. In this report, a previously healthy man in his 40s presented acutely to the emergency department with marked swelling of the neck. He was first assessed by ear, nose, and throat specialists, who initially suspected a deep neck space infection. After this diagnosis was excluded, he was subsequently referred to the clinical immunology team to evaluate and manage a presumed case of idiopathic angioedema. Due to the unusual presentation of neck oedema, which did not align with classic angioedema and given concurrent dermatological and nail manifestations, creatine kinase testing was expedited. Based on these findings, dermatomyositis was provisionally diagnosed. Further workup revealed positivity for small ubiquitin-like modifier-1 activating enzyme (SAE1) autoantibody and a muscle biopsy typical of dermatomyositis. This case underscores the occurrence of neck oedema unrelated to histamine or bradykinin pathways as a rare clinical manifestation of dermatomyositis, presenting with dysphagia and notable muscle involvement that necessitated rapid therapeutic intervention.

Background:

Dermatomyositis is classified as an idiopathic inflammatory myopathy that predominantly targets the skin and skeletal muscles but may also involve organs such as the lungs, heart, gastrointestinal tract, and joints. The annual incidence rate is approximately 10 per million individuals. The disease exhibits a bimodal age distribution, most frequently presenting in children between 5 and 14 years old and in adults aged 45 to 64 years. Typical dermatologic manifestations are Gottron’s papules, heliotrope eruption, shawl sign, and periungual telangiectasias. Additional clinical features include proximal muscle weakness, interstitial lung disease, and difficulties with swallowing (dysphagia). Pseudoangioedema represents a rare clinical presentation in dermatomyositis; one study reported its occurrence in just 8 of 121 patients diagnosed with this condition. When localized to the neck, pseudoangioedema may mimic disorders primarily managed by otolaryngologists or be confused with angioedema resulting from histamine or bradykinin pathways.

Case presentation:

A man in his 40s arrived at the emergency department reporting a 3-week progression of neck swelling and weakness in the hip flexor muscles, as well as mild muscle aches. He did not have any relevant prior medical conditions and had never smoked. Additionally, he was not using a 3-hydroxy-3-methyl-glutaryl-coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase inhibitor. The initial diagnosis was Ludwig’s angina, prompting a CT scan of his neck, which revealed extensive oedema of the strap muscles and inflammatory changes spreading to the retropharyngeal space (see figure 1). On examination, he was afebrile, maintained oxygen saturations at 99% on room air, and was not tachypnoeic. Nailfold capillaroscopy identified both proximal nailfold microhaemorrhages and avascular zones. Laboratory studies revealed a white cell count of 9.1×10^9/L and a C-reactive protein (CRP) level of 55.6 mg/L at presentation. With persistent, unexplained swelling, he was referred to a quaternary care center with ear, nose, and throat (ENT) surgical capabilities for further assessment. ENT conducted a flexible nasendoscopy, which did not show any significant structural abnormalities in the soft palate, vallecula, larynx, or post-cricoid region; however, the procedure was notable for increased secretions and coughing.

The case was referred to the on-call immunologist due to a suspicion of angioedema, as no obvious structural abnormality or infection could be identified. Upon assessment, generalized facial erythema was observed, extending into the nasolabial folds, in addition to a diffusely erythematous, edematous, but non-tender neck. The patient reported no use of medications, such as angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and did not have any history—personal or familial—of angioedema. His overall clinical presentation was not consistent with either hereditary or acquired forms of angioedema. Detailed physical examination uncovered mild nailfold abnormalities compatible with periungual erythema, a cutaneous eruption over the right lateral thigh, and diminished hip flexor strength, graded at 4/5. There were no Gottron’s papules or heliotrope rash observed. A creatine kinase measurement was ordered and, the following day, returned markedly increased at 16 184 U/L. Renal function remained within normal limits and urinalysis for myoglobin was negative. Additionally, liver enzymes were elevated, with an alanine aminotransferase (ALT) of 143 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) measured at 396 U/L, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) raised to 692 U/L.

Investigations:

In response to the suspicion of angioedema, a range of diagnostic investigations was undertaken to identify possible etiologies of spontaneous angioedema. The results demonstrated that both C3 and C4 complement fractions were within normal limits, as were measurements of C1 esterase activity and concentration.

The serological assessment revealed that anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) was positive at a titre of 1:1280, displaying a speckled immunofluorescence pattern. Tests for anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA) returned negative. Additionally, a comprehensive myositis antibody panel—comprising 16 autoimmune inflammatory myopathy-associated antigens and conducted via Euroimmun Euroline immunoassay—indicated a strong positivity for anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibodies, with a signal intensity of 173 (reference range <35). Other myositis-specific antibodies—including anti-Mi-2 alpha (anti-Mi-2α), anti–Transcription Intermediary Factor-1 gamma (anti-TIF1-γ), anti–Histidyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-Jo-1), and anti–Isoleucyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-OJ)—were all negative. The total immunoglobulin G (IgG) concentration, along with IgG subclasses, remained within the expected range.

As part of the immunosuppressive workup, results were negative for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), hepatitis C core antibody (HBcAb), hepatitis B e antibody (HBeAb), hepatitis C virus (HCV), HIV, varicella zoster virus (VZV) IgG, and cytomegalovirus (CMV) IgG/immunoglobulin M (IgM). Although his QuantiFERON-TB Gold result was indeterminate, testing for acid-fast bacilli associated with Mycobacterium tuberculosis on three sputum specimens was negative.

Pulmonary function testing demonstrated a restrictive pattern consistent with extrathoracic origin and failed to show a notable response to bronchodilators. Chest CT imaging identified numerous non-cavitating nodules, predominantly located from the mid to lower lung zones, with the largest nodule in the right lower lobe measuring 15 mm. Additionally, patchy peribronchial consolidation was observed, likely attributable to aspiration pneumonitis; there were no signs of pulmonary fibrosis on radiological assessment. Abdominal CT did not reveal any abdomino-pelvic lymphadenopathy or signs suggestive of primary or metastatic cancer within the abdomen or pelvis. A video fluoroscopic (barium) swallow examination indicated marked pharyngeal dysphagia, resulting in significant residue within the pyriform fossae and post-deglutitive entry of thin and mildly thick liquids to the level of the vocal cords.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the thighs demonstrated findings indicative of active myositis within the bilateral obturator internus and externus muscles, alongside less severe involvement of the hip adductors, gracilis muscles bilaterally, and the distal portions of both iliopsoas muscles (figure 2). Additional evidence for ongoing muscle inflammation was provided by fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)-positron emission tomography (PET) scan, which revealed widespread, abnormal FDG uptake across numerous muscle groups, with the most significant activity noted in the head and neck regions, both deltoids, forearms, chest wall, abdominal area, and pelvis (figure 3). No hypermetabolic lesions were identified elsewhere that would raise suspicion for malignancy. Tumor marker levels, including carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), cancer antigen 19-9 (CA19-9), cancer antigen 125 (CA-125), and prostate-specific antigen (PSA), were within normal limits.

Thigh muscle biopsy demonstrated clusters of necrotic, vacuolated degenerate, myophagocytosed, and regenerating muscle fibers predominantly at the perifascicular area, with interspersed perimysial inflammation composed of scattered histiocytes (figure 4). Immunohistochemical staining for the membrane attack complex revealed complement component 5b-9 (C5b-9) deposition localized to perifascicular endomysial capillaries—a histopathological hallmark of dermatomyositis. The patient was subsequently diagnosed with moderate to severe SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis, which manifested with inflammatory edema, peribronchial consolidation secondary to micro-aspiration, dysphagia, and myositis. Dermatologic signs included a positive Holster sign and periungual erythema.

Differential diagnosis:

Due to his subacute onset of neck swelling accompanied by an increased CRP, initial diagnostic consideration was given to Ludwig angina or idiopathic angioedema. Early in the disease course, significant muscle weakness, such as dysphagia, was not observed. The differential diagnosis encompassed additional inflammatory myopathies (including immune-mediated necrotising myopathy and inclusion body myositis), infectious etiologies (such as cellulitis or erysipelas involving the neck, deep neck space infections, and bacterial sialadenitis), neuromuscular junction abnormalities like myasthenia gravis, delayed drug hypersensitivity reactions (for instance, drug rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms), acute contact dermatitis, orofacial granulomatosis, hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis, and superior vena cava syndrome.

Treatment:

Owing to pronounced involvement of the airway musculature, the patient received intravenous methylprednisolone for 3 days at a daily dosage of 1000 mg. Subsequently, he was transitioned to an oral prednisone taper, which was initiated at 60 mg per day and decreased by 5 mg every 2 to 3 weeks. For immunomodulation, a loading dose of intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) (Gamunex 10%) was administered at 2 g/kg (totaling 165 g), divided over 5 days (35 g per day). This was followed by 5 additional IVIG cycles, each 4 weeks apart, with a cumulative dose of 2 g/kg (165 g), distributed across these sessions. Mycophenolate mofetil was started at 1 g twice daily. Additionally, two intravenous rituximab infusions of 1000 mg each were provided, separated by a 2-week interval. Methotrexate was not selected, as it has established links with interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis, concerns amplified by the patient’s abnormal pulmonary function testing and after deliberation with the patient regarding the potential adverse effects. Given that mycophenolate was well tolerated, azathioprine and tacrolimus were not employed. For prophylaxis against Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim was initially prescribed; however, it was discontinued because the patient developed haemolytic anaemia, despite normal glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) status. Nebulised pentamidine at 300 mg every 4 weeks was subsequently well tolerated.

Following a 21-day hospitalization, the patient was discharged. Persistent dysphagia necessitated administration of oral medications via a nasogastric tube at the time of discharge.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following three weeks of hospital admission and therapy, the patient exhibited partial recovery of hip flexor strength and significant reduction in cervical soft tissue swelling, though some swelling persisted at the time of discharge. Ongoing dysphagia required continued feeding via a nasogastric tube, which remained necessary for an additional two months.

At the three-week follow-up post-discharge, proximal muscle strength had fully normalized, and serum creatine kinase was restored to 37 U/L. An immunology assessment conducted two months after discharge revealed further reduction in cervical swelling, with examination showing only faint violaceous erythema on the face and periungual changes as residual signs. Swallowing abilities improved enough to discontinue the nasogastric tube and transition to a diet of soft solids. The therapeutic regimen was maintained, including mycophenolate mofetil, monthly intravenous immunoglobulin, and a tapering course of prednisone.

At the 3-month follow-up after discharge, the patient exhibited stable proximal muscle strength, a normal chest examination, and full resolution of cervical soft tissue swelling. Prednisone therapy was tapered off without complication by 7 months post-discharge, coinciding with complete resolution of dysphagia. Subsequent pulmonary function assessments indicated slight improvement, although a persistent restrictive pattern remained, believed to correspond to pre-existing physiological characteristics (figure 5a, b). An 11-month post-discharge CT scan of the chest revealed almost total clearance of the basal ground-glass opacities and airspace consolidation, which were previously ascribed to aspiration events. At the 12-month mark, respiratory status continued to be stable with preserved exercise capacity.

With regard to malignancy surveillance, the patient reported no constitutional symptoms at follow-up. Ongoing monitoring will consist of clinical assessment, periodic laboratory investigations, and imaging—such as serial CT scans of the chest. In addition, a gastroscopy and colonoscopy are planned to screen for any hidden gastrointestinal or colorectal cancers.

The therapeutic strategy includes ongoing IVIG administration and a gradual reduction in mycophenolate dosage over the next 6 months, contingent on his clinical evolution. He will remain under close observation to assess whether further adjustments to his pharmacologic regimen are required.

Discussion:

We describe a case involving dermatomyositis associated with anti-SAE1 antibodies, which initially manifested as pronounced cervical soft tissue edema and was erroneously diagnosed as idiopathic angioedema. The eventual identification of dermatomyositis as a diagnostic consideration, together with meticulous clinical assessment that identified subtle dermatological findings, enabled prompt diagnosis and initiation of therapy.

Anti-SAE1 antibodies are directed against the small-ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) activating enzyme—a protein akin in structure to ubiquitin—that plays a critical role in post-translational modification via the process of ‘sumoylation’. Dermatomyositis positive for SAE1 antibodies commonly displays cutaneous manifestations, including heliotrope rash, Gottron’s papules, V-neck sign, and shawl sign. A comprehensive review of 215 reported cases revealed that dysphagia and interstitial lung disease occurred in 46\% and 43\% of patients with SAE1-associated dermatomyositis, respectively, while malignancy was observed in 18\% of cases.

Oedema of the subcutaneous tissue, including swelling of the face or neck, is a rare clinical finding in dermatomyositis. In a sizable cohort comprising 121 individuals diagnosed with dermatomyositis, only 8 (6.61%) exhibited angioedema. Furthermore, a review of publications spanning from 1985 to 2013 identified just 19 documented instances of dermatomyositis presenting with oedema. Notably, antibodies such as anti-melanoma differentiation-associated gene (MDA)-5 and anti-transcriptional intermediary factor 1γ (TIF1-γ) were more frequently linked to cases of angioedema compared to other autoantibodies. Although facial oedema was reported in several patients, reports specifically detailing neck oedema were even more infrequent. Our analysis of 10 reported SAE1-positive dermatomyositis cases identified only three that referenced oedema, and of those, just one noted swelling in the throat area, underscoring the rarity of neck involvement in such cases.79

This case highlights the importance for emergency physicians, ENT specialists, immunologists, and rheumatologists to consider dermatomyositis as a potential differential diagnosis in patients presenting with neck swelling, particularly when faced with unexplained or idiopathic localized ‘angioedema.’ Given that signs such as dysphagia or additional system involvement can be mild or easily overlooked, it is imperative to perform a thorough examination for dermatomyositis stigmata and to assess serum creatine kinase levels. In this patient, the observed subcutaneous oedema is attributed to muscle inflammation due to myositis, distinguishing it from angioedema driven by histaminergic or bradykinin mechanisms.

Following the recommendations of the International Guideline for Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathy-Associated Cancer Screening established by the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS), this individual met two high-risk criteria (presence of dermatomyositis and dysphagia) and two intermediate-risk criteria (positivity for SAE1 antibody, male gender), categorizing him as high risk for malignancy. Consequently, an extensive malignancy workup was undertaken, encompassing CT imaging, tumour marker evaluations, and a PET scan, in addition to scheduling upper and lower GI endoscopies. To date, results from both comprehensive initial and ongoing surveillance remain reassuring.

Currently, there is a paucity of data to inform the most effective pharmacological strategies for managing anti-SAE1–positive dermatomyositis. In the largest cohort described thus far, therapeutic regimens varied considerably. The majority of individuals were administered glucocorticoids alongside a spectrum of steroid-sparing agents, with methotrexate being most frequently utilized, followed by agents such as mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, and azathioprine; in some instances, intravenous immunoglobulin was also employed. In this particular case, the patient received a combination of steroids, mycophenolate mofetil, intravenous immunoglobulin, and rituximab, resulting in remission. The decision to use mycophenolate mofetil was guided by the patient's preference for an oral daily therapy, along with considerations of its favorable tolerability profile and established efficacy in dermatomyositis. Compared to other individuals exhibiting marked tissue oedema associated with dermatomyositis, our patient’s prognosis appears favorable.
