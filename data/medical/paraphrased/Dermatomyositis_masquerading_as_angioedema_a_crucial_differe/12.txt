Title: Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook

Kevin Chan, Gemma Kwan, Hwei Choo Soh, Louise Evans

Abstract:

Summary: Dermatomyositis is an uncommon autoimmune condition primarily involving the skin, musculature, and the respiratory tract. Immediate and aggressive immunosuppressive therapy is often necessary for individuals with dermatomyositis to prevent further disease advancement and damage to multiple organ systems. A previously healthy man in his 40s initially presented to the emergency department due to pronounced neck swelling. He underwent initial assessment by the ear, nose, and throat team, who suspected a deep neck space infection. After this etiology was excluded, the patient was referred to the clinical immunology team for assessment and management of presumed idiopathic angioedema. Due to the unusual characteristics of the cervical edema—which did not correlate with classic angioedema—and in the context of concurrent cutaneous and nail alterations, a creatine kinase test was promptly ordered. A working diagnosis of dermatomyositis was established, later confirmed by the detection of a positive small ubiquitin-like modifier-1 activating enzyme (SAE1) autoantibody and characteristic findings on muscle biopsy. This case illustrates a rare presentation of dermatomyositis with neck swelling unrelated to either histamine or bradykinin pathways, accompanied by dysphagia and significant myopathic features that warranted urgent intervention.

Background:

Dermatomyositis is a form of idiopathic inflammatory myopathy that predominantly impacts the skin and skeletal muscles, but may also involve the lungs, heart, gastrointestinal tract, and joints. Its incidence is approximately 10 per million individuals. The disease demonstrates a bimodal age distribution, with peaks occurring in children between 5 and 14 years and in adults from 45 to 64 years of age. Typical dermatologic signs include Gottron's papules, heliotrope rash, shawl sign, and periungual telangiectasias. Additional manifestations can involve proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, and interstitial lung disease. Pseudoangioedema is a rare manifestation in dermatomyositis, observed in only 8 of 121 cases in one reported series. When pseudoangioedema involves the neck, it may present as a condition more commonly seen in otolaryngology, or it can be misidentified as either histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema.

Case presentation:

A man in his 40s presented to the emergency department after experiencing three weeks of worsening neck swelling and proximal muscle weakness affecting his hip flexors, as well as mild muscle pain. He had no notable past medical conditions and had never smoked. He also denied prior use of any 3-hydroxy-3-methyl-glutaryl-coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase inhibitors. Ludwig’s angina was initially suspected, and a CT scan of the neck revealed widespread edema of the strap muscles, with inflammation extending into the retropharyngeal space (figure 1). On assessment, he was afebrile, with an oxygen saturation of 99% while breathing room air, and displayed no tachypnea. Nailfold capillaroscopy demonstrated proximal nailfold microhaemorrhages and areas lacking vascularization. Laboratory results showed a white blood cell count of 9.1×10^9/L and a C-reactive protein (CRP) level of 55.6 mg/L upon arrival. Due to unresolved neck swelling with no identified cause, he was referred to a quaternary care facility equipped with ear, nose, and throat (ENT) surgical services for additional evaluation. Flexible nasendoscopy by the ENT team revealed no remarkable abnormalities in the soft palate, vallecula, larynx, or post-cricoid region; however, the procedure was notable for increased secretions and a cough reflex during the assessment.

The case was reviewed with the on-call immunologist due to a suspicion of angioedema, since no definite structural abnormality or infectious etiology was identified. Upon assessment, the patient exhibited diffuse facial erythema extending over the nasolabial folds, along with an erythematous, swollen, but non-tender neck. He reported not taking any medications, including angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and there was no history of angioedema in himself or his family. The overall clinical scenario was not typical for either acquired or hereditary angioedema. Detailed physical examination disclosed mild nailfold changes indicative of periungual erythema, in addition to a rash on the right lateral thigh and diminished hip flexor strength at 4/5. No Gottron’s papules or heliotrope eruption were observed. Creatine kinase testing was ordered and subsequently revealed a markedly raised level of 16 184 U/L the following day. Renal function tests were within normal limits and the urine myoglobin test was negative. Laboratory evaluation also demonstrated raised transaminases, with an alanine aminotransferase (ALT) of 143 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) of 396 U/L, and a lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) level elevated at 692 U/L.

Investigations:

Given the preliminary suspicion of angioedema, an array of investigations was carried out to identify potential causes of idiopathic angioedema. The patient’s C3 and C4 complement measurements were within normal limits, and both C1 esterase function and quantitative levels also fell into the expected range.

Testing for anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA) yielded a positive result, demonstrating a titre of 1:1280 and a speckled pattern. Anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA) testing was negative. A myositis autoantibody profile that screened for 16 antigens associated with autoimmune inflammatory myopathies was conducted using the Euroimmun Euroline immunoassay. This assay demonstrated a markedly elevated anti–small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibody level, with a signal intensity of 173 (reference <35). Antibodies against other myositis targets—including anti-Mi-2α, anti–Transcription Intermediary Factor-1 gamma (anti-TIF1-γ), anti–Histidyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-Jo-1), and anti–Isoleucyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-OJ)—were all negative. Serum immunoglobulin G (IgG), along with its subclasses, was measured within normal parameters. As part of the immunosuppression screening, results for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), hepatitis B e antibody (HBeAb), hepatitis C core antibody (HBcAb), hepatitis C virus (HCV), HIV, varicella zoster virus (VZV) IgG, and cytomegalovirus (CMV) IgG/IgM were all negative. The QuantiFERON-TB Gold test was indeterminate; however, three sputum specimens were negative for acid-fast bacilli, ruling out Mycobacterium tuberculosis.

Pulmonary function testing demonstrated an extrathoracic restrictive pattern without a notable response to bronchodilators. Chest CT identified multiple non-cavitating nodules primarily located in the mid to lower lung zones, the largest measuring 15 mm within the right lower lobe. There were patchy regions of peribronchial consolidation, suggestive of aspiration pneumonitis, while no imaging signs of pulmonary fibrosis were detected. Abdominal CT excluded enlargement of abdominopelvic lymph nodes and showed no signs of primary or secondary malignancy within the abdomen or pelvis. A video fluoroscopic (barium) swallow examination revealed marked pharyngeal dysphagia, leading to substantial residue in the pyriform fossae and post-deglutitive penetration of thin and mildly thick liquids to the vocal cords.

Magnetic resonance imaging of the thighs demonstrated findings indicative of active myositis affecting the bilateral obturator internus and externus muscles, as well as less pronounced myositis in the hip adductors, gracilis muscles bilaterally, and both distal iliopsoas muscles (figure 2). Evidence for ongoing muscle inflammation was corroborated by a fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) positron emission tomography (PET) scan, which revealed widespread abnormal FDG uptake involving numerous muscle groups, with maximal intensity seen in the head and neck, bilateral deltoids, forearms, chest wall, abdominal and pelvic regions (figure 3). No evidence of metabolically active lesions was identified elsewhere to indicate malignancy. Assessment of tumor markers including carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), cancer antigen 19-9 (CA19-9), cancer antigen 125 (CA-125), and prostate-specific antigen (PSA) yielded results within normal reference ranges.

Histopathological examination of a thigh muscle biopsy revealed localized areas featuring a moderate number of necrotic, vacuolated, degenerating, myophagocytosed, and regenerating myofibers, distributed predominantly in perifascicular areas, with scattered perimysial infiltrates of isolated histiocytes (figure 4). Immunohistochemical analysis using an antibody against the membrane attack complex identified deposition of complement component 5b-9 (C5b-9) in endomysial capillaries in perifascicular regions, a characteristic finding in dermatomyositis. The final diagnosis was moderate to severe SAE1 antibody–associated dermatomyositis, presenting with inflammatory edema, peribronchial consolidation attributed to micro-aspiration, dysphagia, and myositis. Cutaneous features consisted of a positive Holster sign and periungual erythema.

Differential diagnosis:

Due to the subacute onset of neck swelling and increased CRP, initial diagnostic considerations included Ludwig angina and idiopathic angioedema. Early in his course, muscle weakness and dysphagia were not prominent symptoms. The differential diagnosis encompassed other inflammatory myopathies—such as immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy and inclusion body myositis—as well as infection-related etiologies including cellulitis or erysipelas of the neck, deep neck space infections, and bacterial sialadenitis. Additional considerations were neuromuscular junction conditions like myasthenia gravis, delayed drug hypersensitivity reactions (such as drug rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms), acute contact dermatitis, orofacial granulomatosis, hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis, and superior vena cava syndrome.

Treatment:

Because of considerable involvement of his airway musculature, the patient received intravenous methylprednisolone at 1000 mg per day for three days. Afterward, he transitioned to a prednisone taper, beginning at 60 mg daily and reducing by 5 mg every 2–3 weeks. An initial loading dose of intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG, Gamunex 10%) was administered at 2 g/kg (totaling 165 g), distributed over five days (35 g per day), and this was followed by five additional IVIG cycles spaced four weeks apart, cumulatively totaling another 2 g/kg (165 g) divided among these sessions. Mycophenolate mofetil was initiated at 1 g twice daily, and he received two 1000 mg intravenous infusions of rituximab, separated by two weeks. Methotrexate was not chosen due to its link with interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis—particularly relevant as his pulmonary function tests were abnormal—as well as after a discussion with the patient regarding potential adverse effects. Since mycophenolate was well-tolerated, azathioprine and tacrolimus were not utilized. Sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim was used at first for Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia prophylaxis, but was discontinued following onset of hemolytic anemia (despite no glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase [G6PD] deficiency); he was subsequently able to tolerate nebulized pentamidine 300 mg every four weeks.

He was discharged after a 21-day inpatient stay. His oral medications were administered through nasogastric tube as he had persistent dysphagia at the time of discharge.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following a 3-week hospital stay and ongoing therapy, the patient showed partial recovery of hip flexor strength and substantial reduction in cervical soft tissue edema, though this swelling was still present at discharge. Persistent dysphagia required continued nasogastric feeding, which was maintained for an additional 2 months.

At the 3-week follow-up after discharge, proximal muscle strength was fully restored, and serum creatine kinase levels had returned to 37 U/L. At the immunology assessment conducted 2 months after discharge, cervical swelling had further decreased, with only minimal violaceous erythema on the face and periungual changes noted during examination. Swallowing had improved to the point that the nasogastric tube could be withdrawn, and the patient could advance to a diet of soft solids. The therapeutic regimen included ongoing mycophenolate mofetil, monthly intravenous immunoglobulin, and a tapering dose of prednisone.

At 3 months after discharge, the patient's proximal muscle strength remained unchanged, lung examination was normal, and the swelling of the cervical soft tissue had completely subsided. Prednisone was able to be stopped at 7 months post-discharge, by which point his dysphagia had entirely resolved. Subsequent pulmonary function testing indicated slight improvement, though a persistent restrictive pattern was believed to be consistent with his baseline (figure 5a, b). A follow-up CT scan of the chest performed at 11 months revealed almost complete clearance of the prior basal ground-glass opacities and airspace consolidation, findings that were initially considered secondary to aspiration. One year after diagnosis, the patient maintained stable respiratory function and reported good tolerance for exercise.

Regarding cancer surveillance, the patient reported no constitutional symptoms at follow-up, and he will be observed with ongoing clinical assessments, routine laboratory studies, and appropriate imaging including serial CT scans of the chest. Plans include gastroscopy and colonoscopy to rule out hidden gastrointestinal or colorectal malignancy.

The therapeutic plan calls for continued IVIG treatment and a gradual reduction of mycophenolate over the next 6 months, contingent upon clinical response. He will be monitored closely to assess for any need to adjust his medication regimen.

Discussion:

We describe a patient with dermatomyositis positive for anti-SAE1 antibodies who presented with marked cervical soft tissue edema, which was at first misdiagnosed as idiopathic angioedema. Identification of dermatomyositis as a possible alternative diagnosis, along with thorough physical assessment revealing mild dermatologic findings, facilitated prompt diagnosis and initiation of therapy.

Anti-SAE1 antibodies recognize the small-ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) activating enzyme, a molecule with structural similarity to ubiquitin that plays a role in sumoylation—a post-translational modification process affecting multiple protein substrates. Dermatomyositis associated with SAE1 antibodies commonly exhibits dermatologic manifestations, including heliotrope rash, Gottron’s papules, V-neck sign, and shawl sign. According to a comprehensive review including 215 cases, dysphagia and interstitial lung disease were present in 46% and 43% of individuals with SAE1 dermatomyositis, respectively, while 18% had associated malignancy.

Oedema of the subcutaneous tissues, particularly involving the face or neck, is rarely seen in dermatomyositis. In a substantial study involving 121 individuals with dermatomyositis, just 8 cases (6.61%) exhibited angioedema. A review of published reports from 1985 to 2013 identified only 19 instances of dermatomyositis presenting with oedema. Angioedema has been more frequently associated with certain antibodies, including anti-melanoma differentiation-associated gene (MDA)-5 and anti-transcriptional intermediary factor 1γ (TIF1-γ) antibodies. While facial oedema has been reported in several cases, neck oedema appears much less frequently. In our review of 10 reported cases of SAE1-positive dermatomyositis, oedema was mentioned in only three cases, with just one specifically referring to swelling in the throat area, indicating that neck involvement remains rare.79

This case underscores the importance for emergency physicians, ENT surgeons, immunologists, and rheumatologists to consider dermatomyositis as a potential differential diagnosis in patients presenting with neck swelling, particularly when encountering focal ‘angioedema’ with no clear etiology. Dysphagia and other clinical manifestations may be subtle; therefore, careful assessment for dermatologic or myopathic signs of dermatomyositis, as well as obtaining a creatine kinase measurement, is essential. In this patient, the observed subcutaneous edema was attributable to underlying muscle inflammation related to myositis, rather than to histamine or bradykinin-driven mechanisms seen in typical angioedema.

According to the International Guideline for Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathy-Associated Cancer Screening by the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS), this patient presented with two high-risk features (dermatomyositis, dysphagia) and two intermediate-risk features (positive SAE1 antibody, male gender), thereby classifying him as high risk for malignancy. He underwent comprehensive malignancy evaluation including CT imaging, tumor marker assays, and PET scanning, with further plans for upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopic assessment. Both initial and ongoing surveillance have shown no evidence of malignancy thus far.

Current evidence regarding the most effective pharmacological treatment for anti-SAE1–positive dermatomyositis remains scarce. In the largest cohort published so far, therapeutic strategies varied significantly; the majority of individuals received glucocorticoids in conjunction with various steroid-sparing agents—methotrexate being the most prevalent, followed by mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, and azathioprine. Additionally, intravenous immunoglobulin was reserved for specific cases. In this case, the patient was managed with a regimen consisting of corticosteroids, mycophenolate mofetil, intravenous immunoglobulin, and rituximab, achieving disease remission. The choice of mycophenolate mofetil was influenced by the patient’s preference for an oral therapy, its tolerability profile, and its documented role in the management of dermatomyositis. Compared with other individuals experiencing significant tissue oedema alongside dermatomyositis, this patient demonstrates a relatively favorable prognosis.
