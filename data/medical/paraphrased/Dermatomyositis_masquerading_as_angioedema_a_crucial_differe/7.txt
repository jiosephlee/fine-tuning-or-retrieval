Title: Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook

Kevin Chan, Gemma Kwan, Hwei Choo Soh, Louise Evans

Abstract:

Summary: Dermatomyositis is an uncommon autoimmune condition that primarily impacts the skin, skeletal muscles, and the respiratory tract. Individuals diagnosed with dermatomyositis often need immediate administration of potent immunosuppressive therapy to limit disease advancement and prevent damage to multiple organs. In this report, a previously healthy man in his 40s arrived at the emergency department due to pronounced swelling in the neck. Initial evaluation by ear, nose, and throat specialists suggested a deep neck space infection, but this diagnosis was subsequently excluded. He was then referred to clinical immunology for assessment and management of suspected idiopathic angioedema. Given the atypical presentation of his neck oedema, which was inconsistent with angioedema, and in conjunction with accompanying changes to his skin and nails, a creatine kinase test was urgently performed. Based on clinical findings, a preliminary diagnosis of dermatomyositis was made. Further investigation revealed positivity for the small ubiquitin-like modifier-1 activating enzyme (SAE1) autoantibody and findings on muscle biopsy characteristic of dermatomyositis. This case demonstrates that neck swelling not mediated by histamine or bradykinin can serve as an unusual presenting feature of dermatomyositis, particularly when accompanied by dysphagia and marked muscular involvement necessitating urgent intervention.

Background:

Dermatomyositis is an idiopathic inflammatory myopathy primarily affecting the skin and skeletal muscles, though it may also involve the gastrointestinal tract, joints, lungs, and heart. The condition has an estimated occurrence of approximately 10 cases per million individuals. Its onset displays a bimodal age distribution, most commonly appearing in children between 5 and 14 years, and in adults from 45 to 64 years old. Classic dermatological manifestations encompass Gottron’s papules, the shawl sign, heliotrope eruption, and periungual telangiectasias. Additional clinical signs include proximal muscle weakness, interstitial lung disease, and dysphagia. Pseudoangioedema is a rare manifestation in dermatomyositis; for example, one investigation identified this finding in just 8 out of 121 patients with the disease. When present in the cervical region, pseudoangioedema can mimic primary otolaryngological conditions, or it may be erroneously identified as angioedema mediated by histamine or bradykinin.

Case presentation:

A man in his 40s presented to the emergency department with a 3-week progression of neck swelling and weakness in his proximal hip flexors, accompanied by mild muscle pain. His past medical history was unremarkable, and he had never smoked. He was not on any 3-hydroxy-3-methyl-glutaryl-coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase inhibitors. On initial evaluation, Ludwig’s angina was suspected, prompting a CT scan of the neck. The imaging revealed widespread neck oedema affecting the strap muscles and inflammation extending into the retropharyngeal space (figure 1). The patient was afebrile, maintained an oxygen saturation of 99% on room air, and exhibited no tachypnoea. Nailfold capillaroscopy demonstrated proximal nailfold microhaemorrhages and avascular zones. His white cell count measured 9.1×10^9/L, and his C-reactive protein (CRP) was 55.6 mg/L upon admission. Because the neck swelling persisted without an identifiable source, he was referred to a quaternary care hospital with specialised ear, nose, and throat (ENT) surgical services for advanced work-up. ENT assessment via flexible nasendoscopy found no major anatomical abnormalities in the soft palate, vallecula, larynx, or post-cricoid region; however, the procedure elicited increased secretions and episodes of coughing.

The on-call immunologist was consulted due to concerns about angioedema, as investigations had failed to identify a definitive structural or infectious etiology. Upon further assessment, the patient was observed to exhibit widespread facial erythema, notably extending over the nasolabial folds, as well as an erythematous, edematous, and non-tender neck. The patient reported not taking any medications, specifically denying use of angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and had no known personal or familial incidence of angioedema. Consequently, his clinical presentation did not conform to typical features of either acquired or hereditary angioedema. Comprehensive physical examination identified faint nailfold alterations consistent with periungual erythema, a cutaneous eruption on the right lateral thigh, and reduced hip flexor strength graded at 4/5. There was no evidence of Gottron’s papules or heliotrope rash. Laboratory investigations demonstrated a markedly increased creatine kinase, reaching 16 184 U/L the following day. Renal function tests yielded normal results and the urine screen for myoglobin was negative. Additionally, there was a rise in hepatic transaminases, with an alanine aminotransferase (ALT) measured at 143 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) at 396 U/L, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) at 692 U/L.

Investigations:

In response to the initial suspicion of angioedema, a comprehensive evaluation was undertaken to identify possible etiologies of spontaneous angioedema. Testing revealed that C3 and C4 complement concentrations were within normal limits, and both the activity and quantity of C1 esterase fell within reference ranges.

The patient’s anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) test was positive, with a titre of 1:1280 and a speckled immunofluorescence pattern. Testing for anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA) was negative. A myositis-specific antibody panel, encompassing 16 autoantigens associated with autoimmune inflammatory myopathies, was analyzed using the Euroimmun Euroline immunoassay. Results demonstrated a marked elevation in anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibodies, with a signal intensity measured at 173 (reference range <35). The assay did not detect other myositis-specific antibodies, such as anti-Mi-2 alpha (anti-Mi-2α), anti-Transcription Intermediary Factor-1 gamma (anti-TIF1-γ), anti–Histidyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-Jo-1), and anti–Isoleucyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-OJ). Quantification of immunoglobulin G (IgG), along with its subclasses, indicated values within the expected range.

Screening for potential immunosuppressive viral infections showed the patient to be negative for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), hepatitis C core antibody (HBcAb), hepatitis B e antibody (HBeAb), hepatitis C virus (HCV), human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), varicella zoster virus (VZV) IgG, as well as cytomegalovirus (CMV) IgG and immunoglobulin M (IgM). The QuantiFERON-TB Gold assay yielded an indeterminate result; however, Mycobacterium tuberculosis was not isolated in three separate sputum specimens analyzed for acid-fast bacilli.

Pulmonary function assessment demonstrated a restrictive defect located outside the thoracic cavity, without any notable improvement following bronchodilator administration. Chest CT imaging identified numerous non-cavitating pulmonary nodules predominantly situated in the mid to lower lung zones, the largest of which was 15 mm within the right lower lobe. Additionally, there were irregular regions of peribronchial consolidation consistent with aspiration pneumonitis, but no signs suggestive of pulmonary fibrosis on imaging. CT evaluation of the abdomen did not reveal any enlargement of abdomino-pelvic lymph nodes nor any indication of primary or metastatic malignancies within the abdominal or pelvic regions. Video fluoroscopic barium swallow examination uncovered marked pharyngeal dysphagia, resulting in substantial residue within the pyriform fossae and subsequent penetration of both thin and mildly thick fluids to the level of the vocal cords after swallowing.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the thighs revealed findings indicative of active myositis, most notably affecting the bilateral obturator internus and externus muscles, with less severe involvement observed in the hip adductors, gracilis muscles bilaterally, and the distal iliopsoas muscles (see figure 2). Additional evidence for ongoing muscle inflammation was provided by a fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)-positron emission tomography (PET) scan, which demonstrated widespread abnormal FDG uptake across various muscle groups, with peak intensity in the head and neck, both deltoids, forearms, chest wall, abdomen, and pelvic regions (refer to figure 3). There was no FDG-avid lesion elsewhere to raise suspicion of a malignancy. The levels of tumour markers, specifically carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), cancer antigen 19-9 (CA19-9), cancer antigen 125 (CA-125), and prostate-specific antigen (PSA), were all within the normal reference range.

Histopathological examination of a muscle biopsy from the thigh revealed several foci containing moderate numbers of necrotic, vacuolated, degenerating, myophagocytosed, and regenerating myofibres, displaying a perifascicular distribution pattern, along with scattered perimysial infiltrates primarily composed of dispersed histiocytes (see figure 4). Immunohistochemical analysis targeting the membrane attack complex revealed deposition of complement component 5b-9 (C5b-9) within perifascicular endomysial capillaries, a hallmark finding of dermatomyositis. Consequently, a diagnosis of moderate to severe SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis was established, associated with inflammatory edema, peribronchial consolidation attributed to micro-aspiration, dysphagia, and myositis. Dermatological features included a positive Holster sign as well as periungual erythema.

Differential diagnosis:

At the time of his initial assessment, which was characterized by subacute neck swelling and elevated CRP, Ludwig angina and idiopathic angioedema were high on the list of concerns. Notably, muscle weakness—including difficulty swallowing—was not a major aspect of his early clinical picture. The differential diagnosis was broad and included other inflammatory myopathies (such as immune-mediated necrotising myopathy and inclusion body myositis), infectious etiologies (including cellulitis or erysipelas involving the neck, deep neck space infections, and bacterial sialadenitis), disorders of the neuromuscular junction like myasthenia gravis, delayed drug hypersensitivity reactions (for example, drug rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms), acute contact dermatitis, orofacial granulomatosis, hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis, and superior vena cava syndrome.

Treatment:

Given the extensive involvement of his airway musculature, the patient received intravenous methylprednisolone at a daily dose of 1000 mg for three consecutive days. Subsequently, corticosteroid therapy was transitioned to an oral prednisone taper beginning at 60 mg per day, with a dose reduction of 5 mg every 2 to 3 weeks. A loading course of intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) (Gamunex 10%) was administered at 2 g/kg (cumulative total of 165 g) over a period of 5 days (35 g per day). This was followed by an additional 5 IVIG infusions, each spaced 4 weeks apart, with the total dose of 2 g/kg (= 165 g) distributed across these sessions. Mycophenolate mofetil was started at 1 g twice daily, alongside two intravenous rituximab infusions of 1000 mg each, administered two weeks apart. Methotrexate was intentionally withheld due to its known risk for interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis, especially pertinent given the patient's abnormal pulmonary function tests and after discussion regarding potential adverse effects. Mycophenolate was favored over azathioprine and tacrolimus due to satisfactory tolerability. Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia prophylaxis was initiated with sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim, but this was discontinued after the patient developed haemolytic anaemia (without evidence of glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency); he was subsequently maintained on nebulised pentamidine at 300 mg every 4 weeks.

Following a 21-day hospitalization, he was discharged with persistent dysphagia necessitating administration of oral medications via a nasogastric tube.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following a 3-week period of hospital admission and therapy, the patient showed moderate gains in hip flexor strength and significant reduction in cervical soft tissue swelling, though the swelling had not completely subsided at discharge. Dysphagia was still present, requiring continued use of a nasogastric tube for nutrition for an additional 2 months.

At a follow-up conducted 3 weeks post-discharge, the patient exhibited full restoration of proximal muscle strength, and serum creatine kinase levels had decreased to 37 U/L, within the normal range. At an immunology evaluation conducted 2 months after discharge, further improvement in cervical swelling was observed, with the remaining findings limited to a faint violaceous rash on the face and periungual changes. Swallowing ability had improved enough to discontinue the nasogastric tube and begin eating soft solid foods. The patient continued to receive mycophenolate mofetil, monthly intravenous immunoglobulin, and a gradually tapering dose of prednisone.

Three months after hospital discharge, the patient's proximal muscle strength remained steady, physical examination of the chest was normal, and cervical soft tissue swelling had resolved entirely. Prednisone therapy was discontinued without complication at 7 months post-discharge, coinciding with the complete resolution of dysphagia. Follow-up pulmonary function testing indicated slight improvement but continued to display a residual restrictive pattern, which was believed to represent the patient’s underlying baseline status (see figure 5a, b). An 11-month follow-up CT scan of the chest showed almost complete resolution of the previously detected basal ground-glass opacities and airspace consolidation, which were initially thought to result from aspiration. One year after diagnosis, respiratory status remained stable and exercise capacity was preserved.

In terms of malignancy surveillance, the patient reported no constitutional symptoms at follow-up visits and will remain under observation, including symptom assessment, physical examinations, routine laboratory investigations, and ongoing imaging such as serial CT scans of the chest. Plans are in place to conduct both a gastroscopy and colonoscopy to rule out hidden gastrointestinal or colorectal cancers.

Management includes continued administration of IVIG, with a strategy to gradually reduce the mycophenolate dose over the following 6 months based on the patient's clinical course. Close monitoring will persist to reassess and modify the medication regimen if necessary.

Discussion:

We describe a patient with dermatomyositis associated with anti-SAE1 antibodies who initially manifested with marked cervical soft tissue swelling, a presentation that was first erroneously attributed to idiopathic angioedema. Accurate identification of dermatomyositis in the differential diagnosis, aided by a thorough physical assessment that detected discreet dermatological manifestations, facilitated prompt recognition and initiation of therapy.

Anti-SAE1 antibodies specifically target the small-ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) activating enzyme, which shares structural similarities with ubiquitin and functions in the sumoylation-mediated post-translational modification of multiple substrate proteins. The clinical phenotype of SAE1 antibody–associated dermatomyositis is most often defined by characteristic cutaneous findings, including heliotrope rash, Gottron’s papules, as well as the V-neck and shawl signs. A comprehensive review of 215 reported cases indicated that dysphagia and interstitial lung disease were observed in 46% and 43% of individuals with SAE1 dermatomyositis, respectively, while 18% developed malignancy.

Oedema of the subcutaneous tissue, particularly involving the face or neck, is rarely observed in cases of dermatomyositis. In a sizeable cohort comprising 121 individuals diagnosed with dermatomyositis, just 8 patients (6.61%) exhibited angioedema. An analysis of literature spanning publications from 1985 to 2013 revealed only 19 documented instances where oedema accompanied dermatomyositis. The presence of specific autoantibodies, namely anti-melanoma differentiation-associated gene (MDA)-5 antibody and anti-transcriptional intermediary factor 1γ (TIF1-γ) antibody, appeared to correlate more frequently with angioedema in comparison to other antibody profiles. Although several reports detailed facial oedema, cases specifically describing swelling in the neck area were even less common. In our literature review that considered 10 cases of SAE1-positive dermatomyositis, oedema was described in merely three cases, with just one referencing swelling in the throat region. This observation underscores the rarity of neck involvement in dermatomyositis-related oedema.79

This case underscores the importance for emergency physicians, ENT specialists, immunologists, and rheumatologists to consider dermatomyositis as a potential diagnosis in patients presenting with neck swelling, particularly when localized ‘angioedema’ lacks an identifiable cause. As manifestations such as dysphagia and additional involvement may be subtle, it is essential to perform a thorough examination for clinical features of dermatomyositis and to assess creatine kinase levels. In this patient, the observed subcutaneous oedema was attributed to muscle inflammation resulting from myositis, rather than to angioedema mechanisms involving histamine or bradykinin pathways.

Following the recommendations set by the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS) in the International Guideline for Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathy-Associated Cancer Screening, this patient was classified as high risk for malignancy based on two major risk factors (dermatomyositis, dysphagia) and two intermediate factors (SAE1 antibody positivity, male sex). Accordingly, comprehensive malignancy screening was conducted, including CT imaging, tumor marker evaluation, and PET scanning, alongside planned upper and lower GI endoscopic examinations. To date, both initial and ongoing interval surveillance assessments have yielded reassuring findings.

There is a scarcity of robust data regarding the most effective pharmacological management for anti-SAE1–positive dermatomyositis. The largest existing cohort describes considerable variability in treatment modalities, with the majority of individuals receiving glucocorticoids alongside various steroid-sparing medications—methotrexate being the most frequent, though mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, and azathioprine were also employed. Additionally, intravenous immunoglobulin was administered selectively. In this case, the patient received a regimen including steroids, mycophenolate mofetil, intravenous immunoglobulin, and rituximab, resulting in disease remission. The decision to utilize mycophenolate mofetil reflected the patient’s preference for a once-daily oral medication, as well as its favorable tolerability profile and established role in treating dermatomyositis. Notably, this patient’s outlook appears more favorable compared to others with dermatomyositis complicated by extensive tissue oedema.
