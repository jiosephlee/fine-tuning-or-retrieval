Title: Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook

Kevin Chan, Gemma Kwan, Hwei Choo Soh, Louise Evans

Abstract:

Summary: Dermatomyositis is an uncommon autoimmune disorder primarily involving the skin, musculature, and the respiratory tract. Immediate and aggressive immunosuppressive therapy is typically necessary in dermatomyositis patients to limit disease advancement and prevent damage to multiple organ systems. A man in his 40s with no significant past medical history presented to the emergency department with pronounced cervical swelling. Initially, otolaryngology evaluated him for a possible deep neck space infection; however, this was excluded. He was then referred to clinical immunology for further assessment and management of presumed idiopathic angioedema. Because the cervical edema displayed atypical features and did not match classic angioedema, and in light of associated dermatologic and periungual findings, a creatine kinase test was quickly obtained. Dermatomyositis was suspected, and the diagnosis was later confirmed by detection of the small ubiquitin-like modifier-1 activating enzyme (SAE1) autoantibody and findings on muscle biopsy characteristic of the disease. This report demonstrates that neck swelling not mediated by histamine or bradykinin can be a rare manifestation of dermatomyositis, presenting here with dysphagia and marked muscular involvement that required urgent intervention.

Background:

Dermatomyositis is an idiopathic inflammatory myopathy primarily impacting the skin and musculature, though involvement of the gastrointestinal system, joints, pulmonary structures, and cardiac tissue may also occur. Its incidence is estimated at 10 per million individuals. The disorder displays a bimodal age distribution, with peak occurrence in pediatric patients between 5 and 14 years and in adults from 45 to 64 years. Distinct dermatologic manifestations comprise Gottron’s papules, the shawl sign, heliotrope rash, and periungual telangiectasias. Additional clinical features include proximal muscle weakness, interstitial lung disease, and difficulty swallowing. Pseudoangioedema is rare in dermatomyositis; one investigation documented its presence in just 8 out of 121 dermatomyositis cases. When the neck is involved, pseudoangioedema may mimic primary otolaryngologic conditions or be misinterpreted as histaminergic or bradykinin-induced angioedema.

Case presentation:

A man in his 40s arrived at the emergency department reporting a three-week duration of gradually increasing neck swelling and weakness of his hip flexors, with mild muscle pain. He did not have a history of major illnesses and had never smoked. He was also not on any HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors. The clinical team initially suspected Ludwig’s angina, prompting a CT scan of the neck that revealed generalized swelling affecting the strap muscles and inflammatory changes spreading into the retropharyngeal space (figure 1). He was without fever, maintained an oxygen saturation of 99% on ambient air, and showed no signs of rapid breathing. Nailfold capillaroscopy identified proximal nailfold microhaemorrhages and areas lacking vascularity. On admission, his white blood cell count measured 9.1×10^9/L, and C-reactive protein was 55.6 mg/L. Persistent neck swelling of unclear origin resulted in his transfer to a quaternary care center with ENT surgical expertise for further assessment. ENT performed a flexible nasendoscopy which found no noteworthy anatomical problems in the soft palate, vallecula, larynx, or post-cricoid region, although there was increased secretions and the patient coughed during the procedure.

The on-call immunologist was consulted regarding possible angioedema, as there was no evident structural or infectious etiology. Upon assessment, the patient exhibited diffuse facial erythema, including the nasolabial folds, as well as an erythematous, enlarged, but non-tender neck. He reported not taking any medications such as angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and he lacked both personal and family histories of angioedema. His presentation was unusual for either acquired or hereditary forms of angioedema. Comprehensive physical examination revealed subtle nailfold findings consistent with periungual erythema, a cutaneous eruption over the right lateral thigh, and diminished hip flexor strength, graded at 4/5. There were no Gottron’s papules or heliotrope rash observed. Serum creatine kinase was measured the following day and noted to be markedly elevated at 16,184 U/L. Renal function remained within normal limits, and urine myoglobin screening was negative. Laboratory studies demonstrated raised transaminase levels, with an ALT of 143 U/L, AST of 396 U/L, and LDH elevated to 692 U/L.

Investigations:

Considering the suspicion for angioedema, a range of investigations was conducted to identify potential etiologies of spontaneous angioedema. Complement levels for C3 and C4 were within normal limits, as were both the functional activity and quantification of C1 esterase.

Testing revealed a positive anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) with a titer of 1:1280 and a speckled pattern, while anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA) was negative. A myositis antibody profile encompassing 16 autoantigens related to inflammatory myopathies was run using the Euroimmun Euroline immunoassay; this demonstrated a marked positivity for anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibodies, with signal intensity measured at 173 (reference range <35). There was no reactivity detected for other myositis-specific antibodies such as anti-Mi-2 alpha (anti-Mi-2α), anti-Transcription Intermediary Factor-1 gamma (anti-TIF1-γ), anti-Histidyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-Jo-1), or anti-Isoleucyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-OJ). Immunoglobulin G (IgG) including subclass analysis showed normal values. Screening for immunosuppressive-associated infections revealed negative results for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), hepatitis B core antibody (HBcAb), hepatitis B e antibody (HBeAb), hepatitis C virus (HCV), HIV, varicella zoster virus (VZV) IgG, as well as cytomegalovirus (CMV) IgG and IgM. The QuantiFERON-TB Gold test was indeterminate, however, three sputum samples tested for acid-fast bacilli indicative of Mycobacterium tuberculosis were negative.

Pulmonary function testing demonstrated a restrictive pattern localized to the extrathoracic region, without appreciable response to bronchodilator therapy. Chest CT identified numerous non-cavitating pulmonary nodules predominantly situated in the mid to lower lung zones, the largest of which was 15 mm in diameter within the right lower lobe. There were irregular peribronchial consolidations, likely representing aspiration pneumonitis, but no radiographic evidence of pulmonary fibrosis. Abdominal CT did not show enlarged abdomino-pelvic lymph nodes, nor was there any indication of primary or metastatic neoplastic processes within the abdomen or pelvis. A video fluoroscopic barium swallow assessment demonstrated marked pharyngeal dysphagia, resulting in significant residue within the pyriform fossae and subsequent penetration of thin and mildly thick liquids onto the vocal cords after swallowing.

MRI of the thighs demonstrated imaging findings indicative of active myositis affecting the bilateral obturator internus and externus muscles, with less pronounced involvement observed in the hip adductors, gracilis muscles bilaterally, and distal iliopsoas muscles on both sides (figure 2). Evidence of ongoing muscle inflammation was corroborated by fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)-positron emission tomography (PET), which revealed extensive and abnormal FDG uptake across several muscle groups, with the highest activity in the head and neck regions, both deltoids, forearm muscles, chest wall, abdominal area, and pelvis (figure 3). No FDG-avid lesions were detected elsewhere to imply the presence of malignancy. Tumor markers, specifically carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), cancer antigen 19-9 (CA19-9), cancer antigen 125 (CA-125), and prostate-specific antigen (PSA), were all found to be within normal limits.

Histological analysis of a muscle biopsy from the thigh identified zones with moderate numbers of necrotic, vacuolated, degenerating, myophagocytosed, and regenerating myofibres showing a perifascicular distribution. Scattered perimysial inflammatory infiltrates, mainly consisting of dispersed histiocytes, were also observed (figure 4). Immunohistochemical staining for membrane attack complex demonstrated deposition of complement component 5b-9 (C5b-9) within perifascicular endomysial capillaries—an appearance that is characteristic of dermatomyositis. Based on these findings, a diagnosis of moderate to severe SAE1 antibody–positive dermatomyositis was established, accompanied by inflammatory edema, peribronchial consolidation attributable to micro-aspiration, dysphagia, and myositis. Skin-related findings included a positive Holster sign and periungual erythema.

Differential diagnosis:

Due to his subacute onset characterized by neck swelling and an increased CRP level, Ludwig angina and idiopathic angioedema were considered initially. Muscle weakness and dysphagia were not significant aspects in the early phase. The differential diagnosis encompassed additional inflammatory myopathies such as immune-mediated necrotising myopathy and inclusion body myositis, infectious etiologies including cellulitis or erysipelas of the neck, deep neck space infections, and bacterial sialadenitis, as well as neuromuscular junction disorders like myasthenia gravis. Delayed drug hypersensitivity reactions (such as drug rash accompanied by eosinophilia and systemic symptoms), acute contact dermatitis, orofacial granulomatosis, hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis, and superior vena cava syndrome were also considered.

Treatment:

Because of substantial involvement of the airway musculature, the patient received intravenous methylprednisolone for 3 days at a dosage of 1000 mg per day. This was followed by a tapering regimen of prednisone beginning at 60 mg daily, with reductions of 5 mg every 2 to 3 weeks. He was administered a loading dose of intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) (Gamunex 10%) totaling 2 g/kg (amounting to 165 g) over 5 days (35 g per day), after which he underwent an additional 5 cycles, each 4 weeks apart, at a total dose of 2 g/kg (165 g) divided across those sessions. Mycophenolate mofetil was initiated at 1 g twice daily, and he received two infusions of rituximab at 1000 mg intravenous, spaced 2 weeks apart. Methotrexate was avoided due to its association with interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis, given the patient’s abnormal pulmonary function tests and following discussion regarding potential adverse effects. Azathioprine and tacrolimus were not considered since the patient tolerated mycophenolate well. For prophylaxis against Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim was started initially, but discontinued after the onset of haemolytic anaemia (not related to glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency); subsequently, nebulized pentamidine at a dose of 300 mg every 4 weeks was tolerated.

He was released following a 21-day hospital admission. Due to ongoing dysphagia at discharge, his oral medications were delivered via nasogastric tube.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following a 3-week inpatient course of therapy, the patient exhibited moderate gains in hip flexor strength and significant reduction in cervical soft tissue edema, though the swelling had not completely resolved by discharge. Persistent dysphagia required ongoing nasogastric tube supplementation for an additional two months.

At a follow-up visit three weeks post-discharge, proximal muscle power was fully restored, and serum creatine kinase had decreased to 37 U/L. An immunology assessment conducted two months after discharge noted continued improvement in cervical edema, with examination revealing only persistent violaceous erythema on the face and periungual alterations. Swallowing capacity had improved enough to discontinue nasogastric feeding and initiate a diet of soft solids. The patient continued on therapy with mycophenolate mofetil, monthly intravenous immunoglobulin, and a progressively tapering dose of prednisone.

At three months after discharge, the patient’s proximal muscle strength remained stable, chest examination findings were normal, and the cervical soft tissue swelling had fully subsided. Prednisone was discontinued successfully seven months after discharge, at which point dysphagia had resolved. Subsequent pulmonary function tests indicated modest improvement, but a persistent restrictive pattern was felt to represent his underlying baseline physiology (figure 5a, b). An 11-month follow-up chest CT revealed almost complete resolution of the previously detected basal ground-glass changes and airspace consolidations, which were initially considered due to aspiration. Twelve months after the initial diagnosis, the patient’s respiratory function remained stable and he demonstrated good exercise tolerance.

In terms of cancer surveillance, he reported no constitutional symptoms at follow-up visits and will continue to be monitored based on symptoms, clinical evaluation, periodic laboratory investigations, and relevant imaging such as serial chest CT scans. He is scheduled for gastroscopy and colonoscopy to rule out hidden gastrointestinal or colorectal malignancy.

The management plan included ongoing IVIG and a gradual reduction of mycophenolate dosage over the next six months, dependent upon clinical status. He will be under careful surveillance to assess whether any changes to his medication regimen are warranted.

Discussion:

We describe a patient with dermatomyositis positive for anti-SAE1 antibodies who exhibited marked swelling of the cervical soft tissue, a presentation that was initially misdiagnosed as idiopathic angioedema. The consideration of dermatomyositis in the differential, together with thorough physical assessment that identified subtle cutaneous manifestations, facilitated an accurate and prompt diagnosis and subsequent initiation of therapy.

Anti-SAE1 antibodies target the small-ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) activating enzyme, a protein homologous to ubiquitin and responsible for the post-translational process of ‘sumoylation’ in multiple target proteins. Dermatomyositis with SAE1 antibody positivity usually presents with characteristic skin manifestations, including heliotrope eruption, Gottron’s papules, the V-neck sign, and the shawl sign. According to a comprehensive review of 215 reported cases, dysphagia and interstitial lung disease were found in 46% and 43% of SAE1-associated dermatomyositis cases, respectively, with malignancy noted in 18%.

Swelling of subcutaneous tissues, particularly in the face or neck, is rarely observed in cases of dermatomyositis. In a cohort of 121 individuals diagnosed with dermatomyositis, angioedema was present in just 8 patients (6.61%). A review of literature spanning from 1985 to 2013 identified only 19 reports where dermatomyositis was accompanied by oedema. Antibodies such as anti-melanoma differentiation-associated gene (MDA)-5 and anti-transcriptional intermediary factor 1γ (TIF1-γ) were reported to have stronger associations with angioedema than other antibodies. Facial swelling appeared in several published cases, whereas reports of neck oedema were less common. Our review of 10 documented instances of SAE1-positive dermatomyositis indicated oedema in only three, and just one referenced swelling in the area surrounding the throat, highlighting the rarity of neck involvement.79

This case emphasizes the importance for emergency physicians, ENT specialists, immunologists, and rheumatologists to consider dermatomyositis as a potential cause of neck swelling, particularly when presenting as localized 'angioedema' with no clear etiology or appearing idiopathic. Clinical manifestations such as dysphagia and other associated features may be mild, making it essential to inspect for dermatomyositis-specific physical signs and to order a creatine kinase assay. In this patient, the observed subcutaneous edema resulted from muscular inflammation due to myositis rather than from angioedema mechanisms mediated by histamine or bradykinin.

According to the International Guideline for Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathy-Associated Cancer Screening published by the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS), this individual met two criteria for high risk (dermatomyositis, dysphagia) and two intermediate criteria (SAE1 antibody positivity, male gender), categorizing him as high risk for malignancy. The patient underwent evaluation for cancer, including computed tomography imaging, tumor marker assays, and positron emission tomography scanning, with forthcoming plans for both upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopic examinations. Thorough initial screening and ongoing periodic assessments have thus far provided reassuring results.

There is a scarcity of data regarding optimal medication management in anti-SAE1–positive dermatomyositis. The largest reported series to date demonstrated diverse therapeutic strategies, with glucocorticoids commonly administered alongside various steroid-sparing agents, most frequently methotrexate, but also including mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, and azathioprine; intravenous immunoglobulin was reserved for select individuals. This patient received steroids, mycophenolate mofetil, intravenous immunoglobulin, and rituximab, resulting in remission. The choice of mycophenolate mofetil was influenced by the patient’s preference for a daily oral treatment, its favorable tolerability profile, and established efficacy in dermatomyositis. Notably, this patient’s prognosis is favorable relative to other cases presenting with marked tissue edema associated with dermatomyositis.
