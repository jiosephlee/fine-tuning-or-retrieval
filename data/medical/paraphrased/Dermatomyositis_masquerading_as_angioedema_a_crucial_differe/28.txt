Title: Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook

Kevin Chan, Gemma Kwan, Hwei Choo Soh, Louise Evans

Abstract:

Summary: Dermatomyositis is an uncommon autoimmune condition that primarily involves the skin, muscles, and respiratory tract. Individuals diagnosed with dermatomyositis generally necessitate immediate and aggressive immunosuppressive therapy in order to halt disease advancement and prevent injury to multiple organs. A previously healthy man in his 40s arrived at the emergency department with pronounced swelling in the neck. Initially, ear, nose, and throat specialists evaluated him for a suspected deep neck space infection. Once this possibility was excluded, he was referred to clinical immunology for assessment and management of presumed idiopathic angioedema. Due to the atypical characteristics of his neck edema, which did not align with angioedema, along with observed skin and nail abnormalities, a creatine kinase test was promptly performed. The patient received a preliminary diagnosis of dermatomyositis and was subsequently confirmed to have a positive small ubiquitin-like modifier-1 activating enzyme (SAE1) autoantibody as well as a characteristic muscle biopsy. This case emphasizes that neck swelling not mediated by histamine or bradykinin can be a rare manifestation of dermatomyositis, presenting with dysphagia and considerable muscle involvement that demands urgent intervention.

Background:

Dermatomyositis is an idiopathic inflammatory myopathy that predominantly affects the skin and skeletal muscles but may also involve the gastrointestinal tract, joints, lungs, and cardiac tissue. The condition is reported to have an incidence of approximately 10 per million individuals. Its distribution is bimodal, most frequently presenting in children aged 5 to 14 years as well as adults between 45 and 64 years. Hallmark dermatologic manifestations include Gottron’s papules, the shawl sign, heliotrope rash, and periungual telangiectasia. Additional symptoms encompass proximal muscle weakness, interstitial lung disease, and dysphagia. Pseudoangioedema occurs infrequently in dermatomyositis; one study reported its presence in only 8 out of 121 patients with dermatomyositis. When present in the cervical region, it may initially present as an otolaryngologic issue, or be confused with angioedema due to histamine or bradykinin pathways.

Case presentation:

A man in his 40s came to the emergency department reporting a three-week progression of neck swelling and proximal muscle weakness localized to his hip flexors, as well as mild muscle pain. He had no notable medical history and had never smoked. He was not taking any HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors. Initially, Ludwig’s angina was suspected, prompting a cervical CT scan that revealed extensive neck edema affecting the strap muscles and inflammatory changes reaching the retropharyngeal space (see figure 1). He was afebrile, with a room air oxygen saturation of 99% and normal respiratory rate. Nailfold capillaroscopy revealed proximal microhemorrhages and avascular regions. His white blood cell count measured 9.1×10^9/L, and his CRP level was 55.6 mg/L at the time of presentation. Because the neck swelling persisted without a clear etiology, he was transferred to a quaternary facility offering ENT surgical services for additional assessment. ENT specialists carried out a flexible nasendoscopy, which demonstrated no remarkable structural abnormalities in the soft palate, vallecula, larynx, or post-cricoid region, but the examination was marked by increased secretions and episodes of coughing.

The case was reviewed with the on-call immunologist due to suspicion of angioedema, since no obvious structural or infectious origin was identified. Assessment revealed diffuse redness of the facial skin, including involvement of the nasolabial folds, as well as a neck that was erythematous, edematous, but not tender. The patient stated that he was not taking any medications such as angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and reported no personal or family history of angioedema. Clinically, his presentation was not consistent with either acquired or hereditary angioedema. Detailed examination revealed subtle nailfold findings consistent with periungual erythema, a rash located on the right lateral thigh, and diminished hip flexor strength graded as 4/5. Neither Gottron’s papules nor heliotrope eruption were observed. A creatine kinase test was ordered and results the following day showed a marked increase to 16,184 U/L. Renal function tests were within normal limits, and the urine myoglobin screen was negative. In addition, his transaminases were elevated, with alanine aminotransferase (ALT) at 143 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) at 396 U/L, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) at 692 U/L.

Investigations:

Due to initial suspicion of angioedema, multiple investigations were conducted to identify potential causes of idiopathic angioedema. His complement levels, specifically C3 and C4, were found to be within normal limits, as were both the quantitative and functional assays for C1 esterase.

The patient demonstrated a positive anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) at a titre of 1:1280, exhibiting a speckled pattern. Anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA) testing returned negative. A comprehensive myositis antibody panel analyzing 16 antigens commonly associated with autoimmune inflammatory myopathies was conducted using the Euroimmun Euroline immunoassay. Results showed a strong positivity for anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibody, with a signal intensity of 173 (reference value <35). No reactivity was detected for other myositis-related antibodies, including anti-Mi-2 alpha (anti-Mi-2α), anti-Transcription Intermediary Factor-1 gamma (anti-TIF1-γ), anti–Histidyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-Jo-1), or anti–Isoleucyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-OJ). Immunoglobulin G (IgG) levels, including subclass distribution, remained within normal range.

The immunosuppression screening revealed negative results for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), hepatitis C core antibody (HBcAb), hepatitis B e antibody (HBeAb), hepatitis C virus (HCV), HIV, varicella zoster virus (VZV) IgG, as well as cytomegalovirus (CMV) IgG and immunoglobulin M (IgM). QuantiFERON-TB Gold yielded an indeterminate outcome, while acid-fast bacilli testing for Mycobacterium tuberculosis from three separate sputum specimens was negative.

Pulmonary function testing demonstrated an extrathoracic restrictive defect, without a notable response to bronchodilator therapy. Chest CT identified multiple pulmonary nodules lacking cavitation, primarily distributed in the mid to lower lung zones, the largest being 15 mm in diameter located in the right lower lobe. Additionally, there were patchy regions of peribronchial consolidation, most likely attributable to aspiration pneumonitis, but no radiological signs of pulmonary fibrosis. Abdominal CT revealed no abdomino-pelvic lymphadenopathy and showed no indications of either primary or metastatic cancer in the abdomen or pelvis. The video fluoroscopic (barium) swallow assessment indicated pronounced pharyngeal dysphagia, resulting in considerable residue in the pyriform fossae and post-swallow penetration of both thin and mildly thick liquids to the vocal cords.

MRI imaging of the thighs revealed findings indicative of active myositis within both obturator internus and externus muscles, as well as less pronounced involvement of the hip adductors, bilateral gracilis, and distal segments of the bilateral iliopsoas muscles (see figure 2). Ongoing muscle inflammation was corroborated by an FDG-PET scan, which demonstrated widespread, atypical FDG uptake in several muscle groups, with the greatest intensity in the head and neck, both deltoids, forearms, chest wall, abdomen, and pelvic regions (refer to figure 3). No metabolically active lesions were identified in other areas that would raise concern for malignancy. Serum levels of tumour markers, specifically CEA, CA19-9, CA-125, and PSA, were within normal limits.

Histological examination of a thigh muscle biopsy identified areas containing moderate numbers of necrotic, vacuolated, degenerating, myophagocytosed, and regenerating myofibres arranged in a perifascicular pattern. Additionally, perimysial regions showed scattered inflammatory infiltrates composed of dispersed histiocytes (see figure 4). Membrane attack complex immunohistochemistry displayed deposition of complement component C5b-9 within perifascicular endomysial capillaries, which is characteristic of dermatomyositis. The patient was diagnosed with moderate to severe dermatomyositis associated with SAE1 antibody positivity, exhibiting features of inflammatory edema, peribronchial consolidation likely due to micro-aspiration, dysphagia, and myositis. Dermatologic findings included a positive Holster sign and periungual erythema.

Differential diagnosis:

Due to his subacute onset of neck swelling accompanied by an elevated CRP, initial consideration was given to Ludwig angina as well as idiopathic angioedema. Early in his clinical course, muscle weakness, including difficulty swallowing, was not a predominant symptom. The differential diagnosis encompassed other inflammatory myopathies—such as immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy and inclusion body myositis—potential infectious etiologies (including cellulitis or erysipelas of the neck, infections of the deep neck spaces, and bacterial sialadenitis), disorders of the neuromuscular junction like myasthenia gravis, delayed hypersensitivity reactions to medications (such as drug rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms), acute contact dermatitis, orofacial granulomatosis, hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis, and superior vena cava syndrome.

Treatment:

Because of marked involvement of the airway musculature, the patient received intravenous methylprednisolone for 3 days at a dose of 1000 mg daily. This was transitioned to an oral prednisone taper beginning at 60 mg per day, decreasing by 5 mg every 2 to 3 weeks. An initial intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG, Gamunex 10%) course of 2 g/kg (totaling 165 g) was infused over 5 days (35 g daily), with five subsequent cycles administered 4 weeks apart, with the 2 g/kg dose divided across those treatments. Mycophenolate mofetil was started at 1 g twice daily, and rituximab was given as two 1000 mg intravenous doses, spaced 2 weeks apart. Methotrexate was avoided due to its association with interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis, especially considering the patient's abnormal pulmonary function testing and after discussion with the patient about its side effect profile. Azathioprine and tacrolimus were not pursued, as mycophenolate was well tolerated. For prophylaxis against Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, he was initially started on sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim, which was later stopped because of development of haemolytic anaemia (in the absence of glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase [G6PD] deficiency); he then tolerated inhaled pentamidine 300 mg every 4 weeks.

The patient was discharged following 21 days in the hospital. Due to ongoing dysphagia at discharge, all oral medications were administered via nasogastric tube.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following a three-week period of hospitalisation and therapy, the patient showed partial resolution of hip flexor weakness and significant reduction in cervical soft tissue swelling, though the swelling was not completely resolved at discharge. Persistent dysphagia required ongoing nasogastric feeding, which continued for an additional two months.

Upon reassessment three weeks post-discharge, proximal muscle strength had returned to normal and serum creatine kinase levels had decreased to 37 U/L. At a follow-up immunology appointment two months after discharge, further reduction in cervical swelling was observed, with only mild remaining violaceous erythema on the face and periungual changes detected during physical examination. Swallowing improved to the extent that the nasogastric tube could be withdrawn and the patient was advanced to a diet of soft solids. The treatment regimen of mycophenolate mofetil, monthly intravenous immunoglobulin, and a gradually reducing dose of prednisone was maintained.

Three months after discharge, the patient's proximal muscle strength was unchanged, chest assessment was normal, and swelling in the cervical soft tissues had resolved completely. Prednisone was able to be discontinued at seven months following discharge, at which point the patient's dysphagia was also fully resolved. Repeat pulmonary function testing indicated slight improvement, though a persistent restrictive defect remained, likely reflecting his baseline status (figure 5a, b). A chest CT performed at 11 months showed almost total resolution of prior basal ground-glass changes and airspace consolidation, which were previously thought to be due to aspiration. At one year post-diagnosis, his respiratory function was stable and he was able to tolerate exercise well.

Regarding malignancy screening, he reported no systemic symptoms during follow-up and will continue to be surveilled using symptom assessment, physical examinations, routine bloodwork, and relevant imaging, including serial chest CTs. He will also have upper and lower endoscopies performed to rule out hidden gastrointestinal or colorectal cancer.

The care plan included ongoing IVIG therapy, with a gradual reduction in the mycophenolate dose over the next six months, as dictated by clinical progress. He will be monitored closely to evaluate if modifications to his medication regimen are warranted.

Discussion:

We describe a patient with dermatomyositis positive for anti-SAE1 antibodies who developed marked cervical soft tissue edema, which was initially misdiagnosed as idiopathic angioedema. Dermatomyositis was identified as a possible cause following a thorough physical assessment that detected faint skin findings, enabling prompt diagnosis and initiation of therapy.

Anti-SAE1 autoantibodies recognize the small-ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) activating enzyme, a protein with structural resemblance to ubiquitin, which participates in sumoylation—a post-translational modification affecting multiple proteins. Dermatomyositis associated with SAE1 antibodies generally presents with skin manifestations such as heliotrope eruption, Gottron's papules, the V-neck sign, and the shawl sign. In an extensive review that included 215 individuals, 46% with SAE1 dermatomyositis experienced dysphagia, 43% developed interstitial lung disease, and 18% were diagnosed with malignancy.

Swelling of subcutaneous tissues, particularly in the face or neck, is a rare manifestation in dermatomyositis. Among 121 patients with dermatomyositis enrolled in a large cohort study, only 8 (6.61%) experienced angioedema. A review of the literature encompassing articles from 1985 to 2013 identified merely 19 reported cases of dermatomyositis presenting with oedema. Certain antibodies, namely anti-melanoma differentiation-associated gene (MDA)-5 and anti-transcriptional intermediary factor 1γ (TIF1-γ), were found to be more frequently linked to angioedema compared to other autoantibodies. Several reports noted facial oedema, although involvement of the neck was less commonly observed. In our own review of 10 cases of SAE1-positive dermatomyositis, oedema was documented in only three cases, with just one specifying swelling near the throat, highlighting the rarity of neck involvement.79

This case emphasizes the importance for emergency physicians, ENT specialists, immunologists, and rheumatologists to consider dermatomyositis as a potential differential diagnosis in patients presenting with neck swelling, particularly when localized 'angioedema' lacks a clear cause or is classified as idiopathic. As manifestations such as dysphagia and other symptoms may be mild, careful examination for clinical features of dermatomyositis and ordering creatine kinase testing are essential. The subcutaneous edema observed in this patient was attributed to muscle inflammation associated with myositis, and not to angioedema mediated by histamine or bradykinin.

Following the International Guideline for Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathy-Associated Cancer Screening from the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS), the patient was categorized as high risk for malignancy, based on two high risk features (dermatomyositis and dysphagia) and two intermediate risk factors (presence of SAE1 antibody and male sex). He proceeded with cancer screening including CT imaging, assessment of tumor markers, and a PET scan, with additional plans for upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopy. Comprehensive baseline evaluations and subsequent follow-up have continued to be reassuring thus far.

Available data to inform the best medical treatment for anti-SAE1–positive dermatomyositis are scarce. The largest cohort described thus far demonstrates diverse management strategies, with the majority of patients administered glucocorticoids alongside various steroid-sparing therapies. Methotrexate was the most frequently used immunosuppressant, but mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, and azathioprine were also employed; intravenous immunoglobulin was reserved for certain cases. The patient in this report received glucocorticoids, mycophenolate mofetil, intravenous immunoglobulin, and rituximab, resulting in remission. Selection of mycophenolate mofetil reflected the patient's preference for a daily oral medication and its favorable tolerability profile, as well as documented efficacy in dermatomyositis. Compared to others experiencing substantial tissue edema with dermatomyositis, this patient’s outlook is positive.
