Title: Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook

Kevin Chan, Gemma Kwan, Hwei Choo Soh, Louise Evans

Abstract:

Summary: Dermatomyositis is an uncommon autoimmune disorder that primarily involves the skin, muscles, and respiratory tract. Management typically entails urgent initiation of potent immunosuppressive therapy to limit the advancement of the condition and prevent damage to multiple organ systems. A male patient in his 40s with no prior health issues presented to the emergency department with marked neck swelling. Initially, ear, nose, and throat specialists evaluated him for a possible deep neck space infection. When this diagnosis was excluded, he was subsequently assessed by clinical immunology for potential idiopathic angioedema. Given the atypical features of his neck edema, which did not fit with angioedema and was accompanied by cutaneous and nail changes, a creatine kinase test was rapidly requested. The patient was provisionally diagnosed with dermatomyositis and later confirmed to have a positive small ubiquitin-like modifier-1 activating enzyme (SAE1) autoantibody, as well as characteristic findings on muscle biopsy. This case demonstrates that neck swelling unrelated to histamine or bradykinin pathways is a rare initial manifestation of dermatomyositis, presenting with dysphagia and extensive muscle involvement necessitating immediate therapeutic intervention.

Background:

Dermatomyositis is an idiopathic inflammatory myopathy predominantly affecting the skin and muscles, though it can also involve the gastrointestinal tract, joints, lungs, and heart. The condition occurs at an estimated rate of 10 cases per one million individuals. Its onset demonstrates a bimodal distribution, primarily seen in children between 5 and 14 years of age and in adults from 45 to 64 years old. Classic skin manifestations include Gottron’s papules, the shawl sign, heliotrope eruption, and periungual telangiectasias. Additional features comprise proximal muscle weakness, interstitial lung disease, and dysphagia. Pseudoangioedema is an uncommon manifestation of dermatomyositis; in one study, only 8 of 121 dermatomyositis patients exhibited this feature. When pseudoangioedema involves the neck, it can be misinterpreted as an otolaryngologic pathology or confused with angioedema of either histaminergic or bradykinin origin.

Case presentation:

A man in his 40s arrived at the emergency department reporting a three-week progression of neck swelling, as well as proximal muscle weakness concentrated in the hip flexors, and mild muscle aches. He reported no prior significant health conditions and had never smoked. He denied use of 3-hydroxy-3-methyl-glutaryl-coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase inhibitors. Ludwig’s angina was considered initially, prompting a neck CT scan which revealed widespread edema affecting the strap muscles and inflammatory changes extending into the retropharyngeal area (see figure 1). On examination, he was afebrile, with oxygen saturation of 99% on room air and no evidence of tachypnea. Nailfold capillaroscopy uncovered proximal microhemorrhages at the nailfolds and areas lacking vascularity. His initial laboratory evaluation revealed a white blood cell count of 9.1×10^9/L and a C-reactive protein (CRP) of 55.6 mg/L. Owing to unresolved swelling without an identifiable cause, he was referred to a quaternary center equipped with ear, nose, and throat (ENT) surgical services for comprehensive workup. Flexible nasendoscopy performed by the ENT team identified no major structural anomalies in the soft palate, vallecula, larynx, or post-cricoid region; however, the procedure was notable for increased secretions and induced coughing.

The on-call immunologist was consulted regarding possible angioedema, given the absence of any definitive structural or infectious etiology. Examination revealed diffuse facial erythema, including involvement of the nasolabial folds, as well as a red, swollen, but non-tender neck. The patient reported not taking any medications, specifically denying use of angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and had neither a personal nor family history of angioedema. The overall presentation did not fit classic patterns of either acquired or hereditary angioedema. Detailed physical assessment identified mild nailfold abnormalities consistent with periungual erythema, a rash on the right lateral aspect of the thigh, and decreased strength (4/5) in the hip flexors. No Gottron’s papules or heliotrope rash were observed. Creatine kinase was ordered and the following day returned markedly raised at 16,184 U/L. Renal function was preserved, and myoglobin testing in the urine was negative. Further laboratory evaluation revealed increased liver enzymes, with an ALT of 143 U/L, AST of 396 U/L, and LDH of 692 U/L.

Investigations:

Due to the suspicion of angioedema, multiple investigations were undertaken to identify possible etiologies of spontaneous angioedema. His complement components C3 and C4 were found to be within normal limits, as were both functional and quantitative measurements of C1 esterase.

Testing for autoimmunity revealed a positive anti-nuclear antibody (ANA), with a titre of 1:1280 exhibiting a speckled staining pattern. Anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA) testing returned negative results. A myositis antibody panel encompassing 16 antigens associated with autoimmune inflammatory myopathies was conducted using the Euroimmun Euroline immunoassay. This demonstrated a marked positive result for anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibodies, with a signal intensity of 173 (reference range <35). Results for other myositis antibodies, including anti-Mi-2 alpha (anti-Mi-2α), anti-Transcription Intermediary Factor-1 gamma (anti-TIF1-γ), anti–Histidyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-Jo-1), and anti–Isoleucyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-OJ), were negative. His immunoglobulin G (IgG) concentrations, including subclass evaluations, remained within the normal spectrum. Regarding infectious disease screening, he tested negative for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), hepatitis C core antibody (HBcAb), hepatitis B e antibody (HBeAb), hepatitis C virus (HCV), human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), varicella zoster virus (VZV) IgG, and cytomegalovirus (CMV) IgG and immunoglobulin M (IgM). QuantiFERON-TB Gold testing yielded an indeterminate result; however, three sputum samples tested for acid-fast bacilli indicative of Mycobacterium tuberculosis were negative.

Pulmonary function testing demonstrated an extrathoracic restrictive defect, with absent significant response to bronchodilator administration. Chest CT identified multiple non-cavitating nodules within the lungs, predominantly localized to the mid and lower zones, with the largest lesion measuring 15 mm in the right lower lobe. Patchy peribronchial consolidation, thought to reflect aspiration pneumonitis, was also observed, though there were no imaging findings suggestive of pulmonary fibrosis. Abdominal CT revealed no enlargement of abdominopelvic lymph nodes and showed no evidence for a primary or metastatic malignancy within the abdomen or pelvis. Video fluoroscopic (barium) swallowing assessment detected marked pharyngeal dysphagia, resulting in substantial retention within the pyriform fossae as well as post-swallow penetration of thin and mildly thick fluids to the level of the vocal cords.

Thigh MRI demonstrated findings indicative of active myositis affecting both the obturator internus and externus muscles, along with less pronounced involvement of the hip adductor group, bilateral gracilis, and both distal iliopsoas muscles (see figure 2). Evidence of ongoing muscle inflammation was corroborated by a fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) positron emission tomography (PET) scan, which revealed widespread, abnormal FDG uptake across several muscle groups, with particular intensity in the head and neck, both deltoids, forearm muscles, chest wall, abdominal region, and pelvis (refer to figure 3). There were no areas of metabolic activity to indicate the presence of malignancy. Levels of tumour markers, including carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), cancer antigen 19-9 (CA19-9), cancer antigen 125 (CA-125), and prostate-specific antigen (PSA), were within normal ranges.

Biopsy of thigh muscle identified patches containing moderate numbers of necrotic, vacuolated, degenerating, myophagocytosed, and regenerating myofibres arranged in a perifascicular pattern, as well as scattered perimysial infiltrates composed predominantly of dispersed histiocytes (see figure 4). Immunohistochemical staining for the membrane attack complex demonstrated complement component 5b-9 (C5b-9) present in the endomysial capillaries around perifascicular zones, consistent with a classic feature of dermatomyositis. The patient was ultimately diagnosed with moderate to severe SAE1 antibody–positive dermatomyositis, accompanied by inflammatory edema, peribronchial consolidation attributed to micro-aspiration, dysphagia, and myositis. Cutaneous findings included a positive Holster sign and erythema around the periungual regions.

Differential diagnosis:

Given the subacute onset of neck swelling accompanied by elevated CRP, initial diagnostic considerations included Ludwig angina and idiopathic angioedema. Early on, muscle weakness—such as dysphagia—was not a significant aspect of his clinical picture. The differential diagnosis encompassed various possibilities, including inflammatory myopathies (such as immune-mediated necrotising myopathy and inclusion body myositis), infectious etiologies (for instance, cellulitis/erysipelas involving the neck, deep neck space infections, and bacterial sialadenitis), disorders affecting the neuromuscular junction like myasthenia gravis, delayed drug hypersensitivity reactions (such as drug rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms), acute contact dermatitis, orofacial granulomatosis, hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis, and superior vena cava syndrome.

Treatment:

Given pronounced involvement of his airway musculature, the patient received intravenous methylprednisolone at 1000 mg per day for 3 days. This was followed by a tapering prednisone regimen starting at 60 mg daily, reduced by 5 mg every 2 to 3 weeks. Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) (Gamunex 10%) was administered as a loading dose of 2 g/kg (amounting to a total of 165 g) over 5 days (35 g per day), and an additional 5 courses were provided, each spaced 4 weeks apart, dividing a total dose of 2 g/kg (165 g) among these infusions. Mycophenolate mofetil was initiated at 1 g twice daily, and two intravenous rituximab doses (1000 mg each) were given 2 weeks apart. Methotrexate was avoided owing to its association with interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis, as the patient exhibited abnormal pulmonary function tests and, after discussion, the potential adverse effects were considered. Since mycophenolate was well tolerated, azathioprine and tacrolimus were not utilized. For prophylaxis against Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, the patient initially received sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim, but this was discontinued due to haemolytic anaemia unrelated to G6PD deficiency. He was then able to tolerate nebulized pentamidine at 300 mg every 4 weeks.

Following a 21-day hospital admission, he was discharged. Due to ongoing dysphagia, his oral medications were delivered via nasogastric tube at the time of discharge.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following a three-week hospital admission and therapeutic intervention, the patient exhibited moderate gains in hip flexor muscle strength and significant reduction of cervical soft tissue edema, though this swelling was not completely resolved at discharge. Oropharyngeal dysphagia persisted, requiring ongoing nasogastric tube support for feeding, which was maintained for an additional two months.

At a three-week post-discharge follow-up, full restoration of proximal muscle strength was observed, and the serum creatine kinase level had decreased to 37 U/L. At an immunology assessment two months after leaving hospital, there was continued improvement in neck swelling, with examination revealing only minimal residual violaceous facial rash and periungual alterations. The patient’s swallowing capacity had advanced sufficiently to permit discontinuation of the nasogastric tube and transition to a diet of soft solids. Ongoing management included mycophenolate mofetil, monthly intravenous immunoglobulin, and a gradually decreasing regimen of prednisone.

Three months after discharge, the patient’s proximal muscle strength remained stable, chest auscultation was normal, and cervical soft tissue swelling had fully subsided. Prednisone was discontinued successfully seven months post-discharge, by which time his dysphagia had completely resolved. Pulmonary function testing at follow-up demonstrated slight improvement, with a persistent restrictive pattern believed to represent his baseline physiology (see figure 5a, b). An 11-month chest CT scan showed almost complete resolution of previously noted basal ground-glass changes and airspace consolidation, findings that were initially considered due to aspiration. Twelve months after diagnosis, his respiratory function was unchanged, with good tolerance to exercise.

From an oncologic standpoint, the patient reported no constitutional symptoms during follow-up and will continue to be observed for malignancy through symptom assessment, physical examination, routine laboratory investigations, and periodic imaging, including sequential CT scans of the chest. He is scheduled to undergo both gastroscopy and colonoscopy to assess for hidden gastrointestinal or colorectal cancer.

It was decided to maintain IVIG therapy and gradually decrease the dose of mycophenolate over the next six months as his clinical status allows. He will remain under close surveillance to evaluate the need for any further adjustments to his medication regimen.

Discussion:

We describe a patient with dermatomyositis associated with anti-SAE1 antibodies who initially exhibited significant cervical soft tissue edema, which was first misdiagnosed as idiopathic angioedema. The diagnosis was ultimately made after considering dermatomyositis among the differentials and identifying faint cutaneous manifestations upon thorough physical assessment, enabling prompt initiation of appropriate therapy.

Anti-SAE1 antibodies are directed against the SUMO-activating enzyme, which shares structural homology with ubiquitin and is essential for ‘sumoylation’—a post-translational modification affecting various target proteins. Dermatomyositis with anti-SAE1 seropositivity frequently presents with characteristic skin findings, including heliotrope erythema, Gottron's papules, as well as the V-neck and shawl distribution rashes. In an extensive review involving 215 individuals, dysphagia and interstitial lung disease were observed in 46% and 43% of anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis cases, respectively, with malignancy being noted in 18% of patients.

Edema of the subcutaneous tissue, particularly presenting as swelling of the face or neck, is a rare clinical sign in dermatomyositis. Among 121 individuals included in a large cohort analysis, angioedema occurred in only 8 patients (6.61%). Additionally, a literature review covering the years 1985 to 2013 identified just 19 reported instances of dermatomyositis accompanied by edema. Angioedema was more frequently associated with antibodies such as anti-melanoma differentiation-associated gene (MDA)-5 and anti-transcriptional intermediary factor 1γ (TIF1-γ) compared to other autoantibodies. While several case reports noted facial edema, neck swelling was less commonly documented. In our review of 10 cases of SAE1-positive dermatomyositis, edema was referenced in only three cases, with just one case describing swelling in the throat area, underscoring the rarity of neck involvement.79

This case underscores the importance for emergency medicine providers, ENT specialists, immunologists, and rheumatologists to consider dermatomyositis as part of the differential diagnosis for neck swelling, particularly when faced with localized 'angioedema' that lacks clear cause or is idiopathic in nature. Since symptoms such as dysphagia and other manifestations can be mild, a thorough examination for physical indicators of dermatomyositis and measurement of creatine kinase are essential. The subcutaneous edema observed in this patient was secondary to muscle inflammation related to myositis, rather than a result of histaminergic or bradykinin-driven angioedema.

According to the International Guideline for Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathy-Associated Cancer Screening from the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS), this patient met two criteria for high risk (dermatomyositis, dysphagia) and two for intermediate risk (SAE1 antibody positive, male sex), classifying him as high risk for malignancy. He proceeded with cancer screening, including CT imaging, tumor marker evaluation, and a PET scan, with further plans for upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopy. Extensive initial and ongoing surveillance has thus far been reassuring.

The evidence supporting the best pharmacological management for anti-SAE1–positive dermatomyositis is scarce. The largest cohort published so far demonstrated diverse treatment strategies, with the majority of individuals receiving glucocorticoids alongside various steroid-sparing drugs. Methotrexate was most frequently chosen, though mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, and azathioprine were also utilized; intravenous immunoglobulin was administered in selected instances. In this case, therapy included steroids, mycophenolate mofetil, intravenous immunoglobulin, and rituximab, resulting in remission. The selection of mycophenolate mofetil was guided by the patient’s preference for an oral medication taken daily, its favorable tolerability profile, and its established efficacy in dermatomyositis. Encouragingly, the prognosis in this patient is favorable compared to others who exhibit marked tissue edema in the context of dermatomyositis.
