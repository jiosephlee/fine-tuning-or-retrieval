Title: Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook

Kevin Chan, Gemma Kwan, Hwei Choo Soh, Louise Evans

Abstract:

Summary: Dermatomyositis is an uncommon autoimmune condition primarily impacting the skin, muscles, and respiratory tract. Immediate initiation of aggressive immunosuppressive therapy is generally necessary to prevent further disease advancement and multi-organ involvement in affected individuals. In this report, a previously healthy man in his 40s presented to the emergency department with pronounced swelling of the neck. He was first evaluated by the otolaryngology team for a suspected deep neck space infection. After this etiology was excluded, the patient was referred to clinical immunology for assessment and management of presumed idiopathic angioedema. Given that the features of neck edema were not consistent with angioedema, as well as concomitant alterations in the skin and nails, a creatine kinase test was expeditiously ordered. The initial diagnosis of dermatomyositis was made; subsequent investigations identified a positive small ubiquitin-like modifier-1 activating enzyme (SAE1) autoantibody and characteristic findings on muscle biopsy. This report underscores the presentation of dermatomyositis with neck edema not attributable to histamine or bradykinin pathways, accompanied by dysphagia and substantial muscle involvement that warranted immediate therapeutic intervention.

Background:

Dermatomyositis is an idiopathic inflammatory myopathy that primarily targets the skin and muscles but may also involve the gastrointestinal tract, joints, lungs, and heart. Its incidence is estimated at 10 cases per million people. The condition demonstrates a bimodal age distribution, with peak occurrence among children between 5 and 14 years old, and adults from 45 to 64 years old. Typical skin manifestations include Gottron’s papules, the shawl sign, heliotrope rash, and periungual telangiectasias. Additional clinical features include proximal muscle weakness, interstitial lung disease, and dysphagia. Pseudoangioedema is rare in dermatomyositis; for instance, in one investigation, only 8 of 121 patients diagnosed with dermatomyositis were found to have pseudoangioedema. When this swelling involves the neck, it may mimic a primary otolaryngologic pathology or be misinterpreted as histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema.

Case presentation:

A man in his 40s came to the emergency department after experiencing gradually worsening neck swelling and proximal muscle weakness in his hip flexors for 3 weeks, along with mild muscle aches. He reported no past medical conditions and had never smoked. There was no history of using a 3-hydroxy-3-methyl-glutaryl-coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase inhibitor. The initial working diagnosis was Ludwig’s angina, and a CT scan of the neck revealed generalized soft tissue swelling of the neck, affecting the strap muscles, with inflammatory changes reaching the retropharyngeal space (figure 1). On assessment, he was not feverish, had an oxygen saturation of 99% breathing room air, and did not exhibit tachypnea. Nailfold capillaroscopy identified proximal nailfold microhemorrhages and avascular zones. Laboratory results showed a white cell count of 9.1×10^9/L and a C-reactive protein (CRP) level of 55.6 mg/L at presentation. Since the neck swelling persisted without an identifiable cause, he was referred to a quaternary care center with otolaryngology (ENT) surgical capabilities for advanced evaluation. Flexible nasendoscopy by the ENT team revealed no significant structural abnormalities in the soft palate, vallecula, larynx, or post-cricoid region, though the procedure was notable for increased secretions and frequent coughing.

The on-call immunologist was consulted due to concerns regarding angioedema, since no definitive structural or infectious etiology was identified. On assessment, the patient exhibited diffuse erythema across his face, including the nasolabial folds, as well as a reddened, edematous, but non-tender neck. He reported not taking any medications, specifically excluding angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and there was no history of angioedema in himself or his family. His presentation was considered atypical for both acquired and hereditary forms of angioedema. Comprehensive physical examination revealed mild periungual erythema indicative of nailfold changes, a cutaneous rash located on the right lateral thigh, and decreased hip flexor strength, measured at 4/5. There were no signs of Gottron’s papules or heliotrope eruption. Serum creatine kinase was ordered and, on the following day, found to be markedly elevated at 16,184 U/L. His renal function tests were within normal limits and urine myoglobin was negative. Additionally, laboratory studies revealed raised transaminase levels: alanine aminotransferase (ALT) at 143 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) at 396 U/L, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) at 692 U/L.

Investigations:

Due to the suspicion of angioedema, a series of investigations was undertaken to identify any underlying etiology for spontaneous angioedema. Levels of C3 and C4 complement were found to be within normal parameters, as were both the functional activity and concentration of C1 esterase.

Testing revealed an anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) titer of 1:1280 with a speckled pattern, while anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA) was not detected. A myositis autoantibody panel targeting 16 autoantigens associated with autoimmune inflammatory myopathies was administered using the Euroimmun Euroline immunoassay. This panel showed a marked positivity for anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibodies with a signal intensity of 173 (reference <35), while other myositis-specific antibodies—anti-Mi-2α, anti-TIF1-γ, anti-Jo-1, and anti-OJ—were all negative. Immunoglobulin G (IgG), including all subclasses, was within the normal range.

An immunosuppression screen demonstrated negativity for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), hepatitis C core antibody (HBcAb), hepatitis B e antibody (HBeAb), hepatitis C virus (HCV), HIV, varicella zoster virus (VZV) IgG, and cytomegalovirus (CMV) IgG/IgM. Although his QuantiFERON-TB Gold result was indeterminate, acid-fast bacilli for Mycobacterium tuberculosis from three separate sputum collections were negative.

Pulmonary function testing demonstrated an extrathoracic restrictive deficit without appreciable bronchodilator responsiveness. Chest CT imaging identified several non-cavitating pulmonary nodules predominantly distributed in the mid and lower lung zones, the largest being 15 mm in diameter in the right lower lobe. There were patchy peribronchial consolidations, which were considered likely secondary to aspiration pneumonitis, but no imaging signs of pulmonary fibrosis were observed. Abdominal CT did not reveal abdomino-pelvic lymphadenopathy nor signs of primary or metastatic malignancy within the abdomen or pelvis. Video fluoroscopic (barium) swallow study indicated marked pharyngeal dysphagia, resulting in significant pyriform fossae residue as well as post-swallow penetration of thin and mildly thick liquids onto the vocal cords.

Thigh MRI revealed findings indicative of ongoing myositis affecting both obturator internus and externus muscles, along with less severe inflammation in the hip adductors, gracilis muscles bilaterally, and distal iliopsoas muscles on both sides (see figure 2). Further evidence of active muscle inflammation was provided by fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)-positron emission tomography (PET), which demonstrated widespread and abnormal FDG uptake across various muscle groups, most notably in the head and neck, both deltoids, forearms, chest wall, abdominal region, and pelvis (refer to figure 3). No other areas showed FDG accumulation suggestive of malignancy. Levels of tumour markers—including carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), cancer antigen 19-9 (CA19-9), cancer antigen 125 (CA-125), and prostate-specific antigen (PSA)—were within normal range.

Muscle biopsy from the thigh identified areas with moderate numbers of necrotic, vacuolated, degenerating, myophagocytosed, and regenerating muscle fibres arranged in a perifascicular pattern, accompanied by scattered perimysial inflammation consisting mainly of dispersed histiocytes (see figure 4). Immunohistochemical staining for membrane attack complex revealed complement component 5b-9 (C5b-9) deposits in perifascicular endomysial capillaries, characteristic of dermatomyositis. The diagnosis established was moderate to severe SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis, presenting with inflammatory edema, peribronchial consolidation attributed to micro-aspiration, dysphagia, and myositis. Skin involvement featured a positive Holster sign and periungual erythema.

Differential diagnosis:

Because of his subacute onset of neck swelling accompanied by an elevated CRP, Ludwig angina and idiopathic angioedema were initially considered. Early on, muscle weakness and dysphagia were not significant components of his clinical presentation. The differential diagnosis also encompassed various inflammatory myopathies (such as immune-mediated necrotising myopathy and inclusion body myositis), infectious etiologies (including cellulitis or erysipelas localized to the neck, deep neck space abscesses, and bacterial sialadenitis), neuromuscular junction pathologies like myasthenia gravis, delayed hypersensitivity to medications (for example, drug rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms), acute contact dermatitis, orofacial granulomatosis, hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis, and superior vena cava syndrome.

Treatment:

Given the extensive involvement of airway musculature, the patient received intravenous methylprednisolone for 3 days at a dosage of 1000 mg per day. This was subsequently transitioned to a prednisone taper, initiated at 60 mg daily and decreased by 5 mg every 2 to 3 weeks. Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG, Gamunex 10%) was administered with an initial loading dose of 2 g/kg (total 165 g) divided over 5 days (35 g per day). This was followed by an additional 5 cycles, spaced 4 weeks apart, at a cumulative dose of 2 g/kg (165 g) distributed over these sessions. The patient was started on mycophenolate mofetil, 1 g twice daily, and received two doses of rituximab, 1000 mg intravenously, administered 2 weeks apart. Methotrexate was avoided owing to its association with interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis, especially given the patient's abnormal pulmonary function and after consultation regarding potential side effects. Neither azathioprine nor tacrolimus was prescribed since mycophenolate proved to be well tolerated. For prophylaxis against Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim was initially started, but discontinued following the onset of haemolytic anaemia (with no evidence of glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency). Nebulised pentamidine, 300 mg every 4 weeks, was subsequently well tolerated.

The patient was discharged after a 21-day hospital admission. Due to ongoing dysphagia at discharge, oral medications were provided via nasogastric tube.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following three weeks of inpatient care, the patient exhibited moderate recovery of hip flexor strength and substantial reduction in cervical soft tissue swelling, although some swelling persisted at discharge. Dysphagia remained problematic, leading to continued reliance on nasogastric tube feeding for an additional two months.

At three weeks post-discharge, proximal muscle strength had returned to normal and serum creatine kinase was 37 U/L. During an immunology assessment two months after leaving the hospital, cervical swelling was still resolving, and examination revealed only minor residual violaceous facial erythema and periungual abnormalities. Swallowing abilities had progressed to the extent that the nasogastric tube was removed and the patient could tolerate soft solids. Ongoing treatment included mycophenolate mofetil, monthly intravenous immunoglobulin infusions, and a reducing dose of prednisone.

Three months after discharge, the patient’s proximal muscle strength remained unchanged, chest examination findings were normal, and the cervical soft tissue edema had entirely subsided. Prednisone was able to be stopped successfully at seven months post-discharge, coinciding with complete resolution of dysphagia. Subsequent pulmonary function testing revealed slight improvement, although a residual restrictive defect persisted, which was considered consistent with his baseline physiology (see figure 5a, b). At eleven months, follow-up CT imaging of the chest showed almost total resolution of basal ground-glass opacities and airspace consolidation, which had previously been linked to aspiration. One year after diagnosis, his respiratory status was stable with preserved exercise capacity.

With regard to malignancy screening, the patient reported no constitutional symptoms at follow-up and will continue to undergo surveillance including symptom assessment, physical examination, routine blood work, and serial chest CT scans. Additionally, he is scheduled for gastroscopy and colonoscopy to rule out hidden gastrointestinal or colorectal cancer.

The management plan involves ongoing intravenous immunoglobulin therapy and gradual reduction of the mycophenolate dose over the next six months based on clinical response. He will be closely observed to evaluate whether adjustments to his medication regimen are warranted.

Discussion:

We describe a patient with dermatomyositis positive for anti-SAE1 antibodies who initially presented with marked swelling of cervical soft tissues, which was first considered to be idiopathic angioedema. Identifying dermatomyositis as a possible diagnosis and performing a meticulous physical examination that noted discrete dermatologic findings enabled prompt diagnosis and initiation of appropriate therapy.

Anti-SAE1 antibodies target the small ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) activating enzyme, a molecule related structurally to ubiquitin and responsible for 'sumoylation', a post-translational modification of multiple proteins. The clinical phenotype of SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis often includes dermatologic manifestations such as heliotrope rash, Gottron's papules, the V-neck sign, and the shawl sign. A comprehensive literature analysis involving 215 cases found dysphagia in 46% and interstitial lung disease in 43% of those with SAE1 dermatomyositis, while 18% developed malignancy.

Swelling of the subcutaneous tissue, particularly presenting as facial or neck oedema, rarely occurs in dermatomyositis. Among 121 individuals with dermatomyositis observed in a large cohort study, angioedema was identified in just 8 patients (6.61%). A review of publications from 1985 through 2013 reported only 19 instances in which dermatomyositis was accompanied by oedema. Certain antibodies, notably anti-melanoma differentiation-associated gene (MDA)-5 antibody and anti-transcriptional intermediary factor 1γ (TIF1-γ) antibody, showed a higher association with angioedema than other antibody types. Although facial swelling has been detailed in several reports, neck oedema was cited less often. In our review of 10 cases of SAE1-positive dermatomyositis, oedema was documented in only three cases; among these, only one referenced swelling involving the throat area, indicating that neck region involvement is rare.79

This case highlights to emergency medicine clinicians, otolaryngologists, immunologists, and rheumatologists that dermatomyositis should be considered in the differential diagnosis of neck swelling, particularly in cases of localized ‘angioedema’ that lack a clear etiology or are deemed idiopathic. Symptoms such as dysphagia and other manifestations may be mild or difficult to detect, making it essential to look for clinical signs of dermatomyositis and to obtain a creatine kinase measurement. In this patient, the subcutaneous edema was attributable to muscle inflammation associated with myositis, rather than being caused by histamine or bradykinin-related angioedema.

According to the International Guideline for Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathy-Associated Cancer Screening, published by the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS), this patient had two factors denoting high risk (dermatomyositis and dysphagia), and two intermediate risk factors (SAE1 antibody positivity and male gender), resulting in a classification of high risk for malignancy. Malignancy assessment included computed tomography imaging, tumour marker analyses, and a positron emission tomography scan, with further plans for both upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopic evaluation. Thorough baseline and subsequent screening have remained reassuring up to the present time.

The evidence base for determining the best pharmacological management of anti-SAE1–positive dermatomyositis remains sparse. The largest cohort reported so far demonstrates varied therapeutic strategies, with the majority of patients receiving glucocorticoids alongside different steroid-sparing medications—methotrexate was the most frequently utilized, while mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, and azathioprine were also commonly employed; intravenous immunoglobulin was administered in particular instances. This patient was managed with a combination of steroids, mycophenolate mofetil, intravenous immunoglobulin, and rituximab, resulting in disease remission. The choice of mycophenolate was informed by the patient's preference for a daily oral medication, its favourable tolerability profile, and its established application in dermatomyositis. Compared with other patients presenting with extensive tissue oedema secondary to dermatomyositis, this patient's outlook is comparatively favorable.
