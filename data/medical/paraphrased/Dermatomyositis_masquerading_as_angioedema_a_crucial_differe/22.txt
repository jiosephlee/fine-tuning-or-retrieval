Title: Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook

Kevin Chan, Gemma Kwan, Hwei Choo Soh, Louise Evans

Abstract:

Summary: Dermatomyositis is an uncommon autoimmune condition that predominantly involves the skin, musculature, and respiratory tract. Rapid initiation of aggressive immunosuppressive therapy is generally necessary to curtail disease advancement and prevent damage across multiple organs. A previously healthy man in his 40s presented to the emergency department with pronounced neck swelling. He was initially assessed by otolaryngologists for a suspected deep neck space infection. Once this diagnosis was excluded, he was referred to clinical immunology for further evaluation and management due to suspected idiopathic angioedema. A creatine kinase measurement was ordered promptly given that the neck edema was unusual and did not fit the profile of angioedema, in addition to observed skin and nail abnormalities. The patient was provisionally diagnosed with dermatomyositis, which was later confirmed by detection of small ubiquitin-like modifier-1 activating enzyme (SAE1) autoantibody and characteristic findings on muscle biopsy. This report underscores the atypical occurrence of neck swelling unrelated to histamine or bradykinin, representing a rare initial manifestation of dermatomyositis, presenting with dysphagia and extensive muscle involvement, thereby necessitating urgent therapeutic intervention.

Background:

Dermatomyositis is a form of idiopathic inflammatory myopathy that primarily impacts the skin and skeletal muscles, but may also involve the lungs, heart, joints, and gastrointestinal tract. The disorder has an estimated occurrence of 10 cases per million people. Onset occurs in a bimodal pattern, with incidence peaks among children aged 5–14 years and adults aged 45–64 years. Typical skin findings include Gottron’s papules, heliotrope rash, periungual telangiectasias, and the shawl sign. Additional manifestations can comprise proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, and interstitial lung disease. Pseudoangioedema is a rare feature in dermatomyositis; a study reported its presence in only 8 of 121 patients diagnosed with the condition. When involving the neck, it can mimic disorders usually seen in otolaryngology, or be confused with angioedema triggered by histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated mechanisms.

Case presentation:

A man in his 40s arrived at the emergency department after experiencing progressive neck swelling and weakness of the hip flexor muscles over three weeks, as well as mild muscle pain. He reported no relevant past medical history and had never smoked. There was no history of taking 3-hydroxy-3-methyl-glutaryl-coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase inhibitors. At initial assessment, Ludwig’s angina was considered, and a computed tomography (CT) scan of the neck was performed, which revealed widespread edema in the strap muscles and inflammation that extended into the retropharyngeal space (figure 1). On examination, the patient was afebrile, maintained an oxygen saturation of 99% on room air, and did not exhibit tachypnea. Nailfold capillaroscopy demonstrated proximal nailfold microhemorrhages and areas lacking vascularity. Laboratory tests revealed a white blood cell count of 9.1×10^9/L and a C-reactive protein (CRP) of 55.6 mg/L at presentation. As the neck swelling persisted without a clear etiology, he was referred to a quaternary care facility with access to ear, nose, and throat (ENT) surgical services for advanced assessment. The ENT team performed a flexible nasendoscopy, which did not identify major structural abnormalities of the soft palate, vallecula, larynx, or post-cricoid region; however, they observed excessive secretions and the patient developed a cough during the procedure.

The case was referred to the on-call immunologist due to suspicion of angioedema, as structural or infectious etiologies were not identified. Upon assessment, the patient exhibited diffuse facial erythema, including involvement of the nasolabial folds, in addition to a swollen, erythematous neck that was not tender to palpation. He reported not using any medications such as angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and stated there was no history of angioedema in himself or his family. The presentation did not align with typical features of either hereditary or acquired angioedema. Detailed physical examination identified mild nailfold alterations consistent with periungual erythema, a rash on the right lateral aspect of the thigh, and decreased strength in the hip flexors with a power grading of 4/5. Both Gottron’s papules and heliotrope eruption were absent. Measurement of creatine kinase was performed and, on the following day, found to be markedly increased at 16,184 U/L. Renal parameters remained within normal limits, and urine was negative for myoglobin. Laboratory testing also showed elevated liver enzymes, with alanine aminotransferase (ALT) measured at 143 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) at 396 U/L, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) raised at 692 U/L.

Investigations:

Due to the preliminary suspicion of angioedema, multiple diagnostic studies were conducted to assess possible etiologies of spontaneous angioedema. Complement components C3 and C4 were within normal limits, and both the functional activity and quantitative levels of C1 esterase were also found to be normal.

Antinuclear antibody (ANA) testing yielded a positive result at a titre of 1:1280, exhibiting a speckled pattern. Testing for anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA) returned a negative result. A panel for myositis-related antibodies, targeting 16 antigens associated with autoimmune inflammatory myopathies and processed using the Euroimmun Euroline immunoassay, revealed a strong positivity for anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibodies, with a signal intensity of 173 (normal range <35). No reactivity was observed against other myositis-specific antibodies, including anti-Mi-2α, anti–Transcription Intermediary Factor-1 gamma (anti-TIF1-γ), anti–Histidyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-Jo-1), and anti–Isoleucyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-OJ) antibodies. Immunoglobulin G (IgG) levels and subclasses remained within reference ranges. Screening for immunosuppression showed negative results for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), hepatitis C core antibody (HBcAb), hepatitis B e antibody (HBeAb), hepatitis C virus (HCV), HIV, varicella zoster virus (VZV) IgG, and cytomegalovirus (CMV) IgG/IgM. The QuantiFERON-TB Gold assay was indeterminate, but testing for acid-fast bacilli of Mycobacterium tuberculosis in three separate sputum specimens returned negative.

Pulmonary function testing demonstrated an extrathoracic restrictive defect without a notable response to bronchodilators. Chest CT identified several non-cavitating nodules localized primarily to the mid and lower lung zones, the largest of which was 15 mm within the right lower lobe. There were also scattered peribronchial consolidations, likely consistent with aspiration pneumonitis, and no radiographic signs indicative of pulmonary fibrosis. Abdominal CT did not reveal any abdomino-pelvic lymphadenopathy or signs of primary or secondary malignancy in the abdomen or pelvis. Video fluoroscopic barium swallow study revealed marked pharyngeal dysphagia, resulting in substantial residual material within the pyriform fossae and entry of thin and mildly thick liquids past the vocal cords following the swallow.

Magnetic resonance imaging of the thighs revealed findings indicative of ongoing myositis affecting both the obturator internus and externus muscles, with less pronounced inflammatory changes seen in the hip adductors, gracilis muscles bilaterally, and both distal iliopsoas muscles (figure 2). Evidence of muscle inflammation was corroborated by a fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) positron emission tomography (PET) scan, which demonstrated diffuse, abnormal FDG uptake across multiple muscle groups, with maximal involvement noted in the head and neck region, bilateral deltoids, forearms, chest wall, abdomen, and pelvis (figure 3). No hypermetabolic lesions were detected elsewhere to raise suspicion for malignancy. Laboratory evaluation showed normal levels of tumour markers, including carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), cancer antigen 19-9 (CA19-9), cancer antigen 125 (CA-125), and prostate-specific antigen (PSA).

Histopathological examination of a thigh muscle biopsy identified focal areas containing moderate numbers of necrotic, vacuolated, degenerating, myophagocytosed, and regenerating myofibres arranged in a perifascicular distribution, with scattered inflammatory infiltrates composed primarily of dispersed histiocytes in the perimysium (figure 4). Immunohistochemical staining for the membrane attack complex revealed complement component 5b-9 (C5b-9) deposition within perifascicular endomysial capillaries, a characteristic finding in dermatomyositis. The overall presentation was consistent with moderate to severe dermatomyositis, confirmed as SAE1 antibody positive, accompanied by inflammatory edema, peribronchial consolidation attributed to micro-aspiration, dysphagia, and myositis. Dermatologic features included a positive Holster sign and periungual erythema.

Differential diagnosis:

Given his relatively recent onset of neck swelling accompanied by increased CRP, Ludwig angina and idiopathic angioedema were initially considered. Notably, muscle weakness—including difficulties swallowing—was absent during the early phase of his illness. The differential diagnosis also encompassed various inflammatory myopathies such as immune-mediated necrotising myopathy and inclusion body myositis, infectious etiologies including cellulitis or erysipelas of the neck, deep neck space infections, and bacterial sialadenitis, as well as neuromuscular junction disorders like myasthenia gravis. Other possibilities included delayed hypersensitivity to medications (for example, drug rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms), acute contact dermatitis, orofacial granulomatosis, hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis, and superior vena cava syndrome.

Treatment:

Given the marked involvement of his airway musculature, the patient received intravenous methylprednisolone for three days at a daily dose of 1000 mg. Subsequently, corticosteroid therapy was transitioned to oral prednisone, beginning at 60 mg daily with a gradual reduction of 5 mg every two to three weeks. For immunomodulation, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG, Gamunex 10%) was administered with an initial loading dose of 2 g/kg (totalling 165 g) distributed over five days (35 g per day), then supplemented with an additional five cycles given at four-week intervals, with a cumulative dose of 2 g/kg (165 g) apportioned across these treatments. Mycophenolate mofetil was initiated at a dosage of 1 g twice daily, and he also received two intravenous rituximab infusions of 1000 mg each, spaced two weeks apart. Methotrexate was avoided due to its potential association with interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis, especially in light of the patient’s abnormal pulmonary function tests; this decision was made in collaboration with the patient while discussing possible adverse effects. Since mycophenolate was well-tolerated, neither azathioprine nor tacrolimus was introduced. For Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia prophylaxis, the patient initially received sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim, but this was discontinued upon the onset of haemolytic anaemia in the absence of glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency. He subsequently tolerated nebulised pentamidine 300 mg every four weeks.

The patient was discharged following a 21-day hospitalization. At discharge, oral medications continued to be administered via nasogastric tube because of persistent dysphagia.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following three weeks of inpatient care and therapy, the patient exhibited a moderate recovery in hip flexor strength and significant reduction in cervical soft tissue edema, though some swelling persisted at discharge. However, dysphagia was ongoing, which required continued nasogastric feeding for an additional two months.

At a follow-up appointment three weeks post-discharge, proximal muscle power had completely returned to normal and serum creatine kinase had decreased to 37 U/L. During the immunology evaluation two months after leaving the hospital, cervical swelling showed further improvement, with examination revealing only residual violaceous erythema on the face and periungual alterations. The patient's swallowing function had progressed sufficiently for removal of the nasogastric tube and advancement to a diet of soft solids. The medication regimen at this time included ongoing mycophenolate mofetil, monthly intravenous immunoglobulin infusions, and prednisone with gradual dose reduction.

At three months following discharge, the patient’s proximal muscle strength remained unchanged, chest examination findings were normal, and the previously noted cervical soft tissue swelling had entirely subsided. Prednisone therapy was able to be discontinued at seven months after discharge, at which point dysphagia had completely resolved. Repeat pulmonary function testing indicated mild improvement, though a residual restrictive pattern persisted, considered to represent baseline status (figure 5a, b). Follow-up CT imaging of the chest at eleven months showed almost total resolution of the prior basal ground-glass opacities and airspace consolidation, which had initially been ascribed to aspiration. At the twelve-month mark post-diagnosis, pulmonary function remained stable and exercise capacity was maintained.

Regarding malignancy surveillance, the patient did not report any constitutional symptoms at follow-up and ongoing monitoring was planned to include symptom assessment, physical examination, routine laboratory tests, and serial chest CT scans. He is also scheduled for upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopy to rule out hidden gastrointestinal or colorectal cancers.

The management approach involves ongoing intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) therapy with a planned gradual reduction of the mycophenolate dosage over the next six months, depending on his clinical trajectory. The patient will be regularly reviewed to assess whether modifications to his medication regimen are necessary.

Discussion:

We describe a patient with dermatomyositis testing positive for anti-SAE1 antibodies, who initially presented with marked swelling of the cervical soft tissues that was first diagnosed as idiopathic angioedema. Identification of dermatomyositis in the differential, together with a thorough physical exam that detected subtle skin findings, enabled prompt diagnosis and initiation of therapy.

Anti-SAE1 antibodies are directed against the small-ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) activating enzyme, a molecule structurally analogous to ubiquitin and responsible for modifying various target proteins via sumoylation after translation. Dermatomyositis associated with SAE1 antibodies commonly shows skin manifestations such as heliotrope rash, Gottron’s papules, the V-neck sign, and the shawl sign. According to a large literature review of 215 cases, dysphagia was observed in 46%, interstitial lung disease in 43%, and malignancies in 18% of patients with SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis.

Edema of the subcutaneous tissues, particularly involving the face or neck, is a rare manifestation in dermatomyositis. Within a sizable study including 121 individuals diagnosed with dermatomyositis, angioedema was documented in just 8 cases (6.61%). A survey of the literature spanning 1985 to 2013 identified only 19 reports describing dermatomyositis presenting with edema. Specific antibodies, notably anti-melanoma differentiation-associated gene (MDA)-5 and anti-transcriptional intermediary factor 1γ (TIF1-γ), were observed to have a higher association with angioedema compared to other antibodies. Facial edema was commonly noted in these cases, whereas swelling in the neck region appeared less frequently. In a review of 10 cases of dermatomyositis with SAE1 positivity, only three reported edema, with only a single case referencing throat region swelling—underscoring the rarity of neck involvement.79

This case highlights the importance for emergency physicians, ENT specialists, immunologists, and rheumatologists to consider dermatomyositis as a potential cause of neck swelling, particularly when faced with unexplained or idiopathic localized 'angioedema'. Clinical manifestations such as dysphagia and other related symptoms may be mild, making it essential to evaluate for dermatomyositis-specific physical findings and to obtain a creatine kinase measurement. In the present patient, the subcutaneous oedema was attributable to underlying muscle inflammation associated with myositis, rather than being the result of histamine- or bradykinin-induced angioedema.

Following the International Guideline for Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathy-Associated Cancer Screening provided by the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS), this individual met criteria for two major risk factors (dermatomyositis, dysphagia) and two moderate risk factors (positive SAE1 antibody, male gender), thus being categorized as high risk for malignancy. He underwent cancer screening consisting of computed tomography imaging, tumour marker assays, and a PET scan, with future plans for both upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopies. Broad baseline evaluations and follow-up surveillance continue to provide reassuring results.

Data supporting optimal medical management for anti-SAE1–positive dermatomyositis are scarce. The largest available cohort describes a variety of treatment regimens, with the majority of individuals receiving glucocorticoids in conjunction with steroid-sparing medications—methotrexate being the most prevalent, but also mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, and azathioprine; intravenous immunoglobulin was administered selectively. In this case, the patient received steroids, mycophenolate mofetil, intravenous immunoglobulin, and rituximab, resulting in remission. The choice of mycophenolate mofetil was informed by the patient’s preference for an oral daily therapy, as well as its favorable tolerability profile and established use in dermatomyositis. Compared to other cases exhibiting substantial tissue oedema in dermatomyositis, our patient’s prognosis is notably positive.
