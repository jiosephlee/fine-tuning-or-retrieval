Title: Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook

Kevin Chan, Gemma Kwan, Hwei Choo Soh, Louise Evans

Abstract:

Summary: Dermatomyositis is an uncommon autoimmune condition that commonly impacts the skin, muscles, and respiratory tract. Immediate initiation of aggressive immunosuppressive therapy is generally necessary for individuals diagnosed with dermatomyositis in order to halt disease advancement and prevent damage to multiple organs. In this report, a previously healthy man in his 40s first arrived at the emergency department with marked neck swelling. An initial assessment by the ear, nose, and throat (ENT) surgery team was performed to evaluate for a likely deep neck space infection. Following exclusion of this diagnosis, the patient was referred to clinical immunology for further assessment and management of a suspected case of idiopathic angioedema. In light of the atypical appearance of the cervical edema, which did not fit with angioedema, along with associated alterations in the skin and nailbeds, a creatine kinase measurement was ordered without delay. A working diagnosis of dermatomyositis was established, and further evaluation identified positivity for small ubiquitin-like modifier-1 activating enzyme (SAE1) autoantibody and a muscle biopsy with characteristic findings. This case draws attention to neck edema not mediated by histamine or bradykinin as a rare manifestation of dermatomyositis presenting with dysphagia and substantial muscular involvement that necessitated urgent therapy.

Background:

Dermatomyositis is an idiopathic inflammatory myopathy that primarily targets the skin and skeletal muscles but may also involve organs such as the lungs, heart, joints, and gastrointestinal tract. The condition has an estimated prevalence of 10 cases per one million individuals. Its age of onset demonstrates a bimodal pattern, with a higher frequency observed in children between 5 and 14 years old, as well as in adults from 45 to 64 years old. Typical dermatologic manifestations include Gottron’s papules, shawl sign, heliotrope rash, and periungual telangiectasias. Additional clinical features comprise proximal muscle weakness, interstitial lung disease, and difficulty swallowing. Pseudoangioedema is a rare occurrence in dermatomyositis; one report identified its presence in only 8 out of 121 patients with the disease. When present in the neck, it can mimic a primary otolaryngologic condition or be confused with histamine- or bradykinin-induced angioedema.

Case presentation:

A man in his 40s arrived at the emergency department reporting three weeks of gradually worsening neck swelling and proximal muscle weakness in his hip flexors, also experiencing mild muscle aches. He had no notable medical history and had never smoked. He had not been prescribed any 3-hydroxy-3-methyl-glutaryl-coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase inhibitors. At presentation, Ludwig’s angina was considered, and a neck CT scan was conducted, revealing widespread neck edema affecting the strap muscles, with inflammation spreading into the retropharyngeal space (figure 1). He was not febrile, maintained oxygen saturation at 99% on ambient air, and exhibited no tachypnea. Nailfold capillaroscopy identified proximal microhaemorrhages and avascular regions. His initial laboratory results showed a white blood cell count of 9.1×10^9/L and C-reactive protein (CRP) of 55.6 mg/L. Given the ongoing neck swelling without a clear etiology, he was transferred to a quaternary care hospital offering ear, nose and throat (ENT) surgical expertise for further assessment. ENT performed a flexible nasendoscopy, which found no notable structural abnormalities in the soft palate, vallecula, larynx, or post-cricoid area, but the patient exhibited increased secretions and cough during the procedure.

The case was reviewed with the on-call immunologist due to concern for angioedema, as neither a structural nor infectious etiology was identified. Examination revealed diffuse facial erythema, extending over the nasolabial folds, as well as an erythematous, edematous, but non-tender neck. The patient reported no current medication use, specifically excluding angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and denied both personal and family history of angioedema. His clinical features were not typical for either acquired or hereditary angioedema. Comprehensive physical assessment revealed mild nailfold abnormalities indicative of periungual erythema, a rash on the right lateral thigh, and diminished hip flexor strength rated at 4/5. Gottron’s papules and heliotrope rash were not present. A creatine kinase measurement was ordered and found the following day to be markedly elevated at 16,184 U/L. Renal function was within normal parameters and urine myoglobin was negative. Laboratory evaluation also showed elevated transaminases, with alanine aminotransferase (ALT) at 143 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) at 396 U/L, and increased lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) at 692 U/L.

Investigations:

Given the suspicion for angioedema, a series of investigations were carried out to identify potential causes of spontaneous angioedema. The complement levels for C3 and C4, along with both the function and quantification of C1 esterase, fell within normal parameters.

Testing revealed a positive anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) with a titer of 1:1280, displaying a speckled pattern. Anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA) was negative. A comprehensive myositis antibody panel, comprising 16 antigens associated with autoimmune inflammatory myopathy, was conducted via the Euroimmun Euroline immunoassay. The results showed a pronounced positivity for anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibodies, with a signal intensity of 173 (reference range <35). Other myositis-specific antibodies—including anti-Mi-2α, anti-Transcription Intermediary Factor-1 gamma (anti-TIF1-γ), anti–Histidyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-Jo-1), and anti–Isoleucyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-OJ)—were not detected. Immunoglobulin G (IgG), along with its subclasses, was within normal limits. Screening for immunosuppressive-related infections revealed negative results for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), hepatitis C core antibody (HBcAb), hepatitis B e antibody (HBeAb), hepatitis C virus (HCV), HIV, varicella zoster virus (VZV) IgG, and cytomegalovirus (CMV) IgG and IgM. QuantiFERON-TB Gold assay yielded an indeterminate outcome; however, acid-fast bacilli testing for Mycobacterium tuberculosis on three separate sputum samples was negative.

Pulmonary function testing demonstrated a restrictive pattern located outside the thoracic cavity, and there was no appreciable improvement following bronchodilator administration. Chest CT identified numerous pulmonary nodules lacking cavitation, predominantly situated from the mid to lower zones, with the largest being 15 mm in diameter within the right lower lobe. There were also scattered peribronchial consolidations, likely attributable to aspiration pneumonitis, without any CT signs indicating pulmonary fibrosis. Abdominal CT revealed no abdomino-pelvic lymphadenopathy, nor was there any evidence of primary or secondary malignancy within the abdomen or pelvis. Video fluoroscopic (barium) swallow evaluation indicated marked pharyngeal dysphagia, resulting in substantial residue in the pyriform fossae as well as post-swallow penetration of thin and mildly thick liquids down to the vocal cords.

Thigh MRI revealed findings indicative of ongoing myositis within both obturator internus and externus muscles, as well as less severe involvement affecting the hip adductors, bilateral gracilis muscles, and the distal portions of both iliopsoas muscles (figure 2). Additional evidence of active muscle inflammation was demonstrated on fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)-positron emission tomography (PET), which identified diffuse and abnormal FDG uptake across several muscle groups, with the most pronounced signals localized to the head and neck, both deltoids, forearms, chest wall, abdomen, and pelvis (figure 3). No other areas demonstrated metabolically active disease to suggest a neoplasm. Tumour markers, including carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), cancer antigen 19-9 (CA19-9), cancer antigen 125 (CA-125), and prostate-specific antigen (PSA), were within normal limits.

Biopsy of thigh muscle demonstrated patches containing moderate numbers of necrotic, vacuolated, degenerating, myophagocytosed, and regenerating muscle fibers arranged in a perifascicular pattern, with scattered perimysial inflammatory cells chiefly comprised of dispersed histiocytes (figure 4). Membrane attack complex immunohistochemistry revealed complement component 5b-9 (C5b-9) deposition in the perifascicular endomysial capillaries, a hallmark of dermatomyositis. The final diagnosis was moderate to severe SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis, accompanied by inflammatory edema, peribronchial consolidation attributed to micro-aspiration, dysphagia, and myositis. Skin findings included a positive Holster sign and periungual erythema.

Differential diagnosis:

Due to the subacute onset of neck swelling in conjunction with increased CRP levels, initial considerations included Ludwig angina as well as idiopathic angioedema. In the early course, muscle weakness and dysphagia were not significant features. The differential diagnosis extended to other inflammatory myopathies (such as immune-mediated necrotising myopathy and inclusion body myositis), infectious etiologies (for example, cellulitis or erysipelas of the neck, deep neck space infections, bacterial sialadenitis), disorders of the neuromuscular junction like myasthenia gravis, delayed hypersensitivity to medications (including drug rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms), acute contact dermatitis, orofacial granulomatosis, hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis, and superior vena cava syndrome.

Treatment:

Given substantial involvement of the airway musculature, the patient received intravenous methylprednisolone at 1000 mg daily for 3 days. This was subsequently transitioned to a tapering schedule of oral prednisone, starting at 60 mg daily and reduced by 5 mg increments every 2 to 3 weeks. A loading course of intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) (Gamunex 10%) was administered at 2 g/kg (totaling 165 g) over 5 days (35 g per day), followed by 5 additional cycles every 4 weeks, with the cumulative dose of 2 g/kg (165 g) divided among these infusions. Initiation of mycophenolate mofetil at 1 g twice daily was undertaken, in addition to two intravenous rituximab infusions at 1000 mg, administered two weeks apart. Methotrexate was withheld, owing to its known link with interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis—given the patient’s abnormal lung function testing—and following shared decision-making with the patient that considered its adverse effect profile. Mycophenolate was preferred over azathioprine and tacrolimus, as it was well-tolerated. To prevent Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, initial prophylaxis with sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim was started; however, this was discontinued after the patient developed haemolytic anaemia, despite an absence of glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency. Thereafter, he tolerated monthly nebulized pentamidine at 300 mg. 

After a 21-day hospitalization, the patient was discharged. Due to ongoing dysphagia, all oral medications were administered via nasogastric tube at the time of discharge.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following a three-week inpatient stay with therapy, the patient exhibited moderate recovery in hip flexor strength and significant reduction in cervical soft tissue swelling; however, the swelling had not completely resolved upon discharge. Dysphagia was ongoing, requiring maintenance of nasogastric feeding for an additional two months.

By three weeks post-discharge, proximal muscle strength had returned to normal and serum creatine kinase decreased to 37 U/L. At the two-month immunology follow-up, further improvement in cervical swelling was observed, with examination revealing only minimal violaceous erythema on the face and periungual changes. The patient's swallowing ability progressed, permitting removal of the nasogastric tube and advancement to a diet of soft solids. The medication regimen included continued use of mycophenolate mofetil, monthly intravenous immunoglobulin, and a gradually reducing prednisone dose.

At three months after hospital discharge, the patient’s proximal muscle strength remained unchanged, physical exam of the chest was normal, and the previously noted cervical soft tissue swelling had fully subsided. Prednisone was tapered off successfully seven months after discharge, at which point his dysphagia had entirely resolved. Repeat pulmonary function testing revealed slight improvement, though a residual restrictive pattern persisted, believed to correspond to his baseline physiology (see figure 5a, b). An 11-month follow-up chest CT showed almost complete resolution of the ground-glass opacities and airspace consolidation that were initially ascribed to aspiration. At the one-year mark post-diagnosis, respiratory status was stable, and exercise tolerance was good.

Regarding cancer surveillance, the patient continued to report no constitutional symptoms at follow-up, and surveillance will proceed with periodic symptom assessment, physical exams, routine blood work, and appropriate imaging, including serial chest CT scans. Additionally, he is scheduled for gastroscopy and colonoscopy to rule out hidden gastrointestinal or colorectal cancers.

The treatment plan includes ongoing IVIG administration and a gradual reduction of mycophenolate dosage over the next six months based on his clinical course. Close monitoring will be maintained to assess the necessity for any further alterations in his medication regimen.

Discussion:

We describe a patient with dermatomyositis positive for anti-SAE1 antibodies who presented with marked swelling of the cervical soft tissues, which was initially misdiagnosed as idiopathic angioedema. The eventual identification of dermatomyositis as a possible cause, supported by a meticulous physical examination that identified subtle skin findings, facilitated prompt diagnosis and initiation of therapy.

Anti-SAE1 antibodies are directed against the small-ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) activating enzyme, a protein with structural similarity to ubiquitin that participates in the post-translational modification process known as sumoylation. The dermatomyositis variant associated with SAE1 antibodies generally displays cutaneous manifestations including heliotrope rash, Gottron’s papules, the V-neck sign, and the shawl sign. In a comprehensive review of the literature involving 215 cases, dysphagia and interstitial lung disease were seen in 46% and 43% of SAE1 dermatomyositis patients, respectively, while 18% developed malignancy.

Subcutaneous edema, particularly involving the face or neck, is rarely observed in dermatomyositis. In a substantial study encompassing 121 individuals diagnosed with dermatomyositis, angioedema was identified in just 8 cases (6.61%). An assessment of published reports between 1985 and 2013 revealed only 19 instances in which dermatomyositis presented with oedema. Angioedema has been reported more frequently in association with antibodies such as anti-melanoma differentiation-associated gene (MDA)-5 and anti-transcriptional intermediary factor 1γ (TIF1-γ), compared to other autoantibodies. While facial edema was documented in several cases, edema specifically affecting the neck was noted less often. In our review of 10 SAE1-positive dermatomyositis cases, oedema was mentioned in three, and only one described swelling in the throat area, illustrating the rarity of neck involvement.79

This report highlights for emergency physicians, ENT specialists, immunologists, and rheumatologists that dermatomyositis should be considered as a potential cause of neck swelling, particularly when localized ‘angioedema’ lacks a clear etiology or is deemed idiopathic. Because manifestations such as dysphagia and other clinical features can be subtle, careful evaluation for dermatomyositis signs and measurement of creatine kinase are essential. The patient’s subcutaneous edema originated from muscle inflammation due to myositis, and was not attributable to angioedema mediated by histamine or bradykinin pathways.

Following the International Guideline for Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathy-Associated Cancer Screening issued by the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS), the patient was determined to be at high risk for malignancy, given two high-risk indicators (dermatomyositis and dysphagia) along with two intermediate-risk factors (positive SAE1 antibody and male gender). Malignancy work-up included computed tomography imaging, assessment of tumor markers, and positron emission tomography (PET) scanning, with planned upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopic evaluations. Thorough initial and periodic surveillance has so far provided reassuring results.

The available data on the best pharmacologic management for anti-SAE1–positive dermatomyositis are sparse. In the largest series reported thus far, treatment modalities varied, with the majority of patients receiving glucocorticoids alongside various steroid-sparing medications—most frequently methotrexate, though mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, and azathioprine were also employed; intravenous immunoglobulin was reserved for selected instances. The patient in this case was administered steroids, mycophenolate mofetil, intravenous immunoglobulin, and rituximab, resulting in remission. Mycophenolate mofetil was chosen due to the patient's preference for a daily oral therapy, its favorable tolerability profile, and established efficacy in dermatomyositis. Compared with others who develop pronounced tissue edema secondary to dermatomyositis, this patient’s prognosis is favorable.
