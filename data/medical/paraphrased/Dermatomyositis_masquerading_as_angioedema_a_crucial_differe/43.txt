Title: Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook

Kevin Chan, Gemma Kwan, Hwei Choo Soh, Louise Evans

Abstract:

Summary: Dermatomyositis is an uncommon autoimmune disorder primarily involving the skin, musculature, and respiratory tract. Management typically necessitates immediate initiation of potent immunosuppressive therapy to curtail disease advancement and prevent damage to multiple organs. A male patient in his 40s with no prior significant medical history presented to the emergency department experiencing marked swelling of the neck. Initial assessment by otolaryngology considered a deep neck space infection, but this diagnosis was subsequently excluded. He was then referred to clinical immunology for evaluation and management of presumed idiopathic angioedema. Due to the atypical presentation of neck oedema, which did not align with classic angioedema and was accompanied by cutaneous and nail findings, a creatine kinase assay was rapidly performed. The provisional diagnosis of dermatomyositis was made, later substantiated by detection of the small ubiquitin-like modifier-1 activating enzyme (SAE1) autoantibody and findings on muscle biopsy characteristic of the disease. This report underscores that neck swelling unrelated to histamine or bradykinin mechanisms can represent a rare presentation of dermatomyositis, associated with dysphagia and pronounced muscular involvement that requires urgent therapy.

Background:

Dermatomyositis is an inflammatory myopathy of unknown cause, primarily involving the skin and skeletal muscles, though it may also involve organs such as the gastrointestinal tract, lungs, heart, and joints. Its estimated incidence is approximately 10 cases per million individuals. The disease demonstrates a bimodal age distribution, presenting most frequently in children between 5 and 14 years old and adults from 45 to 64 years old. Key dermatologic signs include Gottron’s papules, the heliotrope rash, shawl sign, and periungual telangiectasias. Additional clinical features comprise proximal muscle weakness, interstitial lung disease, and dysphagia. Pseudoangioedema occurs rarely in dermatomyositis; for instance, one series identified this finding in only 8 out of 121 dermatomyositis patients. When present in the cervical region, pseudoangioedema may mimic otolaryngologic conditions or be misdiagnosed as angioedema related to histamine or bradykinin.

Case presentation:

A man in his 40s arrived at the emergency department reporting a 3-week progression of neck swelling and proximal muscle weakness affecting his hip flexors, also describing mild muscle pain. He had no notable medical background and had never smoked. He was not on any HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors. Ludwig’s angina was considered initially, and a cervical CT scan revealed widespread neck edema involving the strap muscles, with inflammatory changes reaching the retropharyngeal space (figure 1). He showed no fever, maintained an oxygen saturation of 99% while breathing room air, and was not tachypneic. Nailfold capillaroscopy identified microhaemorrhages and avascular zones around his proximal nailfolds. Laboratory tests at presentation showed a white blood cell count of 9.1×10^9/L and a C-reactive protein (CRP) of 55.6 mg/L. Because the neck swelling persisted without a clear origin, he was moved to a quaternary care center with ENT surgical capabilities for further assessment. ENT performed a flexible nasendoscopy, which demonstrated no major structural irregularities in the soft palate, vallecula, larynx, or post-cricoid region, but the procedure was notable for increased secretions and coughing.

Consultation with the on-call immunologist was initiated due to suspected angioedema, given the absence of identifiable structural or infectious etiology. Upon assessment, the patient exhibited diffuse facial erythema, including involvement of the nasolabial folds, as well as a reddened, edematous, but non-tender neck. He reported no current medications, specifically denying use of angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and he did not have a personal or familial history of angioedema. His presentation was not typical for either acquired or hereditary angioedema. Comprehensive examination identified subtle nailfold abnormalities compatible with periungual erythema, in addition to a cutaneous rash on the right lateral thigh and diminished strength of the hip flexors, graded as 4/5. Notably, Gottron’s papules and a heliotrope eruption were not observed. Creatine kinase was ordered and returned the following day markedly increased at 16,184 U/L. Renal function remained within normal parameters and a urine myoglobin test was negative. The patient also demonstrated raised transaminase levels, including alanine aminotransferase (ALT) at 143 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) at 396 U/L, and an elevated lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) at 692 U/L.

Investigations:

Given the initial suspicion of angioedema, a series of investigations were carried out to identify potential etiologies for spontaneous angioedema. The results showed that both C3 and C4 complement levels were within normal limits, as were C1 esterase activity and quantity.

Testing revealed a positive anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) at a titre of 1:1280 with a speckled pattern. The anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA) was negative. A myositis antibody panel, examining 16 antigens associated with autoimmune inflammatory myopathies via the Euroimmun Euroline immunoassay, demonstrated a strong positivity for anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibodies, with a signal intensity of 173 (reference range <35). Other myositis-specific antibodies tested, including anti-Mi-2 alpha (anti-Mi-2α), anti–Transcription Intermediary Factor-1 gamma (anti-TIF1-γ), anti–Histidyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-Jo-1), and anti–Isoleucyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-OJ), were negative. Immunoglobulin G (IgG), including its subclasses, remained within the normal range. Screening for immunosuppression showed negative results for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), hepatitis C core antibody (HBcAb), hepatitis B e antibody (HBeAb), hepatitis C virus (HCV), HIV, varicella zoster virus (VZV) IgG, and cytomegalovirus (CMV) IgG/IgM. The QuantiFERON-TB Gold test was indeterminate, but three sputum samples tested negative for acid-fast bacilli for Mycobacterium tuberculosis.

Pulmonary function testing demonstrated an extrathoracic restrictive defect without appreciable improvement following bronchodilator administration. Chest computed tomography identified several non-cavitating pulmonary nodules concentrated in the middle to lower lung fields, the largest of which was 15 mm located in the right lower lobe, along with irregular peribronchial consolidation suggestive of aspiration pneumonitis; however, there was no imaging evidence consistent with pulmonary fibrosis. Abdominal CT revealed neither abdomino-pelvic lymphadenopathy nor signs of primary or metastatic malignancy in the abdomen or pelvis. A video fluoroscopic (barium) swallow assessment indicated marked pharyngeal dysphagia, resulting in substantial retention in the pyriform fossae and penetration of thin and slightly thick fluids to the vocal cords following swallowing.

MRI examination of the thighs revealed findings indicative of active myositis affecting the bilateral obturator internus and externus muscles, along with milder involvement of the hip adductors, gracilis muscles on both sides, and the distal iliopsoas muscles bilaterally (figure 2). Evidence of ongoing muscle inflammation was corroborated by a fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) positron emission tomography (PET) scan, which demonstrated widespread and abnormal FDG uptake in various muscle groups, with the most significant tracer accumulation seen in the head and neck, both deltoids, forearms, chest wall, abdominal region, and pelvis (figure 3). No sites of metabolically active disease were identified elsewhere that might point towards a neoplastic process. Assessment of tumor markers including carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), cancer antigen 19-9 (CA19-9), cancer antigen 125 (CA-125), and prostate-specific antigen (PSA) yielded normal results.

Thigh muscle biopsy disclosed focal areas containing a moderate quantity of necrotic, vacuolated, degenerating, myophagocytosed, and regenerating myofibers with a perifascicular pattern, in addition to scattered inflammation in the perimysium comprised of dispersed histiocytes (figure 4). Immunohistochemical staining for membrane attack complex revealed deposition of complement component 5b-9 (C5b-9) in perifascicular endomysial capillaries, which is characteristic of dermatomyositis. A diagnosis of moderate to severe SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis was established, presenting with inflammatory edema, peribronchial consolidation secondary to micro-aspiration, dysphagia, and myositis. Cutaneous findings included a positive Holster sign as well as periungual erythema.

Differential diagnosis:

Due to his subacute onset of neck swelling accompanied by increased CRP levels, Ludwig angina and idiopathic angioedema were initially considered. Muscle weakness, including difficulties swallowing, was not markedly present at this stage. The differential diagnosis encompassed other inflammatory muscle disorders—such as immune-mediated necrotising myopathy and inclusion body myositis—as well as infectious etiologies (including neck cellulitis or erysipelas, deep neck space infections, and bacterial sialadenitis), disorders affecting the neuromuscular junction (like myasthenia gravis), delayed hypersensitivity reactions to medications (such as drug rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms), acute contact dermatitis, orofacial granulomatosis, hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis, and superior vena cava syndrome.

Treatment:

Owing to marked involvement of the airway musculature, the patient received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for three days. This was followed by a prednisone taper, beginning at 60 mg per day and reduced by 5 mg every 2 to 3 weeks. An initial loading dose of intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) (Gamunex 10%) was administered at 2 g/kg (totaling 165 g) over 5 days (35 g per day), after which five additional cycles were provided at four-week intervals, each cycle distributing a total of 2 g/kg (165 g) across the doses. The patient was started on mycophenolate mofetil at 1 g twice daily and received two intravenous doses of rituximab at 1000 mg, spaced two weeks apart. Methotrexate was avoided, given its relationship with interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis—especially pertinent due to abnormal lung function tests—and after discussing its side effect profile with the patient. Mycophenolate was continued since it was well-tolerated, therefore azathioprine and tacrolimus were not initiated. For prophylaxis against Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim was started but subsequently discontinued due to the emergence of haemolytic anaemia (with negative glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) testing); nebulised pentamidine at 300 mg every four weeks was then substituted and tolerated.

The patient was discharged following a 21-day hospital admission. Due to persistent dysphagia at discharge, oral medications were given via nasogastric tube.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following a three-week period of inpatient care and therapy, the patient showed partial resolution of hip flexor weakness and significant reduction in cervical soft tissue edema; however, some neck swelling persisted at the time of hospital discharge. Dysphagia continued, making ongoing nasogastric tube feeding necessary for an additional two months.

At the three-week post-discharge follow-up, proximal muscle strength was completely restored, and the serum creatine kinase level had decreased to 37 U/L. During the immunology clinic visit two months after discharge, further reduction in cervical swelling was observed, though mild violaceous facial rash and periungual alterations remained detectable on physical examination. There was marked improvement in the patient’s ability to swallow, permitting discontinuation of nasogastric feeding and initiation of a soft solid diet. Maintenance therapy included mycophenolate mofetil, monthly intravenous immunoglobulin infusions, and prednisone with a tapering regimen.

Three months after discharge, the patient's proximal muscle strength remained unchanged, physical examination of the chest showed no abnormalities, and the swelling of the cervical soft tissue had resolved entirely. Prednisone was discontinued successfully seven months following discharge, at which point dysphagia was no longer present. Pulmonary function tests at that time revealed mild improvement, though a persistent restrictive pattern was thought to be consistent with his baseline physiology (see figure 5a, b). An 11-month follow-up CT of the chest indicated that basal ground-glass opacities and airspace consolidation previously noted, presumed secondary to aspiration, had nearly resolved. At 12 months after diagnosis, respiratory status remained stable, and the patient maintained good tolerance for exercise.

Regarding cancer screening, he reported no constitutional symptoms during follow-up visits and will be monitored based on symptomatology, clinical assessment, periodic blood tests, and relevant imaging studies, including serial CT scans of the chest. Plans include both gastroscopy and colonoscopy to rule out hidden gastrointestinal or colorectal malignancy.

It was decided to continue intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) therapy and gradually reduce the dose of mycophenolate over the next six months, subject to his clinical progress. He will be monitored closely to assess whether changes to his medication regimen are necessary.

Discussion:

We describe a patient with dermatomyositis positive for anti-SAE1 antibodies who presented with significant cervical soft tissue edema, which was initially misdiagnosed as idiopathic angioedema. Consideration of dermatomyositis as a possible diagnosis, along with meticulous physical assessment that identified discreet skin findings, permitted prompt identification and initiation of therapy.

Anti-SAE1 antibodies are directed against the small ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) activating enzyme, a ubiquitin-related protein that participates in post-translational modification processes known as ‘sumoylation’ across various target proteins. Dermatomyositis with SAE1 antibody positivity frequently displays dermatologic manifestations such as a heliotrope rash, Gottron’s papules, and the V-neck and shawl signs. A comprehensive review of 215 cases documented dysphagia and interstitial lung disease in 46% and 43% of patients with SAE1-positive dermatomyositis, respectively, with an associated malignancy rate of 18%.

Swelling of subcutaneous tissues, particularly in the face or neck, rarely occurs in dermatomyositis. Among 121 individuals with dermatomyositis included in a large cohort, angioedema was observed in only 8 cases (6.61%). Additionally, a review covering the period from 1985 to 2013 identified just 19 instances where dermatomyositis presented with oedema. Certain antibodies, such as anti-melanoma differentiation-associated gene (MDA)-5 and anti-transcriptional intermediary factor 1γ (TIF1-γ), are more frequently linked to angioedema compared to other autoantibodies. While facial swelling has been reported in several cases, involvement of the neck with swelling is less commonly noted. Our own literature review examining 10 cases of SAE1-positive dermatomyositis indicated oedema in only three cases, with just one specifying swelling in the throat area, highlighting the rarity of neck involvement.79

This case highlights the importance for emergency physicians, ENT specialists, immunologists, and rheumatologists to consider dermatomyositis as a potential cause of neck swelling, particularly when faced with localized 'angioedema' that cannot be otherwise explained. Dysphagia and other associated manifestations may be minimal or easily overlooked, making careful assessment for physical indicators of dermatomyositis and ordering a creatine kinase measurement essential. The subcutaneous edema observed in this patient was due to muscle inflammation secondary to myositis, as opposed to angioedema mechanisms involving histamine or bradykinin.

Following the International Guideline for Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathy-Associated Cancer Screening published by the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS), this patient presented with two high-risk criteria—dermatomyositis and dysphagia—and two intermediate-risk factors—SAE1 antibody positivity and male gender—classifying him as high risk for malignancy. He underwent malignancy evaluation including computed tomography imaging, tumor marker assays, and positron emission tomography, with additional plans for both upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopy. Thorough initial and ongoing cancer surveillance has been reassuring thus far.

Currently, there is scant data available to inform the best pharmacological management for anti-SAE1–positive dermatomyositis. The largest existing patient series reported diverse therapeutic strategies; most individuals were prescribed glucocorticoids alongside various steroid-sparing drugs, with methotrexate being the most frequent, although mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, and azathioprine were also utilized. Selected patients received intravenous immunoglobulin. In this case, the patient was administered steroids, mycophenolate mofetil, intravenous immunoglobulin, and rituximab, resulting in remission. Mycophenolate mofetil was chosen due to the patient’s preference for a daily oral therapy and its tolerability, as well as its recognized efficacy in dermatomyositis. Compared to other individuals presenting with notable tissue oedema related to dermatomyositis, this patient has fortunately demonstrated a favorable prognosis.
