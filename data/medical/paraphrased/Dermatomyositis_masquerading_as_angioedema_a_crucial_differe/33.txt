Title: Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook

Kevin Chan, Gemma Kwan, Hwei Choo Soh, Louise Evans

Abstract:

Overview: Dermatomyositis is an uncommon autoimmune condition that most often involves the skin, skeletal muscle, and pulmonary system. Management generally demands rapid initiation of potent immunosuppressive therapy to limit progression and mitigate the risk of multiorgan involvement. A previously healthy man in his 40s presented to the emergency department complaining of pronounced cervical swelling. Initial assessment by otolaryngology considered the likelihood of a deep neck space infection, which was subsequently excluded. He was then referred to clinical immunology to evaluate and treat presumed idiopathic angioedema. Owing to the atypical presentation of neck edema—findings that did not align with a diagnosis of angioedema—and in the context of accompanying cutaneous and periungual findings, creatine kinase was checked urgently. Dermatomyositis was provisionally diagnosed and later confirmed by the presence of a small ubiquitin-like modifier-1 activating enzyme (SAE1) autoantibody and characteristic muscle biopsy features. This case underscores non-histaminergic and non-bradykinin-dependent neck swelling as an unusual initial manifestation of dermatomyositis, with clinical features of dysphagia and marked myopathy necessitating emergent intervention.

Background:

Dermatomyositis is a primary inflammatory myopathy of unknown etiology, most frequently impacting the skin and skeletal musculature, but can also involve organs such as the gastrointestinal tract, joints, lungs, and heart. The prevalence is approximately 10 cases per million individuals. The condition demonstrates a bimodal age distribution, manifesting predominantly in children between 5 and 14 years of age and adults from 45 to 64 years. Typical dermatologic manifestations include Gottron’s papules, the shawl sign, heliotrope rash, and periungual telangiectasias. Additional clinical presentations may encompass proximal muscle weakness, interstitial lung disease, and dysphagia. Pseudoangioedema is a rare phenomenon in dermatomyositis, with one analysis revealing its presence in only 8 out of 121 patients with the disease. When the neck is affected, pseudoangioedema can be confused with primary otolaryngologic disorders or misdiagnosed as angioedema driven by histaminergic or bradykinin pathways.

Case presentation:

A man in his 40s came to the emergency department reporting a three-week period of gradually worsening neck swelling and weakness of his hip flexor muscles, along with mild muscle pain. He did not have any relevant medical history and had never smoked. He was not using any HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors. Ludwig’s angina was suspected, prompting a neck CT scan, which revealed widespread edema involving the strap muscles and inflammation extending into the retropharyngeal space (see figure 1). His temperature was normal, oxygen saturation was 99% on room air, and he was not tachypneic. Nailfold capillaroscopy identified microhaemorrhages and avascular zones at his proximal nailfolds. On admission, his white cell count measured 9.1×10^9/L, and his C-reactive protein was 55.6 mg/L. Because the neck swelling persisted without a clear etiology, he was referred to a quaternary hospital with ENT surgical capabilities for more comprehensive assessment. ENT performed a flexible nasendoscopy, which found no significant structural abnormalities in the soft palate, vallecula, larynx, or post-cricoid region; however, the procedure was notable for increased secretions and frequent coughing.

Consultation with the immunologist on duty was sought due to suspected angioedema, given the absence of an evident structural or infectious etiology. Upon examination, the patient displayed diffuse facial erythema, including the nasolabial folds, as well as a swollen, red, but non-tender neck. He reported not taking any medications, such as angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and lacked any personal or familial history of angioedema. His presentation was considered uncharacteristic for either acquired or hereditary angioedema. Comprehensive physical assessment identified mild nailfold abnormalities indicating periungual erythema, a rash localized to the right lateral thigh, and decreased strength in the hip flexors graded at 4/5. Gottron’s papules and heliotrope rash were not observed. A creatine kinase measurement was performed and subsequently noted to be markedly elevated at 16,184 U/L on the following day. Renal function tests were within normal limits, and a urine myoglobin test yielded a negative result. He was also found to have increased liver enzymes, with ALT at 143 U/L, AST at 396 U/L, and an LDH level of 692 U/L.

Investigations:

Due to initial suspicions of angioedema, a series of investigations was undertaken to identify potential causes of spontaneous angioedema. Complement levels, including C3 and C4, were found to be within normal parameters, and both functional and quantitative measurements of C1 esterase fell within reference values.

Testing revealed a positive anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) at a titre of 1:1280, exhibiting a speckled pattern. The patient tested negative for anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA). A myositis antibody panel encompassing 16 antigens associated with autoimmune inflammatory myopathy was performed using the Euroimmun Euroline immunoassay. Results indicated a strong positivity for anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibodies, with a signal intensity of 173 (reference range <35). All other tested myositis-specific antibodies were negative, including anti-Mi-2 alpha (anti-Mi-2α), anti-Transcription Intermediary Factor-1 gamma (anti-TIF1-γ), anti–Histidyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-Jo-1), and anti–Isoleucyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-OJ). Immunoglobulin G (IgG) and its subclasses were measured and found to be normal.

Regarding screening for immunosuppression, results showed negativity for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), hepatitis C core antibody (HBcAb), hepatitis B e antibody (HBeAb), hepatitis C virus (HCV), HIV, varicella zoster virus (VZV) IgG, as well as cytomegalovirus (CMV) IgG and immunoglobulin M (IgM). The patient’s QuantiFERON-TB Gold test was indeterminate; nevertheless, acid-fast bacilli for Mycobacterium tuberculosis were not detected in three separate sputum specimens.

Pulmonary function testing demonstrated a restrictive pattern indicative of extrathoracic involvement, without meaningful response to bronchodilator administration. Chest CT identified several non-cavitating pulmonary nodules primarily in the mid to lower lung zones; the largest nodule measured 15 mm within the right lower lobe. There were scattered regions of peribronchial consolidation, most consistent with aspiration pneumonitis, while pulmonary fibrosis was not observed radiologically. Abdominal CT revealed no abdomino-pelvic lymphadenopathy and showed neither primary nor metastatic malignancies within the abdomen or pelvis. Video fluoroscopic (barium) swallow analysis indicated profound pharyngeal dysphagia, resulting in considerable residue in the pyriform fossae as well as post-swallow penetration of both thin and mildly thickened liquids onto the vocal cords.

MRI imaging of the thighs demonstrated findings indicative of active myositis within both obturator internus and externus muscles, with less severe involvement of the hip adductors, gracilis muscles bilaterally, and distal portions of the iliopsoas muscles (see figure 2). Evidence of ongoing muscle inflammation was also confirmed by fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)-positron emission tomography (PET), which revealed widespread abnormal FDG uptake in numerous muscle groups, most notably in the head and neck, both deltoid muscles, forearms, chest wall, abdominal region, and pelvis (see figure 3). No areas of metabolically active disease were identified to raise suspicion for malignancy. Tumor marker levels, including carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), cancer antigen 19-9 (CA19-9), cancer antigen 125 (CA-125), and prostate-specific antigen (PSA), were all within normal limits.

Histopathological examination of a thigh muscle biopsy showed areas with a moderate number of necrotic, vacuolated, degenerating, myophagocytosed, and regenerating muscle fibers, predominantly localized in the perifascicular area, accompanied by scattered inflammatory cells in the perimysial region consisting mainly of dispersed histiocytes (see figure 4). Immunohistochemical analysis for the membrane attack complex identified deposition of complement component 5b-9 (C5b-9) within the endomysial capillaries of the perifascicular regions, characteristic of dermatomyositis. Based on these findings, a diagnosis of moderate to severe SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis was established, associated with inflammatory edema, peribronchial consolidation attributed to micro-aspiration, dysphagia, and myositis. Dermatologic signs observed included a positive Holster sign and periungual erythema.

Differential diagnosis:

Given his subacute onset of neck swelling accompanied by increased CRP, initial diagnostic consideration focused on Ludwig angina and idiopathic angioedema. Early in his course, muscle weakness—including difficulty swallowing—was not a prominent symptom. The differential diagnosis encompassed other inflammatory muscle disorders, such as immune-mediated necrotising myopathy and inclusion body myositis, as well as infectious etiologies like cellulitis or erysipelas of the neck, deep neck space infections, and bacterial sialadenitis. Neuromuscular junction disorders including myasthenia gravis, delayed drug hypersensitivity reactions like drug rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms, acute contact dermatitis, orofacial granulomatosis, hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis, and superior vena cava syndrome were also considered.

Treatment:

Given the marked involvement of the airway musculature, the patient received intravenous methylprednisolone at 1000 mg per day for 3 days. This was followed by initiation of a prednisone taper beginning at 60 mg daily, decreased by 5 mg every 2 to 3 weeks. A loading dose of intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG, Gamunex 10%) at 2 g/kg (totaling 165 g) was administered over a 5-day period (35 g per day), with an additional five cycles spaced 4 weeks apart, collectively totaling 2 g/kg (=165 g) across these subsequent doses. Mycophenolate mofetil was started at 1 g twice daily, and the patient received two 1000 mg intravenous infusions of rituximab two weeks apart. Methotrexate was not selected due to its known correlation with interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis, the presence of abnormal pulmonary function tests in this patient, and shared decision-making regarding its potential adverse effects. Neither azathioprine nor tacrolimus was used because mycophenolate mofetil was tolerated effectively. For prophylaxis against Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim was initially prescribed but discontinued following the onset of haemolytic anaemia (in the absence of G6PD deficiency); the patient then tolerated nebulised pentamidine at 300 mg every 4 weeks.

After an inpatient course of 21 days, the patient was discharged. Due to ongoing dysphagia, oral medications continued to be provided via nasogastric tube at the time of discharge.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following a 3-week hospital stay and therapy, the patient exhibited significant resolution of cervical soft tissue swelling—though not complete—and partial improvement in hip flexor weakness by discharge. However, dysphagia was ongoing, making continued nasogastric tube feeding necessary for an additional 2 months.

One month post-discharge, the patient’s proximal muscle power returned to normal and serum creatine kinase reached 37 U/L. At a 2-month immunology follow-up, cervical swelling showed ongoing reduction, with only mild residual violaceous erythema on the face and periungual changes remaining on physical examination. Swallowing capability improved enough to discontinue nasogastric feeds and introduce soft solid foods. The treatment regimen consisted of mycophenolate mofetil, monthly intravenous immunoglobulin, and a gradually decreasing dose of prednisone.

Three months after discharge, the patient’s proximal muscle strength remained unchanged, chest auscultation revealed no abnormal findings, and the cervical soft tissue edema had fully subsided. Prednisone therapy was able to be stopped at seven months post-discharge, at which point swallowing difficulties had completely resolved. Follow-up pulmonary function studies indicated slight improvement but continued to show a restrictive pattern considered consistent with his pre-existing physiology (see figure 5a, b). At the 11-month mark, repeat chest CT revealed near-total resolution of basal ground-glass changes and consolidation previously linked to aspiration events. At one year post-diagnosis, his respiratory capacity remained stable and he reported adequate exercise tolerance.

With respect to malignancy screening, the patient reported no constitutional symptoms during follow-up visits and surveillance will proceed based on clinical symptoms, physical examination, routine laboratory monitoring, and serial chest CT imaging as indicated. Additionally, both upper endoscopy and colonoscopy are planned to rule out undetected gastrointestinal or colorectal cancer.

The management strategy includes continued administration of IVIG, and reduction in the dose of mycophenolate over the next six months guided by his clinical progress. He will be regularly assessed to evaluate if adjustments in his pharmacologic regimen are necessary.

Discussion:

We describe a patient with dermatomyositis positive for anti-SAE1 antibodies who presented with marked swelling of the cervical soft tissues, an initial presentation that was misdiagnosed as idiopathic angioedema. A thorough clinical assessment, including careful attention to faint dermatological manifestations, was pivotal in considering dermatomyositis among the differentials, thereby facilitating accurate and prompt diagnosis and management.

Anti-SAE1 antibodies target the small-ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) activating enzyme, a protein akin to ubiquitin and essential in the sumoylation process, which involves post-translational modification of diverse substrates. The presence of SAE1 antibodies in dermatomyositis most often correlates with cutaneous symptoms such as a heliotrope eruption, Gottron’s papules, the V-neck sign, and the shawl sign. According to a comprehensive review covering 215 cases, dysphagia and interstitial lung disease were identified in 46% and 43% of individuals with SAE1-associated dermatomyositis, respectively, while malignancy was observed in 18% of cases.

Edema of the subcutaneous tissues, particularly in the face or neck, occurs infrequently in dermatomyositis. In a cohort study involving 121 individuals diagnosed with dermatomyositis, just 8 patients (6.61%) experienced angioedema. A review of published reports from 1985 to 2013 identified only 19 instances in which dermatomyositis presented with edema. Autoantibodies, including anti-melanoma differentiation-associated gene (MDA)-5 and anti-transcriptional intermediary factor 1γ (TIF1-γ), were noted to be more commonly linked to angioedema than other autoantibodies. Although facial edema has been documented in multiple cases, swelling of the neck has been reported less often. Our own examination of the literature, encompassing 10 cases of SAE1-positive dermatomyositis, indicated that edema was described in only three cases, and among these, a single case referred to swelling in the throat area—underscoring the rarity of neck region involvement.79

This case highlights the importance for emergency physicians, ENT specialists, immunologists, and rheumatologists to consider dermatomyositis in the differential diagnosis of neck swelling, particularly when confronted with unexplained, localized ‘angioedema’. Because symptoms such as dysphagia and other related manifestations may be mild, it is essential to look for physical signs of dermatomyositis and to obtain a creatine kinase measurement. The subcutaneous edema observed in this patient was secondary to muscle inflammation associated with myositis, as opposed to angioedema resulting from histaminergic or bradykinin pathways.

Following the International Guideline for Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathy-Associated Cancer Screening by the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS), the patient was found to have two major risk factors (dermatomyositis and dysphagia) and two moderate risk factors (positive SAE1 antibody, male gender), classifying him as high risk for malignancy. He underwent cancer screening that included CT imaging, evaluation of tumor markers, and a PET scan, with arrangements made for both upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopy. To date, comprehensive baseline and ongoing interval surveillance has remained reassuring.

Available data on the best pharmacological management for anti-SAE1–positive dermatomyositis remains sparse. The largest case series reported to date describes varied treatment modalities; most individuals received glucocorticoids alongside steroid-sparing therapies—methotrexate being the most frequent, with mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, and azathioprine also utilized, and intravenous immunoglobulin administered in particular instances. In this case, the patient underwent therapy with steroids, mycophenolate mofetil, intravenous immunoglobulin, and rituximab, resulting in remission. The selection of mycophenolate mofetil was influenced by the patient's desire for an oral daily medication, as well as its favorable tolerability profile and established efficacy in dermatomyositis. Compared with other individuals exhibiting pronounced tissue oedema in the context of dermatomyositis, this patient's prognosis is comparatively favorable.
