Title: Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook

Kevin Chan, Gemma Kwan, Hwei Choo Soh, Louise Evans

Abstract:

Summary: Dermatomyositis is an uncommon autoimmune condition that primarily impacts the skin, musculature, and the respiratory tract. Management of dermatomyositis generally necessitates urgent initiation of intensive immunosuppressive therapy to limit disease advancement and prevent involvement of multiple organs. A previously healthy man in his 40s presented initially to the emergency department with pronounced cervical swelling. He was first assessed by otolaryngologists for a suspected deep neck space infection; however, after this diagnosis was excluded, he was referred to clinical immunology to evaluate and manage presumed idiopathic angioedema. A creatine kinase measurement was rapidly ordered due to the atypical presentation of neck oedema, which was not compatible with typical angioedema, combined with associated dermatologic and nail findings. A provisional diagnosis of dermatomyositis was established, later confirmed by identification of a positive small ubiquitin-like modifier-1 activating enzyme (SAE1) autoantibody and characteristic muscle biopsy results. This report illustrates an uncommon presentation of dermatomyositis with neck swelling unrelated to histamine or bradykinin pathways, manifesting as dysphagia and extensive muscle involvement requiring urgent intervention.

Background:

Dermatomyositis is an idiopathic inflammatory myopathy that primarily affects the skin and muscles but may also involve the gastrointestinal system, joints, lungs, and heart. The incidence is approximately 10 cases per one million individuals. There is a bimodal age distribution, with peak onset observed in children between 5 and 14 years and adults aged 45 to 64 years. Notable dermatological manifestations include Gottron’s papules, the shawl sign, heliotrope rash, and periungual telangiectasias. Additional clinical features include proximal muscle weakness, interstitial lung disease, and dysphagia. Pseudoangioedema is a rare finding in dermatomyositis; in one study, it was documented in only 8 of 121 patients diagnosed with the condition. When pseudoangioedema affects the neck, it may mimic primary otolaryngologic pathology or be misidentified as either histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema.

Case presentation:

A man in his 40s arrived at the emergency department with a three-week history marked by gradually worsening neck swelling and proximal weakness of his hip flexors, as well as mild muscle pain. He had no notable prior medical conditions and had never smoked. He was not receiving treatment with a 3-hydroxy-3-methyl-glutaryl-coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase inhibitor. The initial diagnosis considered was Ludwig’s angina, prompting a CT scan of the neck that revealed extensive oedema affecting the strap muscles with inflammation spreading into the retropharyngeal space (see figure 1). On admission, he was afebrile, maintained oxygen saturation of 99% while breathing room air, and exhibited no tachypnoea. Nailfold capillaroscopy identified proximal microhaemorrhages and avascular zones. Laboratory evaluation showed a white blood cell count of 9.1×10^9/L and a C-reactive protein (CRP) of 55.6 mg/L. As the neck swelling persisted without an identified cause, he was referred to a quaternary care centre with ear, nose and throat (ENT) surgery capabilities for further assessment. ENT performed flexible nasendoscopy, which did not detect any notable anatomical irregularities in the soft palate, vallecula, larynx, or post-cricoid region; however, increased secretions and episodes of coughing were observed during the procedure.

The on-call immunologist was consulted regarding potential angioedema since neither structural abnormalities nor infection were evident. Examination revealed diffuse erythema involving the face, including the nasolabial folds, as well as a red, swollen, non-tender neck. The patient reported no use of any medications, such as angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs. There was also no personal or familial history of angioedema. The clinical presentation did not match typical acquired or hereditary angioedema. Detailed assessment uncovered subtle nailfold changes indicating periungual erythema, a rash on the right lateral thigh, and decreased hip flexor strength rated at 4/5. Gottron’s papules and heliotrope rash were not observed. Creatine kinase testing was ordered, and the following day the result showed a marked elevation at 16 184 U/L. Renal parameters were within normal limits, and urine myoglobin was negative. Transaminases were raised: ALT measured at 143 U/L, AST at 396 U/L, and LDH was increased to 692 U/L.

Investigations:

Due to suspicion of angioedema, various investigations were carried out to identify potential etiologies for spontaneous angioedema. Testing revealed C3 and C4 complement concentrations within reference ranges, and C1 esterase activity and quantity were also normal.

The patient demonstrated a positive anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) result at a titre of 1:1280 with a speckled pattern. Anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA) testing was negative. A myositis antibody profile encompassing 16 antigens associated with autoimmune inflammatory myopathies was conducted using the Euroimmun Euroline immunoassay. This assay detected a robust positivity for anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibodies, with a signal intensity of 173 (reference range <35). All other tested myositis antibodies—including anti-Mi-2 alpha (anti-Mi-2α), anti-Transcription Intermediary Factor-1 gamma (anti-TIF1-γ), anti-Histidyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-Jo-1), and anti-Isoleucyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-OJ)—were negative. Immunoglobulin G (IgG), including its subclasses, was found to be within normal limits. For infectious disease screening related to immunosuppression, serological tests returned negative for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), hepatitis C core antibody (HBcAb), hepatitis B e antibody (HBeAb), hepatitis C virus (HCV), human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), varicella zoster virus (VZV) IgG, and cytomegalovirus (CMV) IgG/immunoglobulin M (IgM). The QuantiFERON-TB Gold assay yielded an indeterminate result, but three sputum samples tested negative for acid-fast bacilli of Mycobacterium tuberculosis.

Pulmonary function testing demonstrated an extrathoracic restrictive ventilatory defect without a notable bronchodilator effect. Chest CT identified multiple non-cavitating nodules predominately in the mid to lower lung fields, the largest being 15 mm in diameter within the right lower lobe. Additionally, patchy peribronchial consolidation was seen, suggestive of aspiration pneumonitis, but there was no evidence of pulmonary fibrosis on imaging. Abdominal CT found no enlarged abdominopelvic lymph nodes, nor any indications of primary or metastatic malignancy within the abdominal or pelvic regions. A video fluoroscopic barium swallow revealed marked pharyngeal dysphagia, resulting in substantial residue in the pyriform fossae and post-deglutition entry of both thin and mildly thick liquids to the level of the vocal cords.

Magnetic resonance imaging of the thighs revealed findings indicative of active myositis affecting both obturator internus and externus muscles, with less severe involvement of the hip adductor group, bilateral gracilis, and distal portions of the iliopsoas muscles (figure 2). Additional evidence for ongoing muscle inflammation came from fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)-positron emission tomography (PET), which demonstrated widespread and abnormal FDG uptake in several muscle groups, most notably in the head and neck regions, both deltoids, forearms, chest wall, abdomen, and pelvis (figure 3). No hypermetabolic lesions were identified elsewhere to indicate malignancy. Levels of tumor markers, including carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), cancer antigen 19-9 (CA19-9), cancer antigen 125 (CA-125), and prostate-specific antigen (PSA), were all within normal limits.

Thigh muscle biopsy identified moderate foci of necrotic, vacuolated degenerating, myophagocytosed, and regenerating myofibers predominantly in a perifascicular pattern, accompanied by scattered perimysial inflammatory infiltrates comprising dispersed histiocytes (figure 4). Immunohistochemical analysis for membrane attack complex revealed deposition of complement component 5b-9 (C5b-9) in perifascicular endomysial capillaries, a classic finding in dermatomyositis. The final diagnosis was moderate to severe dermatomyositis positive for SAE1 antibody, presenting with inflammatory edema, peribronchial consolidation as a result of micro-aspiration, dysphagia, and myositis. Dermatological findings included a positive Holster sign and periungual erythema.

Differential diagnosis:

Due to his subacute onset of neck swelling and increased CRP, Ludwig angina and idiopathic angioedema were initially suspected. Notably, muscular weakness, including difficulty swallowing, was not a primary symptom at this stage. The differential diagnosis considered additional inflammatory myopathies (such as immune-mediated necrotising myopathy or inclusion body myositis), infectious etiologies (for example, neck cellulitis/erysipelas, deep neck space infections, and bacterial sialadenitis), disorders affecting the neuromuscular junction like myasthenia gravis, delayed hypersensitivity to medications (including drug rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms), acute contact dermatitis, orofacial granulomatosis, hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis, and superior vena cava syndrome.

Treatment:

Given considerable airway muscle involvement, the patient received intravenous methylprednisolone at 1000 mg daily for three days. Subsequently, a prednisone taper was initiated at 60 mg per day, decreasing by 5 mg every 2 to 3 weeks. Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) (Gamunex 10%) was administered as a loading dose of 2 g/kg (totaling 165 g) over five days (35 g per day), followed by five additional cycles spaced four weeks apart, with a cumulative dose of 2 g/kg (165 g) divided among these cycles. Mycophenolate mofetil was started at 1 g twice daily, alongside two doses of rituximab at 1000 mg intravenously, spaced two weeks apart. Methotrexate was avoided due to its link with interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis, taking into account the patient's abnormal lung function results and discussions regarding potential adverse effects. Azathioprine and tacrolimus were not considered as mycophenolate was tolerated without issue. For prophylaxis against Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim was initially prescribed but discontinued owing to the onset of haemolytic anaemia (with confirmed absence of glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase [G6PD] deficiency); nebulised pentamidine at 300 mg every four weeks was subsequently tolerated.

Following a 21-day hospitalization, the patient was discharged. Due to ongoing dysphagia at the time, oral medications were delivered via a nasogastric tube.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following three weeks of inpatient care and therapy, the patient experienced moderate improvement of hip flexor weakness and significant reduction in cervical soft tissue swelling, though the swelling had not completely resolved by discharge. Persistent dysphagia required ongoing nasogastric tube nutrition, which continued for an additional two months.

At three weeks post-discharge, proximal muscle strength was completely restored, and serum creatine kinase decreased to 37 U/L. During immunology follow-up two months after discharge, cervical swelling had further diminished, with examination revealing only mild residual violaceous erythema over the face and periungual changes. Swallowing improved adequately to discontinue the nasogastric tube and advance to a soft solids diet. The patient remained on mycophenolate mofetil, received monthly intravenous immunoglobulin, and continued a prednisone taper.

Three months after discharge, the patient's proximal muscle strength remained unchanged, chest assessment was normal, and the cervical soft tissue edema had resolved entirely. Prednisone therapy was discontinued at seven months post-discharge, at which point dysphagia was no longer present. Pulmonary function testing revealed modest improvement, though a persistent restrictive pattern was believed to be representative of his baseline status (see figure 5a, b). An 11-month follow-up chest CT indicated near-complete clearance of previously noted basal ground-glass opacities and airspace consolidation, findings that were initially ascribed to aspiration. Twelve months after diagnosis, his respiratory capacity was stable with satisfactory exercise tolerance.

Regarding potential malignancy, he reported no constitutional symptoms at follow-up, and surveillance will proceed based on symptom review, physical examination, routine blood analysis, and relevant imaging, including serial CT scans of the chest. He is scheduled for gastroscopy and colonoscopy to evaluate for hidden gastrointestinal or colorectal malignancies.

The management plan includes ongoing IVIG and a gradual reduction in mycophenolate dose over the next six months, contingent upon clinical progress. He will continue to be monitored closely for any indication that adjustments to his medication regimen may be necessary.

Discussion:

We describe a patient with dermatomyositis associated with anti-SAE1 antibodies who exhibited marked cervical soft tissue edema, which was initially misdiagnosed as idiopathic angioedema. The inclusion of dermatomyositis in the differential diagnosis, together with a thorough physical assessment that detected subtle cutaneous findings, enabled prompt diagnosis and initiation of therapy.

Anti-SAE1 antibodies recognize the small-ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) activating enzyme, a molecule sharing structural similarity with ubiquitin and functioning in the post-translational sumoylation of diverse proteins. Classic clinical features of SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis include distinct skin manifestations such as the heliotrope rash, Gottron’s papules, V-neck sign, and shawl sign. In a comprehensive literature analysis of 215 reported cases, dysphagia was present in 46% and interstitial lung disease in 43%, while malignancy was identified in 18% of individuals with SAE1 dermatomyositis.

Edema of the subcutaneous tissues, particularly presenting as swelling of the face or neck, is a rare manifestation in patients with dermatomyositis. In a substantial cohort comprising 121 individuals diagnosed with dermatomyositis, angioedema was observed in only 8 cases (6.61%). Reviewing published articles between 1985 and 2013 yielded a total of just 19 documented instances in which dermatomyositis occurred alongside oedema. It has been noted that antibodies such as anti-melanoma differentiation-associated gene (MDA)-5 and anti-transcriptional intermediary factor 1γ (TIF1-γ) are more frequently associated with angioedema compared to other autoantibodies. While facial swelling has been reported across several cases, involvement of the neck is less commonly cited. In our own review of 10 cases of SAE1-positive dermatomyositis, oedema was described in only three cases, with a single report specifically noting swelling in the throat region, thereby highlighting the rarity of neck involvement.79

This case highlights to emergency physicians, ENT specialists, immunologists, and rheumatologists that dermatomyositis should be considered in the differential diagnosis for neck swelling, particularly when localized 'angioedema' is unexplained or idiopathic. Dysphagia and other manifestations can be subtle, so it is essential to look for clinical signs suggestive of dermatomyositis and to order creatine kinase testing. The subcutaneous edema observed in this patient was due to inflammation associated with myositis, as opposed to histamine- or bradykinin-induced angioedema.

Following the International Guideline for Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathy-Associated Cancer Screening from the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS), the patient met two high risk criteria (dermatomyositis and dysphagia) and two intermediate risk criteria (SAE1 antibody positivity and male gender), categorizing him as high risk for malignancy. Cancer screening was performed with CT imaging, tumor marker assays, and a PET scan, and upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopy was planned. Thorough initial screening and ongoing follow-up evaluations have so far provided reassuring results.

The current data regarding the best pharmacological management for anti-SAE1–positive dermatomyositis are scarce. The largest cohort reported thus far demonstrated variability in treatment modalities, with the majority of individuals being administered glucocorticoids alongside various steroid-sparing agents, most frequently methotrexate, and also including mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, and azathioprine; intravenous immunoglobulin was utilized in select instances. Our patient received a regimen consisting of steroids, mycophenolate mofetil, intravenous immunoglobulin, and rituximab, resulting in remission. The choice of mycophenolate mofetil was influenced by the patient’s preference for daily oral therapy, as well as its favorable tolerability and established efficacy in dermatomyositis. Compared to other cases characterized by substantial tissue oedema associated with dermatomyositis, our patient fortunately demonstrates a comparatively favorable prognosis.
