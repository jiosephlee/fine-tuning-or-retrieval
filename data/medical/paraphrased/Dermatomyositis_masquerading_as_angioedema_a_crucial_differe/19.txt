Title: Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook

Kevin Chan, Gemma Kwan, Hwei Choo Soh, Louise Evans

Abstract:

Summary: Dermatomyositis is an uncommon autoimmune disorder that most frequently involves the skin, skeletal muscles, and the respiratory tract. Prompt initiation of aggressive immunosuppressive therapy is generally necessary in order to slow disease advancement and prevent multisystem involvement. In this report, a previously healthy man in his 40s presented to the emergency department with pronounced swelling of the neck. Initially, ear, nose, and throat (ENT) specialists evaluated him for a suspected deep neck space infection. After this diagnosis was excluded, he was referred to the clinical immunology team to assess and manage a presumed case of idiopathic angioedema. Owing to the atypical features of the neck oedema, which did not align with the expected presentation of angioedema, and in the context of additional cutaneous and nail findings, creatine kinase was checked without delay. Dermatomyositis was considered as a preliminary diagnosis, later confirmed by the detection of a positive small ubiquitin-like modifier-1 activating enzyme (SAE1) autoantibody and findings on muscle biopsy characteristic for the disease. This case demonstrates that neck swelling not mediated by histamine or bradykinin may be a rare initial manifestation of dermatomyositis, presenting here with swallowing difficulties and considerable myopathy, necessitating urgent intervention.

Background:

Dermatomyositis is an idiopathic inflammatory myopathy that primarily targets the skin and muscles, although it may also involve the gastrointestinal tract, joints, lungs, and heart. Its incidence is estimated at 10 cases per million people. The disease exhibits a bimodal age distribution, with onset typically occurring in children aged 5–14 years and adults aged 45–64 years. Notable dermatologic manifestations are Gottron’s papules, the shawl sign, heliotrope rash, and periungual telangiectasias. Additional clinical features include proximal muscle weakness, interstitial lung disease, and dysphagia. Pseudoangioedema is rarely seen in dermatomyositis; one report documented its presence in only 8 of 121 individuals with the diagnosis. When the neck is involved, it may present as if it is an otolaryngologic condition or could be confused with histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema.

Case presentation:

A man in his 40s presented to the emergency department after experiencing progressively worsening neck swelling over three weeks, along with weakness in his hip flexors and mild muscle pain. He reported no relevant past medical history and had never smoked. He was also not using any 3-hydroxy-3-methyl-glutaryl-coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase inhibitors. Ludwig’s angina was initially suspected, and a CT scan of his neck was performed, revealing diffuse edema affecting the strap muscles and inflammation extending into the retropharyngeal space (see figure 1). He remained afebrile, his oxygen saturation was 99% on room air, and he showed no signs of tachypnea. Nailfold capillaroscopy uncovered proximal microhaemorrhages and areas lacking vascularization. On admission, his white cell count was 9.1×10^9/L, and his C-reactive protein (CRP) measured 55.6 mg/L. As the neck swelling persisted without an identified cause, he was referred to a quaternary care center with ENT surgical expertise for further work-up. ENT performed a flexible nasendoscopy, which did not detect any significant structural abnormalities of the soft palate, vallecula, larynx, or post-cricoid area, though increased secretions and coughing were observed during the procedure.

Consultation with the on-call immunologist was sought due to suspected angioedema, given the absence of definitive structural or infectious etiologies. Upon assessment, the patient exhibited widespread erythema on the face, encompassing the nasolabial folds, as well as a swollen, erythematous neck that was not tender. He reported not taking any medications, specifically angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and there was no history—either personal or familial—of angioedema. His overall clinical presentation did not align with typical acquired or hereditary angioedema. Comprehensive physical examination disclosed mild nailfold findings indicative of periungual erythema, a rash localized to the right lateral thigh, and diminished hip flexor strength graded at 4/5. No Gottron’s papules or heliotrope rash were observed. The creatine kinase was ordered and returned markedly elevated at 16,184 U/L on the subsequent day. Renal function remained within normal limits, and urine myoglobin testing was negative. Additional laboratory workup revealed increased transaminase levels: alanine aminotransferase (ALT) was 143 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) measured 396 U/L, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) was raised at 692 U/L.

Investigations:

Due to the initial suspicion for angioedema, multiple investigations were undertaken to evaluate potential causes of spontaneous angioedema. Complement levels, specifically C3 and C4, were found to be within normal limits, and both the function and quantification of C1 esterase inhibitor were also normal.

Testing revealed a positive anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) with a titre of 1:1280, displaying a speckled pattern. Anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA) were not detected. A myositis antibody panel encompassing 16 antigens associated with autoimmune inflammatory myopathies was analyzed using the Euroimmun Euroline immunoassay. This yielded a strong positive result for anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibodies, with a signal intensity of 173 (reference range <35). No reactivity was observed for other myositis-associated antibodies, including anti-Mi-2 alpha (anti-Mi-2α), anti-Transcription Intermediary Factor-1 gamma (anti-TIF1-γ), anti–Histidyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-Jo-1), and anti–Isoleucyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-OJ). Immunoglobulin G (IgG), including subclasses, was found to be within the normal range.

Screening for immunosuppressive-related infections revealed negative results for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), hepatitis C core antibody (HBcAb), hepatitis B e antibody (HBeAb), hepatitis C virus (HCV), HIV, varicella zoster virus (VZV) IgG, and cytomegalovirus (CMV) IgG and immunoglobulin M (IgM). QuantiFERON-TB Gold testing was indeterminate; nonetheless, three separate sputum samples for acid-fast bacilli targeting Mycobacterium tuberculosis were all negative.

Pulmonary function testing demonstrated an extrathoracic restrictive defect without a notable response to bronchodilator therapy. Chest CT identified several non-cavitating nodules primarily distributed in the mid to lower lung zones, with the largest lesion measuring 15 mm located in the right lower lobe, as well as scattered peribronchial consolidation, which was considered most consistent with aspiration pneumonitis. There were no imaging findings to suggest pulmonary fibrosis. Abdominal CT did not reveal abdomino-pelvic lymphadenopathy or evidence of either a primary neoplasm or metastatic disease within the abdomen or pelvis. A video fluoroscopic (barium) swallow examination identified marked pharyngeal dysphagia, resulting in significant retention in the pyriform fossae and post-swallow entry of both thin and mildly thick liquids into the level of the vocal cords.

Thigh MRI revealed imaging features indicative of ongoing myositis within the bilateral obturator internus and externus muscles, along with milder involvement of the hip adductor group, both gracilis muscles, and bilateral distal iliopsoas muscles (figure 2). Evidence of muscle inflammation was corroborated by a fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) PET scan, which demonstrated widespread and abnormal FDG uptake in multiple muscle regions, with the most pronounced accumulation noted in the head and neck, bilateral deltoids, forearms, chest wall, abdomen, and pelvis (figure 3). There was an absence of metabolic activity in other sites to suggest malignancy. Tumor markers—specifically carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), cancer antigen 19-9 (CA19-9), cancer antigen 125 (CA-125), and prostate-specific antigen (PSA)—were all found to be within normal ranges.

Muscle biopsy from the thigh identified zones containing moderate numbers of necrotic, vacuolated, degenerating, myophagocytosed, and regenerating myofibers arranged in a perifascicular pattern, with sporadic perimysial inflammation comprised mainly of scattered histiocytes (figure 4). Membrane attack complex staining showed complement component 5b-9 (C5b-9) localized to perifascicular endomysial capillaries, a characteristic feature of dermatomyositis. The patient was diagnosed with moderate to severe dermatomyositis positive for SAE1 antibodies, manifesting as inflammatory edema, peribronchial consolidation attributable to micro-aspiration, dysphagia, and myositis. Cutaneous findings included a positive Holster sign and periungual erythema.

Differential diagnosis:

Due to his subacute onset featuring neck swelling and an increased CRP, there was early suspicion for Ludwig angina or idiopathic angioedema. Notably, muscle weakness—including difficulty swallowing—was not a significant component initially. The differential diagnosis also included other inflammatory muscle diseases such as immune-mediated necrotising myopathy and inclusion body myositis; infectious etiologies like cellulitis or erysipelas of the neck, deep neck space abscesses, and bacterial sialadenitis; disorders affecting the neuromuscular junction, for example, myasthenia gravis; delayed hypersensitivity reactions to drugs such as drug rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms; acute contact dermatitis; orofacial granulomatosis; hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis; and superior vena cava syndrome.

Treatment:

Given the marked involvement of the airway musculature, the patient received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dosage of 1000 mg per day for three consecutive days. This was subsequently followed by a prednisone taper, beginning with 60 mg daily, decreasing by 5 mg every 2 to 3 weeks. Initial intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) loading (Gamunex 10%) was administered at 2 g/kg (totaling 165 g) over 5 days (35 g per day), and thereafter, the patient underwent 5 additional IVIG cycles, spaced 4 weeks apart, with a cumulative dosage of 2 g/kg (165 g) divided across those cycles. Mycophenolate mofetil therapy was introduced at 1 g twice daily, and rituximab was administered intravenously in two doses of 1000 mg, spaced two weeks apart. Methotrexate was not utilized due to its links with interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis, particularly as the patient exhibited abnormal pulmonary function tests, and after a discussion with the patient about possible adverse effects. Azathioprine and tacrolimus were omitted as mycophenolate was tolerated without issues. Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia prophylaxis commenced with sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim, which was discontinued upon development of haemolytic anaemia (despite no evidence of glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase [G6PD] deficiency); nebulized pentamidine at a dose of 300 mg every 4 weeks was then tolerated.

The patient was discharged after a 21-day hospitalization. Due to ongoing dysphagia, oral medications continued to be delivered via nasogastric tube at the time of discharge.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following three weeks of inpatient care, the patient exhibited moderate gains in hip flexor strength and significant reduction in cervical soft tissue swelling; however, the swelling had not entirely subsided upon discharge. Ongoing dysphagia persisted, requiring continued nutrition via nasogastric tube for an additional two months.

By three weeks post-discharge, the patient’s proximal muscle strength was fully restored, and serum creatine kinase levels had decreased to 37 U/L. At an immunology follow-up two months later, cervical edema showed continued resolution, with clinical examination revealing only faint violaceous erythema on the face and periungual abnormalities. Swallowing had improved to a degree that allowed discontinuation of the nasogastric tube and advancement to a diet including soft solids. The patient’s immunosuppression regimen comprised ongoing mycophenolate mofetil, monthly intravenous immunoglobulin, and a gradually decreasing dose of prednisone.

At three months following discharge, the patient exhibited stable strength in proximal muscles, chest auscultation revealed no abnormalities, and cervical soft tissue edema had fully subsided. Prednisone therapy was discontinued successfully seven months after discharge, coinciding with complete resolution of dysphagia. Subsequent pulmonary function testing indicated slight improvement, yet a remaining restrictive pattern was interpreted as consistent with his baseline physiology (figure 5a, b). At an 11-month follow-up, chest CT scans showed near-complete clearance of earlier basal ground-glass opacities and airspace consolidation, findings originally considered secondary to aspiration. Respiratory status was stable and exercise tolerance was favorable twelve months post-diagnosis.

Regarding cancer surveillance, the patient reported no constitutional complaints during follow-up and will continue to be monitored through symptom assessment, physical examination, periodic laboratory evaluations, and appropriate imaging—including serial chest CT scans. He is scheduled to undergo both gastroscopy and colonoscopy to rule out hidden gastrointestinal or colorectal malignancies.

The management plan includes ongoing IVIG therapy and a gradual reduction in mycophenolate dosage over the next six months contingent upon clinical response. The patient will remain under close observation to assess the necessity for modifications to his pharmacological regimen.

Discussion:

We present the case of a patient with dermatomyositis positive for anti-SAE1 antibodies, who exhibited marked swelling of the cervical soft tissue that was initially interpreted as idiopathic angioedema. The suspicion for dermatomyositis as an alternative diagnosis, coupled with a thorough physical evaluation identifying discreet skin manifestations, enabled prompt identification and management.

Anti-SAE1 antibodies are directed against the small ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) activating enzyme, a protein with structural similarity to ubiquitin, which is responsible for the post-translational 'sumoylation' of various substrates. Dermatomyositis with SAE1 antibody positivity generally displays distinctive dermatologic findings, including heliotrope rash, Gottron’s papules, V-neck sign, and shawl sign. According to a comprehensive review of the literature covering 215 individuals, dysphagia and interstitial lung disease were present in 46% and 43% of SAE1-associated dermatomyositis cases, respectively, and malignancies were documented in 18%.

Edema of the subcutaneous tissues—particularly swelling of the face or neck—is a rare manifestation in dermatomyositis. In a sizeable cohort study involving 121 patients diagnosed with dermatomyositis, only 8 individuals (6.61%) experienced angioedema. Additionally, a review of the literature covering the period from 1985 to 2013 identified just 19 reports where dermatomyositis presented with edema. Among implicated antibodies, anti-melanoma differentiation-associated gene (MDA)-5 antibody and anti-transcriptional intermediary factor 1γ (TIF1-γ) antibody were observed to have a stronger association with angioedema compared to other antibodies. While several case reports documented facial swelling, involvement of the neck was notably less often reported. A review of 10 cases of dermatomyositis positive for SAE1 revealed edema described in only three, with just one specifically citing swelling around the throat area, illustrating the infrequency of neck involvement.79

This case highlights the importance for emergency medicine providers, otolaryngologists, immunologists, and rheumatologists to consider dermatomyositis as part of the differential diagnosis in patients presenting with neck swelling, particularly when such localized 'angioedema' is unexplained or deemed idiopathic. Since symptoms such as dysphagia and other manifestations can be mild, it is essential to assess for physical indicators of dermatomyositis and to order a creatine kinase measurement. In this patient, the subcutaneous oedema was attributable to muscular inflammation resulting from myositis rather than from angioedema mediated by histamine or bradykinin pathways.

Following the International Guideline for Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathy-Associated Cancer Screening established by the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS), the patient exhibited two major risk factors (dermatomyositis and dysphagia) and two intermediate factors (positive SAE1 antibody and male gender), categorizing him as high risk for underlying malignancy. Accordingly, malignancy investigations were undertaken, including computed tomography imaging, tumor marker analysis, and positron emission tomography scanning, with further plans for upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopic evaluation. Extensive baseline and interval surveillance measures have so far been encouraging.

Evidence to support best practices for pharmacological management of anti-SAE1–positive dermatomyositis remains scarce. The largest reported cohort thus far demonstrated varied treatment regimens, most frequently combining glucocorticoids with different steroid-sparing medications—methotrexate was the most commonly used, with mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, and azathioprine employed as well; selected individuals also received intravenous immunoglobulin. The patient discussed received steroids, mycophenolate mofetil, intravenous immunoglobulin, and rituximab and subsequently attained remission. Mycophenolate mofetil was chosen to accommodate the patient’s preference for a daily oral therapy, its favorable tolerability profile, and its established use in dermatomyositis management. Compared to other cases featuring notable tissue oedema in dermatomyositis, our patient is fortunate to have a more favorable prognosis.
