Title: Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook

Kevin Chan, Gemma Kwan, Hwei Choo Soh, Louise Evans

Abstract:

Summary: Dermatomyositis is an uncommon autoimmune disorder that primarily involves the skin, musculature, and respiratory tract. Immediate initiation of high-dose immunosuppression is often necessary to prevent disease advancement and multiple organ complications. A previously healthy man in his 40s presented to the emergency department with marked cervical swelling. Initial assessment was performed by otolaryngology for a suspected deep neck space infection, which was subsequently ruled out. He was then referred to clinical immunology for evaluation and management of possible idiopathic angioedema. Due to the neck edema’s atypical features, inconsistent with angioedema, and the additional presence of cutaneous and nail alterations, creatine kinase was urgently measured. The clinical picture led to a presumptive diagnosis of dermatomyositis, which was later confirmed by detection of a small ubiquitin-like modifier-1 activating enzyme (SAE1) autoantibody and characteristic findings on muscle biopsy. This case underscores that neck swelling not mediated by histamine or bradykinin can be a rare initial sign of dermatomyositis, presenting with dysphagia and prominent myopathy necessitating immediate therapy.

Background:

Dermatomyositis is a type of idiopathic inflammatory myopathy that primarily targets the skin and skeletal muscles, but may also involve organs such as the gastrointestinal system, joints, lungs, and heart. The incidence is approximately 10 cases per one million individuals. The condition displays a bimodal age distribution, predominantly appearing in children between 5 and 14 years of age and adults from 45 to 64 years. Key dermatological manifestations consist of Gottron’s papules, the shawl sign, heliotrope rash, and periungual telangiectasias. Additional symptoms can include proximal muscle weakness, the presence of interstitial lung disease, and difficulty swallowing. Pseudoangioedema is a rare feature in dermatomyositis, observed in just 8 out of 121 patients according to one study. When pseudoangioedema is localized to the neck, it can mimic primary otolaryngological conditions or be confused with angioedema arising from histamine or bradykinin mechanisms.

Case presentation:

A man in his 40s came to the emergency department reporting three weeks of gradually worsening neck swelling and proximal weakness in his hip flexors, along with mild muscle pain. He had no past medical history of note and had never smoked. He was not on any HMG-CoA reductase inhibitor. Ludwig’s angina was initially suspected, prompting a neck CT, which revealed widespread neck edema affecting the strap muscles with inflammatory changes spreading into the retropharyngeal space (figure 1). He did not have a fever, his oxygen saturation was 99% on room air, and he was not breathing rapidly. Nailfold capillaroscopy detected microhaemorrhages and avascular regions at the proximal nailfolds. Laboratory tests showed a white blood cell count of 9.1×10^9/L and a CRP of 55.6 mg/L at presentation. With persistent unexplained swelling, he was referred to a quaternary center equipped with ENT surgical services for additional assessment. ENT performed a flexible nasendoscopy, which did not reveal any major structural anomalies of the soft palate, vallecula, larynx, or post-cricoid area; however, increased secretions and frequent coughing were observed during the procedure.

Consultation with the on-call immunologist was undertaken due to suspected angioedema, as neither structural nor infectious etiologies could be established. Upon assessment, the patient exhibited diffuse facial erythema extending to the nasolabial folds, along with a red, swollen, yet non-tender neck. He reported no current use of medications, including angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and had no personal or familial history of angioedema. Taken together, his presentation did not align with typical acquired or hereditary angioedema. Detailed examination revealed subtle nailfold findings indicative of periungual erythema, a cutaneous rash on the right lateral thigh, and diminished hip flexor strength rated at 4/5. There were no Gottron’s papules or heliotrope rash. A creatine kinase measurement was ordered and, the following day, demonstrated a marked elevation to 16,184 U/L. Renal function remained within normal parameters, and urine testing for myoglobin was negative. Additionally, his transaminase levels were increased: alanine aminotransferase (ALT) measured 143 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) 396 U/L, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) was elevated at 692 U/L.

Investigations:

Due to initial suspicion for angioedema, a range of tests was conducted to identify potential causes of spontaneous angioedema. The patient’s C3 and C4 complement levels were within normal limits, and both functional and quantitative measurements of C1 esterase showed normal values.

Anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) testing yielded a positive result, with a titre of 1:1280 and a speckled pattern. Anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA) testing was negative. A myositis antibody panel comprising 16 antigens associated with autoimmune inflammatory myopathy was analyzed using the Euroimmun Euroline immunoassay. The panel was strongly positive for antibodies against small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1), with a signal intensity of 173 (reference range <35). There was no reactivity for other myositis-specific antibodies, such as anti-Mi-2α, anti-TIF1-γ, anti-Jo-1, and anti-OJ. The patient’s immunoglobulin G (IgG) levels and its subclasses were within normal parameters. For screening of immunosuppression, results were negative for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), hepatitis C core antibody (HBcAb), hepatitis B e antibody (HBeAb), hepatitis C virus (HCV), HIV, varicella zoster virus (VZV) IgG, and cytomegalovirus (CMV) IgG/IgM. The QuantiFERON-TB Gold test was indeterminate, but three sputum samples were negative for acid-fast bacilli indicative of Mycobacterium tuberculosis.

Pulmonary function testing demonstrated a restrictive pattern consistent with extrathoracic involvement, without notable responsiveness to bronchodilator therapy. Chest CT identified numerous pulmonary nodules that were non-cavitating and predominantly situated in the mid to lower lung zones, with the largest measuring 15 mm in the right lower lobe. There were also irregular regions of peribronchial consolidation, likely attributable to aspiration pneumonitis; however, no radiologic signs of pulmonary fibrosis were observed. Abdominal CT showed normal abdomino-pelvic lymph nodes and did not reveal any primary or metastatic malignancy within the abdomen or pelvis. A video fluoroscopic (barium) swallowing study demonstrated pronounced pharyngeal dysphagia, resulting in significant residue in the pyriform fossae as well as post-swallow penetration of both thin and slightly thick fluids onto the vocal cords.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the thighs revealed findings indicative of ongoing myositis within both obturator internus and externus muscles, as well as less pronounced involvement of the hip adductor muscles, bilateral gracilis, and distal iliopsoas muscles bilaterally (see figure 2). Evidence of active muscular inflammation was corroborated by fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) positron emission tomography (PET) scanning, which demonstrated widespread and atypical FDG uptake affecting numerous muscle groups, with greatest intensity seen in the head and neck, both deltoids, forearms, chest wall, abdominal, and pelvic regions (refer to figure 3). No evidence of metabolically active disease suggestive of malignancy was detected elsewhere. Assessed tumour markers—including carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), cancer antigen 19-9 (CA19-9), cancer antigen 125 (CA-125), and prostate-specific antigen (PSA)—were all within normal limits.

A biopsy taken from the thigh muscle demonstrated patches with moderate quantities of necrotic, vacuolated degenerating, myophagocytosed, and regenerating myofibres arranged in a perifascicular pattern, along with scattered inflammatory infiltrates in the perimysial area predominantly made up of isolated histiocytes (figure 4). Immunohistochemical staining for the membrane attack complex identified deposition of complement component 5b-9 (C5b-9) in perifascicular endomysial capillaries, a feature characteristic of dermatomyositis. The patient was diagnosed with moderate to severe dermatomyositis positive for SAE1 antibodies, presenting with inflammatory edema, peribronchial consolidation resulting from micro-aspiration, dysphagia, and myositis. Skin involvement included a positive Holster sign and periungual erythema.

Differential diagnosis:

Due to his gradual onset of neck swelling accompanied by an increased CRP, initial diagnostic considerations included Ludwig angina and idiopathic angioedema. Muscle weakness and swallowing difficulties did not significantly feature in the early stages of his clinical picture. The differential diagnosis encompassed other types of inflammatory myopathies, such as immune-mediated necrotising myopathy and inclusion body myositis; infectious etiologies, including neck cellulitis/erysipelas, deep cervical space infections, and bacterial sialadenitis; neuromuscular junction disorders like myasthenia gravis; delayed hypersensitivity to medication, such as drug rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms; acute contact dermatitis; orofacial granulomatosis; hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis; as well as superior vena cava syndrome.

Treatment:

Because of notable involvement of the airway musculature, the patient received intravenous methylprednisolone at 1000 mg daily for three days. After completion of this course, therapy transitioned to a prednisone taper, beginning at 60 mg daily and reduced by 5 mg every 2 to 3 weeks. A loading dose of intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG; Gamunex 10%) was administered at 2 g/kg (totaling 165 g) over five days (35 g daily), with an additional five treatment cycles given every four weeks at a cumulative dose of 2 g/kg (165 g) divided among those administrations. Initiation of mycophenolate mofetil occurred at 1 g twice daily, and he also received two doses of rituximab at 1000 mg intravenously, given two weeks apart. Methotrexate was withheld given its link with interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis, especially in the setting of the patient’s abnormal pulmonary function tests and shared decision-making regarding adverse effects. Azathioprine and tacrolimus were avoided as mycophenolate was well tolerated. For prophylaxis against Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim was started but discontinued due to development of haemolytic anaemia (without underlying glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency); the patient was able to tolerate nebulized pentamidine at 300 mg every four weeks thereafter.

He was discharged following a 21-day hospital stay. Owing to ongoing dysphagia at discharge, oral medications were provided via nasogastric tube.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following three weeks of hospitalization and medical management, the patient exhibited moderate recovery of hip flexor strength and significant reduction in cervical soft tissue edema; however, the cervical swelling had not completely subsided upon discharge. Persistent dysphagia necessitated ongoing nasogastric tube feeding for an additional two months.

By three weeks post-discharge, proximal muscle power had completely returned and the serum creatine kinase level normalized to 37 U/L. At an immunology follow-up two months after discharge, there was continued improvement in cervical swelling, with physical examination revealing only residual violaceous facial erythema and periungual findings. Swallowing ability had progressed to permit the removal of the nasogastric tube and transition to a soft solid diet. The patient continued receiving mycophenolate mofetil, monthly intravenous immunoglobulin, and a reducing regimen of prednisone.

At 3 months after discharge, the patient’s proximal muscle strength remained unchanged, chest assessment revealed no abnormalities, and the swelling in the cervical soft tissues had fully subsided. Prednisone was able to be stopped 7 months after discharge, with resolution of dysphagia by that time. Subsequent pulmonary function testing indicated slight improvement, but a persistent restrictive pattern remained, believed to represent his usual baseline status (see figure 5a, b). Follow-up chest CT performed at 11 months showed almost complete resolution of prior basal ground-glass opacities and areas of consolidation, which had been initially ascribed to aspiration. Twelve months after the initial diagnosis, his respiratory capacity was stable, and he exhibited satisfactory exercise tolerance.

Regarding cancer surveillance, he reported no constitutional symptoms during follow-up. Continued monitoring is planned based on clinical presentation, physical examination, periodic laboratory investigations, and serial CT chest imaging. Gastroscopy and colonoscopy are scheduled to rule out hidden gastrointestinal or colorectal malignancies.

The management plan includes ongoing IVIG therapy, with intention to gradually reduce the mycophenolate dose over the next 6 months depending on his clinical trajectory. He will remain under close observation to assess the necessity for adjustments in medication.

Discussion:

We describe a patient with dermatomyositis positive for anti-SAE1 antibodies who presented with marked swelling of the cervical soft tissues, which was initially misdiagnosed as idiopathic angioedema. Early identification of dermatomyositis in the differential, along with thorough physical examination uncovering minimal cutaneous findings, facilitated accurate diagnosis and prompt intervention.

Anti-SAE1 antibodies are directed against the small-ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) activating enzyme—a protein structurally analogous to ubiquitin and instrumental in the sumoylation process of various proteins through post-translational modification. Dermatomyositis associated with SAE1 antibodies often presents with characteristic skin manifestations, including heliotrope rash, Gottron’s papules, V-neck sign, and shawl sign. A comprehensive review including 215 individuals revealed that dysphagia and interstitial lung disease occurred in 46% and 43% of SAE1 dermatomyositis cases, respectively, whereas 18% of patients developed malignancies.

Edema of the subcutaneous tissue, particularly in the face or neck, is rarely observed in dermatomyositis. In a cohort study including 121 individuals diagnosed with dermatomyositis, angioedema was noted in just 8 patients (6.61%). A review of the literature covering articles published between 1985 and 2013 identified only 19 reported cases in which dermatomyositis presented with edema. Certain antibodies, such as anti-melanoma differentiation-associated gene (MDA-5) and anti-transcriptional intermediary factor 1γ (TIF1-γ), were found to be more frequently linked to angioedema compared to other antibodies. While several cases documented facial edema, involvement of the neck was cited less often. In our review of 10 cases with SAE1-positive dermatomyositis, only three reported edema, and of those, just one mentioned swelling near the throat, indicating that neck involvement is infrequent.79

This case underscores the importance for emergency physicians, ENT specialists, immunologists, and rheumatologists to consider dermatomyositis as a potential diagnosis in patients presenting with neck swelling, particularly localized 'angioedema' that lacks a clear cause or is deemed idiopathic. Manifestations such as dysphagia and other associated symptoms may be mild, making it essential to look for clinical evidence of dermatomyositis and to obtain a creatine kinase measurement. In this patient, the subcutaneous edema was a result of muscle inflammation secondary to myositis rather than angioedema mediated by histamine or bradykinin.

Following the recommendations of the International Guideline for Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathy-Associated Cancer Screening developed by the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS), this patient was considered at high risk for malignancy, having two major risk factors (dermatomyositis, dysphagia) and two moderate risk factors (positive SAE1 antibody, male sex). Malignancy screening was conducted, including CT imaging, assessment of tumor markers, and a PET scan, with plans to perform upper and lower GI endoscopies. To date, comprehensive initial and subsequent surveillance have provided reassuring findings.

The available data for guiding pharmacologic management in anti-SAE1–positive dermatomyositis are sparse. In the largest cohort reported, a variety of therapies were employed; most individuals received glucocorticoids alongside various steroid-sparing agents, with methotrexate being most frequently utilized, as well as mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, and azathioprine. Intravenous immunoglobulin was administered in selected patients. The individual described here was treated with steroids, mycophenolate mofetil, intravenous immunoglobulin, and rituximab, resulting in remission. Mycophenolate mofetil was chosen given the patient's preference for an oral daily regimen, its favorable tolerability, and established role in dermatomyositis management. Compared to other cases exhibiting pronounced tissue oedema with dermatomyositis, this patient has fortunately demonstrated a favorable prognosis.
