Title: Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook

Kevin Chan, Gemma Kwan, Hwei Choo Soh, Louise Evans

Abstract:

Summary: Dermatomyositis is an uncommon autoimmune condition that generally involves the skin, muscles, and respiratory tract. Individuals affected by dermatomyositis often need urgent and vigorous immunosuppressive therapy to prevent progression and damage to multiple organs. A man in his 40s with no previous health issues presented to the emergency department with pronounced neck swelling. Initial assessment by the otolaryngology team considered a deep neck space infection; however, once this was excluded, he was referred to clinical immunology to evaluate and manage suspected idiopathic angioedema. Due to atypical neck edema, inconsistent with angioedema, and accompanying skin and nail abnormalities, creatine kinase measurement was expedited. The patient was provisionally diagnosed with dermatomyositis and subsequently demonstrated a positive small ubiquitin-like modifier-1 activating enzyme (SAE1) autoantibody and characteristic findings on muscle biopsy. This report underscores a rare presentation of dermatomyositis with neck swelling not mediated by histamine or bradykinin, associated with dysphagia and prominent muscle involvement, necessitating swift therapeutic intervention.

Background:

Dermatomyositis is an idiopathic inflammatory myopathy that primarily targets the skin and skeletal muscles, though it may also involve the gastrointestinal system, joints, pulmonary structures, and cardiac tissue. Its estimated incidence is approximately 10 per million individuals. The disease displays a bimodal age distribution, occurring most commonly in children between 5 and 14 years and in adults from 45 to 64 years old. Classic cutaneous manifestations include Gottron’s papules, the shawl sign, heliotrope rash, and periungual telangiectasia. Additional clinical features are proximal muscle weakness, interstitial lung involvement, and swallowing difficulties. Pseudoangioedema is a rare presentation in dermatomyositis, identified in only 8 of 121 patients in a published series. When present on the neck, it may mimic primary otolaryngologic conditions or be confused with histaminergic or bradykinin-induced angioedema.

Case presentation:

A man in his 40s presented to the emergency department with three weeks of worsening neck swelling and proximal muscle weakness affecting the hip flexors, as well as mild muscle pain. He did not have any notable past medical conditions and reported never having smoked. He was not on any 3-hydroxy-3-methyl-glutaryl-coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase inhibitors. The working diagnosis was Ludwig’s angina, and a CT neck scan was performed, which revealed generalized edema of the strap muscles and inflammatory changes that extended into the retropharyngeal space (figure 1). He remained afebrile, had an oxygen saturation of 99% on room air, and was not tachypneic. Nailfold capillaroscopy revealed proximal nailfold microhemorrhages with areas of avascularity. On admission, his white blood cell count was 9.1×10^9/L and C-reactive protein (CRP) measured 55.6 mg/L. With unresolved neck swelling of unknown etiology, he was referred to a quaternary center with specialized ear, nose, and throat (ENT) surgical services for advanced assessment. ENT performed a flexible nasendoscopy, identifying no major structural abnormalities in the soft palate, vallecula, larynx, or post-cricoid area; however, the patient demonstrated excessive secretions and coughed throughout the procedure.

The case was reviewed with the on-call immunologist due to suspicion of angioedema, as no definitive structural or infectious etiology was identified. Examination showed diffuse facial erythema, including the nasolabial folds, and a neck that was erythematous, swollen, yet nontender. The patient reported not taking any medications such as angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and he had neither personal nor familial history of angioedema. His presentation did not align with typical features of acquired or hereditary angioedema. Comprehensive assessment noted subtle periungual erythema as evidenced by nailfold findings, a rash on the right lateral thigh, and decreased hip flexor strength rated at 4/5. Gottron’s papules and heliotrope rash were not seen. Creatine kinase testing was performed and revealed a markedly raised value of 16 184 U/L the following day. Renal function remained within normal limits, and urine myoglobin screening was negative. He also demonstrated elevated liver enzymes, with alanine aminotransferase (ALT) at 143 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) at 396 U/L, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) at 692 U/L.

Investigations:

Due to the initial suspicion of angioedema, multiple investigations were conducted to identify possible causes of spontaneous angioedema. The patient’s C3 and C4 complement concentrations were found to be within reference ranges, as were C1 esterase levels and function.

Testing for autoimmunity revealed a positive anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) with a titre of 1:1280 and a speckled pattern, while anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA) was negative. A myositis antibody panel, evaluating 16 antigens associated with autoimmune inflammatory myopathies using the Euroimmun Euroline immunoassay, demonstrated a strong positive result for anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibodies, with a signal intensity of 173 (reference <35). Results were negative for other myositis-associated antibodies, such as anti-Mi-2 alpha (anti-Mi-2α), anti-Transcription Intermediary Factor-1 gamma (anti-TIF1-γ), anti–Histidyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-Jo-1), and anti–Isoleucyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-OJ). Levels of immunoglobulin G (IgG) and its subclasses were normal.

For immunosuppression screening, the patient tested negative for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), hepatitis C core antibody (HBcAb), hepatitis B e antibody (HBeAb), hepatitis C virus (HCV), HIV, varicella zoster virus (VZV) IgG, and cytomegalovirus (CMV) IgG/IgM. The QuantiFERON-TB Gold assay was indeterminate, but three sputum samples tested negative for acid-fast bacilli including Mycobacterium tuberculosis.

Pulmonary function testing demonstrated an extrathoracic restrictive pattern, without a notable response to bronchodilator therapy. Chest CT identified several non-cavitating nodules within the lungs, primarily in the middle and lower regions, the largest being 15 mm in diameter in the right lower lobe, alongside patchy peribronchial consolidations suggestive of aspiration pneumonitis; however, there were no radiological signs indicating pulmonary fibrosis. CT imaging of the abdomen did not reveal abdomino-pelvic lymphadenopathy or any evidence of either primary or metastatic cancer within the abdominal or pelvic cavities. A video fluoroscopic barium swallow demonstrated marked pharyngeal dysphagia, resulting in considerable residue within the pyriform fossae and post-swallow penetration of thin and mildly thick liquids to the level of the vocal cords.

Magnetic resonance imaging of the thighs demonstrated findings indicative of active myositis within the bilateral obturator internus and externus muscles, along with less pronounced myositis affecting the hip adductors, both gracilis muscles, and both distal iliopsoas muscles (figure 2). Evidence of ongoing muscle inflammation was further confirmed by a fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)-positron emission tomography (PET) scan, which revealed widespread, atypical FDG uptake across numerous muscle groups, with greatest accumulation noted in the head and neck, both deltoids, forearms, chest wall, abdomen, and pelvis (figure 3). No sites of metabolically active disease were detected elsewhere to imply the presence of malignancy. Serum tumor markers, including carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), cancer antigen 19-9 (CA19-9), cancer antigen 125 (CA-125), and prostate-specific antigen (PSA), were within normal limits.

A biopsy of thigh muscle demonstrated clusters of moderate numbers of necrotic, vacuolated degenerative, myophagocytosed, and regenerating myofibers arranged in a perifascicular pattern, together with scattered perimysial inflammatory infiltrates made up of dispersed histiocytes (figure 4). Immunohistochemical staining for membrane attack complex revealed deposition of complement component 5b-9 (C5b-9) in perifascicular endomysial capillaries, which is characteristic of dermatomyositis. The patient was diagnosed with moderate to severe dermatomyositis associated with SAE1 antibody, accompanied by inflammatory edema, peribronchial consolidation as a result of micro-aspiration, dysphagia, and myositis. Skin findings comprised a positive Holster sign as well as periungual erythema.

Differential diagnosis:

Due to his gradual onset of neck swelling accompanied by an increased CRP, Ludwig angina and idiopathic angioedema were initially suspected. Notably, muscle weakness and difficulty swallowing were not significant elements of his early symptoms. The differential diagnosis comprised alternative inflammatory myopathies (such as immune-mediated necrotising myopathy or inclusion body myositis), infectious etiologies (including cellulitis or erysipelas affecting the neck, deep neck space infections, or bacterial sialadenitis), neuromuscular junction disorders like myasthenia gravis, delayed drug hypersensitivity reactions (for example, drug rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms), acute contact dermatitis, orofacial granulomatosis, hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis, and superior vena cava syndrome.

Treatment:

Owing to pronounced involvement of airway muscles, the patient received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dosage of 1000 mg per day for 3 days. Following this, therapy transitioned to an oral prednisone taper, beginning at 60 mg daily and reduced by 5 mg every 2 to 3 weeks. A loading dose of intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) (Gamunex 10%) was administered at 2 g/kg, equaling a total of 165 g, delivered over 5 days (35 g per day); subsequently, the patient underwent 5 additional cycles, spaced 4 weeks apart, with the total 2 g/kg dose (165 g) divided across these sessions. Mycophenolate mofetil was started at 1 g twice daily, and two intravenous rituximab infusions of 1000 mg were given, spaced 2 weeks apart. Methotrexate was avoided because of its connection to interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis, in the context of abnormal lung function results and discussion with the patient about its side effect profile. Azathioprine and tacrolimus were not prescribed since mycophenolate was tolerated well. Prophylaxis for Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia initially included sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim, which was discontinued following the emergence of haemolytic anaemia (in the absence of glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase [G6PD] deficiency); subsequently, nebulised pentamidine at 300 mg every 4 weeks was tolerated.

The patient was discharged after a hospital stay of 21 days. Due to ongoing dysphagia, his oral medications were delivered via nasogastric tube at the time of discharge.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following three weeks of inpatient care and therapy, the patient showed moderate recovery in hip flexor strength and significant reduction in cervical soft tissue edema; however, some swelling remained unresolved upon discharge. Dysphagia was ongoing and required continued nasogastric tube nutrition for an additional two months.

At a follow-up three weeks post-discharge, proximal muscle power had returned to baseline and serum creatine kinase was 37 U/L. During an immunology assessment two months after discharge, there was ongoing improvement in cervical swelling, with examination revealing only persistent violaceous facial erythema and periungual changes. Swallowing had improved enough to discontinue nasogastric tube feeding and transition to soft-textured foods. The treatment regimen included mycophenolate mofetil, monthly intravenous immunoglobulin, and a progressively tapering prednisone course.

At three months after hospital discharge, the patient’s proximal muscle strength remained unchanged, chest assessment revealed no abnormalities, and the previously noted cervical soft tissue swelling had completely subsided. Prednisone therapy was able to be stopped at seven months post-discharge, at which point the dysphagia had also completely resolved. Follow-up pulmonary function testing indicated mild improvement, but a persistent restrictive pattern, believed to reflect his baseline status (figure 5a, b). A CT scan of the chest performed at eleven months showed almost complete resolution of the basal ground-glass opacities and airspace consolidation that were previously seen, which had initially been linked to aspiration. One year after the diagnosis, his respiratory function continued to be stable and he maintained good exercise capacity.

Regarding cancer surveillance, the patient reported no systemic symptoms during follow-up. Ongoing monitoring will include symptom assessment, physical exams, routine laboratory investigations, and appropriate imaging, such as serial CT chest scans. He is also scheduled for both a gastroscopy and a colonoscopy to screen for possible hidden gastrointestinal or colorectal malignancy.

The management plan includes continuing IVIG therapy, with the intention of gradually reducing the dose of mycophenolate over the next six months, contingent on his clinical status. The patient will be regularly evaluated to assess the necessity for any adjustments to his treatment regimen.

Discussion:

We describe a patient with dermatomyositis positive for anti-SAE1 antibodies, who exhibited marked cervical soft tissue edema that was initially misdiagnosed as idiopathic angioedema. Awareness of dermatomyositis as a potential etiology, together with a thorough physical assessment identifying minimal skin manifestations, enabled prompt identification and management.

Anti-SAE1 antibodies recognize the SUMO-activating enzyme, a protein that bears structural similarity to ubiquitin and mediates the post-translational modification known as sumoylation for various substrates. Dermatomyositis with SAE1 antibody positivity commonly presents with dermatologic findings such as heliotrope eruption, Gottron’s papules, the V-neck pattern, and the shawl sign. According to an extensive literature review including 215 individuals, dysphagia and interstitial lung disease were seen in 46% and 43% of SAE1 dermatomyositis cases, respectively, and malignancy was documented in 18%.

Edema of the subcutaneous tissue, particularly involving the face or neck, is rarely observed in patients with dermatomyositis. In a cohort study including 121 individuals diagnosed with dermatomyositis, angioedema was reported in only 8 cases (6.61%). A review of the literature covering publications from 1985 to 2013 identified just 19 instances of dermatomyositis presenting with edema. It was noted that antibodies such as anti-melanoma differentiation-associated gene (MDA)-5 and anti-transcriptional intermediary factor 1γ (TIF1-γ) were more frequently linked to angioedema than other antibodies. Although facial swelling was reported in several cases, swelling of the neck was documented less often. An analysis of 10 cases of dermatomyositis positive for SAE1 revealed that edema was mentioned in only three, with only one case describing swelling in the throat area, indicating that neck involvement is rare.79

This case highlights to emergency clinicians, otolaryngologists, immunologists, and rheumatologists that dermatomyositis should be considered in the differential diagnosis for neck swelling, particularly when ‘angioedema’ is present without clear etiology or is idiopathic. Because dysphagia and other manifestations may present subtly, physical examination for dermatomyositis features and ordering a creatine kinase measurement are essential. In this instance, subcutaneous edema was due to muscular inflammation associated with myositis, rather than angioedema mediated by histamine or bradykinin pathways.

Following the International Guideline for Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathy-Associated Cancer Screening issued by the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS), the patient met criteria for high cancer risk, possessing two high risk factors (dermatomyositis and dysphagia) as well as two intermediate risk factors (positive SAE1 antibody and male gender). Malignancy evaluation was performed, including computed tomography imaging, assessment of tumor markers, and a PET scan, with additional plans for upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopy. Extensive baseline and follow-up surveillance thus far has remained reassuring.

There is a paucity of data regarding optimal drug therapy for anti-SAE1–positive dermatomyositis. In the largest cohort reported so far, treatment strategies varied; the majority of individuals were given glucocorticoids along with diverse steroid-sparing medications, most frequently methotrexate, as well as mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, and azathioprine. Intravenous immunoglobulin was administered in select cases. The patient in this report received steroids, mycophenolate mofetil, intravenous immunoglobulin, and rituximab, resulting in remission. Mycophenolate mofetil was chosen after considering the patient's preference for an oral daily medication, its tolerability, and its documented effectiveness in dermatomyositis. Compared with other patients exhibiting substantial tissue oedema associated with dermatomyositis, this individual has a favorable prognosis.
