Title: Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook

Kevin Chan, Gemma Kwan, Hwei Choo Soh, Louise Evans

Abstract:

Summary: Dermatomyositis is an uncommon autoimmune condition that mainly involves the skin, muscles, and respiratory tract. Immediate and aggressive immunosuppressive therapy is generally necessary to prevent further disease progression and organ involvement. A male patient in his 40s with no significant prior health issues presented to the emergency department with marked swelling of the neck. Initial assessment by ear, nose, and throat surgeons suggested a possible deep neck space infection; however, this was excluded following evaluation. He was then referred to clinical immunology for further evaluation and management of presumed idiopathic angioedema. Owing to the atypical features of the neck edema—which did not fit angioedema—and associated cutaneous and nail abnormalities, a creatine kinase measurement was rapidly obtained. The initial diagnosis of dermatomyositis was made, which was later supported by the detection of a positive small ubiquitin-like modifier-1 activating enzyme (SAE1) autoantibody and confirmatory findings on muscle biopsy. This case underlines the occurrence of neck swelling unrelated to histamine or bradykinin mechanisms as an unusual initial manifestation of dermatomyositis, accompanied by dysphagia and substantial myopathic involvement that necessitated urgent intervention.

Background:

Dermatomyositis is a form of idiopathic inflammatory myopathy that primarily targets the skin and skeletal muscles, but may also involve the gastrointestinal system, joints, lungs, and cardiac tissue. The condition occurs at a frequency of approximately 10 cases per one million individuals. It displays a bimodal age distribution, most commonly arising in children between 5 and 14 years and adults between 45 and 64 years. Notable dermatologic manifestations include Gottron’s papules, the shawl sign, heliotrope rash, and periungual telangiectasia. Additional clinical features may involve proximal muscle weakness, interstitial lung disease, and difficulty swallowing. Pseudoangioedema is a rare presentation in dermatomyositis; one series reported this finding in only 8 of 121 affected patients. When occurring in the neck, it may be confused for a primary otolaryngologic condition or resemble angioedema due to histamine or bradykinin.

Case presentation:

A man in his 40s came to the emergency department reporting a three-week period of worsening neck swelling and weakness in his hip flexor muscles, along with mild muscle pain. He had no noteworthy past medical conditions and had never smoked. He was not taking a 3-hydroxy-3-methyl-glutaryl-coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase inhibitor. Ludwig’s angina was initially suspected, prompting a neck CT scan that revealed widespread swelling of the neck affecting the strap muscles, with inflammation extending into the retropharyngeal space (figure 1). The patient was afebrile, had oxygen saturation of 99% on room air, and was not tachypnoeic. Nailfold capillaroscopy demonstrated proximal nailfold microhaemorrhages and avascular regions. At presentation, his white blood cell count was 9.1×10⁹/L and his C-reactive protein (CRP) level measured 55.6 mg/L. Since the neck swelling persisted without an identifiable cause, he was moved to a quaternary care hospital with specialized ENT surgical services for additional workup. ENT performed a flexible nasendoscopy, which identified no notable structural anomalies in the soft palate, vallecula, larynx, or post-cricoid area, but did reveal an increase in secretions and coughing during the procedure.

The case was referred to the on-call immunologist due to suspected angioedema, given the lack of identifiable structural or infectious etiology. On examination, the patient exhibited diffuse erythema across the face, extending into the nasolabial folds, as well as an erythematous, edematous, and non-tender neck. He reported not taking any medications, including angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and there was no history of angioedema personally or within his family. His clinical features did not align with those typically seen in either acquired or hereditary angioedema. Comprehensive examination identified subtle changes in the nailfolds suggestive of periungual erythema, in addition to a rash on the lateral aspect of the right thigh and diminished hip flexion strength (power 4/5). Gottron’s papules and heliotrope rash were not observed. Creatine kinase testing was ordered and subsequently demonstrated a marked elevation to 16,184 U/L the following day. Renal function was preserved, and testing for urine myoglobin was negative. Further laboratory assessment revealed increased transaminase levels, with ALT at 143 U/L, AST at 396 U/L, and LDH at 692 U/L.

Investigations:

Due to the preliminary suspicion of angioedema, a variety of investigations were conducted to identify potential causes of spontaneous angioedema. Complement components C3 and C4 were found to be within normal limits, as were C1 esterase inhibitor function and quantitative levels.

The patient exhibited a positive anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) at a titre of 1:1280 with a speckled staining pattern. Testing for anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA) was negative. A myositis antibody panel, assessing 16 autoimmune myopathy-associated antigens via the Euroimmun Euroline immunoassay, revealed a strong positivity for anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibodies, with a signal intensity of 173 (reference range <35). Antibodies against other myositis-specific targets, including anti-Mi-2α, anti–Transcription Intermediary Factor-1 gamma (anti-TIF1-γ), anti–Histidyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-Jo-1), and anti–Isoleucyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-OJ), were absent. Serum immunoglobulin G (IgG), along with its subclasses, was also within normal ranges. Infectious screening for immunosuppressive risk factors indicated negative results for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), hepatitis C core antibody (HBcAb), hepatitis B e antibody (HBeAb), hepatitis C virus (HCV), HIV, varicella zoster virus (VZV) IgG, and both IgG and immunoglobulin M (IgM) to cytomegalovirus (CMV). The QuantiFERON-TB Gold assay was indeterminate; however, three separate sputum specimens tested negative for acid-fast bacilli, ruling out Mycobacterium tuberculosis.

Pulmonary function testing demonstrated a restrictive pattern consistent with extrathoracic involvement, without a significant response to bronchodilator therapy. Chest CT identified multiple non-cavitating nodules in the lungs, predominantly distributed in the mid to lower zones, with the largest nodule measuring 15 mm in the right lower lobe. There were also irregular peribronchial consolidations, likely secondary to aspiration pneumonitis, but no imaging findings indicative of pulmonary fibrosis. CT imaging of the abdomen did not demonstrate enlargement of abdominopelvic lymph nodes or any evidence of primary or secondary malignancy within the abdomen or pelvis. A video fluoroscopic swallow (barium) assessment revealed marked pharyngeal dysphagia, with substantial residue within the pyriform fossae and penetration of thin and mildly thick liquids to the level of the vocal cords following swallowing.

MRI examination of the thighs revealed findings indicative of ongoing myositis in the bilateral obturator internus and externus muscles, with milder inflammatory involvement of the hip adductor group, gracilis muscles on both sides, and distal portions of the bilateral iliopsoas muscles (figure 2). Evidence of active muscle inflammation was corroborated by a fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)-positron emission tomography (PET) scan, which demonstrated widespread and abnormal FDG uptake across various muscle groups, most notably in the head and neck regions, both deltoids, forearms, chest wall, abdomen, and pelvis (figure 3). No areas of increased metabolic activity were observed elsewhere to indicate malignancy. Tumor markers—including carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), cancer antigen 19-9 (CA19-9), cancer antigen 125 (CA-125), and prostate-specific antigen (PSA)—remained within normal limits.

Histopathological assessment of a thigh muscle biopsy identified moderate numbers of necrotic, vacuolated degenerate, myophagocytosed, and regenerative myofibers organized in a perifascicular pattern, along with scattered perimysial inflammation containing dispersed histiocytes (figure 4). Immunohistochemical staining for the membrane attack complex detected deposition of complement component 5b-9 (C5b-9) in perifascicular endomysial capillaries, which is characteristic of dermatomyositis. The patient was diagnosed with moderate to severe SAE1 antibody positive dermatomyositis, presenting with inflammatory edema, peribronchial consolidation resulting from micro-aspiration, dysphagia, and myositis. Dermatological findings were notable for a positive Holster sign and periungual erythema.

Differential diagnosis:

Due to his gradual onset of neck swelling accompanied by elevated CRP, initial diagnostic consideration was given to Ludwig angina as well as idiopathic angioedema. Early in his clinical course, muscle weakness and swallowing difficulties were not significant. The differential diagnosis encompassed other inflammatory myopathies such as immune-mediated necrotising myopathy and inclusion body myositis, infectious etiologies like cellulitis or erysipelas of the neck, deep neck space infections, and bacterial sialadenitis, as well as neuromuscular junction disorders including myasthenia gravis, delayed drug hypersensitivity reactions such as drug rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms, acute contact dermatitis, orofacial granulomatosis, hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis, and superior vena cava syndrome.

Treatment:

Owing to pronounced involvement of the airway musculature, the patient received intravenous methylprednisolone for 3 days at a daily dose of 1000 mg. Subsequently, he was transitioned to a prednisone taper, beginning at 60 mg daily and reduced by 5 mg every 2 to 3 weeks. Initial intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) (Gamunex 10%) was administered as a loading dose of 2 g/kg (totaling 165 g) over 5 days (35 g per day), with five additional cycles provided 4 weeks apart, cumulatively totaling 2 g/kg (165 g) divided across these cycles. Mycophenolate mofetil was initiated at a dosage of 1 g twice daily, and two intravenous rituximab infusions of 1000 mg were given 2 weeks apart. Methotrexate was not prescribed, considering its association with interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis—especially since the patient’s pulmonary function tests were abnormal—and following shared decision-making with the patient regarding potential adverse effects. As mycophenolate was well-tolerated, neither azathioprine nor tacrolimus were initiated. Sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim was started for prophylaxis against Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, but was discontinued after the patient developed haemolytic anaemia (with normal G6PD levels); nebulised pentamidine at 300 mg every 4 weeks was subsequently used without issue.

The patient was discharged after a 21-day hospital admission. Due to persistent dysphagia at the time of discharge, oral medications were administered via a nasogastric tube.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following three weeks of inpatient care and therapy, the patient's hip flexor weakness showed moderate recovery, and the cervical soft tissue edema improved significantly, although it was not completely resolved upon discharge. Ongoing dysphagia necessitated the continued use of a nasogastric tube for feeding for an additional two months.

At three weeks post-discharge, the patient had regained full proximal muscle strength and serum creatine kinase levels had decreased to 37 U/L. During an immunology assessment two months after leaving the hospital, the cervical swelling was still decreasing, with examination revealing only minimal violaceous erythema of the face and periungual changes. The patient's swallowing had improved to the extent that the nasogastric tube could be discontinued and a transition to soft solid foods was made. The patient continued to receive mycophenolate mofetil, monthly intravenous immunoglobulin, and a reducing dose of prednisone.

Three months after discharge, the patient exhibited stable proximal muscle strength, had an unremarkable chest exam, and the cervical soft tissue swelling had entirely subsided. Prednisone was discontinued without complication seven months after discharge, at which point his dysphagia had resolved completely. Pulmonary function testing showed mild improvement, though a persistent restrictive pattern remained, likely representing the patient’s baseline (figure 5a, b). Eleven months later, chest CT demonstrated almost total resolution of the previously seen basal ground-glass opacities and airspace consolidation, which had originally been considered secondary to aspiration. At the 12-month mark post-diagnosis, his respiratory status was stable, with maintained exercise capacity.

Regarding cancer surveillance, the patient reported no constitutional symptoms during follow-up. Continued monitoring was planned, including symptom assessment, physical examinations, routine laboratory tests, and appropriate imaging such as serial CT scans of the chest. He was also scheduled for both gastroscopy and colonoscopy to rule out hidden gastrointestinal or colorectal neoplasia.

Management included ongoing IVIG therapy, with intent to gradually reduce the dose of mycophenolate over the following six months based on clinical response. Close observation was advised to assess whether adjustments in his medication regimen would be necessary.

Discussion:

We describe a patient with dermatomyositis associated with anti-SAE1 antibodies who exhibited marked cervical soft tissue edema that was initially misdiagnosed as idiopathic angioedema. The correct identification of dermatomyositis as a possible etiology, along with meticulous clinical examination that identified faint skin findings, facilitated prompt diagnosis and initiation of therapy.

Anti-SAE1 autoantibodies recognize the small-ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) activating enzyme, which shares structural features with ubiquitin and plays a role in post-translational modification of proteins via sumoylation. Dermatomyositis positive for SAE1 antibodies often presents with skin findings including heliotrope rash, Gottron’s papules, V-neck sign, and shawl sign. In a comprehensive literature review of 215 individuals, 46% experienced dysphagia, 43% had interstitial lung disease, and 18% were found to have associated malignancies.

Swelling of the subcutaneous tissue, particularly involving the face or neck, is a rare manifestation in patients with dermatomyositis. In a sizable cohort consisting of 121 individuals diagnosed with dermatomyositis, angioedema was observed in just 8 cases (6.61%). An examination of the literature spanning articles published from 1985 to 2013 identified only 19 reported instances of dermatomyositis presenting with oedema. Certain autoantibodies, specifically anti-melanoma differentiation-associated gene (MDA)-5 and anti-transcriptional intermediary factor 1γ (TIF1-γ), have been noted to correlate with angioedema more frequently than other antibodies. While facial swelling has been documented in multiple reports, occurrences of neck swelling are less commonly detailed. In our review of the literature covering 10 cases of dermatomyositis positive for SAE1, oedema was described in only three instances, with a single report referencing swelling in the throat area, underscoring the rarity of neck region involvement.79

This case emphasizes for emergency physicians, ENT specialists, immunologists, and rheumatologists that dermatomyositis should be considered among the potential causes for neck swelling, particularly when presenting as localized 'angioedema' without apparent etiology. Because manifestations such as dysphagia and additional involvement may be inconspicuous, it is imperative to look for physical indications of dermatomyositis and to order a creatine kinase assay. In our patient, the subcutaneous edema resulted from muscle inflammation due to myositis, rather than from angioedema related to histaminergic or bradykinin mechanisms.

Following the International Guideline for Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathy-Associated Cancer Screening, provided by the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS), this patient exhibited two high-risk factors (dermatomyositis and dysphagia) and two intermediate risk factors (positive SAE1 antibody and male gender), thereby categorizing him as high risk for malignancy. Cancer screening was performed, including CT imaging, tumour marker analysis, and a PET scan, with additional plans to complete both upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopy. Thorough initial and subsequent surveillance evaluations have thus far remained encouraging.

The available data regarding the best medication regimen for anti-SAE1–positive dermatomyositis are sparse. The largest cohort described thus far demonstrated considerable variation in therapeutic strategies; most individuals were managed with glucocorticoids alongside various steroid-sparing agents, predominantly methotrexate, but also including mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, and azathioprine. In selected instances, intravenous immunoglobulin was administered. In this case, the patient received steroids, mycophenolate mofetil, intravenous immunoglobulin, and rituximab and has attained remission. Mycophenolate mofetil was chosen in view of the patient’s preference for an oral daily medication, its favorable tolerability profile, and its established efficacy in dermatomyositis. Encouragingly, this patient’s prognosis is favorable relative to others with pronounced tissue oedema associated with dermatomyositis.
