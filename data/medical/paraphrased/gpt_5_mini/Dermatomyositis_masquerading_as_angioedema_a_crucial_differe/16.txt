Title: Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook

Kevin Chan, Gemma Kwan, Hwei Choo Soh, Louise Evans

Abstract:

Summary: Dermatomyositis is an uncommon autoimmune disorder that predominantly involves the skin, skeletal muscles, and the respiratory tract. Individuals diagnosed with dermatomyositis often need immediate and aggressive immunosuppressive therapy to prevent disease advancement and injury to multiple organs. A man in his 40s with no significant medical history initially arrived at the emergency department complaining of marked neck swelling. He was first evaluated by otolaryngology for a suspected deep neck space infection, which was later excluded. Subsequently, the patient was referred to clinical immunology to investigate possible idiopathic angioedema. Due to the unusual pattern of neck edema—uncharacteristic for angioedema—and concurrent dermatologic and nail findings, a creatine kinase test was quickly obtained. Based on clinical features, dermatomyositis was suspected, and later, this diagnosis was supported by the presence of small ubiquitin-like modifier-1 activating enzyme (SAE1) autoantibodies and characteristic findings on muscle biopsy. This report illustrates an atypical form of dermatomyositis presenting as neck edema not mediated by histamine or bradykinin, along with dysphagia and extensive muscle involvement necessitating urgent intervention.

Background:

Dermatomyositis is an idiopathic inflammatory myopathy that primarily affects the skin and skeletal muscles, though other organ systems such as the gastrointestinal tract, joints, lungs, and heart may also be involved. Its incidence is estimated at 10 cases per million individuals. The disease exhibits a bimodal age distribution, with incidence peaks in children between 5 and 14 years old, and in adults from 45 to 64 years old. Hallmark dermatological manifestations include Gottron's papules, the shawl sign, heliotrope rash, and periungual telangiectasias. Additional clinical signs may include proximal muscle weakness, interstitial lung disease, and dysphagia. Pseudoangioedema is a rare manifestation in patients with dermatomyositis; one report identified this finding in only 8 out of 121 subjects with the condition. When pseudoangioedema involves the neck, it can mimic disorders of otolaryngological origin or be incorrectly identified as either histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema.

Case presentation:

A man in his 40s presented to the emergency department with a 3-week progression of neck swelling and proximal weakness affecting his hip flexors, accompanied by mild muscle pain. He had no relevant prior medical conditions and had never smoked. He was not on any 3-hydroxy-3-methyl-glutaryl-coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase inhibitors. Ludwig’s angina was initially suspected, and a CT scan of the neck was performed, revealing diffuse edema of the neck involving the strap muscles and inflammatory changes extending into the retropharyngeal space (figure 1). On examination, he was afebrile, maintained oxygen saturation at 99% on room air, and did not exhibit tachypnea. Nailfold capillaroscopy demonstrated proximal nailfold microhemorrhages and avascular regions. Laboratory results included a white cell count of 9.1×10^9/L and a C-reactive protein (CRP) level of 55.6 mg/L at presentation. Owing to ongoing unexplained swelling, he was referred to a quaternary care hospital with ear, nose, and throat (ENT) surgical expertise for further assessment. ENT evaluation with flexible nasendoscopy identified no significant anatomical abnormalities in the soft palate, vallecula, larynx, or post-cricoid space; however, the procedure was notable for increased secretions and episodes of coughing.

The on-call immunologist was consulted regarding suspected angioedema, as there was no evidence of structural abnormality or infection. Upon assessment, the patient displayed widespread facial erythema, including the nasolabial folds, as well as a swollen, erythematous, but non-tender neck. He reported not taking any medications such as angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and denied any personal or family history of angioedema. His presentation did not align with typical features of either acquired or hereditary angioedema. Detailed physical examination uncovered mild nailfold abnormalities consistent with periungual erythema, a rash located on the right lateral thigh, and decreased hip flexor strength rated at 4/5. Gottron’s papules and a heliotrope rash were not observed. Creatine kinase measurement was performed and subsequently found to be markedly raised at 16,184 U/L the following day. Renal function tests remained within normal parameters, and urine myoglobin screening was negative. Liver enzymes were increased, with an alanine aminotransferase (ALT) of 143 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) of 396 U/L, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) measured at 692 U/L.

Investigations:

Due to the primary suspicion of angioedema, multiple investigations were conducted to identify possible etiologies of spontaneous angioedema. Complement levels, including C3 and C4, were within normal limits, and both the functional activity and quantitative measurements of C1 esterase were also normal.

Anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) testing revealed a positive result with a titre of 1:1280, exhibiting a speckled pattern. Testing for anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA) was negative. A myositis-specific antibody panel, comprising 16 antigens related to autoimmune inflammatory myopathies and assessed using the Euroimmun Euroline immunoassay, demonstrated a strong positivity for anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibodies, with a signal intensity of 173 (reference range <35). Antibodies against other myositis targets, including anti-Mi-2α, anti-Transcription Intermediary Factor-1 gamma (anti-TIF1-γ), anti-Histidyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-Jo-1), and anti–Isoleucyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-OJ), were not detected. Serum immunoglobulin G (IgG) and its subclasses were within normal limits.

For the immunosuppression screening, results were negative for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), hepatitis C core antibody (HBcAb), hepatitis B e antibody (HBeAb), hepatitis C virus (HCV), HIV, varicella zoster virus (VZV) IgG, as well as cytomegalovirus (CMV) IgG and IgM. The QuantiFERON-TB Gold assay returned an indeterminate result; nonetheless, three sputum specimens tested negative for acid-fast bacilli, ruling out Mycobacterium tuberculosis.

Pulmonary function testing demonstrated an extrathoracic restrictive defect, without a notable response to bronchodilators. Chest CT identified multiple non-cavitating nodules distributed predominantly in the mid and lower lung zones, the largest being 15 mm within the right lower lobe. There were irregular areas of peribronchial consolidation, most likely related to aspiration pneumonitis, but no imaging signs of pulmonary fibrosis. Abdominal CT did not reveal any abdomino-pelvic lymphadenopathy or signs of either primary or metastatic malignancy within the abdomen or pelvis. Video fluoroscopic barium swallow evaluation uncovered severe pharyngeal dysphagia, resulting in significant pyriform sinus retention and post-swallow penetration of thin and mildly thick liquids to the level of the vocal cords.

MRI of the thighs revealed findings indicative of active myositis involving both obturator internus and externus muscles, with less pronounced involvement affecting the hip adductors, both gracilis muscles, and both distal iliopsoas muscles (figure 2). Evidence for ongoing muscle inflammation was corroborated by fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) positron emission tomography (PET), which demonstrated widespread and abnormal FDG uptake across numerous muscle groups, with maximal activity detected in the head and neck region, both deltoids, forearms, chest wall, abdomen, and pelvis (figure 3). No sites of metabolically active disease were identified elsewhere to raise concern for malignancy. Tumor marker evaluations, including carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), cancer antigen 19-9 (CA19-9), cancer antigen 125 (CA-125), and prostate-specific antigen (PSA), all returned within normal limits.

Histological analysis of a thigh muscle biopsy demonstrated areas with moderate numbers of necrotic, vacuolated, degenerating, myophagocytosed, and regenerating myofibers arranged in a perifascicular pattern, along with scattered perimysial inflammatory infiltrates composed mainly of dispersed histiocytes (figure 4). Immunohistochemical staining for membrane attack complex identified complement component 5b-9 (C5b-9) within the perifascicular endomysial capillaries, which is a characteristic finding in dermatomyositis. The patient was subsequently diagnosed with moderate to severe SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis, presenting with inflammatory edema, peribronchial consolidation secondary to micro-aspiration, dysphagia, and myositis. Dermatologic findings included a positive Holster sign and periungual erythema.

Differential diagnosis:

Due to his subacute onset of neck swelling in conjunction with an elevated CRP, initial considerations included Ludwig angina and idiopathic angioedema. In the early stages, muscle weakness and dysphagia were not significant findings. The differential diagnosis also encompassed other inflammatory myopathies (such as immune-mediated necrotising myopathy and inclusion body myositis), infectious etiologies (including cellulitis or erysipelas of the neck, deep neck space abscesses, and bacterial sialadenitis), disorders affecting the neuromuscular junction like myasthenia gravis, delayed drug hypersensitivity reactions (for example, drug rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms), acute contact dermatitis, orofacial granulomatosis, hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis, as well as superior vena cava syndrome.

Treatment:

Given the marked involvement of the airway muscles, the patient received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dosage of 1000 mg per day for three days. This was followed by a gradual reduction to oral prednisone, initiated at 60 mg daily and tapered by 5 mg every 2 to 3 weeks. An initial loading dose of intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) (Gamunex 10%) at 2 g/kg (totaling 165 g) was administered over five days (35 g per day), and then additional five cycles were delivered at four-week intervals, with a cumulative dose of 2 g/kg (165 g) allocated across these infusions. Mycophenolate mofetil was introduced at 1 g twice daily, and two intravenous rituximab infusions of 1000 mg were given two weeks apart. Methotrexate was not utilized, given its known link with interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis—particularly in the context of the patient’s abnormal pulmonary function—as well as following shared decision-making regarding its potential adverse effects. Alternatives such as azathioprine and tacrolimus were not selected since mycophenolate was well-tolerated. For prevention of Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, prophylaxis was initially provided with sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim but was discontinued after the patient developed haemolytic anaemia (despite normal glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase [G6PD] activity), after which inhaled pentamidine 300 mg every four weeks was started and tolerated.

He was discharged after a 21-day inpatient stay. His oral medications were administered through nasogastric tube as he had persistent dysphagia at the time of discharge.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following three weeks of inpatient care and therapy, the patient experienced noticeable recovery in hip flexor strength and substantial reduction in cervical soft tissue edema, though complete resolution of the swelling had not yet been achieved by discharge. Dysphagia persisted, necessitating ongoing nasogastric tube nutrition for an additional two months.

At the three-week follow-up after leaving the hospital, proximal muscle power had returned to normal and serum creatine kinase levels had decreased to 37 U/L. During an immunology assessment two months after discharge, further improvement in cervical swelling was observed, with examination revealing only faint residual violaceous erythema on the face and periungual changes. Swallowing function had advanced to the extent that the nasogastric tube could be discontinued and the patient was able to start soft solid foods. Therapy with mycophenolate mofetil, monthly intravenous immunoglobulin, and a reducing regimen of prednisone was continued.

At three months after discharge, the patient's proximal muscle strength remained stable, chest assessment revealed no abnormalities, and the cervical soft tissue swelling had completely subsided. Prednisone was tapered off successfully seven months after discharge, by which time his dysphagia had entirely resolved. Subsequent pulmonary function tests indicated slight improvement, though a persistent restrictive pattern remained, believed to represent his baseline physiology (figure 5a, b). At an 11-month follow-up, a CT scan of the chest showed almost total resolution of the basal ground-glass opacities and airspace consolidation previously noted and initially considered due to aspiration. At twelve months post-diagnosis, the patient’s respiratory status was unchanged and he demonstrated good tolerance for exercise.

In terms of malignancy surveillance, he reported no constitutional symptoms during follow-up. Ongoing monitoring will include symptom assessment, physical examinations, regular laboratory work, and relevant imaging such as repeated CT scans of the chest. He is also scheduled for gastroscopy and colonoscopy to rule out hidden gastrointestinal or colorectal cancer.

The treatment plan is to continue IVIG and to reduce the dose of mycophenolate gradually over the following six months, with adjustments guided by his clinical course. He will be followed closely to assess whether further modifications to his medication regimen become necessary.

Discussion:

We describe a patient with dermatomyositis testing positive for anti-SAE1 antibodies who initially presented with marked cervical soft tissue edema, which was first diagnosed as idiopathic angioedema. Dermatomyositis was considered as a differential following identification of faint skin findings on thorough examination, permitting prompt recognition and initiation of appropriate therapy.

Anti-SAE1 antibodies target the small-ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) activating enzyme, a protein that resembles ubiquitin and is responsible for the post-translational sumoylation of multiple substrates. Dermatomyositis associated with SAE1 antibodies frequently exhibits characteristic dermatologic manifestations, including heliotrope rash, Gottron’s papules, as well as the V-neck and shawl signs. According to a comprehensive review comprising 215 patients, 46% with SAE1-positive dermatomyositis exhibited dysphagia, 43% developed interstitial lung disease, and malignancy was found in 18% of cases.

Edema of the subcutaneous tissue, particularly involving the face or neck, is an infrequent presentation in dermatomyositis. In a sizable cohort of 121 dermatomyositis patients, just 8 (6.61%) exhibited angioedema. A review of the literature covering 1985 to 2013 identified only 19 reported instances of dermatomyositis accompanied by edema. Certain antibodies, including anti-melanoma differentiation-associated gene (MDA)-5 and anti-transcriptional intermediary factor 1γ (TIF1-γ), were found to have a stronger correlation with angioedema compared to other antibodies. Although several reports mentioned facial swelling, isolated neck edema was even less commonly observed. Upon reviewing 10 published cases of SAE1-positive dermatomyositis, only three noted the presence of edema, with a single case documenting swelling in the throat area, highlighting the rarity of neck region involvement.79

This case highlights to emergency physicians, ENT specialists, immunologists, and rheumatologists that dermatomyositis should be considered among the differential diagnoses for cervical swelling, particularly when localized 'angioedema' presents without an identifiable cause. As manifestations such as dysphagia and involvement of other systems can be subtle, conducting a physical examination for dermatomyositis features and ordering serum creatine kinase testing are essential. In this patient, the subcutaneous edema resulted from inflammatory changes in muscle consistent with myositis, rather than being due to angioedema mediated by histamine or bradykinin pathways.

Following the International Guideline for Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathy-Associated Cancer Screening from the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS), this patient was categorized as high risk for malignancy, possessing two major risk factors (dermatomyositis, dysphagia) and two intermediate risk factors (SAE1 antibody positivity and male gender). He underwent comprehensive malignancy evaluation, including computed tomography, tumor marker assessment, and a PET scan, with upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopy planned. Extensive initial and subsequent follow-up evaluations have so far remained reassuring.

Data regarding the most effective pharmacological management of anti-SAE1–positive dermatomyositis remains scarce. In the largest reported series, treatment regimens were varied; the majority of individuals were administered glucocorticoids together with diverse steroid-sparing therapies, most frequently methotrexate, but also including agents such as mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, and azathioprine. Intravenous immunoglobulin was utilized selectively. The therapeutic course for our patient involved corticosteroids, mycophenolate mofetil, intravenous immunoglobulin, and rituximab, leading to disease remission. The decision to use mycophenolate mofetil was informed by the patient’s preference for a daily oral medication, as well as its established safety profile and precedent for use in dermatomyositis. Compared to others with dermatomyositis complicated by marked tissue edema, our patient’s outlook is relatively favorable.
