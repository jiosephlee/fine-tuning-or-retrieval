Title: Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook

Kevin Chan, Gemma Kwan, Hwei Choo Soh, Louise Evans

Abstract:

Summary: Dermatomyositis is an uncommon autoimmune disorder that most often involves the skin, musculature, and respiratory tract. Early, aggressive immunosuppressive therapy is generally necessary in patients to prevent disease advancement and damage to multiple organ systems. A previously healthy man in his 40s presented to the emergency department with marked cervical swelling. Initial evaluation by ear, nose, and throat (ENT) specialists focused on a possible deep neck space infection. After infectious causes were excluded, the patient was referred to clinical immunology for further assessment and management of presumed idiopathic angioedema. Due to the atypical presentation of neck edema—which did not align with features typical of angioedema—and the concomitant appearance of cutaneous and nail abnormalities, creatine kinase testing was requested early in the workup. The clinical picture led to a preliminary diagnosis of dermatomyositis, which was later confirmed by detection of a small ubiquitin-like modifier-1 activating enzyme (SAE1) autoantibody and characteristic findings on muscle biopsy. This case illustrates that dermatomyositis can present with cervical swelling not attributable to histamine or bradykinin mechanisms, accompanied by dysphagia and substantial muscle involvement necessitating urgent intervention.

Background:

Dermatomyositis is an idiopathic inflammatory myopathy that primarily affects the skin and muscles, though it can also involve the gastrointestinal tract, joints, lungs, and heart. The disorder occurs at a rate of approximately 10 cases per million individuals. Two age groups are most commonly affected, with one incidence peak in children aged 5–14 years and another in adults aged 45–64 years. Distinctive skin findings associated with the condition include Gottron’s papules, heliotrope rash, the shawl sign, and periungual telangiectasias. Additional clinical features include proximal muscle weakness, interstitial lung disease, and difficulty swallowing. Pseudoangioedema represents a rare manifestation in dermatomyositis; for instance, one study reported its occurrence in only 8 out of 121 patients with this diagnosis. When present in the neck, pseudoangioedema may mimic a primary otolaryngologic pathology or be confused with histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema.

Case presentation:

A man in his 40s presented to the emergency department after experiencing three weeks of gradually worsening neck swelling and weakness in the proximal hip flexors, also reporting mild muscle aches. His past medical history was unremarkable, and he had never smoked. He denied use of any 3-hydroxy-3-methyl-glutaryl-coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase inhibitors. The working diagnosis at presentation was Ludwig’s angina, prompting a CT scan of the neck, which revealed widespread edema of the neck affecting the strap muscles, with inflammatory changes extending into the retropharyngeal space (figure 1). On examination, he was afebrile, maintained an oxygen saturation of 99% on ambient air, and was not tachypneic. Nailfold capillaroscopy uncovered microhemorrhages at the proximal nailfolds and regions lacking vascularity. His laboratory studies showed a white cell count of 9.1×10^9/L and a C-reactive protein (CRP) of 55.6 mg/L at admission. Given persistent swelling with no identified etiology, he was referred to a quaternary center offering ear, nose, and throat (ENT) surgical assessment. Flexible nasendoscopy performed by the ENT team demonstrated no notable structural abnormalities in the soft palate, vallecula, larynx, or post-cricoid area; however, the procedure was marked by increased secretions and the patient coughing during the evaluation.

The case was referred to the on-call immunologist due to concerns about possible angioedema, since no evident structural abnormality or infection was identified. Upon assessment, he exhibited widespread redness involving the facial area, including the nasolabial folds, as well as a diffusely erythematous, enlarged, but non-tender neck. He reported no use of medications, specifically denying angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and had neither a personal nor familial history of angioedema. Collectively, his symptoms were not typical of either acquired or hereditary forms of angioedema. A comprehensive physical evaluation revealed mild nailfold abnormalities consistent with periungual erythema, a cutaneous rash localized to the right lateral thigh, and decreased hip flexor strength measuring 4/5. There was no evidence of Gottron’s papules or heliotrope rash. Laboratory assessment revealed a markedly increased creatine kinase at 16,184 U/L obtained the following day. Kidney function tests were within normal range, and urine myoglobin screening was negative. Additionally, transaminase levels were raised with an alanine aminotransferase (ALT) of 143 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) of 396 U/L, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) of 692 U/L.

Investigations:

Due to the preliminary suspicion of angioedema, multiple investigations were initiated to identify potential causes of spontaneous angioedema. Results showed normal C3 and C4 complement concentrations, and both C1 esterase functional assay and quantification fell within standard limits.

Testing for autoimmunity revealed a positive anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) at a titre of 1:1280, displaying a speckled pattern. Anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA) were absent. A myositis autoantibody panel covering 16 autoantigens associated with autoimmune inflammatory myopathies was conducted using the Euroimmun Euroline immunoassay, demonstrating a strong positivity for anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibodies with a signal intensity of 173 (reference value <35). The panel did not detect other myositis-specific antibodies, including anti-Mi-2α, anti–TIF1-γ, anti-Jo-1, or anti-OJ antibodies. Levels of immunoglobulin G (IgG), along with its subclasses, were within normal ranges. The patient's infectious and immunosuppression screening showed negativity for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), hepatitis C core antibody (HBcAb), hepatitis B e antibody (HBeAb), hepatitis C virus (HCV), HIV, varicella zoster virus (VZV) IgG, and cytomegalovirus (CMV) IgG/IgM. QuantiFERON-TB Gold testing produced an indeterminate result; however, all three sputum samples were negative for acid-fast bacilli consistent with Mycobacterium tuberculosis.

Pulmonary function testing demonstrated an extrathoracic restrictive defect without a notable response to bronchodilators. Chest CT identified multiple non-cavitating pulmonary nodules predominantly in the mid to lower lung zones, the largest measuring 15 mm in the right lower lobe, along with patchy peribronchial consolidations suggestive of aspiration pneumonitis. There were no imaging findings consistent with pulmonary fibrosis. Abdominal CT did not show abdomino-pelvic lymphadenopathy or any primary or metastatic malignancy within the abdomen or pelvis. A video fluoroscopic barium swallow revealed marked pharyngeal dysphagia, resulting in substantial pyriform sinus residue and post-swallow entry of thin and slightly thickened fluids to the level of the vocal cords.

MRI imaging of the thighs demonstrated findings indicative of active myositis within both obturator internus and externus muscles, along with less pronounced involvement of the hip adductors, bilateral gracilis muscles, and bilateral distal iliopsoas muscles (see figure 2). Evidence of ongoing muscle inflammation was corroborated by a fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) positron emission tomography (PET) scan, revealing widespread, abnormal FDG uptake across numerous muscle groups, with the most significant activity observed in the head and neck, both deltoids, forearms, chest wall, abdomen, and pelvis (see figure 3). No areas of metabolic activity suggestive of malignancy were detected elsewhere. Levels of tumor markers including carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), cancer antigen 19-9 (CA19-9), cancer antigen 125 (CA-125), and prostate-specific antigen (PSA) were all within the normal range.

Histopathological examination of a thigh muscle biopsy revealed moderately numerous foci of necrotic, vacuolated, degenerating, myophagocytosed, and regenerating myofibers displaying a perifascicular pattern, along with scattered inflammation in the perimysial region composed predominantly of dispersed histiocytes (see figure 4). Immunohistochemical staining for membrane attack complex highlighted deposition of complement component 5b-9 (C5b-9) within the endomysial capillaries in perifascicular areas, consistent with features commonly seen in dermatomyositis. The patient was ultimately diagnosed with moderate to severe SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis, presenting with inflammatory edema, peribronchial consolidation secondary to micro-aspiration, dysphagia, and myositis. Noted cutaneous signs included a positive Holster sign and periungual erythema.

Differential diagnosis:

Due to the subacute onset of neck swelling in conjunction with increased CRP, initial diagnostic considerations included Ludwig angina and idiopathic angioedema. Early in his illness, significant muscle weakness or dysphagia was not apparent. The differential diagnosis extended to other inflammatory myopathies such as immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy and inclusion body myositis, as well as infectious etiologies including cellulitis or erysipelas of the neck, deep neck space abscesses, and bacterial sialadenitis. Other possibilities taken into account were neuromuscular junction pathologies like myasthenia gravis, delayed hypersensitivity drug reactions (for instance, drug rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms), acute contact dermatitis, orofacial granulomatosis, hypocomplementemic urticarial vasculitis, and superior vena cava syndrome.

Treatment:

Given the pronounced involvement of the airway musculature, the patient received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dosage of 1000 mg per day for three consecutive days. This was subsequently followed by a prednisone taper beginning at 60 mg daily, with a reduction of 5 mg every 2 to 3 weeks. An initial intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) loading dose (Gamunex 10%) of 2 g/kg (equating to a total of 165 g) was administered over a period of 5 days (35 g per day). This was followed by an additional 5 courses of IVIG, each spaced 4 weeks apart, with a cumulative dose of 2 g/kg (165 g) distributed across the infusions. The patient was started on mycophenolate mofetil 1 g twice daily and received rituximab intravenously at 1000 mg on two occasions, separated by 2 weeks. Methotrexate was not selected due to its known association with interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis, taking into account the patient’s abnormal pulmonary function as well as discussions with the patient about potential adverse effects. Azathioprine and tacrolimus were omitted, as mycophenolate was well-tolerated. For prophylaxis against Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, the patient was initially prescribed sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim, which was discontinued after he developed haemolytic anaemia (despite the absence of glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency). Thereafter, he was switched to nebulized pentamidine 300 mg every 4 weeks, which he tolerated.

He was discharged after a 21-day inpatient stay. His oral medications were administered through nasogastric tube as he had persistent dysphagia at the time of discharge.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following three weeks of inpatient care and therapy, the patient exhibited partial recovery in hip flexor strength and significant reduction in cervical soft tissue edema, though the swelling was not completely resolved by discharge. Ongoing dysphagia required continued nasogastric tube feeding, which remained necessary for an additional two months.

At three weeks post-discharge, proximal muscle strength was restored and serum creatine kinase had returned to 37 U/L. During an immunology follow-up two months after discharge, further reduction in cervical swelling was observed, with examination revealing only persistent violaceous erythema of the face and periungual changes. Swallowing function had improved enough to permit removal of the nasogastric tube, and the patient advanced to a diet of soft solids. The therapeutic regimen included maintenance with mycophenolate mofetil, monthly intravenous immunoglobulin, and a gradually decreasing dose of prednisone.

At the three-month follow-up after discharge, the patient’s proximal muscle strength remained stable, chest auscultation revealed no abnormalities, and the cervical soft tissue swelling had entirely subsided. Prednisone therapy was successfully stopped at seven months post-discharge, at which point dysphagia had completely resolved. Repeat pulmonary function testing demonstrated slight improvement, though a persistent restrictive pattern was observed, considered to be representative of his underlying baseline (see figure 5a, b). CT imaging of the chest performed at eleven months showed nearly complete resolution of the basal ground-glass opacities and areas of airspace consolidation that were previously noted and initially linked to aspiration. At the one-year mark post-diagnosis, the patient’s respiratory capacity was stable and he displayed satisfactory exercise tolerance.

Regarding cancer surveillance, the patient reported no constitutional symptoms at follow-up. Ongoing monitoring is planned through assessment of symptoms, physical examination, serial laboratory studies, and appropriate imaging modalities, including repeated CT scans of the chest. Additionally, he is scheduled for gastroscopy and colonoscopy to rule out the presence of hidden gastrointestinal or colorectal malignancy.

The treatment plan includes ongoing intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) while mycophenolate dosage will be gradually reduced over the next six months contingent upon his clinical status. The patient will continue to undergo close monitoring to assess whether changes to his medication regimen are warranted.

Discussion:

We describe a patient with dermatomyositis associated with anti-SAE1 antibodies who presented with marked cervical soft tissue edema that was initially misdiagnosed as idiopathic angioedema. Accurate identification of dermatomyositis as a possible cause, together with thorough physical assessment that detected faint skin findings, allowed for prompt diagnosis and management.

Anti-SAE1 antibodies are directed against the small-ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) activating enzyme, a protein that is structurally analogous to ubiquitin and is involved in the post-translational modification of various proteins through sumoylation. Dermatomyositis associated with SAE1 antibodies commonly displays skin manifestations including heliotrope eruption, Gottron’s papules, V-neck sign, and shawl sign. A comprehensive literature review of 215 cases found that 46% of SAE1 antibody–positive dermatomyositis patients had dysphagia, 43% developed interstitial lung disease, and malignancy was noted in 18% of cases.

Edema of the subcutaneous tissues, particularly affecting the face or neck, is a rare presentation in dermatomyositis. Among 121 individuals with dermatomyositis included in a large cohort, just 8 (6.61%) developed angioedema. Reviewing the literature from 1985 through 2013, only 19 reports of dermatomyositis cases presenting with edema were identified. Certain autoantibodies—specifically anti-melanoma differentiation-associated gene (MDA)-5 and anti-transcriptional intermediary factor 1γ (TIF1-γ)—were linked with a higher incidence of angioedema compared to others. While swelling of the face was documented in several instances, involvement of the neck was noted far less often. In a literature review we conducted encompassing 10 SAE1-positive dermatomyositis cases, only three referenced edema, with a single case citing swelling in the throat area, underscoring the infrequency of neck region involvement.79

This report highlights to emergency clinicians, otolaryngologists, immunologists, and rheumatologists that dermatomyositis should be considered among the differential diagnoses for neck swelling, particularly in cases of focal ‘angioedema’ without an identifiable cause. Manifestations such as dysphagia or additional symptoms may be mild; therefore, evaluation for characteristic physical findings of dermatomyositis and measurement of creatine kinase are essential. In this patient, the observed subcutaneous oedema resulted from inflammatory processes in the muscles due to myositis, and not from angioedema mechanisms involving histamine or bradykinin.

Following recommendations set forth in the International Guideline for Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathy-Associated Cancer Screening by the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS), the patient met criteria for two major risk factors (diagnosis of dermatomyositis and presence of dysphagia) and two intermediate risk factors (positive SAE1 antibody, male gender), categorizing him as high risk for underlying malignancy. Accordingly, he underwent an extensive cancer evaluation comprising computed tomography, assessment of tumor markers, positron emission tomography, and scheduling of upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopy. Baseline and follow-up cancer surveillance have, to this point, been unremarkable.

Evidence informing the best pharmacological management for anti-SAE1–positive dermatomyositis remains sparse. In the largest series published thus far, therapeutic regimens varied, though the majority of individuals were managed with glucocorticoids in conjunction with various steroid-sparing agents, most frequently methotrexate, and less commonly mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, or azathioprine; intravenous immunoglobulin was administered in particular instances. The patient described here received a combination of steroids, mycophenolate mofetil, intravenous immunoglobulin, and rituximab, which resulted in remission. The choice of mycophenolate was influenced by the patient's preference for an oral daily medication, as well as by its tolerability and established application in dermatomyositis. Notably, compared to other individuals experiencing considerable tissue edema associated with dermatomyositis, our patient demonstrates a relatively favorable prognosis.
