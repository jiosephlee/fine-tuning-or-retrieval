Title: Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook

Kevin Chan, Gemma Kwan, Hwei Choo Soh, Louise Evans

Abstract:

Summary: Dermatomyositis is an uncommon autoimmune condition that primarily involves the skin, musculature, and respiratory tract. Individuals diagnosed with dermatomyositis often necessitate rapid initiation of substantial immunosuppressive therapy to mitigate disease advancement and prevent involvement of multiple organs. In this case, a previously healthy man in his 40s presented acutely to the emergency department with marked cervical swelling. Initial assessment by otolaryngology specialists suspected a deep neck space infection; however, after this was excluded, the patient was referred to clinical immunology for further evaluation and management of presumed idiopathic angioedema. Due to the atypical features of the neck edema, which did not correspond to angioedema, and the observation of cutaneous and nail abnormalities, a creatine kinase assay was urgently performed. A provisional diagnosis of dermatomyositis was made, later supported by detection of a small ubiquitin-like modifier-1 activating enzyme (SAE1) autoantibody and classic findings on muscle biopsy. This report draws attention to neck swelling not mediated by histamine or bradykinin as an uncommon presentation of dermatomyositis, associated here with dysphagia and pronounced muscular involvement necessitating urgent intervention.

Background:

Dermatomyositis is an inflammatory myopathy of unknown cause that most commonly involves the skin and muscles, but may also involve the gastrointestinal tract, joints, lungs, and heart. The condition has an estimated incidence of 10 cases per million individuals. It occurs most often in a bimodal age distribution, with peak presentation in children aged 5–14 years and adults between 45–64 years. Notable dermatologic manifestations include Gottron’s papules, heliotrope rash, shawl sign, and periungual telangiectasias. Additional clinical features can involve proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, and interstitial lung disease. Pseudoangioedema is a rare finding in dermatomyositis; a study reported only 8 cases among 121 affected individuals. When involving the neck, pseudoangioedema can be misinterpreted as a primary otolaryngologic issue, or confused with angioedema mediated by histamine or bradykinin.

Case presentation:

A man in his 40s presented to the emergency department reporting a 3-week course of worsening neck swelling and proximal muscle weakness affecting the hip flexors, as well as mild muscle aches. He reported no relevant past medical conditions and had never smoked. He denied any prior use of 3-hydroxy-3-methyl-glutaryl-coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase inhibitors. The working diagnosis was Ludwig’s angina, prompting a neck CT, which revealed diffuse edema involving the strap muscles and inflammatory changes extending into the retropharyngeal space (figure 1). On examination, his temperature was normal, oxygen saturation was 99% while breathing room air, and he was not tachypnoeic. Nailfold capillaroscopy identified proximal nailfold microhaemorrhages and avascular zones. Laboratory studies demonstrated a white blood cell count of 9.1×10⁹/L and a C-reactive protein (CRP) of 55.6 mg/L at presentation. As the cause of the persistent swelling remained unclear, he was referred to a quaternary care hospital with ear, nose, and throat (ENT) surgical services for additional assessment. ENT specialists performed a flexible nasendoscopy, which identified no major structural abnormalities of the soft palate, vallecula, larynx, or post-cricoid region; however, the procedure was notable for excessive secretions and provoked coughing.

The on-call immunologist was consulted for a possible diagnosis of angioedema, as no definitive structural or infectious etiology had been identified. Upon assessment, generalized erythema was observed across the face, involving the nasolabial folds, as well as a diffusely red, swollen, but non-tender neck. The patient reported not taking any medications, including angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and denied any personal or family history of angioedema. In summary, his clinical presentation did not fit the typical pattern for either acquired or hereditary angioedema. Comprehensive physical examination identified mild nailfold changes indicative of periungual erythema, a cutaneous eruption on the right lateral thigh, and decreased strength in hip flexion rated at 4/5. There was no evidence of Gottron’s papules or a heliotrope rash. Creatine kinase was ordered and, on the following day, was markedly elevated at 16,184 U/L. Renal function tests were within normal limits and urine myoglobin testing was negative. Liver enzymes were found to be elevated, with alanine aminotransferase (ALT) at 143 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) at 396 U/L, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) raised to 692 U/L.

Investigations:

Due to the preliminary suspicion of angioedema, a series of investigations were undertaken to identify potential etiologies for spontaneous angioedema. The complement proteins C3 and C4 were found to be within normal parameters, and both functional activity and quantitative levels of C1 esterase inhibitor were normal.

Testing revealed a positive anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) with a titre of 1:1280 and a speckled staining pattern. Anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA) was negative. A myositis-specific antibody panel analyzing 16 antigens associated with autoimmune inflammatory myopathy was conducted using the Euroimmun Euroline immunoassay. This assay demonstrated a strong positivity for anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibodies, with a signal intensity of 173 (reference range <35). No reactivity was detected for other myositis-related antibodies, including anti-Mi-2 alpha (anti-Mi-2α), anti-Transcription Intermediary Factor-1 gamma (anti-TIF1-γ), anti–Histidyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-Jo-1), and anti–Isoleucyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-OJ). His immunoglobulin G (IgG) concentration and subclass distribution were unremarkable. 

With regard to infectious screening for immunosuppression, he tested negative for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), hepatitis C core antibody (HBcAb), hepatitis B e antibody (HBeAb), hepatitis C virus (HCV), HIV, varicella zoster virus (VZV) IgG, and cytomegalovirus (CMV) IgG and immunoglobulin M (IgM). The QuantiFERON-TB Gold assay produced an indeterminate result; nonetheless, examination for Mycobacterium tuberculosis using acid-fast bacilli staining of three sputum samples was negative.

Pulmonary function testing demonstrated a restrictive defect of extrathoracic origin and lacked a notable response to bronchodilator therapy. Chest computed tomography revealed several non-cavitating nodules in the lungs, predominantly situated in the mid and lower zones; the largest measured 15 mm in diameter within the right lower lobe. Additionally, there were irregular peribronchial areas of consolidation, likely attributable to aspiration pneumonitis, but no signs of pulmonary fibrosis were observed radiographically. Abdominal CT imaging did not indicate any enlargement of abdomino-pelvic lymph nodes nor presence of primary or secondary malignant lesions within the abdomen or pelvis. The video fluoroscopic (barium) swallow assessment showed marked pharyngeal dysphagia, resulting in substantial residue within the pyriform fossae and post-swallow entry of thin and mildly thickened liquids into the vocal cords.

Thigh MRI revealed findings indicative of ongoing myositis affecting both the obturator internus and externus muscles, with less pronounced involvement noted in the hip adductors, bilateral gracilis, and the distal portions of both iliopsoas muscles (see figure 2). Additional confirmation of muscle inflammation was provided by an FDG-PET scan, which demonstrated diffuse, abnormal fluorodeoxyglucose uptake across various muscle groups, most markedly observed in the head and neck regions, both deltoid muscles, forearms, chest wall, abdomen, and pelvis (figure 3). No areas of metabolically active pathology were detected elsewhere that would suggest an underlying malignancy. Tumor marker analysis—including CEA, CA19-9, CA-125, and PSA—yielded values within normal limits.

Histological analysis of a thigh muscle biopsy identified areas containing a moderate quantity of necrotic, vacuolated degenerated, myophagocytosed, and regenerating muscle fibers in a perifascicular arrangement, along with scattered perimysial inflammation characterized by dispersed histiocytes (figure 4). Immunohistochemical staining for membrane attack complex revealed the presence of C5b-9 in perifascicular endomysial capillaries, a hallmark of dermatomyositis. The patient was diagnosed with moderate to severe dermatomyositis associated with SAE1 antibodies, presenting with inflammatory edema, peribronchial consolidation attributed to micro-aspiration, dysphagia, and myositis. Cutaneous findings comprised a positive Holster sign and periungual erythema.

Differential diagnosis:

Due to his subacute onset of neck swelling accompanied by an increased CRP, Ludwig angina and idiopathic angioedema were initially considered as possible diagnoses. Muscle weakness, including difficulties with swallowing, was not evident during his early presentation. The differential diagnosis encompassed other inflammatory muscle disorders such as immune-mediated necrotising myopathy and inclusion body myositis; infectious etiologies like cellulitis or erysipelas affecting the neck, deep neck space infections, and bacterial sialadenitis; neuromuscular junction pathologies including myasthenia gravis; delayed drug hypersensitivity reactions such as drug rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms; acute contact dermatitis; orofacial granulomatosis; hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis; and superior vena cava syndrome.

Treatment:

Because of substantial involvement of the airway musculature, the patient received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for 3 days. This was followed by oral prednisone, starting at 60 mg per day, with gradual reduction by 5 mg every 2 to 3 weeks. He was administered a loading dose of intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG; Gamunex 10%) at 2 g/kg (total 165 g), delivered over 5 days (35 g per day), and subsequently underwent 5 additional cycles spaced 4 weeks apart, with the same total dose of 2 g/kg (165 g) divided across those sessions. Treatment was initiated with mycophenolate mofetil at 1 g twice daily, and two doses of rituximab at 1000 mg intravenously, given 2 weeks apart. Methotrexate was avoided due to its association with interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis; considering the patient’s abnormal lung function studies and potential adverse effects, this was discussed with the patient. Azathioprine and tacrolimus were not introduced as the patient tolerated mycophenolate well. For prophylaxis against Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim was prescribed initially, but discontinued after onset of haemolytic anaemia (despite the absence of glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase [G6PD] deficiency), and nebulised pentamidine at 300 mg every 4 weeks was subsequently tolerated.

After 21 days of hospitalization, the patient was discharged. At discharge, oral medications continued to be given via nasogastric tube due to ongoing dysphagia.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following three weeks of inpatient management, the patient showed moderate improvement in hip flexor strength and substantial reduction in cervical soft tissue edema, though the swelling was not entirely resolved by discharge. Ongoing dysphagia necessitated continued use of a nasogastric tube for enteral feeding, which remained in place for an additional two months.

At three weeks post-discharge, the patient’s proximal muscle strength had returned to normal and serum creatine kinase levels had decreased to 37 U/L. An immunology assessment conducted two months after discharge revealed further reduction in cervical swelling, with only residual violaceous erythema on the face and periungual changes observed clinically. The patient’s swallowing had progressed sufficiently to permit removal of the nasogastric tube and transition to a soft diet. Immunosuppressive therapy was maintained with mycophenolate mofetil, monthly intravenous immunoglobulin, and a tapering regimen of prednisone.

At three months after hospital discharge, the patient’s proximal muscle strength had remained stable, his chest assessment revealed no abnormalities, and the cervical soft tissue swelling had resolved entirely. Seven months after discharge, prednisone was discontinued without difficulty, by which time his dysphagia had completely subsided. Pulmonary function tests performed subsequently demonstrated mild improvement but persistent restriction, considered consistent with his baseline physiology (figure 5a, b). A follow-up chest CT at 11 months showed that the basal ground-glass opacities and airspace consolidations, previously believed to result from aspiration, had nearly resolved. Twelve months after the initial diagnosis, his respiratory capacity remained steady with satisfactory exercise tolerance.

Concerning malignancy surveillance, the patient reported no constitutional symptoms at follow-up and will be monitored based on symptom development, clinical evaluation, periodic blood work, and ongoing imaging, including serial CT scans of the chest. Planned investigations include gastroscopy and colonoscopy to rule out hidden gastrointestinal or colorectal cancer.

The treatment plan included continuing IVIG and gradual reduction of mycophenolate over the subsequent six months, contingent upon his clinical course. He will receive close follow-up to assess whether any changes to his medication regimen are warranted.

Discussion:

We describe a patient with dermatomyositis positive for anti-SAE1 antibodies, who initially presented with pronounced cervical soft tissue edema that was first misdiagnosed as idiopathic angioedema. Identification of dermatomyositis as a diagnostic consideration, coupled with detailed physical assessment uncovering faint dermatologic manifestations, facilitated prompt diagnosis and intervention.

Anti-SAE1 antibodies target the small ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) activating enzyme, a protein with homology to ubiquitin, which is responsible for the post-translational 'sumoylation' of various proteins. Dermatomyositis associated with SAE1 antibodies commonly demonstrates dermatological symptoms including heliotrope rash, Gottron’s papules, as well as V-neck and shawl signs. According to an extensive literature review involving 215 individuals, dysphagia and interstitial lung disease were identified in 46% and 43% of SAE1 dermatomyositis cases, respectively, while neoplasms were present in 18%.

Subcutaneous edema, particularly affecting the face or neck, rarely occurs in patients with dermatomyositis. In a sizable observational study including 121 dermatomyositis cases, just 8 individuals (6.61%) presented with angioedema. Reviewing publications from 1985 to 2013 revealed only 19 reports of dermatomyositis accompanied by edema. Autoantibodies such as anti-melanoma differentiation-associated gene (MDA)-5 and anti-transcriptional intermediary factor 1γ (TIF1-γ) are reported to have a higher association with angioedema compared to other antibodies. While facial edema was noted in several case reports, neck edema has been described less often. In our review of 10 SAE1-positive dermatomyositis cases, edema was mentioned in only three, with a single report referencing swelling near the throat, indicating that neck involvement is rare.79

This case emphasizes the importance for emergency physicians, ENT specialists, immunologists, and rheumatologists to consider dermatomyositis as a potential differential diagnosis for neck swelling, particularly in instances of localized ‘angioedema’ that lack a clear cause or are classified as idiopathic. Because manifestations such as dysphagia and other systemic features may be inconspicuous, it is essential to assess for clinical signs of dermatomyositis and to obtain a creatine kinase measurement. In this patient, the observed subcutaneous edema is attributable to inflammatory myopathy rather than angioedema mediated by histamine or bradykinin pathways.

Following the recommendations outlined in the International Guideline for Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathy-Associated Cancer Screening from the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS), the patient presented with two major risk factors (dermatomyositis and dysphagia) as well as two intermediate risk factors (positive SAE1 antibody and male gender), which placed him in the high risk category for malignancy. Cancer screening was carried out with computed tomography imaging, tumor marker assays, and positron emission tomography, and upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopy was planned. Initial and subsequent monitoring have continued to provide reassuring findings thus far.

The available data regarding ideal drug management for anti-SAE1–positive dermatomyositis are sparse. The largest existing case series demonstrated variability in therapeutic strategies, with most individuals treated with glucocorticoids alongside various steroid-sparing medications. Methotrexate was utilized most frequently, though mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, and azathioprine were also administered; intravenous immunoglobulin was reserved for selected patients. Our patient received a combination of steroids, mycophenolate mofetil, intravenous immunoglobulin, and rituximab, resulting in remission. The choice of mycophenolate was influenced by the patient’s preference for a daily oral option as well as its tolerability and established efficacy in dermatomyositis. Compared to other cases of dermatomyositis with marked tissue oedema, our patient’s outlook is favorable.
