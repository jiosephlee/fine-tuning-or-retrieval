Title: Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook

Kevin Chan, Gemma Kwan, Hwei Choo Soh, Louise Evans

Abstract:

Summary: Dermatomyositis is an uncommon autoimmune disorder primarily involving the skin, skeletal muscles, and the respiratory tract. Immediate initiation of aggressive immunosuppressive therapy is typically necessary in these patients to mitigate disease progression and prevent damage to multiple organ systems. A male in his 40s with no significant past medical history initially presented to the emergency department with pronounced neck swelling. He was first evaluated by otolaryngology for a likely deep neck space infection; after this etiology was excluded, clinical immunology was consulted for further workup and management of presumed idiopathic angioedema. Owing to the neck edema’s unusual characteristics and concurrent dermatologic and nail abnormalities, creatine kinase testing was promptly pursued. A presumptive diagnosis of dermatomyositis was made, which was later supported by detection of small ubiquitin-like modifier-1 activating enzyme (SAE1) autoantibodies and a diagnostic muscle biopsy. This case demonstrates that neck swelling unassociated with histamine or bradykinin mechanisms can serve as an atypical presenting symptom of dermatomyositis, accompanied by dysphagia and substantial myopathy, warranting urgent therapy.

Background:

Dermatomyositis is an idiopathic inflammatory condition that primarily targets the skin and muscle tissues, although it may also involve the gastrointestinal system, joints, pulmonary structures, and cardiac tissue. The annual incidence is estimated at 10 cases per million individuals. The disease demonstrates a bimodal age distribution, with peak incidence occurring in children between 5–14 years and adults between 45–64 years. Typical dermatological manifestations include Gottron’s papules, the shawl sign, heliotrope rash, and periungual telangiectasias. Additional symptoms may include weakness in proximal muscles, interstitial lung disease, and difficulty swallowing. Pseudoangioedema is a rare feature in dermatomyositis; one series documented its presence in only 8 out of 121 cases. When the neck is involved, it can mimic otolaryngological conditions or be misinterpreted as angioedema due to histamine or bradykinin.

Case presentation:

A man in his 40s presented to the emergency department with a 3-week progression of neck swelling and proximal muscle weakness, particularly in the hip flexors, along with mild muscle aches. He had no notable prior health issues and had never smoked. He was not on any HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors. Initially, Ludwig’s angina was considered, prompting a cervical CT scan, which revealed widespread edema of the neck affecting the strap muscles, with inflammation spreading into the retropharyngeal space (figure 1). He was not feverish, maintained an oxygen saturation of 99% while breathing ambient air, and was not tachypneic. Nailfold capillaroscopy identified microhaemorrhages and regions without vascularization at the proximal nailfolds. On admission, his white blood cell count was 9.1×10^9/L and his C-reactive protein was 55.6 mg/L. Because the neck swelling persisted without an identifiable cause, he was referred to a quaternary hospital with ear, nose, and throat (ENT) surgical facilities for further assessment. Flexible nasendoscopy by ENT did not detect any significant structural irregularities in the soft palate, vallecula, larynx, or post-cricoid space, but did show increased secretions and the patient coughed during the procedure.

The case was consulted with the on-call immunologist due to suspected angioedema, given the absence of definitive structural or infectious etiologies. Upon assessment, he exhibited diffuse facial erythema, involving the nasolabial folds, and an erythematous, swollen, yet non-tender neck. He reported not taking any medications, including angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and denied both personal and familial history of angioedema. His overall clinical features were uncharacteristic of either acquired or hereditary angioedema. Comprehensive physical examination demonstrated subtle periungual erythema at the nailfolds, a rash localized to the right lateral thigh, and decreased hip flexor strength (power rated at 4/5). There was no evidence of Gottron’s papules or heliotrope rash. Serum creatine kinase was ordered, and results revealed a markedly elevated value of 16,184 U/L the following day. Renal parameters remained within normal limits, and urinary myoglobin was negative. Transaminases were raised, with alanine aminotransferase (ALT) measured at 143 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) at 396 U/L, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) at 692 U/L.

Investigations:

Due to the initial suspicion of angioedema, various investigations were undertaken to evaluate for underlying etiologies of spontaneous angioedema. Complement components C3 and C4 were within normal limits, as were both the quantitative and functional assessments of C1 esterase.

Testing revealed the presence of anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA), exhibiting a titre of 1:1280 with a speckled pattern. Anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA) testing was negative. A myositis antibody panel targeting 16 antigens associated with autoimmune inflammatory myopathies was conducted using the Euroimmun Euroline immunoassay, which yielded a strong positive result for anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibodies, with a signal intensity of 173 (reference value <35). All other myositis-specific antibodies, such as anti-Mi-2 alpha (anti-Mi-2α), anti-Transcription Intermediary Factor-1 gamma (anti-TIF1-γ), anti–Histidyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-Jo-1), and anti–Isoleucyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-OJ), were negative. Immunoglobulin G (IgG), including all subclasses, fell within the normal range.

For infectious screening prior to immunosuppressive therapy, serological tests were negative for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), hepatitis C core antibody (HBcAb), hepatitis B e antibody (HBeAb), hepatitis C virus (HCV), human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), varicella zoster virus (VZV) IgG, and cytomegalovirus (CMV) IgG/IgM. QuantiFERON-TB Gold testing returned indeterminate, but three sputum specimens were negative for acid-fast bacilli suggestive of Mycobacterium tuberculosis.

Pulmonary function testing demonstrated an extrathoracic restrictive defect without a notable bronchodilator effect. Chest CT identified several non-cavitating nodules with a mid to lower lung zone predominance, the largest being 15 mm in diameter in the right lower lobe. There were also irregular areas of peribronchial consolidation, likely indicative of aspiration pneumonitis, but no signs of pulmonary fibrosis were observed on imaging. Abdominal CT revealed neither abdomino-pelvic lymphadenopathy nor evidence of primary or metastatic malignancy in the abdomen or pelvis. The video fluoroscopic (barium) swallow assessment showed marked pharyngeal dysphagia, resulting in substantial pyriform fossa residue and post-swallow entry of thin and moderately thick fluids onto the vocal cords.

MRI of the thighs demonstrated findings indicative of active myositis affecting both obturator internus and externus muscles, with less pronounced involvement noted in the hip adductors, bilateral gracilis muscles, and bilateral distal iliopsoas muscles (figure 2). The presence of ongoing muscle inflammation was corroborated by fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) positron emission tomography (PET) scanning, which revealed widespread and abnormal FDG uptake throughout various muscle groups, most evident in the head and neck, both deltoids, forearms, chest wall, abdomen, and pelvis (figure 3). There was no evidence of metabolically active lesions in other regions that would be suggestive of malignancy. Tumor markers, including carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), cancer antigen 19-9 (CA19-9), cancer antigen 125 (CA-125), and prostate-specific antigen (PSA), were within normal limits.

Thigh muscle biopsy identified areas with moderate numbers of necrotic, vacuolated, degenerative, myophagocytosed, and regenerative myofibers arranged in a perifascicular pattern, alongside scattered perimysial inflammatory cells consisting mainly of dispersed histiocytes (figure 4). Immunohistochemical analysis for membrane attack complex revealed complement component 5b-9 (C5b-9) deposition in perifascicular endomysial capillaries, a feature characteristic of dermatomyositis. The final diagnosis was moderate to severe dermatomyositis associated with SAE1 antibodies, accompanied by inflammatory edema, peribronchial consolidation attributed to micro-aspiration, dysphagia, and myositis. Skin findings consisted of a positive Holster sign and periungual erythema.

Differential diagnosis:

Due to the patient's subacute onset of neck swelling accompanied by increased CRP levels, Ludwig angina and idiopathic angioedema were initially considered. Early in the course, muscle weakness, including difficulties swallowing, were not significant symptoms. The differential diagnosis encompassed other inflammatory myopathies, such as immune-mediated necrotising myopathy and inclusion body myositis; infectious etiologies including neck cellulitis/erysipelas, deep neck space infections, and bacterial sialadenitis; neuromuscular junction pathologies like myasthenia gravis; delayed hypersensitivity reactions—such as drug rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms; acute contact dermatitis; orofacial granulomatosis; hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis; and superior vena cava syndrome.

Treatment:

Owing to substantial involvement of the airway musculature, the patient received intravenous methylprednisolone for 3 days at a dose of 1000 mg per day. This was followed by an oral prednisone taper, initiated at 60 mg daily and decreased by 5 mg every 2 to 3 weeks. A loading dose of intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) (Gamunex 10%) at 2 g/kg (total 165 g) was infused over 5 days (35 g per day), and subsequently, five additional IVIG cycles were administered at 4-week intervals, cumulatively totaling 2 g/kg (165 g), divided among the respective doses. The patient was started on mycophenolate mofetil, 1 g twice daily, and was given two intravenous rituximab doses of 1000 mg each, administered 2 weeks apart. Methotrexate was avoided because of its link to interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis, given the patient’s abnormal pulmonary function tests and after discussion with him regarding its potential adverse effects. Azathioprine and tacrolimus were not considered, as mycophenolate was well-tolerated. For prophylaxis against Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim was prescribed initially; however, this was discontinued following the onset of haemolytic anaemia (without evidence of glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency), and the patient then tolerated nebulised pentamidine at 300 mg every 4 weeks.

He was discharged following a 21-day hospitalization. Due to persistent dysphagia at the time of discharge, his oral medications were administered via nasogastric tube.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following a three-week inpatient stay with ongoing therapy, the patient showed moderate gains in hip flexor strength and significant reduction of cervical soft tissue edema, although complete resolution of the swelling had not yet occurred by discharge. Dysphagia remained, resulting in the continued need for nasogastric tube feeding for an additional two months.

At a follow-up three weeks after leaving the hospital, proximal muscle power was fully restored and serum creatine kinase had decreased to 37 U/L. During an immunology evaluation two months after discharge, cervical swelling was still improving, with the physical examination showing only mild violaceous erythema of the face and periungual changes. Swallowing ability had recovered enough to discontinue nasogastric tube feeds and advance to a soft solids diet. The patient continued on mycophenolate mofetil, received monthly intravenous immunoglobulin, and was maintained on a gradually reduced dose of prednisone.

Three months after discharge, the patient exhibited stable proximal muscle strength, had a normal chest examination, and showed complete resolution of cervical soft tissue swelling. Prednisone was discontinued successfully seven months after discharge, coinciding with full resolution of dysphagia. Pulmonary function tests indicated mild improvement, though a persistent restrictive pattern was observed, considered consistent with his baseline physiology (figure 5a, b). An 11-month follow-up CT scan of the chest revealed almost complete resolution of prior basal ground-glass opacities and airspace consolidation, changes initially associated with aspiration. At twelve months post-diagnosis, respiratory status remained stable and exercise tolerance was good.

Regarding malignancy surveillance, the patient reported no constitutional symptoms at follow-up. Ongoing monitoring will be based on clinical evaluation, symptom review, regular laboratory assessments, and imaging, including periodic CT chest studies. He is scheduled to undergo both gastroscopy and colonoscopy to rule out hidden gastrointestinal or colorectal malignancy.

The therapeutic plan includes continuation of IVIG with gradual tapering of the mycophenolate dose over the next six months, guided by his clinical progress. Close follow-up will be maintained to reassess the necessity of altering his medication regimen.

Discussion:

We describe a patient with dermatomyositis positive for anti-SAE1 antibodies who exhibited marked swelling of the cervical soft tissues, initially diagnosed as idiopathic angioedema. Identification of dermatomyositis as a possible alternative diagnosis, coupled with thorough physical assessment that detected faint cutaneous manifestations, facilitated prompt diagnosis and initiation of therapy.

Anti-SAE1 antibodies target the small ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) activating enzyme—a protein homologous to ubiquitin—that participates in post-translational modification of various proteins through a process known as sumoylation. The clinical picture of SAE1 antibody-associated dermatomyositis most often includes skin findings such as the heliotrope rash, Gottron’s papules, the V-neck sign, and shawl sign. According to a large review of 215 cases, dysphagia and interstitial lung disease were noted in 46% and 43% of individuals, respectively, while associated malignancy was found in 18%.

Edema of the subcutaneous tissue, particularly involving the face or neck, is rarely observed in patients with dermatomyositis. Among 121 individuals with dermatomyositis evaluated in a large cohort, angioedema was documented in just 8 (6.61%). A review of the literature from 1985 to 2013 identified only 19 reported instances of dermatomyositis presenting with edema. Antibodies such as anti-melanoma differentiation-associated gene (MDA)-5 and anti-transcriptional intermediary factor 1γ (TIF1-γ) appeared to be more frequently linked to angioedema compared to other antibody types. While several cases reported facial swelling, swelling of the neck was noted less often. In a review of 10 cases of SAE1-positive dermatomyositis, only three reported edema, with only one case mentioning swelling localized to the throat area, illustrating the rarity of neck involvement.79

This report highlights the importance for emergency physicians, otolaryngologists, immunologists, and rheumatologists to consider dermatomyositis among the differential diagnoses for neck swelling, particularly when the swelling resembles idiopathic or unexplained localized “angioedema.” Dysphagia and other manifestations may be mild or easily overlooked, so it is essential to evaluate for dermatomyositis-specific physical findings and obtain a creatine kinase measurement. In this patient, the observed subcutaneous edema resulted from myositis-associated muscle inflammation, not from histaminergic or bradykinin-induced angioedema mechanisms.

Following the International Guideline for Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathy-Associated Cancer Screening published by the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS), the patient was classified as high risk for malignancy due to two high-risk features (dermatomyositis and dysphagia) and two intermediate-risk features (presence of SAE1 antibody and male gender). As a result, he underwent cancer screening which included CT imaging, evaluation of tumor markers, and a PET scan, with additional plans for both upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopy. To date, thorough initial and ongoing surveillance has provided reassuring results.

Current data guiding pharmacological management for anti-SAE1–positive dermatomyositis remain sparse. The largest reported patient cohort demonstrated considerable variability in therapeutic regimens; the majority were prescribed glucocorticoids along with various steroid-sparing medications, most frequently methotrexate, though mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, azathioprine, and, in certain instances, intravenous immunoglobulin were also administered. In this case, the patient received a combination of steroids, mycophenolate mofetil, intravenous immunoglobulin, and rituximab, resulting in remission. Mycophenolate mofetil was chosen due to the patient’s preference for a daily oral medication, in addition to its favorable tolerability profile and established efficacy in dermatomyositis. This individual’s prognosis is favorable compared to other patients presenting with marked tissue edema associated with dermatomyositis.
