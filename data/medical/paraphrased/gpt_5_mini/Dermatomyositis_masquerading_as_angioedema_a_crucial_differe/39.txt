Title: Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook

Kevin Chan, Gemma Kwan, Hwei Choo Soh, Louise Evans

Abstract:

Summary: Dermatomyositis is an uncommon autoimmune condition primarily impacting the skin, musculature, and respiratory tract. Management typically involves early, aggressive immunosuppression to slow disease advancement and prevent multi-organ involvement. A previously healthy man in his 40s presented to the emergency department with marked cervical swelling. Initially, otolaryngologists evaluated him for a suspected deep neck space infection, but this etiology was excluded. He was then referred to clinical immunology with presumed idiopathic angioedema. Due to the unusual nature of his cervical edema—which did not fit the clinical pattern of angioedema—and associated cutaneous and nail findings, serum creatine kinase was measured. Dermatomyositis was provisionally diagnosed and later confirmed by the detection of small ubiquitin-like modifier-1 activating enzyme (SAE1) autoantibodies and a characteristic muscle biopsy. This case illustrates that neck swelling unrelated to histamine or bradykinin can represent a rare initial manifestation of dermatomyositis, here presenting with dysphagia and pronounced muscular involvement necessitating immediate therapy.

Background:

Dermatomyositis is an idiopathic inflammatory condition primarily affecting the skin and skeletal muscles, though it may also involve the gastrointestinal system, joints, pulmonary structures, and cardiac tissue. Its incidence is approximately 10 cases per million individuals. The condition exhibits a bimodal age distribution, most commonly presenting in children between 5 and 14 years of age, and in adults between 45 and 64 years. Classic dermatologic manifestations include Gottron’s papules, heliotrope rash, shawl sign, and periungual telangiectasia. Additional clinical features comprise proximal muscle weakness, interstitial lung involvement, and dysphagia. Pseudoangioedema occurs infrequently in dermatomyositis, as evidenced by a study reporting its presence in just 8 of 121 patients diagnosed with the disease. When pseudoangioedema affects the cervical region, it can mimic primary otolaryngologic conditions or be erroneously identified as histamine- or bradykinin-mediated angioedema.

Case presentation:

A man in his 40s presented to the emergency department reporting a three-week period of gradually worsening neck swelling and proximal muscle weakness localized to his hip flexors, along with mild muscle aches. His medical history was unremarkable and he had never smoked. He was not taking any HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors. Initially, Ludwig’s angina was suspected and a neck CT scan was obtained, which demonstrated widespread edema of the strap muscles in the neck and inflammation reaching the retropharyngeal space (figure 1). On assessment, he was afebrile, had oxygen saturation of 99% while breathing room air, and did not exhibit tachypnea. Nailfold capillaroscopy revealed proximal microhaemorrhages and avascular zones at his nailfolds. His presenting white blood cell count was 9.1×10^9/L, with a CRP level of 55.6 mg/L. Due to unresolved neck swelling without an identifiable cause, he was referred to a quaternary care center with ENT surgical capabilities for additional investigation. ENT performed flexible nasendoscopy, which did not detect any notable structural irregularities of the soft palate, vallecula, larynx, or post-cricoid region, but the procedure was notable for heightened secretions and repeated coughing.

The on-call immunologist was consulted regarding potential angioedema due to the absence of an identifiable structural or infectious cause. Examination showed widespread facial erythema, including involvement of the nasolabial folds, and the neck was red, swollen, but painless. The patient reported not taking any medications such as angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and had neither personal nor family history of angioedema. Clinically, his presentation did not match typical features of either acquired or hereditary angioedema. On detailed assessment, subtle nailfold abnormalities indicative of periungual erythema were seen, there was a rash over the right lateral thigh, and hip flexor strength was diminished at 4/5. Gottron’s papules and heliotrope eruption were not present. Measurement of creatine kinase the following day revealed a substantial increase to 16 184 U/L. Renal function tests were normal and urine tested negative for myoglobin. Transaminases were elevated, with an alanine aminotransferase (ALT) of 143 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) of 396 U/L, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) raised to 692 U/L.

Investigations:

Due to suspicion of angioedema, multiple investigations were conducted to identify possible etiologies of idiopathic angioedema. The patient’s C3 and C4 complement concentrations were within normal limits, as were both functional and quantitative measurements of C1 esterase.

Anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) testing returned positive, exhibiting a titre of 1:1280 and a speckled pattern. The anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA) assay was negative. A comprehensive myositis antibody panel targeting 16 antigens associated with autoimmune inflammatory myopathies was completed using the Euroimmun Euroline immunoassay. Results showed a strong positive for anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibodies, with a signal intensity of 173 (reference range <35). All other myositis antibodies, including anti-Mi-2 alpha (anti-Mi-2α), anti-Transcription Intermediary Factor-1 gamma (anti-TIF1-γ), anti–Histidyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-Jo-1), and anti–Isoleucyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-OJ), were negative. His immunoglobulin G (IgG), as well as IgG subclasses, were found to be normal.

With regard to immunosuppression screening, the patient was negative for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), hepatitis C core antibody (HBcAb), hepatitis B e antibody (HBeAb), hepatitis C virus (HCV), HIV, varicella zoster virus (VZV) IgG, and cytomegalovirus (CMV) IgG/immunoglobulin M (IgM). The QuantiFERON-TB Gold test was indeterminate; nonetheless, three sputum samples tested negative for acid-fast bacilli for Mycobacterium tuberculosis.

Pulmonary function testing demonstrated a restrictive pattern located outside the thoracic cavity, and there was no notable bronchodilator effect observed. Chest CT imaging identified multiple pulmonary nodules without cavitation, predominantly situated in the middle and lower lung zones; the largest nodule was 15 mm in the right lower lobe. There were also scattered peribronchial consolidations consistent with aspiration pneumonitis, but pulmonary fibrosis was not seen radiologically. Abdominal CT revealed no enlargement of abdomino-pelvic lymph nodes and did not show any evidence of primary or metastatic malignancy within the abdominal or pelvic regions. The video fluoroscopic (barium) swallow assessment detected marked pharyngeal dysphagia, resulting in significant pyriform fossa residue and after-swallow penetration of thin and mildly thick liquids onto the vocal cords.

MRI of the thighs revealed findings compatible with ongoing myositis in both obturator internus and externus muscles, alongside less pronounced inflammatory involvement of the hip adductor muscles, gracilis muscles bilaterally, and the distal iliopsoas muscles on both sides (figure 2). Evidence of active muscle inflammation was corroborated by fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)-PET imaging, demonstrating widespread and atypical FDG uptake across various muscle groups, with maximal intensity observed in the head and neck, both deltoid regions, forearms, chest wall, abdomen, and pelvis (figure 3). No sites of metabolically active disease indicative of malignancy were identified. Serum tumour markers—carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), cancer antigen 19-9 (CA19-9), cancer antigen 125 (CA-125), and prostate-specific antigen (PSA)—were all within normal limits.

Histopathological evaluation of a thigh muscle biopsy demonstrated areas containing moderate numbers of necrotic, vacuolated degenerative, myophagocytosed, and regenerating muscle fibers predominantly in a perifascicular pattern, with scattered perimysial infiltrates composed of dispersed histiocytes (figure 4). Immunohistochemical staining for the membrane attack complex indicated deposition of complement component 5b-9 (C5b-9) in the perifascicular endomysial capillaries, a hallmark of dermatomyositis. The clinical picture was consistent with moderate to severe dermatomyositis positive for SAE1 antibody, accompanied by inflammatory edema, peribronchial consolidation secondary to micro-aspiration, dysphagia, and myositis. Dermatological findings included a positive Holster sign and periungual erythema.

Differential diagnosis:

Given his gradual onset of neck swelling and increased CRP, initial suspicions included Ludwig angina and idiopathic angioedema. Early in the course, muscle weakness—such as difficulty swallowing—was not particularly evident. The differential diagnosis encompassed additional inflammatory myopathies (such as immune-mediated necrotising myopathy and inclusion body myositis), infectious etiologies (including cellulitis/erysipelas involving the neck, deep neck space infections, or bacterial sialadenitis), disorders of the neuromuscular junction like myasthenia gravis, delayed hypersensitivity reactions to medications (for instance, drug rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms), acute contact dermatitis, orofacial granulomatosis, hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis, and superior vena cava syndrome.

Treatment:

Owing to pronounced involvement of the airway musculature, the patient received intravenous methylprednisolone at a daily dose of 1000 mg for 3 days. This was transitioned to a prednisone taper, initiated at 60 mg daily and gradually decreased by 5 mg every 2 to 3 weeks. He was administered an intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG; Gamunex 10%) loading dose of 2 g/kg (total 165 g) spread over 5 days (35 g daily), and then received an additional 5 treatment cycles, each 4 weeks apart, at a cumulative dose of 2 g/kg (165 g) apportioned across these infusions. Mycophenolate mofetil was started at 1 g twice daily. In addition, he received two 1000 mg intravenous doses of rituximab separated by 2 weeks. Methotrexate was omitted because of its association with interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis, as the patient demonstrated abnormal pulmonary function, and this was also reviewed with the patient considering its potential adverse effects. Azathioprine and tacrolimus were avoided since mycophenolate was well-tolerated. For prophylaxis against Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim was commenced initially, but was discontinued after development of haemolytic anaemia (in the absence of glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency), following which the patient tolerated nebulized pentamidine 300 mg every 4 weeks.

He was discharged after an inpatient admission lasting 21 days. Due to persistent dysphagia at the time of discharge, oral medications were delivered via a nasogastric tube.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following a three-week hospital stay and ongoing management, the patient exhibited partial recovery of hip flexor strength and significant reduction in cervical soft tissue swelling, though some swelling persisted at discharge. Due to ongoing dysphagia, the patient required continued nasogastric feeding for an additional two months.

At a follow-up visit three weeks post-discharge, proximal muscle strength had returned to baseline, and serum creatine kinase levels were normal at 37 U/L. During an immunology assessment two months after discharge, further resolution of cervical swelling was noted, with only faint violaceous discoloration on the face and periungual abnormalities detectable on physical examination. Swallowing had improved to the extent that the nasogastric tube could be discontinued and the diet advanced to soft solids. The patient remained on mycophenolate mofetil, monthly infusions of intravenous immunoglobulin, and a gradually decreasing dose of prednisone.

Three months after discharge, the patient’s proximal muscle strength remained stable, chest assessment was normal, and cervical soft tissue swelling had completely subsided. Prednisone therapy was successfully withdrawn at seven months after discharge, at which point his dysphagia had entirely resolved. Repeat pulmonary function testing indicated slight improvement but continued to show a restrictive pattern believed to reflect his baseline physiology (see figure 5a, b). Follow-up chest CT at eleven months revealed nearly complete resolution of prior basal ground-glass opacities and airspace consolidation, findings initially linked to aspiration. One year post-diagnosis, his respiratory status remained unchanged, with preserved exercise tolerance.

Regarding malignancy, the patient reported no constitutional symptoms during follow-up and will be surveilled based on symptoms, physical examination, routine laboratory work, and appropriate imaging, including serial CT scans of the chest. He is scheduled for gastroscopy and colonoscopy to rule out hidden gastrointestinal or colorectal cancers.

The management plan included ongoing IVIG therapy and gradual reduction of mycophenolate dosage over the next six months, contingent on his clinical course. He will continue to be closely monitored to assess whether modifications to his medication regimen are necessary.

Discussion:

We describe a case involving dermatomyositis associated with anti-SAE1 antibodies that presented with marked swelling of the cervical soft tissues. This presentation was initially interpreted as idiopathic angioedema. The consideration of dermatomyositis in the differential, along with a thorough physical exam that identified subtle dermatologic findings, facilitated prompt diagnosis and management.

Anti-SAE1 antibodies target the SUMO activating enzyme, which is structurally akin to ubiquitin and functions in the post-translational sumoylation of multiple proteins. Dermatomyositis patients positive for SAE1 antibodies commonly exhibit skin manifestations, including heliotrope rash, Gottron’s papules, as well as V-neck and shawl signs. A comprehensive review of 215 cases noted the prevalence of dysphagia in 46% and interstitial lung disease in 43% among SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis patients, while malignancies were documented in 18% of cases.

Edema of the subcutaneous tissue, particularly affecting the face or neck, is rarely observed in cases of dermatomyositis. Among a large cohort of 121 individuals diagnosed with dermatomyositis, angioedema occurred in only 8 patients (6.61%). A review of publications from 1985 to 2013 identified just 19 reported cases where dermatomyositis presented with edema. Antibodies such as anti-melanoma differentiation-associated gene (MDA)-5 and anti-transcriptional intermediary factor 1γ (TIF1-γ) have shown a higher association with angioedema compared to other autoantibodies. Facial edema has been noted in several reports, while edema involving the neck has been documented less often. Our analysis of 10 cases involving SAE1-positive dermatomyositis revealed that only three mentioned edema, with just one specifically describing swelling localized to the throat area, suggesting that neck involvement is rare.79

This case highlights for emergency physicians, otolaryngologists, immunologists, and rheumatologists that dermatomyositis should be included in the differential diagnosis for neck swelling, particularly when there is localized 'angioedema' that lacks a clear etiology or appears idiopathic. Clinical features such as dysphagia and other manifestations may be mild, making it essential to search for physical findings of dermatomyositis and to obtain a creatine kinase measurement. In the patient described, subcutaneous edema resulted from muscular inflammation due to myositis, not from angioedema mechanisms related to histamine or bradykinin.

Following the recommendations set by the International Guideline for Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathy-Associated Cancer Screening from the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS), the patient was determined to have two high-risk factors (dermatomyositis and dysphagia) and two intermediate-risk factors (SAE1 antibody positivity and male gender), classifying him as high risk for malignancy. He underwent an evaluation for occult cancer, including CT imaging, assessment of tumor markers, and a PET scan, with further plans for both upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopy. Extensive initial and follow-up malignancy surveillance has been unremarkable to date.

There is a scarcity of data available to determine the best pharmacological treatment for anti-SAE1–positive dermatomyositis. In the largest reported cohort, therapeutic regimens varied widely, with the majority of individuals receiving glucocorticoids alongside several steroid-sparing agents—methotrexate being the most frequently used, followed by mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, and azathioprine; intravenous immunoglobulin was administered selectively. In this case, the patient was managed with steroids, mycophenolate mofetil, intravenous immunoglobulin, and rituximab, resulting in remission. Mycophenolate mofetil was chosen due to the patient's preference for an oral daily medication, its favorable tolerability, and its established role in treating dermatomyositis. Compared to others experiencing substantial tissue oedema associated with dermatomyositis, this patient fortunately exhibits a relatively favorable outcome.
