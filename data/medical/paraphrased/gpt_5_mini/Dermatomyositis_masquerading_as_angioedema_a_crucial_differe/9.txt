Title: Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook

Kevin Chan, Gemma Kwan, Hwei Choo Soh, Louise Evans

Abstract:

Summary: Dermatomyositis is an uncommon autoimmune disorder that most commonly involves the skin, skeletal muscles, and respiratory tract. Rapid initiation of aggressive immunosuppressive therapy is often necessary for affected individuals to prevent disease progression and damage to multiple organ systems. A man in his 40s with no prior medical history presented to the emergency department with pronounced swelling of the neck. Initial evaluation by the ear, nose, and throat team focused on a likely diagnosis of deep neck space infection. Once infection was excluded, he was subsequently referred to clinical immunology to assess and manage suspected idiopathic angioedema. Due to the unusual presentation of neck edema—which did not conform to typical angioedema—and associated dermatological and periungual findings, a creatine kinase measurement was rapidly obtained. Provisional diagnosis of dermatomyositis was made; subsequent testing confirmed the presence of small ubiquitin-like modifier-1 activating enzyme (SAE1) autoantibody and a muscle biopsy demonstrated classic findings. This report emphasizes an atypical manifestation of dermatomyositis characterized by neck swelling not mediated by histamine or bradykinin, as well as dysphagia and marked myopathic features necessitating urgent intervention.

Background:

Dermatomyositis is an idiopathic inflammatory condition primarily affecting the skin and skeletal muscles, but it may also involve organs such as the gastrointestinal tract, joints, lungs, and heart. The disease occurs at a rate of about 10 cases per million individuals. Its occurrence is noted to be bimodal, showing higher incidence among children between 5 and 14 years old and adults aged 45 to 64 years. Distinctive skin manifestations include Gottron’s papules, the shawl sign, heliotrope rash, and periungual telangiectasias. Additional clinical features encompass proximal muscle weakness, interstitial lung involvement, and difficulty swallowing. Pseudoangioedema is rarely seen in dermatomyositis; in one study, only 8 of 121 patients with the condition demonstrated this sign. When localized to the neck, pseudoangioedema may be misinterpreted as a primary disorder of the otolaryngologic system or confused with angioedema due to histamine or bradykinin pathways.

Case presentation:

A man in his 40s presented to the emergency department with a three-week progression of neck swelling and weakness in the proximal muscles of his hip flexors, accompanied by mild muscle pain. He did not have any relevant past medical conditions and had never smoked. He had not used any 3-hydroxy-3-methyl-glutaryl-coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase inhibitors. Ludwig’s angina was initially suspected, and a CT scan of the neck revealed widespread oedema affecting the strap muscles, with inflammation spreading into the retropharyngeal space (see figure 1). He was not febrile, maintained an oxygen saturation of 99% on room air, and exhibited no tachypnoea. Capillaroscopy of his nailfolds revealed microhaemorrhages and avascular regions. At presentation, his white blood cell count was 9.1×10^9/L and C-reactive protein (CRP) was 55.6 mg/L. As the neck swelling persisted without a clear etiology, he was referred to a quaternary care facility with specialized ear, nose, and throat (ENT) surgical services for more detailed assessment. The ENT team conducted a flexible nasendoscopy, which demonstrated no major structural abnormalities in the soft palate, vallecula, larynx, or post-cricoid region, but the procedure was notable for increased secretions and the presence of coughing during the exam.

The on-call immunologist was consulted due to suspicion of angioedema, given the absence of identifiable structural or infectious causes. Upon evaluation, the patient demonstrated widespread facial erythema, including involvement of the nasolabial folds, as well as a diffusely erythematous, swollen, but non-tender neck. He reported that he was not taking any medications, such as angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and there was no known history of angioedema either personally or within the family. Collectively, his clinical features did not fit the typical presentation of either acquired or hereditary angioedema. Comprehensive physical assessment identified mild changes in the nailfolds consistent with periungual erythema, a rash localized to the right lateral thigh, and decreased hip flexor strength measuring 4/5. There was no evidence of Gottron’s papules or heliotrope eruption. Testing revealed that creatine kinase was markedly elevated at 16,184 U/L the following day. Renal parameters were within normal limits and urinary myoglobin was negative. Additionally, the patient exhibited increased transaminase levels with alanine aminotransferase (ALT) at 143 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) at 396 U/L, and a lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) value of 692 U/L.

Investigations:

Because angioedema was initially suspected, a range of investigations was undertaken to identify potential etiologies of spontaneous angioedema. Testing showed normal C3 and C4 complement concentrations, and both C1 esterase inhibitor quantitative and functional assays returned within reference limits.

Anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) testing was positive with a titre of 1:1280, exhibiting a speckled pattern, while anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA) was negative. A myositis antibody panel (Euroimmun Euroline immunoassay), targeting 16 antigens associated with autoimmune inflammatory myopathies, demonstrated a strong positive for anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibodies, with a signal intensity of 173 (reference range <35). No reactivity was identified against other myositis-specific antibodies, including anti-Mi-2 alpha (anti-Mi-2α), anti-Transcription Intermediary Factor-1 gamma (anti-TIF1-γ), anti–Histidyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-Jo-1), and anti–Isoleucyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-OJ). Immunoglobulin G (IgG) and its subclasses were all within normal ranges.

His immunosuppressive risk profile included negative results for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), hepatitis C core antibody (HBcAb), hepatitis B e antibody (HBeAb), hepatitis C virus (HCV) antibody, HIV, varicella zoster virus (VZV) IgG, as well as cytomegalovirus (CMV) IgG and IgM. The QuantiFERON-TB Gold test was indeterminate; however, three sputum cultures for acid-fast bacilli did not detect Mycobacterium tuberculosis.

Pulmonary function testing demonstrated an extrathoracic restrictive defect, without a notable response to bronchodilators. Chest CT identified several non-cavitating pulmonary nodules predominantly in the mid to lower lung fields, with the largest measuring 15 mm in the right lower lobe. Additionally, there were scattered regions of peribronchial consolidation suggestive of aspiration pneumonitis, but there was an absence of imaging features consistent with pulmonary fibrosis. CT imaging of the abdomen did not show abdomino-pelvic lymphadenopathy or signs of primary or metastatic cancer within the abdomen or pelvis. A videofluoroscopic (barium) swallow evaluation revealed marked pharyngeal dysphagia, resulting in significant residue in the pyriform sinuses and post-swallow penetration of thin and mildly thick fluids to the level of the vocal cords.

Magnetic resonance imaging of the thighs demonstrated findings indicative of active myositis within both obturator internus and externus muscles, along with less pronounced involvement affecting the hip adductors, gracilis muscles bilaterally, and the distal segments of both iliopsoas muscles (figure 2). Further evidence of ongoing muscle inflammation was provided by a fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)-positron emission tomography (PET) scan, which revealed widespread abnormal FDG uptake across multiple muscle groups, most prominently observed in the head and neck, both deltoids, forearms, chest wall, abdomen, and pelvic regions (figure 3). No other regions demonstrated metabolically active lesions to raise suspicion for malignancy. Tumor markers such as carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), cancer antigen 19-9 (CA19-9), cancer antigen 125 (CA-125), and prostate-specific antigen (PSA) were all within normal reference ranges.

Thigh muscle biopsy identified areas exhibiting moderate amounts of necrotic, vacuolated, degenerating, myophagocytosed, and regenerating myofibers localized perifascicularly, along with sporadic perimysial inflammation made up of scattered histiocytes (figure 4). Immunohistochemical staining for membrane attack complex revealed deposition of complement component 5b-9 (C5b-9) in endomysial capillaries in perifascicular regions, a finding characteristic of dermatomyositis. Based on these data, the patient was diagnosed with moderate to severe SAE1 antibody–positive dermatomyositis featuring inflammatory edema, peribronchial consolidation due to micro-aspiration, dysphagia, and myositis. Skin findings included a positive Holster sign and periungual erythema.

Differential diagnosis:

Due to his subacute onset of neck swelling accompanied by an increased CRP, initial considerations included Ludwig angina and idiopathic angioedema. Significant muscle weakness, such as difficulty swallowing, was not a main symptom in the early stages. The differential diagnosis also encompassed additional inflammatory myopathies (such as immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy and inclusion body myositis), infectious processes (including neck cellulitis or erysipelas, infections within the deep neck spaces, and bacterial sialadenitis), disorders affecting the neuromuscular junction like myasthenia gravis, delayed hypersensitivity reactions to medications (for example, drug rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms), acute contact dermatitis, orofacial granulomatosis, hypocomplementemic urticarial vasculitis, and superior vena cava syndrome.

Treatment:

Given extensive involvement of airway musculature, the patient received intravenous methylprednisolone at 1000 mg per day for 3 days. Following this, therapy transitioned to an oral prednisone taper, beginning at 60 mg daily with dose reductions of 5 mg every 2 to 3 weeks. Initial immunomodulation included administration of intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG, Gamunex 10%) with a total loading dose of 2 g/kg (165 g in total), delivered over 5 days at 35 g daily. This was succeeded by five additional IVIG cycles, administered at 4-week intervals, with a cumulative dose of 2 g/kg (165 g) divided among the cycles. Mycophenolate mofetil was started at a dose of 1 g twice daily, and the patient also received two 1000 mg intravenous rituximab infusions spaced 2 weeks apart. Methotrexate was avoided because of its link to interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis, particularly considering the patient's abnormal pulmonary function tests and after discussion with the patient regarding the risk profile. Azathioprine and tacrolimus were not chosen since mycophenolate was well tolerated. For prophylaxis against Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim was initially used; however, this was discontinued due to onset of haemolytic anaemia (not associated with glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency), after which nebulized pentamidine at 300 mg every 4 weeks was tolerated.

He was discharged after a 21-day inpatient stay. His oral medications were administered through nasogastric tube as he had persistent dysphagia at the time of discharge.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following three weeks of inpatient management, the patient experienced partial recovery of hip flexor strength and significant reduction in cervical soft tissue edema, though some swelling persisted upon discharge. Dysphagia remained unresolved, leading to ongoing dependence on nasogastric tube feeding for an additional two months.

At a follow-up appointment three weeks post-discharge, proximal muscle strength had completely returned, and serum creatine kinase decreased to 37 U/L. During immunology assessment two months after discharge, further improvement in cervical swelling was noted, with only minimal violaceous erythema over the face and periungual alterations on examination. Swallowing abilities had advanced to permit removal of the nasogastric tube and the initiation of a soft solid diet. The patient continued treatment with mycophenolate mofetil, monthly intravenous immunoglobulin, and a gradually decreasing prednisone regimen.

At 3 months after hospital discharge, the patient's proximal muscle strength remained unchanged, chest auscultation revealed no abnormalities, and the cervical soft tissue swelling had fully resolved. Prednisone was discontinued without issue at 7 months post-discharge, at which time the patient's dysphagia had also completely resolved. Subsequent pulmonary function testing indicated slight improvement, although a persistent restrictive defect was noted and considered to represent the patient's baseline condition (see figure 5a, b). At an 11-month follow-up, chest CT demonstrated almost total resolution of the previously identified basal ground-glass changes and areas of consolidation, which were originally linked to aspiration. One year after the initial diagnosis, the patient’s pulmonary status remained stable, and he reported good exercise tolerance.

In terms of cancer surveillance, he reported no constitutional symptoms at follow-up and will undergo ongoing monitoring with symptom assessment, physical examination, serial laboratory studies, and relevant imaging, including regular CT scans of the chest. Additionally, he is scheduled for both gastroscopy and colonoscopy to rule out hidden gastrointestinal or colorectal cancers.

Plans were established to continue IVIG therapy and begin reducing the dose of mycophenolate over the subsequent 6 months, contingent on the patient's clinical evolution. Ongoing close monitoring was arranged to assess whether adjustments to his medication regimen would be necessary.

Discussion:

We describe a case involving dermatomyositis with anti-SAE1 antibody positivity that manifested as marked swelling of the cervical soft tissues, which was initially misdiagnosed as idiopathic angioedema. Identification of dermatomyositis as a possible diagnosis, along with a thorough clinical examination that uncovered subtle skin findings, enabled a prompt diagnosis and initiation of appropriate therapy.

The anti-SAE1 antibody targets the small-ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) activating enzyme, a protein related to ubiquitin that plays a role in the post-translational modification of proteins via the process of sumoylation. The dermatomyositis subtype associated with SAE1 antibodies generally presents with cutaneous manifestations, including the heliotrope rash, Gottron’s papules, V-neck sign, and shawl sign. According to an extensive literature review of 215 reported cases, dysphagia and interstitial lung disease were observed in 46% and 43% of individuals, respectively, while malignancy was noted in 18% of those with SAE1 dermatomyositis.

Subcutaneous oedema, particularly presenting as facial or cervical swelling, is a rare manifestation in patients with dermatomyositis. In a comprehensive cohort analysis including 121 dermatomyositis cases, angioedema was observed in just 8 individuals (6.61%). Additionally, a literature survey spanning publications from 1985 to 2013 identified only 19 reported instances of oedema linked to dermatomyositis. Certain antibodies, specifically anti-melanoma differentiation-associated gene (MDA)-5 antibody and anti-transcriptional intermediary factor 1γ (TIF1-γ) antibody, appeared to be more frequently related to angioedema compared to other serological markers. While facial oedema was reported in multiple instances, swelling involving the neck was less commonly noted. In our review of 10 documented SAE1-positive dermatomyositis cases, oedema was reported in three patients, with only one case indicating swelling localized to the throat, highlighting the rarity of neck involvement.79

This case highlights the importance for emergency physicians, otolaryngologists, immunologists, and rheumatologists to consider dermatomyositis as a potential differential diagnosis in patients presenting with neck swelling, particularly in cases of localized 'angioedema' that lack an obvious cause or are deemed idiopathic. Because dysphagia and other systemic features may present subtly, it is essential to look for clinical indications of dermatomyositis and obtain a creatine kinase measurement. In this patient, the observed subcutaneous edema was attributed to underlying muscle inflammation associated with myositis, rather than being a result of histaminergic or bradykinin-related angioedema.

Following the recommendations provided by the International Guideline for Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathy-Associated Cancer Screening issued by the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS), the patient was classified as high risk for malignancy, given the presence of two high-risk factors (dermatomyositis, dysphagia) as well as two intermediate risk factors (positive SAE1 antibody, male gender). As part of malignancy evaluation, he underwent imaging with CT scans, assessment of tumor markers, and a PET scan, along with plans to perform upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopies. Thorough baseline and ongoing surveillance have, thus far, continued to be reassuring.

Evidence guiding the choice of pharmacological treatment in anti-SAE1–positive dermatomyositis remains scarce. In the largest cohort reported, therapeutic strategies varied widely; the majority of individuals received glucocorticoids together with various steroid-sparing medications. Methotrexate was most frequently used, though other agents included mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, and azathioprine. Selected cases also involved administration of intravenous immunoglobulin. Our patient was managed with a regimen comprising steroids, mycophenolate mofetil, intravenous immunoglobulin, and rituximab, resulting in remission. The decision to use mycophenolate mofetil was influenced by the patient’s preference for a once-daily oral medication as well as its favorable tolerability and established application in dermatomyositis. Compared to other individuals presenting with pronounced tissue edema due to dermatomyositis, our patient’s prognosis is comparatively favorable.
