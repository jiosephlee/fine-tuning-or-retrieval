Title: Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook

Kevin Chan, Gemma Kwan, Hwei Choo Soh, Louise Evans

Abstract:

Summary: Dermatomyositis is an uncommon autoimmune condition that generally involves the skin, musculature, and pulmonary system. Management often necessitates immediate, high-dose immunosuppressive therapy to limit disease advancement and prevent multi-organ involvement. In this report, a previously healthy man in his 40s came to the emergency department with pronounced cervical swelling. Initial assessment was conducted by otolaryngologists for a suspected deep neck space infection, which was subsequently ruled out. The patient was then evaluated by clinical immunology for possible idiopathic angioedema. Due to the unusual characteristics of the neck edema, which did not fit the pattern of angioedema, along with dermatological and nail manifestations, a creatine kinase test was rapidly ordered. He was provisionally diagnosed with dermatomyositis, later confirmed by a positive small ubiquitin-like modifier-1 activating enzyme (SAE1) autoantibody and characteristic findings on muscle biopsy. This case illustrates non-histaminergic, non-bradykinin-induced neck swelling as an atypical presentation of dermatomyositis, associated with dysphagia and marked myopathy, emphasizing the need for early intervention.

Background:

Dermatomyositis is an idiopathic inflammatory myopathy that primarily affects the skin and muscles but may also involve the gastrointestinal tract, joints, lungs, and heart. The condition occurs at an estimated rate of 10 cases per one million individuals. Its prevalence follows a bimodal pattern, most frequently appearing in children aged 5–14 years and adults between 45–64 years. Typical dermatologic manifestations include Gottron’s papules, shawl sign, heliotrope rash, and periungual telangiectasias. Additional clinical features may comprise proximal muscle weakness, interstitial lung disease, and difficulty swallowing. Pseudoangioedema is rare in dermatomyositis; a study documented its occurrence in only 8 out of 121 patients with the disease. When it involves the neck, it can present as a condition commonly evaluated by otolaryngologists or resemble histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema.

Case presentation:

A man in his 40s came to the emergency department reporting three weeks of gradually worsening neck swelling and weakness in his hip flexor muscles, together with mild muscle aches. He had no past medical conditions and had never smoked. He was not receiving any HMG-CoA reductase inhibitor therapy. Initially, Ludwig’s angina was suspected and a computed tomography scan of the neck was performed, revealing widespread edema within the strap muscles as well as inflammation extending into the retropharyngeal space (figure 1). He exhibited no fever, maintained an oxygen saturation of 99% on room air, and did not have increased respiratory rate. Nailfold capillaroscopy showed proximal microhemorrhages and regions lacking blood vessels at the nailfolds. On admission, his leukocyte count was 9.1×10^9/L and his CRP measured 55.6 mg/L. Because the neck swelling persisted without a clear etiology, he was referred to a quaternary care facility with ENT surgical expertise for additional assessment. ENT conducted a flexible nasendoscopy, which found no major anatomical abnormalities in the soft palate, vallecula, larynx, or post-cricoid region, but there were increased secretions and the patient coughed throughout the examination.

The case was presented to the on-call immunologist due to suspicion of angioedema, given the absence of identifiable structural or infectious etiology. Upon assessment, the patient exhibited diffuse facial erythema, including the nasolabial regions, and an erythematous, swollen, non-tender neck. He stated he was not taking any medications, such as angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and there was no history of angioedema in himself or his family. His overall clinical picture was uncharacteristic of both acquired and hereditary angioedema. Comprehensive physical examination showed discrete nailfold abnormalities consistent with periungual erythema, a rash located on the right lateral thigh, and diminished hip flexor strength rated at 4/5. Neither Gottron’s papules nor heliotrope rash were present. Creatine kinase was measured and found to be markedly increased at 16,184 U/L the following day. Renal function remained within normal limits and urine myoglobin testing was negative. Transaminase levels were raised, with alanine aminotransferase (ALT) at 143 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) at 396 U/L, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) was also elevated at 692 U/L.

Investigations:

Due to initial suspicion of angioedema, a series of investigations were undertaken to identify possible etiologies of spontaneous angioedema. The patient’s C3 and C4 complement concentrations were found to be within normal limits, as were both the functional activity and quantitative levels of C1 esterase.

Testing revealed a positive anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) at a titre of 1:1280 with a speckled staining pattern. Anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA) testing was negative. A myositis antibody panel, comprising 16 antigens associated with autoimmune inflammatory myopathies and conducted using the Euroimmun Euroline immunoassay, demonstrated a strong positivity for anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibodies, with a signal intensity measured at 173 (reference <35). The patient tested negative for other myositis-specific antibodies, including anti-Mi-2 alpha (anti-Mi-2α), anti-Transcription Intermediary Factor-1 gamma (anti-TIF1-γ), anti–Histidyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-Jo-1), and anti–Isoleucyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-OJ). His immunoglobulin G (IgG) and subclass levels were all within standard reference ranges. Screening for immunosuppression revealed negative results for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), hepatitis C core antibody (HBcAb), hepatitis B e antibody (HBeAb), hepatitis C virus (HCV), HIV, varicella zoster virus (VZV) IgG, and cytomegalovirus (CMV) IgG/immunoglobulin M (IgM). The QuantiFERON-TB Gold test yielded an indeterminate result; however, acid-fast bacilli were not detected for Mycobacterium tuberculosis on three separate sputum specimens.

Pulmonary function testing demonstrated an extrathoracic restrictive defect without a notable response to bronchodilators. Chest CT identified multiple non-cavitating nodules within the lungs, predominantly located in the mid to lower zones, with the largest nodule measuring 15 mm in the right lower lobe. There were patchy peribronchial consolidations, suspected to be secondary to aspiration pneumonitis, but no radiological findings consistent with pulmonary fibrosis. Abdominal CT revealed no abdomino-pelvic lymphadenopathy, nor evidence of primary or metastatic cancer within the abdomen or pelvis. A video fluoroscopic (barium) swallow study detected pronounced pharyngeal dysphagia, resulting in significant residue in the pyriform fossae and post-swallow penetration of thin and mildly thick liquids to the level of the vocal cords.

Thigh MRI demonstrated findings indicative of active myositis affecting the bilateral obturator internus and externus muscles, with less pronounced involvement observed in the hip adductors, bilateral gracilis, and bilateral distal iliopsoas muscles (see figure 2). Additional evidence of ongoing muscle inflammation was obtained with fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)-PET imaging, which revealed widespread and abnormal FDG uptake across numerous muscle groups, predominantly located in the head and neck regions, bilateral deltoids, forearms, chest wall, abdomen, and pelvis (refer to figure 3). No hypermetabolic lesions were identified elsewhere to indicate malignancy. Tumor marker evaluation, including carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), cancer antigen 19-9 (CA19-9), cancer antigen 125 (CA-125), and prostate-specific antigen (PSA), yielded results within normal limits.

Histological examination of a thigh muscle specimen identified areas containing moderate numbers of necrotic, vacuolated degenerating, myophagocytosed, and regenerating myofibers predominantly in perifascicular areas, accompanied by scattered perimysial inflammation characterized by dispersed histiocytes (figure 4). Immunohistochemical analysis for membrane attack complex demonstrated deposition of complement component 5b-9 (C5b-9) within perifascicular endomysial capillaries, a finding commonly associated with dermatomyositis. The final diagnosis was moderate to severe dermatomyositis with SAE1 antibody positivity, inflammatory edema, peribronchial consolidation secondary to micro-aspiration, dysphagia, and myositis. Dermatologic findings included a positive Holster sign and periungual erythema.

Differential diagnosis:

Given his gradual onset of neck swelling along with elevated CRP levels, there was initial suspicion for Ludwig angina or idiopathic angioedema. Muscle weakness, including difficulty swallowing, was not a significant aspect in the early stage of his presentation. The differential diagnoses considered included additional inflammatory myopathies (such as immune-mediated necrotising myopathy or inclusion body myositis), infections (including cellulitis or erysipelas of the neck, deep neck space infections, or bacterial sialadenitis), disorders affecting the neuromuscular junction like myasthenia gravis, delayed drug hypersensitivity reactions (for example, drug rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms), acute contact dermatitis, orofacial granulomatosis, hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis, and superior vena cava syndrome.

Treatment:

Given marked involvement of airway muscles, the patient received intravenous methylprednisolone at 1000 mg daily for 3 days. This was subsequently transitioned to a prednisone taper, beginning at 60 mg per day and decreasing by 5 mg every 2 to 3 weeks. He was administered loading dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) (Gamunex 10%) at 2 g/kg (totalling 165 g) over 5 days (35 g daily), after which he underwent 5 additional cycles spaced 4 weeks apart, also at a cumulative dose of 2 g/kg (165 g), divided across these cycles. Initiation of mycophenolate mofetil was at 1 g twice daily, and two doses of rituximab, 1000 mg IV each, were given 2 weeks apart. Methotrexate was excluded from treatment due to its relationship with interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis, considering the patient’s abnormal pulmonary function test results and after discussing side effects with him. Azathioprine and tacrolimus were also not pursued as mycophenolate was tolerated without issue. For prophylaxis against Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim was initially started but discontinued following the onset of haemolytic anaemia (with no underlying glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency); the patient then tolerated nebulised pentamidine 300 mg every 4 weeks.

He was released following a 21-day hospitalisation. Owing to persistent dysphagia at discharge, his oral medications were delivered via nasogastric tube.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following three weeks of inpatient care, the patient exhibited moderate recovery of hip flexor strength and significant reduction of cervical soft tissue swelling, though complete resolution of the swelling had not occurred by discharge. Ongoing dysphagia necessitated continued nasogastric tube nutrition for an additional two months.

At the three-week post-discharge evaluation, strength in proximal muscles was fully restored and serum creatine kinase had decreased to 37 U/L. During immunology follow-up two months after discharge, further improvements in cervical swelling were observed; residual findings included a faint violaceous rash on the face and periungual abnormalities. The patient's swallowing function progressed, permitting discontinuation of the nasogastric tube and transition to a soft solids diet. Ongoing treatment consisted of mycophenolate mofetil, monthly intravenous immunoglobulin, and a gradually decreasing dose of prednisone.

Three months after discharge, the patient’s proximal muscle strength remained stable, chest auscultation was normal, and the cervical soft tissue edema had entirely subsided. Prednisone was discontinued without issue seven months after discharge, at which time dysphagia was fully resolved. Pulmonary function testing revealed slight improvement, though a residual restrictive pattern persisted and was considered consistent with the patient’s baseline physiology (figure 5a, b). An 11-month follow-up chest CT demonstrated near-total resolution of the previous basal ground-glass changes and airspace consolidation, which were originally thought to be secondary to aspiration. Twelve months following diagnosis, respiratory status continued to be stable, with preserved exercise capacity.

Regarding malignancy screening, the patient reported no constitutional symptoms during follow-up and will be monitored through symptom assessment, physical examinations, regular blood work, and periodic imaging including serial CT scans of the chest. Further evaluation will include gastroscopy and colonoscopy to rule out hidden gastrointestinal or colorectal malignancy.

The treatment plan is to maintain IVIG and gradually reduce the dose of mycophenolate over the next six months, contingent upon clinical response. He will be observed carefully to determine if adjustments in pharmacotherapy are necessary.

Discussion:

We describe a patient with dermatomyositis associated with anti-SAE1 antibodies, who presented with marked swelling of cervical soft tissues, which was initially misdiagnosed as idiopathic angioedema. The consideration of dermatomyositis in the differential, together with a thorough physical examination identifying subtle dermatologic manifestations, facilitated an accurate and prompt diagnosis and initiation of therapy.

Anti-SAE1 antibodies are directed at the small ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) activating enzyme, a molecule that shares structural features with ubiquitin and plays a role in the post-translational sumoylation of multiple substrates. Dermatomyositis positive for SAE1 antibodies commonly presents with distinct skin findings including heliotrope rash, Gottron’s papules, V-neck pattern, and shawl sign. According to an extensive review involving 215 individuals, 46% of those with SAE1 antibody dermatomyositis experienced dysphagia, 43% developed interstitial lung disease, and malignancy was noted in 18%.

Edema of the subcutaneous tissue, including swelling of the face or neck, is rarely observed in dermatomyositis. In a sizeable cohort comprising 121 individuals diagnosed with dermatomyositis, angioedema was documented in only 8 patients (6.61%). A review of literature spanning 1985 to 2013 identified just 19 instances where dermatomyositis presented with edema. Certain autoantibodies, such as anti-MDA-5 and anti-TIF1-γ, have been reported to show greater association with angioedema than other antibody types. Although facial swelling has been reported in multiple cases, involvement of the neck is described less often. In our review of 10 cases of dermatomyositis with SAE1 positivity, edema was noted in only three cases, and among these, one referred to swelling near the throat, indicating that edema affecting the neck region is infrequently encountered.79

This case highlights the importance for emergency physicians, ENT specialists, immunologists, and rheumatologists to consider dermatomyositis as a potential differential diagnosis for neck swelling, particularly when presenting as localized ‘angioedema’ without a clear cause or is idiopathic. Clinical features such as dysphagia and other manifestations may be subtle; therefore, a careful assessment for physical signs of dermatomyositis and ordering a creatine kinase test are essential. In this patient, the subcutaneous edema was a result of muscle inflammation attributable to myositis, rather than angioedema mediated by histamine or bradykinin.

Following the International Guideline for Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathy-Associated Cancer Screening developed by the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS), the patient was deemed high risk for malignancy based on two high risk factors—dermatomyositis and dysphagia—and two intermediate risk factors—presence of SAE1 antibody and male sex. As a result, malignancy workup included CT imaging, evaluation of tumor markers, and a PET scan, with additional plans for both upper and lower GI endoscopy. Comprehensive baseline and subsequent interval surveillance have so far been reassuring.

Currently, there is a scarcity of data to inform the best pharmacological management for anti-SAE1–positive dermatomyositis. The largest study available revealed varied treatment strategies, with the majority of patients receiving glucocorticoids alongside several steroid-sparing agents, most frequently methotrexate; other agents included mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, and azathioprine, while intravenous immunoglobulin was administered in selected instances. In this case, the patient was managed with steroids, mycophenolate mofetil, intravenous immunoglobulin, and rituximab, leading to remission. The choice of mycophenolate was influenced by the patient's preference for an oral daily medication, as well as its tolerability and established efficacy in dermatomyositis. Compared to others with notable tissue oedema associated with dermatomyositis, our patient fortunately demonstrates a favourable outlook.
