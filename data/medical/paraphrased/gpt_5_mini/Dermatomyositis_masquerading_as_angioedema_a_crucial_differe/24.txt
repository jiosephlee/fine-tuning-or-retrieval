Title: Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook

Kevin Chan, Gemma Kwan, Hwei Choo Soh, Louise Evans

Abstract:

Dermatomyositis is an uncommon autoimmune disorder primarily impacting the skin, skeletal muscles, and respiratory tract. Management generally necessitates rapid initiation of aggressive immunosuppressive therapy to prevent disease advancement and damage to multiple organ systems. A previously healthy male in his 40s presented to the emergency department with pronounced swelling of the neck. Initial evaluation by otolaryngology suggested a possible deep neck space infection; however, this was excluded. He was subsequently referred to clinical immunology for assessment and management of presumed idiopathic angioedema. Due to the unusual characteristics of the neck oedema, which did not align with angioedema, and the presence of skin and nail abnormalities, a creatine kinase test was ordered. The patient was initially diagnosed with dermatomyositis, later confirmed by the detection of small ubiquitin-like modifier-1 activating enzyme (SAE1) autoantibody and characteristic muscle biopsy findings. This report illustrates a rare form of neck swelling not related to histamine or bradykinin as a presenting symptom of dermatomyositis, featuring dysphagia and marked muscular involvement that necessitated urgent treatment.

Background:

Dermatomyositis is an idiopathic inflammatory myopathy that primarily involves the skin and muscles, although it can also impact the gastrointestinal system, joints, pulmonary tissue, and cardiac structures. Its occurrence is estimated at 10 cases per million individuals. The disease demonstrates a bimodal age distribution, with peak incidence occurring in children between 5 and 14 years of age as well as in adults aged 45 to 64 years. Typical dermatologic manifestations are Gottron’s papules, the shawl sign, heliotrope rash, and periungual telangiectasias. Additional clinical features include proximal muscle weakness, interstitial lung involvement, and difficulties with swallowing. Pseudoangioedema is a rare presentation in dermatomyositis, with one report identifying this feature in only 8 of 121 dermatomyositis patients. When present on the neck, it may mimic an otolaryngologic condition or be confused with angioedema caused by histamine or bradykinin.

Case presentation:

A man in his 40s arrived at the emergency department after experiencing three weeks of progressive swelling in the neck and weakness in his hip flexor muscles, with mild muscle pain. His past medical history was unremarkable and he had never smoked. He was not receiving therapy with a 3-hydroxy-3-methyl-glutaryl-coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase inhibitor. Ludwig’s angina was initially suspected, prompting a CT scan of the neck which revealed widespread oedema affecting the strap muscles, along with inflammation that extended into the retropharyngeal space (figure 1). At presentation, he did not have a fever, was breathing comfortably with 99% oxygen saturation on room air, and was not tachypnoeic. Nailfold capillaroscopy showed proximal microhaemorrhages and avascular areas. Laboratory results included a white cell count of 9.1×10^9/L and a CRP of 55.6 mg/L. Ongoing neck swelling without a clear etiology led to his transfer to a quaternary hospital with ENT surgical capabilities for additional assessment. ENT evaluation using flexible nasendoscopy identified no significant structural issues in the soft palate, vallecula, larynx, or post-cricoid region, although there were notable increased secretions and frequent coughing during the procedure.

The immunologist on call was consulted due to suspected angioedema, as investigations did not identify any obvious structural or infectious cause. On assessment, the patient exhibited diffuse facial erythema, which extended across the nasolabial folds, and his neck appeared erythematous, swollen, yet non-tender. He reported not taking any medications, including angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and had neither a personal nor familial history of angioedema. Taken together, his symptoms did not fit typical presentations of acquired or hereditary angioedema. Detailed examination revealed mild periungual erythema indicative of nailfold changes, a rash on the lateral aspect of his right thigh, and reduced hip flexor strength rated at 4/5. There was no evidence of Gottron’s papules or heliotrope rash. Creatine kinase was measured and found to be markedly elevated at 16,184 U/L the following day. Renal function was intact and his urine myoglobin test was negative. Additionally, his transaminase levels were increased, with an ALT of 143 U/L, AST of 396 U/L, and LDH raised to 692 U/L.

Investigations:

Due to the preliminary suspicion of angioedema, a battery of tests was conducted to identify potential etiologies for spontaneous angioedema. The patient exhibited normal C3 and C4 complement values, as well as normal C1 esterase functional activity and quantitative levels.

Laboratory evaluation revealed a positive anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) with a titre of 1:1280, displaying a speckled pattern. Testing for anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA) yielded negative results. A comprehensive myositis antibody panel, targeting 16 antigens associated with autoimmune inflammatory myopathies, was administered using the Euroimmun Euroline immunoassay. This demonstrated a robust positivity for anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibodies, with a signal intensity of 173 (reference range <35). Results for other myositis-specific antibodies—including anti-Mi-2 alpha (anti-Mi-2α), anti-Transcription Intermediary Factor-1 gamma (anti-TIF1-γ), anti–Histidyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-Jo-1), and anti–Isoleucyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-OJ)—were all negative. Measurement of immunoglobulin G (IgG), encompassing subclasses, was within normal limits.

For the immunosuppression evaluation, serological tests for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), hepatitis C core antibody (HBcAb), hepatitis B e antibody (HBeAb), hepatitis C virus (HCV), HIV, varicella zoster virus (VZV) IgG, and cytomegalovirus (CMV) IgG/immunoglobulin M (IgM) were negative. The QuantiFERON-TB Gold assay returned indeterminate; however, all three sputum samples were negative for acid-fast bacilli (AFB), indicating no evidence of Mycobacterium tuberculosis.

Pulmonary function testing demonstrated a restrictive defect outside the thoracic cavity, with minimal responsiveness to bronchodilators. Chest CT imaging identified several non-cavitating pulmonary nodules predominantly situated in the middle and lower lung zones, with the largest nodule measuring 15 mm in diameter within the right lower lobe. Additionally, patchy peribronchial consolidation was observed, most likely attributable to aspiration pneumonitis, and no radiologic signs of pulmonary fibrosis were detected. Abdominal CT scan showed no abdomino-pelvic lymphadenopathy or any evidence of a primary tumor or metastatic disease within the abdomen or pelvis. Video fluoroscopic (barium) swallowing assessment revealed pronounced pharyngeal dysphagia, resulting in considerable residue within the pyriform fossae as well as penetration of thin and mildly thick fluids past the vocal cords following swallowing.

MRI examination of the thighs revealed findings indicative of ongoing myositis involving the bilateral obturator internus and externus muscles, along with less severe involvement of the hip adductors, gracilis muscles on both sides, and the distal segments of the bilateral iliopsoas muscles (figure 2). Additional evidence of muscle inflammation was provided by a fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)-positron emission tomography (PET) scan, which demonstrated widespread, abnormal FDG uptake across several muscle groups, most notably within the head and neck, bilateral deltoids, forearms, thoracic wall, abdominal region, and pelvis (figure 3). There was an absence of metabolically active lesions suggestive of malignancy elsewhere. Serum tumor markers, including carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), cancer antigen 19-9 (CA19-9), cancer antigen 125 (CA-125), and prostate-specific antigen (PSA), were within normal reference ranges.

Histological assessment of a thigh muscle biopsy identified areas containing moderate numbers of necrotic, vacuolated degenerating, myophagocytosed, and regenerating myofibers in a perifascicular pattern, accompanied by scattered perimysial inflammatory infiltrates composed of dispersed histiocytes (figure 4). Immunohistochemical staining for membrane attack complex confirmed deposition of complement component 5b-9 (C5b-9) within perifascicular endomysial capillaries, a hallmark of dermatomyositis. The patient was ultimately diagnosed with moderate to severe dermatomyositis positive for SAE1 antibody, associated with inflammatory edema, peribronchial consolidation attributed to micro-aspiration, dysphagia, and myositis. Dermatologic findings included a positive Holster sign and periungual erythema.

Differential diagnosis:

Considering his subacute onset of neck swelling accompanied by increased CRP levels, initial suspicions included Ludwig angina and idiopathic angioedema. Early symptoms did not prominently feature muscle weakness or dysphagia. The differential diagnosis encompassed various possibilities, such as alternative inflammatory myopathies (including immune-mediated necrotising myopathy and inclusion body myositis), infectious etiologies (like cellulitis or erysipelas involving the neck, deep neck space infections, and bacterial sialadenitis), neuromuscular junction disorders such as myasthenia gravis, delayed hypersensitivity to medications (for example, drug rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms), acute contact dermatitis, orofacial granulomatosis, hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis, as well as superior vena cava syndrome.

Treatment:

Because of pronounced involvement of the airway muscles, the patient received intravenous methylprednisolone at 1000 mg per day for three days. Following this, he was transitioned to a prednisone taper, initiating at 60 mg daily and reduced by 5 mg every 2 to 3 weeks. Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG, Gamunex 10%) was administered with an initial loading dose of 2 g/kg (total 165 g) over five days (35 g daily), then continued with five additional cycles spaced four weeks apart, at a cumulative dose of 2 g/kg (165 g) divided across these infusions. Mycophenolate mofetil was started at 1 g twice daily, and rituximab was given in two intravenous doses of 1000 mg, two weeks apart. Methotrexate was avoided due to its link with interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis, particularly since the patient demonstrated abnormal pulmonary function tests and after consultation with him regarding possible side effects. Azathioprine and tacrolimus were not pursued, as mycophenolate was tolerated without issue. For prophylaxis against Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim was initiated but discontinued due to haemolytic anaemia (which occurred despite the absence of glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency); he subsequently tolerated inhaled pentamidine at 300 mg every four weeks.

The patient was discharged after a 21-day hospital admission. Due to ongoing dysphagia, oral medications at discharge were administered via nasogastric tube.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following three weeks of inpatient care and therapy, the patient exhibited moderate recovery of hip flexor strength and significant reduction in cervical soft tissue edema, although some swelling persisted upon discharge. Difficulty swallowing remained, requiring ongoing nasogastric feeding for an additional two months.

At three weeks post-discharge, proximal muscle strength had completely returned and serum creatine kinase measured 37 U/L, within normal limits. Two months following discharge, during immunology follow-up, cervical edema showed further improvement, with only mild residual violaceous discoloration of the face and periungual changes observed clinically. Swallowing had progressed adequately to permit nasogastric tube removal and advancement to a soft solid diet. The patient continued receiving mycophenolate mofetil, monthly intravenous immunoglobulin, and a gradually decreasing dose of prednisone.

Three months after hospital discharge, his proximal muscle strength remained unchanged, chest examination revealed no abnormal findings, and the cervical soft tissue swelling had fully subsided. Prednisone was discontinued successfully at seven months post-discharge, at which point his dysphagia had completely resolved. Pulmonary function testing revealed a slight improvement, with a persistent restrictive pattern considered consistent with his underlying physiology (figure 5a, b). At an eleven-month follow-up, chest CT scan showed near-total resolution of the prior basal ground-glass opacities and airspace consolidation, previously thought to be due to aspiration. One year after initial diagnosis, his respiratory status was stable and he maintained good exercise tolerance.

Regarding malignancy surveillance, he reported no constitutional symptoms at follow-up. Monitoring will proceed based on symptom assessment, physical examination, periodic blood testing, and pertinent imaging studies, including serial CT scans of the chest. He is scheduled for gastroscopy and colonoscopy to investigate for possible hidden gastrointestinal or colorectal cancer.

The management plan includes continued intravenous immunoglobulin therapy, with a gradual reduction of mycophenolate dose over the next six months, contingent on clinical progression. Ongoing close monitoring is planned to assess the necessity of any changes to his medication regimen.

Discussion:

We present a case involving dermatomyositis positive for anti-SAE1 antibodies, manifesting as marked cervical soft tissue edema that was initially misdiagnosed as idiopathic angioedema. The possibility of dermatomyositis was considered during the differential diagnosis process, and close physical inspection—identifying faint dermatologic manifestations—facilitated prompt diagnosis and initiation of therapy.

Anti-SAE1 antibodies are directed against the small-ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) activating enzyme, a protein analogous in structure to ubiquitin that mediates the post-translational modification of numerous substrates through ‘sumoylation’. Dermatomyositis cases positive for SAE1 antibodies are classically defined by cutaneous findings, including the heliotrope rash, Gottron’s papules, and both the V-neck and shawl signs. According to a comprehensive literature review including 215 subjects, dysphagia was observed in 46% and interstitial lung disease in 43% of SAE1 dermatomyositis cases, while associated malignancies were noted in 18%.

Subcutaneous oedema, particularly presenting as swelling of the face or neck, is rarely observed in cases of dermatomyositis. A cohort involving 121 individuals diagnosed with dermatomyositis indicated that angioedema occurred in only 8 (6.61%) participants. An examination of published reports from 1985 to 2013 identified just 19 instances of dermatomyositis presenting with oedema. Antibodies such as anti-melanoma differentiation-associated gene (MDA)-5 and anti-transcriptional intermediary factor 1γ (TIF1-γ) were noted to be more frequently linked to angioedema than other antibody types. While facial oedema was noted in several reports, neck oedema was mentioned less often. Our review of 10 cases of SAE1-positive dermatomyositis revealed oedema in only three cases, with just one specifying swelling near the throat, underscoring the rarity of neck involvement.79

This case highlights to emergency physicians, otolaryngologists, immunologists, and rheumatologists that dermatomyositis should be considered among the differential diagnoses for neck swelling, particularly when there is localized "angioedema" that lacks a clear cause or is deemed idiopathic. Clinical manifestations such as dysphagia and involvement of other organ systems may be minimal, emphasizing the importance of evaluating for dermatomyositis physical findings and obtaining a creatine kinase measurement. In this patient, subcutaneous edema resulted from muscular inflammation associated with myositis, not from angioedema mediated by histamine or bradykinin pathways.

According to the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS) International Guideline for Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathy-Associated Cancer Screening, the patient was classified as high risk for malignancy due to two high risk criteria (dermatomyositis, dysphagia) and two intermediate risk features (presence of SAE1 antibodies, male gender). As a result, comprehensive malignancy screening was performed, including computed tomography imaging, tumor marker evaluation, and positron emission tomography, with additional plans for upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopic assessment. Ongoing baseline and periodic monitoring have, to this point, been reassuring.

Data regarding the ideal pharmacological management of anti-SAE1–positive dermatomyositis remain sparse. The largest available cohort demonstrated varied therapeutic strategies; glucocorticoids were typically administered alongside multiple steroid-sparing agents, with methotrexate being the most frequent, followed by mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, and azathioprine. Selected patients also received intravenous immunoglobulin. The patient described here was managed with steroids, mycophenolate mofetil, intravenous immunoglobulin, and rituximab, ultimately achieving remission. The choice of mycophenolate mofetil was influenced by the patient’s preference for an oral daily therapy, its favorable tolerability profile, and its established efficacy in dermatomyositis. Compared to others with substantial tissue oedema associated with dermatomyositis, this patient fortunately exhibits a favorable clinical outlook.
