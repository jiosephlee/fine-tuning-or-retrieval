Title: Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook

Kevin Chan, Gemma Kwan, Hwei Choo Soh, Louise Evans

Abstract:

Dermatomyositis is an uncommon autoimmune disorder primarily involving the skin, muscles, and respiratory tract. Individuals diagnosed with dermatomyositis generally necessitate immediate, aggressive immunosuppressive therapy to minimize progression and prevent damage to multiple organ systems. This report describes a previously healthy man in his 40s who initially presented to the emergency department with marked neck swelling. He was first assessed by ear, nose, and throat surgeons for a suspected deep neck space infection. Once this diagnosis was ruled out, he was referred to clinical immunology for evaluation and management of presumed idiopathic angioedema. Due to the unusual characteristics of his neck oedema—which did not align with typical angioedema—and concurrent skin and nail abnormalities, a creatine kinase measurement was promptly ordered. The patient received a preliminary diagnosis of dermatomyositis, later confirmed by the presence of a positive small ubiquitin-like modifier-1 activating enzyme (SAE1) autoantibody and characteristic muscle biopsy findings. This case illustrates that neck swelling not mediated by histamine or bradykinin represents an atypical manifestation of dermatomyositis, with dysphagia and substantial muscular involvement necessitating urgent intervention.

Background:

Dermatomyositis is an idiopathic inflammatory condition that primarily affects the skin and muscles, though it may also involve the gastrointestinal system, joints, lungs, and heart. Its incidence is estimated at 10 cases per million people. The disease shows a bimodal age distribution, occurring most frequently in children between 5 and 14 years of age and in adults between 45 and 64 years. Distinctive dermatological signs include Gottron’s papules, the shawl sign, heliotrope rash, and periungual telangiectasias. Additional clinical manifestations can encompass proximal muscle weakness, interstitial lung disease, and difficulty swallowing. Pseudoangioedema is a rare presentation in dermatomyositis; one report documented its presence in only 8 out of 121 individuals diagnosed with this condition. When involving the neck, it can mimic primary otolaryngologic disease or be misinterpreted as angioedema due to histaminergic or bradykinin mechanisms.

Case presentation:

A man in his 40s came to the emergency department reporting a three-week duration of gradually worsening neck swelling and proximal weakness in his hip flexors, together with mild muscle aches. His medical background was unremarkable, and he had never smoked. He was not using any HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors. The initial working diagnosis was Ludwig’s angina; a CT scan of the neck revealed widespread edema affecting the strap muscles, with inflammation extending to the retropharyngeal region (figure 1). He did not have a fever, maintained oxygen saturations of 99% while breathing ambient air, and did not exhibit tachypnea. Examination via nailfold capillaroscopy detected proximal microhaemorrhages and areas of avascularity in the nailfolds. Laboratory findings included a white blood cell count of 9.1×10⁹/L and a C-reactive protein level of 55.6 mg/L at the time of admission. Since the neck swelling persisted without an identifiable etiology, he was referred to a quaternary facility specializing in ENT surgery for further assessment. Flexible nasendoscopy carried out by the ENT team showed no significant structural abnormalities in the soft palate, vallecula, larynx, or post-cricoid region; however, the patient exhibited increased secretions and coughing during the procedure.

The case was referred to the on-call immunologist due to suspected angioedema, given the absence of an identifiable structural or infectious etiology. Upon assessment, the patient exhibited diffuse facial erythema, which extended over the nasolabial folds, and had an erythematous, enlarged, non-tender neck. He reported that he was not taking any medications, such as angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and there was no history of angioedema in himself or among his relatives. His presentation did not conform to typical features of either acquired or hereditary angioedema. Comprehensive examination identified mild nailfold abnormalities consistent with periungual erythema, a cutaneous eruption on the right lateral thigh, and diminished hip flexor strength measured at 4/5. Gottron’s papules and a heliotrope rash were not observed. A creatine kinase test was performed, and results the following day showed a marked elevation at 16,184 U/L. His renal function tests were within normal limits and urine myoglobin screen was negative. Additionally, laboratory studies revealed raised transaminases with ALT at 143 U/L, AST at 396 U/L, and an LDH of 692 U/L.

Investigations:

Because of the initial suspicion for angioedema, multiple investigations were carried out to identify potential causes of spontaneous angioedema. Complement levels C3 and C4 were found to be within normal limits, and both function and concentration of C1 esterase were also normal.

Testing for anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) revealed a positive result with a titre of 1:1280 and a speckled pattern. The patient tested negative for anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA). A myositis antibody panel targeting 16 antigens associated with autoimmune inflammatory myopathies was analyzed using the Euroimmun Euroline immunoassay. This showed a marked positivity for anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibodies, with signal intensity measured at 173 (reference value <35). All other myositis-specific antibodies, including anti-Mi-2 alpha (anti-Mi-2α), anti-Transcription Intermediary Factor-1 gamma (anti-TIF1-γ), anti–Histidyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-Jo-1), and anti–Isoleucyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-OJ), were negative. The patient's immunoglobulin G (IgG), as well as its subclasses, were within normal ranges.

Regarding infectious screening pertinent to immunosuppression, results were negative for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), hepatitis C core antibody (HBcAb), hepatitis B e antibody (HBeAb), hepatitis C virus (HCV), HIV, varicella zoster virus (VZV) IgG, and cytomegalovirus (CMV) IgG/immunoglobulin M (IgM). The QuantiFERON-TB Gold test returned indeterminate findings; however, testing for acid-fast bacilli associated with Mycobacterium tuberculosis from three separate sputum specimens was negative.

Pulmonary function testing demonstrated an extrathoracic restrictive defect, without a notable response to bronchodilator administration. Chest CT identified multiple non-cavitating nodules predominantly located in the mid to lower lung fields, the largest being 15 mm in the right lower lobe, as well as scattered areas of peribronchial consolidation consistent with possible aspiration pneumonitis; there were no radiological features suggestive of pulmonary fibrosis. Abdominal CT did not reveal enlarged abdomino-pelvic lymph nodes nor signs of a primary tumor or metastatic disease within the abdomen or pelvis. A video fluoroscopic (barium) swallow evaluation demonstrated marked pharyngeal dysphagia, resulting in significant residue in the pyriform sinuses and post-swallow penetration of both thin and mildly thick liquids to the level of the vocal cords.

Magnetic resonance imaging of the thighs revealed findings indicative of active myositis involving both obturator internus and externus muscles, with less severe inflammation noted in the hip adductors, bilateral gracilis muscles, and bilateral distal iliopsoas muscles (figure 2). Further evidence of ongoing muscle inflammation was provided by fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)-positron emission tomography (PET), which demonstrated widespread and abnormal FDG uptake across several muscle groups, most pronounced in the head and neck, both deltoids, forearms, chest wall, abdomen, and pelvis (figure 3). No metabolically active lesions were observed outside the musculoskeletal system that would suggest the presence of malignancy. Levels of tumour markers such as carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), cancer antigen 19-9 (CA19-9), cancer antigen 125 (CA-125), and prostate-specific antigen (PSA) were within normal ranges.

A biopsy from the thigh muscle identified areas with moderate quantities of necrotic, vacuolated degenerative, myophagocytosed, and regenerating muscle fibers in a perifascicular pattern, along with scattered perimysial inflammation composed of dispersed histiocytes (figure 4). Immunohistochemical analysis for membrane attack complex revealed deposition of complement component 5b-9 (C5b-9) in perifascicular endomysial capillaries, a hallmark of dermatomyositis. The patient was diagnosed with moderate to severe SAE1 antibody positive dermatomyositis characterized by inflammatory edema, peribronchial consolidation due to micro-aspiration, dysphagia, and myositis. Skin involvement consisted of a positive Holster sign and periungual erythema.

Differential diagnosis:

Due to his subacute onset of neck swelling accompanied by increased CRP, initial suspicions included Ludwig angina and idiopathic angioedema. In the early stages, muscle weakness and difficulty swallowing were not significant symptoms. The differential diagnosis considered various possibilities, such as other inflammatory myopathies (including immune-mediated necrotising myopathy and inclusion body myositis), infectious etiologies (such as neck cellulitis/erysipelas, deep neck space infection, and bacterial sialadenitis), neuromuscular junction disorders like myasthenia gravis, delayed hypersensitivity reactions to drugs (for example, drug rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms), acute contact dermatitis, orofacial granulomatosis, hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis, and superior vena cava syndrome.

Treatment:

Because of marked involvement of the airway musculature, the patient received intravenous methylprednisolone at 1000 mg per day for 3 days. This was transitioned to an oral prednisone regimen beginning at 60 mg daily, with a gradual taper of 5 mg every 2 to 3 weeks. A loading course of intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG, Gamunex 10%) was administered, totaling 2 g/kg (165 g) over 5 days (35 g per day), followed by an additional 5 treatment cycles given 4 weeks apart, each with a combined total of 2 g/kg (165 g) divided between those cycles. Mycophenolate mofetil was initiated at 1 g twice daily, and rituximab was given intravenously at 1000 mg, with two doses administered 2 weeks apart. Methotrexate was avoided owing to its known risk of interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis, considering the patient's abnormal pulmonary function and in agreement with the patient after discussion of its potential adverse effects. Azathioprine and tacrolimus were not started, as the patient exhibited good tolerance to mycophenolate. For Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia prophylaxis, sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim was initially prescribed, but discontinued due to onset of haemolytic anaemia (despite the absence of glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency); the patient subsequently tolerated nebulized pentamidine at 300 mg every 4 weeks.

The patient was discharged following a 21-day hospital admission. Due to ongoing dysphagia at discharge, his oral medications were administered via a nasogastric tube.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following three weeks of inpatient care, the patient's hip flexor weakness showed moderate recovery, and there was significant reduction in cervical soft tissue edema, though this had not entirely subsided by discharge. The dysphagia persisted, requiring ongoing nasogastric tube feeding, which continued for an additional two months.

At three weeks post-discharge, the patient’s proximal muscle strength was fully restored and the serum creatine kinase level had decreased to 37 U/L. During the immunology clinic appointment two months after discharge, the cervical swelling was further diminished, with only mild residual violaceous erythema on the face and some periungual changes observed during examination. Swallowing function had improved enough to discontinue the nasogastric tube and start a soft solid diet. The treatment regimen at that time consisted of mycophenolate mofetil, monthly intravenous immunoglobulin, and a tapering dose of prednisone.

Three months after discharge, his proximal muscle strength remained unchanged, chest auscultation revealed no abnormalities, and the cervical soft tissue swelling had resolved entirely. Prednisone was discontinued without complication seven months following discharge, at which point his dysphagia had completely subsided. Subsequent pulmonary function testing demonstrated slight improvement, though a persistent restrictive pattern was believed to reflect his underlying baseline condition (figure 5a, b). An 11-month follow-up chest CT showed almost total resolution of the basal ground-glass opacities and airspace consolidation previously noted, which were initially considered secondary to aspiration. At one year after diagnosis, his respiratory status was stable and he exhibited good exercise capacity.

Regarding malignancy, he reported no constitutional symptoms during follow-up and will continue surveillance based on symptomatology, physical examination, serial blood tests, and pertinent imaging such as repeated chest CT scans. Gastroscopy and colonoscopy are scheduled to rule out any hidden gastrointestinal or colorectal neoplasm.

The care plan includes ongoing IVIG therapy and a gradual reduction of the mycophenolate dose over the next six months, contingent on his clinical trajectory. He will undergo close monitoring to assess whether adjustments to his medication regimen are required.

Discussion:

We describe a patient with dermatomyositis positive for anti-SAE1 antibodies who initially presented with marked swelling of the cervical soft tissues, which was first misdiagnosed as idiopathic angioedema. The diagnosis was clarified following consideration of dermatomyositis in the differential and a detailed physical examination that detected subtle cutaneous manifestations, permitting prompt identification and initiation of appropriate therapy.

Anti-SAE1 antibodies recognize the small-ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) activating enzyme, a protein sharing structural similarities with ubiquitin and involved in the post-translational sumoylation of multiple substrate proteins. Dermatomyositis with SAE1 antibody positivity commonly displays cutaneous signs, such as heliotrope rash, Gottron’s papules, V-neck sign, and shawl sign. According to an extensive literature review of 215 reported cases, dysphagia and interstitial lung disease were observed in 46% and 43% of patients with SAE1 dermatomyositis, respectively, and malignancy was noted in 18%.

Dermatomyositis rarely presents with subcutaneous tissue edema, particularly facial or cervical swelling. Among 121 dermatomyositis patients in a large cohort study, angioedema was observed in just 8 individuals (6.61%). An examination of literature from 1985 to 2013 identified only 19 reported cases where dermatomyositis occurred with edema. The presence of anti-melanoma differentiation-associated gene (MDA-5) antibody and anti-transcriptional intermediary factor 1γ (TIF1-γ) antibody has been linked more frequently to angioedema than other autoantibodies. Several reports described facial edema, whereas neck swelling appeared less commonly in case descriptions. In our review of 10 published cases involving SAE1-positive dermatomyositis, edema was noted in three, and only one referenced swelling near the throat, indicating that neck region involvement remains rare.79

This case highlights for emergency physicians, ENT specialists, immunologists, and rheumatologists that dermatomyositis should be considered among the differential diagnoses for neck swelling, particularly when presenting as localized ‘angioedema’ without a clear cause or classified as idiopathic. Because dysphagia and other manifestations can be subtle, it is essential to evaluate for clinical signs of dermatomyositis and to order a creatine kinase measurement. In this patient, the observed subcutaneous oedema was secondary to muscle inflammation attributable to myositis, rather than resulting from histaminergic or bradykinin-dependent angioedema.

Following the International Guideline for Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathy-Associated Cancer Screening from the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS), the patient possessed two major risk factors (dermatomyositis, dysphagia) and two intermediate risk factors (presence of SAE1 antibody, male sex), which classified him as high risk for malignancy. He proceeded with comprehensive cancer screening, including CT imaging, tumor marker evaluation, and PET scanning, and was scheduled for both upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopies. Extensive initial and interval monitoring has, to this point, provided continued reassurance.

There is a scarcity of data to inform the best pharmacological management of anti-SAE1–positive dermatomyositis. The largest case series published so far revealed variability in therapeutic strategies, with the majority of individuals treated with glucocorticoids alongside various steroid-sparing agents—most frequently methotrexate, but also including mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, and azathioprine; intravenous immunoglobulin was administered in selected instances. The patient described here received steroids, mycophenolate mofetil, intravenous immunoglobulin, and rituximab, resulting in remission. Mycophenolate was chosen, reflecting the patient’s preference for a daily oral medication, as well as its tolerability and established role in dermatomyositis therapy. This patient has fortunately experienced a favorable outcome compared to others presenting with substantial tissue oedema associated with dermatomyositis.
