Title: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Tobias Evason Fredriksen, Burhan-Ud-Din Mian, Szilveszter Dolgos

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late 40s with no prior health issues presented with rapidly worsening sepsis four days after sustaining a dog bite. She developed disseminated intravascular coagulation, pancytopenia, and acute kidney injury that progressed to complete anuria, resulting in reliance on dialysis. Blood cultures identified Capnocytophaga canimorsus bacteremia. While she showed initial improvement following antibiotics and supportive measures, she subsequently experienced abrupt, isolated haemolysis along with ongoing thrombocytopaenia. Laboratory investigations and renal biopsy confirmed thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), leading to a diagnosis of complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS). Haematological remission was attained after plasma exchange and commencement of eculizumab, with partial recovery of renal function. This report demonstrates that C. canimorsus infection can act as a rare precipitating factor for CM-HUS in adults with competent immune systems, and discusses the patient’s diagnostic evaluation, the complexities involved in distinguishing among potential diagnoses, and the multifaceted clinical course and management of TMA secondary to sepsis.

Background:

Capnocytophaga canimorsus is a slow-growing, encapsulated, gram-negative bacillus commonly present in the oropharyngeal flora of dogs and cats. Infections in humans are uncommon, with transmission occurring not only through bites and scratches but also via close contact. The clinical spectrum is broad, encompassing manifestations from isolated meningitis to rapidly progressive sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), multiorgan dysfunction, and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Even though it is generally sensitive to a range of antibiotics, mortality rates can reach up to 30%.

Among Capnocytophaga species, C. canimorsus is most often implicated in serious zoonotic infections in humans. Its demanding growth requirements and slow culture positivity often delay diagnosis, which has led to a growing role for molecular techniques such as real-time nanopore sequencing.

In the current report, infection with C. canimorsus initially manifested as septic shock, marked leukopaenia, and coagulopathy indicative of DIC. While sepsis-related multi-organ failure dominated the initial progression, subsequent onset of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and worsening acute kidney injury (AKI) was indicative of an overlapping TMA. This progression underscores how complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) can be concealed during the acute phases of sepsis and DIC.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late 40s with no significant past medical history and two uneventful previous pregnancies arrived at the emergency department due to a swiftly deteriorating clinical status. Four days before admission, her dog had bitten her upper lip, for which she received a tetanus vaccine as a preventive measure. Although phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) was prescribed to her on the day of the bite, she never commenced the antibiotic because she was unable to pick up her medication. She subsequently developed a high-grade fever (>40 °C), general malaise, and decreased oral intake, and later sought care when she noticed numbness and a change in color in her feet. During assessment in a primary-care emergency setting, she was found to have hypoglycemia, unresponsive to oral glucose and glucagon, reaching a minimum glucose value of 0.6 mmol/L, which necessitated immediate transfer to our emergency department.

Upon admission, the patient was conscious and interactive but exhibited signs of sepsis, including mottling and coolness in her lower limbs. She denied any use of herbal products or other substances, and no family members had experienced comparable symptoms or illness. Initial laboratory evaluation demonstrated profound systemic abnormalities, highlighted by pronounced inflammation, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury, elevated liver enzymes, and evidence of coagulopathy. Arterial blood gas assessment indicated a metabolic acidosis, with a pH measured at 7.27 (normal range 7.35–7.43), alongside an increased lactate concentration of 5.7 mmol/L (normal <2.5 mmol/L). Persistent hypoglycaemia was noted, unresponsive to large amounts of intravenous glucose, and was suspected to be secondary to sepsis. Upon her arrival, the patient demonstrated sufficient urine output; urinalysis revealed proteinuria (2+) and hematuria (3+).

Her range of symptoms—including fever, pancytopenia, coagulopathy accompanied by acute kidney injury (AKI), and lactic acidosis—were considered indicative of disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) secondary to bacteremia and early sepsis (quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) score 1/3). After both clinical and imaging assessments, a surgical etiology for her mottled limbs, such as necrotizing fasciitis, was excluded. Owing to the acute severity of her state, empiric intravenous broad-spectrum piperacillin/tazobactam (Tazocin) was initiated to address pathogens related to dog exposures, including Capnocytophaga species. From the beginning, C. canimorsus was suspected, and this was confirmed seven days later when both aerobic and anaerobic blood cultures grew the organism. The patient was then moved to the intensive care unit (ICU) for more intensive observation.

Given her pancytopenia and coagulation abnormalities, the hematology team was consulted. The differential diagnoses explored included acute leukemia and hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis. Hemolytic uremic syndrome (HUS) was also entertained but ultimately deemed improbable in light of the presence of leukopenia, inflammatory markers, normal haptoglobin, and a non-compensatory reticulocyte count. Flow cytometry analysis was performed and bone marrow biopsy was initially planned; however, results revealed no elevated CD34+ blasts or aberrant phenotypes, so the biopsy was cancelled. The overall clinical scenario was in keeping with sepsis. Meanwhile, supportive transfusions of platelets and fibrinogen were provided as needed while further diagnostics were pending.

Following admission to the ICU, the patient became entirely anuric and experienced a rapid decline in kidney function. Efforts at aggressive volume resuscitation combined with loop diuretics proved ineffective. Subsequently, she developed fluid overload due to septic cardiomyopathy, necessitating the initiation of continuous renal replacement therapy. Testing for autoimmunity—including antinuclear antibodies (ANA) and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA)—returned negative results.

By the third day in the ICU, the patient exhibited widespread cutaneous bleeding with ecchymoses, as well as distal cyanosis and ischemic changes in multiple fingers and toes, though peripheral pulses and sensory function remained intact. These signs were indicative of microthrombotic processes consistent with disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). Even with involvement of multiple organ systems, the patient did not require vasopressors other than stress-dose corticosteroids given early in her septic course. In addition, she was treated with albumin infusions and other blood products.

Following eight days of inpatient care, she was moved to the nephrology unit for further management of her dialysis-dependent acute kidney injury and continued intravenous antibiotics. At the time of transfer, she was haemodynamically stable but exhibited anuria, widespread swelling, and numerous haematomas, while remaining conscious and oriented. Her antibiotic regimen was narrowed to intravenous ampicillin and subsequently switched to oral Apocillin in accordance with blood culture findings.

Upon admission to the nephrology ward, laboratory investigations revealed an improving inflammatory response, a reactive leukocytosis, rising platelet count, and stable haemoglobin levels. Tests of renal function continued to demonstrate elevated nitrogenous waste, but coagulation profiles had returned to normal, suggesting a resolving coagulopathy. Although she showed initial signs of clinical improvement, her condition evolved when she experienced a sudden isolated decline in haemoglobin (from 111 to 68 g/L over six days), without any signs of active bleeding or circulatory compromise. Platelet counts remained consistently low (30–40×10⁹/L), haptoglobin levels dropped to undetectable, and LDH concentrations increased, all supportive of persistent haemolysis (see figure 1).

The direct antiglobulin test (DAT) (also known as the Coombs test) yielded a positive result for IgG, prompting concern for autoimmune haemolytic anaemia. High-dose prednisolone therapy was initiated in conjunction with standard prophylactic measures, and multiple blood transfusions were administered over a two-day period. As haemolysis persisted despite corticosteroid treatment and platelet counts remained persistently low (41–46 × 10⁹/L), additional workup was indicated. Examination of a peripheral blood smear demonstrated 15%–20% schistocytes, indicative of MAHA and suggestive of TMA (see figure 2). The initial positive DAT was most likely a false positive resulting from penicillin exposure or previous transfusions, as subsequent testing returned negative.

Complement assays and ADAMTS13 activity were ordered for evaluation. The locally performed complement measurements (C3 and C4) were within normal ranges, and functional testing of the classical, alternative, and lectin pathways also yielded normal results. ADAMTS13 activity was subsequently found to be 43.5%, which essentially excluded thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), as TTP is generally defined by activity below 10%. During the interim period while these data were pending, plasma exchange (PEX) was begun as a bridging strategy ahead of administration of the initial eculizumab dose.

Following an increase in platelet count to 77×10⁹/L, renal biopsy was undertaken. Histology revealed pronounced acute tubular necrosis, glomerular ischemia with the presence of fibrin thrombi, and occasional crescentic formations, findings all indicative of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

In this instance, the diagnostic features were most evident by the light microscopic findings presented in figure 3. Immunofluorescence and electron microscopy were not included; immunofluorescence failed to detect any immune complex deposits, and electron microscopy did not demonstrate changes consistent with primary glomerular pathology.

Shiga toxin-producing E. coli-associated HUS (STEC-HUS) was ruled out on the basis of a negative stool PCR analysis.

After the second and third plasma exchange (PEX) procedures, the patient experienced a return of spontaneous urine output. The induction phase of eculizumab therapy (900 mg administered intravenously once per week for four weeks) was commenced after the patient received the initial doses of meningococcal vaccines: Menveo (covering serogroups A, C, Y, and W) and Bexsero (serogroup B). Plans were made to administer the second doses of these vaccines as an outpatient in eight weeks. Since vaccination had not occurred at least two weeks prior to the onset of complement inhibition, the patient was placed on prophylactic Apocillin.

For further assessment to exclude hereditary complement-mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), comprehensive complement studies were referred to the Skåne Laboratory at Lund University, Sweden. To ensure patient safety and address practical concerns, three PEX treatments using Octaplas were carried out and a single induction dose of eculizumab was provided prior to obtaining laboratory specimens. Functional complement evaluation revealed a significantly decreased alternative pathway activity (quantitative AH50) at 6%, with normal levels of complement factors H, I, and B. Testing for antifactor H antibodies returned negative.

Outcome and follow-up:

After intensive medical intervention, the patient is currently stable with satisfactory urine production. Her renal function remains impaired, with a serum creatinine of 175 µmol/L, and she is scheduled for follow-up in the nephrology outpatient setting (see figure 4). Maintenance therapy with eculizumab (1200 mg intravenously every two weeks) is ongoing and has been continued for 4 months following induction. Comprehensive genetic analysis of the complement system revealed no pathogenic mutations; these findings were relayed verbally by the reference laboratory, while the official written documentation was still awaited at manuscript submission. Given these results, complement inhibition is being maintained because of the disease’s severity, with plans to reconsider cessation if kidney function shows stabilization. Detection of deleterious complement gene mutations would support prolonged therapy with close observation for recurrence. In this case, however, no such mutations were detected.

Differential diagnosis of TMA

TMAs encompass several overlapping clinical entities.

When adults present with MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI, the foremost concern is to rule out thrombotic TTP, which is characterized by a marked deficiency in ADAMTS13 activity (<10%). Clinical prediction tools such as the PLASMIC score can aid in risk assessment pending laboratory confirmation, which remains definitive. In this case, the patient’s PLASMIC score was 4 out of 7, indicating a low likelihood of TTP, and her measured ADAMTS13 activity was 43.5%, making TTP improbable and informing subsequent evaluation.

Consideration was also given to STEC-HUS. However, PCR testing for Shiga toxin was negative in this patient. Additionally, there was no preceding gastrointestinal illness, and microbiological studies were unrevealing, making STEC-HUS an unlikely diagnosis.

The persistence of hemolysis, presence of schistocytes, requirement for dialysis due to AKI, and renal biopsy findings demonstrating TMA collectively supported a diagnosis of CM-HUS (see figure 5). This conclusion was reinforced by a negative PCR test for STEC and marked improvement following initiation of eculizumab. Her previously unremarkable health history lessens the likelihood of an inherent complement abnormality necessitating an additional trigger, and instead favors CM-HUS precipitated by infection.

CM-HUS is identified clinically and histopathologically as a diagnosis of exclusion, and complement components C3 and C4 may remain within normal limits throughout the illness. It is important to recognize that normal C3 and C4 concentrations do not rule out CM-HUS, since values can stay within the standard range during the disease. Interestingly, haemolysis only developed once the patient’s sepsis began to resolve, indicating that the CM-HUS was likely initiated by infection rather than being a pre-existing condition.

For this patient, C3, C4, and ADAMTS13 activity were measured directly prior to the initial PEX procedure. Additional, more detailed complement analyses were carried out eight days following the last PEX session and four days after the patient received the first eculizumab dose. The diminished alternative pathway activity observed is most plausibly attributed to the effects of these therapies rather than primary complement deficiency or overactivation.

Pathophysiology of CM-HUS: complement dysregulation and infectious triggers

CM-HUS, previously referred to as 'atypical HUS,' is caused by unregulated activation of the alternative complement pathway, typically in individuals with genetic susceptibility. Genetic variants or autoantibodies that impair complement regulatory proteins—such as factors H, I, B, or membrane cofactor protein (CD46)—are found in approximately 50% to 70% of cases. Despite this, the condition shows incomplete penetrance; clinical onset generally necessitates an environmental trigger, with infections, pregnancy, autoimmune conditions, or specific drugs being the most frequent.

Capnocytophaga canimorsus, though seldom documented, has been demonstrated to activate complement pathways, particularly in hosts with underlying susceptibilities. Its susceptibility to complement-mediated killing varies: many isolates are sensitive, yet some capsule-containing strains exhibit partial resistance, underscoring complement as a crucial aspect of immune defense. This results in a clinical dilemma: complement activation is essential for eradicating the organism, yet in predisposed individuals, it can precipitate uncontrolled endothelial injury and thrombotic microangiopathy.

This report further contributes to the limited but increasing evidence associating C. canimorsus with complement-mediated TMAs. Bjørkto et al notably documented a case of C. canimorsus-associated CM-HUS in a patient with a complement factor B variant who responded favorably to eculizumab. Unlike the aforementioned case, our patient did not possess any identified pathogenic or likely pathogenic complement gene alterations, pointing towards infection-induced CM-HUS occurring in the absence of a known inherited complement defect.

Therapeutic strategy and clinical dilemma

Managing CM-HUS during ongoing infection involves distinct difficulties. Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody that inhibits complement component 5, is considered the primary treatment for CM-HUS and leads to significant improvement in both hematological and renal parameters, especially when started promptly. Initiating therapy later correlates with suboptimal renal recovery and consequently an elevated likelihood of progression to end-stage kidney disease.

By contrast, plasma exchange (PEX) is a crucial intervention in TTP, but its impact in CM-HUS is only temporary. Its effectiveness in CM-HUS is primarily through removal of circulating complement components, inflammatory molecules, or autoantibodies, but it does not correct the fundamental complement dysregulation. In our case, urine output resumed following the last sessions of PEX yet prior to administration of eculizumab, indicative of short-lived stabilization. Long-term improvement in hematological and renal status was achieved solely after inhibiting complement activity.

A major therapeutic challenge is administering complement inhibition in the setting of active bacteremia, as terminal complement proteins are vital for protection against encapsulated organisms such as Neisseria and Capnocytophaga, which raises concern over infections linked to eculizumab use. In this patient, eculizumab was given only after blood cultures had cleared, thus reducing the possibility of unchecked infection. Immunization, antibiotic prophylaxis, and vigilant surveillance are critically important when utilizing complement-blocking agents.

Conclusion and broader implications

Timely identification of CM-HUS and early commencement of complement inhibition are critical factors influencing positive outcomes. In this case, the patient attained hematological remission along with partial restoration of renal function, aligning with existing data that underscore the significance of renal status and the interval before starting eculizumab treatment as primary prognostic indicators.

While CM-HUS linked to C. canimorsus is exceedingly uncommon, this report underscores the need to consider infectious etiologies—including atypical pathogens—in individuals who develop new TMA. Greater clinical vigilance and advancements in molecular diagnostics could facilitate the recognition of additional cases.
