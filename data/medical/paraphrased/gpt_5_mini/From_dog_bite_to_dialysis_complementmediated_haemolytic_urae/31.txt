Title: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Tobias Evason Fredriksen, Burhan-Ud-Din Mian, Szilveszter Dolgos

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late 40s with no significant past medical history presented with rapidly worsening sepsis four days after being bitten by a dog. Her clinical course included disseminated intravascular coagulation, pancytopenia, and acute kidney injury, which progressed to complete anuria requiring dialysis. Blood cultures revealed Capnocytophaga canimorsus bacteraemia. Although she initially responded to antibiotics and supportive therapy, she subsequently experienced abrupt isolated haemolysis and ongoing thrombocytopaenia. Laboratory assessment and renal biopsy confirmed thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), and a diagnosis of complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) was established. Treatment with plasma exchange and eculizumab resulted in haematologic remission and partial recovery of renal function. This report illustrates C. canimorsus infection as an uncommon precipitant of CM-HUS in immunocompetent adults and details the patient's diagnostic process, the complexities encountered in differential diagnosis, and the intricate nature of management and therapeutic decision-making in sepsis-associated TMA.

Background:

Capnocytophaga canimorsus is an encapsulated, gram-negative bacillus that grows slowly and is commonly found in the oropharyngeal flora of cats and dogs. In humans, infections are uncommon, and may arise through animal bites, scratches, or even close proximity. The clinical manifestations are diverse, ranging from isolated meningitis to rapid-onset sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), failure of multiple organ systems, and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Although this organism is generally susceptible to a wide range of antibiotics, reported mortality rates may be as high as 30%.

Among the Capnocytophaga species, C. canimorsus is most often implicated in severe zoonotic disease in humans. Due to its demanding growth requirements and the slow pace at which it develops in cultures, identification is frequently delayed, leading to increased reliance on molecular diagnostics, such as real-time nanopore sequencing.

In the present case, infection with C. canimorsus manifested as septic shock with profound leukopenia and coagulopathy indicative of DIC. While sepsis and associated organ dysfunction predominated initially, subsequent findings of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and aggravating acute kidney injury (AKI) were suggestive of an overlaying TMA. This underscores how complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) may be concealed in the early stages by concurrent sepsis and DIC.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late 40s with no significant medical history and two previous uneventful pregnancies came to the emergency department due to rapid clinical deterioration. Four days before admission, she suffered a dog bite on her upper lip and subsequently received a tetanus vaccine as a preventative measure. Phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) was prescribed at the time, but she did not start the antibiotic because she was unable to pick up the medication. She subsequently experienced high fever (>40 °C), general malaise, decreased oral intake, and eventually developed numbness and discoloration in her feet, which prompted her to seek care. At a primary-care emergency unit, she was discovered to be hypoglycemic, unresponsive to both oral glucose and glucagon therapy, with a lowest blood glucose measurement of 0.6 mmol/L, necessitating immediate transfer to our emergency department.

Upon presentation, the patient was conscious and interactive, yet displayed signs of sepsis, including mottling and coolness in the distal portions of both lower limbs. She reported no use of herbal supplements or other substances, and there was no history of similar symptoms among her family members. Initial laboratory evaluations indicated significant multisystem dysfunction, comprising pronounced inflammation, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury (AKI), elevated hepatic transaminases, and evidence of coagulopathy. Table 1 provides a summary of critical findings.

Arterial blood gas assessment demonstrated metabolic acidosis with a pH measured at 7.27 (reference range 7.35–7.43), alongside a lactate concentration of 5.7 mmol/L (normal <2.5 mmol/L). Persistent hypoglycaemia was noted, despite administration of large quantities of intravenous glucose, attributed to underlying sepsis. Upon admission, urinary output remained sufficient; urinalysis detected proteinuria (2+) and hematuria (3+).

Her symptoms, which included fever, pancytopenia, coagulopathy accompanied by acute kidney injury (AKI) and lactic acidosis, were considered indicative of disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) caused by bacteraemia and early sepsis (quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) score 1 out of 3). After both clinical assessment and radiological imaging, potential surgical sources for her mottled extremities, such as necrotising fasciitis, were excluded. In light of the seriousness of her case, intravenous broad-spectrum piperacillin/tazobactam (Tazocin) therapy was initiated to target pathogens commonly associated with dogs, including Capnocytophaga species. C. canimorsus was suspected early on and subsequently confirmed through both aerobic and anaerobic blood culture results obtained seven days later. The patient was transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) for more intensive observation.

Hematology was consulted because of her pancytopenia and coagulopathy. The potential diagnoses considered included acute leukaemia and haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis, while haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS) was also part of the differential but considered less likely given the presence of leukopaenia, increased inflammatory markers, normal haptoglobin, and a lack of reticulocyte compensation. Flow cytometric analysis was undertaken and a bone marrow biopsy was planned; however, the biopsy was cancelled after the results showed no increase in CD34+ blasts or abnormal immunophenotypes. The clinical findings remained most consistent with sepsis. In the interim, the patient received supportive transfusions of platelets and fibrinogen while laboratory results were pending.

Following admission to the ICU, the patient became fully anuric with a swift decline in renal function. Attempts at intensive fluid resuscitation combined with loop diuretics proved ineffective. Subsequently, septic cardiomyopathy developed along with volume overload, necessitating the commencement of continuous renal replacement therapy. Autoimmune screening for antinuclear antibodies (ANA) and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA) returned negative results.

By ICU day 3, she exhibited widespread cutaneous bleeding manifested as ecchymoses, as well as distal cyanosis and ischemia involving multiple toes and fingers; however, peripheral pulses and sensation remained intact. These clinical signs suggested microthrombotic processes associated with DIC. Despite involvement of multiple organ systems, the patient did not require vasopressors except for stress-dose corticosteroids during the acute septic phase. She was administered albumin infusions along with additional blood products.

Following eight days in the hospital, she was relocated to the nephrology unit for continued dialysis-dependent acute kidney injury management and intravenous antibiotics. Upon admission to nephrology, she was hemodynamically stable but remained anuric, exhibited generalized edema and several hematomas, yet was alert and oriented. Based on blood culture findings, her antibiotics were stepped down to intravenous ampicillin, and subsequently changed to oral Apocillin.

Laboratory assessments on the nephrology ward demonstrated an improved inflammatory profile, reactive leukocytosis, recovering platelet counts, and stable hemoglobin levels. Renal function tests indicated sustained azotemia; however, her coagulation studies returned to normal, suggesting her coagulopathy was resolving. Despite initial clinical gains, her status altered with the occurrence of a sudden, isolated decrease in hemoglobin (from 111 to 68 g/L over a six-day period) in the absence of any clinical signs of bleeding or hemodynamic compromise. Platelet counts remained consistently low (30–40×10⁹/L), haptoglobin was depleted, and LDH levels increased, signifying continued hemolysis (figure 1).

A direct antiglobulin test (DAT) (Coombs test) returned positive for IgG, indicating the possibility of autoimmune haemolytic anaemia. The patient began treatment with high-dose prednisolone in addition to routine prophylaxis, and received multiple blood transfusions over a 2-day period. Since corticosteroids did not ameliorate the haemolytic process and platelet counts persisted at 41–46 × 10⁹/L, further assessment was indicated. Examination of the peripheral blood smear showed 15%–20% schistocytes, in keeping with microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA) and suggestive of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) (figure 2). The initial DAT likely yielded a false positive result due to penicillin administration or prior transfusions; repeat DAT testing was negative.

Complement testing and measurement of ADAMTS13 activity were subsequently performed. Results from available conventional complement studies (C3/C4) were within normal range, as were functional tests evaluating classical, alternative, and lectin pathway activity. Shortly thereafter, ADAMTS13 activity was reported as 43.5%, which excluded a diagnosis of thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), given that TTP generally exhibits activity less than 10%. While waiting for these results, plasma exchange (PEX) was commenced to bridge to the first dose of eculizumab.

After the platelet count had risen to 77×10⁹/L, a renal biopsy was conducted, revealing pronounced acute tubular necrosis, ischemic glomeruli containing fibrin thrombi, and some crescent formation, findings in line with thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

The most illustrative diagnostic evidence was provided by the light microscopy findings presented in figure 3. Immunofluorescence and electron microscopy results are not displayed; the former showed an absence of immune complex deposits and the latter did not indicate any signs of primary glomerular disease.

Infection with Shiga toxin-producing E. coli-related hemolytic uremic syndrome (STEC-HUS) was excluded based on a negative stool polymerase chain reaction (PCR) test.

After the second and third plasma exchange (PEX) sessions, the patient began to produce urine spontaneously again. Once the initial doses of meningococcal vaccines—Menveo (covering serogroups A, C, Y, and W) and Bexsero (for serogroup B)—were administered, eculizumab induction therapy was started at 900 mg intravenously per week over a four-week period. The follow-up vaccine doses were scheduled for eight weeks later in an outpatient setting. Because the vaccines had not been given two weeks prior to starting complement inhibition, prophylactic Apocillin was initiated.

To exclude a diagnosis of hereditary complement-mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), comprehensive complement studies were requested from Skåne Laboratory at Lund University, Sweden. For reasons related to logistics and ensuring the patient’s safety, three plasma exchange treatments using Octaplas and one induction dose of eculizumab were administered before blood sampling. Functional complement assessment included quantifying alternative pathway activity (AH50), which was significantly decreased (6%), while levels of complement factors H, I, and B were within normal limits and antifactor H antibodies were absent.

Outcome and follow-up:

After the intensive medical therapy, the patient is clinically well and producing sufficient urine. Renal function remains impaired (serum creatinine 175 µmol/L), and she is scheduled for ongoing monitoring in the nephrology outpatient service (figure 4). She is maintained on eculizumab therapy (1200 mg intravenously every two weeks), four months following the initial induction regimen. Broader genetic analysis of the complement system failed to detect any disease-causing mutations; these findings were relayed orally by the reference lab, and the formal written documentation was still awaited when this report was prepared. In light of these results and the significant severity of her illness, complement inhibition will be sustained, with a plan to re-evaluate cessation once kidney function stabilizes. If pathogenic variants within complement genes had been found, extended treatment and vigilant surveillance for recurrence would have been recommended. In this individual, however, such variants were absent.

Differential diagnosis of TMA

TMAs encompass several overlapping clinical entities.

When adults present with MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI, it is essential to first rule out thrombotic TTP, which is characterized by marked ADAMTS13 deficiency (<10%). Tools such as the PLASMIC clinical score assist in initial risk assessment during the interval before laboratory confirmation; definitive diagnosis relies on laboratory data. The PLASMIC score in this case was 4 out of 7, suggesting a low likelihood of TTP, and the patient’s ADAMTS13 activity measured 43.5%, further reducing suspicion of TTP and informing subsequent evaluation.

STEC-HUS remains a differential to be considered. However, the patient’s Shiga toxin PCR was negative, and no antecedent gastrointestinal symptoms were reported, diminishing the probability of STEC-HUS.

The pattern of ongoing hemolysis, schistocyte formation, dialysis-requiring AKI, and renal biopsy consistent with TMA ultimately pointed to CM-HUS as the diagnosis (figure 5). Additional support for this diagnosis came from the exclusion of STEC-HUS based on PCR results and the patient’s swift improvement following initiation of eculizumab. The lack of significant prior health concerns lessens the chance of an existing complement abnormality that would necessitate a secondary trigger, supporting a model of infection-induced CM-HUS.

CM-HUS is identified clinically and pathologically only after excluding other possibilities, and complement concentrations (C3/C4) can stay within normal limits throughout its progression. Crucially, having normal C3 and C4 levels does not rule out CM-HUS, since these markers may not be affected during the illness. Of note, hemolysis was observed only after sepsis had started to resolve, indicating that the CM-HUS was likely precipitated by infection instead of being previously present.

For this patient, measurements of basic complement components (C3/C4) and ADAMTS13 activity were taken right before initiating the first session of plasma exchange (PEX). More comprehensive complement assays were carried out 8 days following the last PEX and 4 days after the administration of the initial eculizumab dose. The decline observed in alternative pathway activity is best explained by the impact of therapeutic intervention, rather than a result of underlying complement consumption or activation.

Pathophysiology of CM-HUS: complement dysregulation and infectious triggers

Complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS), previously referred to as ‘atypical HUS,’ stems from uncontrolled activation of the alternative complement pathway, frequently in patients with underlying genetic susceptibility. Approximately 50–70% of individuals with CM-HUS possess genetic alterations or autoantibodies against complement regulatory proteins such as factor H, factor I, factor B, or membrane cofactor protein (CD46). Despite this, disease expression is not universal; additional external factors—including infections, pregnancy, autoimmune conditions, or specific drugs—are generally needed to precipitate clinical disease.

Capnocytophaga canimorsus has been infrequently documented to stimulate complement pathways, especially in patients with increased susceptibility. Importantly, C. canimorsus exhibits varied sensitivity to serum complement, with most strains being susceptible, yet certain capsule-bearing isolates showing partial resistance; as a result, complement-mediated immune responses play a crucial role in host protection. This introduces a clinical dilemma: complement activation is beneficial for eliminating the pathogen but can simultaneously provoke unchecked endothelial injury and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) in those at risk.

The present case further contributes to emerging evidence associating C. canimorsus with complement-mediated TMAs. Bjørkto et al notably described a patient with CM-HUS triggered by C. canimorsus, who carried a factor B complement variant and responded to eculizumab therapy. Unlike the case by Bjørkto et al, our patient lacked any identifiable pathogenic or likely pathogenic complement genetic variants, thereby supporting the occurrence of infection-induced CM-HUS in the absence of a known inherited complement disorder.

Therapeutic strategy and clinical dilemma

The treatment of CM-HUS concurrent with active infection is particularly complex. Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody directed against terminal complement protein C5, represents the main therapeutic agent for CM-HUS and has been shown to significantly enhance both haematological and renal outcomes, with the greatest benefits observed when started promptly. If administration is delayed, there is an increased likelihood of partial renal recovery, which heightens the chance of developing end-stage renal disease.

By comparison, plasma exchange (PEX) is crucial for survival in TTP but yields only transient improvements in CM-HUS, primarily through the removal of complement components, inflammatory substances, or autoantibodies from the circulation, rather than addressing the fundamental complement dysregulation. In the patient described, urine output resumed following the last rounds of PEX but before the use of eculizumab, indicating only a brief period of clinical stabilization. It was only following initiation of complement inhibition that lasting haematological and renal improvements were observed.

A major therapeutic concern arises when considering complement inhibition in the presence of ongoing bacteraemia, as terminal components of complement are vital for protection against encapsulated organisms such as Neisseria and Capnocytophaga. This underscores the potential risk of infection with eculizumab therapy. In this instance, administration of eculizumab was deferred until blood cultures confirmed the absence of bacteraemia, thereby reducing the hazard of uncontrolled infection. When using complement inhibitors, measures such as immunization, prophylactic antimicrobials, and vigilant surveillance are mandatory.

Conclusion and broader implications

Timely identification of CM-HUS and rapid commencement of complement inhibition are critical for optimal outcomes. In this case, the patient attained hematologic remission and partial improvement of kidney function, aligning with existing data that indicate renal status and the interval to eculizumab initiation are primary prognostic factors.

While CM-HUS triggered by C. canimorsus is exceedingly uncommon, this report underscores the need to recognize infectious causes—including rare pathogens—in patients with newly diagnosed TMA. Enhanced awareness and the use of molecular diagnostic methods may facilitate the detection of additional similar cases.
