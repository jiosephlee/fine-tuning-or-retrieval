Title: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Tobias Evason Fredriksen, Burhan-Ud-Din Mian, Szilveszter Dolgos

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late 40s with no prior medical issues presented with rapidly worsening sepsis four days after sustaining a dog bite. Her condition was complicated by disseminated intravascular coagulation, pancytopenia, and acute kidney injury, leading to complete loss of urine output and requirement for dialysis. Blood cultures identified Capnocytophaga canimorsus bacteraemia. Although her condition initially improved with antibiotics and supportive measures, she subsequently experienced abrupt onset of isolated haemolysis and ongoing thrombocytopaenia. Laboratory investigations and renal biopsy revealed features consistent with thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), leading to a diagnosis of complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS). Treatment with plasma exchange and the initiation of eculizumab resulted in haematological recovery and partial restoration of renal function. This report illustrates that C. canimorsus infection can act as an uncommon precipitant of CM-HUS in previously healthy adults and describes the patient’s diagnostic evaluation, complexities in differential diagnosis, and the multifaceted clinical progression and management of TMA occurring post-sepsis.

Background:

Capnocytophaga canimorsus is an encapsulated, gram-negative bacillus that grows slowly and constitutes part of the normal oral flora in dogs and cats. Infections in humans are infrequent and may be transmitted not only through bites or scratches but also via close interactions. The clinical spectrum is broad, spanning from isolated meningitis to severe sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), multiple organ dysfunction, and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Although this organism is generally sensitive to numerous antibiotics, the mortality rate can reach up to 30%.

C. canimorsus is the Capnocytophaga species most commonly implicated in invasive zoonoses affecting humans. Its fastidious growth and prolonged incubation in culture can delay diagnosis, leading to greater reliance on molecular technologies, such as real-time nanopore sequencing.

In the case presented, infection with C. canimorsus manifested as septic shock accompanied by pronounced leukopenia and coagulopathy typical of DIC. The initial phase was primarily characterized by sepsis with multiorgan involvement, while subsequent emergence of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and exacerbating acute kidney injury (AKI) indicated an additional TMA process. This underscores the possibility that complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) might remain unrecognized during the early stages of sepsis and DIC.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late 40s, previously in good health and with a history of two uncomplicated pregnancies, presented to the emergency department in a state of rapidly deteriorating health. Four days before admission, she sustained a dog bite on her upper lip and received a tetanus vaccine as preventive care. Phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) was prescribed at the time of the bite, but she did not begin antibiotic therapy because she was unable to obtain the medication. Subsequently, she experienced a high fever exceeding 40 °C, general malaise, and decreased oral intake. She sought care after developing numbness and discoloration in her feet. At a primary-care emergency service, hypoglycaemia was identified, which was resistant to treatment with oral glucose and glucagon, reaching a minimum of 0.6 mmol/L; this led to her being urgently transferred to our emergency department.

Upon arrival, the patient was awake and able to respond, but exhibited signs of sepsis, including mottling and coolness in the lower limbs. She denied the use of herbal products or other substances, and there was no history of similar illness among her family members. Initial laboratory assessments indicated significant systemic disturbance, characterized by pronounced inflammation, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury (AKI), elevated liver enzymes, and coagulopathy. Arterial blood gas demonstrated metabolic acidosis with a pH of 7.27 (reference range: 7.35–7.43) and a lactate concentration of 5.7 mmol/L (reference: <2.5 mmol/L). Despite administration of large quantities of intravenous glucose, she remained hypoglycemic, which was attributed to sepsis. At the time of admission, she had preserved urine output; urinalysis was positive for protein (2+) and blood (3+).

Her symptom profile—fever, pancytopenia, coagulopathy accompanied by acute kidney injury (AKI) and lactic acidosis—suggested disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) as a complication of bacteraemia and early sepsis, with a quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) score of 1 out of 3. Both clinical assessment and imaging ruled out a surgical cause for her mottled extremities, including necrotizing fasciitis. Because of the severity at presentation, intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam (Tazocin) with broad-spectrum activity was initiated to cover pathogens commonly associated with dogs, among them Capnocytophaga species. Suspected C. canimorsus was subsequently confirmed via aerobic and anaerobic blood cultures seven days later. The patient was moved to the intensive care unit (ICU) for enhanced observation.

Haematology was brought in due to her pancytopenia and coagulopathy. Possible diagnoses were acute leukaemia and haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis, with haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS) considered but deemed improbable on account of leukopaenia, raised inflammatory markers, normal haptoglobin, and non-compensatory reticulocyte levels. Flow cytometry was carried out, and a bone marrow biopsy was planned; however, the absence of CD34+ blast proliferation or abnormal phenotypes on flow cytometry led to cancellation of the procedure. Her clinical course was consistent with sepsis. While laboratory investigations were pending, she received supportive transfusions of thrombocytes and fibrinogen.

Following her admission to the ICU, the patient became entirely anuric and her kidney function deteriorated rapidly. Despite vigorous intravenous fluid administration in combination with loop diuretics, there was no clinical improvement. Subsequently, she developed septic cardiomyopathy associated with fluid overload, necessitating the start of continuous renal replacement therapy. Screening for autoimmunity, including antinuclear antibodies (ANA) and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), yielded negative results.

By the third day of her ICU stay, she exhibited widespread cutaneous bleeding, ecchymoses, and acrocyanotic as well as ischemic changes of multiple toes and fingers, yet peripheral pulses and sensory function remained intact. These signs were indicative of microthrombotic events within the framework of disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). Although several organ systems were involved, the patient did not require vasopressors apart from the stress-dose corticosteroids given during the early septic period. She was also administered albumin infusions in addition to other blood components.

Following eight days of hospitalization, the patient was moved to the nephrology unit for further management of dialysis-dependent AKI and to continue intravenous antibiotic treatment. At the time of transfer, she remained clinically stable though anuric, presented with widespread edema and multiple hematomas, but was awake and fully oriented. The antibiotic regimen was narrowed to intravenous ampicillin, and subsequently switched to oral Apocillin in alignment with blood culture findings.

While on the nephrology unit, laboratory assessments indicated a decline in inflammatory markers, a reactive leukocytosis, normalization of platelet counts, and steady hemoglobin values. Renal function remained impaired with ongoing azotemia, but coagulation studies returned to normal, consistent with resolution of coagulopathy. A summary of these laboratory findings during this phase is presented in Table 2.

Despite initial signs of improvement, her condition subsequently deteriorated, marked by a sudden, isolated decline in hemoglobin (from 111 to 68 g/L over six days) without overt bleeding or hemodynamic compromise. Platelet counts remained low (30–40×10⁹/L), haptoglobin was not detectable, and LDH levels increased, all suggestive of sustained hemolysis (see figure 1).

A direct antiglobulin test (DAT) (Coombs test) returned positive for IgG, indicating possible autoimmune haemolytic anaemia. The patient was commenced on high-dose prednisolone together with standard prophylactic measures, and multiple blood transfusions were administered over a 2-day period. Due to ongoing haemolysis unresponsive to steroid treatment and persistent thrombocytopenia (platelet count 41–46 × 10⁹/L), additional investigations were undertaken. Examination of the peripheral blood smear showed 15%–20% schistocytes, consistent with microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA) and indicative of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) (figure 2). The initial positive DAT was probably attributable to penicillin use or recent transfusions, as subsequent testing was negative.

Complement assays and ADAMTS13 activity levels were ordered. Routine complement tests available onsite (C3/C4) were normal, and functional testing of the classical, alternative, and lectin complement pathways demonstrated no abnormalities. ADAMTS13 activity was reported soon thereafter as 43.5%, effectively excluding thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), which is typically associated with activity less than 10%. In the interim, plasma exchange (PEX) was initiated as a temporary measure before the administration of the first dose of eculizumab.

When the platelet count had risen to 77×10⁹/L, a renal biopsy was conducted, revealing marked acute tubular necrosis, ischemic glomeruli containing fibrin thrombi, and scattered crescent formation, findings characteristic of TMA.

The most illustrative diagnostic evidence was provided by the light microscopy findings depicted in figure 3. Immunofluorescence and electron microscopy data are not included, as immunofluorescence showed absence of immune complex deposition and electron microscopy revealed no indications of primary glomerular disease.

Shiga toxin-producing E. coli-associated HUS (STEC-HUS) was excluded due to a negative stool PCR result.

After the second and third sessions of plasma exchange (PEX), the patient resumed spontaneous urine output. Once the first doses of Menveo (covering serogroups A, C, Y, and W) and Bexsero (serogroup B) meningococcal vaccines were administered, eculizumab induction was started at 900 mg intravenously once weekly for four weeks. The follow-up doses of these vaccines were scheduled to occur eight weeks later, on an outpatient basis. Since the vaccines were not provided two weeks before the initiation of complement inhibition, prophylactic Apocillin was administered.

Comprehensive complement analysis to exclude hereditary complement-mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) was submitted to the Skåne Laboratory at Lund University, Sweden. For logistical and patient safety reasons, three Octaplas-based PEX treatments and one eculizumab induction dose were performed prior to sample collection. Functional complement assessment included measurement of the alternative pathway activity (quantitative AH50), which was significantly diminished at 6%, while complement factors H, I, and B remained within normal ranges, and antifactor H antibodies were not detected.

Outcome and follow-up:

After receiving intricate medical care, the patient demonstrates stable overall health and sufficient urine production. However, her renal function remains impaired, as indicated by a serum creatinine of 175 µmol/L, and she will receive ongoing follow-up in the nephrology outpatient setting (figure 4). Eculizumab maintenance therapy continues at a dose of 1200 mg intravenously every two weeks, extending for four months after induction. Comprehensive genetic testing for complement system abnormalities did not detect any disease-causing variants; these findings were relayed verbally by the reference laboratory, with the official written report still awaited at the time of manuscript submission. In light of these findings, complement inhibition therapy is being maintained due to the seriousness of her condition, with plans to reconsider discontinuation if her renal function achieves stability. Discovery of pathogenic complement gene mutations would support prolonged treatment and vigilant monitoring for disease recurrence, but none were present in this case.

Differential diagnosis of TMA

TMAs encompass several overlapping clinical entities.

When adults present with MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI, the foremost concern is ruling out thrombotic TTP, which is characterized by profound ADAMTS13 deficiency (<10%). Clinical scoring systems, such as PLASMIC, assist in risk stratification while laboratory confirmation is pending. In this case, the patient’s PLASMIC score was 4 out of 7, indicating a low likelihood of TTP, and her ADAMTS13 activity measured 43.5%, thus TTP was considered improbable, prompting alternative diagnostic investigation.

Another key diagnosis to consider is STEC-HUS. The patient’s PCR for Shiga toxin returned negative. Additionally, lack of gastrointestinal symptoms preceding the presentation and negative microbiological tests made STEC-HUS unlikely.

In conclusion, the combination of ongoing hemolysis, presence of schistocytes, AKI requiring dialysis, and kidney biopsy findings compatible with TMA collectively favored a diagnosis of CM-HUS (figure 5). This diagnosis was reinforced by PCR-confirmed absence of STEC and the patient's rapid clinical response to eculizumab. The absence of significant prior medical history lessens the possibility of a chronic complement abnormality requiring an additional trigger and instead supports an infection-mediated CM-HUS.

CM-HUS is identified as a clinical and pathological diagnosis reached after other causes have been excluded, and complement component concentrations (C3/C4) may remain within normal limits throughout the illness. Of note, the presence of normal C3 and C4 values does not rule out CM-HUS, since these measurements can stay in the normal range during active disease. Additionally, the timing of haemolysis, which became evident only after the resolution of sepsis, points to CM-HUS precipitated by infection rather than an underlying disorder.

For this case, standard complement assays (C3/C4) and ADAMTS13 activity were measured just prior to starting the first PEX procedure. More comprehensive complement studies were obtained eight days following the last PEX session and four days subsequent to initiation of eculizumab therapy. The decreased activity detected in the alternative complement pathway most likely reflects the effects of therapeutic intervention, as opposed to inherent consumption or activation of complement.

Pathophysiology of CM-HUS: complement dysregulation and infectious triggers

Complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS), formerly referred to as ‘atypical HUS,’ results from inappropriate activation of the alternative complement pathway, frequently in individuals with underlying genetic susceptibility. Approximately 50% to 70% of patients exhibit genetic mutations or autoantibodies against complement regulatory proteins—including factors H, I, B, or membrane cofactor protein (CD46). Nevertheless, the condition exhibits incomplete penetrance, with clinical onset usually necessitating an external precipitating factor, most often infections, pregnancy, autoimmune conditions, or specific pharmacologic agents.

Cases involving Capnocytophaga canimorsus are uncommon, but this organism is capable of engaging complement pathways, particularly in those at increased risk. C. canimorsus is notable for its variable sensitivity to serum complement: while numerous isolates are susceptible to complement, some capsule-producing strains demonstrate partial resistance. Thus, complement function plays a key role in immune defense. However, this protective response poses a clinical dilemma—in vulnerable hosts, complement activation may eradicate the microbe, but can concurrently provoke excessive endothelial injury and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

Our report contributes to limited existing literature associating C. canimorsus infection with complement-driven TMAs. A noteworthy example by Bjørkto et al. described an individual developing CM-HUS following C. canimorsus infection, who possessed a complement factor B alteration and responded well to eculizumab therapy. Unlike this published case, our patient did not have any detectable pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in complement-associated genes, underscoring the possibility of infection-induced CM-HUS occurring in the absence of an identifiable inherited predisposition.

Therapeutic strategy and clinical dilemma

The management of CM-HUS in the presence of ongoing infection poses distinct difficulties. Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody directed at terminal complement component 5, represents the standard therapy for CM-HUS and significantly enhances both haematological and renal outcomes, especially if administered promptly. Starting treatment late correlates with partial renal recovery and therefore an increased likelihood of progression to end-stage renal disease.

PEX, by comparison, is vital in TTP, but offers only temporary improvement in CM-HUS. Its primary benefit arises from removal of circulating complement fragments, inflammatory substances, or autoantibodies, yet it does not address the fundamental dysfunction of the complement system. In this particular case, the patient experienced a return of diuresis following the last PEX treatments but prior to initiation of eculizumab, which demonstrated a brief period of stabilization. Persistent haematological and renal improvement was only achieved following inhibition of complement.

A major therapeutic issue is the use of complement blockade during active bacteraemia, since terminal complement elements play a crucial role in immunity against encapsulated bacteria—including Neisseria and Capnocytophaga—thus creating concerns regarding the heightened risk of infection associated with eculizumab. Here, eculizumab therapy began only after negative blood cultures were obtained, reducing the risk of uncontrolled infection. Measures such as immunization, antimicrobial prophylaxis, and vigilant surveillance remain indispensable for patients receiving complement inhibitors.

Conclusion and broader implications

Timely diagnosis of CM-HUS and rapid commencement of complement inhibition therapy are critical factors influencing positive outcomes. In our case, the patient attained hematological remission and partial restoration of renal function, aligning with existing literature that emphasizes renal status and prompt initiation of eculizumab as key prognostic factors.

While CM-HUS related to C. canimorsus is exceedingly uncommon, this report underscores the necessity of evaluating potential infectious precipitants—including rare etiologies—in individuals presenting with newly developed TMA. Enhanced recognition and use of molecular diagnostic techniques may facilitate the detection of additional cases.
