Title: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Tobias Evason Fredriksen, Burhan-Ud-Din Mian, Szilveszter Dolgos

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late 40s with no prior health issues presented with rapidly advancing sepsis four days after sustaining a dog bite. She subsequently developed disseminated intravascular coagulation, pancytopenia, and acute kidney injury, which progressed to complete anuria and required dialysis. Blood cultures identified Capnocytophaga canimorsus bacteraemia. Although her condition initially stabilized with antibiotic and supportive treatments, she later experienced abrupt isolated haemolysis and ongoing thrombocytopaenia. Laboratory evaluations and renal biopsy confirmed a thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), leading to a diagnosis of complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS). After beginning plasma exchange and eculizumab therapy, she achieved hematologic remission and her renal function showed partial recovery. This report demonstrates that C. canimorsus infection can serve as an uncommon precipitant of CM-HUS in immunocompetent adults, and discusses the diagnostic evaluation, differential diagnostic complexities, and management challenges encountered when TMA arises following sepsis.

Background:

Capnocytophaga canimorsus is an encapsulated, slow-growing, gram-negative bacillus commonly present in the oropharyngeal microbiota of dogs and cats. Infections in humans are uncommon and may be transmitted through animal bites, scratches, or even close proximity. The spectrum of disease ranges widely, including isolated meningitis, severe sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), failure of multiple organs, and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Although the organism responds to a variety of antibiotics, the reported mortality rate can be as high as 30%.

Among Capnocytophaga species, C. canimorsus is most often associated with invasive zoonotic infection in people. Its demanding culture requirements and slow growth can lead to diagnostic delays, increasingly driving reliance on molecular approaches like real-time nanopore sequencing.

In the patient described, infection with C. canimorsus manifested as septic shock accompanied by marked leukopaenia and a coagulopathy characteristic of DIC. The early course was dominated by sepsis affecting multiple organ systems; subsequently, the emergence of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), reduced platelet count, and worsening acute kidney injury (AKI) indicated a coincident TMA, underscoring that complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) may be masked during the initial phases of sepsis and DIC.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late 40s, previously healthy and with a history of two uncomplicated pregnancies, arrived at the emergency department following a swift decline in her condition. Four days before admission, she sustained a dog bite to her upper lip and was given a tetanus vaccine for prophylaxis. Phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) was prescribed at the time of the bite, but she did not start the antibiotic as she was unable to pick up the medication. Subsequently, she experienced a high-grade fever (>40 °C), general malaise, and decreased oral intake. Medical care was sought when she noticed numbness and discoloration in her feet. At an initial emergency visit in primary care, hypoglycaemia was detected, unresponsive to oral glucose and glucagon, with a minimum blood glucose value of 0.6 mmol/L, which necessitated urgent referral to the emergency department.

Upon admission, the patient was awake and engaged, though she exhibited signs of sepsis, including mottled and cool skin on her lower limbs. She reported no use of herbal products or other substances, and there was no history of similar illness among her family members. Initial laboratory assessments demonstrated significant systemic disturbances, including pronounced inflammation, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury (AKI), elevated hepatic enzymes, and evidence of coagulopathy. Arterial blood gas analysis identified metabolic acidosis, with a pH measured at 7.27 (normal range: 7.35–7.43), accompanied by a raised lactate concentration of 5.7 (<2.5 mmol/L). Despite administration of large doses of intravenous glucose, her hypoglycaemia persisted and was attributed to sepsis. On admission, urine output was adequate; urinalysis indicated the presence of protein (2+) and blood (3+).

In light of her symptom complex—which comprised fever, pancytopenia, coagulopathy alongside acute kidney injury (AKI) and lactic acidosis—her clinical status was considered indicative of disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) attributed to bacteraemia and early sepsis (quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) score 1/3). After both clinical assessment and radiological imaging, surgical etiologies for her mottled extremities, such as necrotising fasciitis, were excluded. Owing to the acuity of her symptoms, empiric broad-spectrum intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam (Tazocin) was initiated to target dog-related organisms, including Capnocytophaga spp. Importantly, C. canimorsus was hypothesized as the culprit organism from presentation, with confirmation obtained via both aerobic and anaerobic blood cultures seven days later. She was then moved to the intensive care unit (ICU) for enhanced monitoring.

Considering the observed pancytopenia and coagulopathy, a haematology consult was sought. Differential diagnoses comprised acute leukaemia and haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis. Haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS) was also contemplated, but was deemed less probable given her leukopaenia, elevated inflammatory markers, normal haptoglobin, and absence of compensatory reticulocytosis. Flow cytometry was performed, and plans were made for bone marrow biopsy; nevertheless, normal findings—with neither an increase in CD34+ blasts nor any aberrant phenotypes—resulted in the procedure being withheld. From a clinical standpoint, her presentation was aligned with sepsis. During this period, thrombocyte and fibrinogen replacement therapies were provided as supportive measures while awaiting investigative results.

Following her admission to the intensive care unit, the patient became completely anuric, with renal function deteriorating swiftly. Despite attempts at vigorous fluid resuscitation combined with loop diuretic therapy, there was no improvement. She went on to develop septic cardiomyopathy accompanied by fluid overload, necessitating the commencement of continuous renal replacement therapy. Serological testing for autoimmunity, including antinuclear antibody (ANA) and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA), yielded negative results.

By ICU day 3, she exhibited widespread cutaneous bleeding with ecchymoses, along with cyanotic and ischemic changes involving multiple distal toes and fingers, although pulses and sensory function remained intact. These manifestations were indicative of microthrombotic events occurring as part of disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). Although there was evidence of multiple organ systems being affected, vasopressor therapy was not required apart from stress-dose corticosteroids administered during the early septic period. She additionally received infusions of albumin and other blood products.

Eight days after admission, the patient was moved to the nephrology service for continued management of dialysis-dependent AKI and intravenous antibiotics. At the time of transfer, she remained hemodynamically stable, though anuric, displaying widespread edema and numerous hematomas, but remained alert and oriented. Antibiotic therapy was narrowed to intravenous ampicillin and was subsequently switched to oral Apocillin in response to blood culture findings.

During her stay on the nephrology ward, laboratory data demonstrated an improving inflammatory profile, a reactive leukocytosis, recovering platelet count, and steady hemoglobin values. Renal function tests continued to reveal significant azotemia. Coagulation tests, however, normalized, pointing toward resolution of the previous coagulopathy. Table 2 provides a summary of her laboratory findings at this point.

Despite initial clinical improvement, her condition deteriorated when she experienced a rapid and isolated decline in hemoglobin levels (from 111 to 68 g/L over six days) without signs of active bleeding or hemodynamic compromise. Platelet counts remained consistently low (30–40×10⁹/L), haptoglobin levels were undetectable, and LDH increased, all consistent with persistent hemolysis (see figure 1).

The direct antiglobulin test (DAT, also known as the Coombs test) returned positive for IgG, indicating a possible diagnosis of autoimmune haemolytic anaemia. High-dose prednisolone therapy was begun together with standard prophylactic measures, and multiple blood transfusions were administered over a 2-day period. Since the haemolysis did not improve with corticosteroids and platelet counts remained low (41–46 × 10⁹/L), additional investigations were performed. Examination of a peripheral blood smear showed 15%–20% schistocytes, consistent with microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA) and suggestive of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) (see figure 2). The initial DAT result was likely falsely positive due to exposure to penicillin or previous transfusions; repeat DAT testing yielded a negative result.

Complement levels and ADAMTS13 activity were submitted for evaluation. Locally accessible complement testing (C3/C4) was found to be within reference range, and assessments of classical, alternative, and lectin pathway function were also normal. The ADAMTS13 activity result arrived shortly thereafter at 43.5%, effectively excluding thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), since TTP is characterized by activity below 10%. While these laboratory results were pending, plasma exchange (PEX) was commenced as a temporary measure prior to administration of the initial dose of eculizumab.

When the platelet count rose to 77×10⁹/L, a renal biopsy was undertaken, revealing marked acute tubular necrosis, glomerular ischemia with the presence of fibrin thrombi, and occasional crescent formations, findings that were consistent with thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

The definitive pathological features in this patient were most clearly visualized by light microscopy, as presented in figure 3. Immunofluorescence studies are not depicted, since they showed no deposition of immune complexes, and electron microscopy did not identify evidence of primary glomerular disease.

A stool PCR test was negative, thereby excluding Shiga toxin-producing E. coli-associated hemolytic uremic syndrome (STEC-HUS).

After the second and third plasma exchange (PEX) treatments, the patient began to urinate spontaneously again. Once the initial doses of meningococcal vaccines—Menveo (covering serogroups A, C, Y, and W) and Bexsero (targeting serogroup B)—were given, induction therapy with eculizumab commenced at 900 mg intravenously each week for four weeks. The follow-up vaccine doses were scheduled for 8 weeks later to be administered in the outpatient setting. Since vaccination had not occurred at least two weeks prior to initiating complement inhibition, Apocillin was prescribed as prophylaxis.

In order to exclude a diagnosis of hereditary complement-mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), advanced complement analysis was sent to the Skåne Laboratory at Lund University, Sweden. Due to patient safety and logistical factors, three PEX procedures using Octaplas and one eculizumab induction infusion had already taken place prior to specimen collection. Functional evaluation of the complement system, including alternative pathway activity measured by quantitative AH50, demonstrated a significant reduction (6%). Levels of complement factors H, I, and B were within normal ranges, and testing for antifactor H antibodies was negative.

Outcome and follow-up:

After intensive medical treatment, the patient is now clinically stable and producing sufficient urine. Renal function remains impaired, indicated by a serum creatinine of 175 µmol/L, and follow-up will take place in the nephrology outpatient setting (figure 4). She is maintained on eculizumab, receiving 1200 mg intravenously every 2 weeks, ongoing for four months since initiation. Comprehensive complement genetic analysis did not detect any pathogenic mutations; these findings were verbally relayed by the reference laboratory, while the official written report was still pending at the time of manuscript submission. With this information, complement inhibition is being continued owing to the severity of her presentation, with plans to evaluate withdrawal if kidney function becomes stable. Should pathogenic complement gene variants have been found, prolonged therapy and vigilant monitoring for relapse would be preferred. However, such mutations were not present in this individual.

Differential diagnosis of TMA

TMAs encompass several overlapping clinical entities.

In adults presenting with MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury, the foremost concern is to rule out thrombotic TTP, which is characterized by markedly reduced ADAMTS13 activity (<10%). Clinical scoring systems such as PLASMIC assist in risk stratification pending laboratory confirmation, which remains essential for definitive diagnosis. In this case, the patient had a PLASMIC score of 4 out of 7 (indicating a low likelihood of TTP), and their ADAMTS13 activity was measured at 43.5%, reducing the suspicion for TTP and directing further investigations.

STEC-HUS was also considered, but PCR testing for Shiga toxin was negative. Coupled with the lack of preceding gastrointestinal symptoms and negative microbiological findings, STEC-HUS was deemed unlikely.

Ultimately, the ongoing hemolysis, presence of schistocytes, AKI requiring dialysis, and renal biopsy findings characteristic of TMA collectively favored a diagnosis of CM-HUS (see figure 5). The diagnosis was further supported by negative Shiga toxin PCR and marked clinical improvement following eculizumab therapy. The patient’s minimal medical history does not suggest a chronic complement defect necessitating a ‘second hit,’ but instead supports CM-HUS precipitated by infection.

CM-HUS is diagnosed clinicopathologically by ruling out other conditions, and complement levels (C3 and C4) can be within normal limits throughout the disease. It is important to recognize that normal C3 and C4 values do not rule out CM-HUS, since these parameters may not deviate from reference ranges even during the illness. Interestingly, evidence of haemolysis became apparent only as the patient’s sepsis improved, supporting the notion that the CM-HUS was precipitated by infection, rather than being a chronic process.

In this case, routine complement evaluations (C3 and C4) and measurement of ADAMTS13 activity were performed immediately prior to the initial plasma exchange (PEX) session. Comprehensive complement analyses were carried out eight days after the last PEX session and four days following administration of the first eculizumab dose. The decrease seen in the alternative pathway is likely attributable to therapeutic intervention, as opposed to inherent complement consumption or activation.

Pathophysiology of CM-HUS: complement dysregulation and infectious triggers

Complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS), previously referred to as ‘atypical HUS,’ results from uncontrolled activation of the alternative complement pathway, frequently in individuals with underlying genetic susceptibility. Approximately 50% to 70% of affected patients possess genetic mutations or autoantibodies targeting complement regulatory components, including factors H, I, B, or membrane cofactor protein (CD46). Nonetheless, disease manifestation is incompletely penetrant, with clinical episodes typically precipitated by external factors such as infections, pregnancy, autoimmune disorders, or certain pharmaceuticals.

Capnocytophaga canimorsus, although infrequently documented in this context, can also initiate complement pathway activation, especially in individuals who are particularly vulnerable. This organism demonstrates variable susceptibility to serum complement activity; while many strains are readily neutralized, capsule-expressing variants display some degree of resistance, underscoring the role of complement in the host’s immune defense. This dynamic presents a clinical dilemma: although complement activation is crucial for pathogen elimination, excessive activation may result in unchecked endothelial injury and the development of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) among predisposed hosts.

The present case further contributes to the emerging literature associating C. canimorsus with complement-mediated TMAs. For example, Bjørkto et al detailed a case in which a patient developed CM-HUS related to C. canimorsus infection and carried a complement factor B mutation; this patient responded favorably to eculizumab therapy. Distinct from that report, our patient lacked pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in complement genes, highlighting a scenario of infection-induced CM-HUS in the absence of identifiable hereditary complement abnormalities.

Therapeutic strategy and clinical dilemma

Managing CM-HUS amid ongoing infection poses distinct difficulties. Eculizumab—a monoclonal antibody directed at terminal complement component 5—remains the preferred therapy for CM-HUS and leads to significant improvement in haematologic and renal outcomes, especially when started promptly. Postponing eculizumab is linked to suboptimal renal recovery and an increased likelihood of developing end-stage kidney disease.

By comparison, plasma exchange (PEX) is critical for survival in TTP, but its effect in CM-HUS is transient. PEX acts primarily by eliminating complement fragments, proinflammatory mediators, or autoantibodies circulating in the blood, rather than correcting the fundamental complement defect. In this particular patient, urine output resumed following the final PEX treatments but prior to eculizumab administration, signifying only short-term improvement. Sustained haematologic and renal response was achieved only with complement inhibition.

A major therapeutic issue arises with complement inhibition during an active bloodstream infection, since terminal complement components are essential in combating encapsulated pathogens such as Neisseria and Capnocytophaga, prompting concern over eculizumab-related infectious complications. For this patient, eculizumab was given once blood cultures cleared, thereby reducing the likelihood of uncontrolled infection. When prescribing complement inhibitors, vaccination, antimicrobial prophylaxis, and vigilant monitoring remain vital.

Conclusion and broader implications

Timely identification of CM-HUS and early commencement of complement inhibition are key factors for achieving positive outcomes. The patient described attained hematologic remission and showed partial recovery of renal function, in line with existing data indicating that both kidney status and promptness in starting eculizumab are critical prognostic indicators.

While CM-HUS linked to C. canimorsus is exceedingly uncommon, this report underscores the need to consider infectious causes—even rare ones—in individuals presenting with new TMA. Advances in awareness and molecular testing may facilitate detection of additional cases.
