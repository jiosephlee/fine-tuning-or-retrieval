Title: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Tobias Evason Fredriksen, Burhan-Ud-Din Mian, Szilveszter Dolgos

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late 40s with no prior health issues presented with rapidly worsening sepsis four days after sustaining a dog bite. She subsequently developed disseminated intravascular coagulation, pancytopenia, and acute kidney injury, which progressed to complete anuria requiring dialysis. Blood cultures identified Capnocytophaga canimorsus bacteraemia. Although there was initial clinical improvement with antibiotics and supportive measures, she experienced abrupt isolated haemolysis and ongoing thrombocytopaenia. Diagnostic workup, including laboratory studies and renal biopsy, confirmed thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), leading to a diagnosis of complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS). The initiation of plasma exchange and eculizumab treatment resulted in haematologic remission and partial recovery of renal function. This report underscores that C. canimorsus infection can be an uncommon precipitant of CM-HUS in otherwise healthy adults and details the diagnostic evaluation, challenges with differential diagnosis, and the complexities involved in managing TMA post-sepsis.

Background:

Capnocytophaga canimorsus is an encapsulated, gram-negative bacillus that grows slowly and is typically present within the oropharyngeal flora of dogs and cats. Although infections in humans are uncommon, transmission may occur through bites, scratches, or close proximity to animals. Clinical manifestations range widely, including isolated cases of meningitis as well as severe presentations with fulminant sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), multiorgan dysfunction, and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Even though this organism is generally sensitive to many antibiotics, the associated mortality rate can reach up to 30%.

Among the Capnocytophaga genus, C. canimorsus is most commonly implicated in invasive zoonotic disease in people. Diagnostic delays are frequent due to its demanding growth requirements and slow culture rate, which has led to greater reliance on molecular diagnostics such as real-time nanopore sequencing.

In the case described, infection with C. canimorsus resulted in septic shock, marked leukopaenia, and coagulopathy characteristic of DIC. While sepsis and multiorgan dysfunction predominated initially, subsequent findings of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and progressive acute kidney injury (AKI) indicated an additional TMA process. This case illustrates that complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) may be masked by the early stages of sepsis and DIC.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late 40s, previously in good health and with a history of two uneventful pregnancies, arrived at the emergency department with rapidly deteriorating symptoms. Four days earlier, she had sustained a dog bite to her upper lip and received a tetanus vaccine as preventative care. She was prescribed phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) at that time but was unable to begin antibiotic therapy as she did not obtain the prescription. Subsequently, she developed a high-grade fever exceeding 40 °C, general malaise, and decreased oral intake. She eventually presented for medical evaluation after noticing numbness and discoloration in her feet. At an urgent primary care visit, she was found to be hypoglycaemic, unresponsive to oral glucose and glucagon, with a recorded lowest blood glucose level of 0.6 mmol/L, necessitating immediate referral to our emergency department.

Upon admission, the patient was conscious and able to respond, but showed signs consistent with sepsis, including mottling and coolness in the lower limbs. She denied use of herbal medications or other substances, and there was no family history of similar illness or symptoms. Initial laboratory evaluation demonstrated significant systemic abnormalities, with pronounced inflammation, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury, elevated liver enzymes, and coagulation disturbances. Arterial blood gas measurement indicated metabolic acidosis, with a pH of 7.27 (reference range 7.35–7.43) and a raised lactate concentration of 5.7 (<2.5 mmol/L). Despite receiving large quantities of intravenous glucose, persistent hypoglycaemia was noted, which was attributed to her septic state. Upon presentation, her urine output was preserved; urinalysis revealed proteinuria (2+) and hematuria (3+).

Her combination of symptoms—including fever, pancytopenia, coagulopathy along with acute kidney injury (AKI) and lactic acidosis—was interpreted as disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) resulting from bacteraemia and early sepsis (quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) score 1/3). Following clinical and radiological assessment, a surgical etiology for her mottled extremities, such as necrotising fasciitis, was excluded. Considering the severity of her initial presentation, empirical intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam (Tazocin) was initiated for coverage of pathogens associated with canine exposure, including Capnocytophaga species. C. canimorsus was suspected early and was subsequently confirmed seven days later by aerobic and anaerobic blood cultures. She was later admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) for enhanced monitoring.

In light of her pancytopenia and coagulopathy, the hematology service was consulted. The differential diagnosis included acute leukemia and haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis. Haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS) was also considered, but was deemed less likely due to the presence of leukopaenia, elevated inflammatory markers, normal haptoglobin, and non-compensatory reticulocytes. Flow cytometry was performed and a bone marrow biopsy was scheduled; however, normal findings—without increased CD34+ blasts or abnormal phenotypes—prompted cancellation of the planned biopsy. Clinically, the presentation aligned with sepsis. While awaiting definitive diagnostic results, supportive transfusions of platelets and fibrinogen were given.

Upon admission to the ICU, the patient became completely anuric, with her renal function deteriorating rapidly. Attempts at vigorous fluid replacement coupled with loop diuretics were unsuccessful. Subsequently, she developed septic cardiomyopathy complicated by volume overload, necessitating continuous renal replacement therapy. Testing for autoimmune markers, including antinuclear antibodies (ANA) and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), yielded negative results.

By ICU day 3, she exhibited widespread cutaneous bleeding, ecchymoses, and ischemic, cyanotic changes on multiple distal toes and fingers, while maintaining intact pulses and sensation. These observations suggested microthrombotic activity within the setting of disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). Even with multisystem organ involvement, she did not require vasopressor therapy, aside from the administration of stress-dose corticosteroids during early sepsis. The patient was also treated with albumin infusions and various other blood products.

After eight days of hospital admission, she was moved to the nephrology service due to continued dialysis-dependent acute kidney injury and the need for intravenous antibiotics. Upon arrival, she was hemodynamically stable but remained anuric, exhibited generalized edema, and had multiple hematomas, though she was awake and oriented. Based on blood culture findings, her antibiotic regimen was reduced to intravenous ampicillin, which was subsequently switched to oral Apocillin.

During her stay in nephrology, laboratory assessments demonstrated a downtrending inflammatory response, a reactive leukocytosis, platelet count recovery, and stable hemoglobin levels. Renal parameters continued to show elevated nitrogenous waste products, while coagulation studies normalized, reflecting improvement of her coagulopathy. Despite initial clinical improvement, her status worsened when she experienced an abrupt, isolated hemoglobin decline (from 111 to 68 g/L over six days), without overt bleeding or signs of cardiovascular compromise. Thrombocytopenia persisted with platelet counts between 30 and 40 × 10⁹/L. Haptoglobin dropped to undetectable levels and LDH increased, all consistent with ongoing hemolysis (see figure 1).

The direct antiglobulin (Coombs) test was positive for IgG, indicating a possible diagnosis of autoimmune haemolytic anaemia. High-dose prednisolone was initiated along with standard prophylactic measures, and multiple blood transfusions were administered over a 2-day period. Since the haemolysis did not improve with corticosteroids and thrombocytopenia persisted (platelets 41–46 × 10⁹/L), additional diagnostic workup was pursued. Examination of a peripheral blood smear showed 15%–20% schistocytes, supporting the presence of MAHA and implicating TMA (see figure 2). The initial positive DAT was likely falsely elevated due to either penicillin exposure or recent transfusions; repeat testing returned negative results.

Further testing included complement studies and ADAMTS13 activity. Basic complement assays (C3/C4) and functional analyses of the classical, alternative, and lectin pathways were all unremarkable. The ADAMTS13 activity came back at 43.5%, which ruled out TTP, as values below 10% are generally diagnostic for TTP. While pending these results, plasma exchange (PEX) was commenced as interim management before the administration of the first eculizumab dose.

After the patient's platelet count rose to 77×10⁹/L, a renal biopsy was conducted, which revealed marked acute tubular necrosis, ischaemic changes in glomeruli with fibrin thrombi, and occasional crescent formations, findings indicative of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

Immunofluorescence and electron microscopy data are not presented, since immunofluorescence showed no immune complex deposition, and electron microscopy did not demonstrate signs of a primary glomerulopathy.

Shiga toxin-producing E. coli-associated hemolytic uremic syndrome (STEC-HUS) was excluded due to a negative stool PCR assay.

After the patient underwent the second and third sessions of plasma exchange (PEX), spontaneous urine output resumed. Once the initial doses of meningococcal vaccines—Menveo (covering serogroups A, C, Y, and W) and Bexsero (targeting serogroup B)—had been administered, eculizumab induction therapy was started at a dose of 900 mg intravenously weekly for four weeks. The follow-up vaccine doses were scheduled for eight weeks later, to be given in the outpatient setting. As the vaccines had not been provided at least two weeks prior to starting complement inhibition, prophylactic Apocillin was prescribed.

Comprehensive complement analysis to exclude hereditary complement-mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) was ordered from the Skåne Laboratory at Lund University in Sweden. For reasons related to logistics and patient safety, three Octaplas-based PEX sessions were carried out and a single induction dose of eculizumab administered before specimen collection. Functional evaluation of the complement system encompassed measurement of alternative pathway activity (quantitative AH50), which was significantly decreased to 6%, while levels of complement factors H, I, and B remained within the normal range and testing for antifactor H antibodies yielded negative results.

Outcome and follow-up:

After receiving extensive medical treatment, the patient is clinically stable with satisfactory urinary output. Renal function remains compromised, as evidenced by a serum creatinine level of 175 µmol/L, and she is scheduled for follow-up in the nephrology outpatient department (figure 4). Eculizumab therapy is ongoing at a maintenance dose of 1200 mg intravenously every two weeks, continuing for four months after induction. Comprehensive genetic analysis of complement-related genes revealed no pathogenic mutations; this information was provided orally by the reference laboratory, with the official written report still awaited at the time of submission. Given the severity of her condition, complement inhibition is being maintained, with a reassessment planned if her renal function stabilizes to determine whether therapy can be discontinued. Detection of a pathogenic complement gene alteration would support prolonged treatment and vigilant relapse surveillance, but no such variants were found in her case.

Differential diagnosis of TMA

TMAs encompass several overlapping clinical entities.

The foremost concern in adults presenting with MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI is to rule out thrombotic TTP, which is characterized by pronounced ADAMTS13 deficiency (<10%). While tools such as the PLASMIC score assist in clinical stratification during the wait for laboratory confirmation, final diagnosis relies on definitive lab results. In this case, the patient had a PLASMIC score of 4 out of 7, reflecting a low likelihood of TTP, and her ADAMTS13 activity measured at 43.5%, effectively discounting TTP and informing the direction for further investigations.

STEC-HUS was also considered, but Shiga toxin PCR was negative in this patient. The lack of preceding gastrointestinal symptoms, combined with negative microbiological tests, makes STEC-HUS improbable.

In conclusion, the combination of ongoing hemolysis, presence of schistocytes, requirement for dialysis due to AKI, and renal biopsy findings compatible with TMA, substantiated a diagnosis of CM-HUS (figure 5). The exclusion of STEC based on PCR results and marked clinical improvement following initiation of eculizumab provided additional support. The absence of significant medical history diminishes the likelihood of an underlying complement abnormality requiring a ‘second hit,’ reinforcing the probability of an infection-induced CM-HUS.

CM-HUS is diagnosed based on clinicopathological criteria after other causes have been ruled out, and complement concentrations (C3/C4) may remain normal throughout the course of the illness. It is important to recognize that normal C3/C4 values do not exclude CM-HUS, since these levels can stay within normal limits during the disease process. Of note, the onset of haemolysis coincided with improvement in sepsis, implying that CM-HUS was precipitated by infection rather than being a pre-existing condition.

For our patient, measurements of basic complement components (C3/C4) and ADAMTS13 activity were performed immediately prior to the initial PEX treatment. Comprehensive complement testing was completed 8 days following the last PEX procedure and 4 days after the first administration of eculizumab. The decrease detected in the alternative pathway is most likely attributable to therapeutic intervention, as opposed to inherent depletion or activation of complement.

Pathophysiology of CM-HUS: complement dysregulation and infectious triggers

Complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS), formerly known as 'atypical HUS,' results from abnormal activation of the alternative complement pathway, usually in individuals with a genetic predisposition. Approximately 50% to 70% of affected patients possess genetic variants or autoantibodies targeting complement regulatory proteins, such as factor H, factor I, factor B, or membrane cofactor protein (CD46). Nevertheless, the disease does not always manifest, and its onset typically depends on external factors, most frequently infection, pregnancy, autoimmune conditions, or specific medications.

C. canimorsus, while seldom documented, has the capacity to stimulate complement pathways, particularly in individuals who are more susceptible. Importantly, this organism demonstrates varying levels of sensitivity to serum complement; most isolates are susceptible, but some capsule-bearing strains show partial resistance. As a result, complement-mediated immunity plays a critical role in defense against C. canimorsus. This produces a clinical dilemma: although complement activation facilitates pathogen elimination, it may concurrently precipitate unchecked endothelial injury and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) in predisposed hosts.

The current case expands the limited but increasing evidence associating C. canimorsus with complement-driven TMAs. Bjørkto et al presented a noteworthy example involving a patient who developed CM-HUS following C. canimorsus infection, carrying a complement factor B variant, and who responded positively to eculizumab treatment. Unlike this previously documented case, our patient did not display pathogenic or likely pathogenic genetic alterations in complement-related genes, thereby highlighting CM-HUS triggered solely by infection in the absence of proven inherited complement defects.

Therapeutic strategy and clinical dilemma

Managing CM-HUS amidst ongoing infection entails distinct difficulties. Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody that inhibits terminal complement component 5, is considered the treatment of choice for CM-HUS and significantly enhances hematological and renal outcomes, particularly when started promptly. A delay in administration correlates with partial renal recovery and increases the likelihood of progressing to end-stage renal disease.

PEX, conversely, is vital in TTP management but offers only short-lived improvement in CM-HUS by removing circulating complement fragments, inflammatory factors, or autoantibodies, without addressing the fundamental defect in complement regulation. In our case, diuretic activity resumed following the final PEX sessions, before eculizumab administration, indicating only a brief period of stabilization. Lasting hematologic and renal improvement occurred exclusively after complement inhibition was established.

An important therapeutic concern is whether to implement complement blockade during active bacteremia, as terminal complement activity is essential for protection against encapsulated organisms such as Neisseria and Capnocytophaga, thereby increasing the risk for infections associated with eculizumab. For this patient, eculizumab was given after blood cultures turned negative, reducing the potential for uncontrolled infection. Use of vaccinations, antimicrobial prophylaxis, and vigilant observation are crucial when administering complement inhibitors.

Conclusion and broader implications

Timely identification of CM-HUS and the rapid start of complement inhibition remain critical factors for optimal outcomes. In this case, the patient attained hematological remission and partial improvement in renal function, in alignment with existing data indicating that renal status and the interval to initiation of eculizumab treatment are primary prognostic indicators.

While CM-HUS related to C. canimorsus is very uncommon, this report underscores the necessity of evaluating infectious causes—even rare pathogens—in individuals presenting with acute TMA. Increased vigilance and the use of molecular diagnostic tools may facilitate the recognition of additional similar instances.
