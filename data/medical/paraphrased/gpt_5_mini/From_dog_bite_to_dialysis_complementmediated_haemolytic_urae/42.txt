Title: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Tobias Evason Fredriksen, Burhan-Ud-Din Mian, Szilveszter Dolgos

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late 40s with no prior medical issues presented with rapidly worsening sepsis four days after sustaining a dog bite. She experienced disseminated intravascular coagulation, pancytopenia, and acute kidney injury, which progressed to complete anuria and dependence on dialysis. Capnocytophaga canimorsus was identified in her blood cultures. Although she initially responded to antibiotic therapy and supportive measures, she subsequently developed sudden, isolated haemolysis and ongoing thrombocytopaenia. Laboratory assessments and renal biopsy confirmed thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), leading to a diagnosis of complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS). Hematological improvement was seen following plasma exchange and initiation of eculizumab, and partial recovery of renal function occurred. This report emphasizes C. canimorsus infection as an unusual cause of CM-HUS in otherwise healthy adults and details the patient’s diagnostic pathway, the challenges involved in distinguishing among differential diagnoses, and the intricacies of both her clinical progression and treatment strategy for TMA arising after sepsis.

Background:

Capnocytophaga canimorsus is a slow-growing, encapsulated, gram-negative bacillus commonly present in the oropharyngeal flora of cats and dogs. Infections in humans are uncommon and can be transmitted through bites, scratches, or even close physical exposure. The clinical spectrum is diverse, spanning from isolated meningitis to rapidly progressive sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), multiple organ dysfunction, and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Although this organism is generally sensitive to a wide array of antibiotics, the associated mortality rate can reach 30%.

Among Capnocytophaga species, C. canimorsus is most frequently implicated in invasive zoonotic infections affecting humans. Due to its demanding growth requirements and prolonged culture times, diagnostic confirmation is often delayed, which has led to increased reliance on molecular diagnostics, such as real-time nanopore sequencing.

In the current case, infection with C. canimorsus manifested as septic shock accompanied by marked leukopaenia and coagulopathy characteristic of DIC. While the early phase of illness was primarily defined by sepsis-induced multiorgan dysfunction, subsequent onset of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and worsening acute kidney injury (AKI) indicated the presence of a concomitant TMA. This progression underscores the potential for complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) to be masked by the initial presentation of sepsis and DIC.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late 40s, previously in good health and with a history of two uneventful pregnancies, arrived at the emergency department with acute and rapidly deteriorating symptoms. Four days before her admission, she sustained a dog bite to her upper lip and was given tetanus vaccine as a preventive measure. She was prescribed phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) at the time of injury but was unable to initiate the antibiotic as the prescription was not collected. Subsequently, she experienced a high-grade fever (>40 °C), generalized malaise, and decreased oral intake, ultimately seeking medical care after developing numbness and discoloration in her feet. Upon assessment at a primary-care emergency service, she was noted to be hypoglycemic, unresponsive to both oral glucose and glucagon, with a lowest recorded blood glucose of 0.6 mmol/L. This led to her urgent transfer to our emergency department.

Upon admission, the patient was conscious and communicative, but exhibited signs of sepsis, including mottled and cool skin on her lower limbs. She reported no intake of herbal products or other substances, and there was no history of similar symptoms among family members. Initial laboratory investigations demonstrated significant systemic dysfunction, characterized by intense inflammation, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury (AKI), elevated liver enzymes, and evidence of coagulopathy. Table 1 provides a summary of the primary findings.

Arterial blood gas analysis showed a metabolic acidosis with a pH of 7.27 (reference range: 7.35–7.43), and lactate was increased at 5.7 mmol/L (normal: <2.5 mmol/L). Despite administration of large amounts of intravenous glucose, she continued to experience hypoglycaemia, presumed secondary to sepsis. On admission, her urine output was sufficient; urinalysis revealed proteinuria (2+) and hematuria (3+).

Based on her range of symptoms—including fever, pancytopenia, coagulopathy accompanied by acute kidney injury (AKI), and lactic acidosis—the clinical assessment pointed towards disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) arising from bacteraemia and early sepsis (qSOFA score of 1 out of 3). After clinical and imaging workup, surgical etiologies for her mottled limbs, such as necrotising fasciitis, were excluded. Owing to the critical nature of her case, empirical treatment with intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam (Tazocin) was initiated to provide coverage against pathogens associated with dogs, including Capnocytophaga species. It is worth noting that C. canimorsus was suspected early and subsequently confirmed via both aerobic and anaerobic blood cultures seven days later. She was then moved to the intensive care unit (ICU) for more intensive observation.

Due to the combined pancytopenia and coagulopathy, haematology specialists were brought into the case. Differential considerations comprised acute leukaemia as well as haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis. Haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS) was also evaluated, but deemed less probable in light of leukopaenia, elevated inflammatory parameters, normal haptoglobin, and unremarkable reticulocyte counts. Flow cytometric analysis was conducted and a bone marrow biopsy was scheduled; however, in light of typical findings—absence of increased CD34+ blasts or abnormal cell phenotypes—the biopsy was discontinued. The clinical picture ultimately aligned with sepsis. During the diagnostic workup, supportive transfusions of thrombocytes and fibrinogen were administered.

Following admission to the ICU, the patient exhibited complete anuria accompanied by a swift decline in kidney function. Despite vigorous fluid resuscitation efforts and simultaneous use of loop diuretics, there was no improvement. In due course, she manifested septic cardiomyopathy associated with significant fluid accumulation, necessitating the commencement of continuous renal replacement therapy. Serologic evaluations for autoimmune etiologies, including antinuclear antibodies (ANA) and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), returned negative results.

By the third day of her ICU stay, she experienced widespread cutaneous bleeding, ecchymosis, and distal cyanosis and ischemia involving multiple toes and fingers; nevertheless, peripheral pulses and sensory function remained intact. These clinical features aligned with microthrombotic processes typical of disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). Although several organ systems were affected, she did not require vasopressors apart from the stress-dose corticosteroids given early during sepsis. The therapeutic regimen also included infusions of albumin as well as various blood products.

Following eight days of hospitalisation, the patient was moved to the nephrology unit due to ongoing dialysis-dependent acute kidney injury (AKI) and continued need for intravenous antibiotics. At the time of transfer, she was hemodynamically stable but remained anuric, exhibited generalized edema, and presented with multiple hematomas, though she was awake and oriented. In response to blood culture findings, antibiotic therapy was stepped down to intravenous ampicillin and subsequently to oral Apocillin.

While on the nephrology service, laboratory assessments revealed a decreasing inflammatory response, reactive leukocytosis, platelet recovery, and stable hemoglobin concentrations. Renal function markers demonstrated continued azotaemia; however, coagulation studies returned to within normal limits, consistent with resolving coagulopathy. Subsequently, after an initial period of improvement, her condition worsened with the acute onset of an isolated hemoglobin decline (from 111 to 68 g/L over six days) in the absence of overt bleeding or signs of hemodynamic compromise. Thrombocytopenia persisted (platelet count between 30–40×10⁹/L), haptoglobin levels became undetectable, and LDH increased, collectively indicative of ongoing haemolysis (see figure 1).

The direct antiglobulin test (DAT), also known as the Coombs test, returned positive for IgG, leading to suspicion of autoimmune haemolytic anaemia. High-dose prednisolone therapy was commenced in addition to standard prophylactic measures, and multiple transfusions of blood were administered over the course of two days. As there was no clinical improvement in the haemolysis with corticosteroids and platelet counts remained low (41–46 × 10⁹/L), further diagnostic work-up was indicated. Examination of the peripheral blood smear showed 15%–20% schistocytes, indicating microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA) suggestive of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) (figure 2). The initial DAT result was probably a false positive attributable either to penicillin use or preceding transfusions; repeat DAT was negative.

Complement panel and ADAMTS13 activity testing were ordered. Standard local complement levels (C3 and C4) were found to be normal, as were the functional assays assessing the classical, alternative, and lectin complement pathways. Subsequently, ADAMTS13 activity measured at 43.5%, essentially excluding thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), since TTP is generally associated with activity less than 10%. While awaiting these diagnostic results, plasma exchange (PEX) was implemented as a temporary measure before the administration of the initial eculizumab dose.

After the platelet count rose to 77×10⁹/L, a renal biopsy was conducted, revealing marked acute tubular necrosis, ischemic glomeruli containing fibrin thrombi, and some crescent formation—findings indicative of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

The most informative diagnostic features in this instance were those seen on light microscopy, as illustrated in figure 3. Results from immunofluorescence and electron microscopy are omitted; immunofluorescence was negative for immune complex deposition, while electron microscopy did not demonstrate primary glomerular disease.

Stool PCR testing was negative, thereby excluding Shiga toxin-producing E. coli-associated hemolytic uremic syndrome (STEC-HUS).

After the second and third plasma exchange (PEX) treatments, the patient resumed spontaneous urination. Eculizumab induction therapy was commenced following administration of the initial doses of meningococcal vaccines—Menveo (targeting serogroups A, C, Y, and W) and Bexsero (for serogroup B)—at a dose of 900 mg intravenously weekly for four weeks. The subsequent vaccine doses were scheduled to be administered eight weeks later in the outpatient setting. Since the vaccines had not been given at least two weeks prior to the initiation of complement inhibition, prophylactic Apocillin was prescribed.

Comprehensive complement analysis to exclude hereditary complement-mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) was performed at Skåne Laboratory, Lund University, Sweden. For logistical reasons and to ensure patient safety, three sessions of PEX using Octaplas and one eculizumab induction dose were administered before obtaining the samples. The complement function panel included quantitative measurement of alternative pathway activity (AH50), which showed a significant reduction (6%), whereas levels of complement factors H, I, and B were within normal range, and antifactor H antibody testing was negative.

Outcome and follow-up:

After undergoing intensive medical treatment, the patient maintains stable overall health with sufficient urine production. Her renal function remains impaired, as indicated by a serum creatinine level of 175 µmol/L, and ongoing evaluation in the nephrology outpatient clinic is scheduled (see figure 4). Eculizumab therapy is being continued at a maintenance dose of 1200 mg intravenously every two weeks, extending for four months after induction. Comprehensive complement genetic analysis revealed no pathogenic mutations; the laboratory conveyed these findings verbally, with the official written documentation pending submission. Given the disease’s severity, complement inhibition therapy is being sustained, with reassessment planned if her kidney function stabilizes to determine possible cessation. Discovery of complement gene pathogenic variants would support prolonged treatment with vigilant surveillance for recurrence, yet no such mutations were detected in this case.

Differential diagnosis of TMA

TMAs encompass several overlapping clinical entities.

When adults present with MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI, the foremost concern is to rule out thrombotic TTP, which is characterized by a marked reduction in ADAMTS13 activity (<10%). Clinical tools such as the PLASMIC score can assist in initial assessment while waiting for laboratory confirmation, which ultimately establishes the diagnosis. The patient had a PLASMIC score of 4 out of 7, indicating a low likelihood of TTP, with ADAMTS13 activity measured at 43.5%, thus TTP was considered improbable and further evaluation was pursued.

Consideration of STEC-HUS is also crucial in such cases. The patient's PCR testing for Shiga toxin was negative. Additionally, the absence of preceding gastrointestinal symptoms and the negative microbiology results make STEC-HUS unlikely.

In the end, the combination of ongoing haemolysis, presence of schistocytes, AKI requiring dialysis, and renal biopsy demonstrating features consistent with TMA led to the diagnosis of CM-HUS (figure 5). This was further corroborated by a negative PCR for STEC and marked clinical improvement following initiation of eculizumab. The lack of significant prior medical history decreases the probability of an underlying complement defect needing a ‘second hit,’ pointing instead towards infection-induced CM-HUS.

CM-HUS is identified as a clinicopathological diagnosis that requires ruling out other conditions, and C3/C4 complement levels may remain normal throughout the illness. It is crucial to understand that normal C3 and C4 values do not rule out CM-HUS, since they may stay within normal limits during the disease. Of particular note, hemolysis developed only after the patient's sepsis started resolving, indicating that the CM-HUS was likely triggered by infection, as opposed to being an underlying chronic condition.

For this patient, standard complement assessments (C3/C4) and ADAMTS13 activity measurements were conducted immediately prior to the first plasma exchange (PEX). Comprehensive complement analysis occurred eight days following the final PEX session and four days after the initial administration of eculizumab. The decrease in alternative pathway activity observed is most likely attributable to the effects of treatment, rather than to inherent complement deficiency or activation.

Pathophysiology of CM-HUS: complement dysregulation and infectious triggers

Complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS), formerly known as ‘atypical HUS’, develops as a result of inappropriate activation of the alternative complement pathway, predominantly in individuals with a genetic susceptibility. Genetic variants or autoantibodies impacting complement regulatory proteins—such as factors H, I, B, or membrane cofactor protein (CD46)—are identified in 50–70% of cases. Nevertheless, the condition does not display full penetrance, and clinical disease typically emerges only in the presence of a precipitating factor, including infections, pregnancy, autoimmune processes, or exposure to certain medications.

While rarely documented, C. canimorsus is capable of activating complement cascades, especially in vulnerable individuals. Importantly, C. canimorsus demonstrates variable sensitivity to serum, as many isolates are complement sensitive but some capsule-positive strains are more resistant, underscoring the importance of complement-mediated host defense mechanisms. This creates a clinical dilemma in which the complement system simultaneously eradicates the organism but may provoke unchecked endothelial injury and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) in predisposed patients.

The present case contributes to the limited but expanding reports linking C. canimorsus to complement-mediated TMAs. An especially instructive report by Bjørkto et al described CM-HUS secondary to C. canimorsus in a patient with a complement factor B variant, who responded to eculizumab therapy. In contrast, our patient was not found to carry any pathogenic or likely pathogenic complement gene abnormalities, thus supporting a scenario of infection-induced CM-HUS without clear genetic complement predisposition.

Therapeutic strategy and clinical dilemma

The management of CM-HUS amid ongoing infection is particularly complex. Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody directed against complement component 5, represents the primary therapy for CM-HUS and substantially improves both hematologic and renal outcomes, with optimal results seen when given early. Initiating treatment later is linked to incomplete kidney recovery and a higher likelihood of developing end-stage renal disease.

PEX, on the other hand, is essential for survival in TTP, but confers only short-term benefit in cases of CM-HUS. Its role is largely limited to eliminating circulating complement fragments, inflammatory molecules, or autoantibodies without resolving the core defect in the complement cascade. In this patient, resumption of diuresis followed completion of the final PEX courses, but preceded eculizumab administration, illustrating only temporary improvement. Persistent normalization of hematological parameters and kidney function was achieved solely after inhibiting complement.

A principal therapeutic challenge arises in the context of active bloodstream infection, as terminal complement pathways play a crucial role in defending against encapsulated bacteria such as Neisseria and Capnocytophaga; this raises potential infectious concerns related to eculizumab use. Here, eculizumab was started once blood cultures showed no growth, thereby reducing the risk for uncontrolled infection. When using complement inhibitors, vaccination, prophylactic antimicrobials, and vigilant observation are indispensable.

Conclusion and broader implications

Timely identification of CM-HUS and early commencement of complement inhibition are critical factors associated with positive outcomes. In this case, the patient reached hematologic remission and experienced partial improvement in kidney function, in line with existing data indicating that prognosis primarily depends on renal status and the interval before starting eculizumab treatment.

While CM-HUS linked to C. canimorsus is exceedingly uncommon, this report underscores the need to consider infectious causes—including atypical ones—in individuals presenting with newly developed TMA. Enhanced clinician awareness and advanced molecular diagnostic methods may facilitate recognition of additional cases.
