Title: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Tobias Evason Fredriksen, Burhan-Ud-Din Mian, Szilveszter Dolgos

Abstract:

Summary: A previously well woman in her late 40s presented with rapidly worsening sepsis four days following a dog bite. She developed disseminated intravascular coagulation, pancytopenia, and acute kidney injury, which advanced to complete anuria requiring dialysis. Blood cultures identified Capnocytophaga canimorsus bacteraemia. Although her condition initially improved with antimicrobial therapy and supportive measures, she subsequently experienced sudden isolated haemolysis and persistent thrombocytopaenia. Laboratory investigations and renal biopsy revealed thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), and a diagnosis of complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) was established. Treatment with plasma exchange and initiation of eculizumab led to hematologic remission and partial restoration of renal function. This report underscores C. canimorsus as an uncommon cause of CM-HUS in otherwise healthy adults and details the patient's diagnostic evaluation, the difficulties encountered with differential diagnosis, and the complexity inherent to the clinical course and management of TMA following sepsis.

Background:

Capnocytophaga canimorsus is an encapsulated, gram-negative bacillus that grows slowly and is a constituent of the normal oropharyngeal flora in cats and dogs. Infections in humans are uncommon and can be acquired through bites, scratches, or even close contact. The range of clinical manifestations is broad, from isolated meningitis to rapidly progressing sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), failure of multiple organs, and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Despite being generally sensitive to multiple antibiotics, the case fatality rate can reach up to 30%.

Among Capnocytophaga species, C. canimorsus is most commonly associated with severe zoonotic infections in humans. Due to its exacting culture requirements and sluggish growth in the laboratory, diagnosis is often delayed, leading to a greater reliance on molecular diagnostics such as real-time nanopore sequencing.

In the present case, the patient exhibited septic shock, marked leukopenia, and coagulopathy indicative of DIC. While initial progression was characterized by sepsis-driven multiorgan dysfunction, subsequent emergence of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and worsening acute kidney injury (AKI) was consistent with superimposed TMA. This sequence of events underscores the possibility that complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) may remain masked during the early stages dominated by sepsis and DIC.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late 40s with no significant past medical history and two previous uncomplicated pregnancies presented to the emergency department in rapidly deteriorating condition. Four days before admission, she sustained a dog bite to her upper lip and received tetanus prophylaxis. She was prescribed phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) at that time but did not begin antibiotic therapy, as she was unable to obtain the medication. She subsequently developed a high fever (exceeding 40 °C), general malaise, decreased oral intake, and later sought care after experiencing numbness and discoloration of her feet. At an initial emergency primary care visit, she was found to be hypoglycemic, unresponsive to both oral glucose and glucagon, with her blood glucose reaching a low of 0.6 mmol/L, necessitating urgent transfer to our emergency department.

Upon admission, the patient was conscious and interactive but demonstrated clinical signs of sepsis, including mottling and coolness of the lower limbs. She denied use of herbal products or other substances, and there was no family history of similar illnesses. Initial laboratory investigations indicated significant systemic disturbances, with pronounced inflammation, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury, elevated liver enzymes, and evidence of coagulopathy. Arterial blood gas evaluation showed metabolic acidosis with a pH of 7.27 (reference range 7.35–7.43) and a raised lactate concentration of 5.7 mmol/L (reference <2.5 mmol/L). Despite administration of high-dose intravenous glucose, her hypoglycaemia persisted, attributed to sepsis. Upon presentation, her urine output was sufficient, and urinalysis detected proteinuria (2+) and hematuria (3+).

Her combination of symptoms—including fever, pancytopenia, coagulopathy associated with acute kidney injury (AKI), and lactic acidosis—was interpreted as disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) likely resulting from bacteremia and early sepsis, indicated by a quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) score of 1 out of 3. A surgical etiology for her mottled extremities, such as necrotising fasciitis, was excluded based on both clinical examination and imaging. Owing to the severity of her clinical status, empiric broad-spectrum intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam (Tazocin) was administered to target pathogens typically associated with dogs, including Capnocytophaga species. C. canimorsus infection was strongly suspected from the beginning and was definitively identified in both aerobic and anaerobic blood cultures after 7 days. She was then moved to the intensive care unit (ICU) for heightened observation.

In light of the pancytopenia and coagulation abnormalities, the haematology team was involved. Differential diagnoses at this stage included acute leukemia and haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis. Haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS) was also entertained as a possibility but was considered less likely, given the presence of leukopenia, elevated inflammatory markers, normal haptoglobin, and non-compensatory reticulocyte response. Flow cytometry was conducted and a bone marrow biopsy was planned; however, as flow cytometry revealed normal findings—showing no increase in CD34+ blasts or abnormal cell populations—the biopsy was deferred. Clinically, her findings were in line with sepsis. Supportive care was provided during this period, including platelet and fibrinogen transfusions while further results were awaited.

Following admission to the ICU, she experienced complete anuria alongside a swift decline in kidney function. Vigorous intravenous fluid administration and simultaneous use of loop diuretics failed to yield improvement. Subsequently, she developed septic cardiomyopathy accompanied by volume overload, necessitating the commencement of continuous renal replacement therapy. Testing for autoimmunity, including antinuclear antibodies (ANA) and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), produced negative results.

On the third day in the ICU, she presented with widespread cutaneous bleeding, extensive ecchymoses, and acral cyanosis with ischemic changes involving several fingers and toes, though distal pulses and sensation remained intact. These observations were indicative of microthrombotic processes in association with disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). Despite the extent of organ involvement, vasopressor therapy was not required except for stress-dose corticosteroids provided in the early septic course. She was also treated with albumin and various blood product infusions.

After an 8-day hospitalization, the patient was moved to the nephrology unit due to her ongoing dialysis-dependent acute kidney injury and requirement for intravenous antibiotics. Upon arrival, she was stable from a clinical perspective, but remained anuric, exhibited generalized edema, and had multiple hematomas; however, she was alert and oriented. Antibiotic therapy was stepped down to intravenous ampicillin, later switched to oral Apocillin, as guided by blood culture results.

While on the nephrology service, laboratory assessments demonstrated a favorable trend in inflammatory markers, reactive leukocytosis, increasing platelet counts, and a steady haemoglobin level. Renal function tests continued to show azotaemia, though her coagulation studies returned to normal, suggesting an improvement in her coagulopathy. Her relevant laboratory data at this point are detailed in Table 2.

After an initial period of clinical improvement, the scenario changed with the onset of a sudden, isolated haemoglobin decrease (from 111 to 68 g/L over 6 days), despite an absence of overt bleeding or signs of haemodynamic compromise. The platelet count remained constantly reduced (30–40×10⁹/L), while haptoglobin was undetectable and LDH increased, all findings consistent with ongoing haemolysis (see figure 1).

The direct antiglobulin test (DAT) (Coombs test) returned positive for IgG, which raised the possibility of autoimmune haemolytic anaemia. High-dose prednisolone was initiated in conjunction with standard prophylactic measures, and the patient received multiple blood transfusions over a period of 2 days. Since the haemolytic activity did not improve with corticosteroid treatment and platelet counts persisted at low levels (41–46 × 10⁹/L), additional investigations were indicated. Examination of the peripheral blood smear showed 15%–20% schistocytes, findings compatible with MAHA and indicative of TMA (figure 2). The original DAT result was likely falsely positive due to either penicillin administration or preceding transfusions; a subsequent DAT was negative.

Complement analyses, including C3 and C4 levels, returned within normal range, and functional testing of the classical, alternative, and lectin complement pathways also showed normal results. Shortly thereafter, ADAMTS13 activity was measured at 43.5%, which effectively excluded thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), as TTP is usually characterized by activity levels under 10%. Pending these laboratory results, plasma exchange (PEX) was started as interim treatment before administration of the initial dose of eculizumab.

After the platelet count increased to 77×10⁹/L, a renal biopsy was undertaken, revealing marked acute tubular necrosis, glomeruli exhibiting ischemia with fibrin thrombi, and focal crescent formation, all indicative of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

Immunofluorescence and electron microscopy images are not included, as immunofluorescence detected no immune complex deposition and electron microscopy revealed no features of a primary glomerular disorder.

Shiga toxin-producing E. coli-associated HUS (STEC-HUS) was ruled out on the basis of a negative stool PCR analysis.

After the patient’s second and third sessions of plasma exchange (PEX), spontaneous urine output was restored. Eculizumab induction therapy was started (900 mg intravenously once a week for 4 weeks) following administration of the initial doses of meningococcal vaccines—Menveo (covering serogroups A, C, Y, and W) and Bexsero (targeting serogroup B). The second vaccine doses were scheduled to be administered eight weeks later on an outpatient basis. Because the vaccines had not been given at least two weeks before the initiation of complement inhibition, prophylactic Apocillin was prescribed.

To exclude hereditary complement-mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), comprehensive complement assays were sent to Skåne Laboratory at Lund University, Sweden. In consideration of logistical factors and the patient's safety, three sessions of PEX using Octaplas, as well as one induction dose of eculizumab, were completed before samples were collected. Functional complement assessment demonstrated substantially diminished alternative pathway activity (AH50 quantitatively measured at 6%), with normal concentrations of complement factors H, I, and B, and negative antifactor H antibody results.

Outcome and follow-up:

After undergoing comprehensive medical treatment, the patient remains clinically stable and exhibits sufficient urine output. Renal function is still impaired, as evidenced by a serum creatinine of 175 µmol/L, and ongoing nephrology follow-up has been arranged (see figure 4). She is receiving maintenance therapy with eculizumab at a dose of 1200 mg intravenously every two weeks, now four months after the induction phase. Expanded genetic testing of complement-related genes failed to detect any pathogenic mutations; these findings were conveyed orally by the reference laboratory, although the official written documentation was still pending at the time of this submission. Given these results, complement inhibition therapy is being sustained due to the disease’s severity, with periodic reassessment and consideration for discontinuation should renal function stabilize. Discovery of pathogenic variants in complement genes would have supported more prolonged treatment, along with vigilant monitoring for recurrence. Nonetheless, no such genetic abnormalities were present in this patient.

Differential diagnosis of TMA

TMAs encompass several overlapping clinical entities.

The foremost step in assessing adults who present with MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI is ruling out thrombotic TTP, which is characterized by marked ADAMTS13 deficiency (<10%). Clinical prediction tools such as the PLASMIC score may assist in risk stratification during the interim until laboratory confirmation is available. In this case, the patient’s PLASMIC score was 4 out of 7 (indicating a low likelihood for TTP), and her ADAMTS13 activity measured 43.5%, findings that made TTP improbable and prompted evaluation for other causes.

STEC-HUS also warrants consideration. PCR testing for Shiga toxin was negative in this patient. Furthermore, the lack of preceding gastrointestinal symptoms, coupled with negative microbiological studies, suggested that STEC-HUS was unlikely.

In summary, ongoing hemolysis, the presence of schistocytes, AKI necessitating dialysis, and renal biopsy findings indicative of TMA collectively favored a diagnosis of CM-HUS (see figure 5). This was further corroborated by the negative Shiga toxin PCR and the prompt clinical response to eculizumab initiation. Additionally, her unremarkable past medical history diminishes the possibility of an underlying complement abnormality necessitating a 'second hit,' which further points toward an infection-induced CM-HUS.

CM-HUS is diagnosed by exclusion, as there are no definitive markers, and complement components C3 and C4 may remain within normal limits throughout the illness. Significantly, normal C3 and C4 levels do not rule out CM-HUS, since these can persist within standard ranges during active disease. In this case, haemolysis developed only after the patient’s sepsis began to resolve, pointing toward infection-induced CM-HUS instead of a longstanding condition.

For this patient, initial assessments—including C3, C4, and ADAMTS13 activity—were conducted directly prior to the first plasma exchange (PEX) treatment. Broader complement investigations were carried out 8 days after the final PEX and 4 days following the initial administration of eculizumab. The diminished alternative complement pathway identified is best explained by therapeutic interventions, rather than by underlying complement depletion or ongoing activation.

Pathophysiology of CM-HUS: complement dysregulation and infectious triggers

CM-HUS (previously referred to as ‘atypical HUS’) results from abnormal regulation of the alternative complement pathway, frequently in individuals with a genetic susceptibility. Approximately 50–70% of patients possess genetic alterations or autoantibodies targeting complement regulators, including factor H, factor I, factor B, or membrane cofactor protein (CD46). Nonetheless, penetrance of the disease remains incomplete, and clinical symptoms generally occur only following an external precipitant such as infection, pregnancy, autoimmune disorders, or specific medications.

C. canimorsus, though infrequently documented, has been shown to activate complement pathways, especially in vulnerable hosts. C. canimorsus demonstrates variable sensitivity to serum complement: while many strains are susceptible, some that express a capsule exhibit partial resistance, highlighting the significant role of complement in host defense. This situation poses a clinical dilemma, as complement activation is essential for eliminating the organism but may also induce severe endothelial injury and TMA in patients predisposed to such events.

The current case contributes to a limited, yet expanding collection of reports that associate C. canimorsus with complement-mediated TMAs. Of note, Bjørkto et al documented a patient experiencing C. canimorsus-induced CM-HUS who carried a complement factor B mutation and responded to eculizumab therapy. Unlike the patient in that account, no pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in complement genes were detected in our patient, suggesting a form of infection-associated CM-HUS that arises independently of recognizable inherited complement abnormalities.

Therapeutic strategy and clinical dilemma

Managing CM-HUS amid ongoing infection presents significant difficulties. Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody that inhibits the terminal complement component 5, is the first-line therapy for CM-HUS and greatly enhances both haematologic and renal outcomes, especially if started promptly. Postponed treatment is linked to suboptimal kidney recovery and consequently increases the likelihood of developing end-stage renal disease.

Conversely, while plasma exchange (PEX) is critical in the management of TTP, its benefits in CM-HUS are transient, primarily due to removal of circulating complement factors, inflammatory cytokines, or autoantibodies, without directly correcting the intrinsic complement pathway abnormality. In the present case, diuresis resumed following the last PEX sessions and prior to administration of eculizumab, indicating only fleeting improvement. Lasting hematologic and renal recovery was achieved only after complement inhibition.

An important therapeutic consideration is initiating complement blockade during episodes of active bacteremia, since terminal components of complement play a vital role in host defense against encapsulated organisms such as Neisseria and Capnocytophaga; this heightens concerns regarding infection risk with eculizumab. Here, eculizumab was started only after blood cultures cleared, reducing the likelihood of uncontrolled infection. When employing complement inhibitors, vaccination, antibiotic prophylaxis, and vigilant monitoring remain necessary.

Conclusion and broader implications

Timely identification of CM-HUS and early commencement of complement inhibition are key factors associated with a positive prognosis. In this case, the patient attained haematological remission alongside partial improvement in renal function, aligning with existing data that indicate both baseline renal function and the interval before eculizumab administration are crucial prognostic indicators.

While CM-HUS related to C. canimorsus infection is exceedingly uncommon, this report underscores the need to evaluate potential infectious precipitants—even rare ones—in individuals presenting with newly diagnosed TMA. Enhanced clinical vigilance and advanced molecular diagnostic techniques may facilitate detection of additional similar cases.
