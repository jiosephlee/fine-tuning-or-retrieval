Title: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Tobias Evason Fredriksen, Burhan-Ud-Din Mian, Szilveszter Dolgos

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late 40s with no prior health issues presented with rapidly advancing sepsis four days after sustaining a dog bite. She developed disseminated intravascular coagulation, pancytopenia, and acute kidney injury, which progressed to complete anuria requiring dialysis. Blood cultures identified Capnocytophaga canimorsus bacteremia. Although she initially improved with antibiotics and supportive treatment, she subsequently experienced acute isolated haemolysis and continued thrombocytopaenia. Laboratory data and renal biopsy revealed thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), leading to a diagnosis of complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS). Hematological remission occurred after plasma exchange and the start of eculizumab, and there was a partial recovery of renal function. This report underscores C. canimorsus infection as an uncommon precipitant of CM-HUS in otherwise immunocompetent adults and details the patient's diagnostic process, the difficulties in differential diagnosis, and the intricacies of managing TMA secondary to sepsis.

Background:

Capnocytophaga canimorsus is an encapsulated, gram-negative bacillus that grows slowly and is part of the typical oropharyngeal microbiota in dogs and cats. While infections in humans are infrequent, transmission can occur through bites, scratches, or mere close interaction with these animals. The clinical spectrum is broad, encompassing isolated cases of meningitis as well as rapidly progressing sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), failure of multiple organ systems, and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Although the organism responds to a wide range of antibiotics, mortality rates may be as high as 30%.

Among Capnocytophaga species, C. canimorsus is most commonly associated with severe zoonotic infections in people. Its demanding growth requirements and slow proliferation in culture frequently delay diagnosis, leading to a shift toward advanced molecular diagnostics, including real-time nanopore sequencing.

In the case discussed, infection with C. canimorsus manifested as septic shock with marked leukopaenia and coagulopathy compatible with DIC. Sepsis-related involvement of multiple organs predominated initially; however, subsequent emergence of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and worsening acute kidney injury (AKI) indicated the presence of overlapping TMA. This underscores the potential for complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) to remain unrecognized during the early phases of sepsis and DIC.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late 40s with no significant past medical history and two previous normal pregnancies presented to the emergency department with an acutely deteriorating condition. She had sustained a dog bite to her upper lip four days earlier and was administered a tetanus vaccine as preventive treatment. Although phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) was prescribed on the day of the bite, she did not begin antibiotic therapy because she was unable to obtain her medication. Subsequently, she developed a high fever exceeding 40 °C, generalized malaise, and decreased oral intake. Medical care was sought after she noticed numbness and discoloration in her feet. In a primary-care emergency setting, she was noted to be hypoglycemic with glucose levels as low as 0.6 mmol/L, which did not respond to oral glucose or glucagon administration. This led to her urgent referral to our emergency department.

Upon admission, the patient was awake and communicative, though exhibited signs of sepsis, including mottled and cool skin on her lower limbs. She denied using herbal products or other substances, and none of her family members had experienced illness or comparable symptoms. Initial laboratory testing demonstrated pronounced systemic dysfunction, including significant inflammation, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury (AKI), elevated liver enzymes, and coagulopathy. Arterial blood gas demonstrated a metabolic acidosis with a pH of 7.27 (normal range: 7.35–7.43) and raised lactate at 5.7 mmol/L (reference <2.5 mmol/L). Despite receiving large amounts of intravenous glucose, she continued to have hypoglycaemia, which was attributed to sepsis. Diuresis was preserved upon presentation; urinalysis identified protein (2+) and blood (3+) in the urine.

Her array of symptoms—including fever, pancytopenia, coagulopathy, acute kidney injury (AKI), and lactic acidosis—was interpreted as disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) secondary to bacteraemia and early sepsis, with a quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) score of 1 out of 3. Possible surgical etiologies for her mottled extremities, such as necrotizing fasciitis, were excluded following clinical and imaging evaluations. Owing to the severity of her presentation, broad-spectrum intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam (Tazocin) was initiated to target pathogens associated with dogs, including Capnocytophaga species. C. canimorsus was specifically suspected from the beginning and was confirmed in both aerobic and anaerobic blood cultures after 7 days. She was later moved to the intensive care unit (ICU) for enhanced monitoring.

Haematology was consulted due to the presence of pancytopenia and coagulopathy. Differential diagnoses considered were acute leukemia and haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis. Haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS) was entertained as a possibility but deemed unlikely given the findings of leukopenia, elevated inflammatory markers, normal haptoglobin, and non-compensatory reticulocyte count. Flow cytometry was performed, and a bone marrow biopsy was initially planned; however, as results showed no elevation in CD34+ blasts or abnormal immunophenotypes, the biopsy was subsequently cancelled. The overall clinical scenario aligned with sepsis. While awaiting definitive results, supportive transfusions of platelets and fibrinogen were provided.

Following admission to the ICU, the patient developed complete anuria accompanied by a rapid decline in renal function. Attempts at aggressive intravenous fluid administration in conjunction with loop diuretics were ineffective. She later manifested fluid overload secondary to septic cardiomyopathy, necessitating initiation of continuous renal replacement therapy. Autoimmune serologies, including antinuclear antibodies (ANA) and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), returned negative results.

By ICU day 3, she presented with widespread cutaneous bleeding, ecchymosis, and cyanotic, ischemic changes in several toes and fingers, although peripheral pulses and sensation remained intact. These signs indicated microthrombosis within the clinical context of disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). Even though multiple organ systems were affected, vasopressors were not required except for stress-dose corticosteroids given during her initial septic period. The patient also received albumin as well as other transfused blood products.

Following eight days of hospitalization, she was moved to the nephrology unit to continue dialysis-dependent acute kidney injury (AKI) management and receive intravenous antibiotics. Upon arrival, she remained haemodynamically stable but was anuric, exhibited widespread edema, and multiple hematomas, while maintaining full alertness and orientation. Antibiotic therapy was narrowed to intravenous ampicillin and subsequently transitioned to oral Apocillin in alignment with blood culture findings.

In the nephrology unit, laboratory investigations demonstrated a down-trending inflammatory response, reactive leukocytosis, normalization of platelet counts, and stable hemoglobin concentration. Renal function tests continued to reveal significant azotemia, though coagulation studies returned to normal, reflecting resolution of coagulopathy. A summary of her laboratory parameters at this juncture is provided in Table 2.

After an initial period of stabilization, her condition worsened with the onset of a pronounced isolated hemoglobin decline (from 111 to 68 g/L within six days), despite no clinical evidence of bleeding or cardiovascular compromise. Platelet counts persisted at a reduced level (30–40 × 10⁹/L), haptoglobin was no longer detectable, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) concentrations increased, all consistent with active hemolysis (see figure 1).

A direct antiglobulin test (DAT), also known as the Coombs test, returned positive for IgG, suggesting a possible diagnosis of autoimmune haemolytic anaemia. High-dose prednisolone was initiated together with standard prophylactic measures, and multiple blood transfusions were administered over a 2-day period. Since the haemolytic process did not improve with corticosteroid therapy and platelet counts remained low at 41–46 × 10⁹/L, further investigation was deemed necessary. Examination of a peripheral blood smear identified 15%–20% schistocytes, which is indicative of MAHA and points toward TMA (see figure 2). The original DAT result was probably falsely positive due to penicillin exposure or previous transfusions; subsequent testing was negative.

Complement assays and ADAMTS13 activity were sent for further assessment. Routine complement studies (C3/C4) available locally were within the normal range, and functional analyses for the classical, alternative, and lectin pathways also showed normal results. Shortly thereafter, ADAMTS13 activity was determined to be 43.5%, making the diagnosis of thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) unlikely, as TTP characteristically presents with levels below 10%. During the interval while awaiting these findings, plasma exchange (PEX) was started to provide interim treatment before administering the first dose of eculizumab.

After the platelet count rose to 77×10⁹/L, a renal biopsy was undertaken, which revealed pronounced acute tubular necrosis, glomeruli displaying ischemic changes with fibrin thrombi, and sporadic crescent formation—all indicative of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

The diagnosis in this instance was primarily supported by the histopathological characteristics observed under light microscopy, as depicted in figure 3. Immunofluorescence and electron microscopy were not presented, given that immunofluorescence did not show any immune complex deposits, and electron microscopy failed to reveal any signs of primary glomerular disease.

Infection with Shiga toxin-producing E. coli causing hemolytic uremic syndrome (STEC-HUS) was excluded, as stool PCR testing returned negative results.

After the second and third plasma exchange (PEX) treatments, the patient began to experience spontaneous diuresis again. Eculizumab was started after administration of the initial doses of meningococcal vaccines: Menveo (covering serogroups A, C, Y, and W) and Bexsero (covering serogroup B). Eculizumab induction consisted of intravenous 900 mg weekly for four weeks. The follow-up doses of both vaccines were scheduled to be given eight weeks later in an outpatient setting. Apocillin prophylaxis was initiated, since the vaccinations had not occurred two weeks prior to beginning complement inhibition.

To exclude hereditary complement-mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), advanced complement analysis was sent to the Skåne Laboratory at Lund University, Sweden. Given logistical constraints and patient safety requirements, three PEX sessions with Octaplas and one eculizumab induction dose were administered before sample collection. The complement work-up included quantitative testing of alternative pathway activity (AH50), which was significantly diminished (6%). Complement factors H, I, and B were within the normal range, and testing for antifactor H antibodies was negative.

Outcome and follow-up:

After undergoing complex medical treatment, the patient exhibits stable overall health and maintains sufficient urine output. However, her renal function is still impaired, as indicated by a serum creatinine of 175 µmol/L. She will continue to be monitored through follow-up appointments in the nephrology outpatient clinic (figure 4). The patient remains on maintenance eculizumab therapy, receiving 1200 mg intravenously every 2 weeks, and this regimen has been maintained for 4 months after induction. Comprehensive genetic testing for complement abnormalities did not reveal any pathogenic mutations; these findings were relayed verbally by the reference laboratory, and the written report was awaited at submission. In light of these results, complement inhibition therapy is being sustained due to the severity of her condition, with plans to review the necessity for ongoing treatment should kidney function stabilize. Detection of pathogenic variants in complement genes would support prolonged therapy with vigilant surveillance for recurrence, but such mutations were not found in this patient.

Differential diagnosis of TMA

TMAs encompass several overlapping clinical entities.

In adults presenting with MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI, it is essential to first rule out thrombotic TTP, characterized by marked ADAMTS13 deficiency (<10%). Clinical prediction tools such as the PLASMIC score can assist in stratifying patients while waiting for laboratory confirmation, which remains the gold standard for diagnosis. In this case, the patient's PLASMIC score was 4 out of 7, indicating a low likelihood of TTP, and ADAMTS13 activity was measured at 43.5%, thus TTP was considered improbable and further investigative steps were pursued.

Another differential diagnosis to consider is STEC-HUS. The patient's PCR testing for Shiga toxin was negative. In combination with the absence of gastrointestinal symptoms preceding presentation and negative microbiologic assays, STEC-HUS was deemed unlikely.

The combination of ongoing hemolysis, presence of schistocytes, AKI requiring dialysis, and renal biopsy findings indicative of TMA ultimately favored a diagnosis of CM-HUS (see figure 5). This diagnosis was supported by negative Shiga toxin PCR and the patient’s prompt improvement following initiation of eculizumab. Her unremarkable prior medical history lessens the likelihood of an underlying complement abnormality necessitating a “second hit,” which aligns with an infection-associated CM-HUS.

CM-HUS is identified as a clinicopathological diagnosis by exclusion, with complement levels (C3/C4) potentially remaining within normal ranges throughout its course. It is crucial to recognize that normal C3 and C4 values do not eliminate the possibility of CM-HUS, as these parameters can persist within normal limits during the illness. In this instance, haemolysis was detected only after the sepsis started to resolve, supporting the hypothesis of infection-induced CM-HUS as opposed to an underlying condition.

For this patient, standard complement studies (C3/C4) as well as ADAMTS13 activity measurements were acquired directly prior to the initial PEX treatment. Broader complement analysis was carried out eight days following the last PEX procedure and four days after initiating eculizumab therapy. The decreased activity in the alternative pathway is interpreted as being attributable primarily to therapeutic intervention rather than to underlying complement activation or depletion.

Pathophysiology of CM-HUS: complement dysregulation and infectious triggers

Complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS), formerly known as ‘atypical HUS’, results from abnormal activation of the alternative complement pathway and often occurs in individuals with underlying genetic susceptibilities. Approximately 50% to 70% of affected individuals possess either genetic variants or autoantibodies that disrupt the regulation of complement proteins such as factors H, I, B, or membrane cofactor protein (CD46). Nonetheless, the presence of such genetic factors alone is insufficient for disease development; an external precipitant—most frequently infection, pregnancy, autoimmune condition, or specific drug exposure—is generally necessary for clinical presentation.

Though infrequent, infections with C. canimorsus have the capacity to stimulate complement activation, particularly in patients with heightened vulnerability. Importantly, C. canimorsus strains differ in their sensitivity to complement-mediated serum killing: while many strains are susceptible, certain capsular variants display partial resistance, underscoring the role of complement as a key mechanism of host defense. This dynamic presents a clinical challenge—activation of complement may help eliminate the infection but, in those predisposed, could also promote unchecked endothelial injury and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

The current report contributes to a limited but expanding group of cases associating C. canimorsus with complement-mediated TMAs. For example, Bjørkto et al described a case in which a patient with C. canimorsus-related CM-HUS carrying a complement factor B variant responded favorably to eculizumab therapy. Unlike that report, our patient had no identifiable pathogenic or likely pathogenic mutations in complement genes, indicating a scenario where infection served as the sole trigger for CM-HUS in the absence of a detectable hereditary complement abnormality.

Therapeutic strategy and clinical dilemma

Managing CM-HUS in the setting of ongoing infection is particularly complex. Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody directed against the terminal complement protein C5, constitutes the primary treatment for CM-HUS and leads to significant improvements in both hematologic and renal parameters, especially when administered promptly. Initiation of therapy at a later stage is linked to suboptimal renal recovery and an elevated likelihood of advancing to end-stage renal disease.

While plasma exchange (PEX) is lifesaving in cases of TTP, it confers only short-term benefits for patients with CM-HUS. This temporary improvement is largely attributed to the removal of circulating complement fragments, inflammatory mediators, or autoantibodies, without correcting the fundamental complement pathway defect. In this patient, diuresis resumed following the completion of PEX sessions but prior to the introduction of eculizumab, indicating merely fleeting stabilization. Persistent hematologic and renal recovery was achieved only with complement inhibition.

An important clinical challenge involves employing complement blockade during active bacteremia, as terminal complement activity is essential for host defense against encapsulated bacteria, including Neisseria and Capnocytophaga, thus raising concern about infectious complications associated with eculizumab. In this instance, eculizumab was started after blood cultures had become negative, thereby reducing the risk of ongoing infection. Immunization, antibiotic prophylaxis, and careful surveillance are imperative when utilizing complement inhibitors.

Conclusion and broader implications

Timely identification of CM-HUS and early commencement of complement inhibition therapy are key factors associated with improved prognosis. In this case, the patient experienced hematological remission and a partial return of renal function, aligning with existing literature indicating that both baseline renal status and the interval before starting eculizumab are crucial prognostic variables.

While CM-HUS linked to C. canimorsus is exceedingly uncommon, this report underscores the need to assess for infectious precipitants—even atypical ones—in individuals with newly diagnosed TMA. Greater clinical vigilance combined with advanced molecular diagnostic techniques may facilitate detection of additional instances.
