Title: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Tobias Evason Fredriksen, Burhan-Ud-Din Mian, Szilveszter Dolgos

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late 40s with no prior health issues presented with rapidly advancing sepsis four days after being bitten by a dog. She experienced disseminated intravascular coagulation, pancytopenia, and acute kidney injury, which progressed to complete anuria and dependence on dialysis. Blood cultures identified Capnocytophaga canimorsus bacteraemia. Although she initially showed clinical improvement with antibiotic treatment and supportive measures, she subsequently developed abrupt, isolated haemolysis along with ongoing thrombocytopaenia. Laboratory analyses and renal biopsy confirmed a thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), leading to a diagnosis of complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS). After undergoing plasma exchange and beginning eculizumab therapy, she attained haematological remission and experienced partial renal recovery. This report underscores C. canimorsus infection as an uncommon precipitant of CM-HUS in immunocompetent adults and details the patient’s diagnostic process, the complexities in differential diagnosis, and the intricate clinical and therapeutic management of TMA following sepsis.

Background:

Capnocytophaga canimorsus is a slow-growing, encapsulated, gram-negative bacillus commonly present in the oral flora of dogs and cats. Human infection is uncommon and may be acquired through animal bites, scratches, or even close proximity. The clinical manifestations are diverse, including isolated meningitis as well as fulminant sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), multiorgan dysfunction, and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Although this organism is generally susceptible to a wide range of antibiotics, the mortality rate may reach up to 30%.

Among Capnocytophaga species, C. canimorsus is most often associated with severe zoonotic infections in humans. Because of its fastidious growth characteristics and prolonged incubation required for cultures, diagnosis is frequently delayed, leading to an increased reliance on molecular approaches such as real-time nanopore sequencing.

In our reported case, infection with C. canimorsus manifested as septic shock with pronounced leukopaenia and coagulopathy suggestive of DIC. The initial phase was marked by sepsis-related multiorgan impairment; however, subsequent onset of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and aggravation of acute kidney injury (AKI) pointed toward an overlapping TMA. This underscores that complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) may be concealed during the early phases of sepsis and DIC.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late 40s, previously in good health and with a history of two straightforward pregnancies, arrived at the emergency department with a rapidly deteriorating health status. Four days earlier, she sustained a dog bite to her upper lip and subsequently received a tetanus vaccine for prophylaxis. Phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) was prescribed the same day; however, she did not begin the antibiotic regimen because she was unable to retrieve her prescription. She went on to develop a high-grade fever (>40 °C), general malaise, and decreased oral intake. Medical help was sought after she noticed numbness and discoloration in her feet. Upon presentation at the primary-care emergency unit, significant hypoglycemia was identified, unresponsive to both oral glucose and glucagon, with blood glucose dropping to a minimum of 0.6 mmol/L, resulting in her immediate transfer to our emergency department.

Upon presentation, the patient was awake and interactive but demonstrated signs of sepsis, including mottled and cold lower limbs. She reported no use of herbal medicines or other substances, and there were no similar illnesses among family members. Initial laboratory studies indicated profound systemic abnormalities, notable for significant inflammation, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury, elevated liver enzymes, and coagulation abnormalities. Arterial blood gas revealed a metabolic acidosis, with a pH measured at 7.27 (reference range 7.35–7.43) and an increased lactate concentration of 5.7 (<2.5 mmol/L). Persistent hypoglycaemia was observed, even with administration of large amounts of intravenous glucose, attributed to sepsis. At admission, her urine output was sufficient; urinalysis detected protein at 2+ and blood at 3+.

Her array of clinical findings—including fever, pancytopenia, coagulopathy accompanied by acute kidney injury (AKI) and lactic acidosis—was regarded as disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) attributed to bacteraemia and early sepsis, with a quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) score of 1 out of 3. After both physical examination and imaging studies, a surgical etiology for her mottled limbs—such as necrotising fasciitis—was excluded. Owing to the seriousness of her presentation, broad-spectrum intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam (Tazocin) was initiated, targeting pathogens commonly associated with dog bites, such as Capnocytophaga species. C. canimorsus was considered highly likely from the initial assessment and was verified one week later through both aerobic and anaerobic blood cultures. She was subsequently admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) for enhanced surveillance.

In view of her pancytopenia and coagulopathy, consultation with haematology services was obtained. Potential causes included acute leukaemia and haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis. Haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS) was part of the differential diagnosis, though it was deemed less probable given the presence of leukopaenia, elevated inflammatory markers, unremarkable haptoglobin levels, and absence of a compensatory reticulocyte response. Flow cytometry was conducted and a bone marrow biopsy was considered; however, biopsy was not performed after flow cytometry revealed normal findings, including no rise in CD34+ blasts and no atypical immunophenotypes. The overall clinical picture remained consistent with sepsis. During this period, while awaiting further test results, supportive transfusions of platelets and fibrinogen were administered.

Following admission to the intensive care unit, the patient exhibited complete anuria and a rapid decline in kidney function. Intensive fluid resuscitation combined with loop diuretic therapy was ineffective. Subsequently, she manifested septic cardiomyopathy accompanied by fluid overload, necessitating commencement of continuous renal replacement therapy. Autoimmune markers, including antinuclear antibodies (ANA) and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), were both negative.

By the third day in the ICU, she experienced widespread bleeding into the skin characterized by ecchymoses, along with distal cyanotic and ischemic manifestations involving multiple fingers and toes; however, peripheral pulses and sensory function remained intact. These signs were compatible with microthrombotic processes secondary to disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). Despite multiple organ involvement, she did not require vasopressors other than stress-dose corticosteroids administered during the early phase of sepsis. She was also treated with albumin infusions as well as additional blood products.

Following 8 days of hospitalisation, the patient was moved to the nephrology ward to continue management of dialysis-dependent acute kidney injury and intravenous antibiotic treatment. Upon arrival, she was clinically stable yet remained anuric, exhibited widespread oedema and multiple haematomas, but was alert and oriented. Antibiotic therapy was stepped down to intravenous ampicillin and subsequently switched to oral Apocillin, guided by blood culture findings.

While in the nephrology unit, laboratory analyses revealed improving markers of inflammation, a reactive leukocytosis, rising platelet counts, and stable hemoglobin values. Renal function tests showed ongoing azotaemia, whereas coagulation assays returned to normal, indicating a resolving coagulopathic process. Her laboratory parameters during this period are outlined in Table 2.

After an initial period of clinical stability, her status changed due to a sudden and significant decrease in hemoglobin levels (111 g/L dropping to 68 g/L over 6 days), despite the absence of bleeding or hemodynamic compromise. Platelet counts remained consistently low (30–40×10⁹/L), haptoglobin was no longer detectable, and LDH values increased, consistent with active hemolysis (see figure 1).

A direct antiglobulin test (DAT) (Coombs test) yielded a positive result for IgG, prompting consideration of autoimmune haemolytic anaemia. High-dose prednisolone was commenced with standard prophylactic measures, and multiple blood transfusions were administered over a two-day period. When haemolysis persisted despite corticosteroid therapy and platelet counts remained depressed (41–46 × 10⁹/L), additional diagnostic workup was undertaken. Examination of the peripheral blood smear showed 15%–20% schistocytes, findings compatible with microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA) suggestive of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) (figure 2). The initial positive DAT was most likely attributable to penicillin exposure or earlier transfusions, as repeat testing was negative.

Complement investigations and measurement of ADAMTS13 activity were requested. Commonly available complement assays (C3/C4) were found to be within normal ranges, and functional assessments for the classical, alternative, and lectin pathways showed no abnormalities. The ADAMTS13 activity was subsequently reported as 43.5%, which effectively excluded thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), since TTP characteristically demonstrates activity lower than 10%. While results were pending, plasma exchange (PEX) was started as an interim measure prior to the initial administration of eculizumab.

After the platelet count had risen to 77×10⁹/L, a renal biopsy was conducted, revealing pronounced acute tubular necrosis, glomeruli with ischemic changes containing fibrin thrombi, and isolated crescent formation, indicative of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

Light microscopy, illustrated in figure 3, most clearly reflected the diagnostic findings in this patient. Neither immunofluorescence nor electron microscopy are presented, as immunofluorescence did not show immune complex deposits, and electron microscopy found no signs suggestive of a primary glomerular disorder.

A stool PCR test was negative, thereby excluding Shiga toxin-producing E. coli-associated hemolytic uremic syndrome (STEC-HUS).

After the second and third sessions of plasma exchange (PEX), the patient began to experience spontaneous diuresis again. Eculizumab was started after administration of the initial doses of meningococcal vaccines—Menveo (targeting serogroups A, C, Y, and W) and Bexsero (covering serogroup B)—with an induction regimen of 900 mg intravenously weekly for four weeks. Plans for administering the second vaccine doses eight weeks later were made for outpatient follow-up. Since the vaccines had not been given two weeks prior to initiating complement inhibition, prophylactic Apocillin was prescribed.

To exclude hereditary complement-mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), comprehensive complement assessment was sent to Skåne Laboratory at Lund University, Sweden. Due to logistical reasons and patient safety concerns, three plasma exchange sessions with Octaplas and one initial dose of eculizumab were administered before the sample collection. Functional complement evaluation included measurement of alternative pathway activity (quantitative AH50), which was significantly diminished (6%), whereas complement factors H, I, and B were within normal limits and antifactor H antibodies tested negative.

Outcome and follow-up:

After undergoing intensive medical therapy, the patient exhibits a satisfactory overall status and maintains sufficient urine production. Her renal function remains impaired, as indicated by a serum creatinine of 175 µmol/L, and ongoing follow-up will take place at the nephrology outpatient clinic (see figure 4). She is maintained on eculizumab therapy (1200 mg intravenously every 2 weeks) for 4 months following induction. Comprehensive genetic analysis of the complement system did not reveal any pathogenic mutations; preliminary verbal results were relayed by the reference laboratory, though the official written report had not been received by the time this manuscript was prepared. Given these findings, therapy with complement inhibition is being maintained in light of the disease's severity, with plans to reassess the necessity for continued treatment should renal function stabilize. The presence of complement gene mutations would support prolonged therapy and vigilant surveillance for recurrence. Nonetheless, such genetic alterations were not detected in this individual.

Differential diagnosis of TMA

TMAs encompass several overlapping clinical entities.

In adults presenting with MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI, the foremost concern is to rule out thrombotic TTP, which is characterized by marked deficiency of ADAMTS13 (<10%). Tools such as the PLASMIC score are useful for risk stratification during the waiting period for laboratory confirmation, which remains essential for definitive diagnosis. In this case, the patient’s PLASMIC score was 4 out of 7, reflecting a low probability for TTP, and the measured ADAMTS13 activity was 43.5%, rendering TTP an unlikely cause and informing subsequent diagnostic evaluation.

STEC-HUS also merits consideration. However, PCR testing for Shiga toxin returned negative for this patient. Combined with the absence of a preceding gastrointestinal illness and negative microbial assays, STEC-HUS was effectively excluded.

The combination of ongoing haemolysis, schistocytosis, AKI requiring dialysis, and kidney biopsy showing TMA pointed toward a diagnosis of CM-HUS (see figure 5). Diagnosis was further substantiated by negative STEC PCR and prompt clinical improvement following eculizumab initiation. Given her minimal medical history, the possibility of a chronic complement abnormality necessitating a ‘second hit’ appears less likely, with findings more compatible with infection-induced CM-HUS.

CM-HUS is established as a diagnosis of exclusion using both clinical and pathological features, and complement concentrations (C3/C4) can remain within normal limits throughout its course. It is important to recognize that normal C3 and C4 values do not eliminate the possibility of CM-HUS, as these measurements may fall within standard ranges during the illness. Of particular note, hemolysis emerged only as the patient’s sepsis was resolving, implying that CM-HUS was likely initiated by the infection rather than representing an underlying disorder.

In this case, baseline complement assays (C3/C4) and ADAMTS13 activity were collected immediately prior to the initial plasma exchange (PEX) procedure. Comprehensive complement evaluation was undertaken eight days following the last PEX and four days subsequent to the administration of the first eculizumab dose. The identified reduction in alternative pathway activity is most likely attributable to therapeutic interventions rather than to inherent complement consumption or activation.

Pathophysiology of CM-HUS: complement dysregulation and infectious triggers

Complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS), previously known as 'atypical HUS,' results from improper regulation of the alternative complement pathway, frequently occurring in individuals with a genetic predisposition. Approximately 50–70% of affected patients possess genetic abnormalities or autoantibodies involving complement regulators such as factor H, factor I, factor B, or membrane cofactor protein (CD46). Nevertheless, the syndrome exhibits incomplete penetrance, and clinical expression usually follows an external precipitant—most often infections, pregnancy, autoimmune conditions, or certain pharmaceuticals.

Capnocytophaga canimorsus, although infrequently documented in this context, can stimulate complement activation, especially in those with underlying susceptibility. Of note, the organism shows variability in serum susceptibility; whereas many strains are complement-sensitive, some capsulated isolates are partially resistant, underscoring the significance of complement in host defense. This presents a clinical dilemma: complement activity is essential for bacterial clearance but may concurrently provoke unchecked endothelial injury and TMA in predisposed patients.

The present case contributes to the limited, yet expanding, literature associating C. canimorsus infection with complement-driven TMAs. Bjørkto et al reported a notable case of CM-HUS instigated by C. canimorsus in a patient who carried a complement factor B mutation, and was treated effectively with eculizumab. Unlike their report, no pathogenic or likely pathogenic genetic alterations in complement-related genes were found in our patient, reinforcing the concept of infection-induced CM-HUS occurring in the absence of a detectable inherited complement disorder.

Therapeutic strategy and clinical dilemma

Managing CM-HUS in the setting of ongoing infection poses distinct challenges. Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody directed against terminal complement component 5, is regarded as the primary treatment for CM-HUS and significantly enhances haematological and renal outcomes, especially when administered promptly. Postponed initiation correlates with less complete renal recovery and an increased likelihood of advancing to end-stage kidney disease.

PEX serves as a lifesaving intervention in TTP, but its effect in CM-HUS is largely temporary, primarily facilitating removal of circulating complement fragments, inflammatory mediators, or autoantibodies without addressing the core defect in complement regulation. In the presented patient, diuresis resumed following the last PEX sessions but preceding eculizumab administration, illustrating only momentary improvement. Lasting haematological and renal recovery was achieved solely after the introduction of complement inhibition.

A major therapeutic consideration concerns complement inhibition during concurrent bacteraemia, since terminal complement components play an essential role in protecting against encapsulated pathogens such as Neisseria and Capnocytophaga, thereby elevating the risk of infection associated with eculizumab use. In this scenario, eculizumab therapy was commenced once blood cultures were negative, thereby reducing the danger of ongoing infection. Immunisation, antimicrobial prophylaxis, and vigilant monitoring are indispensable when employing complement inhibitors.

Conclusion and broader implications

Timely identification of CM-HUS and early commencement of complement inhibition represent the primary factors associated with positive outcomes. In line with existing data that emphasize renal status and the interval to eculizumab initiation as key prognostic indicators, our patient demonstrated haematological remission alongside partial improvement in renal function.

While CM-HUS associated with C. canimorsus is highly uncommon, this report underscores the need to account for infectious causes—including atypical pathogens—when assessing patients with newly diagnosed TMA. Enhanced recognition and molecular testing have the potential to detect additional cases of this nature.
