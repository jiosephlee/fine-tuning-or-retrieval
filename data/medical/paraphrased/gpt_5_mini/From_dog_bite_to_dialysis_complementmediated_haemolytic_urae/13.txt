Title: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Tobias Evason Fredriksen, Burhan-Ud-Din Mian, Szilveszter Dolgos

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late 40s with no prior health issues presented with rapidly worsening sepsis four days after sustaining a dog bite. She subsequently developed disseminated intravascular coagulation, pancytopenia, and acute kidney injury, which advanced to complete anuria requiring dialysis. Blood cultures identified Capnocytophaga canimorsus bacteraemia. Although she initially improved with antibiotic therapy and supportive measures, she later experienced sudden-onset isolated haemolysis and continued thrombocytopaenia. Findings from laboratory tests and a renal biopsy confirmed thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), leading to a diagnosis of complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS). After receiving plasma exchange and starting eculizumab, she achieved haematological remission and had partial recovery of kidney function. This report illustrates C. canimorsus infection as an uncommon cause of CM-HUS in immunocompetent adults, and describes the patient’s diagnostic process, the diagnostic challenges encountered, and the complexities involved in managing TMA secondary to sepsis.

Background:

Capnocytophaga canimorsus is a slow-growing, encapsulated, gram-negative bacillus commonly present in the oropharyngeal microbiota of dogs and cats. Infections in humans are uncommon and can be acquired through animal bites, scratches, or even through close proximity to pets. The spectrum of clinical manifestations is wide, including isolated cases of meningitis, severe sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), multiple organ dysfunction, and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Mortality rates can reach up to 30%, even though the organism is generally sensitive to a broad range of antibiotics.

Among the Capnocytophaga genus, C. canimorsus is most frequently associated with severe zoonotic infections in humans. The detection of this organism can be protracted because it is fastidious and exhibits slow in vitro growth, which has led to a growing reliance on molecular diagnostic tools such as real-time nanopore sequencing.

In this case, infection with C. canimorsus manifested as septic shock, marked leukopenia, and a coagulopathy indicative of DIC. The initial phase was characterized by sepsis-induced failure of multiple organs; however, subsequent findings of microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA), decreased platelet count, and worsening acute kidney injury (AKI) pointed to the additional development of TMA. This illustrates how complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS) can be masked in the early stages dominated by sepsis and DIC.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late 40s with no significant past medical history and two previous uneventful pregnancies presented to the emergency department due to a rapidly declining condition. She had sustained a dog bite to her upper lip four days before admission and received a tetanus vaccine for prophylaxis. Although phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) was prescribed at that time, she did not begin the antibiotic since she was unable to retrieve the medication. Subsequently, she developed a high-grade fever (exceeding 40 °C), general malaise, and poor oral intake. Medical care was sought after she experienced numbness and discoloration in her feet. When assessed at a primary-care emergency facility, hypoglycemia was detected, which did not respond to oral glucose or glucagon; the lowest measured blood glucose was 0.6 mmol/L. This necessitated her urgent transfer to our emergency department.

Upon presentation, the patient was conscious and oriented but exhibited signs of sepsis, including mottling and coolness of both lower limbs. She denied any use of herbal medications or other substances, and there was no history of similar symptoms among her family members. Initial laboratory assessment demonstrated significant multisystem involvement characterized by pronounced inflammation, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury, elevated hepatic enzymes, and evidence of coagulopathy. Arterial blood gas analysis indicated a metabolic acidosis, with a pH measured at 7.27 (reference range: 7.35–7.43), and an increased lactate concentration of 5.7 mmol/L (normal <2.5 mmol/L). Despite the administration of high-dose intravenous glucose, she remained hypoglycemic, which was attributed to sepsis. On initial evaluation, urinary output was satisfactory; urine dipstick analysis demonstrated proteinuria (2+) and hematuria (3+).

Owing to her combination of symptoms—including fever, pancytopenia, coagulopathy with acute kidney injury and lactic acidosis—her clinical picture was attributed to disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) secondary to bacteraemia and early sepsis (quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment [qSOFA] score 1/3). Potential surgical etiologies for her mottled extremities, such as necrotising fasciitis, were excluded through both physical examination and imaging studies. Given the seriousness of her symptoms, empiric broad-spectrum intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam (Tazocin) was initiated to target pathogens associated with canine exposure, including Capnocytophaga species. Suspicion for C. canimorsus existed from the outset and was confirmed one week later via both aerobic and anaerobic blood cultures. She was then moved to the intensive care unit (ICU) for enhanced monitoring.

With the presence of pancytopenia and coagulopathy, a haematology consult was requested. Differential diagnosis included acute leukemia and haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis, with haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS) also considered; however, HUS appeared less likely due to findings such as leukopenia, elevated inflammatory markers, normal haptoglobin, and non-compensatory reticulocytes. Flow cytometry was performed and bone marrow biopsy was planned, but after normal flow cytometry results—without increased CD34+ blasts or abnormal phenotypes—the biopsy was cancelled. The overall clinical picture continued to be most consistent with sepsis. Pending further results, she received supportive care with thrombocyte and fibrinogen transfusions.

Following admission to the ICU, the patient developed complete anuria accompanied by a swift decline in renal function. Attempts at vigorous intravenous fluid replacement in combination with loop diuretics proved ineffective. She then manifested septic cardiomyopathy and significant volume overload, necessitating the start of continuous renal replacement therapy. Autoimmune serologies, including antinuclear antibody (ANA) and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA), returned negative results.

By the third day in the ICU, the patient experienced widespread cutaneous hemorrhage characterized by ecchymosis, as well as distal cyanosis and ischemic changes involving multiple fingers and toes; however, distal pulses and sensory function remained intact. These clinical findings were compatible with microvascular thrombotic events associated with disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). In spite of multiorgan dysfunction, the patient did not require vasopressors, apart from the initial administration of stress-dose corticosteroids during early sepsis. She was also treated with albumin infusions in addition to various blood products.

Following eight days of hospitalization, she was moved to the nephrology service due to persistent dialysis-dependent AKI and the need for continued intravenous antibiotics. At the time of transfer, the patient exhibited hemodynamic stability, remained anuric, displayed diffuse oedema, and had numerous hematomas, but was alert and oriented. Antibiotic treatment was narrowed to intravenous ampicillin and subsequently switched to oral Apocillin according to blood culture findings.

During her stay in nephrology, laboratory investigations revealed an improving inflammatory response, reactive leukocytosis, rising platelet count, and stable hemoglobin levels. Renal tests indicated ongoing azotemia, while coagulation studies normalized, consistent with resolution of the previous coagulopathy. Despite initial progress, her clinical status changed when she experienced a sudden, marked decrease in hemoglobin (from 111 g/L to 68 g/L over six days), in the absence of clinically evident bleeding or signs of hemodynamic compromise. Platelet counts remained consistently reduced (30–40×10⁹/L), haptoglobin levels became undetectable, and lactate dehydrogenase increased, all findings compatible with continued hemolysis (see figure 1).

The direct antiglobulin test (DAT), also known as the Coombs test, yielded a positive result for IgG, which led to concerns regarding possible autoimmune haemolytic anaemia. Treatment was initiated with high-dose prednisolone and standard prophylactic measures, in addition to administering multiple blood transfusions over a two-day period. Despite these interventions, the haemolytic activity persisted without improvement under corticosteroid therapy and thrombocytopenia continued, with platelet counts ranging from 41 to 46 × 10⁹/L, prompting additional investigation. Examination of the peripheral blood smear identified 15%–20% schistocytes, indicating microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA) consistent with thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) (see figure 2). It was subsequently considered that the initial DAT result was most likely a false positive related to penicillin use or previous transfusions; a repeat DAT was negative.

Complement assays and measurement of ADAMTS13 activity were ordered. The routinely available complement levels (C3 and C4) were found to be within normal range, and functional testing of the classical, alternative, and lectin pathways also showed normal activity. Subsequent ADAMTS13 activity testing yielded a result of 43.5%, which essentially excluded thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), as TTP generally exhibits activity less than 10%. During the interval awaiting confirmation of these results, plasma exchange (PEX) was commenced as interim therapy before administering the initial dose of eculizumab.

Following an increase in the platelet count to 77×10⁹/L, a renal biopsy was carried out, revealing marked acute tubular necrosis, ischemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi, and occasional crescent formation, consistent with a diagnosis of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

Shiga toxin-producing E. coli-associated HUS (STEC-HUS) was ruled out on the basis of a negative stool PCR analysis.

After the second and third sessions of plasma exchange (PEX), the patient’s spontaneous urine output resumed. Once the first doses of meningococcal vaccines—Menveo (covering serogroups A, C, Y, and W) and Bexsero (targeting serogroup B)—had been administered, eculizumab induction therapy commenced at 900 mg intravenously once per week for 4 weeks. Plans for the second vaccine doses were scheduled for 8 weeks later in the outpatient setting. Since the vaccines had not been provided at least 2 weeks prior to initiation of complement inhibition, prophylactic Apocillin was prescribed.

Comprehensive complement testing was ordered to exclude hereditary complement-mediated TMA, with samples sent to the Skåne Laboratory at Lund University, Sweden. Given logistical challenges and patient safety considerations, three sessions of PEX using Octaplas and one induction dose of eculizumab were performed before specimen collection. Functional complement assays showed a substantially reduced alternative pathway activity (quantitative AH50) at 6%, while levels of complement factors H, I, and B remained within normal limits, and testing for antifactor H antibodies was negative.

Outcome and follow-up:

After extensive medical treatment, the patient’s overall condition is stable, and her urine output is sufficient. However, renal function remains impaired, evidenced by a serum creatinine of 175 µmol/L, and she will continue to be monitored in the nephrology outpatient setting (see figure 4). She is maintained on eculizumab therapy at a dose of 1200 mg intravenously every two weeks, extending four months beyond induction. Comprehensive genetic analysis of complement-related genes did not detect any disease-causing variants; these findings were relayed verbally by the reference laboratory, with the official written report pending submission. Given the serious nature of her presentation, ongoing complement inhibition is recommended, with plans to reconsider cessation if her renal parameters stabilize. The detection of pathogenic variants would support more prolonged treatment and vigilant surveillance for recurrence, but such variants were absent in this individual.

Differential diagnosis of TMA

TMAs encompass several overlapping clinical entities.

In adult patients who present with microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury (AKI), the foremost concern is ruling out thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), which is characterized by a pronounced deficiency in ADAMTS13 activity (<10%). Tools such as the PLASMIC score are useful for stratification prior to obtaining definitive laboratory confirmation. In this case, the patient’s PLASMIC score was 4 out of 7, indicating a low likelihood of TTP, and the measured ADAMTS13 activity was 43.5%, further diminishing the probability of TTP and prompting additional investigation.

Another differential diagnosis to consider is Shiga toxin-producing Escherichia coli hemolytic uremic syndrome (STEC-HUS). This was considered unlikely as the patient’s PCR testing for Shiga toxin returned negative, and there was no evidence of a preceding gastrointestinal illness or supportive microbiological results.

In this patient, the combination of ongoing hemolysis, schistocytes on blood smear, dependence on dialysis for AKI, and renal biopsy findings indicative of thrombotic microangiopathy pointed toward complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS; see figure 5). The exclusion of STEC by PCR and her marked improvement with initiation of eculizumab further supported this diagnosis. The patient’s previously unremarkable medical history argues against a chronic complement abnormality requiring a secondary trigger and instead favors an infection-related onset of CM-HUS.

CM-HUS represents a clinical and pathological diagnosis of exclusion, and complement components C3 and C4 may remain within normal limits throughout the illness. Thus, the presence of normal C3 and C4 values does not rule out CM-HUS, since these markers can be in the reference range during the entire disease course. Of note, the onset of haemolysis was observed only after the patient’s sepsis began to resolve, indicating an infection-induced CM-HUS as opposed to a pre-existing form.

For this patient, standard complement assessments (C3 and C4) along with ADAMTS13 activity were measured immediately prior to initiation of the first plasma exchange (PEX) session. Extended complement analysis was carried out eight days following the completion of PEX and four days after the initial administration of eculizumab. The reduction noted in the alternative pathway is most likely attributable to effects of treatment, rather than to innate complement consumption or activation.

Pathophysiology of CM-HUS: complement dysregulation and infectious triggers

Complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS), formerly known as ‘atypical HUS,’ results from aberrant activation of the alternative complement pathway, frequently in individuals with an underlying genetic susceptibility. Approximately 50% to 70% of cases involve genetic alterations or autoantibodies directed against complement regulatory components such as factor H, factor I, factor B, or membrane cofactor protein (CD46). Despite the presence of these predisposing factors, penetrance is variable, with overt disease generally requiring an inciting event—most often infections, pregnancy, autoimmune conditions, or certain drugs.

Capnocytophaga canimorsus, though infrequently documented, is capable of stimulating complement pathways, especially in vulnerable populations. This organism exhibits heterogeneous sensitivity to serum complement: while many strains are complement-sensitive, certain capsule-bearing isolates display partial resistance, underscoring the role of complement-mediated immunity in controlling infection. This dynamic creates a clinical dilemma: activation of the complement system is necessary for pathogen clearance but, in certain hosts, may precipitate dysregulated endothelial injury and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

Our case contributes to the limited but increasing number of reports that associate C. canimorsus with complement-mediated TMAs. For instance, Bjørkto et al. detailed a case in which C. canimorsus-induced CM-HUS occurred in a patient carrying a complement factor B mutation and was managed successfully with eculizumab. Distinct from that report, comprehensive genetic testing in our patient did not reveal any pathogenic or likely pathogenic complement gene alterations, supporting the concept of infection-induced CM-HUS occurring in the absence of a proven hereditary complement predisposition.

Therapeutic strategy and clinical dilemma

Managing CM-HUS in the presence of ongoing infection poses distinct difficulties. Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody directed against complement component 5, represents the standard therapy for CM-HUS and significantly enhances both hematological and renal outcomes, especially when commenced promptly. Postponed administration is linked to suboptimal renal recovery and a consequent increase in the likelihood of developing end-stage kidney disease.

On the other hand, plasma exchange (PEX) is lifesaving for TTP, yet in CM-HUS it offers only short-term improvement, primarily by removing circulating complement byproducts, inflammatory mediators, or autoantibodies, rather than addressing the primary defect within the complement pathway. For our patient, urine output resumed after the last PEX treatments but prior to eculizumab, signifying merely a temporary stabilization. Lasting hematological and renal improvement was observed only subsequent to complement inhibition.

A major therapeutic challenge involves the administration of complement blockade during ongoing bacteremia, since terminal complement proteins are crucial for protection against encapsulated organisms such as Neisseria and Capnocytophaga, heightening concerns regarding the infectious risk associated with eculizumab. In this scenario, eculizumab therapy began once blood cultures had cleared, thereby reducing the threat of uncontrolled infection. Immunization, antimicrobial prophylaxis, and vigilant follow-up are vital when prescribing complement inhibitors.

Conclusion and broader implications

Timely identification of CM-HUS and rapid commencement of complement inhibition therapy are critical factors influencing positive outcomes. In this case, the patient reached hematological remission and experienced partial improvement of renal function, which aligns with existing data that indicate renal status and the timing of eculizumab initiation as primary prognostic indicators.

While CM-HUS associated with C. canimorsus is exceedingly uncommon, this report underscores the need to assess for infectious causes—including rare pathogens—in individuals presenting with newly diagnosed TMA. Enhanced clinical vigilance and advancements in molecular diagnostic techniques may facilitate recognition of additional similar cases.
