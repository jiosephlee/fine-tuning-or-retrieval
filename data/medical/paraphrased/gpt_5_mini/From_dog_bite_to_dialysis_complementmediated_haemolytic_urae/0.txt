Title: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Tobias Evason Fredriksen, Burhan-Ud-Din Mian, Szilveszter Dolgos

Abstract:

Summary: An otherwise healthy woman in her late 40s developed rapidly advancing sepsis four days after sustaining a dog bite. Her clinical course was marked by disseminated intravascular coagulation, pancytopenia, and acute kidney injury, which progressed to complete anuria, necessitating dialysis. Blood cultures identified Capnocytophaga canimorsus bacteraemia. Although initial management with antibiotics and supportive interventions led to some clinical stabilization, she subsequently experienced abrupt onset of isolated haemolysis alongside ongoing thrombocytopaenia. Laboratory evaluation and renal biopsy demonstrated evidence of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), leading to a diagnosis of complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS). Hematologic remission was obtained following plasma exchange and eculizumab therapy, with partial renal function recovery. This case exemplifies C. canimorsus infection as an uncommon precipitant of CM-HUS in immunocompetent adults, detailing the diagnostic evaluation, complexities in differential diagnosis, and the intricate nature of both the clinical progression and treatment strategies for TMA in the context of sepsis.

Background:

Capnocytophaga canimorsus is an encapsulated, gram-negative rod that grows slowly and is typically part of the normal oropharyngeal microbiota in both dogs and cats. Although infections in humans are uncommon, transmission may result from animal bites, scratches, or even close proximity to these animals. The spectrum of clinical manifestations is broad, encompassing isolated cases of meningitis as well as rapidly progressive sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), multiorgan dysfunction, and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Mortality rates remain significant, reaching up to 30% despite general susceptibility to a wide range of antibiotics.

Among the Capnocytophaga genus, C. canimorsus is most frequently associated with invasive zoonotic infections in humans. Its exact identification is often delayed due to both its demanding growth requirements and its prolonged cultivation time in standard laboratory cultures, leading to the growing adoption of molecular diagnostic approaches, such as real-time nanopore sequencing.

In our reported case, C. canimorsus infection manifested with septic shock, profound leukopaenia, and a coagulopathy compatible with DIC. The initial disease course was dominated by multiorgan dysfunction related to sepsis; however, the subsequent onset of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), a drop in platelet count, and worsening acute kidney injury (AKI) pointed toward an additional TMA process. This progression underscores how complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) can be masked by the overlapping features of early sepsis and DIC.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late 40s with no significant past medical history and two prior uneventful pregnancies presented to the emergency department due to a swiftly deteriorating health status. Four days before hospitalization, she suffered a dog bite to her upper lip and received a tetanus vaccine as a preventive measure. Although phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) was prescribed immediately, she did not begin the antibiotic regimen because she was unable to pick up the medication. Subsequently, she developed a high-grade fever (exceeding 40 °C), general malaise, and decreased oral intake, only seeking medical evaluation after experiencing numbness and discoloration in her feet. At the initial primary-care emergency visit, she was found to be hypoglycaemic, unresponsive to both oral glucose and glucagon administration, with a minimum blood glucose level of 0.6 mmol/L, leading to her urgent transfer to our emergency department.

Upon admission, the patient was conscious and interactive; however, she displayed signs consistent with sepsis, including mottling and coolness in the lower limbs. She denied the use of herbal preparations or any other substances, and there was no history of similar illnesses among family members. Initial laboratory investigations demonstrated profound systemic disturbance characterized by severe inflammation, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury (AKI), elevated hepatic enzymes, and evidence of coagulopathy. Arterial blood gas assessment showed a metabolic acidosis, with a pH measured at 7.27 (reference range 7.35–7.43), and a lactate concentration elevated to 5.7 (<2.5 mmol/L). Despite receiving substantial intravenous glucose administration, hypoglycaemia persisted and was attributed to the underlying sepsis. On presentation, urine output was adequate; urinalysis revealed proteinuria (2+) and hematuria (3+).

Based on her array of symptoms—fever, pancytopenia, coagulopathy accompanied by AKI and lactic acidosis—her clinical status was interpreted as DIC secondary to bacteraemia and incipient sepsis (quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) score 1/3). A potential surgical etiology for her mottled extremities, such as necrotising fasciitis, was excluded following comprehensive clinical examination and imaging studies. Due to the gravity of her illness, empirical broad-spectrum intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam (Tazocin) was initiated, providing coverage for pathogens typically associated with dog exposure, including Capnocytophaga species. C. canimorsus infection was suspected from the outset, and subsequent blood cultures, both aerobic and anaerobic, confirmed the diagnosis 7 days later. She was then admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) for more intensive observation and management.

With concurrent pancytopenia and coagulopathy, consultation with haematology was requested. The differential included acute leukaemia and haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis. Haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS) was also entertained, although this was deemed less probable given the presence of leukopaenia, elevated inflammatory parameters, normal haptoglobin, and a lack of reticulocyte response. Flow cytometry was carried out and a bone marrow biopsy was initially considered; however, normal findings—including absence of elevated CD34+ blasts or aberrant phenotypes—resulted in the biopsy being deferred. The overall clinical picture aligned most closely with sepsis. During the period in which laboratory results were pending, the patient received supportive therapy, including thrombocyte and fibrinogen transfusions.

Following admission to the ICU, the patient experienced complete cessation of urine output and a rapid decline in renal function. Efforts to restore volume status using intensive fluid administration combined with loop diuretics were ineffective. She subsequently developed septic cardiomyopathy complicated by fluid overload, necessitating initiation of continuous renal replacement therapy. Autoimmune screening, including antinuclear antibodies (ANA) and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), yielded negative results.

By the third ICU day, the patient manifested widespread cutaneous hemorrhage, ecchymosis, and distal cyanosis and ischemia affecting multiple toes and fingers, although distal pulses and sensory function remained intact. These clinical features were indicative of microthrombosis associated with DIC. Despite significant organ dysfunction, the patient did not require vasopressor support aside from stress-dose corticosteroids used during the early septic phase. Management also included the administration of albumin as well as other blood components.

Following an 8-day hospital stay, the patient was moved to the nephrology ward for continued management of dialysis-dependent AKI and administration of intravenous antibiotics. Upon arrival, she remained haemodynamically stable but exhibited anuria, widespread oedema, and several haematomas, while maintaining full alertness and orientation. Guided by blood culture findings, her antibiotic regimen was narrowed first to intravenous ampicillin, and subsequently to oral Apocillin.

During her time in the nephrology ward, laboratory investigations revealed a downward trend in inflammatory markers, a reactive leukocytosis, gradual recovery of platelet counts, and stable haemoglobin concentrations. Renal function assessments demonstrated ongoing azotaemia. Meanwhile, coagulation indices returned to normal, suggesting the coagulopathy was resolving. Table 2 provides a summary of her laboratory values during this period.

Although her condition initially appeared to improve, her clinical course took a turn when she experienced an abrupt, isolated decline in haemoglobin levels (from 111 to 68 g/L over 6 days) without any clinical signs of active bleeding or haemodynamic compromise. Platelet counts remained consistently low (30–40×10⁹/L), while haptoglobin levels became undetectable and LDH concentrations increased, collectively signifying sustained haemolysis (figure 1).

The direct antiglobulin test (DAT) (Coombs test) demonstrated positivity for IgG, prompting consideration of autoimmune haemolytic anaemia. Treatment commenced with high-dose prednisolone in conjunction with standard prophylactic measures, and multiple blood transfusions were administered over a 48-hour period. Due to continued haemolysis unresponsive to corticosteroids and persistently low platelet counts (41–46 × 10⁹/L), an expanded diagnostic workup was undertaken. Examination of the peripheral blood smear showed 15%–20% schistocytes, indicating the presence of MAHA and raising the suspicion for TMA (see figure 2). The initial DAT result was probably a false positive, possibly attributable to prior penicillin exposure or transfusions; subsequent testing yielded negative results.

Further diagnostic procedures included assessments of complement components and ADAMTS13 activity. Standard complement measurements (C3 and C4) were within reference intervals, and functional assessments of the classical, alternative, and lectin complement pathways were unremarkable. The ADAMTS13 activity result became available soon after, measuring 43.5%, thus excluding thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), which generally exhibits activities below 10%. While these findings were pending, plasma exchange (PEX) was started as an interim therapy prior to the administration of the initial eculizumab dose.

Following an increase in thrombocyte count to 77×10⁹/L, a renal biopsy was undertaken. Histopathological examination revealed marked acute tubular necrosis, glomerular ischemia with the presence of fibrin thrombi, and sporadic crescent formation, findings indicative of TMA.

The most significant diagnostic evidence was provided by the light microscopy images depicted in figure 3. Immunofluorescence studies, which did not detect any immune complex deposition, and electron microscopy, which showed no signs of primary glomerulopathy, are not included.

A diagnosis of Shiga toxin-producing E. coli-associated HUS (STEC-HUS) was excluded due to a negative stool PCR test.

After the patient underwent the second and third PEX procedures, normal spontaneous urine output resumed. Eculizumab induction therapy was begun (900 mg intravenously once a week for 4 weeks) after administration of the first doses of meningococcal vaccines—Menveo (covering serogroups A, C, Y, and W) and Bexsero (for serogroup B). The subsequent doses of these vaccines were scheduled for 8 weeks later in an outpatient setting. Because vaccination had not occurred 2 weeks before initiation of complement inhibition, prophylactic Apocillin was administered.

To exclude hereditary complement-mediated TMA, comprehensive complement analysis was conducted at the Skåne Laboratory (Lund University, Sweden). Due to practical and safety factors, three Octaplas-based PEX sessions and one eculizumab induction dose had already taken place prior to sample collection. Functional assessment of complement included measurement of alternative pathway activity (quantitative AH50), which was notably suppressed (6%), with normal concentrations of complement factors H, I, and B, and a negative test for antifactor H antibodies.

Outcome and follow-up:

After extensive medical intervention, the patient currently exhibits stable overall health and maintains satisfactory urine output. Despite this, renal function is still compromised, as indicated by a serum creatinine level of 175 µmol/L. Ongoing care will be managed through the nephrology outpatient clinic (figure 4). The patient remains on maintenance eculizumab therapy (1200 mg intravenously every 2 weeks), scheduled to continue for 4 months following induction. Comprehensive genetic testing for complement system abnormalities has not revealed any pathogenic mutations; this information was provided orally by the reference laboratory, with the formal written documentation still awaited at the time of manuscript submission. In light of these findings, complement inhibition therapy is being sustained due to the disease’s severity, with plans to reconsider its cessation if there is stabilization of renal function. The detection of pathogenic variants in complement-related genes would have supported a longer duration of therapy along with vigilant monitoring for potential relapse. However, such variants were absent in this case.

Differential diagnosis of TMA

TMAs represent a spectrum of closely related clinical syndromes.

When adults present with MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI, the immediate concern is to rule out thrombotic TTP, which is characterized by marked ADAMTS13 deficiency (<10%). Tools such as the PLASMIC scoring system assist in preliminary assessment prior to definitive laboratory confirmation. In this case, the patient’s PLASMIC score was 4/7, indicating a low likelihood of TTP, and ADAMTS13 activity measured 43.5%, further decreasing the suspicion for TTP and directing the diagnostic evaluation accordingly.

STEC-HUS should also be considered in the differential diagnosis. However, PCR testing for Shiga toxin was negative in this patient. Moreover, the lack of a preceding gastrointestinal illness and negative microbiologic findings diminish the possibility of STEC-HUS.

The collective presentation of ongoing hemolysis, presence of schistocytes, dialysis-dependent AKI, and renal biopsy findings indicative of TMA all pointed towards a diagnosis of CM-HUS (figure 5). This diagnosis was reinforced by the negative PCR for STEC and the patient’s prompt response to eculizumab treatment. Additionally, the patient’s limited medical background makes the scenario of an underlying complement abnormality less plausible, instead favoring an infection-induced onset of CM-HUS.

CM-HUS is diagnosed based on clinical and pathological findings after excluding other potential causes, and it should be noted that complement measurements (C3/C4) may remain within normal limits throughout the illness. Thus, even when complement levels are unremarkable, CM-HUS cannot be ruled out, since these values can stay within the reference range during the entire disease process. Moreover, in this case, haemolysis manifested only after the patient's sepsis began to resolve, which points toward an infection-induced CM-HUS rather than an underlying, pre-existing form.

For our case, standard complement assessments (C3/C4) as well as measurement of ADAMTS13 activity were carried out directly prior to initiating the first plasma exchange (PEX) treatment. Comprehensive complement analysis took place 8 days post the final PEX and 4 days after the administration of the first dose of eculizumab. The diminished activity observed in the alternative complement pathway most likely reflects a consequence of therapeutic interventions, as opposed to an inherent depletion or spontaneous activation of the complement system.

Pathophysiology of CM-HUS: complement dysregulation and infectious triggers

CM-HUS (previously known as ‘atypical HUS’) results from uncontrolled activation of the alternative complement pathway, typically in individuals with underlying genetic susceptibility. Genetic mutations or autoantibodies that impair complement regulators—including factor H, factor I, factor B, or membrane cofactor protein (CD46)—can be detected in approximately 50%–70% of cases. Despite this association, penetrance is variable, and overt disease usually requires a secondary insult, such as infection, pregnancy, autoimmune disorders, or exposure to specific drugs.

Although infrequently documented, C. canimorsus has the capacity to stimulate complement pathways, particularly in hosts with increased vulnerability. The pathogen demonstrates heterogeneous serum sensitivity; most isolates are susceptible to complement-mediated lysis, whereas certain capsule-bearing strains possess partial resistance, highlighting the critical role of complement in host defence. This dynamic introduces a clinical dilemma: effective complement activation is necessary for pathogen clearance but, in predisposed individuals, may provoke unchecked endothelial injury and TMA.

The current case contributes to the limited but expanding evidence associating C. canimorsus infection with complement-mediated TMAs. Bjørkto et al, for instance, detailed a case involving CM-HUS following C. canimorsus infection in a patient harboring a complement factor B mutation, where eculizumab therapy proved beneficial. Unlike this previously documented scenario, genetic analysis in our case did not reveal any pathogenic or likely pathogenic complement gene variants, suggesting an infection-driven CM-HUS occurring independently of hereditary complement dysregulation.

Therapeutic strategy and clinical dilemma

Managing CM-HUS amid active infection entails distinct difficulties. Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody that inhibits the terminal complement component 5, constitutes the primary therapeutic approach for CM-HUS and leads to significant improvements in both haematological and renal parameters, especially with early administration. Conversely, postponement of therapy initiation correlates with suboptimal renal recovery and elevates the likelihood of progression to end-stage kidney disease.

On the other hand, while plasma exchange (PEX) is critical and life-saving in TTP, its effect in CM-HUS is limited and short-lived. PEX transiently removes circulating complement components, inflammatory factors, or autoantibodies, but fails to rectify the inherent dysregulation of the complement cascade. In the described case, diuresis was observed to resume following the final sessions of PEX but prior to the start of eculizumab therapy, suggesting only a fleeting period of stabilization. Durable haematological and renal improvements manifested only after the introduction of complement inhibition.

An important clinical consideration involves the administration of complement inhibitors in the presence of ongoing bacteraemia. Terminal complement factors play a pivotal role in host defence against encapsulated organisms, such as Neisseria and Capnocytophaga, thus raising concerns regarding infectious complications linked to eculizumab therapy. In this report, eculizumab treatment commenced only after blood cultures were confirmed negative, thereby reducing the risk of exacerbating the infection. Use of vaccines, antimicrobial prophylaxis, and vigilant follow-up are vital adjuncts when prescribing complement-blocking agents.

Conclusion and broader implications

Timely diagnosis of CM-HUS and early commencement of complement inhibition represent the key factors linked to positive clinical outcomes. In this case, the patient attained haematological remission alongside partial improvement in renal function, mirroring existing data which indicate that renal status and the interval before eculizumab therapy are critical prognostic indicators.

Despite the rarity of C. canimorsus-associated CM-HUS, this case underscores the need to consider infectious causes—including atypical pathogens—in patients who develop TMA of recent onset. Enhanced clinical awareness and advancements in molecular diagnostic methods could facilitate the detection of additional instances of this condition.
