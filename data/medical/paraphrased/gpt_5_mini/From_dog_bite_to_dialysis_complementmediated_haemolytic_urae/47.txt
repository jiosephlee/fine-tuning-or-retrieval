Title: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Tobias Evason Fredriksen, Burhan-Ud-Din Mian, Szilveszter Dolgos

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late 40s with no prior health issues developed rapidly worsening sepsis four days after being bitten by a dog. She experienced disseminated intravascular coagulation, pancytopenia, and acute kidney injury, eventually progressing to complete absence of urine output and dependence on dialysis. Blood cultures identified Capnocytophaga canimorsus bacteraemia. Although her condition initially improved with antibiotics and supportive measures, she subsequently developed abrupt, isolated haemolysis and sustained thrombocytopaenia. Laboratory tests and findings from a renal biopsy established a diagnosis of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), specifically complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS). Treatment with plasma exchange and initiation of eculizumab led to resolution of haematological abnormalities and partial recovery of renal function. This report emphasizes that C. canimorsus can serve as an unusual cause of CM-HUS in otherwise healthy adults and details the patient’s diagnostic evaluation, the complexities encountered in differential diagnosis, and the multifaceted clinical and therapeutic challenges posed by TMA after sepsis.

Background:

Capnocytophaga canimorsus is an encapsulated, slow-growing, gram-negative bacillus present in the normal oropharyngeal flora of cats and dogs. Infections in humans are uncommon and can be acquired through bites, scratches, or even close proximity. The spectrum of clinical manifestations is broad, ranging from localized meningitis to rapidly progressive sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), failure of multiple organ systems, and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Although this organism generally responds to a wide range of antibiotics, the mortality rate may be as high as 30%.

Among the Capnocytophaga genus, C. canimorsus is most often implicated in severe zoonotic disease in humans. Due to its demanding growth requirements and slow proliferation in cultures, identification is frequently delayed, leading to increased adoption of molecular diagnostic techniques, including real-time nanopore sequencing.

In the case described, C. canimorsus infection was associated with septic shock, profound leukopaenia, and coagulopathy indicative of DIC. The early phase of illness was primarily characterized by sepsis-driven involvement of multiple organs, while the subsequent onset of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), reduced platelet count, and worsening acute kidney injury (AKI) pointed to a secondary TMA. This progression underscores the possibility that complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) can be masked in the initial period dominated by sepsis and DIC.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late 40s with no prior health issues and a history of two uneventful pregnancies arrived at the emergency department due to a rapidly deteriorating clinical state. She had sustained a dog bite to her upper lip four days earlier and received a tetanus vaccine for prophylaxis. Although she was prescribed phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) at that time, she did not begin the antibiotic therapy as she was unable to obtain the medication. She subsequently developed a high fever (>40 °C), general malaise, decreased oral intake, and only sought further care when she experienced numbness and discoloration in her feet. At a primary care emergency facility, she was found to be hypoglycemic, with levels unresponsive to oral glucose and glucagon, reaching a low of 0.6 mmol/L, which resulted in her urgent referral to our emergency department.

Upon admission, the patient was awake and answered appropriately, though she showed signs consistent with sepsis, including mottled, cool lower legs. She denied any use of herbal products or other substances, and there was no history of similar illnesses among her family members. Initial laboratory investigations demonstrated profound systemic dysfunction, with pronounced inflammation, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury, elevated liver enzymes, and evidence of coagulopathy. Table 1 presents the main laboratory results.

Arterial blood gas analysis identified metabolic acidosis with a pH of 7.27 (reference: 7.35–7.43) and a lactate concentration of 5.7 mmol/L (reference: <2.5 mmol/L). Despite administration of high-dose intravenous glucose, her hypoglycaemia persisted and was considered secondary to sepsis. Upon arrival, diuresis remained sufficient; urinalysis showed protein at 2+ and blood at 3+.

Based on her array of symptoms—fever, pancytopenia, coagulopathy with acute kidney injury (AKI) and lactic acidosis—her clinical picture was regarded as disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) resulting from bacteraemia and early sepsis (quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) score 1 out of 3). A surgical etiology for her mottled limbs, such as necrotising fasciitis, was excluded following both clinical assessment and imaging studies. Owing to the seriousness of her case, empiric intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam (Tazocin) was initiated to provide coverage for pathogens linked to dog exposures, including Capnocytophaga species. Importantly, suspicion for C. canimorsus was raised from the outset, and after seven days, the organism was identified on both aerobic and anaerobic blood cultures. She was thereafter admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) for enhanced surveillance.

Hematology was consulted given the findings of pancytopenia and coagulopathy. The working differential diagnoses included acute leukemia and haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis. Haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS) was evaluated, but judged less probable because of leukopaenia, elevated inflammatory parameters, normal haptoglobin, and low, non-compensatory reticulocyte count. Flow cytometric analysis was carried out, and bone marrow biopsy scheduled; however, since results showed normal findings—with neither increased CD34+ blasts nor abnormal cellular profiles—the biopsy was subsequently cancelled. Overall, the clinical course was deemed consistent with sepsis. Pending further investigations, supportive transfusions of platelets and fibrinogen were provided.

Following admission to the intensive care unit, the patient became fully anuric and her renal function deteriorated rapidly. Despite attempts at aggressive intravenous fluid replacement in combination with loop diuretics, there was no improvement. In the ensuing period, she developed septic cardiomyopathy complicated by fluid overload, necessitating commencement of continuous renal replacement therapy. Serologic evaluations for autoimmunity, including antinuclear antibody (ANA) and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA) assays, were negative.

By ICU day 3, she exhibited widespread cutaneous bleeding with ecchymoses, along with cyanotic and ischemic changes on the distal aspects of multiple toes and fingers; notably, pulses and sensory function remained intact. These symptoms indicated ongoing microvascular thrombotic processes occurring in the setting of disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). Although multiple organ systems were compromised, vasopressors were not required other than the administration of stress-dose corticosteroids during her early septic period. The patient was also given albumin infusions in addition to other transfused blood products.

Following eight days of hospital admission, the patient was moved to the nephrology unit due to dialysis-dependent acute kidney injury (AKI) and the need for ongoing intravenous antibiotics. Upon arrival, she remained haemodynamically stable but was anuric, exhibited generalized swelling, and had multiple bruises, although she was conscious and oriented. Based on blood culture findings, her antibiotic regimen was stepped down to intravenous ampicillin and subsequently switched to oral Apocillin.

During her stay in the nephrology ward, laboratory assessments demonstrated improvement in markers of inflammation, the presence of reactive leukocytosis, platelet counts trending upward, and stable hemoglobin concentration. Renal function parameters, however, continued to reflect azotaemia. Coagulation studies returned to within normal ranges, suggesting the coagulopathy was resolving. Table 2 presents the relevant laboratory data at this time.

Despite an initial positive trajectory, her condition subsequently worsened when she experienced an abrupt and significant decline in hemoglobin (from 111 to 68 g/L across 6 days) in the absence of overt bleeding or cardiovascular compromise. Her platelet count stayed consistently low (30–40×10⁹/L), haptoglobin dropped below detectable limits, and LDH levels increased, pointing toward persistent haemolysis (figure 1).

A direct antiglobulin test (DAT) (Coombs test) showed positivity for IgG, which indicated a potential diagnosis of autoimmune haemolytic anaemia. The patient was started on high-dose prednisolone and routine prophylactic measures, in addition to receiving multiple blood transfusions over a 2-day span. As the haemolysis did not improve with corticosteroid treatment and platelet counts remained decreased (41–46 × 10⁹/L), more comprehensive assessment was undertaken. Examination of the peripheral blood smear demonstrated 15%–20% schistocytes, findings compatible with MAHA and suggestive of TMA (figure 2). The initial DAT result was most likely falsely positive due to either penicillin exposure or previous transfusions; a subsequent DAT was negative.

Complement studies and ADAMTS13 activity were requested for further investigation. Standard locally performed complement assays (C3/C4) returned within the reference range, and functional assessments of the classical, alternative, and lectin complement pathways were also normal. The report of ADAMTS13 activity, received soon thereafter, showed a value of 43.5%, thereby excluding thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), which typically manifests with activity levels less than 10%. During the interval in which these results were pending, plasma exchange (PEX) was commenced as a temporary treatment, preceding initiation of eculizumab therapy.

When the platelet count rose to 77×10⁹/L, a renal biopsy was conducted, revealing pronounced acute tubular necrosis, glomerular ischemia with fibrin thrombi, and occasional crescent formation, indicative of TMA.

The principal diagnostic evidence in this patient was provided by light microscopy findings illustrated in figure 3. Immunofluorescence and electron microscopy were excluded from presentation, as immunofluorescence showed an absence of immune complex deposition and electron microscopy found no signs of primary glomerular disease.

A negative stool PCR test excluded Shiga toxin-producing E. coli-associated HUS (STEC-HUS).

After the second and third plasma exchange (PEX) procedures, the patient resumed spontaneous urine output. Eculizumab induction therapy was started (900 mg intravenously weekly for four weeks) following administration of the initial doses of meningococcal vaccines—Menveo (covering serogroups A, C, Y, and W) and Bexsero (serogroup B). Plans were made to administer the second vaccine doses eight weeks later in the outpatient setting. Since the vaccines had not been provided two weeks before initiating complement inhibition, prophylactic Apocillin was administered.

Comprehensive complement studies were ordered from the Skåne Laboratory at Lund University, Sweden, to exclude hereditary complement-mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). For logistical and patient safety reasons, three sessions of PEX using Octaplas and a single induction dose of eculizumab were completed prior to sample collection. The functional complement assessment included quantitative alternative pathway activity (AH50), which was profoundly diminished (6%), whereas levels of complement factors H, I, and B were within normal ranges, and testing for antifactor H antibodies yielded negative results.

Outcome and follow-up:

After undergoing intensive medical treatment, the patient is stable overall, exhibiting sufficient urine output. Renal function remains impaired, indicated by a serum creatinine level of 175 µmol/L, and she will be monitored in the nephrology outpatient clinic (figure 4). Eculizumab maintenance therapy (1200 mg intravenously every 2 weeks) is ongoing and has continued for 4 months following initial treatment. Comprehensive genetic testing of the complement system has not revealed any disease-causing mutations; preliminary results were delivered verbally by the reference laboratory, with the formal written documentation awaited at the time of submission. Given the gravity of her clinical presentation, complement inhibition is being maintained, with future reevaluation planned should kidney function stabilize. The discovery of pathogenic complement gene changes would support prolonged therapy and vigilant surveillance for recurrence; however, none were found in this individual.

Differential diagnosis of TMA

TMAs encompass several overlapping clinical entities.

When an adult presents with MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI, the immediate focus is ruling out thrombotic TTP, which is characterized by a marked reduction in ADAMTS13 activity (<10%). While clinical scoring systems such as PLASMIC can aid in stratifying patients pending laboratory confirmation, definitive diagnosis relies on test results. This individual had a PLASMIC score of 4 out of 7, indicating a low likelihood of TTP, and an ADAMTS13 activity of 43.5%, making TTP improbable and prompting additional evaluation.

STEC-HUS also warrants consideration. Shiga toxin PCR testing was negative, and the absence of preceding gastrointestinal symptoms coupled with negative microbial investigations diminished the suspicion for STEC-HUS.

Persisting hemolysis, schistocytes, ongoing dialysis-dependent AKI, and kidney histology demonstrating TMA all supported a diagnosis of CM-HUS (figure 5). The diagnosis was bolstered by negative STEC PCR testing and rapid clinical recovery after initiation of eculizumab. The patient’s negligible past medical history argues against an underlying complement disorder requiring a ‘second hit’ and favors an infection-induced case of CM-HUS.

CM-HUS is a clinicopathological diagnosis made after other causes have been excluded, and complement components C3 and C4 may remain normal throughout the illness. Therefore, normal values of C3 and C4 do not rule out CM-HUS, as these parameters can stay within normal limits during its progression. Of particular note, signs of haemolysis emerged only after the patient's sepsis began to resolve, indicating that CM-HUS was likely triggered by infection, rather than being present beforehand.

In this case, the patient had standard complement assays (C3 and C4) and measurement of ADAMTS13 activity drawn immediately prior to initiating the first plasma exchange (PEX). Broader complement studies were obtained 8 days following the last PEX procedure and 4 days after the initial administration of eculizumab. The detected suppression of the alternative pathway is most likely attributable to therapeutic interventions rather than primary complement exhaustion or activation.

Pathophysiology of CM-HUS: complement dysregulation and infectious triggers

Complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS), formerly known as 'atypical HUS', develops due to abnormal activation of the alternative complement pathway, typically occurring in individuals with underlying genetic susceptibilities. Approximately 50% to 70% of affected patients possess genetic mutations or autoantibodies targeting complement regulatory proteins—including factor H, factor I, factor B, or membrane cofactor protein (CD46). Nevertheless, expression of disease is not universal among carriers, with clinical onset generally necessitating an additional precipitating factor such as infection, pregnancy, autoimmune disorders, or certain pharmaceuticals.

Capnocytophaga canimorsus has infrequently been documented to stimulate complement pathways, especially in hosts with heightened vulnerability. Importantly, this organism exhibits heterogeneity in its sensitivity to serum complement: while numerous isolates are susceptible, some encapsulated strains demonstrate partial complement resistance. Accordingly, complement function plays a pivotal role in host immunity. This presents a clinical dilemma: though complement activation is necessary for pathogen eradication, in individuals with predisposition, this response may provoke excessive endothelial injury and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

This case further expands the limited but emerging literature associating C. canimorsus with complement-mediated TMA. Of note, Bjørkto et al previously described a case of CM-HUS triggered by C. canimorsus in a patient carrying a complement factor B mutation, which was successfully managed using eculizumab. Distinct from that earlier report, our patient exhibited no pathogenic or potentially pathogenic genetic abnormalities in complement-related genes, indicating the occurrence of infection-induced CM-HUS absent of documented inherited complement dysfunction.

Therapeutic strategy and clinical dilemma

Managing CM-HUS amid ongoing infection is particularly challenging. Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody directed at terminal complement component 5, represents the established treatment for CM-HUS, leading to significant improvements in hematologic and renal parameters, especially with early administration. Initiating eculizumab later increases the likelihood of incomplete renal recovery and heightens the risk for advancing to end-stage kidney disease.

PEX is crucial for survival in TTP but yields only short-term benefits in CM-HUS by removing circulating complement fragments, inflammatory molecules, or autoantibodies; however, it does not address the fundamental complement system abnormality. In the presented case, the patient began producing urine following the final PEX sessions and before receiving eculizumab, indicating brief stabilization only. Long-lasting hematological and renal improvement was observed only after complement inhibition was implemented.

A major therapeutic consideration involves administering complement inhibitors during active bacteremia, as terminal complement components play a vital role in defending against encapsulated bacteria such as Neisseria and Capnocytophaga, thereby increasing the concern for eculizumab-associated infectious complications. In this instance, eculizumab was started only after the patient's blood cultures showed no growth, thus reducing the likelihood of ongoing infection. Employing vaccination, antimicrobial prophylaxis, and vigilant observation is imperative when complement inhibitors are used.

Conclusion and broader implications

Timely identification of CM-HUS and early commencement of complement inhibition are critical for a positive prognosis. The patient described attained haematological remission and experienced partial improvement in renal function, which aligns with existing data indicating that both renal status and the interval before starting eculizumab are key prognostic factors.

While CM-HUS linked to C. canimorsus is exceedingly uncommon, this report underscores the need to evaluate infectious causes—including rare pathogens—in individuals presenting with new TMA. Greater clinical vigilance and advancements in molecular diagnostic techniques could facilitate the recognition of additional instances.
