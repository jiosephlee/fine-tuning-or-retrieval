Title: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Tobias Evason Fredriksen, Burhan-Ud-Din Mian, Szilveszter Dolgos

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late 40s with no prior health issues presented with rapidly worsening sepsis four days after sustaining a dog bite. Her clinical course was complicated by disseminated intravascular coagulation, pancytopenia, and acute kidney injury, leading ultimately to complete anuria requiring dialysis. Blood cultures identified Capnocytophaga canimorsus bacteraemia. Although her condition showed initial improvement with antibiotic therapy and supportive measures, she subsequently developed abrupt, isolated haemolysis and persistent thrombocytopaenia. Laboratory testing and renal biopsy established a diagnosis of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), specifically complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS). Haematological remission was achieved after plasma exchange and the initiation of eculizumab, with partial restoration of renal function. This case illustrates C. canimorsus infection as an unusual cause of CM-HUS in immunocompetent adults and describes the diagnostic evaluation, challenges in establishing a differential diagnosis, and the intricate nature of the clinical progression and management of TMA after sepsis.

Background:

Capnocytophaga canimorsus is a slow-growing, encapsulated gram-negative bacillus commonly present in the normal oral flora of dogs and cats. Human cases are uncommon, with transmission possible not only through bites and scratches but also via close exposure. The clinical spectrum is broad, ranging from isolated episodes of meningitis to rapidly progressive sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), multiorgan dysfunction, and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Even though the organism typically remains sensitive to many antibiotics, the mortality rate may reach up to 30%.

Among Capnocytophaga species, C. canimorsus is most frequently associated with invasive zoonotic infections in humans. Diagnostic efforts may be hampered by its fastidious growth characteristics and prolonged incubation time in culture, leading to increased reliance on molecular diagnostic modalities such as real-time nanopore sequencing.

In the patient described, infection with C. canimorsus manifested as septic shock, profound leukopaenia, and a coagulopathy indicating DIC. Initially, sepsis with multiorgan involvement predominated, but subsequent onset of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), a drop in platelet count, and aggravating acute kidney injury (AKI) pointed to a secondary TMA. This underscores the potential for complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) to be concealed during the early period of sepsis and DIC.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late 40s with no significant medical history and two previous uncomplicated pregnancies arrived at the emergency department due to a swiftly deteriorating clinical status. Four days earlier, she had sustained a dog bite on her upper lip and was administered a tetanus vaccine as prophylaxis. Although phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) was prescribed at the time, she did not commence antibiotic therapy because she was unable to obtain the medication. Subsequently, she experienced a fever exceeding 40 °C, malaise, and decreased oral intake, ultimately seeking care when she noticed numbness and discoloration in her feet. At a primary care emergency facility, she was found to have hypoglycemia unresponsive to both oral glucose and glucagon, reaching a low point of 0.6 mmol/L, which led to her urgent transfer to our emergency department.

Upon presentation, the patient was conscious and interacted appropriately, but exhibited signs of sepsis, including mottling and coolness in her lower limbs. She denied any use of herbal products or other agents, and there was no history of similar illness among family members. Initial laboratory assessment indicated profound systemic abnormalities: intense inflammatory response, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury (AKI), elevated hepatic enzymes, and evidence of coagulopathy. Arterial blood gas demonstrated a metabolic acidosis with a pH of 7.27 (reference range 7.35–7.43) and a lactate concentration of 5.7 (<2.5 mmol/L). Despite administration of substantial intravenous glucose doses, she remained hypoglycaemic, likely attributable to sepsis. On admission, the patient’s urine output was satisfactory; urinalysis detected protein (2+) and blood (3+).

The combination of symptoms—fever, pancytopenia, coagulopathy accompanied by acute kidney injury and lactic acidosis—was assessed as disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) likely resulting from bacteraemia and the initial stages of sepsis, with a quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) score of 1 out of 3. Clinical and imaging studies excluded a surgical etiology for her mottled extremities, such as necrotising fasciitis. Owing to the severity of her illness, intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam (Tazocin) was promptly administered to target pathogens commonly found in dogs, including Capnocytophaga species. Importantly, Capnocytophaga canimorsus was considered from the beginning and was confirmed after seven days by both aerobic and anaerobic blood cultures. The patient was subsequently admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) for enhanced surveillance.

In light of the pancytopenia and coagulopathy, the haematology service was consulted. Potential diagnoses included acute leukaemia and haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis. Although haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS) was also entertained, it was deemed improbable due to findings such as leukopaenia, elevated inflammatory markers, normal haptoglobin, and non-compensatory reticulocyte count. Flow cytometric analysis was undertaken, and a bone marrow biopsy was scheduled; however, as flow cytometry revealed no abnormal phenotypes nor increased CD34+ blasts, the biopsy was subsequently canceled. The clinical scenario aligned with sepsis. While awaiting laboratory results, the patient received supportive transfusions of platelets and fibrinogen.

Following admission to the intensive care unit, the patient experienced complete cessation of urine output and a rapid decline in kidney function. Intensive intravenous fluids were administered in combination with loop diuretics, but these measures did not improve her condition. She subsequently manifested septic cardiomyopathy complicated by fluid overload, necessitating the start of continuous renal replacement therapy. Screening for autoimmunity, including antinuclear antibodies (ANA) and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), yielded negative results.

By the third day of her ICU stay, the patient developed widespread cutaneous bleeding with ecchymoses, as well as distal cyanosis and ischemic changes involving multiple toes and fingers, although peripheral pulses and sensation were maintained. These manifestations were attributed to microthrombotic processes in the setting of disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). Despite the involvement of multiple organ systems, she did not need vasopressor therapy except for the initial stress-dose corticosteroids administered during the early phase of sepsis. She also received infusions of albumin along with other blood products.

Following eight days in hospital, she was moved to the nephrology ward to continue treatment for dialysis-dependent acute kidney injury and to receive intravenous antibiotics. Upon arrival, she was haemodynamically stable though anuric, with widespread oedema and multiple bruises; she was fully alert and oriented. Antibiotic therapy was narrowed to intravenous ampicillin, then transitioned to oral Apocillin, guided by blood culture findings.

During her nephrology admission, laboratory studies demonstrated an improving inflammatory response, reactive leukocytosis, rising platelet counts, and stable haemoglobin levels. Renal function tests continued to display elevated nitrogenous waste products, but coagulation studies had returned to normal, signifying a resolution of the coagulopathy. Her laboratory results at this stage are presented in Table 2.

Although her condition initially improved, her course was complicated by a sudden isolated decrease in haemoglobin (from 111 to 68 g/L over six days), occurring in the absence of any signs of bleeding or circulatory compromise. Thrombocytopenia persisted (30–40×10⁹/L); her haptoglobin levels became undetectable and lactate dehydrogenase rose, all consistent with ongoing haemolysis (see figure 1).

A direct antiglobulin test (DAT), also known as the Coombs test, returned positive for IgG, indicating a possible diagnosis of autoimmune haemolytic anaemia. Treatment with high-dose prednisolone was begun in conjunction with routine prophylaxis, and multiple blood transfusions were administered over a period of two days. Since haemolysis did not improve following corticosteroid intervention and platelet counts remained persistently low (41–46 × 10⁹/L), additional investigations were deemed necessary. Examination of a peripheral blood smear demonstrated 15%–20% schistocytes, which is characteristic of MAHA and suggests TMA (figure 2). The initial DAT result was likely falsely positive due to penicillin exposure or previous transfusions; repeat DAT was negative.

Complement assays and ADAMTS13 activity measurements were sent. Routine complement tests (C3/C4) available at the facility were within reference ranges, and functional testing of the classical, alternative, and lectin complement pathways also yielded normal results. ADAMTS13 activity was subsequently reported at 43.5%, excluding thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), since TTP typically manifests with activity less than 10%. During this period, plasma exchange (PEX) was started as a bridging intervention before administering the first dose of eculizumab.

After the platelet count rose to 77×10⁹/L, a renal biopsy was undertaken, which revealed marked acute tubular necrosis, ischemic-appearing glomeruli containing fibrin thrombi, and occasional crescents, all indicative of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

The definitive diagnostic features in this instance were most clearly observed on light microscopy as depicted in figure 3. Neither immunofluorescence nor electron microscopy findings are presented here; the immunofluorescence study identified no immune complex deposits, and electron microscopy did not show findings consistent with primary glomerular disease.

Stool PCR testing for Shiga toxin-producing E. coli (STEC-HUS) returned negative, excluding this etiology.

After the second and third plasma exchange (PEX) treatments, the patient began to pass urine spontaneously again. Initial meningococcal vaccinations—Menveo (targeting serogroups A, C, Y, and W) and Bexsero (for serogroup B)—were administered, after which induction therapy with eculizumab (900 mg intravenously weekly for four weeks) commenced. Plans were made for outpatient administration of the second vaccine doses eight weeks later. Apocillin prophylaxis was prescribed because the vaccines were not given two weeks prior to the start of complement inhibition.

Comprehensive complement analysis to exclude inherited complement-mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) was forwarded to Skåne Laboratory at Lund University in Sweden. Due to logistical reasons and concerns for the patient’s safety, three PEX sessions using Octaplas were completed and a single induction dose of eculizumab was provided before the sample was collected. Functional complement assays revealed significantly diminished alternative pathway activity (AH50 quantitation at 6%), with normal levels of complement factors H, I, and B, and absence of antifactor H antibodies.

Outcome and follow-up:

After the course of complex medical management, the patient demonstrates overall stability and satisfactory urine production. Her renal function remains impaired, as indicated by a serum creatinine of 175 µmol/L, and she is scheduled for continued monitoring in the nephrology outpatient clinic (figure 4). Maintenance therapy with eculizumab is ongoing (1200 mg intravenously every 2 weeks), extending 4 months beyond the induction phase. Comprehensive genetic analysis of the complement system revealed no pathogenic mutations; these findings were relayed verbally by the reference laboratory, with the formal report still pending at submission. Given these circumstances, complement inhibition is being maintained due to the significant severity of her clinical presentation, with plans to reevaluate the need for ongoing therapy should renal function stabilize. The presence of disease-causing complement gene variants would suggest prolonged therapy and vigilant surveillance for recurrence; however, such variants were not detected in this individual.

Differential diagnosis of TMA

TMAs encompass several overlapping clinical entities.

When adults present with MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI, the foremost concern is to rule out thrombotic TTP, which is characterized by marked ADAMTS13 deficiency (<10%). While awaiting definitive laboratory confirmation, clinical tools such as the PLASMIC score assist in initial assessment. In this case, the patient’s PLASMIC score was 4 out of 7, indicating a low probability of TTP, and ADAMTS13 activity measured 43.5%, thereby making TTP an unlikely diagnosis and directing further investigation.

STEC-HUS was also considered, but the patient’s PCR test for Shiga toxin was negative. The lack of preceding gastrointestinal symptoms and negative stool microbiology provided additional evidence against STEC-HUS.

Ultimately, the combination of ongoing hemolysis, schistocyte presence, severe AKI necessitating dialysis, and kidney biopsy findings consistent with TMA indicated a diagnosis of CM-HUS (figure 5). This diagnosis was corroborated by PCR exclusion of STEC-HUS and notable improvement following initiation of eculizumab. Her otherwise unremarkable medical background reduced the likelihood of a pre-existing complement abnormality requiring an additional insult, favoring the interpretation of infection-induced CM-HUS.

CM-HUS is identified through clinicopathological exclusion, and complement components C3 and C4 may remain within normal limits throughout the illness. Crucially, CM-HUS cannot be ruled out based on normal C3 and C4 values, as these may not deviate from standard ranges at any stage. Significantly, haemolysis manifested only as the sepsis was resolving, pointing towards an infection-induced CM-HUS rather than an underlying condition.

For this patient, initial complement evaluation (C3/C4) and ADAMTS13 activity measurements were carried out directly prior to the first plasma exchange (PEX) session. Expanded complement testing took place eight days following the last PEX session and four days after the initial administration of eculizumab. The reduction noted in the alternative complement pathway likely reflects the impact of therapy rather than inherent complement consumption or activation.

Pathophysiology of CM-HUS: complement dysregulation and infectious triggers

Complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS), formerly known as ‘atypical HUS’, results from improper regulation of the alternative complement pathway, often due to underlying genetic susceptibility. Approximately 50–70% of affected individuals possess genetic mutations or autoantibodies that impair complement regulatory proteins, including factor H, factor I, factor B, or membrane cofactor protein (CD46). However, not all genetically predisposed individuals will develop the disease, as clinical onset typically necessitates an environmental precipitant, most frequently infections, pregnancy, autoimmune conditions, or use of certain drugs.

Capnocytophaga canimorsus, while infrequently reported, is capable of activating complement pathways, particularly in hosts who are at risk. The organism demonstrates differing levels of susceptibility to serum, as the majority of strains are sensitive to complement, though some capsule-bearing variants exhibit partial resistance. This underscores the critical role of complement in host defense. This dynamic creates a clinical dilemma: complement activation is required for pathogen elimination, but in predisposed patients, it may also lead to excessive endothelial injury and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

This case contributes further evidence to the emerging association between C. canimorsus and complement-mediated TMA. For instance, Bjørkto et al detailed a patient with C. canimorsus–induced CM-HUS carrying a complement factor B mutation who was effectively managed with eculizumab. Unlike the aforementioned case, no pathogenic or likely pathogenic complement gene abnormalities were found in our patient, indicating that infection alone can precipitate CM-HUS in the absence of identified inherited complement defects.

Therapeutic strategy and clinical dilemma

Managing CM-HUS amid active infection involves distinctive complexities. Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody directed against terminal complement component 5, represents the mainstay of CM-HUS therapy, resulting in significant improvement in haematological parameters and kidney function, especially with early administration. Late commencement is linked to incomplete renal recovery, heightening the likelihood of eventual end-stage kidney disease.

PEX, conversely, is critical for survival in TTP, but confers only short-lived improvement in CM-HUS, primarily through elimination of circulating complement by-products, inflammatory mediators, or autoantibodies, without addressing the core defect in complement regulation. In this case, the patient regained urine output following the final rounds of PEX, prior to receiving eculizumab, indicative of brief stabilisation only. Durable haematological and renal responses were observed solely following complement inhibition.

An important treatment challenge is the deployment of complement inhibition during ongoing bacteraemia, as terminal complement activity is essential in host defence against encapsulated organisms—such as Neisseria and Capnocytophaga—prompting concerns about eculizumab’s infection-related risks. Here, eculizumab therapy commenced after clearance of blood cultures, thereby limiting the potential for unrestrained infection. Immunisation, antimicrobial prophylaxis, and vigilant surveillance remain vital when administering complement inhibitors.

Conclusion and broader implications

Timely identification of CM-HUS and rapid commencement of complement inhibition represent the key factors associated with a positive prognosis. The patient experienced resolution of hematological abnormalities and partial improvement in renal function, aligning with existing literature which indicates that renal status and the interval before starting eculizumab therapy are crucial prognostic variables.

Despite the rarity of CM-HUS linked to C. canimorsus, this report underscores the need to contemplate infectious etiologies—even uncommon ones—in individuals who develop TMA for the first time. Enhanced clinical vigilance combined with advances in molecular diagnostic methods could facilitate the recognition of additional similar cases.
