Title: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

Eiichi Kakehi, Yukako Matsumoto, Shigehisa Sakurai, Kazuhiko Kotani

Abstract:

Summary: We describe an unusual presentation of acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) diagnosed at the same time as systemic Behçet’s disease (BD), categorized as definite BD, which was further complicated by syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). A male patient in his 60s arrived with fever, headache, and mucocutaneous symptoms such as oral aphthae, folliculitis-like eruptions, genital ulcerations, as well as hyponatraemia. BD was diagnosed on the basis of these mucocutaneous findings. Although brain MRI was unremarkable, cerebrospinal fluid studies demonstrated aseptic meningitis, consistent with acute neuro-BD. Diagnostic laboratory results confirmed SIADH. Treatment with colchicine alone resulted in improvement of the mucocutaneous and neurological symptoms as well as normalization of serum sodium, without the requirement for corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive therapy. This report highlights a possible link between acute neuro-BD and SIADH and emphasizes the necessity for clinicians to identify simultaneous neurological and endocrine involvement in patients presenting with new-onset BD.

Background:

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a persistent, multisystem inflammatory condition marked by recurrent lesions of the oral and genital mucosa, uveitis, and cutaneous manifestations. Neurologic complications, known as neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), are observed in about 10%–20% of BD cases, often appearing years after the initial diagnosis. Acute neuro-BD generally manifests as meningoencephalitis with parenchymal brain involvement, most frequently affecting the brainstem. Although the simultaneous onset of BD and neuro-BD is infrequent, when both are present, evaluation should include a thorough assessment of mucocutaneous findings alongside neurological features, such as headache, mood or behavioral disturbances, psychiatric symptoms, and focal neurological deficits.

The syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH), a common etiology of hyponatremia, is typically associated with disorders such as central nervous system (CNS) inflammation, neoplasms, or the use of certain drugs. Due to the inflammatory processes in acute neuro-BD, patients with this condition may be predisposed to SIADH. While SIADH has been described in BD patients receiving agents like cyclophosphamide, the incidence of SIADH purely as a consequence of acute neuro-BD-related CNS inflammation—without medication-induced factors—is not well established.

We present an unusual case featuring the concurrent diagnosis of BD and acute neuro-BD, further complicated by the development of SIADH. This report underscores the need to recognize SIADH as a possible complication of acute neuro-BD, especially in individuals exhibiting hyponatremia and vague CNS symptoms.

Case presentation:

A man of Asian descent in his 60s arrived at the emergency department exhibiting symptoms over the past three days, including fever, headache, fatigue, diminished appetite, continual hiccups, and joint pain localized to the shoulders and ankles. He further described the presence of oral aphthae.

His past medical history was notable for type 2 diabetes mellitus, for which he was untreated, due to a lack of awareness about his condition and failure to adhere to recommended dietary or exercise regimens. His family history revealed no instances of BD. Socially, he drank alcohol occasionally, did not smoke, and denied recent travel, animal exposure, or contact with ill persons. He was employed in metalworking and had not had recent sexual intercourse.

During assessment, the patient was conscious but showed signs of fatigue. His vital parameters were as follows: temperature of 38.1°C, blood pressure at 135/73 mm Hg, heart rate measured at 119 beats per minute with a regular rhythm, respiratory rate of 19 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation at 96% breathing ambient air. On inspection, oral aphthae were present, along with rashes resembling folliculitis located on the face and trunk (see figure 1A, B and C). Findings from cardiovascular, pulmonary, and abdominal examinations were normal. Manual testing of muscle strength in both upper and lower limbs revealed no weakness. There was an absence of peripheral edema, and clinical evaluation indicated the patient was euvolemic. He was admitted for additional workup.

Investigations:

Initial blood tests demonstrated significant hyponatraemia (124 mmol/L), increased creatine kinase (1349 U/L), aspartate aminotransferase (77 U/L), alanine aminotransferase (64 U/L), C-reactive protein (CRP) (180 mg/L; reference range <3 mg/L), and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (70 mm/hour; reference range <15 mm/hour), while renal function remained within normal limits. The patient exhibited hyperglycaemia (plasma glucose 14.2 mmol/L) and a raised HbA1c of 8.2%, signifying suboptimal glycaemic regulation. Serological testing for hepatitis B and C returned negative results.

Assessment of endocrine function showed thyroid-stimulating hormone at 1.11 mIU/L (reference range 0.50–5.00 mIU/L), free T4 at 1.25 ng/dL (reference range 0.90–1.70 ng/dL), and serum cortisol at 42 µg/dL (reference range >6 µg/dL), all within reference ranges. Serum osmolality measured low at 267 mOsm/kg (reference <270 mOsm/kg), whereas urine osmolality was high at 900 mOsm/kg (reference >300 mOsm/kg); urine sodium was elevated at 88 mmol/L (reference >20 mmol/L), indicative of defective water elimination. Despite the presence of hypo-osmolar hyponatraemia, antidiuretic hormone (ADH) was increased (22.4 pg/mL). Electrocardiogram findings were normal, and imaging studies, including chest X-ray as well as CT scans of the head, chest, and abdomen, showed no abnormalities. Collectively, these findings were consistent with a diagnosis of SIADH.

By the third day, his headache and vertigo intensified. Although he did not demonstrate neck rigidity or cranial nerve deficits, central nervous system (CNS) inflammation was considered. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain showed no notable abnormalities within the hypothalamus or other regions (figure 2). Analysis of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) demonstrated a slightly raised opening pressure (17 cmH₂O), elevated protein concentration (0.97 g/L), and mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL). Testing for bacterial and mycobacterial organisms, as well as herpes simplex virus (HSV), varicella zoster virus (VZV), and cryptococcal antigen in CSF yielded negative results. CSF cytological examination did not detect malignant cells.

Additional assessments were conducted to investigate potential systemic diseases. Evaluation for sarcoidosis showed lysozyme within normal limits (5.1 µg/mL; reference 5–10) and normal angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) (11.4 U/L; reference 7–25). Hematological malignancy screening identified a raised soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) level (704 U/mL; reference 122–496 U/mL), with absence of human T-cell leukemia virus type 1 antibody.

On the fifth day, he developed a painful ulcer on the scrotum (figure 1D). Considering this together with oral aphthae and rash resembling folliculitis, the findings were indicative of a systemic type of BD. The patient did not show a hypersensitivity response to pinprick. Examination by ophthalmology excluded uveitis. HLA-B51 testing was positive.

Differential diagnosis:

Upon admission, the presence of fever, headache, general malaise, and hyponatraemia raised suspicion for either a central nervous system infection or a systemic inflammatory condition. Initial consideration was given to viral meningoencephalitis, especially infections due to HSV or VZV. Nonetheless, cerebrospinal fluid analysis returned negative results for both viral antigens and antibodies, thereby eliminating these etiologies. Bacterial meningitis was also part of the differential diagnosis but was excluded owing to the lack of neutrophilic predominance, only mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, and negative CSF culture findings. The possibility of tuberculous meningitis was dismissed as tests for acid-fast bacilli and tuberculosis DNA yielded negative results.

A primary malignancy affecting the CNS was contemplated due to the patient's neurological manifestations. Nevertheless, brain MRI did not detect any abnormalities, and CSF cytological examination failed to identify malignant cells. Although the soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) level was slightly raised, this isolated finding did not provide sufficient grounds to support CNS lymphoma.

Consideration was also given to autoimmune disorders; these were excluded following the absence of hallmark systemic features and negative results on autoantibody screening panels. Neurosarcoidosis remained a possibility, as MRI might appear normal in its early phase, but subsequent laboratory findings rendered this diagnosis improbable. The moderately elevated sIL-2R was interpreted as a nonspecific observation.

A definitive diagnosis of Behçet’s Disease (BD) was established based on the patient's clinical picture, which included fever, fatigue, oral aphthae (assigned two points), genital aphthosis (two points), and a folliculitis-like eruption (one point). According to the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease, the patient’s cumulative score was five points (a score of ≥4 signifies definite BD). Notably, genital aphthosis is a highly specific feature of BD, further substantiating this diagnosis. Following thorough exclusion of infectious, malignant, and autoimmune etiologies, a final diagnosis of acute neuro-Behçet’s Disease was made, characterized by CNS involvement alongside mucocutaneous manifestations.

Treatment:

On the first day of hospitalization, clinicians suspected an underlying inflammatory disorder, although its precise cause could not yet be determined. Cefepime was begun as empirical antimicrobial treatment after obtaining blood cultures, aiming to cover possible bacterial pathogens in the absence of an identified infectious site.

By the third day, the patient experienced increased headache and vertigo, raising concern for possible central nervous system infection or inflammation. Acyclovir was prescribed empirically but later discontinued once laboratory evaluations excluded HSV and VZV encephalitis.

On the eighth day, colchicine was introduced following the diagnosis of systemic Behçet’s disease and acute neuro-Behçet’s disease. Topical corticosteroid ointment was used to treat oral aphthae, while silver sulfadiazine cream was applied to genital and skin lesions. Management of SIADH involved conservative measures, including restricting fluid intake to under 800 mL per day, daily monitoring of serum sodium, and ensuring sodium levels were not corrected too rapidly. Neither hypertonic saline nor vasopressin receptor antagonists were required.

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient’s fever subsided and his appetite returned the day after colchicine therapy commenced. During the subsequent two weeks, there was gradual improvement in vertigo, general malaise, hiccups, and mucocutaneous manifestations. By day 16, his serum sodium had corrected to 136 mmol/L and ADH levels had fallen to 1.8 pg/mL. Myogenic enzyme values normalized as well.

Notably, no systemic corticosteroids or additional immunosuppressive agents were administered throughout treatment. The patient was discharged following a one-month hospital admission, during which acute-phase recovery was achieved, hyponatraemia was closely managed, and physical rehabilitation was conducted to address loss of function. Colchicine was continued for roughly four years, during which there was no evidence of BD or neurological relapse. Once BD was stable, biguanide therapy was started, and glycaemic control has remained adequate.

Discussion:

This case featured the concurrent identification of systemic BD and acute neuro-BD, a clinical presentation that is seldom encountered. Additionally, it illustrated that acute neuro-BD may be associated with SIADH as a complication. Improvement of mucocutaneous manifestations, neurological symptoms, and hyponatraemia following the initiation of colchicine monotherapy raises the possibility of a direct link between BD, neuro-BD, and SIADH. In patients with BD who develop fever or headache in the context of hyponatraemia, clinicians should maintain suspicion for acute neuro-BD as a possible etiology.

The simultaneous onset of systemic BD and acute neuro-BD in this patient is particularly remarkable, as evidenced by the presence of mucocutaneous lesions and involvement of the central nervous system. Generally, CNS manifestations and other major organ involvement are reported to develop approximately 4 years after BD is initially diagnosed, and presenting neurological symptoms are rare, seen in fewer than 5% of cases. Headaches attributed directly to neuro-BD occur in only about 10% of patients, rendering early recognition of neuro-BD in this scenario notably difficult. Acute neuro-BD typically manifests as a self-limited meningoencephalitis, which may or may not be accompanied by focal neurological deficits. Brain MRI in this patient did not reveal any abnormalities in common BD lesion sites, such as the hypothalamus. Acute neuro-BD can mimic aseptic meningitis and may be present even in the absence of parenchymal brain lesions; a normal MRI, especially early in the disease course, does not rule out the diagnosis. This lack of characteristic imaging findings contributed to the diagnostic complexity. Although HLA-B51 testing was conducted, it did not aid in confirmation, reflecting the international BD guidelines that emphasize clinical criteria rather than reliance on HLA-B51 status. This case underscores the necessity of considering neuro-BD in BD patients with mucocutaneous signs who develop unexplained neurological symptoms.

During the initial workup for the differential diagnosis, we primarily suspected an infectious etiology for CNS inflammation, given the presence of unexplained fever, neurological manifestations, and hyponatraemia; however, no specific infectious agent was identified. Other possible causes, such as connective tissue disorders and neoplastic conditions—including sarcoidosis and hematologic malignancies—were also considered. Consequently, as part of a wide-ranging preliminary assessment, we measured autoimmune serological markers as well as serum ACE and sIL-2R to screen for these conditions. The decision to carry out these tests was based more on the need to exclude diagnoses rather than on a strong clinical suspicion. Ultimately, we did not find any clinical, imaging, or CSF evidence supporting these alternative diagnoses, and these investigations did not significantly influence our final diagnostic conclusion. The sensitivity of ACE testing for sarcoidosis is low, and while sIL-2R may be useful in assessing malignant lymphoma, minor elevations are non-specific and may simply indicate underlying inflammation. In this instance, the lack of genital ulceration during early symptom presentation complicated the diagnosis of BD, prompting consideration of a wide range of possibilities. This diagnostic approach underscores the necessity of refraining from indiscriminate testing and reinforces the need for clinicians to select investigations guided by relevant clinical signs and interpret results judiciously.

While drug-induced SIADH has been documented in patients with BD who are treated with agents such as cyclophosphamide, the relationship between acute neuro-BD and SIADH is not well elucidated. Neuro-BD involves inflammation of the central nervous system, which may play a role in disrupted ADH regulation. Multiple studies have demonstrated that interleukin-6 (IL-6), a proinflammatory cytokine commonly elevated in neuroinflammatory diseases, is linked to increased secretion of ADH. In BD, abnormal activation of monocytes results in spontaneous IL-6 synthesis, and heightened IL-6 concentrations have been observed in the cerebrospinal fluid of individuals experiencing acute neuro-BD. These observations imply that IL-6-driven inflammation could be a factor in the development of SIADH under these circumstances. In the current case, IL-6 values in serum and CSF were not assessed; nevertheless, a significantly raised serum CRP might indicate an underlying inflammatory response mediated by IL-6. While SIADH may arise in association with a variety of disorders—including CNS inflammation, respiratory pathology, malignancy, and certain pharmacologic agents—the current data do not permit a definitive causal relationship between SIADH and acute neuro-BD to be inferred. The temporal occurrence noted in this patient may simply represent a general reaction to CNS inflammation, as opposed to a mechanism specific to neuro-BD.

This case demonstrates the possible adverse effects of hyponatraemia induced by SIADH in acute neuro-BD. Prior literature has noted that persistent hiccups can be a significant clinical indicator of severe hyponatraemia resulting from SIADH linked to central nervous system inflammation, such as meningitis. Furthermore, during the initial assessment for fever, arthralgia, and generalized fatigue, we measured CK levels, suspecting the possibility of inflammatory or metabolic muscle injury, which uncovered rhabdomyolysis—a recognized but frequently neglected complication of marked hyponatraemia that is seldom routinely tested for. Although these clinical features are established, they are often undervalued consequences of profound hyponatraemia. Healthcare professionals should recognize that symptoms like hiccups or rhabdomyolysis, which may appear unrelated, can signify underlying electrolyte imbalances, particularly when occurring in the setting of neuroinflammatory disease.

Corticosteroids remain the primary therapeutic approach for acute neuro-BD, with most patients showing a favorable response. Nonetheless, spontaneous remission has been observed in instances characterized exclusively by meningoencephalitis-like manifestations. In the case described, colchicine administered alone led to improvements in both mucocutaneous and neurological symptoms, as well as correction of hyponatraemia. Colchicine acts as an anti-inflammatory agent through mechanisms including inhibition of neutrophil migration and activation and downregulation of cytokine production, such as IL-1β and IL-6. Although colchicine is frequently employed to treat mucocutaneous symptoms in BD, systematic reviews have demonstrated its lack of efficacy for acute neuro-BD, indicating that immunosuppressive agents are generally necessary for effective disease management. In previously published cases, colchicine has typically served as an adjunct to corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive medications rather than being utilized alone. While it is not possible to rule out spontaneous recovery in this patient, the observations support that colchicine could be considered as a treatment for acute neuro-BD and associated complications like SIADH, especially in situations where corticosteroid use is either unsuitable or contraindicated.

Conclusion:

We presented an uncommon instance in which Behçet’s disease (BD) and acute neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) were diagnosed simultaneously, and in which the neuro-BD was further complicated by syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). This report illustrates that SIADH can develop as a complication during the acute phase of neuro-BD, emphasizing the need for clinicians to recognize this possible link. Treatment with colchicine alone resulted in a swift resolution of mucocutaneous lesions, neurological symptoms, and hyponatraemia, indicating that colchicine may serve as an effective initial therapy for neuro-BD. For patients with BD who display vague central nervous system symptoms—such as fever and headache—alongside hyponatraemia, clinicians should maintain a strong clinical suspicion for acute neuro-BD, especially in cases complicated by SIADH.
