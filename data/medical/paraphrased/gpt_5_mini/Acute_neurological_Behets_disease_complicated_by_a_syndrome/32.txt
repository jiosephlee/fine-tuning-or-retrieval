Title: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

Eiichi Kakehi, Yukako Matsumoto, Shigehisa Sakurai, Kazuhiko Kotani

Abstract:

Summary: We describe an uncommon presentation of acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) identified simultaneously with systemic Behçet’s disease (BD), meeting criteria for definite BD, and complicated by syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). A male patient in his 60s came in with fever, headache, and mucocutaneous symptoms such as oral aphthae, folliculitis-like eruptions, genital ulcers, and hyponatraemia. The diagnosis of BD was made based on his mucocutaneous manifestations. Brain MRI findings were normal, but cerebrospinal fluid evaluation indicated aseptic meningitis, which supported the diagnosis of acute neuro-BD. Laboratory analysis satisfied the criteria for SIADH. Treatment with colchicine alone resulted in resolution of mucocutaneous and neurologic symptoms as well as hyponatraemia, and neither corticosteroids nor additional immunosuppressive therapy was necessary. This case indicates a potential link between acute neuro-BD and SIADH and highlights the need to identify overlapping neurological and endocrine symptoms in patients with recent BD diagnoses.

Background:

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a persistent, multisystem inflammatory condition marked by repeated episodes of oral and genital ulceration, ocular inflammation (uveitis), and dermatologic findings. Neurological manifestations, collectively termed neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), develop in about 10%–20% of cases, generally emerging several years after the primary diagnosis. The acute form of neuro-BD commonly manifests as meningoencephalitis and parenchymal lesions within the central nervous system, most often involving the brainstem. While a delayed onset is frequently observed, it is uncommon for BD and neuro-BD to be identified at the same time. In such presentations, healthcare providers should carefully evaluate both mucocutaneous signs and neurological manifestations, such as headache, alterations in behavior, psychiatric disturbances, and focal neurologic impairments.

Syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) represents a recognized etiology of hyponatraemia and frequently arises secondary to central nervous system (CNS) inflammation, neoplastic processes, or exposure to certain drugs. Due to the inflammatory process present in acute neuro-BD, these individuals may have a predisposition for SIADH. Although cases of SIADH have been described in BD patients undergoing therapy with agents such as cyclophosphamide, the emergence of SIADH purely in the context of CNS inflammation related to acute neuro-BD, without pharmacologic provocateurs, remains uncertain.

We present an unusual instance where both BD and acute neuro-BD were established concurrently, complicated by the development of SIADH. This report underscores the need to recognize SIADH as a possible complication of acute neuro-BD, especially in individuals with vague CNS complaints accompanied by hyponatraemia.

Case presentation:

A man of Asian descent in his 60s arrived at the emergency department reporting three days of fever, headache, generalized weakness, decreased appetite, persistent hiccups, and joint pain involving his shoulders and ankles. He additionally noted the presence of oral aphthous ulcers.

His past medical history was notable for type 2 diabetes mellitus, for which he was not undergoing therapy, attributable to limited understanding of his diagnosis and insufficient adherence to diet and exercise recommendations. There was no significant family history, with no relatives diagnosed with BD. He reported occasional alcohol intake, no tobacco use, no recent travel, and no contact with animals or ill persons. He was employed in metalworking and denied any recent sexual encounters.

Upon evaluation, the patient was awake but showed signs of fatigue. His vital measurements were as follows: temperature at 38.1°C, blood pressure at 135/73 mm Hg, heart rate of 119 beats per minute with a regular rhythm, respiratory rate of 19 per minute, and oxygen saturation at 96% while breathing ambient air. Physical assessment noted the presence of oral aphthae and rashes resembling folliculitis on both the face and torso (see figure 1A, B, and C). Examination of the cardiovascular, respiratory, and abdominal systems revealed no abnormalities. Strength testing of the upper and lower limbs showed no evidence of muscle weakness. There was no peripheral edema observed, and clinical evaluation indicated normal fluid balance. The patient was subsequently hospitalized for further diagnostic workup.

Investigations:

The initial laboratory assessment showed significant hyponatraemia at 124 mmol/L, along with increased creatine kinase at 1349 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase at 77 U/L, alanine aminotransferase at 64 U/L, C-reactive protein (CRP) at 180 mg/L (reference range <3 mg/L), and erythrocyte sedimentation rate at 70 mm/hour (reference range <15 mm/hour); renal function remained normal. The patient exhibited hyperglycaemia (plasma glucose 14.2 mmol/L) and elevated HbA1c of 8.2%, reflecting suboptimal glucose management. Testing for hepatitis B and C was negative.

Endocrine studies found thyroid-stimulating hormone to be within reference limits (1.11 mIU/L; reference 0.50–5.00 mIU/L), free T4 at 1.25 ng/dL (reference 0.90–1.70 ng/dL), and serum cortisol at 42 µg/dL (reference >6 µg/dL). Serum osmolality measured low at 267 mOsm/kg (reference <270 mOsm/kg), whereas urine osmolality was raised to 900 mOsm/kg (reference >300 mOsm/kg), and urinary sodium concentration was also elevated at 88 mmol/L (reference >20 mmol/L), suggestive of compromised water elimination. Although the patient had hypo-osmolar hyponatraemia, the antidiuretic hormone (ADH) was found to be increased at 22.4 pg/mL. The ECG demonstrated no abnormalities, and imaging—including chest X-ray and CT scans of the head, chest, and abdomen—were all unremarkable. Collectively, these data were indicative of SIADH.

On the third day, the patient's headache and vertigo intensified. Despite the absence of neck rigidity or signs of cranial nerve dysfunction, central nervous system inflammation was considered. MRI of the brain did not show any notable anomalies in the hypothalamus or other regions (figure 2). Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis demonstrated a slightly raised opening pressure (17 cmH₂O), elevated protein concentration (0.97 g/L), and a mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL). Tests and cultures for bacteria, mycobacteria, herpes simplex virus (HSV), varicella zoster virus (VZV), and cryptococcal antigen in the CSF were all negative. Cytological evaluation of the CSF excluded the presence of malignant cells.

Additional assessments were conducted to investigate potential systemic diseases. Evaluation for sarcoidosis showed normal levels of lysozyme (5.1 µg/mL; reference 5–10) and angiotensin-converting enzyme (11.4 U/L; reference 7–25). Hematologic malignancy screening revealed a raised soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) (704 U/mL; reference range, 122–496 U/mL) with a negative result for human T-cell leukemia virus type 1 antibody.

On the fifth day, a painful ulcer was noted on the scrotum (figure 1D). Considering the oral aphthae and skin eruptions resembling folliculitis, these findings were indicative of a systemic variant of BD. The patient did not show any pathergy reaction. Eye examination detected no uveitis. Testing for HLA-B51 was positive.

Differential diagnosis:

At presentation, the constellation of fever, headache, general malaise, and hyponatraemia raised suspicion for either central nervous system infection or a systemic inflammatory process. Viral meningoencephalitis, primarily attributed to HSV or VZV, was initially considered. Nevertheless, cerebrospinal fluid analysis did not detect viral antigens or antibodies, thereby ruling out these etiologies. Bacterial meningitis was also contemplated but subsequently dismissed due to the lack of neutrophil dominance, only mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, and sterile CSF cultures. Tuberculous meningitis was excluded based on negative results for acid-fast bacilli staining and tuberculosis DNA assays.

Given the neurological manifestations, a primary malignancy of the CNS was also entertained. Yet, brain MRI did not reveal any abnormalities, and cytological analysis of the CSF did not find evidence of malignant cells. Although soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) levels were mildly raised, this finding alone was not sufficient to confirm CNS lymphoma.

Potential autoimmune disorders were investigated; however, these were ruled out owing to both the lack of characteristic systemic features and negative autoantibody panels. Neurosarcoidosis remained a differential diagnosis for a period, recognizing that early MRI imaging might be unremarkable, but later laboratory tests did not support this diagnosis. The slight elevation in sIL-2R was interpreted as non-specific.

Definite Behçet’s disease (BD) was diagnosed in this individual on the basis of clinical signs: fever and fatigue, recurrent oral aphthae (scoring two points), genital aphthosis (two points), and a folliculitis-like skin eruption (one point). The International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease attributed a total of five points to these features (a score of ≥4 denotes definite BD). Notably, genital aphthosis is considered a distinguishing characteristic for BD, and its presence, together with the other findings, strongly indicated this condition. Once infectious, neoplastic, and autoimmune origins had been excluded, a diagnosis of acute neuro-Behçet’s disease was made, with CNS involvement occurring alongside mucocutaneous manifestations.

Treatment:

On the first day of hospitalization, clinicians considered the possibility of an underlying inflammatory process, but its origin was not determined. After obtaining blood cultures, cefepime was started empirically to address a possible bacterial infection, despite no clear source of infection.

By day 3, the patient experienced an escalation of headache and vertigo, raising concerns for central nervous system inflammation or infectious involvement. Empirical treatment with acyclovir was given but later ceased once diagnostic testing excluded HSV and VZV encephalitis.

On day 8, colchicine therapy commenced following the diagnosis of systemic Behçet’s disease (BD) and acute neuro-BD. Oral aphthae were managed with topical corticosteroid ointment, while genital ulcers and skin lesions received treatment with silver sulfadiazine cream. Syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) was addressed non-pharmacologically through fluid restriction (less than 800 mL per day), with careful monitoring of daily serum sodium and avoidance of rapid correction. Neither hypertonic saline nor vasopressin receptor antagonists were necessary.

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient's fever subsided, and his appetite improved the day following the initiation of colchicine. Over the next two weeks, his symptoms—including vertigo, general malaise, hiccups, and mucocutaneous lesions—gradually subsided. By the sixteenth day, his serum sodium had returned to 136 mmol/L, and ADH concentrations decreased to 1.8 pg/mL. Levels of myogenic enzymes also normalized.

Notably, systemic corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive agents were not administered throughout the course of treatment. He was discharged after being hospitalized for one month, during which he recovered from the acute episode, had his hyponatraemia closely managed and monitored, and participated in rehabilitation to address physical function deficits. Colchicine therapy was maintained for roughly four years, with no relapse of BD or neurological symptoms during that period. Once BD was stable, biguanide medication was commenced, and satisfactory glycaemic control has been preserved since.

Discussion:

In this instance, both systemic Behçet’s disease (BD) and acute neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) were identified simultaneously, a circumstance rarely encountered in clinical practice. Additionally, this case illustrated that acute neuro-BD can be associated with syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). The remission of mucocutaneous features, neurologic symptoms, and hyponatraemia following monotherapy with colchicine points to a potential direct relationship among BD, neuro-BD, and SIADH. Therefore, when BD patients develop fever or headache in conjunction with hyponatraemia, clinicians should include acute neuro-BD in the differential diagnosis.

This case is distinguished by the concurrent onset of systemic BD and acute neuro-BD, as indicated by the presence of mucocutaneous lesions and central nervous system (CNS) involvement. Ordinarily, major organ manifestations such as CNS lesions tend to emerge approximately four years following the initial diagnosis of BD; presentations with neurological signs at disease onset are unusual, reported in fewer than 5% of cases. Only about 10% of individuals experience headache that can be directly ascribed to neuro-BD. Thus, early detection of neuro-BD in this context posed a significant diagnostic difficulty. Acute neuro-BD frequently manifests as self-limited meningoencephalitis, sometimes accompanied by focal neurologic deficits. In the present patient, brain MRI showed no definitive changes at classical BD sites, including the hypothalamus. Acute neuro-BD may appear as aseptic meningitis in the absence of brain parenchymal lesions, and a normal MRI, especially in initial stages, does not rule out the condition. The absence of characteristic radiological findings added further complexity to diagnosis. Although HLA-B51 testing was undertaken, it did not assist in confirming the diagnosis, consistent with international BD diagnostic criteria that focus primarily on clinical presentation rather than HLA-B51 status. This case emphasizes the necessity of considering neuro-BD in individuals exhibiting mucocutaneous findings and vague neurologic complaints.

At the outset of the differential diagnosis, infection was prioritized as the likely source of CNS inflammation due to the patient's combination of persistent fever, neurological manifestations, and hyponatraemia. Despite this initial suspicion, no definitive pathogens were identified. Additional etiologies for CNS inflammation, including connective tissue disorders and neoplastic processes, were considered; both sarcoidosis and hematologic malignancies were included among the differential possibilities. Consequently, a broad array of screening assessments—such as autoimmune serologic testing, and measurement of serum ACE and soluble IL-2 receptor (sIL-2R) levels—were undertaken. These evaluations were conducted selectively and primarily to exclude potential causes, rather than being guided by compelling clinical indications. Ultimately, neither clinical, imaging, nor CSF data supported these alternative diagnoses, and these particular investigations did not substantively inform the final diagnostic outcome. It is important to note that serum ACE has suboptimal sensitivity for sarcoidosis, and while sIL-2R can assist in lymphoma assessment, minor increases are not specific and may simply indicate inflammation. In this instance, the absence of genital ulcers early in the course contributed to diagnostic ambiguity regarding BD, necessitating a wider range of considerations. The approach in this case underscores the necessity of avoiding non-selective diagnostic testing, reinforcing that such tests should be ordered only when substantiated by relevant clinical findings and interpreted judiciously.

While drug-induced SIADH has been documented in individuals with Behçet’s disease (BD) receiving agents such as cyclophosphamide, the relationship between acute neuro-BD and SIADH remains largely unclear. Neuro-BD involves central nervous system (CNS) inflammation, which can potentially lead to abnormal ADH secretion. Multiple reports have indicated that interleukin-6 (IL-6), a proinflammatory cytokine often increased in neuroinflammatory conditions, correlates with heightened ADH production. In BD, monocyte dysfunction results in spontaneous IL-6 release, and raised levels of IL-6 have been identified in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) of individuals with acute neuro-BD. These observations imply that IL-6–driven inflammation may play a role in the development of SIADH in this context. Although IL-6 concentrations were not assessed in either serum or CSF in this patient, the significantly increased serum CRP could indicate an underlying inflammatory response mediated by IL-6. SIADH is known to be associated with a variety of causes, including CNS inflammation, lung disease, malignancy, and certain drugs; however, the current evidence does not support a direct causative association between SIADH and acute neuro-BD. The chronological correlation seen here may simply represent a general reaction to CNS inflammation rather than a process unique to neuro-BD.

This case demonstrates the possible complications associated with hyponatraemia resulting from SIADH in the context of acute neuro-BD. Prior publications have noted that ongoing hiccups might represent a significant clinical indicator of severe hyponatraemia due to SIADH stemming from central nervous system inflammation, including meningitis. Furthermore, creatine kinase was assessed as part of the initial work-up for fever, arthralgia, and general fatigue—symptoms potentially linked to inflammatory or metabolic muscle injury—and this identified rhabdomyolysis, which is a recognized but frequently neglected consequence of severe hyponatraemia that is not commonly screened for. These findings are established yet often overlooked clinical effects of marked hyponatraemia. It is important for clinicians to recognize that symptoms which appear unrelated, such as persistent hiccups or rhabdomyolysis, may signal underlying disturbances in electrolytes, especially in patients with neuroinflammatory disorders.

Corticosteroids remain the primary therapeutic approach for acute neuro-BD, with most individuals exhibiting a positive response. Nevertheless, spontaneous remission has occasionally been observed in cases limited to meningoencephalitis-like presentations. In this instance, the patient experienced improvement in mucocutaneous and neurological symptoms, as well as correction of hyponatraemia, following colchicine monotherapy. Colchicine mediates anti-inflammatory actions by preventing neutrophil migration and activation, and by inhibiting cytokine production, including IL-1β and IL-6. Although colchicine is commonly prescribed for BD-associated mucocutaneous lesions, systematic reviews indicate that it is not effective as monotherapy for acute neuro-BD, for which immunosuppressive agents are typically necessary to achieve sufficient disease control. Indeed, reported cases have generally described colchicine as an adjunct to corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive treatments, rather than being used alone. Despite the possibility that spontaneous improvement may have occurred, these observations imply that colchicine may serve as a potential therapeutic option for acute neuro-BD and related issues such as SIADH, especially when corticosteroid use is not appropriate or contraindicated.

Conclusion:

We presented an uncommon instance in which Behçet’s disease (BD) and acute neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) were simultaneously identified, with syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) arising as a complication of neuro-BD. This case underscores that SIADH can accompany acute neuro-BD, emphasizing the need for clinicians to recognize this possible correlation. Administration of colchicine alone resulted in prompt resolution of mucocutaneous lesions, neurological symptoms, and hyponatraemia, implying that colchicine could serve as an effective initial treatment for neuro-BD. When BD patients develop vague central nervous system complaints such as headache and fever along with hyponatraemia, acute neuro-BD complicated by SIADH should be strongly considered in the differential diagnosis.
