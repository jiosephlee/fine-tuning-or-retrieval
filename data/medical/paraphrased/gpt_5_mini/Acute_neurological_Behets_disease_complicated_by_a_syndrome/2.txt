Title: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

Eiichi Kakehi, Yukako Matsumoto, Shigehisa Sakurai, Kazuhiko Kotani

Abstract:

Summary: This report details an uncommon presentation of acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) occurring simultaneously with systemic Behçet’s disease (BD)—classified as definite BD—complicated further by the onset of syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). A male patient in his 60s arrived with symptoms of fever and headache, alongside mucocutaneous signs such as oral aphthae, folliculitis-like eruptions, genital ulcers, and evidence of hyponatraemia. Diagnosis of BD was established on the basis of mucocutaneous findings. Although brain MRI did not reveal abnormalities, cerebrospinal fluid analysis identified aseptic meningitis, providing support for a diagnosis of acute neuro-BD. Additional laboratory testing satisfied the diagnostic criteria for SIADH. Treatment with colchicine alone was effective in resolving the patient’s cutaneous, neurological, and electrolyte abnormalities, obviating the need for corticosteroids or further immunosuppressive therapies. This case highlights a potential link between acute neuro-BD and SIADH, and emphasizes the clinical significance of identifying both neurological and endocrine involvement at the initial presentation of BD.

Background:

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a persistent, systemic inflammatory condition that is marked by episodic oral and genital ulcerations, uveitis, and dermatological manifestations. Neurological manifestations, collectively termed neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), affect around 10%–20% of individuals with BD, often emerging years after the disease is initially recognized. The acute phase of neuro-BD frequently manifests as meningoencephalitis with parenchymal lesions, particularly impacting the brainstem. While it is uncommon for BD and neuro-BD to be identified simultaneously, such cases do occur. When confronted with concurrent diagnoses, healthcare providers must thoroughly evaluate both the mucocutaneous presentation and neurological findings, which can include headache, behavioral disturbances, psychiatric manifestations, and localized neurological impairments.

The syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) represents a frequent etiology of hyponatraemia, commonly arising in the setting of central nervous system (CNS) inflammation, malignancy, or as an adverse effect of specific pharmacotherapies. In light of the proinflammatory milieu associated with acute neuro-BD, patients with this condition could potentially develop SIADH. Although there are documented cases of SIADH in BD patients undergoing cyclophosphamide therapy, the incidence of SIADH directly attributable to CNS inflammation from acute neuro-BD, in the absence of medication triggers, is not well established.

Here, we describe an unusual case involving simultaneous diagnoses of BD and acute neuro-BD, further complicated by the development of SIADH. This case underscores the necessity of considering SIADH in the differential diagnosis of acute neuro-BD, especially when evaluating patients with ambiguous CNS complaints and hyponatraemia.

Case presentation:

A man of Asian descent in his sixth decade arrived at the emergency department, reporting three days of fever, headache, general fatigue, diminished appetite, continuous hiccups, and joint pain in the shoulders and ankles. He further mentioned experiencing oral aphthous ulcers.

His past medical history was significant for type 2 diabetes mellitus; however, he was not on any therapeutic regimen, largely due to insufficient understanding of his diagnosis and a lack of adherence to dietary and exercise recommendations. There was no notable family history of BD. The patient drank alcohol occasionally, did not smoke, had neither recently traveled nor had contact with animals or sick individuals. Employed in the metalworking sector, he reported no recent sexual encounters.

Upon evaluation, the patient was conscious but showed signs of tiredness. Recorded vital parameters included a body temperature of 38.1°C, blood pressure measuring 135/73 mm Hg, regular heart rate at 119 beats per minute, respiratory rate at 19 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation of 96% while breathing ambient air. Physical assessment identified oral aphthous ulcers and folliculitis-like eruptions on the facial and truncal regions (figure 1A, B and C). Cardiopulmonary and abdominal assessments yielded normal results. Manual muscle strength testing demonstrated intact strength in both upper and lower limbs. There was an absence of peripheral edema, and his clinical volume assessment indicated euvolemia. Consequently, he was hospitalized for further diagnostic workup.

Investigations:

Initial laboratory investigations showed significant hyponatraemia (124 mmol/L), alongside increased levels of creatine kinase (1349 U/L), aspartate aminotransferase (77 U/L), alanine aminotransferase (64 U/L), C-reactive protein (CRP) (180 mg/L; reference range, <3 mg/L), and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (70 mm/hour; reference range, <15 mm/hour), while renal function remained within normal limits. The patient exhibited hyperglycaemia (plasma glucose 14.2 mmol/L) and a raised HbA1c of 8.2%, suggestive of suboptimal glycaemic control. Serological tests for hepatitis B and C were negative.

Endocrine assessment demonstrated normal values for thyroid-stimulating hormone (1.11 mIU/L; reference, 0.50–5.00 mIU/L), free T4 (1.25 ng/dL; reference, 0.90–1.70 ng/dL), and serum cortisol (42 µg/dL; reference, >6 µg/dL). Serum osmolality was reduced (267 mOsm/kg; reference, <270 mOsm/kg), while urine osmolality was markedly elevated (900 mOsm/kg; reference, >300 mOsm/kg), accompanied by a high urinary sodium level (88 mmol/L; reference, >20 mmol/L), indicating a disturbance in water excretion. Despite the presence of hypo-osmolar hyponatraemia, antidiuretic hormone (ADH) was found to be elevated (22.4 pg/mL). Electrocardiogram results were normal, and imaging studies, including chest X-ray and computed tomography of the head, chest, and abdomen, were unremarkable. Collectively, these findings were consistent with a diagnosis of SIADH.

By the third day, the patient’s headache and vertigo had intensified. Despite the absence of neck rigidity or evidence of cranial nerve dysfunction, central nervous system (CNS) inflammation was considered as a possible diagnosis. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain did not demonstrate any noteworthy abnormalities in the hypothalamus or other brain areas (figure 2). Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) studies showed a mildly increased opening pressure (17 cmH₂O), elevated protein concentration (0.97 g/L), and a slight lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL). Microbiological tests on the CSF—including cultures and specific assays for bacterial and mycobacterial organisms, herpes simplex virus (HSV), varicella zoster virus (VZV), and cryptococcal antigen—were negative. CSF cytological analysis detected no evidence of malignant cells.

Additional diagnostic workup was undertaken to rule out underlying systemic pathologies. Evaluation for sarcoidosis demonstrated normal values for lysozyme (5.1 µg/mL; reference 5–10) and angiotensin-converting enzyme (11.4 U/L; reference 7–25). Screening for hematologic malignancy showed an increased level of soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) (704 U/mL; reference 122–496 U/mL), whereas testing for human T-cell leukemia virus type 1 antibody was negative.

On the fifth day, the patient developed a painful ulcer on the scrotum (figure 1D). Taken together with the presence of oral aphthae and folliculitis-like skin lesions, these manifestations raised the suspicion of systemic BD. There was no evidence of pathergy on skin testing. Examination by ophthalmology excluded the presence of uveitis. Testing for HLA-B51 was positive.

Differential diagnosis:

Upon initial assessment, the concurrence of fever, headache, general malaise, and hyponatraemia indicated a potential central nervous system (CNS) infection or a systemic inflammatory condition. Viral meningoencephalitis, especially due to HSV or VZV, was suspected first; nevertheless, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis was negative for viral antigens and antibodies, thereby ruling out these viral etiologies. Consideration was also given to bacterial meningitis, but this diagnosis was discounted due to the absence of neutrophilic predominance, the presence of only mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, and negative CSF cultures. Tuberculous meningitis was similarly excluded based on negative acid-fast staining and tuberculosis DNA assays.

Given the neurologic symptoms, a primary CNS malignancy was also contemplated. However, both brain MRI and CSF cytology did not reveal any pathological abnormalities or malignant cells. Although the serum sIL-2R was slightly increased, this isolated result was not sufficient to diagnose CNS lymphoma.

The possibility of autoimmune disorders was explored; these were dismissed in light of the lack of hallmark systemic features and negative results on screening autoantibody tests. Neurosarcoidosis remained in the differential, considering that MRI may appear normal early on, yet subsequent laboratory findings argued against this diagnosis. The marginally elevated sIL-2R was interpreted as non-specific.

A definitive diagnosis of BD was made according to clinical manifestations, including fever and fatigue, oral aphthae (two points), genital aphthosis (two points), and a rash resembling folliculitis (one point). Utilizing the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease, the patient accumulated a total of five points (≥4 signifying a confirmed diagnosis). Notably, genital aphthosis is recognized as a highly specific feature of BD, further substantiating this diagnosis. Following systematic exclusion of infectious, neoplastic, and autoimmune etiologies, acute neuro-BD was diagnosed on the basis of CNS symptoms in association with characteristic mucocutaneous findings.

Treatment:

Upon admission (day 1), clinicians suspected an underlying inflammatory disorder, although its precise cause was not immediately identified. Empiric cefepime therapy was commenced following the collection of blood cultures, aimed at addressing a possible bacterial infection even though no clear infectious site was evident.

By day 3, the patient's headache and vertigo had intensified, raising concerns for central nervous system inflammation or infection. As a precaution, acyclovir was introduced, but subsequently discontinued when diagnostic evaluations excluded HSV and VZV encephalitis.

On day 8, following the confirmation of systemic BD and acute neuro-BD, colchicine treatment was initiated. Management of oral aphthous ulcers involved the application of topical corticosteroid ointment, while genital and skin lesions were treated with silver sulfadiazine cream. The syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) was approached with conservative measures, including fluid intake restriction (<800 mL/day), daily serum sodium assessments, and careful avoidance of rapid sodium correction. There was no necessity for hypertonic saline administration or the use of vasopressin receptor antagonists.

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient experienced resolution of fever and improvement in appetite the day following the initiation of colchicine therapy. Over the next 2 weeks, symptoms such as vertigo, general malaise, hiccups, and mucocutaneous lesions showed progressive amelioration. By the 16th day, his serum sodium concentration had returned to a normal value of 136 mmol/L, and ADH levels had declined to 1.8 pg/mL. Myogenic enzyme concentrations also normalized.

Notably, systemic corticosteroids and additional immunosuppressive agents were not administered throughout the course of treatment. The patient was discharged after a hospitalization period of 1 month, during which the acute phase was resolved, hyponatraemia was closely observed and addressed, and rehabilitation addressed declines in physical capability. Colchicine treatment was sustained for about 4 years, with no relapses of BD or neurological symptoms recorded during that period. Once BD was stabilized, biguanide therapy commenced, resulting in maintained satisfactory glycaemic control.

Discussion:

In this instance, systemic BD and acute neuro-BD were identified concurrently, a particularly uncommon clinical occurrence. The case further illustrated that acute neuro-BD may be accompanied by SIADH. The alleviation of mucocutaneous symptoms, neurological deficits, and hyponatraemia following colchicine monotherapy indicates a likely direct relationship among BD, neuro-BD, and SIADH. Thus, clinicians should entertain the possibility of acute neuro-BD in BD patients who exhibit fever or headache in association with hyponatraemia.

This report is significant for documenting the concurrent onset of systemic BD and acute neuro-BD, evidenced by both mucocutaneous involvement and central nervous system manifestations. Conventionally, serious organ involvement such as CNS pathology emerges roughly four years after the initial BD diagnosis, with neurological symptoms at initial presentation observed in less than 5% of cases. Additionally, only about 10% of individuals experience headaches attributable directly to neuro-BD, making early identification especially difficult in this scenario. Acute neuro-BD most frequently presents as a self-limited meningoencephalitic process, occasionally accompanied by focal neurological impairments. In this patient, MRI did not disclose pathognomonic changes in standard BD-associated regions, such as the hypothalamus. Acute neuro-BD may manifest as aseptic meningitis without visible parenchymal abnormalities, and unremarkable brain imaging—especially early in the clinical course—cannot reliably exclude this diagnosis. The absence of definitive radiological features added further diagnostic complexity. Testing for HLA-B51 was conducted but did not assist in confirmation, reflecting the international diagnostic standards for BD, which prioritize clinical presentation over genetic testing. Overall, this case underscores the need to consider neuro-BD in individuals with mucocutaneous disease who develop vague neurological symptoms.

During the early stages of forming the differential diagnosis, we gave primary consideration to infection as the source of CNS inflammation, prompted by the concurrence of unexplained fever, neurological manifestations, and hyponatraemia. Nonetheless, our investigations did not detect any particular infectious agent. Alternative etiologies for CNS inflammation were also explored, such as connective tissue disorders and neoplastic conditions, including sarcoidosis and haematological malignancies. Consequently, we conducted an extensive initial screening by measuring autoimmune serological markers, serum ACE, and sIL-2R. These laboratory assessments were implemented selectively, as part of an exclusionary process rather than out of high clinical suspicion. Ultimately, neither clinical evaluation, imaging, nor CSF analysis yielded findings to corroborate these conditions, and these ancillary investigations offered little toward establishing the diagnosis. Given the limited sensitivity of ACE for sarcoidosis, and considering that while sIL-2R can be helpful in suspected malignant lymphoma, its mild elevation is not specific and may simply indicate general inflammatory activity. Notably, the lack of genital ulcers in the early presentation complicated the identification of BD, necessitating a comprehensive differential. This experience underscores the necessity for judicious use of diagnostic testing and reiterates that investigations should be reserved for cases with appropriate clinical indications, with results interpreted prudently.

While instances of drug-induced SIADH have been described in individuals with BD who are treated with agents such as cyclophosphamide, the connection between acute neuro-BD and SIADH remains inadequately elucidated. Neuro-BD involves inflammation within the CNS, a process that could potentially lead to abnormal regulation of ADH secretion. Previous research has demonstrated that interleukin-6 (IL-6), a proinflammatory cytokine commonly elevated in neuroinflammatory conditions, is linked to increased ADH secretion. In the context of BD, dysregulated monocyte activity results in spontaneous production of IL-6, and heightened IL-6 concentrations have been observed in the CSF of those experiencing acute neuro-BD episodes. Collectively, these observations imply that IL-6–driven inflammation may play a role in the development of SIADH in this context. Although IL-6 was not quantified in either serum or CSF for the current case, the significantly increased serum CRP may serve as an indirect indicator of an IL-6–dependent inflammatory process. Despite SIADH being known to arise in association with multiple conditions—including CNS inflammation, pulmonary disease, neoplasia, and use of certain drugs—the evidence from this case does not establish a direct causative association between SIADH and acute neuro-BD. The temporal relationship observed here might represent a general inflammatory reaction within the CNS rather than a mechanism specific to neuro-BD.

This case further highlights the risks associated with hyponatraemia due to SIADH in patients with acute neuro-BD. Earlier literature has indicated that persistent hiccups can act as a significant clinical indicator of severe hyponatraemia arising from SIADH secondary to central nervous system inflammation, such as in cases of meningitis. Additionally, during our initial assessment for fever, arthralgia, and generalized fatigue—which could signify either inflammatory or metabolic muscle injury—we evaluated CK levels. This assessment revealed rhabdomyolysis, a recognized yet frequently neglected complication of severe hyponatraemia that is not commonly screened for. Although these clinical features are documented consequences of marked hyponatraemia, they often go underrecognized in practice. It is essential for clinicians to recognize that symptoms appearing to be unrelated, like hiccups or rhabdomyolysis, might indicate significant electrolyte imbalances, especially amidst neuroinflammatory disorders.

Corticosteroids typically represent the first-line therapy for acute neuro-BD, yielding favorable responses in most cases. Nevertheless, spontaneous improvement has occasionally been documented, especially in patients exhibiting only meningoencephalitis-like features. In the case discussed here, treatment with colchicine alone resulted in amelioration of both mucocutaneous and neurological manifestations, as well as correction of hyponatraemia. Colchicine’s anti-inflammatory properties derive from its ability to block neutrophil chemotaxis and activation, alongside the inhibition of cytokine release such as IL-1β and IL-6. While colchicine is commonly administered for mucocutaneous symptoms in BD, comprehensive reviews have indicated that it does not effectively manage acute neuro-BD; immunosuppressive medications are typically required for satisfactory disease regulation. Reported cases show colchicine has predominantly been employed as an adjunct to corticosteroids or additional immunosuppressants, rather than as a stand-alone therapy. Although spontaneous remission in this case cannot be definitively ruled out, the observations here raise the possibility that colchicine may serve as a viable option for treating acute neuro-BD and associated conditions like SIADH, especially in situations where corticosteroids are inappropriate or contraindicated.

Conclusion:

We presented an uncommon instance where BD and acute neuro-BD were diagnosed simultaneously, with SIADH arising as a complication of neuro-BD. This report underscores that SIADH can manifest secondary to acute neuro-BD, emphasizing the necessity for clinicians to recognize this possible link. Administration of colchicine as a single agent resulted in swift resolution of both mucocutaneous and neurological symptoms, as well as correction of hyponatraemia, indicating colchicine's potential utility as an initial treatment option for neuro-BD. For BD patients exhibiting general CNS complaints—such as headache and fever—accompanied by hyponatraemia, it is crucial for healthcare professionals to consider acute neuro-BD, particularly when SIADH is also present.
