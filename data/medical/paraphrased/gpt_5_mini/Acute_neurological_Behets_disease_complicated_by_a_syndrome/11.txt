Title: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

Eiichi Kakehi, Yukako Matsumoto, Shigehisa Sakurai, Kazuhiko Kotani

Abstract:

Summary: We describe an uncommon presentation of acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) identified simultaneously with systemic Behçet’s disease (BD), meeting criteria for definite BD, and accompanied by syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). A male patient in his 60s developed fever, headache, and mucocutaneous features including oral aphthous ulcers, folliculitis-like eruptions, genital ulcers, and hyponatraemia. The BD diagnosis was established based on the mucocutaneous signs. Brain MRI revealed no abnormalities, but cerebrospinal fluid analysis demonstrated aseptic meningitis, supporting the presence of acute neuro-BD. Laboratory investigations met the criteria for SIADH. Initiation of colchicine alone resulted in complete improvement of mucocutaneous, neurological, and electrolyte abnormalities, without requiring corticosteroid therapy or additional immunosuppressives. This report highlights a potential link between acute neuro-BD and SIADH, and emphasizes the need to consider coexisting neurological and endocrine involvement in cases of newly diagnosed BD.

Background:

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a persistent, multisystemic inflammatory condition marked by recurrent oral and genital ulcerations, uveitis, and cutaneous manifestations. Involvement of the nervous system, known as neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), affects around 10%–20% of individuals and generally arises years after the original BD diagnosis. The acute form of neuro-BD often manifests as meningoencephalitis and parenchymal lesions within the brain, with the brainstem being a commonly affected area. While it is more typical for neurological symptoms to appear after mucocutaneous signs, simultaneous presentation of BD and neuro-BD is unusual. In such presentations, healthcare providers should evaluate both the skin and mucosal findings, as well as neurological complaints such as headaches, alterations in behavior, psychiatric symptoms, and localized neurological deficits.

Syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) is a recognized etiology of hyponatraemia, most frequently arising due to central nervous system (CNS) inflammatory processes, neoplasms, or certain pharmacologic agents. Owing to the inflammatory process inherent to acute neuro-BD, these patients may be predisposed to developing SIADH. While prior reports have described SIADH in BD patients treated with agents like cyclophosphamide, its development solely attributable to CNS inflammation from acute neuro-BD, without medicinal influence, remains insufficiently documented.

Here, we describe an unusual case involving the concurrent diagnosis of BD and acute neuro-BD complicated by SIADH. This report underscores the necessity of considering SIADH as a possible complication of acute neuro-BD, especially in patients who exhibit non-specific central nervous system symptoms alongside hyponatraemia.

Case presentation:

A man of Asian descent in his 60s presented to the emergency department after experiencing fever, headache, generalized fatigue, diminished appetite, ongoing hiccups, and joint pain involving the shoulders and ankles for 3 days. He also mentioned the presence of oral aphthous ulcers.

His past medical history was notable for type 2 diabetes mellitus, but he was not on therapy due to poor awareness of his diagnosis and inadequate adherence to dietary and exercise recommendations. Family history was noncontributory, with no cases of BD among his relatives. He reported occasional alcohol intake, denied tobacco use, recent travel, animal contact, or exposure to ill individuals. He worked in metal fabrication and had not engaged in sexual intercourse recently.

During assessment, the patient was alert but showed signs of tiredness. His vital measurements included a temperature of 38.1°C, blood pressure recorded at 135/73 mm Hg, a regular heart rate of 119 beats per minute, respiratory rate at 19 breaths per minute, and an oxygen saturation of 96% while breathing ambient air. On physical examination, there were oral aphthous ulcers present along with folliculitis-like eruptions noted on the face and trunk (see figure 1A, B, and C). Findings from the cardiopulmonary and abdominal exams were within normal limits. Manual testing of muscle strength showed no weakness in either the upper or lower limbs. There was no evidence of peripheral edema, and clinical assessment indicated the patient was euvolemic. He was hospitalized for additional diagnostic work-up.

Investigations:

Initial laboratory analysis demonstrated significant hyponatremia (124 mmol/L), raised creatine kinase (1349 U/L), as well as elevated aspartate aminotransferase (77 U/L), alanine aminotransferase (64 U/L), C-reactive protein (CRP) (180 mg/L; reference value <3 mg/L), and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (70 mm/hour; reference value <15 mm/hour). Renal function tests were within normal limits. The patient exhibited hyperglycemia with a plasma glucose of 14.2 mmol/L, and a HbA1c of 8.2% pointed to suboptimal glycemic control. Screening for hepatitis B and C returned negative results.

Assessment of endocrine parameters showed thyroid-stimulating hormone within reference range (1.11 mIU/L; reference: 0.50–5.00 mIU/L), free T4 (1.25 ng/dL; reference: 0.90–1.70 ng/dL), and a normal serum cortisol (42 µg/dL; reference: >6 µg/dL). Measured serum osmolality was reduced (267 mOsm/kg; reference: <270 mOsm/kg), whereas urine osmolality was elevated (900 mOsm/kg; reference: >300 mOsm/kg), and urinary sodium was increased (88 mmol/L; reference: >20 mmol/L), reflecting an inability to excrete free water. Although the patient was hypo-osmolar with hyponatremia, antidiuretic hormone (ADH) was elevated at 22.4 pg/mL. Electrocardiogram (ECG), chest radiograph, and computed tomography scans of the head, chest, and abdomen did not reveal abnormalities. Collectively, these results were consistent with syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH).

By the third day, the patient reported intensification of headache and vertigo. Despite the absence of neck rigidity or signs of cranial nerve dysfunction, central nervous system (CNS) inflammation remained a concern. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain did not demonstrate notable changes in the hypothalamus or other cerebral areas (figure 2). Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) studies demonstrated a mildly raised opening pressure (17 cmH₂O), elevated protein level (0.97 g/L), and a modest increase in lymphocytes (11 cells/µL). Testing of CSF cultures and polymerase chain reaction (PCR) assays for bacterial and mycobacterial organisms, herpes simplex virus (HSV), varicella zoster virus (VZV), and cryptococcal antigen were all negative. CSF cytology was negative for malignant cells.

Further workup was pursued to identify possible underlying systemic conditions. Sarcoidosis screening included measurement of lysozyme (5.1 µg/mL; reference range 5–10) and angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) (11.4 U/L; reference range 7–25), both within normal limits. Evaluation for hematological malignancies demonstrated an elevated soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) level (704 U/mL; reference, 122–496 U/mL) with a negative human T-cell leukemia virus type 1 (HTLV-1) antibody.

On the fifth day, the patient developed a painful ulceration of the scrotum (figure 1D). Alongside the presence of oral aphthae and skin eruptions resembling folliculitis, these manifestations indicated the possibility of a systemic Behçet's disease (BD) variant. The patient did not display pathergy upon pinprick testing. Ophthalmologic assessment found no evidence of uveitis. Genetic testing revealed positivity for HLA-B51.

Differential diagnosis:

Upon initial assessment, the constellation of fever, headache, general malaise, and hyponatraemia pointed toward a central nervous system infection or a systemic inflammatory process. Viral meningoencephalitis, especially due to HSV or VZV, was the primary suspicion. Nonetheless, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis failed to detect viral antigens or antibodies, thereby effectively ruling out these infections. Bacterial meningitis was similarly contemplated but discounted given the lack of neutrophilic predominance, presence of only mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, and negative CSF culture results. Tuberculous meningitis was also excluded based on negative acid-fast staining and tuberculosis DNA assays.

Consideration was given to a primary malignancy of the central nervous system because of the neurological presentation. However, MRI of the brain was unremarkable, and no malignant cells were observed in CSF cytology. Although the serum sIL-2R was slightly raised, this alone did not justify a diagnosis of CNS lymphoma.

Autoimmune disorders were assessed but were excluded on the grounds of absence of typical systemic manifestations and negative autoimmune antibody screens. Neurosarcoidosis remained in the differential, as MRI findings might remain normal early on, but later laboratory values argued against this condition. The slight elevation in sIL-2R was interpreted as a non-specific marker.

Ultimately, the diagnosis of definite Behçet’s disease (BD) was made, supported by clinical symptoms including fever, malaise, oral aphthae (two points), genital aphthosis (two points), and a rash resembling folliculitis (one point). Using the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease, the patient attained a total score of five (with four or more indicating a definitive diagnosis). Genital aphthosis, recognized as a particularly specific indicator for BD, reinforced this diagnosis. After infectious, malignant, and autoimmune etiologies were excluded, a diagnosis of acute neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) was confirmed, characterized by neurological manifestations occurring alongside mucocutaneous signs.

Treatment:

Upon admission (day 1), there was suspicion of a systemic inflammatory disorder, though its specific cause could not be determined. Broad-spectrum antibiotics with cefepime were started empirically after obtaining blood cultures, aiming to cover potential bacterial pathogens in the absence of an identifiable source of infection.

By day 3, the patient's headache and vertigo intensified, raising concern for involvement of the central nervous system via inflammation or infection. Empirical acyclovir therapy was given but later discontinued after diagnostic studies excluded HSV and VZV encephalitis.

Colchicine therapy was initiated on day 8 following the diagnosis of systemic Behçet’s disease (BD) and acute neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD). Topical corticosteroid ointment was applied for oral aphthous ulcers, while genital and skin manifestations were addressed with silver sulfadiazine cream. Management of SIADH focused on conservative fluid restriction (<800 mL per day), with vigilant daily assessment of serum sodium and prevention of overly rapid sodium correction. Neither hypertonic saline nor vasopressin receptor antagonists were necessary.

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient’s fever subsided, and his appetite returned the day after beginning colchicine therapy. Over the next two weeks, there was gradual improvement in his symptoms, including vertigo, general fatigue, hiccups, and mucocutaneous lesions. By the sixteenth day, his serum sodium had reached 136 mmol/L, and ADH levels had fallen to 1.8 pg/mL. Levels of myogenic enzymes also normalized.

Notably, the treatment did not involve systemic corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive agents. The patient was discharged following a one-month hospitalization, during which recovery from the acute illness was achieved, careful monitoring and management of hyponatremia was conducted, and rehabilitation was provided to address the associated physical deconditioning. Colchicine therapy was maintained for about four years, without any recurrence of Behçet's disease or neurological manifestations throughout that period. After his Behçet's disease stabilized, biguanide treatment was commenced, resulting in sustained glycaemic control.

Discussion:

This report details a rare case in which systemic BD and acute neuro-BD were identified concurrently. It further illustrates that acute neuro-BD may be associated with the development of SIADH. Improvement of mucocutaneous manifestations, neurological symptoms, and hyponatraemia following colchicine monotherapy implies a potential direct link between BD, neuro-BD, and SIADH. In individuals with BD exhibiting fever or headache in conjunction with hyponatraemia, clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for acute neuro-BD as an underlying etiology.

What makes this case particularly significant is the concurrent presentation of systemic BD and acute neuro-BD, evidenced by both mucocutaneous involvement and CNS pathology. Ordinarily, major organ complications such as CNS disease manifest roughly 4 years after BD diagnosis, and initial neurological presentations are seen in less than 5% of cases. Moreover, only about 10% of patients experience neuro-BD–related headaches. Consequently, promptly identifying neuro-BD at presentation posed a significant diagnostic challenge. The most frequent presentation of acute neuro-BD is a self-limited meningoencephalitis, sometimes accompanied by focal neurological symptoms. In this instance, brain MRI did not reveal abnormal findings at commonly affected BD sites, including the hypothalamic area. Acute neuro-BD can manifest as aseptic meningitis even in the absence of parenchymal lesions, and a normal MRI early in the disease course does not rule out the diagnosis. This absence of imaging abnormalities added further complexity to the diagnostic process. Although HLA-B51 genotyping was undertaken, it did not assist in confirming the diagnosis, which is consistent with international BD criteria that emphasize clinical findings rather than genetic markers such as HLA-B51. This case underscores the necessity of considering neuro-BD in patients presenting with mucocutaneous symptoms and vague neurological complaints.

At the outset of our differential diagnosis, we focused on infectious etiologies for CNS inflammation, given the patient’s persistent fever of unknown origin, neurological manifestations, and hyponatraemia, yet no causative pathogen was identified. Alternative explanations, such as connective tissue disorders and neoplastic conditions—including sarcoidosis and hematologic malignancies—were also considered. Consequently, as part of our comprehensive initial work-up, we assessed autoimmune serological markers along with serum ACE and sIL-2R, using these as general screening rather than guided by strong clinical suspicion. These investigations were selected primarily to rule out possible conditions. Ultimately, there were no supportive clinical signs, imaging studies, or CSF findings for these potential diagnoses, and the ancillary tests failed to significantly inform the final diagnostic outcome. The utility of ACE in diagnosing sarcoidosis is restricted by its limited sensitivity, while although sIL-2R can assist in the workup for malignant lymphoma, mild increases lack specificity and often indicate only generalized inflammatory activity. In this situation, the lack of genital ulcers during the early symptomatic period made Behçet’s disease less apparent, prompting an extensive differential. This approach underscores the necessity of avoiding arbitrary laboratory testing and illustrates the need for targeted investigations based on specific clinical indicators, with cautious interpretation of results.

While SIADH induced by medications has been observed in patients with BD, particularly with agents like cyclophosphamide, the relationship between acute neuro-BD and SIADH is not yet well defined. Neuro-BD is typified by inflammation within the central nervous system, which could potentially result in inappropriate secretion of ADH. Previous studies have demonstrated a link between the proinflammatory cytokine interleukin-6 (IL-6)—often elevated in neuroinflammatory conditions—and increased ADH production. In the context of BD, dysregulated monocyte activity results in unchecked IL-6 synthesis, and increased concentrations of IL-6 have been reported in the cerebrospinal fluid of individuals experiencing acute neuro-BD. These observations point toward a possible role for IL-6-driven inflammation in the development of SIADH in this clinical scenario. Although IL-6 was not quantified in the blood or CSF for this patient, a significantly raised serum CRP could be indicative of an inflammatory state mediated by IL-6. Despite SIADH being linked with multiple etiologies such as CNS inflammation, pulmonary disorders, malignancy, and certain drugs, the evidence from this case does not establish a definitive causative connection between SIADH and acute neuro-BD. The temporal correlation identified here might instead signify a general response to CNS inflammation, rather than a process unique to this disease.

This case further demonstrates the range of complications associated with SIADH-induced hyponatraemia during acute neuro-BD. Persistent hiccups have previously been identified as an important clinical indicator of severe hyponatraemia resulting from SIADH secondary to central nervous system inflammation, such as in cases of meningitis. Furthermore, creatine kinase was assessed during the initial workup for fever, arthralgia, and general fatigue—symptoms potentially attributable to inflammatory or metabolic injury to muscle—leading to the identification of rhabdomyolysis, a well-recognized but frequently missed consequence of severe hyponatraemia that is not typically screened for. While these findings are established, their significance as clinical indicators of marked hyponatraemia is often underestimated. It is important for clinicians to recognize that seemingly unrelated symptoms, like hiccups or rhabdomyolysis, can signal underlying electrolyte abnormalities, particularly within neuroinflammatory contexts.

Corticosteroids remain the primary therapy for acute neuro-BD, with the majority of patients showing favorable responses. Nonetheless, there have been instances of spontaneous remission in cases limited to meningoencephalitis-like features. In the case discussed, monotherapy with colchicine led to improvements in both mucocutaneous and neurological manifestations, as well as correction of hyponatraemia. Colchicine mediates its anti-inflammatory actions by restricting neutrophil chemotaxis and activation, in addition to suppressing production of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-6. While colchicine is commonly employed for managing mucocutaneous symptoms of BD, systematic reviews have demonstrated its lack of efficacy for acute neuro-BD, indicating that immunosuppressive therapy is usually necessary for optimal disease management. Notably, previously reported cases have typically utilized colchicine in conjunction with corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants, not as a sole agent. Although spontaneous clinical improvement cannot be entirely ruled out in the present case, these findings point toward colchicine as a potential therapeutic alternative for acute neuro-BD and associated conditions like SIADH, especially in scenarios where corticosteroid use is inadvisable or contraindicated.

Conclusion:

We reported an unusual instance where Behçet's disease (BD) and acute neuro-Behçet's disease (neuro-BD) were diagnosed simultaneously, with the clinical course further complicated by the development of syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). This case underscores that SIADH can arise as a complication during acute neuro-BD, emphasizing the necessity for healthcare professionals to recognize this possible link. Treatment with colchicine alone resulted in swift resolution of mucocutaneous lesions, neurological symptoms, and hyponatraemia, indicating that colchicine may serve as a useful initial therapy for neuro-BD. When patients with BD display vague central nervous system signs such as fever and headache along with hyponatraemia, clinicians should strongly consider acute neuro-BD associated with SIADH as part of their differential diagnosis.
