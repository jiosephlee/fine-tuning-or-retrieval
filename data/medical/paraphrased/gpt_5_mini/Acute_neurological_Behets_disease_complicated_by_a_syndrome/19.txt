Title: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

Eiichi Kakehi, Yukako Matsumoto, Shigehisa Sakurai, Kazuhiko Kotani

Abstract:

Summary: We present an unusual case of acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) diagnosed simultaneously with systemic Behçet’s disease (BD), classified as definite BD, and complicated by syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). A man in his 60s developed fever, headache, and mucocutaneous signs such as oral aphthae, folliculitis-like eruptions, genital ulceration, and hyponatraemia. The diagnosis of BD was established based on mucocutaneous presentation. Brain MRI revealed no pathological changes, while cerebrospinal fluid analysis demonstrated aseptic meningitis, corroborating acute neuro-BD. Laboratory investigations satisfied criteria for SIADH. Colchicine alone resulted in resolution of the mucocutaneous features, neurological symptoms, and hyponatraemia, without utilizing corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive therapies. This report highlights a potential link between acute neuro-BD and SIADH and emphasizes the importance of considering concomitant neurological and endocrine manifestations in patients newly diagnosed with BD.

Background:

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a persistent, multisystem inflammatory condition, marked by repeated episodes of oral and genital ulceration, uveitis, and various cutaneous manifestations. Neurological involvement, known as neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), is seen in about 10%–20% of cases, most often emerging several years post-diagnosis. Acute neuro-BD frequently manifests as meningoencephalitis accompanied by parenchymal brain lesions, with the brainstem being commonly affected. While neuro-BD usually appears after BD has been established for some time, simultaneous diagnosis of BD and neuro-BD is rare. In such scenarios, clinicians are advised to evaluate both mucocutaneous signs and neurological complaints—including headache, behavioral alterations, psychiatric disturbances, and localized neurological deficits.

Syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) is a recognized etiology of hyponatraemia, frequently resulting from inflammation within the central nervous system (CNS), malignancies, or specific pharmacological agents. Given the inflammatory nature of acute neuro-BD, these patients may be susceptible to SIADH. Although SIADH has been documented in BD cases undergoing therapy with agents such as cyclophosphamide, there is limited evidence regarding its development exclusively due to CNS inflammation from acute neuro-BD in the absence of drug-related triggers.

We present an unusual case in which BD and acute neuro-BD were diagnosed concurrently and complicated by SIADH. This report underscores the necessity of considering SIADH as a possible complication of acute neuro-BD, especially in patients exhibiting general CNS symptoms and hyponatraemia.

Case presentation:

A man of Asian descent in his 60s arrived at the emergency department with a three-day history characterized by fever, headache, generalized fatigue, decreased appetite, continuous hiccups, and joint pains involving his shoulders and ankles. Additionally, he complained of oral aphthous ulcers.

His past medical history was notable for type 2 diabetes mellitus, which remained untreated due to limited understanding of his diagnosis and insufficient adherence to diet and exercise recommendations. There was no family history suggestive of BD among his relatives. The patient drank alcohol infrequently, did not smoke, and denied recent travel, animal exposures, or contact with ill individuals. He was employed in the metalworking sector and reported no recent sexual activity.

Upon assessment, the patient was conscious but showed signs of tiredness. Recorded vital signs included a temperature of 38.1°C, blood pressure measuring 135/73 mm Hg, heart rate of 119 beats per minute with a regular rhythm, respiratory rate of 19 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation at 96% while breathing ambient air. The physical exam identified oral aphthae and rashes resembling folliculitis on the face and trunk (see figure 1A, B, and C). No abnormal findings were noted during the examination of the cardiovascular, respiratory, or abdominal systems. Manual testing of muscle strength demonstrated that the muscles of both the upper and lower limbs were intact. There was an absence of peripheral edema, and clinically he was considered euvolemic. The patient was admitted for further investigation.

Investigations:

Initial laboratory evaluations demonstrated significant hyponatraemia (124 mmol/L), increased levels of creatine kinase (1349 U/L), aspartate aminotransferase (77 U/L), alanine aminotransferase (64 U/L), C-reactive protein (CRP) (180 mg/L; reference, <3 mg/L) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (70 mm/hour; reference, <15 mm/hour), with preserved renal function. Additionally, the patient exhibited hyperglycaemia (plasma glucose 14.2 mmol/L) and a raised HbA1c of 8.2%, signifying poor glycaemic management. Results for hepatitis B and C markers were negative.

Hormonal assessment showed thyroid-stimulating hormone within the normal range (1.11 mIU/L; reference, 0.50–5.00 mIU/L), free T4 at 1.25 ng/dL (reference, 0.90–1.70 ng/dL) and serum cortisol at 42 µg/dL (reference, >6 µg/dL). The serum osmolality was decreased (267 mOsm/kg; reference, <270 mOsm/kg) whereas the urine osmolality was markedly raised (900 mOsm/kg; reference, >300 mOsm/kg) and urinary sodium was increased (88 mmol/L; reference, >20 mmol/L), indicative of compromised water excretion. In the setting of hypo-osmolar hyponatraemia, antidiuretic hormone (ADH) concentration was elevated (22.4 pg/mL). The patient’s ECG findings were within normal limits, and imaging studies including chest X-ray and CT scans of the head, chest and abdomen showed no abnormalities. Collectively, these results were consistent with a diagnosis of SIADH.

On the third day, his headache and dizziness became more severe. Despite the absence of neck rigidity or signs of cranial nerve dysfunction, central nervous system inflammation was considered. MRI of the brain demonstrated no notable changes affecting the hypothalamus or other areas (figure 2). Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) assessment indicated a slightly raised opening pressure (17 cmH₂O), elevated protein concentration (0.97 g/L), and minor lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL). All CSF cultures and assays for bacterial and mycobacterial organisms, herpes simplex virus (HSV), varicella zoster virus (VZV), and cryptococcal antigen returned negative results. No malignant cells were observed on CSF cytology.

Additional evaluations were undertaken to rule out systemic illnesses. Tests targeting sarcoidosis showed lysozyme at 5.1 µg/mL (reference range: 5–10) and angiotensin-converting enzyme at 11.4 U/L (reference range: 7–25), both within normal limits. Investigations for hematological malignancies found soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) at 704 U/mL (reference: 122–496 U/mL), and human T-cell leukemia virus type 1 antibody was negative.

By day 5, he developed a painful ulcer in the scrotal region (figure 1D). Together with the presence of oral aphthous ulcers and rashes resembling folliculitis, these signs pointed to a systemic variant of BD. No pinprick hypersensitivity was identified. Eye examination did not show uveitis. He was HLA-B51 positive.

Differential diagnosis:

Upon admission, the presence of fever, headache, general malaise, and hyponatremia raised concern for a central nervous system infection or systemic inflammatory condition. Viral meningoencephalitis, especially from HSV or VZV, was initially considered. Nevertheless, cerebrospinal fluid analyses did not detect any viral antigens or antibodies, thereby ruling out these etiologies. Bacterial meningitis was also suspected but excluded, given the lack of a neutrophilic predominance in the CSF, the presence of mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, and negative culture results. Tuberculous meningitis was discounted because acid-fast bacilli staining and mycobacterial DNA assays were negative.

Given the neurological presentation, a primary malignancy of the CNS was entertained. However, brain MRI revealed no abnormalities and CSF cytology was devoid of malignant cells. While there was a mild elevation in sIL-2R, this alone was inadequate to establish CNS lymphoma as the diagnosis.

Consideration was also given to autoimmune disease; however, this was excluded due to a lack of suggestive systemic signs and negative results on screening for autoantibodies. Neurosarcoidosis remained in the differential because early MRI imaging can appear unremarkable, though subsequent laboratory evaluations reduced its likelihood. The slightly increased sIL-2R was interpreted as a non-specific laboratory abnormality.

A definitive diagnosis of Behçet’s disease was made based on the clinical presentation, which included fever, fatigue, oral aphthae (2 points), genital aphthosis (2 points), and a folliculitis-like eruption (1 point). Using the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease, he accumulated five points (with four or more necessary for a definite diagnosis). Genital aphthosis is especially characteristic of Behçet’s disease, lending significant weight to this diagnosis. After infectious, neoplastic, and autoimmune etiologies were excluded, a diagnosis of acute neuro-Behçet's disease was confirmed, given CNS involvement with associated mucocutaneous signs.

Treatment:

Upon admission on day 1, an underlying inflammatory process was considered, although the precise cause was indeterminate. Cefepime was started as empirical antimicrobial therapy after obtaining blood cultures, aimed at a possible bacterial infection despite no definitive infectious site identified.

By day 3, the patient’s headache and vertigo had intensified, raising suspicion for central nervous system inflammation or infection. Empirical treatment with acyclovir was begun but discontinued once diagnostic tests excluded HSV and VZV encephalitis.

On day 8, colchicine was commenced based on the diagnosis of systemic Behçet’s disease and acute neuro-Behçet’s disease. Topical corticosteroid ointment was applied to treat oral aphthous ulcers. Genital and skin lesions were addressed with silver sulfadiazine cream. Syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) was managed conservatively through fluid restriction (<800 mL/day), daily monitoring of serum sodium, and precautions to prevent rapid sodium correction. Hypertonic saline and vasopressin receptor antagonists were not necessary.

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient’s fever subsided and his appetite returned the day after initiating colchicine treatment. Over the subsequent two weeks, his symptoms—including vertigo, generalized fatigue, hiccups, and lesions involving mucosal and cutaneous surfaces—progressively resolved. On day 16, serum sodium was restored to 136 mmol/L, and ADH concentrations had decreased to 1.8 pg/mL. Levels of myogenic enzymes likewise normalized.

Notably, systemic corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive agents were not employed during his care. Following a one-month hospitalization, during which the acute episode resolved, hyponatremia was meticulously monitored and addressed, and physical rehabilitation was conducted to address functional decline, the patient was discharged. Colchicine was maintained for approximately four years, during which there was no recurrence of BD or neurologic symptoms. Once BD had stabilized, biguanide treatment was initiated, resulting in effective glycemic control thereafter.

Discussion:

This case illustrates the concurrent identification of systemic BD and acute neuro-BD, a rare clinical occurrence. Additionally, it highlights that acute neuro-BD may be accompanied by SIADH. Improvement in mucocutaneous manifestations, neurological signs, and hyponatraemia following the initiation of colchicine monotherapy indicates a potential direct link between BD, neuro-BD, and SIADH. Clinicians should suspect acute neuro-BD in patients with BD who exhibit fever or headache together with hyponatraemia.

Of particular note in this case is the simultaneous recognition of systemic BD and acute neuro-BD, corroborated by the presence of mucocutaneous lesions and central nervous system involvement. Ordinarily, major organ involvement, including CNS abnormalities, becomes apparent roughly four years after BD is initially diagnosed, and neurologic symptoms at onset are infrequent, occurring in fewer than 5% of cases. Headaches directly attributable to neuro-BD are uncommon, affecting only about 10% of patients. Thus, the initial identification of neuro-BD in this instance presented significant challenges. Most often, acute neuro-BD manifests as meningoencephalitis that resolves spontaneously, with or without focal neurologic deficits. In this patient, brain MRI did not show abnormalities at sites typically implicated in BD, such as the hypothalamic area. Acute neuro-BD can appear as aseptic meningitis without detectable parenchymal lesions, and normal brain MRI findings—especially in early disease—do not rule out the diagnosis. The absence of radiological evidence added difficulty to the diagnostic process. Although HLA-B51 was tested, the results did not aid in making the diagnosis, which is consistent with international BD classification criteria that prioritize clinical assessment over HLA-B51 status. This case underscores the necessity of considering neuro-BD in patients presenting with mucocutaneous symptoms and ambiguous neurological complaints.

At the outset of the differential diagnosis, we initially favored infection as the primary cause of CNS inflammation due to the coexistence of unexplained fever, neurological manifestations, and hyponatraemia; nonetheless, no specific infectious agent was identified. Additional potential etiologies, such as connective tissue disorders and malignancy—including sarcoidosis and hematologic cancers—were also taken into account. Thus, we conducted a comprehensive initial work-up that included assessment of autoimmune serological markers, serum ACE, and sIL-2R to screen for these conditions. These investigations were employed selectively within an exclusion framework rather than being driven by high clinical suspicion. Ultimately, there was an absence of clinical, imaging, or CSF findings supportive of these diagnoses, and these tests did not provide valuable information for establishing the final diagnosis. While serum ACE demonstrates low sensitivity for detecting sarcoidosis, sIL-2R can aid in evaluating malignant lymphoma, though modest elevations are non-specific and can merely indicate inflammation. In the present case, the lack of genital ulcerations early in the disease course complicated the consideration of BD, resulting in a more extensive differential. This case underscores the necessity to avoid unwarranted laboratory testing and underscores that diagnostic studies should be reserved for when there are substantiated clinical indications, with findings interpreted judiciously.

While drug-induced SIADH has previously been documented in individuals with BD treated with agents like cyclophosphamide, the link between acute neuro-BD and SIADH remains largely unclear. Neuro-BD involves inflammation within the central nervous system, which could potentially alter ADH regulation. Multiple investigations have demonstrated that interleukin-6 (IL-6), a proinflammatory cytokine commonly elevated in neuroinflammatory conditions, is correlated with increased secretion of ADH. In BD, atypical activation of monocytes results in spontaneous production of IL-6, and raised IL-6 concentrations have been identified in the cerebrospinal fluid of those experiencing acute neuro-BD. These observations imply that IL-6-driven inflammation may play a role in the development of SIADH in such cases. Although serum or CSF IL-6 levels were not determined in this instance, the significantly increased serum CRP could be indicative of an underlying inflammatory response mediated by IL-6. Despite SIADH being associated with numerous factors such as CNS inflammation, pulmonary pathology, cancer, and certain drugs, current evidence does not establish a definitive causative relationship between SIADH and acute neuro-BD. The temporal pattern seen in this report may suggest a general reaction to CNS inflammation rather than a process unique to the disease itself.

This case further demonstrates the possible complications associated with hyponatraemia resulting from SIADH in the context of acute neuro-BD. Previous literature has highlighted that persistent hiccups may represent a significant clinical indicator of severe hyponatraemia due to SIADH secondary to central nervous system inflammation, such as meningitis. As part of the initial assessment for fever, arthralgia, and generalized fatigue—symptoms which may stem from inflammatory or metabolic muscle injury—we measured CK and discovered rhabdomyolysis, a known but frequently neglected consequence of severe hyponatraemia that is not typically considered in routine evaluation. Although these clinical presentations are documented, their significance is often underestimated. It is important for clinicians to recognize that symptoms such as hiccups or rhabdomyolysis, which may appear unrelated, can be manifestations of underlying electrolyte imbalances, particularly in patients with neuroinflammatory diseases.

Corticosteroids are typically regarded as the primary therapy for acute neuro-BD, with most patients demonstrating a positive response. Nevertheless, spontaneous remission has been documented in patients exhibiting only meningoencephalitis-like features. In this report, administration of colchicine alone led to improvement in both mucocutaneous and neurological signs, as well as in hyponatraemia. Colchicine’s anti-inflammatory properties stem from its capacity to block neutrophil movement and activation, and to reduce production of cytokines like IL-1β and IL-6. While colchicine is commonly prescribed for managing mucocutaneous symptoms of BD, systematic reviews indicate it does not provide benefit for acute neuro-BD, where immunosuppressive medications are typically necessary for sufficient disease management. Indeed, existing literature describes colchicine being employed alongside corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive agents, rather than independently. Although the possibility of spontaneous improvement cannot be ruled out in this scenario, these observations imply that colchicine could represent a viable therapeutic alternative for acute neuro-BD and associated complications such as SIADH, particularly in circumstances where corticosteroids are not suitable or are contraindicated.

Conclusion:

We reported an uncommon occurrence where BD and acute neuro-BD were diagnosed simultaneously, with SIADH arising as a complication of neuro-BD. This case illustrates that SIADH can develop secondary to acute neuro-BD and underscores the need for clinicians to recognize this possible link. Treatment with colchicine alone resulted in prompt resolution of mucocutaneous symptoms, neurological findings, and hyponatraemia, indicating that colchicine may serve as an effective initial therapy for neuro-BD. For patients with BD presenting with general CNS symptoms like fever and headache alongside hyponatraemia, clinicians should be vigilant for acute neuro-BD in cases where SIADH is also present.
