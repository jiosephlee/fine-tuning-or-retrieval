Title: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

Eiichi Kakehi, Yukako Matsumoto, Shigehisa Sakurai, Kazuhiko Kotani

Abstract:

Summary: We describe an unusual presentation of acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), occurring simultaneously with systemic Behçet’s disease (BD), classified as definite BD, and accompanied by syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). A man in his sixth decade presented with fever, headache, and mucocutaneous manifestations, including oral aphthae, rashes resembling folliculitis, genital ulcers, and hyponatraemia. BD was diagnosed based on the presence of mucocutaneous symptoms. Brain MRI findings were unremarkable, while cerebrospinal fluid analysis demonstrated aseptic meningitis, providing evidence for acute neuro-BD. Laboratory evaluations fulfilled the diagnostic criteria for SIADH. Administration of colchicine as a single agent resolved the mucocutaneous symptoms, neurological features, and hyponatraemia, with no requirement for corticosteroids or alternative immunosuppressive therapy. This report highlights a potential link between acute neuro-BD and SIADH and emphasizes the necessity of identifying concurrent neurological and endocrine complications in patients with newly diagnosed BD.

Background:

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a chronic inflammatory condition affecting multiple organ systems, defined by recurrent oral and genital ulcerations, uveitis, and various cutaneous manifestations. Neurological manifestations, collectively termed neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), arise in roughly 10%–20% of cases and are most often observed several years following BD diagnosis. Acute neuro-BD is characterized by meningoencephalitis and lesions within the brain parenchyma, frequently affecting the brainstem region. While simultaneous onset of BD and neuro-BD is rare—given that neuro-BD more commonly develops in a delayed fashion—clinicians facing such presentations should carefully evaluate for both mucocutaneous and neurological signs, such as headaches, personality or psychiatric changes, and localized neurological deficits.

The syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH), a recognized contributor to hyponatraemia, is frequently associated with inflammatory processes of the central nervous system (CNS), malignancies, or particular medications. Considering the inflammatory mechanisms at play in acute neuro-BD, individuals may be susceptible to SIADH development. Although SIADH has previously been observed among BD patients treated with agents like cyclophosphamide, its emergence in the context of acute neuro-BD, without the influence of pharmacological triggers and attributable strictly to CNS inflammation, is not well established.

Here, we present an unusual case involving the simultaneous diagnosis of BD and acute neuro-BD with concurrent SIADH. This report underscores the need to recognize SIADH as a possible complication of acute neuro-BD, especially in patients who exhibit non-specific CNS manifestations accompanied by hyponatraemia.

Case presentation:

A man of Asian descent in his 60s arrived at the emergency department, reporting three days of fever, headache, overall fatigue, diminished appetite, ongoing hiccups, and joint pain localized to the shoulders and ankles. He additionally complained of oral aphthous ulcers.

His past medical history revealed untreated type 2 diabetes mellitus, stemming from minimal awareness of his diagnosis and lack of adherence to recommended dietary and exercise routines. There were no significant familial predispositions, with no relatives diagnosed with BD. He drank alcohol sporadically, did not use tobacco, had no recent history of travel, and reported no contact with animals or ill persons. His occupation was in metalworking, and he had not participated in sexual activity recently.

Upon evaluation, the patient was alert but showed signs of fatigue. Recorded vital parameters included a temperature of 38.1°C, blood pressure measured at 135/73 mm Hg, regular heart rate of 119 bpm, respiratory rate at 19 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation of 96% breathing room air. The physical exam identified oral aphthae and skin lesions resembling folliculitis on the face and trunk (figure 1A, B, and C). Examination of the heart, lungs, and abdomen did not reveal any abnormal findings. Manual testing of muscle strength demonstrated normal function in both upper and lower limbs. There was no evidence of peripheral edema, and clinical assessment indicated the patient was euvolemic. He was hospitalized for additional investigation.

Investigations:

Initial laboratory assessment demonstrated significant hyponatraemia (124 mmol/L), increased creatine kinase (1349 U/L), aspartate aminotransferase (77 U/L), alanine aminotransferase (64 U/L), C-reactive protein (CRP) at 180 mg/L (reference <3 mg/L), and erythrocyte sedimentation rate of 70 mm/hour (reference <15 mm/hour), while renal function remained within normal limits. The patient was also found to have hyperglycaemia with plasma glucose at 14.2 mmol/L and HbA1c of 8.2%, reflecting inadequate glycaemic management. Markers for hepatitis B and C returned negative.

Assessment of endocrine parameters showed thyroid-stimulating hormone at 1.11 mIU/L (reference 0.50–5.00 mIU/L), free T4 at 1.25 ng/dL (reference 0.90–1.70 ng/dL), and serum cortisol at 42 µg/dL (reference >6 µg/dL), all within reference ranges. Laboratory values indicated low serum osmolality (267 mOsm/kg; reference <270 mOsm/kg), increased urine osmolality (900 mOsm/kg, reference >300 mOsm/kg), and elevated urinary sodium (88 mmol/L; reference >20 mmol/L), suggesting defective water clearance. Antidiuretic hormone (ADH) was raised at 22.4 pg/mL in the context of hypo-osmolar hyponatraemia. Electrocardiogram findings were normal, and imaging studies including chest X-ray as well as CT scans of the head, chest, and abdomen were unremarkable. Collectively, these findings were in keeping with a diagnosis of SIADH.

By the third day, his headache and vertigo intensified. Despite the absence of neck rigidity and cranial nerve deficits, central nervous system inflammation was considered. MRI of the brain did not demonstrate significant lesions in the hypothalamus or other regions (figure 2). Analysis of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) indicated a mildly increased opening pressure at 17 cmH₂O, elevated protein concentration at 0.97 g/L, and slight lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL). Tests and cultures of CSF for bacterial and mycobacterial organisms, herpes simplex virus (HSV), varicella zoster virus (VZV), and cryptococcal antigen all returned negative results. CSF cytology detected no malignant cells.

Additional testing was undertaken to assess for systemic disorders. Evaluation for sarcoidosis showed lysozyme within the normal range (5.1 µg/mL; reference 5–10) and normal angiotensin-converting enzyme levels (11.4 U/L; reference 7–25). Screening for hematological malignancy indicated an elevated soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) level (704 U/mL; reference, 122–496 U/mL), and testing for human T-cell leukemia virus type 1 antibody was negative.

On the fifth day, a painful ulcer developed on the scrotum (figure 1D). Along with oral aphthous lesions and rash resembling folliculitis, these manifestations raised suspicion for a systemic variant of BD. The patient did not display pinprick hypersensitivity. Eye examination showed absence of uveitis. Testing for HLA-B51 was positive.

Differential diagnosis:

Upon admission, the presence of fever, headache, generalized malaise, and hyponatremia raised suspicion for either a central nervous system infection or a systemic inflammatory disorder. Viral meningoencephalitis, especially from HSV or VZV, was initially presumed. Nonetheless, viral antigen and antibody tests in CSF yielded negative results, thus ruling out these pathogens. Bacterial meningitis was contemplated but excluded due to the lack of neutrophilic predominance, the presence of only slight lymphocytic pleocytosis, and negative results from CSF cultures. Tuberculous meningitis was also deemed unlikely following negative acid-fast staining and tuberculosis DNA assays.

A primary malignancy of the CNS was considered in light of neurologic manifestations; however, brain MRI was unremarkable, and no malignant cells were identified on CSF cytology. Although the soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) concentration was mildly increased, this finding alone did not substantiate a diagnosis of CNS lymphoma.

Potential autoimmune etiologies were also assessed but dismissed because there were no typical systemic features and autoantibody panels were negative. Neurosarcoidosis remained a consideration, since MRI findings may be non-revealing in early disease; nevertheless, subsequent laboratory evidence rendered this diagnosis improbable. The modest sIL-2R elevation was viewed as a nonspecific marker.

This case was ultimately diagnosed as definite Behçet’s disease (BD) based on clinical presentation, which included fever and fatigue, the presence of oral aphthae (scoring two points), genital aphthosis (two points), and a folliculitis-like eruption (one point). According to the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease, a total of five points (where four or more confirms diagnosis) were accumulated. Of note, genital aphthosis is a particularly specific feature of BD, and the overall constellation of findings made BD highly likely. Following thorough exclusion of infectious, malignant, and autoimmune etiologies, a diagnosis of acute neuro-Behçet’s disease was made, characterized by central nervous system involvement occurring concurrently with mucocutaneous lesions.

Treatment:

On the first day after admission, clinicians considered the possibility of an underlying inflammatory disorder, though its precise cause was not yet determined. Cefepime was prescribed empirically once blood cultures had been obtained, aiming to treat a potential bacterial infection even though no definitive infectious source had been identified.

By the third day, the patient's headache and vertigo had intensified, raising suspicion for central nervous system involvement by inflammation or infection. Acyclovir was started on an empirical basis, but later discontinued once testing excluded HSV and VZV encephalitis.

On day eight, following confirmation of systemic BD with acute neuro-BD, colchicine therapy was initiated. Topical corticosteroid ointment was applied for oral aphthous ulcers. Genital ulcers and skin lesions were treated using silver sulfadiazine cream. SIADH was addressed conservatively with a fluid restriction protocol of less than 800 mL per day, meticulous daily assessment of serum sodium, and caution to avoid rapid sodium correction. The use of hypertonic saline or vasopressin receptor antagonists was unnecessary.

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient's fever subsided and his appetite returned the day following the initiation of colchicine therapy. Over the subsequent two weeks, symptoms such as vertigo, fatigue, hiccups, and mucocutaneous manifestations progressively resolved. On day 16, his serum sodium level normalized to 136 mmol/L and ADH concentrations decreased to 1.8 pg/mL. Levels of myogenic enzymes also fell within normal limits.

Notably, treatment did not involve systemic corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive agents. After a one-month hospital stay, during which he recovered from the acute phase, his hyponatraemia was closely observed and managed, and he underwent rehabilitation to address decreased physical capacity, the patient was discharged. Colchicine therapy was maintained for approximately four years, during which neither BD flare nor neurological symptoms recurred. Upon stabilization of his BD, biguanide treatment was started, resulting in sustained effective glycaemic control.

Discussion:

This case features the concurrent identification of systemic Behçet’s disease (BD) and acute neuro-BD—a presentation that is infrequently encountered. Additionally, the episode underscores that acute neuro-BD can occur in conjunction with SIADH. The patient’s improvement in mucocutaneous findings, neurologic symptoms, and hyponatraemia following initiation of colchicine monotherapy implies a possible direct relationship among BD, neuro-BD, and SIADH. For patients with BD who develop fever or headache in combination with hyponatraemia, clinicians should include acute neuro-BD among the differential diagnoses.

This report is distinguished by the concurrent recognition of systemic BD and acute neuro-BD, corroborated by both mucocutaneous involvement and CNS symptoms. Ordinarily, manifestations affecting major organs like the CNS emerge approximately 4 years post BD diagnosis, while initial presentations with neurological involvement are rare, appearing in less than 5% of BD cases. A direct association between headache and neuro-BD is observed in about 10% of affected individuals. Consequently, identifying neuro-BD at presentation in this scenario proved inherently difficult. The acute neuro-BD typically manifests as a self-limited meningoencephalitis and may occur with or without localized neurological deficits. In this patient, cranial MRI disclosed no notable abnormalities in usual BD-affected regions, such as the hypothalamus. Acute neuro-BD can manifest as aseptic meningitis without brain parenchymal involvement, and a normal MRI early in the disease does not exclude the diagnosis; this absence of imaging findings added to the diagnostic challenge. HLA-B51 testing was undertaken but yielded no diagnostic value—consistent with international BD guidelines, which do not mandate HLA-B51 and predominantly use clinical criteria. The case emphasizes the need to consider neuro-BD when patients with mucocutaneous findings develop vague neurologic complaints.

At the outset of the differential diagnosis, infection was the leading consideration for the source of CNS inflammation due to the patient's unexplained fever, neurological manifestations, and hyponatraemia; nonetheless, no causative organism was detected. Alternative etiologies for CNS inflammation, such as connective tissue disorders and neoplastic processes, were also evaluated, with sarcoidosis and hematologic malignancies specifically included in the differential. Consequently, as part of a comprehensive initial workup, autoimmune serological tests, serum angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE), and soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) levels were obtained as screening tools. These tests were selected on an exclusionary basis rather than stemming from significant clinical suspicion. Neither clinical assessment, imaging, nor CSF analysis provided evidence for these conditions, and these investigations ultimately did not impact the determination of the diagnosis. Since ACE demonstrates limited sensitivity for sarcoidosis and slight elevations of sIL-2R, though potentially useful in malignancy evaluation, are non-specific and may only reflect general inflammatory activity, their diagnostic utility was restricted. In this instance, the lack of genital ulcers during the early stage of symptom development rendered Behçet's disease (BD) a less obvious diagnosis, thus prompting a broad investigative approach. This case underscores the necessity for judicious test ordering, recommending that clinicians undertake such investigations only in the presence of relevant clinical indicators and interpret results carefully.

While cases of drug-induced SIADH in BD patients taking agents such as cyclophosphamide have been described, the link between acute neuro-BD and SIADH is not well elucidated. Neuro-BD involves central nervous system inflammation, which could play a role in abnormal ADH regulation. Evidence from multiple studies indicates that interleukin-6 (IL-6), a proinflammatory cytokine commonly elevated in neuroinflammatory conditions, correlates with increased ADH secretion. In BD, excessive monocyte activation leads to spontaneous IL-6 generation, and high CSF IL-6 concentrations have been observed in individuals experiencing acute neuro-BD episodes. These data imply that IL-6–driven inflammation may be implicated in SIADH within this context. Although IL-6 was not quantified in serum or CSF in the current report, the significantly raised serum CRP level could indicate a concurrent IL-6–related inflammatory process. SIADH is linked to numerous causes, including CNS inflammation, lung disease, malignancy, and specific medications, yet the evidence presented here does not establish a definitive causal relationship between SIADH and acute neuro-BD. The timing seen in this case may simply represent a general reaction to CNS inflammation, as opposed to a mechanism specific to neuro-BD.

This case demonstrates the possible complications arising from hyponatraemia due to SIADH in acute neuro-BD. Prior studies have indicated that persistent hiccups can be a significant clinical indicator of severe hyponatraemia associated with SIADH secondary to CNS inflammation, such as meningitis. Furthermore, during the initial assessment for fever, arthralgia, and general fatigue—which could be linked to inflammatory or metabolic injury to muscle—we measured CK levels and identified rhabdomyolysis. This is a recognized, though frequently overlooked, complication of severe hyponatraemia that is not commonly assessed. These symptoms are established yet often undervalued clinical consequences of profound hyponatraemia. Physicians should recognize that symptoms like hiccups or rhabdomyolysis, which may seem unrelated, can actually point to underlying disturbances in electrolytes, especially within the context of neuroinflammatory disease.

Corticosteroids are typically regarded as the primary therapy for acute neuro-BD, with most individuals experiencing a positive response. Nonetheless, spontaneous improvement has been documented in patients whose clinical presentation is restricted to meningoencephalitis-like symptoms. In the current case, colchicine administered alone was associated with amelioration of mucocutaneous and neurological manifestations, as well as normalization of hyponatraemia. Colchicine possesses anti-inflammatory properties, achieved through the inhibition of neutrophil migration and activation, as well as suppression of cytokine production—including IL-1β and IL-6. While colchicine is commonly used to address mucocutaneous symptoms in BD, systematic reviews have indicated its lack of efficacy in acute neuro-BD, with immunosuppressive medications typically required for sufficient control of the disease. Indeed, previous reports have described colchicine being employed alongside corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants rather than as a sole agent. Although spontaneous remission cannot be ruled out in this instance, our observations indicate that colchicine could represent a promising therapeutic option for acute neuro-BD and associated conditions such as SIADH, particularly in situations where corticosteroid treatment is not feasible or contraindicated.

Conclusion:

We report an unusual instance where Behçet's disease (BD) and acute neuro-BD were diagnosed simultaneously, with syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) complicating the neuro-BD. This case illustrates that SIADH can present as a consequence of acute neuro-BD, emphasizing the need for clinicians to be vigilant for this association. Treatment with colchicine alone resulted in swift resolution of mucocutaneous findings, neurologic symptoms, and hyponatremia, indicating colchicine could be an effective early intervention for neuro-BD. Clinicians should consider the possibility of acute neuro-BD complicated by SIADH in patients with BD who develop hyponatremia along with non-specific central nervous system manifestations such as headache and fever.
