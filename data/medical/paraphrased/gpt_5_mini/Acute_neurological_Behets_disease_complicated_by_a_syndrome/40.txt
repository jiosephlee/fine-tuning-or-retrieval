Title: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

Eiichi Kakehi, Yukako Matsumoto, Shigehisa Sakurai, Kazuhiko Kotani

Abstract:

Summary: We describe an unusual presentation of acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) identified at the same time as systemic Behçet’s disease (BD), meeting criteria for definite BD, and further complicated by syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). A male patient in his 60s was admitted with fever, headache, and mucocutaneous symptoms such as oral aphthae, folliculitis-like eruptions, genital ulcers, and low sodium levels. BD was diagnosed based on the mucocutaneous manifestations. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain showed no lesions, while cerebrospinal fluid analysis indicated aseptic meningitis, confirming acute neuro-BD. Laboratory assessment met the diagnostic standards for SIADH. Administration of colchicine alone resulted in the improvement of mucocutaneous, neurological, and electrolyte abnormalities, and no corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive therapies were required. This report indicates a potential link between acute neuro-BD and SIADH, highlighting the necessity to recognize both neurological and endocrine symptoms in individuals with a new diagnosis of BD.

Background:

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a persistent, multisystem inflammatory condition marked by repeated oral and genital ulcerations, uveitis, and dermatologic manifestations. Neurological complications, identified as neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), arise in roughly 10%–20% of individuals, generally manifesting years after BD is first identified. Acute neuro-BD most commonly features meningoencephalitis and brain parenchymal lesions, with a predilection for the brainstem. Although neuro-BD frequently develops some time following BD diagnosis, simultaneous identification of both BD and neuro-BD is unusual. When this occurs, thorough evaluation of both mucocutaneous signs and neurological indicators—such as headaches, behavioral or psychiatric symptoms, and localized neurological deficits—is warranted.

Syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) is a recognized etiology of hyponatraemia and is frequently linked to disorders such as CNS inflammation, malignancies, or as a side effect of certain drugs. The inflammatory component of acute neuro-BD may predispose patients to SIADH. Reports exist describing SIADH in BD patients undergoing treatments like cyclophosphamide; however, SIADH occurring in the context of CNS inflammation from acute neuro-BD, independent of medication triggers, is not well established.

Here, we present an unusual case wherein BD and acute neuro-BD were simultaneously diagnosed and complicated by SIADH. This case underscores the need to include SIADH in the differential when evaluating patients with acute neuro-BD who develop unexplained CNS manifestations and low serum sodium levels.

Case presentation:

A man of Asian descent in his 60s arrived at the emergency department following three days of fever, headache, generalized fatigue, reduced appetite, ongoing hiccups, and joint pain involving his shoulders and ankles. He additionally noted the presence of oral aphthous ulcers.

His past medical history was significant for type 2 diabetes mellitus, which was untreated due to his poor understanding of the diagnosis and lack of adherence to dietary or exercise recommendations. There was no notable family history, including no relatives diagnosed with BD. He drank alcohol occasionally, did not smoke, and there was no history of recent travel, animal contact, or exposure to ill individuals. He was employed in metalworking and reported no recent sexual encounters.

Upon assessment, the patient was awake but displayed signs of tiredness. His vital parameters measured as follows: temperature 38.1°C, blood pressure 135/73 mm Hg, pulse rate 119 beats per minute (regular), respiratory rate 19 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation 96% while breathing ambient air. Physical inspection showed the presence of oral aphthous ulcers and rashes resembling folliculitis located on the face and torso (refer to figure 1A, B, and C). Cardiopulmonary and abdominal assessments yielded normal results. Strength testing of the upper and lower limbs did not indicate any muscle weakness. No peripheral edema was noted, and clinical evaluation suggested that his volume status was euvolemic. He was hospitalized for additional investigation.

Investigations:

Initial laboratory assessment demonstrated pronounced hyponatraemia (124 mmol/L), elevated creatine kinase (1349 U/L), increased aspartate aminotransferase (77 U/L), alanine aminotransferase (64 U/L), C-reactive protein (180 mg/L; reference, <3 mg/L), and a heightened erythrocyte sedimentation rate (70 mm/hour; reference, <15 mm/hour), while renal function remained within normal limits. The patient exhibited hyperglycaemia (plasma glucose 14.2 mmol/L) alongside an elevated HbA1c of 8.2%, suggestive of inadequate glycaemic control. Tests for hepatitis B and C yielded negative results.

Endocrinological testing showed thyroid-stimulating hormone at 1.11 mIU/L (reference, 0.50–5.00 mIU/L), free T4 at 1.25 ng/dL (reference, 0.90–1.70 ng/dL), and serum cortisol at 42 µg/dL (reference, >6 µg/dL), all within standard parameters. Serum osmolality was decreased (267 mOsm/kg; reference, <270 mOsm/kg), whereas urine osmolality was markedly increased (900 mOsm/kg; reference, >300 mOsm/kg) and urinary sodium concentration was elevated (88 mmol/L; reference, >20 mmol/L), indicating defective water elimination. In the presence of hypo-osmolar hyponatraemia, antidiuretic hormone (ADH) was raised (22.4 pg/mL). ECG findings were unremarkable, and imaging, including chest X-ray as well as head, chest, and abdominal CT scans, showed no abnormalities. Collectively, these results indicated a diagnosis of SIADH.

By the third day, the patient’s headache and vertigo intensified. Despite lacking neck stiffness or signs of cranial nerve dysfunction, central nervous system (CNS) inflammation was considered. Brain MRI showed no notable abnormalities in the hypothalamus or other regions (figure 2). Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis demonstrated mildly raised opening pressure (17 cmH₂O), increased protein concentration (0.97 g/L), and slight lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL). CSF cultures and assays for bacterial and mycobacterial pathogens, along with herpes simplex virus (HSV), varicella zoster virus (VZV), and cryptococcal antigen, yielded negative results. CSF cytology found no evidence of malignant cells.

Additional tests were undertaken to assess for systemic diseases. Evaluation for sarcoidosis revealed lysozyme at 5.1 µg/mL (reference range: 5–10) and angiotensin-converting enzyme at 11.4 U/L (reference: 7–25), both within normal limits. Screening for hematological malignancies showed an elevated soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) at 704 U/mL (reference: 122–496 U/mL), while testing for human T-cell leukemia virus type 1 antibody was negative.

On day 5, the patient developed a painful scrotal ulcer (figure 1D). Taken together with oral aphthae and folliculitis-like skin eruptions, these signs supported the diagnosis of a systemic form of Behçet’s disease (BD). Pinprick hypersensitivity was absent. Ophthalmologic evaluation found no evidence of uveitis. Testing for HLA-B51 returned positive.

Differential diagnosis:

Upon presentation, the constellation of fever, headache, general malaise, and hyponatraemia raised suspicion for a central nervous system infection or a systemic inflammatory condition. Viral meningoencephalitis, notably due to HSV or VZV, was initially considered. Nonetheless, cerebrospinal fluid analysis returned negative for viral antigens and antibodies, effectively ruling out these etiologies. Bacterial meningitis was another diagnostic possibility, but this was discounted in view of the lack of neutrophilic dominance, only mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, and negative CSF cultures. Tuberculous meningitis was also excluded on account of negative acid-fast bacillus staining and absence of tuberculosis DNA in the CSF.

A primary CNS neoplasm was evaluated as a cause due to the neurologic manifestations, yet MRI imaging of the brain did not demonstrate any abnormalities, and cytological examination of the CSF did not identify malignant cells. Although the serum sIL-2R level was modestly increased, this was not sufficient for the diagnosis of CNS lymphoma.

Consideration was also given to autoimmune conditions, but these were dismissed because of the lack of systemic signs typically seen in such diseases and negative autoantibody screenings. Neurosarcoidosis remained among the differential possibilities, as MRI can be unrevealing in early stages; however, further laboratory findings did not support this diagnosis, and the slight sIL-2R elevation was interpreted as nonspecific.

The patient ultimately met criteria for definite Behçet’s disease (BD) according to his symptomatology, which included fever, fatigue, oral aphthae (two points), genital aphthosis (two points), and a folliculitis-like eruption (one point), fulfilling a total of five points per the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (with ≥4 required for definite diagnosis). Notably, genital aphthosis is a particularly specific manifestation of BD, supporting the diagnosis. With infectious, neoplastic, and autoimmune origins ruled out, acute neuro-Behçet’s disease was diagnosed, in light of the CNS findings alongside the mucocutaneous symptoms.

Treatment:

On the first day following admission, clinicians suspected an underlying inflammatory disorder, although its precise cause could not be determined. Empirical cefepime therapy was begun after obtaining blood cultures, aiming to treat a possible bacterial infection even in the absence of a definitive infectious source.

By the third day, the patient experienced increasing headache and vertigo, indicating possible central nervous system inflammation or infection. Empirical acyclovir therapy was administered but subsequently withdrawn after diagnostic tests excluded HSV and VZV encephalitis.

On day eight, colchicine therapy was initiated subsequent to the diagnosis of systemic Behçet’s disease (BD) and acute neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD). Aphthous ulcers in the oral cavity were managed with topical corticosteroid ointment, while genital ulcers and skin lesions were treated with silver sulfadiazine cream. SIADH was managed conservatively, primarily through restricting fluid intake to less than 800 mL/day, closely monitoring serum sodium concentrations daily, and preventing rapid sodium correction. Neither hypertonic saline nor vasopressin receptor antagonists were necessary.

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient's fever subsided and his appetite returned the day following initiation of colchicine therapy. Over the subsequent two weeks, his clinical manifestations—including vertigo, generalized malaise, hiccups, and mucocutaneous lesions—progressively improved. By day 16, his serum sodium had reached 136 mmol/L, and ADH concentrations fell to 1.8 pg/mL. Levels of myogenic enzymes normalized as well.

Notably, no systemic corticosteroids or immunosuppressive agents were administered during his treatment. The patient was discharged after a one-month hospital stay, during which he recovered from the acute episode, hyponatraemia was closely monitored and managed, and he received rehabilitation for diminished physical function. Colchicine therapy was continued for about four years, with no recurrence of Behçet’s disease (BD) or neurological symptoms during this period. After stabilization of BD, biguanide treatment was introduced, leading to effective glycaemic control thereafter.

Discussion:

This report describes a rare situation in which both systemic BD and acute neuro-BD were diagnosed concurrently. It also illustrates that acute neuro-BD may present with concurrent SIADH. The improvement of both mucocutaneous and neurological findings, as well as correction of hyponatraemia after colchicine alone, points to a possible direct link between BD, neuro-BD, and SIADH. Clinicians should suspect acute neuro-BD as a potential etiology when individuals with BD develop fever or headache together with hyponatraemia.

This case stands out due to the concurrent identification of systemic BD and acute neuro-BD, evidenced by the presence of mucocutaneous lesions alongside central nervous system signs. Typically, major organ involvement such as CNS disease develops around four years after BD is first recognized, and neurological symptoms at presentation are unusual, occurring in less than 5% of cases. Headaches attributable to neuro-BD are seen in only about 10% of patients. As such, recognizing neuro-BD at the time of initial evaluation in this individual was particularly challenging. Acute neuro-BD most frequently appears as a self-limited meningoencephalitis, sometimes accompanied by focal neurological deficits. In this case, brain MRI did not reveal abnormal findings at usual BD-associated regions, including the hypothalamus. Acute neuro-BD can manifest as aseptic meningitis without detectable brain parenchymal changes on imaging, and normal MRI results—especially early in the disease—do not exclude its diagnosis. The absence of characteristic radiologic findings further complicated making the diagnosis. Although HLA-B51 analysis was completed, it did not assist in confirming the diagnosis, which is consistent with international BD criteria that do not require HLA-B51 and focus primarily on clinical presentation. This case underscores the need to consider neuro-BD when patients with BD and mucocutaneous features develop vague neurological complaints.

At the outset of our differential diagnosis, we emphasized infectious etiologies for CNS inflammation due to the patient's fever of unknown origin, neurologic manifestations, and hyponatremia; despite this, no definitive pathogen was detected. We further evaluated alternative sources of CNS inflammation, such as connective tissue disorders and neoplastic processes, considering sarcoidosis and hematologic malignancy within the differential. Consequently, we initiated a broad diagnostic approach, assessing autoimmune serological markers along with serum ACE and sIL-2R as preliminary screening tools. These assessments were employed selectively in an exclusionary context rather than in response to prominent clinical suspicion. Ultimately, there were no clinical, imaging, or CSF findings indicative of these conditions, and these tests did not significantly aid in reaching a diagnosis. Serum ACE demonstrates only moderate sensitivity for sarcoidosis, and while sIL-2R can assist in the evaluation for malignant lymphoma, minor elevations lack specificity and may simply indicate inflammatory states. In this scenario, the initial absence of genital ulcers complicated the consideration of BD, necessitating an extensive differential workup. This diagnostic journey underscores the necessity of avoiding excessive, non-targeted investigations, reinforcing that such tests should be reserved for situations supported by clinical indicators and interpreted judiciously.

While medication-induced SIADH has previously been documented in individuals with BD treated with agents such as cyclophosphamide, the relationship between acute neuro-BD and SIADH remains largely unclear. Neuro-BD is defined by inflammatory processes within the CNS, which may play a role in altering ADH regulation. Multiple investigations have indicated that interleukin-6 (IL-6), a proinflammatory cytokine commonly increased in neuroinflammatory conditions, correlates with elevated ADH secretion. In Behçet’s Disease, inappropriate activation of monocytes results in spontaneous IL-6 synthesis, and raised IL-6 levels have been identified in the CSF of patients experiencing acute neuro-BD. These observations point to a possible involvement of IL-6–driven inflammation in the development of SIADH in such cases. Although IL-6 concentrations were not evaluated in either the serum or CSF of the current patient, a notably high serum CRP may be indicative of an underlying inflammatory process mediated by IL-6. SIADH is linked to numerous factors, including CNS inflammation, pulmonary pathology, cancer, and specific pharmaceuticals. Based on the current data, it is not possible to confirm a direct cause-and-effect relationship between SIADH and acute neuro-BD. The timing of the SIADH onset in this patient may simply represent a general reaction to CNS inflammation rather than a mechanism unique to neuro-BD.

This case demonstrates the possible complications of hyponatraemia induced by SIADH in the context of acute neuro-BD. Prior publications have documented that ongoing hiccups may represent a significant clinical indicator of severe hyponatraemia resulting from SIADH associated with CNS inflammation, such as meningitis. Additionally, during our initial assessment of fever, arthralgia, and general fatigue—which may be due to either inflammatory or metabolic muscle injury—we included CK measurement, which identified rhabdomyolysis. This is a recognized but frequently neglected complication of severe hyponatraemia that often escapes routine evaluation. These presentations are well-documented but commonly underestimated clinical outcomes of marked hyponatraemia. It is important for clinicians to recognize that seemingly unrelated manifestations, like hiccups or rhabdomyolysis, may point to underlying electrolyte imbalances, especially when neuroinflammatory diseases are present.

Corticosteroids remain the cornerstone of therapy for acute neuro-BD, with most patients showing a positive response. Nevertheless, there have been instances where symptoms resembling meningoencephalitis have resolved without intervention. In this report, treatment with colchicine alone resulted in improvements in both mucocutaneous and neurological manifestations, as well as in hyponatraemia. Colchicine mediates its anti-inflammatory action by blocking neutrophil migration and activation, and by reducing cytokine production, including IL-1β and IL-6. While colchicine is commonly employed to address mucocutaneous symptoms in BD, systematic reviews indicate that it does not provide benefit in acute neuro-BD, and that immunosuppressive agents are typically necessary to control the disease effectively. In previously described cases, colchicine has generally been administered alongside corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants rather than on its own. Although the possibility of spontaneous improvement cannot be ruled out in this patient, the current observations imply that colchicine may represent a viable therapeutic option for acute neuro-BD and associated issues such as SIADH, especially when corticosteroids are unsuitable or contraindicated.

Conclusion:

We reported an uncommon presentation of Behçet’s disease (BD) and acute neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) diagnosed simultaneously, in which syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) emerged as a complication of neuro-BD. This case underscores that SIADH can arise secondary to acute neuro-BD, emphasizing the necessity for healthcare providers to recognize this possible link. Treatment with colchicine alone resulted in prompt resolution of both mucocutaneous and neurological symptoms, as well as normalization of hyponatraemia, implying that colchicine could serve as an effective initial therapy for neuro-BD. For patients with BD who display vague central nervous system complaints such as fever and headache accompanied by hyponatraemia, clinicians should remain alert to the potential for acute neuro-BD when SIADH is present.
