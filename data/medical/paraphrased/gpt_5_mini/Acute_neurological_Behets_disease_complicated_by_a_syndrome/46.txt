Title: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

Eiichi Kakehi, Yukako Matsumoto, Shigehisa Sakurai, Kazuhiko Kotani

Abstract:

Summary: We present an uncommon instance of acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) identified simultaneously with systemic Behçet’s disease (BD), categorized as definite BD, and accompanied by syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). A male patient in his 60s came to medical attention with fever, headache, and mucocutaneous signs, including oral aphthous ulcers, folliculitis-like eruptions, genital ulcers, and hyponatraemia. The diagnosis of BD was established based on these mucocutaneous symptoms. While brain MRI showed no pathologic findings, analysis of cerebrospinal fluid indicated aseptic meningitis, consistent with acute neuro-BD. Laboratory evaluation verified the presence of SIADH. Treatment with colchicine alone resulted in the improvement of mucocutaneous manifestations, neurological symptoms, and hyponatraemia, without the need for corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive therapy. This case points to a potential link between acute neuro-BD and SIADH and highlights the need for clinicians to be aware of coexistent neurological and endocrine features in patients with newly diagnosed BD.

Background:

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a persistent, multisystem inflammatory condition marked by repeated oral and genital ulcerations, uveitis, and various cutaneous manifestations. Neurological complications, identified as neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), affect about 10%–20% of individuals with BD and generally arise several years after the disease is first diagnosed. Acute neuro-BD most often presents with meningoencephalitis and parenchymal lesions of the central nervous system, frequently localized to the brainstem. While neuro-BD usually develops after the onset of other BD symptoms, the simultaneous diagnosis of BD and neuro-BD is unusual. When both occur together, a thorough evaluation should encompass both mucocutaneous findings and neurological signs, including headache, alterations in behavior, psychiatric manifestations, and focal neurological deficits.

The syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) is a recognized etiology of hyponatraemia and commonly arises secondary to disorders such as central nervous system (CNS) inflammation, neoplasia, or the administration of specific medications. In light of the inflammatory features of acute neuro-BD, those affected may be predisposed to SIADH. There are reports of SIADH developing in BD patients treated with agents like cyclophosphamide; however, SIADH resulting exclusively from CNS inflammation due to acute neuro-BD, without pharmacologic contributors, remains insufficiently documented.

We describe an uncommon presentation in which BD and acute neuro-BD were identified at the same time, complicated by the onset of SIADH. This case underscores the need to recognize SIADH as a possible complication of acute neuro-BD, especially in patients exhibiting nonspecific neurological symptoms along with hyponatraemia.

Case presentation:

A man of Asian descent in his 60s arrived at the emergency department experiencing three days of fever, headache, profound fatigue, decreased appetite, continuous hiccups, and joint pain involving the shoulders and ankles. He additionally noted the presence of oral aphthous ulcers.

His past medical history was significant for type 2 diabetes mellitus; however, he was not receiving any medical therapy due to minimal awareness of his diagnosis and lack of adherence to recommended dietary or exercise regimens. There was no notable family history, including Behçet’s disease among his relatives. He drank alcohol on occasion, was a non-smoker, and denied recent travel or contact with animals or ill individuals. His occupation was in the metalworking sector, and he reported no recent sexual activity.

During evaluation, the patient was awake but exhibited signs of fatigue. Recorded vital signs included a temperature of 38.1°C, blood pressure measured at 135/73 mm Hg, a regular heart rate of 119 beats per minute, a respiratory rate of 19 per minute, and oxygen saturation at 96% while breathing ambient air. Examination showed the presence of oral aphthae and folliculitis-like skin lesions on his face and trunk (see figure 1A, B, and C). Both the cardiopulmonary and abdominal assessments were within normal limits. Manual muscle testing demonstrated that strength in both the upper and lower limbs was intact. No peripheral edema was found, and clinical evaluation indicated that his volume status was euvolemic. He was hospitalized for further diagnostic workup.

Investigations:

Initial laboratory assessment demonstrated significant hyponatraemia (124 mmol/L), increased creatine kinase (1349 U/L), aspartate aminotransferase (77 U/L), alanine aminotransferase (64 U/L), C-reactive protein (CRP) at 180 mg/L (reference: <3 mg/L), and elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate (70 mm/hour; reference: <15 mm/hour), with preservation of renal function. Additionally, the patient exhibited hyperglycaemia (plasma glucose of 14.2 mmol/L) and a raised HbA1c of 8.2%, reflecting suboptimal glycaemic regulation. Testing for hepatitis B and C returned negative.

Hormonal analysis showed thyroid-stimulating hormone within the reference range (1.11 mIU/L; reference: 0.50–5.00 mIU/L), normal free T4 (1.25 ng/dL; reference: 0.90–1.70 ng/dL), and serum cortisol (42 µg/dL; reference: >6 µg/dL). The serum osmolality was decreased (267 mOsm/kg; reference: <270 mOsm/kg), while urine osmolality was elevated (900 mOsm/kg, reference: >300 mOsm/kg), and urinary sodium was increased (88 mmol/L; reference: >20 mmol/L), suggesting deficient renal water excretion. In the context of hypo-osmolar hyponatraemia, antidiuretic hormone (ADH) levels were found to be raised (22.4 pg/mL). Electrocardiogram findings were normal, and imaging via chest X-ray and computed tomography of the head, chest, and abdomen revealed no abnormalities. The overall clinical picture was consistent with SIADH.

On the third day, his headache and vertigo became more severe. Despite the absence of neck rigidity and cranial nerve deficits, central nervous system inflammation was considered. MRI of the brain demonstrated no notable pathology involving the hypothalamus or other regions (see figure 2). Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) examination revealed a mildly elevated opening pressure (17 cmH₂O), increased protein concentration (0.97 g/L), and slight lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL). Tests including CSF culture, assays for bacterial and mycobacterial organisms, as well as herpes simplex virus (HSV), varicella zoster virus (VZV), and cryptococcal antigen, returned negative results. Cytological evaluation of CSF did not detect any malignant cells.

Additional assessments were conducted to rule out systemic illnesses. Sarcoidosis workup indicated normal lysozyme (5.1 µg/mL; reference range 5–10) and angiotensin-converting enzyme (11.4 U/L; reference range 7–25) levels. Hematologic malignancy screening found elevated soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) at 704 U/mL (reference range 122–496 U/mL), with a negative human T-cell leukemia virus type 1 antibody.

By day 5, he developed a painful ulcer on the scrotum (figure 1D). This, together with oral aphthae and rashes resembling folliculitis, pointed toward a systemic presentation of BD. There was no evidence of pinprick hypersensitivity. Eye examination excluded uveitis. HLA-B51 testing was positive.

Differential diagnosis:

Upon admission, the constellation of fever, headache, malaise, and low serum sodium raised concern for either a central nervous system infection or a systemic inflammatory process. Viral meningoencephalitis, especially from HSV or VZV, was the initial suspicion. Yet, cerebrospinal fluid analysis did not detect viral antigens or antibodies, which effectively ruled out these etiologies. Consideration was also given to bacterial meningitis, but was excluded due to lack of neutrophil predominance, a slight lymphocytic pleocytosis, and negative cultures from CSF samples. Tuberculous meningitis was discounted following negative acid-fast staining and negative PCR for tuberculosis DNA.

A primary neoplasm of the CNS was included in the differential given the neurologic findings, but brain MRI was unremarkable and CSF cytology did not identify malignant cells. Although soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) levels were mildly raised, this isolated abnormality was not sufficient to make the diagnosis of CNS lymphoma.

Autoimmune pathologies were also explored but excluded owing to both unremarkable screening for autoantibodies and lack of systemic clinical manifestations typical for such diseases. Neurosarcoidosis was contemplated, as imaging may be normal during early disease; nevertheless, further laboratory evaluations did not support this possibility. The minor increase in sIL-2R was interpreted as non-specific.

The patient ultimately met criteria for definite Behçet’s disease (BD) based on clinical manifestations that included fever, fatigue, recurrent oral aphthae (two points), genital ulceration (two points), and a rash resembling folliculitis (one point). According to the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease, he had a cumulative score of five points (≥4 points signifies a definite diagnosis). Notably, the presence of genital ulcerations is highly specific for BD, further supporting this diagnosis. With infectious, neoplastic, and other autoimmune etiologies ruled out, a final diagnosis of acute neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) was made based on central nervous system involvement coexisting with mucocutaneous symptoms.

Treatment:

On the first day of hospitalization, clinicians suspected a possible underlying inflammatory disorder, but the precise cause was still undetermined. After obtaining blood cultures, empirical treatment with cefepime was commenced to address a potential bacterial infection, even though no clear infectious source had been identified.

By day 3, the patient experienced increased headache and vertigo, raising concerns for central nervous system infection or inflammation. Acyclovir was started empirically; however, it was stopped when investigations excluded HSV and VZV encephalitis.

On day 8, colchicine therapy was initiated upon the diagnosis of systemic Behçet’s disease and acute neuro-Behçet’s disease. Oral aphthae received topical corticosteroid ointment, while genital ulcers and skin lesions were managed with silver sulfadiazine cream. SIADH was addressed with conservative measures: restricting fluid intake to less than 800 mL per day, meticulous daily serum sodium monitoring, and avoiding rapid sodium correction. There was no need to use hypertonic saline or vasopressin receptor antagonists.

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient experienced resolution of fever and improved appetite within one day of initiating colchicine therapy. Over the subsequent two weeks, his symptoms—including vertigo, malaise, hiccups, and mucocutaneous lesions—showed gradual improvement. By the sixteenth day, serum sodium levels had returned to 136 mmol/L, and ADH concentration had decreased to 1.8 pg/mL. Levels of myogenic enzymes also normalized.

Of note, systemic corticosteroids and other immunosuppressive agents were not administered during the course of treatment. Following a one-month hospitalization—in which acute recovery was attained, hyponatraemia was meticulously monitored and treated, and physical rehabilitation addressed the resulting functional impairment—the patient was discharged. Colchicine treatment was continued for roughly four years, during which neither BD nor neurological symptoms recurred. After stabilization of BD, biguanide therapy was started, and optimal glycaemic control has been sustained since.

Discussion:

In this report, systemic BD and acute neuro-BD were identified at the same time, an occurrence that is uncommon. The case further illustrated that acute neuro-BD may be accompanied by SIADH. The improvement of mucocutaneous manifestations, neurological symptoms, and hyponatraemia after initiating colchicine alone suggests a potential direct relationship between BD, neuro-BD, and SIADH. Clinicians should be alert to the possibility of acute neuro-BD as an underlying etiology in patients with BD who develop fever or headache along with hyponatraemia.

A distinguishing aspect of this case is the concurrent diagnosis of systemic BD and acute neuro-BD, as indicated by mucocutaneous involvement and central nervous system symptoms. Usually, involvement of major organs like the CNS develops a median of four years after BD is first recognized, while neurological symptoms at presentation are infrequent, affecting less than 5% of patients. Moreover, only a small subset—about 10%—experience headaches that can be directly attributed to neuro-BD. Thus, recognizing neuro-BD at onset posed a significant diagnostic challenge. The most frequent presentation of acute neuro-BD is as a meningoencephalitis that is self-limited, sometimes featuring focal neurological impairments. In this case, MRI of the brain did not reveal any definitive lesions in areas typically affected in BD, such as the hypothalamus. Early-stage acute neuro-BD can manifest as aseptic meningitis without parenchymal changes on imaging, and an unremarkable MRI should not preclude the diagnosis, adding another layer of difficulty. Although testing for HLA-B51 was undertaken, it was not helpful for diagnosis, which is consistent with international BD diagnostic criteria that prioritize clinical manifestations over genetic markers. This experience underscores the need to consider neuro-BD in patients presenting with both mucocutaneous involvement and vague neurological complaints.

At the outset of the differential diagnosis, we focused on infection as the likely etiology for CNS inflammation due to the presence of fever without a clear source, neurological manifestations, and hyponatremia; nevertheless, no causative pathogen was identified. Alternative etiologies for CNS inflammation, such as connective tissue disorders and neoplasms—including sarcoidosis and hematologic malignancies—were also taken into account. Accordingly, a comprehensive initial assessment was conducted, which included testing for autoimmune serologies, as well as measuring serum ACE and sIL-2R levels as part of screening. These assessments were used selectively within an exclusionary framework rather than being prompted by a compelling clinical suspicion. Ultimately, there were no clinical, imaging, or cerebrospinal fluid findings to corroborate these conditions, and the results from these ancillary studies did not play a significant role in establishing the diagnosis. It should be noted that ACE is not highly sensitive for sarcoidosis, and while sIL-2R can assist in assessing malignant lymphoma, modest increases are nonspecific and may simply indicate inflammatory processes. In this patient’s course, the absence of genital ulcers at the onset of neurological symptoms rendered Behçet’s disease (BD) an uncertain consideration and resulted in a wide-ranging differential diagnosis. This case underscores the need to refrain from performing broad, non-targeted investigations and reinforces that such tests should be ordered only when justified by relevant clinical features and interpreted with careful consideration.

While SIADH related to pharmacologic agents, such as cyclophosphamide, has been described in individuals with Behçet’s disease (BD), the relationship between acute neuro-BD and SIADH is not well elucidated. Neuro-BD involves inflammatory processes within the central nervous system (CNS) which may disrupt normal ADH regulation. Multiple reports have indicated that interleukin-6 (IL-6), a proinflammatory cytokine often elevated in neuroinflammatory conditions, correlates with heightened ADH secretion. In BD, dysregulated monocyte activity results in constitutive IL-6 production, and patients experiencing acute neuro-BD have demonstrated elevated IL-6 concentrations in cerebrospinal fluid. These observations raise the possibility that inflammation mediated by IL-6 could play a role in the development of SIADH in this context. Although IL-6 levels were not assessed in serum or cerebrospinal fluid for this particular case, the substantially increased serum CRP may be indicative of an ongoing IL-6–driven inflammatory process. SIADH has been associated with a broad spectrum of disorders, including CNS inflammation, respiratory pathology, malignancy, and certain medications; however, the evidence presented here does not permit a definitive causal relationship to be drawn between SIADH and acute neuro-BD. The temporal overlap seen may simply represent a generalized response to CNS inflammatory activity rather than a process specific to BD.

This case further demonstrates the range of complications associated with hyponatraemia due to SIADH occurring alongside acute neuro-BD. Prior accounts have highlighted persistent hiccups as a valuable indicator of severe hyponatraemia arising from SIADH secondary to central nervous system inflammation, including meningitis. In the current evaluation, creatine kinase (CK) was assessed in the context of fever, joint pain, and generalized fatigue—symptoms which might be linked to inflammatory or metabolic injury of muscle tissue. This assessment disclosed rhabdomyolysis, a known but frequently overlooked consequence of marked hyponatraemia that is not routinely assessed. Such clinical manifestations are well-recognized yet commonly underestimated implications of profound hyponatraemia. It is important for clinicians to recognize that atypical symptoms, such as hiccups or rhabdomyolysis, can point to underlying disturbances in electrolyte balance, especially in patients with neuroinflammatory disorders.

Corticosteroids represent the standard therapy for acute neuro-BD, with most individuals exhibiting a positive response. Nevertheless, there have been accounts of cases with isolated meningoencephalitis-like symptoms resolving without intervention. In this report, colchicine administered as monotherapy led to improvements in both mucocutaneous and neurological manifestations, as well as correction of hyponatraemia. The anti-inflammatory properties of colchicine stem from its ability to impede neutrophil migration and activation, and diminish cytokine production, including IL-1β and IL-6. While colchicine is commonly employed for mucocutaneous involvement in BD, evidence from systematic reviews indicates it is not effective for acute neuro-BD and that immunosuppressants are typically necessary for sufficient disease management. Previously documented cases have involved colchicine as a supplementary agent alongside corticosteroids or other immunosuppressives, rather than as monotherapy. Although spontaneous improvement cannot be discounted in this instance, these findings imply that colchicine could serve as a viable alternative for treating acute neuro-BD and associated complications such as SIADH, especially in situations where corticosteroid use is unwarranted or contraindicated.

Conclusion:

We reported an uncommon instance of Behçet’s disease (BD) with concurrent onset of acute neuro-BD, further complicated by SIADH. This case illustrates that SIADH can develop as a consequence of acute neuro-BD, underscoring the necessity for clinicians to recognize this potential relationship. Treatment with colchicine alone resulted in prompt resolution of mucocutaneous signs, neurological symptoms, and hyponatraemia, indicating colchicine might serve as a viable option for early neuro-BD therapy. For BD patients exhibiting non-specific central nervous system findings such as fever and headache along with hyponatraemia, clinicians should strongly consider the possibility of acute neuro-BD accompanied by SIADH.
