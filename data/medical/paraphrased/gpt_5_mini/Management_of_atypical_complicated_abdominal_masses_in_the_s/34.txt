Title: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

Rosalba De Nola, Emma Bassi, Cataldo Paletta, Mario Vicino

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her 40s, who had not previously given birth, reported abdominal pain and shortness of breath in her second trimester. Ultrasound identified two sizable abdominal lesions: the superior mass occupied the left subphrenic area and extended toward the uterine fundus, while the inferior lesion was situated within the pouch of Douglas. The patient underwent laparotomy; the pelvic mass was retained within the pelvis. She received routine prenatal care and had a planned caesarean section, resulting in both maternal and neonatal outcomes being excellent. For pregnant patients with large, symptomatic masses, thorough assessment—including bimanual examination and imaging with modalities like ultrasound and non-contrast MRI—is critical. Surgical intervention should only be pursued when malignancy or clinical complications are suspected, prioritizing pregnancy preservation and avoiding unnecessary procedures. The choice of delivery method must be guided by the size, quantity, and anatomical distribution of uterine masses.

Background:

Managing atypical or symptomatic abdominal masses in pregnancy is fraught with considerable clinical difficulties. Diagnostic modalities are largely restricted to physical assessment, ultrasonography (US) with colour Doppler, and MRI without contrast. Preoperative US is especially useful for detecting symptomatic uterine leiomyomas situated on the anterior uterine wall in pregnant patients, including both submucosal and pedunculated types. In cases involving multiple, sizeable, intramural, or posterior leiomyomas, MRI surpasses US in diagnostic precision, allowing for exact measurement of the distance separating the myoma from the uterine cavity.

The utility of serum tumour markers during pregnancy remains controversial. Specifically, CA-125 levels naturally increase during the first trimester before returning to baseline, making a single measurement insufficient for diagnosis; however, serial testing can reveal important trends that aid in differential diagnosis and ongoing monitoring. Thorough multidisciplinary risk analysis is essential for optimal management, taking into account obstetric risks alongside the infrequency and complexity of these presentations.

Case presentation:

A woman in her forties, gravida 1, para 0, abortus 1, and with obesity (body mass index of 33), presented in the second trimester of pregnancy at 21 weeks’ gestation, complaining of acute, severe abdominal pain localized to both the upper left quadrant and the pelvis (Visual Analogue Scale=5 at both locations), accompanied by mild shortness of breath. Routine screening tests during the first and second trimester indicated low risk for aneuploidies, which was corroborated by non-invasive prenatal testing, and fetal morphology appeared normal.

She was initially seen in the emergency department, where a comprehensive multidisciplinary assessment was performed. Her oxygen saturation was within normal limits (median 97%), thoracic examination demonstrated normal heart rate (median 74 beats per minute) and blood pressure (median 100/60 mm Hg). Electrocardiography revealed no abnormalities. Subsequently, she was moved to the Obstetrics and Gynecology unit. Her abdomen appeared more distended than would be expected for her gestational age, featuring a tender swelling with a tense, elastic mass measuring 18 cm and occupying the entire upper left quadrant, but without signs suggestive of ascites.

On bimanual vaginal examination, a fixed, rounded, and firm mass measuring approximately 9 cm was palpated in the lesser pelvis, partially protruding into the posterior fornix.

For symptom relief, she was treated with intravenous paracetamol (1000 mg twice daily) and placed in a semi-orthostatic position, a strategy aimed at potentially reducing diaphragmatic compression from the enlarged uterus and abdominal masses. This regimen provided modest improvement, lowering her pain score from 5 to 1 on the Visual Analogue Scale, without the need for supplemental oxygen or ventilatory support.

During her admission, she developed a mild fever. SARS-CoV-2 PCR testing via nasopharyngeal swab yielded a negative result, while urine culture identified infection with Klebsiella pneumoniae. Laboratory evaluation indicated iron deficiency anemia, with haemoglobin at 9.9 g/L (reference range 12.0–16.6 g/L) and serum ferritin at 25 mcg/L (reference range 15–300 mcg/L); there was also a mild increase in C-reactive protein to 1.39 mg/dL (reference range 0–0.3 mg/dL), and elevated neutrophil count (total white blood cells 11.29×10^3 /uL [reference 4–10×10^3 /uL] with neutrophils at 9.27×10^3 /uL [reference 2.8–5.5×10^3 /uL]), in keeping with physiological changes related to pregnancy.

Investigations:

Ultrasonography revealed two sizable, unilocular cystic lesions with smooth borders and peripheral blood flow. The superior cyst measured 18×12×7 cm and stretched from the left subphrenic area to the fundus of the uterus. The inferior cyst measured 9.5×8×8 cm and appeared attached to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma exhibiting cystic changes (58×71 mm).

Serum levels of tumour markers including CA 19–9, CA 15–3, CEA, β2-microglobulin, and LDH were all within reference ranges, while CA-125 was found to be elevated at 41.8 U/mL (normal <35 U/mL), with a rise noted from 36 U/mL measured two weeks previously. Thus, there was a mild upward trend in this marker’s serum level. The patient subsequently underwent a non-contrast MRI, which revealed thick walls (1.4 cm), inhomogeneous fluid signal on T1 and T2 fat-saturated sequences, and regions of hyperintense T1-T2 weighted solid tissue (refer to figure 1; only T2 fat-sat images provided). The boundary of the lower cyst was indistinct, appearing continuous with the posterior leiomyoma previously observed via ultrasonography. No lymphadenopathy was detected on MRI.

Differential diagnosis:

On ultrasound examination, the cystic formations closely resembled two large decidualised endometriomas; additionally, the lower cyst appeared to be attached to an intramural-subserosal leiomyoma located on the posterior uterine wall, measuring 58×71 mm.

Magnetic resonance imaging revealed two rounded, unilocular adnexal cysts featuring hypertrophied vessels and heterogeneous fluid content (refer to figure 1). The inferior mass appeared to be affixed to a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma, which exhibited cystic degeneration and measured 58×71 mm.

Owing to the considerable size of the gravid uterus and the large masses, a connecting stalk or nourishing vessel could not be visualized for the upper mass. This steered the diagnostic consideration toward ovarian lesions—predominantly endometriomas—especially in light of mildly elevated CA-125 values noted in the second trimester (see discussion).

Evaluation of both sonographic and MRI characteristics led to differential diagnoses encompassing decidualised endometriomas and leiomyomas undergoing cystic degeneration. The slight rise in CA-125 supported endometrioma as the diagnosis for the upper cyst, whereas its anatomical continuity with the uterus implied a leiomyomatous etiology for the lower mass. The patient’s dyspnoea was thought to stem from mechanical compression caused by the expansive abdominal masses, with no signs of pulmonary or cardiac compromise observed.

Treatment:

Given the progression of her symptoms—namely, increasing abdominal pain attributable to the masses and dyspnea due to compressive effects on the lungs—as well as the need for definitive diagnosis, risk of complications such as rupture, torsion, and infection, and anticipated uterine expansion with advancing gestation, the patient consented to an exploratory laparotomy at 21 weeks and 6 days gestation utilizing a longitudinal xiphoid-to-pubic incision.

Initially, two peritoneal fluid samples were obtained for microbiological and cytological evaluation. The abdominal cavity was then systematically examined, including confirmation of ongoing fetal cardiac activity.

Unexpectedly, the mass located cranially was found to be an unusually large, cystic, and taut-elastic uterine leiomyoma with pedunculated attachment and pronounced vascularity, displaying signs of twisting and leakage—features likely responsible for the inpatient fever (refer to figure 2). Consequently, the surgical team opted to carefully remove the mass by sequentially clamping at its point of uterine attachment (figure 2). Following transection of the stalk, the exposed base was meticulously sutured with a single absorbable stitch. The caudal mass, originating from the uterine torus, was left untouched to reduce obstetric complications. This lesion appeared immobile with a smooth exterior and soft consistency, resembling a sizeable intramural-subserosal cystic leiomyoma.

The procedure took 2 hours and 25 minutes. Of note, prophylactic progesterone (100 mg, intramuscular) was administered beginning intraoperatively and continued daily at the same dosage until discharge. Post-discharge, hydroxyprogesterone caproate (341 mg, 1 ampoule intramuscularly every 3 days, per manufacturer instructions) was used up to the planned delivery. Perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis involved intravenous cefazolin 2 g, followed by postoperative antibiotics: cefazolin 1 g intravenously three times daily plus clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily during hospitalization, as directed by urine culture sensitivities indicating Klebsiella pneumoniae highly susceptible to both cephalosporins and clarithromycin.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following surgery, the patient's anaemia worsened (Hb 7.5 g/L, HCT 24.3%) and iron deficiency persisted, necessitating intravenous administration of ferric carboxymaltose (1 g) along with two successful blood transfusions, which resulted in improved hemoglobin (Hb 10.0 g/L, HCT 30.4%). She was discharged without any complications 10 days postoperatively and commenced close obstetric follow-up every three weeks. This surveillance documented fetal growth at the 86th percentile, according to the WHO fetal growth chart calculator (https://srhr.org/fetalgrowthcalculator/#/), as well as regular assessments of the amniotic fluid index.

Peritoneal fluid analysis yielded negative results on both cultural and cytological examination. Pathology confirmed benign uterine leiomyomas with ischaemic necrosis in proximity to the twisted pedicle.

The presence of the caudal myoma was anticipated to significantly impede fetal head descent during the second stage of labor. Therefore, an elective caesarean section was scheduled using the existing surgical incision upon the fetus reaching 37 weeks' gestation. This approach was chosen to lower the risk of uterine scar rupture, and resulted in a favorable neonatal outcome.

At present, the patient remains free of symptoms associated with the myoma, and the remaining pelvic myoma has slightly decreased in size to 7.5 cm. She is successfully breastfeeding, and prefers to delay any further surgical intervention due to partial postpartum regression of the leiomyomas, the absence of related symptoms, and fulfillment of her maternal goals. The infant continues to thrive.

Discussion:

Myomas can often be misdiagnosed as adnexal masses: the incidence during pregnancy is 0.3–10.1% and 1–6%, respectively .13

In this case, initial assessment identified abdominal masses that resembled two large decidualised endometriomas, aside from the lower solid portion of the pelvic mass, which appeared to be a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma with cystic degeneration measuring 58×71 mm, as observed on both ultrasound and MRI scans. Given the lack of clear recommendations for managing pelvic masses during pregnancy, the multidisciplinary team judged that elective surgery might lower the likelihood of preterm birth.

Typically in pregnancy, serum CA-125 concentrations exhibit two peaks—one during the first trimester (median 23 U/mL, p<0.00001) and a second during the third trimester (median 21 U/mL, p<0.001), both higher than levels seen in the second trimester (median 14.0 U/mL). However, this particular case showed a measurement of 41.8 U/mL, exceeding the median for the second trimester.

CA-125 primarily serves as a tumor marker for ovarian cancer (current standard threshold ≥35 U/mL), especially in women older than 50 years, in whom it demonstrates improved area under the curve, positive predictive value, sensitivity, and specificity. Nevertheless, elevated CA-125 may also be associated with various benign disorders, such as endometriosis and adenomyosis (>19 U/mL), pelvic inflammatory disease, menstruation, early and late pregnancy (median values 23 and 21 U/mL), risk of miscarriage, and myoma (threshold <19 U/mL).57

The principal factors guiding the clinical approach included the identification of large, vascular masses displaying both solid and cystic regions, which carried a real risk for complications such as rupture, torsion, or infection. The patient's worsening pain and dyspnoea, the likelihood of mass enlargement with increased risk for hypoxia, necrosis, and degeneration, minimally elevated tumor markers (CA-125=41.8 U/mL), and limited intra-abdominal space to accommodate ongoing uterine growth were also significant considerations.

Owing to the potential neoplastic nature and indeterminate etiology of the lesions, the patient underwent a non-contrast MRI following the ultrasound to improve diagnostic clarity. Current guidelines state that gadolinium-based contrast MRI should be avoided in pregnancy except in critical situations involving rheumatological, inflammatory, or infiltrative dermatologic disorders, or significant risk of stillbirth or neonatal demise. Consequently, the multidisciplinary team opted for a non-contrast MRI and ensured magnetic field strength did not exceed 1.5 T, which is generally regarded as safe after the first trimester.

In this case, the multidisciplinary team—including surgeons, gynecologists, and obstetricians—chose to perform a longitudinal laparotomic surgical intervention after securing formal written informed consent from the patient. Intraoperatively, the superior lesion was found to have a pedicle, substantially increasing the risk of an acute, classic complication. This finding provided the basis for restricting the procedure to an upper myomectomy. Additionally, fetal viability was continuously assessed with repeated transvaginal ultrasounds during the operation, complemented by the prophylactic intraoperative use of progesterone.

Zhao et al’s multicenter study emphasizes the necessity of monitoring and documenting fetal heart rate before, during, and after surgery.

Typically, surgical intervention is postponed until after delivery, favoring medical or conservative management. Nonetheless, Sakinci et al’s recent investigation at a tertiary center demonstrated no statistically significant difference between women who had cesarean section (CS) with concurrent myomectomy and those who underwent CS alone without myoma, with respect to preoperative and postoperative hemoglobin levels or blood transfusion frequency (p >0.05). However, length of hospital stay and surgical duration were notably greater in patients who underwent CS myomectomy (p = 0.001 and p = 0.001, respectively). The average myoma measured 8.3 ± 4.1 cm, and there was a statistically significant positive association between myoma size and duration of hospitalization (p = 0.01).

The same investigation identified a statistically significant inverse association between myoma size and gestational age at delivery (p = 0.035).

Uterine leiomyomas exhibit a non-linear pattern of size fluctuation throughout pregnancy and the postpartum period. Specifically, leiomyoma dimensions increase weekly by 6.90%, with a 6.12% (p<0.001) growth noted between the first and second trimesters, and a 3.10%±0.39% increment recorded from the second to the third trimesters (p<0.05). As such, the rate of leiomyoma enlargement is markedly higher during the first trimester compared to later stages of gestation. Conversely, in the third trimester and at six weeks postpartum, a substantial reduction in myoma size is observed, with a mean decrease of 44.25%±36.05% (p<0.05).

These studies emphasize the critical role of meticulous ultrasound assessment of the dimensions, anatomical position, quantity, and proximity of uterine leiomyomas to the placenta prior to surgical management.

When uterine leiomyomas are diagnosed in pregnancy, conservative management is generally favored over surgery, owing to risks such as significant hemorrhage, fetal loss, preterm delivery, and uterine rupture. Nevertheless, myomectomy may be warranted in cases involving complicated lesions, such as torsion of a pedunculated leiomyoma, or in instances of ongoing symptoms unresponsive to conservative therapies. The surgical intervention should be as brief as possible to safeguard pregnancy, and may be accomplished via laparoscopy—ideally using the open Hasson technique—particularly for earlier gestations (first and early second trimester), smaller lesion sizes, and when myomas are located outside the myometrial wall. This method is associated with improved outcomes related to maternal morbidity and the possibility of vaginal birth. Notwithstanding, the current case illustrates a frequent selection bias favoring laparotomy over laparoscopy when faced with larger, multiple, intramural, or critically positioned myomas, situations which may be linked to a greater incidence of cesarean section due to a large leiomyoma adhered to the posterior isthmic uterine wall, posing an impediment to vaginal delivery.
