Title: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

Rosalba De Nola, Emma Bassi, Cataldo Paletta, Mario Vicino

Abstract:

A nulliparous woman in her 40s presented during her second trimester with abdominal pain and shortness of breath. Ultrasound imaging identified two sizable intra-abdominal masses: the upper mass filled the entire left subphrenic area and extended to the uterine fundus, while the lower mass was situated within the pouch of Douglas. She underwent a laparotomy, with the pelvic (caudal) mass left undisturbed. The patient received routine obstetric care and proceeded to an elective caesarean section, yielding favorable maternal and neonatal outcomes. For pregnant patients with large, symptomatic masses, thorough assessment utilizing bimanual examination and appropriate imaging techniques, such as ultrasonography and non-contrast magnetic resonance imaging, is crucial. Surgical intervention should only be pursued when malignancy is suspected or there are clinical complications, with the priority being preservation of pregnancy and avoidance of unnecessary procedures. The choice of delivery method should be guided by the size, quantity, and location of uterine masses.

Background:

Diagnosing and treating atypical or symptomatic abdominal masses in pregnant patients involves considerable clinical difficulty. The available diagnostic methods are restricted to physical examination, ultrasound (US) with color Doppler, and MRI without contrast. Ultrasound performed preoperatively is particularly effective for detecting symptomatic uterine leiomyomas situated on the anterior aspect of the uterus, including submucosal and pedunculated types. In contrast, MRI delivers enhanced diagnostic precision for multiple, large, intramural, or posterior leiomyomas, as it allows for exact localization and measurement of the distance between the myoma and the uterine cavity.

The value of serum tumor markers during pregnancy continues to be controversial. For example, CA-125 levels naturally increase during early gestation and return to baseline thereafter, making single readings unreliable for diagnosis. Repeated measurements over time, however, can provide meaningful patterns useful for both differential diagnosis and ongoing monitoring. Effective management necessitates coordinated, multidisciplinary evaluation of risks, weighing obstetric complications against the uncommon and complex nature of these cases.

Case presentation:

A woman in her forties, with obesity (BMI 33) and a gynecological history of G1P0A1, presented in her second trimester (gestational age 21 weeks) reporting intense, sudden abdominal pain in both the upper left quadrant and pelvic region (Visual Analogue Scale rated 5 at each site) alongside mild shortness of breath. Routine screening in both the first and second trimester had indicated low risk for fetal aneuploidies (as confirmed by non-invasive prenatal testing), with fetal anatomy scans revealing no abnormalities.

Initially, she was evaluated in the emergency department by a multidisciplinary team. Oxygen saturation was within normal limits (median value 97%), thoracic examination showed a normal heart rate (median value 74 bpm) and blood pressure (median value 100/60 mmHg). ECG findings were unremarkable. Subsequently, she was admitted to the Obstetrics and Gynecology Department. Her abdominal distention was more pronounced than anticipated for her gestational age; palpation revealed a tender, taut-elastic swelling occupying the entire upper left quadrant and measuring 18 cm, with no evidence of ascites.

On bimanual pelvic examination, a fixed mass about 9 cm in diameter was palpated in the lesser pelvis. The mass was firm, rounded, and partially protruded into the posterior fornix.

Pain management was initiated with intravenous paracetamol (1000 mg twice daily) and positioning in a semi-upright posture, presumed to help mitigate diaphragmatic compression from both the gravid uterus and large abdominal masses. This intervention led to partial symptom relief without the need for supplemental oxygen or ventilation, lowering pain scores from 5 to 1 (Visual Analogue Scale).

While hospitalized, the patient developed a mild fever. Testing for SARS-CoV-2 by nasopharyngeal PCR was negative; however, urine cultures revealed Klebsiella pneumoniae infection. Laboratory findings indicated iron deficiency anemia with hemoglobin at 9.9 g/L (normal range 12.0–16.6 g/L), and serum ferritin at 25 mcg/L (normal 15–300 mcg/L). C-reactive protein was mildly elevated at 1.39 mg/dL (normal 0–0.3 mg/dL). White blood cell count was 11.29×10^3/uL (normal 4–10×10^3/uL) with neutrophils at 9.27×10^3/uL (normal 2.8–5.5×10^3/uL), which were considered consistent with physiologic changes during pregnancy.

Investigations:

Ultrasound revealed two sizable unilocular cystic lesions with smooth borders and peripheral blood flow. The superior lesion measured 18×12×7 cm and stretched from the left subphrenic space down to the uterine fundus. The inferior lesion was 9.5×8×8 cm and appeared attached to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration (58×71 mm).

Assessment of tumour markers, including CA 19–9, CA 15–3, CEA, β2-microglobulin, and LDH, showed values within reference ranges, while CA-125 was increased (41.8 U/mL; normal <35 U/mL), with a rising trend compared to 36 U/mL recorded two weeks prior. This indicated a mild upward trend in the serum level of CA-125. Consequently, the patient underwent a non-contrast MRI, which revealed thickened walls (1.4 cm), heterogenous fluid within T1 and T2 fat-sat weighted sequences, and localized hyperintense solid portions in T1-T2 weighted images (refer to figure 1; only T2 fat-sat images presented). The boundary of the lower lesion was ambiguous and appeared contiguous with the previously visualized posterior leiomyoma seen on ultrasonography. No lymphadenopathy was detected on MRI.

Differential diagnosis:

During ultrasonographic assessment, the cystic lesions resembled two large decidualised endometriomas, with the inferior cyst apparently attached to a posterior uterine intramural-subserosal leiomyoma measuring 58×71 mm.

Magnetic resonance imaging revealed two round, unilocular adnexal cysts containing hypertrophied vessels and heterogenous fluid (see figure 1). The inferior lesion was observed to be contiguous with a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma undergoing cystic degeneration, measuring 58×71 mm.

The considerable size of the gravid uterus and the sizeable masses precluded identification of any connecting stalk or tumor-feeding vessel for the upper lesion, guiding the diagnosis toward ovarian origins, particularly endometriomas, supported by a mild increase in CA-125 levels during the second trimester (see discussion).

Given the sonographic and MRI findings, the differential diagnosis comprised decidualised endometriomas and cystic degenerating leiomyomas. A mild elevation of CA-125 was consistent with an endometrioma for the upper cyst, whereas the anatomical continuity with uterine tissue indicated a myomatous etiology for the lower mass. The patient's dyspnoea was deemed secondary to mechanical compression from the sizeable abdominal lesions, without any signs of cardiac or pulmonary impairment.

Treatment:

Given the progression of the patient’s clinical status—including abdominal pain from mass effect and dyspnoea secondary to restrictive lung disease related to the size of the abdominal masses—the requirement to establish a definitive diagnosis, the potential risk of complications (such as rupture, torsion, and infection), and the possibility of continued uterine enlargement with fetal development, a laparotomy was performed via a longitudinal incision from xiphoid to pubic region at 21 weeks and 6 days gestation after obtaining informed consent.

Initially, two specimens of free peritoneal fluid were collected for both microbiological cultures and cytological analysis. The surgical exploration of the abdomen confirmed ongoing fetal cardiac activity.

Unexpectedly, the superior mass was identified as a large, pedunculated, atypically cystic, and firm-elastic uterine leiomyoma with significant vascularity, which appeared initially torsed and leaking. This finding likely explained the patient’s fever during her admission (refer to figure 2). As a result, the surgical team elected to delicately excise the mass from its uterine attachment using a double clamp technique (see figure 2). After transecting the stalk, the exposed base was meticulously secured with a single absorbable suture. The inferior mass, originating from the uterine torus, was left intact to reduce obstetric risk; this lesion appeared immobile with a smooth surface and soft consistency, consistent with a large, cystic intramural-subserosal leiomyoma.

The procedure was completed in 2 hours and 25 minutes. Importantly, the patient was given prophylactic progesterone (100 mg intramuscularly) intraoperatively and continued with 100 mg intramuscular injections daily until discharge. Subsequently, hydroxyprogesterone caproate was administered intramuscularly at 341 mg (1 vial IM every 3 days per package insert) until scheduled delivery. Intraoperative antimicrobial prophylaxis consisted of intravenous cefazolin 2 g, and postoperative antibiotic therapy included intravenous cefazolin 1 g three times daily and clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily throughout the hospitalisation, following the antibiogram of the urine culture (Klebsiella pneumoniae highly sensitive to both cephalosporins and clarithromycin).

Outcome and follow-up:

Following surgery, progressive anemia (Hb 7.5 g/L, HCT 24.3%) and ongoing iron deficiency necessitated intravenous administration of ferric carboxymaltose (1 g) along with two blood transfusions, both of which were effective (Hb increased to 10.0 g/L, HCT to 30.4%). The patient was discharged 10 days postoperatively without any complications and subsequently participated in intensive obstetric monitoring every three weeks, which indicated fetal growth at the 86th percentile according to the WHO fetal growth chart calculator (https://srhr.org/fetalgrowthcalculator/#/), as well as tracking the amniotic fluid index.

Peritoneal fluid analyses returned negative results for culture and cytology. Pathological assessment identified benign uterine leiomyomas and regions of ischemic necrosis adjacent to the torsioned pedicle.

The inferiorly located myoma would have posed a significant barrier to fetal head passage during labor's expulsive phase. Accordingly, an elective cesarean section was performed on the pre-existing surgical scar upon the fetus reaching 37 weeks' gestation, thus reducing the likelihood of uterine scar rupture and resulting in an optimal neonatal outcome.

At present, the patient does not experience symptoms attributable to myoma, although the remaining pelvic myoma has slightly diminished in size to 7.5 cm. The patient is breastfeeding successfully and has opted not to schedule further surgery at this time, given partial postpartum regression of the residual leiomyomas, the absence of clinical symptoms, and the satisfaction of achieving motherhood. The infant is progressing appropriately.

Discussion:

Myomas can often be misdiagnosed as adnexal masses: the incidence during pregnancy is 0.3–10.1% and 1–6%, respectively .13

In this case, the initial assessment revealed abdominal masses resembling two large decidualised endometriomas, except for the caudal solid segment of the pelvic lesion, which on both ultrasound and MRI was consistent with a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma with cystic degeneration measuring 58×71 mm. Due to the lack of specific guidelines for managing pelvic masses during pregnancy, a multidisciplinary team determined that planned surgical intervention might lower the likelihood of preterm birth.

Serum CA-125 typically displays two elevations during pregnancy: one peak in the first trimester (median 23 U/mL, p<0.00001) and another in the third trimester (median 21 U/mL, p<0.001), both of which are higher compared to values in the second trimester (median 14.0 U/mL). However, in this patient, the measured CA-125 level was 41.8 U/mL, exceeding the expected median for the second trimester.

CA-125 is primarily utilized as a tumor marker for ovarian malignancy, with a standard threshold of ≥35 U/mL, especially in women over 50 years of age where its performance characteristics—including area under the curve, positive predictive value, sensitivity, and specificity—are enhanced. Nevertheless, CA-125 can also be elevated in numerous benign conditions, such as endometriosis and adenomyosis (>19 U/mL), pelvic inflammatory disease, menstruation, as well as early and late stages of pregnancy (median values 23 and 21 U/mL, respectively), risk of miscarriage, and myomas (cut-off <19 U/mL).57

The main factors influencing the clinical approach included the presence of large, vascularized lesions with mixed solid and cystic morphology, which carried an immediate risk of complications such as rupture, torsion, or infection; the patient’s worsening pain and dyspnoea; the potential for the masses to enlarge further, thereby increasing the likelihood of hypoxia, necrosis, and degeneration; a mildly elevated CA-125 level (41.8 U/mL); and concerns about restricted space for subsequent normal uterine growth.

Given the suspicion of a neoplastic process and the indeterminate nature of the masses, an MRI without contrast was performed to achieve a more accurate diagnosis following the ultrasound evaluation. Current guidelines advise against gadolinium-based contrast MRI in pregnancy except when absolutely necessary, such as for rheumatologic, inflammatory, or infiltrative dermatological conditions, or in situations involving risk of stillbirth or neonatal mortality. Consequently, the multidisciplinary team opted for a non-contrast MRI and avoided using MRI scanners above 1.5-T, which are deemed reasonably safe for patients beyond the first trimester.

In this case, the interdisciplinary team—including surgeons, gynecologists, and obstetricians—chose to implement a longitudinal laparotomic surgical approach after acquiring formal written informed consent from the patient. During intraoperative evaluation, the upper mass displayed a pedicle, leading to an elevated risk for acute and typical complications. This finding justified the decision to restrict the intervention to an upper myomectomy. In addition, fetal well-being was closely followed throughout the procedure using serial and periodic transvaginal ultrasound assessments, alongside the preventative administration of intraoperative progesterone.

Zhao et al, in a large multicenter investigation, highlighted the necessity to assess and document fetal heart rate preoperatively, intraoperatively, and postoperatively.

Typically, surgical management is deferred until after delivery, with medical and expectant strategies being favored. Nevertheless, Sakinci et al recently conducted research in a tertiary care facility, finding no statistically significant differences between women undergoing caesarean section (CS) with concurrent myomectomy and those without myoma undergoing CS alone in terms of pre- and postoperative hemoglobin levels or rates of blood transfusion (p > 0.05). Conversely, duration of hospital stay and operative time were notably increased in the CS with myomectomy cohort (p = 0.001 and p = 0.001, respectively). The average myoma diameter measured 8.3 ± 4.1 cm, with a significant positive association between the size of the myoma and length of hospitalization (p = 0.01).

The same study demonstrated a statistically significant negative association between myoma size and gestational week at delivery (p = 0.035).

Uterine leiomyomas display a non-linear pattern of growth and regression during pregnancy and the postpartum period. Specifically, leiomyoma size increases by 6.90% per week overall, with a 6.12% weekly increase between the first and second trimesters (p<0.001), and a 3.10%±0.39% increase from the second to third trimesters (p<0.05). Thus, the rate of myoma enlargement is notably greater in the first trimester compared to later stages of pregnancy. Conversely, in the third trimester and up to 6 weeks postpartum, leiomyoma size markedly declines, with a mean reduction of 44.25%±36.05% (p<0.05).

These studies underscore the necessity for thorough ultrasonographic assessment to document the size, location, number, and proximity of uterine myomas relative to the placenta prior to considering surgical intervention.

When uterine myomas are identified during pregnancy, conservative management is generally preferred over surgery, given the risks of significant hemorrhage, pregnancy loss, preterm delivery, and uterine rupture. Nonetheless, myomectomy should be considered in situations involving complicated masses, such as torsion of a pedunculated myoma or persistent, unresponsive symptoms despite conservative measures. The surgical procedure should be minimized in duration to safeguard the pregnancy and may be performed via laparoscopy, ideally using an open Hasson technique for patients at an earlier gestational stage (first or early second trimester), for smaller myomas, and when the lesion is not intramural. This method offers improved outcomes regarding maternal morbidity and likelihood of vaginal birth. Still, this case highlights a typical selection bias where laparotomy is favored over laparoscopy for larger, multiple, intramural, or critically located myomas, situations linked to increased rates of cesarean section, especially when a sizable myoma is adherent to the posterior isthmic uterine wall, potentially impeding vaginal delivery.
