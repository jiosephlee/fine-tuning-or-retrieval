Title: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

Rosalba De Nola, Emma Bassi, Cataldo Paletta, Mario Vicino

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her 40s, who had not previously given birth, presented during her second trimester with abdominal pain and shortness of breath. Ultrasound identified two sizeable abdominal masses: the upper mass occupied the entire left subphrenic region and extended to the fundus of the uterus, while the lower mass was located in the pouch of Douglas. The patient underwent laparotomy, with the pelvic mass left undisturbed. She received standard obstetric monitoring thereafter, and an elective caesarean section was performed, resulting in favorable outcomes for both mother and infant. When large symptomatic masses arise during pregnancy, thorough assessment—including bimanual examination and safe imaging techniques like ultrasound and non-contrast MRI—is crucial. Surgical intervention should be reserved for cases with oncological suspicion or clinical complications, with a priority on preserving the pregnancy and minimizing unnecessary treatment. Delivery decisions should take into account the mass size, quantity, and anatomical position.

Background:

Diagnosing and managing unusual or symptomatic abdominal masses in pregnancy poses considerable clinical difficulties. Available diagnostic tools are limited to physical assessment, ultrasound (US) including colour Doppler, and non-contrast magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Preoperative US is especially helpful for detecting symptomatic uterine leiomyomas positioned on the anterior surface of the pregnant uterus, regardless of whether they are submucosal or pedunculated. MRI is more accurate for evaluating multiple, sizable, intramural, or posterior fibroids, as it allows for precise measurement of the distance between the lesion and the uterine cavity.

The utility of serum tumour markers during pregnancy is controversial. For instance, CA-125 levels normally increase in the first trimester and return to baseline thereafter. Thus, single values lack diagnostic significance, whereas repeated measurements can reveal patterns that assist in differential diagnosis and ongoing monitoring. Effective management requires thorough multidisciplinary evaluation of risks, carefully weighing obstetric considerations against the infrequency and complexity of these cases.

Case presentation:

An obese woman in her 40s, with a body mass index of 33 and obstetric history G1P0A1, presented during her second trimester (21 weeks gestational age) complaining of intense, sudden-onset abdominal pain in both the upper left quadrant and pelvic region (Visual Analogue Scale score 5 for each), accompanied by mild shortness of breath. Previous first and second trimester screenings indicated low risk for chromosomal abnormalities (supported by non-invasive prenatal testing), and normal fetal morphology was observed.

Upon initial evaluation in the emergency department, she underwent comprehensive assessment by multiple specialties. She exhibited normal oxygen saturation (average 97%), thoracic physical examination revealed a normal heart rate (average 74 bpm), and her blood pressure was measured at 100/60 mm Hg. ECG findings were unremarkable. Subsequently, she was transferred to the Obstetrical and Gynaecological Division. Abdominal examination revealed enlargement beyond what is typical for her gestational age, characterized by a sensitive swelling and a firm, elastic mass extending through the upper left quadrant, measuring 18 cm, with no clinical indications of ascites.

Bimanual vaginal examination detected a non-mobile, approximately 9 cm mass within the lesser pelvis; the mass was round, firm, and partly projected towards the posterior vaginal fornix.

The patient was treated with intravenous paracetamol (1000 mg twice daily) and placed in a semi-orthostatic position, which theoretically reduced diaphragmatic pressure caused by both the gravid uterus and substantial abdominal masses. This resulted in a modest alleviation of symptoms, with pain decreasing from 5 to 1 on the Visual Analogue Scale, without requiring supplemental oxygen or respiratory support.

During hospitalization, she developed a low-grade fever. Severe acute respiratory syndrome Coronavirus 2 testing via nasopharyngeal PCR was negative; however, urine cultures identified Klebsiella pneumoniae infection. Blood analysis revealed iron deficiency anaemia, with haemoglobin at 9.9 g/L (reference range 12.0–16.6 g/L) and serum ferritin 25 mcg/L (reference range 15–300 mcg/L); C reactive protein was mildly elevated at 1.39 mg/dL (reference range 0–0.3 mg/dL); and both leukocyte and neutrophil counts were increased—white blood cells at 11.29×10³/uL (reference range 4–10×10³/uL) and neutrophils at 9.27×10³/uL (reference range 2.8–5.5×10³/uL), which was attributed to pregnancy-associated changes.

Investigations:

Ultrasonography revealed two sizeable, unilocular cystic lesions characterized by smooth borders and vascularization along their peripheries. The superior lesion was measured at 18×12×7 cm, spanning from the left subphrenic space down to the uterine fundus. The inferior lesion was 9.5×8×8 cm and appeared attached to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma displaying cystic transformation (58×71 mm).

Assessment of tumor markers (CA 19–9, CA 15–3, CEA, β2-microglobulin, and LDH) showed values within reference ranges, except for CA-125, which was elevated at 41.8 U/mL (normal <35 U/mL), having risen from 36 U/mL two weeks prior. This reflected a mild upward trend in this marker's serum concentration. Consequently, the patient underwent MRI without administration of contrast, which revealed cyst walls of 1.4 cm thickness, inhomogeneous fluid signal on T1 and T2 fat-saturated sequences, and areas of focal hyperintensity on both T1 and T2-weighted solid components (refer to figure 1; only T2 fat-saturated images depicted). The border between the inferior lesion and adjacent structures was indistinct, and it appeared to be contiguous with the posterior leiomyoma previously identified on ultrasound. MRI showed no evidence of lymphadenopathy.

Differential diagnosis:

During ultrasonography, the cystic lesions appeared similar to two large decidualised endometriomas, with the lower cyst noted to be adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine leiomyoma measuring 58×71 mm.

Magnetic resonance imaging revealed two oval-shaped, unilocular adnexal cysts accompanied by enlarged vessels and non-uniform internal fluid (refer to figure 1); the lower lesion was contiguous with a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration and measuring 58×71 mm.

Owing to the considerable size of both the gravid uterus and the masses, no connective stalk or tumor-supplying vessel could be discerned for the upper mass, steering diagnostic considerations towards ovarian lesions—particularly endometriomas—supported by a modest increase in CA-125 detected in the second trimester of pregnancy (see discussion).

Considering the findings from sonography and MRI, possible diagnoses included decidualised endometriomas and leiomyomas undergoing cystic degeneration. The minimal rise in CA-125 lent support to the diagnosis of endometrioma for the upper cyst, whereas tissue continuity with the uterus suggested a myomatous etiology for the lower lesion. The patient's dyspnoea was presumed to be caused by direct mechanical pressure from the large abdominal masses, as neither pulmonary nor cardiac impairment was identified.

Treatment:

Given the deterioration in clinical status (including abdominal pain caused by the masses and dyspnoea attributed to restrictive lung disease secondary to the abdominal mass size), the urgent need for definitive diagnosis, potential complications (such as rupture, torsion, and infection), and the likelihood of further uterine enlargement with advancing gestation, a laparotomy was performed with a longitudinal xiphoid-to-pubic incision at 21 weeks and 6 days’ gestation after obtaining informed consent.

Initially, two samples of free peritoneal fluid were obtained for culture and cytological analysis. The abdominal cavity was then explored, and fetal cardiac activity was confirmed.

Unexpectedly, the superior mass was a large, pedunculated, atypical, cystic, and taut-elastic uterine leiomyoma that was highly vascularized, appeared to have been recently twisted, and exhibited leakage—potentially accounting for the patient’s febrile episodes during hospitalization (refer to figure 2). As a result, the surgical team elected to carefully excise the mass at its attachment to the uterus using a double clamp technique (see figure 2). After resection of the stalk, the base was meticulously sutured with a single absorbable suture. The inferior mass, arising from the uterine torus, was preserved to reduce obstetric risk. This lesion was immobile, with a smooth external surface and soft consistency, resembling a sizable cystic intramural-subserosal leiomyoma.

The operation was completed in 2 hours and 25 minutes. Importantly, intraoperative progesterone prophylaxis was administered as 100 mg intramuscularly and maintained with daily 100 mg intramuscular injections until the patient’s discharge. Afterwards, hydroxyprogesterone caproate was given intramuscularly at a dose of 341 mg (1 ampoule every three days, per package instructions) until scheduled delivery. Prophylactic cefazolin (2 g IV) was provided intraoperatively and continued postoperatively with cefazolin 1 g IV three times daily plus clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily, as per urine culture sensitivity (Klebsiella pneumoniae highly susceptible to cephalosporins and clarithromycin), for the duration of hospitalization.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following the operation, the patient's anaemia worsened (Hb 7.5 g/L, HCT 24.3%) and iron deficiency persisted, prompting treatment with intravenous ferric carboxymaltose (1 g) and two successful blood transfusions (resulting in Hb 10.0 g/L, HCT 30.4%). She was discharged 10 days postoperatively with no complications, then underwent close obstetrical monitoring every three weeks, demonstrating fetal growth in the 86th percentile per the WHO fetal growth chart calculator (available online at https://srhr.org/fetalgrowthcalculator/#/) and stable amniotic fluid index.

Peritoneal fluid analysis yielded negative results on both culture and cytology. Histological evaluation identified benign uterine leiomyomas with regions of ischaemic necrosis adjacent to the twisted pedicle.

The location of the caudal myoma posed a significant barrier to the fetal head descending during the second stage of labor. Therefore, an elective caesarean section was carried out on the same incision at 37 weeks gestation, reducing the risk of uterine scar rupture and achieving a positive neonatal outcome.

At present, the patient has no symptoms attributable to myoma, although the remaining pelvic myoma has decreased slightly in size to 7.5 cm. She is breastfeeding successfully and has elected not to schedule further surgery, given the partial regression of residual leiomyomas postpartum, lack of symptoms, and fulfillment of her desire for motherhood. The infant is thriving.

Discussion:

Myomas can often be misdiagnosed as adnexal masses: the incidence during pregnancy is 0.3–10.1% and 1–6%, respectively .13

In this patient, the initial assessment revealed abdominal masses resembling two large decidualised endometriomas; however, the lower solid portion of the pelvic mass was reminiscent of a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma with cystic degeneration, measuring 58×71 mm, observed on both ultrasound and MRI. Given the lack of established protocols for managing pelvic masses during pregnancy, the multidisciplinary team opted for planned surgical intervention, hypothesizing it might reduce the likelihood of preterm birth.

Typically, maternal serum CA-125 levels exhibit two peaks during pregnancy: one in the first trimester (median 23 U/mL, p<0.00001) and another in the third trimester (median 21 U/mL, p<0.001), relative to the second trimester (median 14.0 U/mL). Contrasting this, the patient’s CA-125 was recorded at 41.8 U/mL, exceeding the median value expected in the second trimester.

CA-125 is primarily utilized as a tumor marker for ovarian cancer (current standard cut-off ≥35 U/mL), especially in women over age 50, where it demonstrates greater diagnostic accuracy and predictive value. However, elevated CA-125 levels can also be associated with various benign conditions, such as endometriosis and adenomyosis (>19 U/mL), pelvic inflammatory disease, menstruation, early and near-term pregnancy (median 23 and 21 U/mL, respectively), risk of miscarriage, and myoma (cut-off <19 U/mL).57

The principal factors guiding the clinical approach included the detection of large, highly vascularized masses characterized by both solid and cystic components, posing significant risk for complications such as rupture, torsion, or infection; the patient's deteriorating symptoms (increased pain and shortness of breath); the potential for ongoing tumor growth that could elevate the likelihood of hypoxia, necrosis, and degeneration; mildly elevated oncological markers (CA-125=41.8 U/mL); and concern regarding limited space available to accommodate further physiologic expansion of the uterus.

Given the potential neoplastic nature and ambiguous etiology of the masses, the patient underwent a non-contrast MRI to improve diagnostic specificity following ultrasonography. Current guidelines indicate that gadolinium-enhanced MRI should not be performed during pregnancy unless deemed strictly necessary for conditions such as rheumatological, inflammatory, or infiltrative dermatological diseases, or in cases of threatened fetal loss or neonatal mortality. Accordingly, the multidisciplinary team proceeded with non-contrast MRI, ensuring avoidance of scanning with more than 1.5-T field strength, which is considered to be relatively safe after the first trimester.

In this case, the multidisciplinary team—including surgeons, gynecologists, and obstetricians—chose to proceed with longitudinal laparotomy after securing formal written informed consent from the patient. During the surgical evaluation, the superior lesion was found to have a pedicle, posing a considerable risk for an acute, typical complication. This finding was the basis for restricting the procedure solely to an upper myomectomy. In addition, fetal viability was assessed intraoperatively with serial and periodic transvaginal ultrasound examinations, and intraoperative progesterone was administered prophylactically.

Zhao et al.'s large multicentric study underscores the requirement to monitor and record fetal heart rate before, during, and after surgical intervention.

Typically, surgery is postponed until after delivery, favoring medical and expectant management. However, Sakinci et al. reported from a tertiary center that no statistically significant difference was observed in preoperative and postoperative hemoglobin levels or blood transfusion rates (p >0.05) between women who had cesarean section (CS) combined with myomectomy and those who underwent only CS without myoma. Nevertheless, both hospitalization length and surgical time were significantly increased in the CS plus myomectomy group (p = 0.001 for both comparisons). The average myoma measured 8.3 ± 4.1 cm, with a statistically significant positive correlation between the size of myoma and duration of hospital stay (p = 0.01).

The aforementioned study additionally demonstrated a statistically significant inverse relationship between myoma size and gestational week at delivery (p = 0.035).

Uterine leiomyomas exhibit non-linear alterations in size during both pregnancy and the postpartum period. Specifically, leiomyomas were found to expand by 6.90% per week, with a growth rate of 6.12% (p<0.001) observed between the first and second trimesters, and 3.10%±0.39% between the second and third trimesters (p<0.05). This indicates that the increase in leiomyoma size is most pronounced during the first trimester compared to subsequent gestational periods. Conversely, in the third trimester and at 6 weeks postpartum, myoma size decreased significantly, with a mean reduction of 44.25%±36.05% (p<0.05).

These publications underscored the necessity for thorough ultrasound evaluation regarding the dimensions, anatomical position, number, and placental proximity of uterine myomas prior to proceeding with surgical intervention.

For uterine myomas detected during pregnancy, a conservative approach is typically favored over surgery due to elevated risks of substantial hemorrhage, pregnancy termination, preterm delivery, and uterine rupture. Nevertheless, myomectomy may be indicated in situations involving complicated masses such as torsion of a pedunculated myoma or ongoing symptoms unresponsive to conservative management. To optimize pregnancy preservation, surgical procedures should be kept brief and may also be conducted laparoscopically, with the open Hasson technique being preferable in cases involving earlier gestational stages (first and early second trimesters), smaller masses, and particular locations (excluding intramural myomas). This technique is associated with improved maternal morbidity outcomes and facilitates vaginal birth. The current case illustrates typical selection bias favoring laparotomy over laparoscopic methods when confronted with larger, multiple, intramural, or critically situated myomas, which may correspond with increased cesarean section rates, as demonstrated by the presence of a sizable myoma adherent to the posterior wall at the isthmic region of the uterus, serving as a barrier to vaginal delivery.
