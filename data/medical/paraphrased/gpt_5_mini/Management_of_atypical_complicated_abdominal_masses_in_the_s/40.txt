Title: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

Rosalba De Nola, Emma Bassi, Cataldo Paletta, Mario Vicino

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her 40s, nulliparous, experienced abdominal pain and dyspnea during her second trimester of pregnancy. Ultrasound examination identified two substantial abdominal masses: the superior mass occupied the entire left subphrenic area and extended to the uterine fundus, while the inferior mass was situated within the pouch of Douglas. Laparotomy was performed, leaving the pelvic mass undisturbed. She continued with routine obstetric monitoring and underwent a planned caesarean section, achieving optimal outcomes for both mother and newborn. When large symptomatic masses arise during pregnancy, thorough assessment—including bimanual examination and safe imaging techniques like ultrasonography and non-contrast MRI—is crucial. Surgical intervention should be reserved for suspected malignancy or when clinical complications occur, with efforts made to sustain pregnancy and avoid unnecessary treatment. The chosen delivery method should be based on the number, size, and precise location of the uterine masses.

Background:

Managing and diagnosing unusual or symptomatic abdominal masses in pregnancy is clinically demanding. Available diagnostic tools are confined to physical examination, ultrasound (US) with colour Doppler, and non-contrast MRI. Preoperative US is especially effective in detecting symptomatic uterine leiomyomas located on the anterior aspect of the pregnant uterus, including submucosal or pedunculated types. For multiple, large, intramural, or posterior leiomyomas, MRI provides greater accuracy, as it can precisely measure the myoma’s proximity to the uterine cavity.

The utility of serum tumour markers in pregnancy is still controversial. For instance, CA-125 concentrations naturally increase during the initial trimester and return to normal later. Thus, a single measurement is not conclusive; however, serial assays may supply valuable information for differential diagnosis and ongoing assessment. Best practice requires comprehensive multidisciplinary evaluation to balance obstetric considerations against the infrequency and complexity of these situations.

Case presentation:

An obese woman in her 40s, with a body mass index of 33 and a history of G1P0A1, presented in the second trimester of pregnancy at 21 weeks’ gestation, reporting sudden, severe abdominal pain in the upper left quadrant and pelvis (Visual Analogue Scale score 5 at both locations), accompanied by mild shortness of breath. Routine screening during the first and second trimesters showed low risk for aneuploidies (confirmed with non-invasive prenatal testing) and normal fetal anatomy.

She was initially evaluated in the emergency department by a multidisciplinary team. Her oxygen saturation was within normal range (median 97%), and physical examination revealed normal heart rate (median 74 bpm) and blood pressure (median 100/60 mm Hg). The ECG was unremarkable. Subsequently, she was admitted to the Obstetrical and Gynecological Division. Examination of the abdomen revealed a greater-than-expected enlargement for her gestational age, with a tender and taut-elastic mass measuring 18 cm, filling the upper left quadrant but without clinical evidence of ascites.

On bimanual vaginal examination, a fixed, rounded, and firm mass approximately 9 cm in size was found within the lesser pelvis, partially protruding at the posterior fornix.

Pain management included intravenous paracetamol 1000 mg twice daily, along with a semi-orthostatic position, intended to reduce diaphragmatic pressure from the gravid uterus and large abdominal masses. These interventions resulted in modest symptom improvement and reduction of pain from 5 to 1 on the Visual Analogue Scale, without requiring supplemental oxygen or ventilatory support.

While hospitalized, the patient developed a mild fever. SARS-CoV-2 PCR testing from a nasopharyngeal swab yielded a negative result; however, urine cultures identified a Klebsiella pneumoniae infection. Laboratory findings included iron deficiency anemia (haemoglobin 9.9 g/L [normal: 12.0–16.6 g/L]; serum ferritin 25 mcg/L [normal: 15–300 mcg/L]), a slightly elevated C-reactive protein at 1.39 mg/dL (normal: 0–0.3 mg/dL), and increased neutrophil count (white blood cells 11.29×103 /uL [normal: 4–10×103 /uL]; neutrophils 9.27×103 /uL [normal: 2.8–5.5×103 /uL]), compatible with physiological changes of pregnancy.

Investigations:

Ultrasonography revealed two sizable, unilocular cystic lesions exhibiting smooth borders and peripheral blood flow. The superior lesion measured 18×12×7 cm, extending from the left subphrenic area down to the uterine fundus. The inferior lesion had dimensions of 9.5×8×8 cm and appeared attached to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration (size 58×71 mm).

Tumor marker assays for CA 19–9, CA 15–3, CEA, β2-microglobulin, and LDH fell within reference ranges, whereas CA-125 was elevated at 41.8 U/mL (normal <35 U/mL) and had risen from 36 U/mL measured two weeks prior, indicating a modest upward trend in this marker's serum concentration. Consequently, an MRI without contrast was performed, revealing thickened walls (1.4 cm), inhomogeneous fluid content on T1 and T2 fat-saturated sequences, as well as focal T1-T2 weighted hyperintense solid regions (refer to figure 1; only T2 fat-sat images included). The delineation of the lower mass was ambiguous, as it appeared continuous with the posterior leiomyoma noted previously on ultrasound. MRI showed no evidence of lymphadenopathy.

Differential diagnosis:

During the ultrasound assessment, the cystic lesions closely resembled two large decidualised endometriomas. Additionally, the more caudally situated cyst appeared to be attached to a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine leiomyoma, which measured 58×71 mm.

MRI demonstrated the presence of two rounded, unilocular adnexal cysts, both exhibiting prominent vessels and heterogeneous internal fluid (refer to figure 1). The inferior mass appeared to be adherent to a posterior uterine intramural-subserosal myoma, which itself had undergone cystic degeneration and measured 58×71 mm.

Owing to the considerable space occupied by the gravid uterus and the voluminous masses, there was no visible connecting stalk or feeding vessel for the superior lesion, thereby suggesting an ovarian origin—most notably endometriomas—particularly given the mild CA-125 elevation noted in the second trimester (see discussion).

Sonographic and MRI findings indicated that potential diagnoses included decidualised endometriomas as well as cystically degenerating leiomyomas: the slight increase in CA-125 supported the likelihood of endometrioma for the superior cyst, whereas the caudal mass's direct association with uterine tissue was more consistent with a myomatous origin. The patient’s dyspnoea was considered secondary to the physical compression exerted by the large abdominal masses, as there was no indication of cardiac or pulmonary abnormalities.

Treatment:

Given the progression of her symptoms—including abdominal pain from the masses and shortness of breath related to restrictive lung disease caused by their size—as well as the need for definitive diagnosis, concern over possible complications (rupture, torsion, infection), and anticipated uterine growth associated with advancing gestation, the patient underwent a laparotomy via a longitudinal incision from xiphoid to pubic bone at 21 weeks and 6 days following informed consent.

Initially, two specimens of free peritoneal fluid were obtained for bacteriological and cytological assessment. The abdominal cavity was then examined, and fetal cardiac activity was confirmed.

Unexpectedly, the upper (cranial) mass was identified as a large, pedunculated, unusual cystic and firm-elastic uterine leiomyoma with extensive vascularity, which was twisted and leaking—possibly accounting for the fever noted during hospitalization (see figure 2). As a result, the surgical team elected to carefully remove the mass at its uterine site with a double clamp (see figure 2). Upon division of its stalk, the base was meticulously ligated using a single absorbable suture. The lower (caudal) mass, arising from the uterine torus, was left untouched to reduce obstetric risk. This mass was immobile with a smooth contour and soft consistency, resembling a large cystic intramural-subserosal leiomyoma.

The operative time totaled 2 hours and 25 minutes. Of note, prophylactic progesterone was administered intramuscularly at a dose of 100 mg, initiated during the procedure and continued daily until discharge. Subsequently, the patient was started on intramuscular hydroxyprogesterone caproate (341 mg, 1 vial, every 3 days as per package instructions), maintained until the scheduled delivery. Cefazolin 2 g intravenously was used for intraoperative antibiotic prophylaxis; postoperative treatment consisted of cefazolin 1 g intravenously three times daily plus clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily during hospitalization, guided by urine culture findings (Klebsiella pneumoniae highly susceptible to cephalosporins and clarithromycin).

Outcome and follow-up:

Following surgery, the patient’s anaemia worsened (Hb 7.5 g/L, HCT 24.3%) and iron deficiency persisted, necessitating intravenous iron therapy (ferric carboxymaltose 1 g) and two successful blood transfusions (resulting in Hb 10.0 g/L, HCT 30.4%). She was discharged 10 days postoperatively without complications and began strict obstetrical monitoring every three weeks, which demonstrated fetal growth at the 86th percentile per the WHO fetal growth chart calculator (accessible at https://srhr.org/fetalgrowthcalculator/#/) and a stable amniotic fluid index.

Analysis of the peritoneal fluid yielded negative results for both culture and cytology. Histopathological assessment showed benign uterine leiomyomas with regions of ischemic necrosis adjacent to the twisted pedicle.

The location of the caudal myoma would have significantly impeded the descent of the fetal head during the second stage of labor. Therefore, an elective caesarean section was scheduled at 37 weeks gestation using the previous surgical scar, thereby minimizing the likelihood of uterine scar rupture; neonatal outcomes were excellent.

At present, the patient reports no symptoms attributable to the myoma, though the remaining pelvic myoma has slightly decreased in size to 7.5 cm. She is breastfeeding successfully and has opted against scheduling further surgery, given partial regression of the residual leiomyomas postpartum, her lack of symptoms, and completion of childbearing. The infant is developing normally.

Discussion:

Myomas can often be misdiagnosed as adnexal masses: the incidence during pregnancy is 0.3–10.1% and 1–6%, respectively .13

In this particular case, the initial assessment revealed abdominal masses presenting as two large decidualized endometriomas, except for the distal solid portion of the pelvic mass, which resembled a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration measuring 58×71 mm on both ultrasound and MRI scans. Given the lack of established treatment protocols for pelvic masses during pregnancy, the multidisciplinary team determined that performing elective surgery could potentially reduce the likelihood of preterm birth.

Throughout pregnancy, serum CA-125 concentrations typically exhibit two peaks: one during the first trimester (median 23 U/mL, p<0.00001) and a second in the third trimester (median 21 U/mL, p<0.001), relative to the second trimester values (median 14.0 U/mL). In contrast, this patient demonstrated a serum CA-125 level of 41.8 U/mL, which exceeds the median value typically observed during the second trimester.

CA-125 is primarily utilized as a tumor marker for ovarian cancer (commonly, a threshold of ≥35 U/mL is used), particularly in women older than 50 years (where it offers improved area under the curve, positive predictive value, sensitivity, and specificity). Nevertheless, elevated CA-125 may also be seen in various benign conditions, including endometriosis and adenomyosis (>19 U/mL), pelvic inflammatory disease, menstruation, pregnancy (both early and near term; medians 23 and 21 U/mL, respectively), increased risk of miscarriage, and myoma (cut-off <19 U/mL).57

The primary factors guiding the clinical approach included the identification of large, vascularized masses exhibiting both solid and cystic areas, presenting an immediate risk for complications such as rupture, torsion, or infection; the patient's worsening symptoms of pain and shortness of breath; the potential for further enlargement of the masses, thereby increasing the risk of hypoxia, necrosis, and degeneration; a mild elevation in oncologic biomarkers (CA-125=41.8 U/mL); and restricted space, which might hinder normal uterine growth.

Given the uncertainty regarding the etiology and neoplastic potential of the masses, the patient was subsequently scheduled for an MRI without contrast to improve diagnostic precision following sonographic evaluation. Based on current guidelines, MRI with gadolinium contrast is not advised during pregnancy unless absolutely essential—specifically in cases involving rheumatological, inflammatory, or infiltrative dermatological diseases, or concerns of stillbirth or neonatal demise. Consequently, the care team elected to perform a non-contrast MRI, ensuring the field strength did not exceed 1.5 Tesla, as this is generally regarded as safe beyond the first trimester.

In this case, the multidisciplinary team—including surgeons, gynecologists, and obstetricians—chose to undertake a longitudinal laparotomy after securing written informed consent from the patient. During the operation, the upper lesion was found to have a pedicle, which posed a considerable risk for an acute, classic complication. This finding led to the decision to confine surgical intervention to an upper myomectomy. Additionally, fetal viability was monitored throughout the procedure via intermittent transvaginal ultrasound examinations, and intraoperative progesterone was administered as a prophylactic measure.

A large multicenter investigation by Zhao et al emphasizes the necessity of assessing and recording fetal heart rate before, during, and after the surgical intervention.

Typically, surgery is postponed until after delivery, with a preference for medical management and observation. Nonetheless, Sakinci et al, in a recent study performed at a tertiary institution, demonstrated no statistically significant difference between women who underwent caesarean section (CS) combined with myomectomy versus those without myomas undergoing CS alone in terms of preoperative and postoperative hemoglobin values or blood transfusion requirements (p >0.05). However, length of hospital stay and duration of surgery were significantly greater in the CS with myomectomy group (p =0.001 and p =0.001, respectively). The average myoma diameter was 8.3 ± 4.1 cm, and a significant positive relationship was observed between myoma size and hospital stay (p =0.01).

The same study demonstrated a statistically significant inverse relationship between the dimensions of the myoma and the gestational age at delivery (p = 0.035).

Uterine leiomyomas exhibit a non-linear pattern of size alterations throughout pregnancy and the postpartum period. Specifically, leiomyoma size increases by 6.90% per week overall, with a rise of 6.12% (p<0.001) from the first to the second trimester, and a further increase of 3.10%±0.39% between the second and third trimesters (p<0.05). This data indicates that leiomyomas expand at a more rapid rate during the first trimester compared to later stages of pregnancy. Conversely, in the third trimester and at 6 weeks post-delivery, the size of the myomas significantly decreased, showing a mean reduction of 44.25%±36.05% (p<0.05).

These reports highlight the necessity of thorough ultrasound (US) assessment of uterine myomas regarding their size, number, position, and proximity to the placenta prior to any surgical intervention.

For myomas identified during pregnancy, a conservative management strategy is commonly chosen over surgery due to the potential for severe hemorrhage, miscarriage, preterm birth, and uterine rupture. Nonetheless, myomectomy may be warranted in instances where there is torsion of a pedunculated myoma or persistent symptoms that do not improve with conservative measures. Surgical intervention should be kept brief to protect the pregnancy, and laparoscopic techniques—preferably utilizing the open Hasson method—are suitable when gestational age is lower (during the first and early second trimester), the myoma is of smaller size, and the lesion is not intramural. This approach is associated with better maternal outcomes and likelihood of vaginal delivery. The present case, however, illustrates a frequent bias toward choosing laparotomy over laparoscopy when dealing with large, multiple, intramural, or challenging myoma locations. Such scenarios are often linked to a greater incidence of cesarean section (CS), particularly when a sizable myoma is adherent to the posterior wall of the uterine isthmus, creating an obstruction to vaginal delivery.
