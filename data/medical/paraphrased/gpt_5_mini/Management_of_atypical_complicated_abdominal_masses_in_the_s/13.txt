Title: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

Rosalba De Nola, Emma Bassi, Cataldo Paletta, Mario Vicino

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her 40s who had not previously given birth presented in her second trimester with complaints of abdominal pain and shortness of breath. Ultrasound examination identified two substantial abdominal masses: the superior mass spanned the entire left subphrenic region and extended down to the uterine fundus, while the inferior mass occupied the pouch of Douglas. Laparotomy was performed, but the caudal pelvic lesion was left undisturbed. The patient subsequently received standard prenatal monitoring and delivered via scheduled caesarean section, with both maternal and neonatal outcomes being excellent. When encountering large, symptomatic masses in pregnancy, it is critical to conduct thorough evaluation, utilizing bimanual examination along with safe imaging modalities like ultrasonography and non-contrast MRI. Surgical intervention should be reserved for cases where there is suspicion of malignancy or the occurrence of clinical complications, prioritizing pregnancy preservation and minimizing unnecessary procedures. The approach to delivery should be individualized based on the size, quantity, and anatomical location of the uterine masses.

Background:

The evaluation and treatment of unusual or symptomatic abdominal masses in pregnancy pose considerable clinical difficulties. Diagnostic approaches are restricted to physical examination, ultrasonography (US) with color Doppler, and non-contrast magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Preoperative US is especially helpful for detecting symptomatic uterine leiomyomas situated on the anterior surface of the pregnant uterus, including both submucosal and pedunculated types. In contrast, MRI is superior for characterizing multiple, large, intramural, or posterior leiomyomas, as it allows for precise measurement of the separation between the fibroid and the uterine cavity.

The utility of serum tumor markers during gestation is still debated. In particular, CA-125 levels physiologically increase in the first trimester and return to baseline thereafter. As a result, a single measurement is not diagnostic, while serial assessments may offer important trends to aid in differential diagnosis and monitoring. Effective management demands thorough multidisciplinary risk evaluation in order to navigate the obstetric risks and the infrequent and complex nature of these presentations.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 40s with obesity (body mass index 33), gravida 1 para 0 abortus 1, presented to our clinic at 21 weeks of gestation (second trimester) due to acute, severe abdominal pain affecting both the left upper quadrant and the pelvic region (Visual Analogue Scale score of 5 for each area), accompanied by mild shortness of breath. Routine screening during the first and second trimesters showed a low risk of aneuploidy (as verified by non-invasive prenatal testing) and normal fetal anatomy.

Initially, she presented to the emergency department for comprehensive evaluation by multiple specialties. Her oxygen saturation was within normal limits, with a median of 97%. On thoracic examination, she demonstrated a normal heart rate (median 74 bpm) and blood pressure (median 100/60 mm Hg). The ECG findings were unremarkable. Subsequently, she was admitted to the Obstetrics and Gynecology Department. Abdominal assessment revealed distention greater than anticipated for the gestational age, characterized by a tender, firm, elastic mass occupying the entire upper left quadrant and measuring 18 cm in size, with no clinical indications of ascites.

The bimanual vaginal examination showed the presence of a fixed mass in the lesser pelvis of about 9 cm, rounded and hard, that partially bulged at the posterior fornix.

Analgesia with intravenous paracetamol at a dose of 1000 mg twice daily, along with positioning the patient in a semi-orthostatic posture—presumed to alleviate diaphragmatic compression due to the gravid uterus and large abdominal masses—resulted in modest symptom relief without the need for supplemental oxygen or ventilatory assistance. Pain intensity decreased from 5 to 1 on the Visual Analogue Scale.

During hospitalization, the patient experienced a low-grade fever. A nasopharyngeal swab for SARS-CoV-2 using PCR testing yielded a negative result, while urine cultures identified a Klebsiella pneumoniae infection. Laboratory findings revealed iron deficiency anemia with hemoglobin measured at 9.9 g/L (normal range 12.0–16.6 g/L) and serum ferritin at 25 mcg/L (normal values 15–300 mcg/L). There was a mild elevation in C reactive protein at 1.39 mg/dL (reference 0–0.3 mg/dL), as well as increased neutrophil counts: total leukocytes 11.29×10^3 /uL (normal 4–10×10^3 /uL) with neutrophils at 9.27×10^3 /uL (normal 2.8–5.5×10^3 /uL), findings compatible with changes associated with pregnancy.

Investigations:

Ultrasound revealed two sizable, unilocular cystic lesions with smooth contours and peripheral blood flow. The superior lesion measured 18×12×7 cm and spanned from the left subphrenic area to the uterine fundus. The inferior lesion was 9.5×8×8 cm and appeared to be attached to a posterior intramural-subserosal fibroid exhibiting cystic changes (58×71 mm).

Tumor marker assessments for CA 19–9, CA 15–3, CEA, β2-microglobulin, and LDH were all within reference ranges, while the CA-125 level was found to be elevated at 41.8 U/mL (normal <35 U/mL), having risen from 36 U/mL measured two weeks prior, indicating a mild upward trend. An MRI scan without contrast was subsequently performed, revealing the cysts possessed thick walls (1.4 cm), demonstrated inhomogeneous fluid signals on T1 and T2 fat-saturated sequences, and contained focal areas that were hyperintense on both T1- and T2-weighted images (refer to figure 1; only T2 fat-saturated sequences shown). The caudal lesion’s borders could not be distinctly defined, and it appeared continuous with the previously noted posterior leiomyoma on ultrasound. MRI did not demonstrate any lymphadenopathy.

Differential diagnosis:

During ultrasonographic assessment, the cystic masses resembled two large decidualized endometriomas, with the lower cyst observed to be attached to an intramural-subserosal leiomyoma on the posterior uterine wall measuring 58×71 mm.

Magnetic resonance imaging revealed two well-defined, unilocular adnexal cysts accompanied by enlarged vessels and heterogeneous internal fluid (refer to figure 1); the more caudal lesion appeared to be affixed to a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration, also measuring 58×71 mm.

Owing to the significant size of both the gravid uterus and the large masses, neither a connecting stalk nor a vessel supplying the tumor could be identified for the superior mass. This finding led to a diagnostic suspicion of ovarian masses, with a particular consideration for endometriomas, especially given the mildly increased CA-125 level observed during the second trimester of pregnancy (refer to discussion).

Imaging findings from ultrasound and MRI prompted a differential diagnosis that included decidualized endometriomas and cystic degenerating leiomyomas. The modest rise in CA-125 was more indicative of an endometrioma in the upper cystic lesion, whereas the anatomical continuity with uterine tissue pointed towards a myomatous origin for the inferior mass. The patient’s dyspnoea was ascribed to the mechanical effect of the large intra-abdominal masses, in the absence of any cardiopulmonary impairment.

Treatment:

Given the progression of her symptoms—including abdominal pain attributable to the mass and dyspnoea from restrictive lung involvement related to the enlarging abdominal masses—the need for definitive diagnosis, the potential for complications such as rupture, torsion, or infection, as well as anticipated uterine growth alongside fetal development, laparotomy was performed with a longitudinal xiphoid-to-pubic incision at 21 weeks and 6 days gestation following informed consent.

First, we collected two samples of the free peritoneal fluid for cultural and cytological purposes. Then, we explored the abdominal cavity, verifying the presence of fetal cardiac activity.

Unexpectedly, the cranial lesion was identified as a large pedunculated uterine leiomyoma with cystic features and a tense-elastic consistency. It was highly vascularized and had evidence of prior torsion and leakage, which likely explained the patient's episodes of fever during admission (refer to figure 2). Accordingly, the surgical team opted to delicately excise the mass from its site of uterine attachment using a double-clamp technique (see figure 2). Following transection of the pedicle, the base was meticulously secured with a single absorbable suture. In contrast, the caudal lesion, which arose from the uterine torus, was left untouched in order to reduce obstetric complications. This lesion was immobile, smooth-surfaced, and soft to the touch, resembling a large cystic leiomyoma with an intramural-subserosal component.

The operation took 2 hours and 25 minutes. The patient was administered progesterone prophylaxis at a dose of 100 mg intramuscularly, beginning during the procedure and then continued daily with 100 mg intramuscularly until discharge. Following this, hydroxyprogesterone caproate was given intramuscularly at 341 mg (1 vial) every three days, as specified in the package insert, and maintained until planned delivery. For antibiotic prophylaxis during the surgery, cefazolin 2 g was given intravenously, after which postoperative antibiotic therapy consisted of cefazolin 1 g intravenously three times daily plus clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily throughout hospitalization, based on the antibiogram results of the urine culture showing Klebsiella pneumoniae highly susceptible to both cephalosporins and clarithromycin.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following surgery, the patient experienced worsening anemia (Hb 7.5 g/L, HCT 24.3%) alongside persistent iron deficiency, necessitating administration of intravenous iron (ferric carboxymaltose 1 g) and two units of blood transfusion, which successfully raised hemoglobin to 10.0 g/L and hematocrit to 30.4%. She was discharged in stable condition 10 days postoperatively and subsequently underwent close obstetric monitoring every 3 weeks, which demonstrated fetal growth (at the 86th percentile per WHO fetal growth chart calculator, accessible at https://srhr.org/fetalgrowthcalculator/#/) and normal amniotic fluid index.

Cultures and cytological analysis of the peritoneal fluid yielded negative results. Pathological assessment identified benign leiomyomas of the uterus and zones of ischemic necrosis adjacent to the torsioned pedicle.

The inferiorly located myoma would likely have presented a significant barrier to descent of the fetal head during the second stage of labor. Therefore, once the fetus attained 37 weeks gestational age, an elective cesarean section was performed via the existing surgical incision, thereby reducing the likelihood of uterine scar dehiscence and resulting in an excellent outcome for the newborn.

At present, the patient does not experience any myoma-associated symptoms, despite a modest decrease in the size of the remaining pelvic myoma to 7.5 cm. She is breastfeeding successfully but chooses not to schedule further surgical intervention at this time, given the partial postpartum shrinkage of the remaining leiomyomas, her lack of symptoms, and her satisfaction with having completed childbearing. The infant’s development is proceeding normally.

Discussion:

Myomas can often be misdiagnosed as adnexal masses: the incidence during pregnancy is 0.3–10.1% and 1–6%, respectively .13

In this case, the primary assessment revealed abdominal masses resembling two large decidualized endometriomas, with the exception of a caudal solid portion of the pelvic lesion, which mimicked a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration, measuring 58×71 mm on both ultrasound and MRI. Due to a lack of established guidelines for managing pelvic masses during pregnancy, the multidisciplinary team judged that elective surgical intervention might reduce the likelihood of preterm birth.

Serum CA-125 levels typically display two elevations throughout pregnancy: the first peak occurs in the initial trimester (median 23 U/mL, p<0.00001), and the second arises during the third trimester (median 21 U/mL, p<0.001), both notably higher than the median concentration observed in the second trimester (14.0 U/mL). In contrast, the patient discussed in this case exhibited a serum CA-125 level of 41.8 U/mL, which exceeds the median for the second trimester.

CA-125 serves primarily as a tumor marker for ovarian cancer, with the commonly accepted threshold being ≥35 U/mL, particularly in women over 50 years of age, in whom it demonstrates higher area under the curve, sensitivity, specificity, and positive predictive value. Nevertheless, elevated CA-125 can also be associated with various benign conditions, including endometriosis and adenomyosis (levels exceeding 19 U/mL), pelvic inflammatory disease, menstruation, early and late pregnancy (median concentrations of 23 and 21 U/mL, respectively), risk of miscarriage, as well as myoma (with a cut-off below 19 U/mL).57

The main factors guiding the clinical management included the identification of large, highly vascular masses containing both solid and cystic elements, which carried a significant risk of complications such as rupture, torsion, or infection. Additionally, the patient’s clinical status was deteriorating, with increasing pain and dyspnea. There was concern for potential further mass enlargement, increasing the likelihood of hypoxia, necrosis, and degeneration. Oncological markers were mildly elevated (CA-125=41.8 U/mL), and there was insufficient space to accommodate ongoing physiological uterine expansion.

Given the potential neoplastic etiology and indeterminate source of the lesions, an unenhanced MRI was subsequently conducted following the ultrasound assessment to improve diagnostic precision. In line with current guidelines, gadolinium-enhanced MRI is contraindicated in pregnancy except in cases of absolute necessity—such as rheumatological, inflammatory or infiltrative dermatologic diseases, or when there is a risk of stillbirth or neonatal mortality. Consequently, the multidisciplinary team opted for a non-contrast MRI and avoided imaging with MRI strengths greater than 1.5 Tesla, as this is considered acceptably safe beyond the first trimester.

In this case, after securing specific written informed consent from the patient, the multidisciplinary team—including surgeons, gynecologists, and obstetricians—decided to undertake longitudinal laparotomy. During the operation, the upper lesion demonstrated a pedicle, as identified intraoperatively, which carried a substantial risk for an acute, classic complication. Therefore, the surgical intervention was restricted to performing myomectomy on the upper lesion. Fetal viability was continuously evaluated using serial intermittent transvaginal ultrasound examinations throughout the surgery, and intraoperative progesterone was administered prophylactically.

According to a large multicentric study conducted by Zhao et al, it is mandatory to check and document the fetal heart rate before, during and after the surgery.

Typically, surgical intervention is postponed until after delivery, with a preference for conservative and medical management. Nonetheless, Sakinci et al recently published findings from a tertiary care setting demonstrating no significant difference between women who underwent caesarean section (CS) with concomitant myomectomy and those who had CS alone without myomas regarding preoperative and postoperative haemoglobin levels or blood transfusion requirements (p >0.05). However, both the length of hospital stay and operative time were notably increased among those who underwent CS plus myomectomy (p =0.001 for both outcomes). The average size of the myomas in their cohort was 8.3 ± 4.1 cm, and there was a statistically significant positive association between myoma size and hospitalisation duration (p =0.01).

The same research study also showed a statistically significant and inverse correlation between the size of the myoma and the delivery week (p = 0.035).

Uterine leiomyomas exhibit a non-linear pattern of growth throughout pregnancy and the postpartum period. Specifically, leiomyoma size expands at a rate of 6.90% per week, with an increase of 6.12% (p<0.001) observed between the first and second trimesters and a rise of 3.10%±0.39% between the second and third trimesters (p<0.05). Consequently, the rate of leiomyoma enlargement is notably greater during the initial trimester compared to later stages of gestation. Conversely, in the third trimester and at 6 weeks postpartum, myoma size diminishes significantly, with a mean reduction of 44.25%±36.05% (p<0.05).

These papers showed the importance of an accurate US examination of the size, location, number and distance of uterine myomas from the placenta before the surgical approach.

When uterine myomas are identified during pregnancy, conservative management is generally favored over surgery because of potential risks such as significant hemorrhage, loss of pregnancy, premature labor, and uterine rupture. Nevertheless, surgical removal of the myoma may be warranted if there are complicated tumors, such as in cases involving torsion of a pedunculated fibroid, or if ongoing symptoms persist despite conservative measures. Any surgical intervention should be kept as brief as possible to safeguard the pregnancy, and laparoscopic procedures—particularly using the open Hasson method—may be selected for cases with earlier gestational age (first or early second trimester), smaller lesions, and when the fibroid is not intramural. This minimally invasive strategy is associated with reduced maternal complications and an increased likelihood of vaginal delivery. Despite these considerations, the case under discussion illustrates a typical scenario in which laparotomy is chosen over laparoscopy for larger, multiple, intramural, or critically located fibroids, circumstances which are often linked to elevated cesarean section rates; this was exemplified by a large myoma attached to the posterior wall at the isthmic region of the uterus, potentially posing a significant obstruction to vaginal delivery.
