Title: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

Rosalba De Nola, Emma Bassi, Cataldo Paletta, Mario Vicino

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her 40s who had never given birth presented in her second trimester with abdominal pain and shortness of breath. Ultrasound identified two sizable abdominal masses: the superior mass filled the entire left subphrenic region and extended toward the fundus of the uterus, while the inferior mass was located in the pouch of Douglas. The patient underwent a laparotomy, leaving the lower pelvic mass intact. She subsequently received routine prenatal care and had an elective caesarean section, which resulted in excellent outcomes for both mother and child. For pregnant patients with large, symptomatic masses, thorough assessment—including bimanual examination and safe imaging like ultrasound or non-contrast MRI—is critical. Surgical intervention should be reserved for situations with oncologic concern or clinical complications, prioritizing pregnancy preservation and avoiding unnecessary procedures. The delivery method should be based on the size, number, and location of uterine masses.

Background:

The evaluation and treatment of unusual or symptomatic abdominal masses in pregnancy involve considerable clinical difficulties. Available diagnostic modalities are largely restricted to physical examination, ultrasound (US) with color Doppler, and non-contrast magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Preoperative US proves particularly useful in detecting symptomatic uterine leiomyomas situated on the anterior surface of the pregnant uterus, including both submucosal and pedunculated variants. For larger, multiple, intramural, or posteriorly positioned fibroids, MRI provides enhanced diagnostic accuracy by enabling precise measurement of the separation between the fibroid and the uterine cavity.

The utility of serum tumor markers as diagnostic tools during pregnancy is controversial. For instance, CA-125 concentrations typically increase during the first trimester and return to baseline levels thereafter. As such, a single measurement lacks diagnostic value, whereas monitoring levels over time can offer valuable information for differential diagnosis and longitudinal assessment. A multidisciplinary approach to risk assessment is crucial, permitting a careful balance between the potential obstetric risks and the infrequent yet complicated nature of such clinical cases.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 40s, obese with a BMI of 33 and gravida 1, para 0, abortus 1, presented in the second trimester of pregnancy (gestational age: 21 weeks) due to intense, sudden abdominal pain affecting both the upper left quadrant and pelvic region (Visual Analogue Scale score of 5 at each site), accompanied by mild shortness of breath. Her routine first and second trimester screenings indicated a reduced likelihood for aneuploidy (validated by non-invasive prenatal testing) and normal fetal morphology.

She was initially evaluated in the emergency department by a multidisciplinary team. Oxygen saturation was normal (median 97%), and examination of the thorax demonstrated median heart rate of 74 bpm and blood pressure of 100/60 mm Hg. ECG findings were within normal limits. Subsequently, she was admitted to the Obstetrical and Gynecological Division. Her abdomen appeared more distended than anticipated for her gestational age, with a palpable, tender, taut-elastic mass in the entire upper left quadrant measuring 18 cm; there were no clinical signs of ascites.

On bimanual pelvic exam, a fixed, rounded, hard mass about 9 cm in diameter was identified in the lesser pelvis, protruding partially into the posterior fornix.

Pain management included intravenous paracetamol (1000 mg twice daily) and semi-upright positioning, possibly to decrease diaphragmatic pressure exerted by the gravid uterus and large abdominal masses. These interventions led to modest symptom relief without supplemental oxygen or ventilatory assistance, lowering pain scores from 5 to 1 as measured by the Visual Analogue Scale.

During hospitalization, the patient developed a mild fever. A nasopharyngeal swab PCR test for SARS-CoV-2 was negative; however, urine samples revealed a Klebsiella pneumoniae infection. Bloodwork indicated iron deficiency anemia with hemoglobin (Hb) at 9.9 g/L (normal range 12.0–16.6 g/L) and serum ferritin of 25 mcg/L (normal range 15–300 mcg/L). C-reactive protein was mildly elevated at 1.39 mg/dL (normal range 0–0.3 mg/dL). White blood cell count was 11.29×10^3 /uL (normal range 4–10×10^3 /uL) with neutrophils at 9.27×10^3 /uL (normal range 2.8–5.5×10^3 /uL), findings that were interpreted as pregnancy-associated changes.

Investigations:

Ultrasound revealed two sizable unilocular cystic lesions with smooth borders and peripheral blood flow. The superior mass measured 18×12×7 cm and extended from the left subphrenic area to the uterine fundus. The inferior mass measured 9.5×8×8 cm, appearing to be attached to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration (58×71 mm).

Laboratory analysis of tumor markers (CA 19–9, CA 15–3, CEA, β2-microglobulin, and LDH) showed values within normal parameters. CA-125, however, was raised at 41.8 U/mL (normal <35 U/mL), with a progression from 36 U/mL recorded two weeks prior, indicating a mild upward trend. Consequently, the patient underwent a non-contrast MRI, which demonstrated thick walls (1.4 cm), T1 and T2 fat-saturated sequences with heterogeneous fluid and areas of focal hyperintensity on both T1 and T2-weighted images in solid portions (refer to figure 1; only T2 fat-sat images provided). The separation of the caudal lesion was not well-defined, and it appeared continuous with the posterior leiomyoma noted previously on ultrasound. The MRI showed no evidence of lymphadenopathy.

Differential diagnosis:

On ultrasound examination, the cystic lesions resembled two large decidualised endometriomas. The more caudal cyst appeared to be attached to an intramural-subserosal fibroid located on the posterior uterine wall, which measured 58×71 mm.

Magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated two rounded, unilocular adnexal cysts featuring hypertrophied blood vessels and heterogeneous fluid contents (see figure 1). The caudal lesion was observed as adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma showing cystic degeneration, measuring 58×71 mm.

Given the considerable size of the gravid uterus and the adjacent masses, no distinct connecting pedicle or feeding vessel could be visualised for the superior lesion. This finding supported the consideration of ovarian masses—particularly endometriomas—especially in the context of mild CA-125 elevation during the second trimester of pregnancy (see discussion).

Considering the sonographic and MRI findings, the primary differential diagnoses were decidualised endometriomas and leiomyomas undergoing cystic degeneration. The modest increase in CA-125 provided support for endometrioma as the diagnosis for the upper cyst, whereas the continuity with uterine tissue pointed toward a myomatous origin for the caudal lesion. The patient's dyspnoea was thought to result from mechanical compression caused by the large abdominal masses, as there was no evidence to suggest pulmonary or cardiac impairment.

Treatment:

Because of deteriorating symptoms—including abdominal pain caused by the masses and dyspnoea associated with restrictive lung disease due to the mass size—along with the requirement for definitive diagnosis, potential for complications such as rupture, torsion, or infection, and anticipated uterine growth as the fetus developed, the patient underwent a laparotomy with a midline xiphoid-to-pubis incision at 21 weeks and 6 days following informed consent.

Initially, two samples of free peritoneal fluid were obtained for microbiological and cytological analysis. The abdominal cavity was then inspected, and fetal cardiac activity was confirmed.

Unexpectedly, the superior mass was identified as a large, pedunculated, atypical cystic and tense-elastic uterine leiomyoma with marked vascularity; it was initially twisted and leaking, likely accounting for the fever experienced during hospitalization (refer to figure 2). Consequently, the surgical team proceeded to carefully detach the mass from its uterine attachment using a double clamp (see figure 2). After transecting the stalk, the exposed base was meticulously secured with a single absorbable suture. The inferior mass, originating from the uterine torus, was retained to reduce obstetric risk; this mass was immobile, exhibited a smooth exterior, and possessed a soft, cystic consistency suggestive of a substantial intramural-subserosal leiomyoma.

The operation took 2 hours and 25 minutes. The patient received intraoperative prophylaxis with 100 mg of intramuscular progesterone, followed by daily doses of 100 mg intramuscularly until hospital discharge. Subsequently, hydroxyprogesterone caproate (341 mg, 1 ampoule intramuscularly every 3 days per the package instructions) was administered until the planned delivery. Intraoperative antibiotic prophylaxis involved cefazolin 2 g intravenously, succeeded by postoperative treatment with cefazolin 1 g intravenously three times daily plus clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily during hospitalization, selected in accordance with urine culture findings (Klebsiella pneumoniae highly sensitive to cephalosporins and clarithromycin).

Outcome and follow-up:

Postoperatively, the patient's anaemia worsened (Hb 7.5 g/L, HCT 24.3%) and iron deficiency persisted, necessitating intravenous administration of ferric carboxymaltose (1 g) and two successful blood transfusions, which improved her Hb to 10.0 g/L and HCT to 30.4%. She was discharged 10 days after surgery without complications and began rigorous obstetric follow-up every three weeks. Fetal growth (86th percentile, as per WHO fetal growth chart calculator, https://srhr.org/fetalgrowthcalculator/#/) and amniotic fluid index were monitored.

Analysis of the peritoneal fluid was negative for both culture and cytology. Pathologic evaluation demonstrated benign uterine leiomyomas and ischaemic necrosis near the region of the twisted pedicle.

The lower myoma was expected to significantly hinder the descent of the fetal head during the second stage of labor; therefore, an elective caesarean section was performed at the same surgical site once the fetus reached 37 weeks gestation. This approach reduced the risk of uterine scar rupture and resulted in an excellent neonatal outcome.

At present, the patient is asymptomatic regarding myomas, and the remaining pelvic myoma has decreased slightly in size to 7.5 cm. She has been breastfeeding successfully, and due to partial postpartum reduction in leiomyomas, absence of symptoms, and completion of her family, she opts to defer any further surgery for now. The infant is thriving.

Discussion:

Myomas can often be misdiagnosed as adnexal masses: the incidence during pregnancy is 0.3–10.1% and 1–6%, respectively .13

In this case, the initial assessment identified abdominal masses resembling two large decidualised endometriomas, with the exception of a caudal solid portion of the pelvic mass that appeared similar to a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration, measuring 58×71 mm, as observed on both ultrasonography and MRI. Due to a lack of established management protocols for pelvic masses during pregnancy, the multidisciplinary team judged that elective surgical intervention might reduce the likelihood of preterm birth.

Throughout pregnancy, serum CA-125 concentrations typically display two peaks: one during the first trimester (median 23 U/mL, p<0.00001) and another in the third trimester (median 21 U/mL, p<0.001), both higher than second trimester values (median 14.0 U/mL); however, the patient in the current report exhibited a level of 41.8 U/mL, exceeding the median for the second trimester.

CA-125 serves primarily as a tumor marker for ovarian malignancy (standard threshold ≥35 U/mL), particularly in women over 50 years old (with improved area under the curve, positive predictive value, sensitivity, and specificity). Nonetheless, elevated CA-125 can be associated with numerous benign processes such as endometriosis and adenomyosis (>19 U/mL), pelvic inflammatory disease, menstruation, both early and late stages of pregnancy (median values 23 and 21 U/mL, respectively), risk of spontaneous abortion, as well as myoma (threshold <19 U/mL).57

The clinical decision was primarily influenced by several factors: the presence of large, highly vascular masses exhibiting both solid and cystic characteristics, which posed an immediate risk for complications such as rupture, torsion, or infection; deterioration in the patient’s symptoms, including increased pain and shortness of breath; the likelihood of further mass enlargement that could predispose to hypoxia, tissue necrosis, and degeneration; a mild elevation in tumor markers (CA-125 = 41.8 U/mL); and restricted space which could impede normal uterine growth.

Because of the uncertain nature and potential neoplastic etiology of the masses, a non-contrast MRI was conducted following the ultrasound examination to improve diagnostic precision. Current guidelines recommend avoiding gadolinium-enhanced MRI during pregnancy unless absolutely necessary for conditions involving rheumatological, inflammatory, or infiltrative dermatological disorders, or in cases where there is risk of stillbirth or neonatal death. Accordingly, the multidisciplinary team opted for a non-enhanced MRI, ensuring the use of a magnet not exceeding 1.5-Tesla, as this modality is considered sufficiently safe after the first trimester.

In this case, the multidisciplinary team—including surgeons, gynecologists, and obstetricians—decided on a longitudinal laparotomic surgical approach after securing the patient’s written informed consent. At the time of surgery, the superior lesion was found to have a pedicle, which represented a considerable risk for an acute, typical complication. As a result, the decision was made to restrict the intervention to an upper myomectomy. Intraoperative fetal viability was monitored continuously and intermittently using transvaginal ultrasonography, and progesterone was administered prophylactically during surgery.

A substantial multicenter study by Zhao et al underscores the necessity of documenting the fetal heart rate before, during, and after surgical intervention.

Typically, surgical intervention is postponed until after delivery, with a preference for medical management or expectant observation, although Sakinci et al recently published findings from a tertiary center indicating no statistically significant difference between women who underwent cesarean section (CS) with concomitant myomectomy and those without myomas undergoing only CS, regarding preoperative and postoperative hemoglobin levels or the need for blood transfusion (p >0.05). However, duration of hospitalization and surgery were significantly longer for patients who had CS with myomectomy (p =0.001 and p =0.001, respectively). The average myoma size was 8.3 ± 4.1 cm, and there was a statistically significant positive correlation between myoma size and length of hospital stay (p =0.01).

The same investigation identified a significant inverse relationship between myoma size and the gestational age at delivery (p = 0.035).

Uterine leiomyomas exhibit a non-linear pattern of size variation throughout pregnancy and the postpartum period. Specifically, leiomyoma dimensions expand by approximately 6.90% weekly: 6.12% (p<0.001) occurs between the first and second trimesters, while a change of 3.10%±0.39% is observed from the second to the third trimesters (p<0.05). Consequently, the increase in size is substantially greater during the initial trimester compared to later stages of pregnancy. Conversely, myoma size diminishes significantly during the third trimester and up to 6 weeks postpartum, with a mean reduction of 44.25%±36.05% (p<0.05).

These studies underscore the necessity for a meticulous ultrasonographic assessment of uterine myomas, evaluating parameters such as size, position, quantity, and proximity to the placenta prior to surgical intervention.

For uterine myomas identified during gestation, non-surgical management is generally preferred owing to the elevated risks of severe hemorrhage, pregnancy loss, preterm birth, and uterine rupture associated with surgical intervention. Nonetheless, myomectomy may be warranted in situations involving complicated masses, such as torsion of a pedunculated myoma, or ongoing symptoms unresponsive to conservative measures. Surgical intervention should be performed as efficiently as possible to maintain the pregnancy, and a laparoscopic approach—ideally using the open Hasson method—is suitable during early gestation (first and early second trimesters), with smaller masses and where the myoma is not intramural. This technique is associated with improved outcomes, specifically regarding maternal morbidity and the potential for vaginal delivery. Nonetheless, as illustrated by the current case, there is a prevalent tendency toward selecting laparotomy over laparoscopy for larger, multiple, intramural, or critically located myomas, which may contribute to a higher frequency of cesarean sections due to a sizeable myoma affixed to the posterior aspect of the isthmic region of the uterus, thereby impeding vaginal delivery.
