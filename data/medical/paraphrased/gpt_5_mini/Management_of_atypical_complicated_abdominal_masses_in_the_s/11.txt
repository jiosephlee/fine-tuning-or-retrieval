Title: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

Rosalba De Nola, Emma Bassi, Cataldo Paletta, Mario Vicino

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her 40s, who had not previously given birth, developed abdominal pain and shortness of breath while in her second trimester of pregnancy. Ultrasonography identified two sizable abdominal masses: the superior lesion occupied the entire left subphrenic region and extended up to the uterine fundus, while the inferior mass was located within the pouch of Douglas. The patient was managed with laparotomy, during which the caudal pelvic mass was left untouched in situ. She received routine obstetric monitoring and later underwent a scheduled caesarean section, achieving favorable outcomes for both mother and child. For pregnant patients with large, symptomatic masses, it is crucial to perform a thorough assessment—including bimanual examination and safe imaging studies such as ultrasonography and non-contrast MRI. Surgical intervention should be reserved for situations involving suspicion of malignancy or when clinical complications arise, with the objective of protecting the pregnancy and minimizing unnecessary procedures. The choice of delivery method should be individualized, taking into account the size, number, and anatomical positioning of uterine masses.

Background:

The evaluation and treatment of unusual or symptomatic abdominal masses in pregnancy are associated with considerable clinical difficulties. Diagnostic modalities are limited primarily to physical examination, ultrasound (US) with color Doppler, and non-contrast magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Preoperative US is especially helpful for detecting symptomatic uterine leiomyomas situated on the anterior surface of the enlarging uterus, regardless of whether they are submucosal or pedunculated. MRI offers enhanced diagnostic capability for identifying multiple, sizable, intramural, or posterior leiomyomas by accurately assessing their distance from the uterine cavity.

The utility of serum tumor markers in diagnosing such cases during pregnancy is still controversial. CA-125 levels, in particular, are known to physiologically increase in the first trimester and return to baseline later in gestation. Consequently, single measurements lack diagnostic value, while repeat assessments may offer helpful information for differential diagnosis and ongoing monitoring. Appropriate management hinges on comprehensive multidisciplinary evaluation to judiciously weigh obstetric risks given the infrequency and complexity of these conditions.

Case presentation:

A woman in her forties with obesity (body mass index 33), gravida 1 para 0 abortus 1, presented during her second trimester at 21 weeks’ gestation with intense, sudden-onset abdominal pain localized to both the left upper quadrant and pelvic region (Visual Analogue Scale score of 5 at each location), accompanied by mild shortness of breath. First and second trimester routine prenatal screenings indicated low aneuploidy risk (verified by non-invasive prenatal testing), and fetal anatomical evaluation was unremarkable.

Initially, she presented to the emergency department for comprehensive multidisciplinary assessment: oxygen saturation was within normal limits (median 97%), and thoracic examination showed a regular heart rate (median 74 bpm) along with a blood pressure reading of 100/60 mm Hg (median). The ECG findings were unremarkable. Subsequently, she was admitted to the Obstetrical and Gynaecological Department. Abdominal inspection revealed greater distension than anticipated for her gestational age, with a tender, swollen, tense-elastic mass in the entire upper left quadrant, measuring 18 cm, but without clinical evidence of ascites.

The bimanual vaginal examination showed the presence of a fixed mass in the lesser pelvis of about 9 cm, rounded and hard, that partially bulged at the posterior fornix.

Administration of analgesics (paracetamol 1000 mg intravenously twice daily) along with a semi-orthostatic posture—intended to potentially reduce diaphragmatic pressure from the gravid uterus and extensive abdominal masses—led to a modest alleviation of symptoms in the absence of supplemental oxygen or ventilatory intervention, with pain intensity decreasing from 5 to 1 on the Visual Analogue Scale.

While hospitalized, the patient developed a mild febrile episode. Consequently, a nasopharyngeal swab PCR for severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 was conducted and yielded a negative result. However, urine cultures revealed a Klebsiella pneumoniae infection. Laboratory evaluation demonstrated iron deficiency anaemia, with a haemoglobin concentration of 9.9 g/L (reference range 12.0–16.6 g/L) and serum ferritin at 25 mcg/L (reference 15–300 mcg/L); there was also a mild elevation in C reactive protein at 1.39 mg/dL (normal 0–0.3 mg/dL), and increased neutrophil count (total white blood cells 11.29×10^3/uL [normal 4–10×10^3/uL]; neutrophils 9.27×10^3/uL [normal 2.8–5.5×10^3/uL]), in keeping with changes commonly observed in pregnancy.

Investigations:

Ultrasound revealed two sizable, unilocular cystic lesions characterized by smooth borders and peripheral blood flow. The superior lesion measured 18×12×7 cm and spanned from the left subphrenic area down to the uterine fundus. The inferior lesion, measuring 9.5×8×8 cm, appeared to be attached to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration (dimensions 58×71 mm).

Tumor marker evaluation—including CA 19–9, CA 15–3, CEA, β2-microglobulin, and LDH—showed values within normal ranges; however, CA-125 was raised at 41.8 U/mL (reference <35 U/mL), and this value had increased from 36 U/mL measured two weeks previously, reflecting a mild upward trend. An MRI without contrast was subsequently performed, revealing lesions with thickened walls (1.4 cm), inhomogeneous fluid signal on T1 and T2 fat-sat sequences, and areas of focal hyperintensity within solid components on both T1 and T2 weighted images (refer to figure 1; only T2 fat-sat sequences provided). The interface of the caudal mass was indistinct and appeared continuous with the posterior leiomyoma previously described by ultrasound. MRI did not show any lymphadenopathy.

Differential diagnosis:

On ultrasonographic assessment, the cystic lesions resembled two large decidualized endometriomas, with the more inferior cyst noted to be adherent to an intramural-subserosal leiomyoma located on the posterior uterine wall, which measured 58×71 mm.

Magnetic resonance imaging revealed two well-defined, unilocular adnexal cysts characterized by hypertrophic vessels and heterogeneous fluid content (see figure 1); the caudal cyst appeared to be attached to a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration, measuring 58×71 mm.

Because the enlarged pregnant uterus and substantial masses limited visualization, neither a connecting stalk nor a vessel supplying the upper mass could be detected, favoring an ovarian origin—particularly endometriomas—given the mildly elevated CA-125 observed during the second trimester (refer to discussion).

The ultrasound and MRI findings suggested a differential including decidualized endometriomas and cystically degenerating leiomyomas: the minor CA-125 increase supported an endometrioma diagnosis for the upper cystic mass, while the lower mass’s anatomic continuity with the uterus pointed toward a myomatous source. The patient's dyspnoea was believed to result from physical compression by the large intra-abdominal masses, in the absence of cardiac or pulmonary pathology.

Treatment:

Given the progression of symptoms—including abdominal pain associated with the mass and dyspnoea from restrictive lung disease attributable to the increasing size of the abdominal masses—as well as the need for definitive diagnosis, potential complications such as rupture, torsion, and infection, and the anticipated uterine expansion as the fetus developed, surgical intervention was undertaken. After obtaining informed consent, a laparotomy utilizing a longitudinal xipho-pubic incision was performed at 21 weeks and 6 days of gestation.

First, we collected two samples of the free peritoneal fluid for cultural and cytological purposes. Then, we explored the abdominal cavity, verifying the presence of fetal cardiac activity.

Unexpectedly, the cranial mass was identified as a large, pedunculated, atypical cystic and tense-elastic uterine leiomyoma that was highly vascular, previously torsed and leaking, which likely accounted for the febrile episodes observed during hospitalization (refer to figure 2). Consequently, the surgical team opted for a careful excision of the mass at its uterine base, employing a double clamping technique (see figure 2). After transecting the stalk, the exposed base was meticulously closed with a single absorbable suture. The caudal mass, which arose from the uterine torus, was left in place to reduce the risk to the pregnancy. This lesion was immobile, had a smooth surface, and a soft consistency, resembling a sizable cystic intramural-subserosal leiomyoma.

The operation took 2 hours and 25 minutes. The patient began receiving progesterone prophylaxis at a dose of 100 mg intramuscularly during the procedure, which was continued once daily at the same dose via intramuscular injection until hospital discharge. Subsequently, she was given intramuscular caproate hydroxyprogesterone at a dose of 341 mg (one vial) every three days, following the manufacturer's instructions, up until planned delivery. For infection prevention, intraoperative cefazolin was administered at 2 g intravenously, followed postoperatively by cefazolin 1 g IV three times daily in combination with clarithromycin 500 mg orally twice daily throughout the hospital stay, as guided by urine culture results showing Klebsiella pneumoniae that was highly sensitive to both cephalosporins and clarithromycin.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following surgery, the patient experienced aggravated anaemia (Hb 7.5 g/L, HCT 24.3%), as well as ongoing iron deficiency, necessitating intravenous administration of ferric carboxymaltose at a dose of 1 g and two successful blood transfusions, resulting in improvement (Hb 10.0 g/L, HCT 30.4%). She was discharged 10 days postoperatively without any complications, and subsequently underwent close obstetric monitoring every 3 weeks, which demonstrated fetal growth at the 86th percentile based on the WHO fetal growth chart calculator (accessible at https://srhr.org/fetalgrowthcalculator/#/), as well as regular assessments of amniotic fluid index.

Cultural and cytological analyses of the peritoneal fluid yielded negative results. Histopathological assessment identified benign uterine leiomyomas and zones of ischemic necrosis adjacent to the twisted pedicle.

The presence of the caudal myoma could have significantly impeded descent of the fetal head during the expulsive stage of labor. Consequently, a planned caesarean section was carried out at the same uterine scar once the fetus reached 37 weeks gestation, reducing the likelihood of uterine scar rupture and resulting in a favorable neonatal outcome.

At present, the patient is asymptomatic with respect to her myoma, despite a minor decrease in the remaining pelvic myoma’s size to 7.5 cm. She is breastfeeding successfully, but opts against scheduling further surgical intervention at this time, given the partial postpartum shrinkage of the residual leiomyomas, her lack of clinical symptoms, and having achieved her goal of motherhood. The infant is developing normally.

Discussion:

Myomas can often be misdiagnosed as adnexal masses: the incidence during pregnancy is 0.3–10.1% and 1–6%, respectively .13

In this case, initial assessment identified abdominal masses resembling two large decidualised endometriomas, apart from a lower solid portion of the pelvic mass that was suggestive of a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma with cystic degeneration, measuring 58×71 mm, as demonstrated by both ultrasonography and MRI. Given the lack of established guidelines for managing pelvic masses during pregnancy, the multidisciplinary team determined that planned surgical intervention might reduce the likelihood of preterm birth.

Throughout pregnancy, serum CA-125 typically exhibits two elevation points: one during the first trimester (median 23 U/mL, p<0.00001) and another in the third trimester (median 21 U/mL, p<0.001), both being higher relative to levels seen in the second trimester (median 14.0 U/mL). In contrast, this particular case demonstrated a CA-125 concentration of 41.8 U/mL, which exceeds the median reported for the second trimester.

CA-125 primarily serves as a tumor marker for ovarian cancer, with the conventional threshold set at ≥35 U/mL. Its diagnostic accuracy—including higher area under the curve, positive predictive value, sensitivity, and specificity—is notably greater in women over the age of 50. Nevertheless, elevated CA-125 levels may also be observed in a variety of non-malignant conditions such as endometriosis and adenomyosis (levels exceeding 19 U/mL), pelvic inflammatory disease, menstruation, early and late stages of pregnancy (median values of 23 and 21 U/mL, respectively), increased miscarriage risk, as well as myomas (with a threshold of <19 U/mL).57

The main factors guiding the clinical approach included the identification of large, vascular masses displaying both solid and cystic features, which carried a tangible risk of adverse events such as rupture, torsion, or infection. Additionally, the patient's clinical status was concerning, with escalating pain and shortness of breath. There was potential for the masses to increase in size, elevating the likelihood of hypoxic injury, necrosis, and tissue degeneration. Laboratory evaluation showed mildly elevated tumor markers (CA-125=41.8 U/mL). Furthermore, the presence of these masses constrained the available space needed for the uterus to continue normal physiologic growth.

Given the potential neoplastic etiology and indeterminate source of the masses, the patient proceeded to a non-contrast MRI following the ultrasound to improve diagnostic precision. Current guidelines advise against gadolinium-enhanced MRI during pregnancy except when absolutely indicated, such as in rheumatologic, inflammatory, or infiltrative dermatologic disorders, or with threats of stillbirth or neonatal mortality. Consequently, the multidisciplinary team chose to conduct an unenhanced MRI, utilizing a magnet strength not exceeding 1.5 Tesla, which is regarded as relatively safe after the first trimester.

In this case, the multidisciplinary team, which included surgeons, gynecologists, and obstetricians, decided to carry out a longitudinal laparotomic surgery after obtaining the patient’s specific written informed consent. During the procedure, the upper mass was found to possess a pedicle, increasing the risk of a common acute complication. Consequently, the intervention was restricted to removal of the upper myoma only. In addition, repeated and interval transvaginal ultrasonography was used intraoperatively to track fetal viability, and prophylactic progesterone was administered during surgery.

According to a large multicentric study conducted by Zhao et al, it is mandatory to check and document the fetal heart rate before, during and after the surgery.

Typically, surgical intervention is postponed until after delivery, with a preference for medical management and observation. However, Sakinci et al recently reported findings from a tertiary care center indicating no statistically significant difference in preoperative or postoperative hemoglobin levels or the need for blood transfusions (p >0.05) between women undergoing caesarean section (CS) with myomectomy and those with only CS without myomas. Despite this, the CS myomectomy group experienced significantly increased durations of both surgery and hospitalization (p =0.001 and p =0.001, respectively). The average myoma diameter measured 8.3 ± 4.1 cm, and a statistically significant positive association was observed between myoma size and length of hospital stay (p =0.01).

The same research study also showed a statistically significant and inverse correlation between the size of the myoma and the delivery week (p = 0.035).

Uterine leiomyomas exhibit a non-linear pattern of growth during both pregnancy and the puerperium. Specifically, leiomyoma size increases weekly by 6.90%, with growth rates of 6.12% (p<0.001) observed between the first and second trimesters and 3.10%±0.39% between the second and third trimesters (p<0.05). Consequently, the rate at which uterine leiomyomas enlarge is notably greater in the first trimester compared to later stages of gestation. Conversely, during the third trimester and at 6 weeks postpartum, myoma size decreases significantly, with a mean reduction of 44.25%±36.05% (p<0.05).

These papers showed the importance of an accurate US examination of the size, location, number and distance of uterine myomas from the placenta before the surgical approach.

When uterine myomas are identified during pregnancy, management typically favors conservative measures rather than surgical intervention, owing to potential complications such as significant hemorrhage, miscarriage, preterm delivery, and uterine rupture. Nevertheless, surgical removal of the myoma (myomectomy) may be warranted in situations involving complicated lesions, such as torsion of a pedunculated myoma, or in the presence of symptoms that persist despite conservative therapy. The operation should be as brief as feasible to minimize risk to the pregnancy, and it may be undertaken laparoscopically—ideally utilizing the open Hasson technique—when gestational age is low (during the first and early second trimesters), the myoma is small in volume, and it is located in a position other than intramural. This minimally invasive approach is associated with improved outcomes with respect to maternal morbidity and the likelihood of vaginal delivery. The current case, however, demonstrates a frequent tendency to select laparotomy over laparoscopy for larger, multiple, intramural, or critically positioned myomas. Such cases, like the present one involving a large myoma attached to the posterior wall of the uterine isthmus, are at greater risk for cesarean section, as the mass may impede the passage necessary for vaginal delivery.
