Title: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

Rosalba De Nola, Emma Bassi, Cataldo Paletta, Mario Vicino

Abstract:

A woman in her 40s, who had not previously given birth, presented with abdominal pain and shortness of breath during her second trimester. Ultrasound imaging identified two sizable masses in her abdomen: the upper mass filled the entire left subphrenic region and extended up to the fundus of the uterus, while the lower mass occupied the pouch of Douglas. The patient was treated with a laparotomy, leaving the lower mass undisturbed within the pelvic cavity. She continued to have routine obstetric care and underwent a planned caesarean section, resulting in favorable outcomes for both mother and child. In instances of large, symptomatic masses occurring during pregnancy, thorough assessment—including bimanual examination and safe imaging techniques such as ultrasonography and non-contrast MRI—is crucial. Surgery should only be performed if there is suspicion of malignancy or when clinical complications arise, with the priority placed on maintaining the pregnancy and preventing unnecessary interventions. Decisions regarding the delivery method should take into account the size, number, and location of any uterine masses.

Background:

Diagnosing and treating atypical or symptomatic abdominal masses in pregnant patients is associated with notable clinical complexity. Available diagnostic modalities include physical examination, ultrasound (US) with color Doppler imaging, and non-contrast magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Preoperative US is especially useful in detecting symptomatic uterine leiomyomas situated on the anterior aspect of the pregnant uterus, regardless of whether they are submucosal or pedunculated. For multiple, large, intramural, or posterior fibroids, MRI provides more precise diagnostic information by accurately measuring the separation between the myoma and the uterine cavity.

The clinical value of serum tumor markers during pregnancy is still controversial. For instance, CA-125 concentrations naturally increase during the first trimester before returning to baseline levels. As a result, a single measurement lacks diagnostic specificity; however, serial assessment can yield helpful information for differential diagnosis and monitoring. Effective management should involve a comprehensive multidisciplinary evaluation of risks to appropriately weigh the rare and intricate nature of such cases against possible obstetric complications.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 40s, with obesity (BMI 33) and gravidity/parity status of G1P0A1, presented in her second trimester (gestational age 21 weeks) experiencing severe, sudden abdominal pain localized to the upper left quadrant and pelvic regions, each scoring 5 on the Visual Analogue Scale, accompanied by mild shortness of breath. Her routine screenings during the first and second trimesters indicated low risk for chromosomal abnormalities, as confirmed by non-invasive prenatal testing, with no anomalies identified in fetal morphology.

Upon initial admission to the emergency department for comprehensive, multidisciplinary assessment, her oxygen saturation was within normal limits (average 97%), with thoracic examination revealing a regular heart rate (average 74 bpm) and blood pressure (average 100/60 mmHg). Electrocardiogram results were unremarkable. She was subsequently moved to the Obstetrical and Gynecological unit. Abdominal examination revealed enlargement disproportionate to gestational age, notable for tender swelling and a firm, elastic mass occupying the entire upper left quadrant, measuring 18 cm, but without evidence of ascites on clinical exam.

A bimanual vaginal exam identified a non-mobile, solid, rounded mass approximately 9 cm in diameter in the lesser pelvis, protruding partly into the posterior vaginal fornix.

Pain management included paracetamol (1000 mg IV twice daily) and positioning in a semi-upright posture, theorized to alleviate diaphragmatic compression caused by the gravid uterus and sizable intra-abdominal masses; these interventions resulted in minor symptom improvement, with pain reduced from 5 to 1 on the Visual Analogue Scale, and no additional respiratory support was necessary.

During her admission, she developed a low-grade fever. A nasopharyngeal swab for SARS-CoV-2 PCR was performed, yielding negative results. Conversely, urine cultures revealed infection with Klebsiella pneumoniae. Laboratory testing showed iron deficiency anemia, with hemoglobin at 9.9 g/L (normal range 12.0–16.6 g/L) and serum ferritin at 25 mcg/L (normal range 15–300 mcg/L). Additionally, C-reactive protein was mildly elevated at 1.39 mg/dL (normal range 0–0.3 mg/dL), and leukocyte count was increased: total white blood cells at 11.29×10^3/uL (normal 4–10×10^3/uL) and neutrophils at 9.27×10^3/uL (normal 2.8–5.5×10^3/uL), which was interpreted as consistent with pregnancy-associated changes.

Investigations:

Ultrasound revealed two substantial, unilocular cystic lesions with smooth borders and peripheral blood flow. The superior lesion measured 18×12×7 cm and extended from the left subphrenic area to the uterine fundus. The inferior lesion measured 9.5×8×8 cm and appeared attached to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration (58×71 mm).

Tumour markers, including CA 19–9, CA 15–3, CEA, β2-microglobulin, and LDH, were within normal parameters, while CA-125 was increased (41.8 U/mL; reference <35 U/mL) and had risen from 36 U/mL recorded two weeks prior, indicating a mild upward trend in this serum marker. Consequently, the patient underwent a non-contrast MRI, which demonstrated thickened walls (1.4 cm), heterogeneous fluid contents on T1 and T2 fat-saturated sequences, and focal hyperintense solid elements on T1-T2 weighted imaging (refer to figure 1; only T2 fat-sat images are provided). The boundary between the caudal mass was indistinct, suggesting continuity with the previously assessed posterior leiomyoma seen on ultrasound. No lymphadenopathy was observed in the MRI study.

Differential diagnosis:

During ultrasonographic assessment, the cystic lesions appeared similar to two large, decidualised endometriomas; the more caudal cyst was noted to be attached to a posteriorly located intramural-subserosal leiomyoma measuring 58×71 mm.

Magnetic resonance imaging revealed two well-circumscribed, unilocular adnexal cysts exhibiting hypertrophied vasculature and heterogeneous fluid contents (refer to figure 1). The caudal lesion was visualised as being adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma displaying cystic degeneration, also measuring 58×71 mm.

Given the considerable size and ‘steric bulk’ of the gravid uterus, as well as the substantial mass dimensions, no obvious connecting pedicle or feeding vessels could be discerned for the upper cystic lesion, supporting the provisional diagnosis of ovarian masses—particularly endometriomas—supported by a mild CA-125 elevation noted during the second trimester (see discussion).

Differential diagnoses, informed by ultrasonographic and MRI findings, encompassed decidualised endometriomas and cystic degenerating leiomyomas. The minor rise in CA-125 levels was more consistent with endometrioma in the upper cyst, whereas tissue continuity with the uterus was more indicative of a myomatous etiology for the lower lesion. The patient’s dyspnoea was ascribed to the physical mass effect of the sizable abdominal lesions, with no evidence suggestive of pulmonary or cardiac impairment.

Treatment:

Given the deterioration of her clinical status (abdominal pain linked to the mass and dyspnoea related to restrictive lung disease due to the mass size), the need for a definitive diagnosis, the potential for adverse events (such as rupture, torsion, or infection), and the anticipation of continued uterine growth in tandem with fetal development, the patient underwent a laparotomy with a longitudinal incision from the xiphoid to the pubic symphysis at 21 weeks and 6 days after providing informed consent.

Initially, two samples of free peritoneal fluid were collected for microbiological and cytological evaluation. This was followed by an exploration of the abdominal cavity, during which fetal cardiac activity was confirmed.

Unexpectedly, the cranial mass was identified as a large, pedunculated uterine leiomyoma with atypical cystic features and a taut-elastic consistency. It showed marked vascularity and was initially noted to be twisted and leaking, which could explain the patient’s fever during hospitalization (refer to figure 2). Accordingly, the surgical team opted to gently excise the mass from its uterine base using a double clamp (see figure 2). After severing the stalk, the exposed base was meticulously closed with a single absorbable suture. The caudal mass, which originated from the uterine torus, was left untouched to reduce obstetric risks. It appeared immobile, with a smooth surface and soft consistency, resembling a sizable cystic intramural-subserosal leiomyoma.

The operative duration was 2 hours and 25 minutes. Importantly, the patient received prophylactic progesterone 100 mg intramuscularly, beginning intraoperatively and continued at 100 mg intramuscularly daily until discharge. Subsequently, intramuscular hydroxyprogesterone caproate (341 mg, 1 ampoule every three days, as specified by the manufacturer) was administered until planned delivery. Intraoperative antibiotic prophylaxis included cefazolin 2 g intravenously, followed by postoperative antibiotic therapy with cefazolin 1 g IV three times daily plus clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily during hospitalization, tailored according to urine culture sensitivities (Klebsiella pneumoniae highly susceptible to cephalosporins and clarithromycin).

Outcome and follow-up:

Following the surgical procedure, the patient's anemia worsened (Hb 7.5 g/L, HCT 24.3%) and iron deficiency remained, prompting administration of intravenous ferric carboxymaltose at a dose of 1 g and two successful blood transfusions (Hb 10.0 g/L, HCT 30.4%). She was discharged uneventfully 10 days postoperatively and began an intensive obstetric follow-up every 3 weeks, which demonstrated fetal growth at the 86th percentile per the WHO fetal growth chart calculator (https://srhr.org/fetalgrowthcalculator/#/) and normal amniotic fluid index.

Testing of the peritoneal fluid yielded negative results for both culture and cytology. Histopathological evaluation identified benign uterine leiomyomas with regions of ischemic necrosis in proximity to the twisted pedicle.

The inferiorly situated myoma would have posed a significant barrier to fetal head descent during the second stage of labor. Therefore, an elective cesarean section was scheduled using the previous surgical incision at the point when the fetus attained 37 weeks' gestation, in order to reduce the likelihood of uterine scar rupture and achieve a favorable neonatal outcome.

Currently, the patient denies any myoma-related symptoms, and the remaining pelvic myoma has decreased slightly in size to 7.5 cm. She continues to breastfeed effectively and prefers to defer further surgical intervention, considering the partial postpartum involution of the remaining leiomyomas, her lack of symptoms, and having completed her childbearing. The child is developing appropriately.

Discussion:

Myomas can often be misdiagnosed as adnexal masses: the incidence during pregnancy is 0.3–10.1% and 1–6%, respectively .13

In this case, the initial assessment identified abdominal masses resembling two large decidualised endometriomas, with the exception of a caudal solid portion of the pelvic mass, which resembled a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration and measuring 58×71 mm as seen on both ultrasound and MRI scans. Due to a lack of established treatment protocols for pelvic masses during pregnancy, the multidisciplinary team judged that planned surgical intervention might reduce the likelihood of preterm birth.

Throughout pregnancy, serum CA-125 typically displays two elevations, one in the first trimester (median 23 U/mL, p<0.00001) and another in the third trimester (median 21 U/mL, p<0.001), both higher than values found in the second trimester (median 14.0 U/mL). However, in the current case, CA-125 was measured at 41.8 U/mL, surpassing the usual median for the second trimester.

CA-125 is predominantly utilized as a tumor marker for ovarian malignancy (current standard threshold ≥35 U/mL), particularly effective in women over age 50 (offering greater area under the curve, positive predictive value, sensitivity, and specificity), though it is also associated with several non-malignant conditions including endometriosis and adenomyosis (>19 U/mL), pelvic inflammatory disease, menstrual periods, early and late pregnancy (median 23 and 21 U/mL, respectively), risk of miscarriage, and myoma (cut-off <19 U/mL).57

The primary factors guiding clinical management included the presence of large, highly vascular masses exhibiting both solid and cystic components, which posed a significant risk of complications such as rupture, torsion, or infection. Additionally, the patient's deteriorating symptoms, notably increasing pain and dyspnoea, and the potential for further mass expansion raised concerns about hypoxia, necrosis, and degeneration. Slightly elevated tumour markers (CA-125=41.8 U/mL) and the limited available space for ongoing physiological uterine growth also influenced the decision-making process.

Given the potential neoplastic nature and unclear etiology of the masses, an MRI without contrast was performed following the ultrasound to improve diagnostic accuracy. Current guidelines indicate that gadolinium-enhanced MRI should be avoided during pregnancy unless absolutely indicated, particularly in rheumatological, inflammatory, or infiltrative skin diseases, or when there is a risk of stillbirth or neonatal death. Consequently, the multidisciplinary team proceeded with a non-contrast MRI and avoided the use of equipment with a magnetic field strength exceeding 1.5-T, which is considered acceptably safe after the first trimester.

In this case, the multidisciplinary team, which included surgeons, gynecologists, and obstetricians, decided to proceed with a longitudinal laparotomic surgical approach after obtaining a specific written informed consent from the patient. Intraoperatively, the superior lesion was found to have a pedicle, which represented a considerable risk for acute and classic complications. This factor was the reason for restricting the intervention to a myomectomy of the upper mass. Continuous fetal viability was assessed by intermittent transvaginal ultrasound during the procedure, and intraoperative prophylactic progesterone was administered.

As highlighted by a substantial multicenter investigation by Zhao et al, it is required to assess and record fetal heart rate prior to, throughout, and following surgical intervention.

Typically, surgical treatment is postponed until after delivery, favoring medical or expectant management. Nonetheless, Sakinci et al, in a recent tertiary center study, found no statistically significant differences in preoperative and postoperative hemoglobin levels or transfusion requirements (p >0.05) between women who underwent both cesarean section (CS) and myomectomy, compared to those undergoing only CS without myomas. However, both hospital stay and operative time were significantly increased in the CS myomectomy group (p =0.001 and p =0.001, respectively). The average myoma diameter was 8.3 ± 4.1cm, and a statistically significant positive association between myoma size and duration of hospitalization was reported (p =0.01).

The aforementioned study identified a statistically significant negative association between myoma size and the gestational week at delivery (p = 0.035).

Uterine leiomyomas display non-linear growth patterns throughout pregnancy and the postpartum period. Specifically, their size increases by 6.90% weekly, with an increase of 6.12% (p<0.001) from the first to the second trimester, and 3.10%±0.39% between the second and third trimesters (p<0.05). This indicates that leiomyomas tend to expand more rapidly during the first trimester compared to subsequent stages of gestation. Conversely, there is a significant reduction in myoma size in the third trimester and within six weeks following delivery, with a mean decrease of 44.25%±36.05% (p<0.05).

These studies underscore the necessity for a thorough ultrasound evaluation regarding the size, anatomical location, quantity, and the spatial relationship of uterine myomas to the placenta prior to considering surgical intervention.

When uterine myomas are identified during pregnancy, conservative management is typically favored over surgical procedures, given the elevated risks of serious hemorrhage, miscarriage, premature labor, and uterine rupture. Nonetheless, myomectomy may be warranted for cases involving complicated lesions, such as torsion of a pedunculated myoma, or ongoing symptoms unresponsive to conservative measures. The operative intervention should be minimized in duration to safeguard pregnancy, and laparoscopic surgery can be undertaken, ideally utilizing the open Hasson technique, especially in early gestation (first and early second trimester), when the myoma is smaller and located in a specific (non-intramural) area. This strategy is associated with improved maternal outcomes and increased likelihood of vaginal delivery. Still, this case highlights a typical bias toward opting for laparotomy rather than laparoscopy in situations with large, multiple, intramural, or anatomically critical myomas, which may increase the incidence of cesarean section, due to the presence of a substantial myoma adhering to the posterior wall at the isthmic region of the uterus, obstructing vaginal birth.
