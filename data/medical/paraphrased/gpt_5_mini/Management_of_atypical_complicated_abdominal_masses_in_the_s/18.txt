Title: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

Rosalba De Nola, Emma Bassi, Cataldo Paletta, Mario Vicino

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her 40s, with no prior pregnancies, presented in her second trimester with complaints of abdominal pain and shortness of breath. Ultrasound imaging identified two sizable intra-abdominal masses: the superior lesion filled the entire left subphrenic region and extended to the fundus of the uterus, while the inferior lesion occupied the pouch of Douglas. The patient underwent a laparotomy, leaving the caudal mass undisturbed within the pelvis. Thereafter, she received routine antenatal care and proceeded to an elective caesarean section, which led to optimal outcomes for both mother and neonate. When evaluating large symptomatic masses during gestation, thorough assessment—including bimanual palpation and safe imaging like ultrasonography and non-contrast MRI—is crucial. Surgical intervention should be reserved for instances where malignancy is suspected or clinical issues arise, prioritizing the maintenance of pregnancy and avoiding unnecessary procedures. Delivery decisions should take into account the size, count, and anatomical placement of any uterine masses.

Background:

Identifying and treating atypical or symptomatic abdominal masses during gestation poses notable clinical difficulties. Diagnostic modalities are largely confined to physical examination, ultrasound (US) with color Doppler, and MRI without contrast. Preoperative US is especially helpful for detecting symptomatic uterine leiomyomas on the anterior aspect of the pregnant uterus, including submucosal or pedunculated variants. MRI, in contrast, demonstrates enhanced accuracy when evaluating multiple, sizable, intramural, or posterior leiomyomas, as it is able to delineate the myoma's proximity to the uterine cavity with precision.

The utility of serum tumor markers in pregnancy is still contentious. Specifically, CA-125 typically elevates during the first trimester and returns to baseline thereafter, limiting the diagnostic value of single measurements. However, serial assessments may offer valuable insights for differentiation and monitoring. Management necessitates thorough multidisciplinary evaluation to navigate the obstetric risks inherent to these uncommon and complex cases.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 40s with obesity (BMI 33) and a history of one pregnancy, no live births, and one abortion (G1P0A1) presented at 21 weeks’ gestation during her second trimester due to intense, sudden-onset abdominal pain localized to the upper left quadrant and pelvic region (Visual Analogue Scale score of 5 at both locations), as well as mild shortness of breath. Routine screening during the first and second trimesters had shown a low probability of chromosomal abnormalities (as corroborated by non-invasive prenatal testing) and normal fetal anatomy.

Initially, she presented to the emergency department for a comprehensive multidisciplinary assessment: her oxygen saturation was within normal limits (median 97%), physical examination of the chest showed a regular heart rate (median 74 bpm) and blood pressure (median 100/60 mm Hg). Electrocardiogram results were unremarkable. Subsequently, she was moved to the Obstetrics and Gynaecology Department. Her abdomen appeared more distended than typically seen at her stage of pregnancy, with a tender, taut-elastic swelling in the entire upper left quadrant, measuring 18 cm, and no clinical evidence of ascites.

The bimanual vaginal examination showed the presence of a fixed mass in the lesser pelvis of about 9 cm, rounded and hard, that partially bulged at the posterior fornix.

Administration of analgesics (paracetamol 1000 mg intravenously twice daily) along with maintaining the patient in a semi-orthostatic position—which was intended to reduce pressure on the diaphragm caused by the gravid uterus and sizable abdominal masses—led to a modest improvement in symptoms without the need for supplemental oxygen or ventilatory assistance. Pain severity, as measured by the Visual Analogue Scale, decreased from 5 to 1.

While hospitalized, the patient developed a low-grade fever. A nasopharyngeal swab for severe acute respiratory syndrome Coronavirus 2 by PCR testing returned negative results; however, urine culture identified an infection with Klebsiella pneumoniae. Laboratory findings revealed iron deficiency anaemia, with a haemoglobin (Hb) level of 9.9 g/L (reference range 12.0–16.6 g/L) and serum ferritin at 25 mcg/L (reference 15–300 mcg/L). Additionally, C-reactive protein was mildly elevated at 1.39 mg/dL (normal range 0–0.3 mg/dL). There was also a rise in neutrophil count: total white blood cells measured at 11.29×10^3/uL (normal 4–10×10^3/uL), with neutrophils at 9.27×10^3/uL (normal 2.8–5.5×10^3/uL), findings attributed to pregnancy-related physiological changes.

Investigations:

Ultrasound revealed two sizeable, unilocular cystic lesions exhibiting smooth borders and peripheral blood flow. The upper lesion measured 18×12×7 cm and extended from the left subphrenic space down to the uterine fundus. The lower lesion, measuring 9.5×8×8 cm, appeared attached to a posterior intramural-subserosal leiomyoma with cystic degeneration measuring 58×71 mm.

Evaluation of tumour markers (CA 19–9, CA 15–3, CEA, β2-microglobulin, and LDH) yielded values within normal limits, but the CA-125 was found to be raised at 41.8 U/mL (reference value <35 U/mL), and it had increased from a value of 36 U/mL noted two weeks earlier, reflecting a slight upward trend in this serum marker. Subsequently, the patient underwent a non-contrast MRI, which demonstrated thickened walls (1.4 cm) and inhomogeneous fluid signal on T1- and T2-weighted fat-suppressed images, along with areas of T1/T2 hyperintense solid components (see figure 1, T2 fat-sat sequences presented). The demarcation of the caudal lesion was indistinct and appeared continuous with the previously identified posterior leiomyoma on ultrasound. MRI did not reveal any evidence of lymphadenopathy.

Differential diagnosis:

On ultrasonography, the cystic lesions resembled two large decidualized endometriomas; the more caudal cyst appeared to be attached to an intramural-subserosal leiomyoma located on the posterior uterine wall, measuring 58×71 mm.

Magnetic resonance imaging revealed two rounded, unilocular adnexal cysts displaying thickened vessels and heterogeneous internal fluid (refer to figure 1). The lower mass appeared contiguous with a posterior uterine intramural-subserosal myoma demonstrating cystic degeneration, with dimensions of 58×71 mm.

Because of the significant size of the gravid uterus and the large lesions present, a connecting stalk or any vascular supply to the upper mass could not be detected, thereby suggesting an ovarian origin—particularly endometriomas—given the mild elevation of CA-125 noted in the second trimester (see discussion).

On ultrasound and MRI, the features supported a differential diagnosis between decidualised endometriomas and cystic degenerating leiomyomas: the modest rise in CA-125 levels made an endometrioma more likely for the superior cystic lesion, whereas its apparent continuity with uterine tissue pointed toward a leiomyomatous etiology for the lower mass. The patient’s dyspnoea was ascribed to the physical pressure from these extensive abdominal lesions, as no cardiopulmonary impairment was identified.

Treatment:

Given the progression of her symptoms—including abdominal pain associated with the mass and dyspnoea from restrictive lung disease secondary to the mass size—as well as the need for definitive diagnosis, the potential for complications such as rupture, torsion, and infection, and the anticipation of further uterine enlargement with fetal development, the patient consented to and subsequently underwent a laparotomy via a longitudinal xiphoid-to-pubic incision at 21 weeks and 6 days gestation.

First, we collected two samples of the free peritoneal fluid for cultural and cytological purposes. Then, we explored the abdominal cavity, verifying the presence of fetal cardiac activity.

Unexpectedly, the cranial lesion was identified as a large pedunculated leiomyoma of the uterus, exhibiting atypical cystic features and a firm, elastic consistency; it was highly vascular, showed initial torsion and evidence of leakage, factors that likely accounted for the patient's febrile episodes during hospitalization (refer to figure 2). Consequently, the surgical team opted for cautious removal of the mass from its uterine base using a double clamping technique (see figure 2). After the pedicle was transected, the exposed origin was meticulously secured with a single absorbable suture. The caudal lesion, arising from the uterine torus, was deliberately left intact to reduce obstetric complications. This lesion demonstrated immobility, a smooth external surface, and a soft, cystic intramural-subserosal leiomyoma-like consistency.

The procedure was completed in 2 hours and 25 minutes. Progesterone prophylaxis at a dose of 100 mg was administered intramuscularly beginning during the operation and subsequently continued daily at the same dosage until the patient was discharged. Thereafter, the patient was treated with intramuscular caproate hydroxyprogesterone (341 mg per vial, administered intramuscularly every three days as specified in the package insert) up to the planned delivery. For antibiotic prophylaxis, cefazolin 2 g was given intravenously during surgery, followed by postoperative antibiotics comprising cefazolin 1 g intravenously three times daily plus clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily throughout the hospitalization. This regimen was chosen in accordance with the urine culture antibiogram, which showed Klebsiella pneumoniae highly susceptible to cephalosporins and clarithromycin.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following the procedure, the patient's anemia worsened (hemoglobin 7.5 g/L, hematocrit 24.3%) and iron deficiency persisted, necessitating intravenous administration of ferric carboxymaltose (1 g) along with two successful blood transfusions (resulting in hemoglobin 10.0 g/L, hematocrit 30.4%). She was discharged without any complications 10 days postoperatively and subsequently underwent close obstetric monitoring every three weeks, during which assessments demonstrated fetal growth (86th percentile per the WHO fetal growth chart calculator, accessible at https://srhr.org/fetalgrowthcalculator/#/) and evaluation of the amniotic fluid index.

Both culture and cytology of the peritoneal fluid yielded negative results. Histopathological analysis identified benign uterine leiomyomas and zones of ischemic necrosis adjacent to the torsed pedicle.

The inferiorly located myoma would have significantly obstructed descent of the fetal head in the second stage of labor. Therefore, a scheduled cesarean delivery was carried out through the existing surgical incision when the fetus reached 37 weeks’ gestation, thereby reducing the potential for uterine scar rupture and resulting in an optimal neonatal outcome.

At present, the patient experiences no symptoms attributable to the myoma, although the remaining pelvic myoma has diminished modestly to 7.5 cm in diameter. She has been breastfeeding successfully; however, she opts not to proceed with additional surgery at this time, given the partial postpartum regression of the leiomyomas, her lack of clinical symptoms, and having achieved her desired maternal goals. The infant is developing normally.

Discussion:

Myomas can often be misdiagnosed as adnexal masses: the incidence during pregnancy is 0.3–10.1% and 1–6%, respectively .13

In this case, the first assessment revealed large abdominal masses resembling two significantly enlarged decidualized endometriomas. However, the lower solid section of the pelvic mass resembled a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration, measuring 58×71 mm as observed on both ultrasound and MRI. Since there are no established protocols for managing pelvic masses during pregnancy, the multidisciplinary team determined that planned surgery might reduce the likelihood of preterm labor.

Throughout pregnancy, serum CA-125 levels typically exhibit two elevations: the first occurring in the initial trimester (median 23 U/mL, p<0.00001) and the second during the third trimester (median 21 U/mL, p<0.001), both significantly higher compared to the median concentration in the second trimester (14.0 U/mL). In contrast, the patient described here demonstrated a CA-125 level of 41.8 U/mL, which exceeds the usual median seen in the second trimester.

CA-125 primarily serves as a tumor marker for ovarian carcinoma, with the commonly accepted threshold being ≥35 U/mL, particularly in women over 50 years of age, where it demonstrates greater area under the curve, sensitivity, specificity, and positive predictive value. Nevertheless, elevated CA-125 levels can also be associated with various benign conditions, including endometriosis and adenomyosis (values typically exceeding 19 U/mL), pelvic inflammatory disease, menstruation, early and late pregnancy (median levels of 23 and 21 U/mL, respectively), increased risk of miscarriage, as well as myoma (often with a cut-off below 19 U/mL).57

The main factors influencing the clinical management included the identification of large, highly vascular masses exhibiting both solid and cystic characteristics, which presented a significant risk for complications such as rupture, torsion, or infection. Additionally, the patient's symptoms, including increasing pain and dyspnoea, potential for further mass enlargement with increased likelihood of hypoxia, necrosis, and degeneration, marginal elevation of tumor markers (CA-125 at 41.8 U/mL), and limited intra-abdominal space which could compromise future physiological uterine expansion, were considered.

Because the masses had an unclear etiology and a neoplastic process could not be excluded, an MRI without contrast was conducted following the ultrasound to improve diagnostic assessment. Current guidelines advise against the use of gadolinium contrast in MRI during pregnancy unless there is a compelling indication, such as rheumatologic, inflammatory, or infiltrative skin diseases, or concerns regarding stillbirth or neonatal mortality. As a result, the multidisciplinary team opted for a non-contrast MRI, steering clear of using MRI machines stronger than 1.5 Tesla, a threshold deemed relatively safe beyond the first trimester.

In this case, the multidisciplinary team—including surgeons, gynecologists, and obstetricians—decided to undertake a longitudinal laparotomy following the acquisition of specific written informed consent from the patient. During the operation, the superior lesion was found to have a pedicle, which substantially increased the likelihood of a classic acute complication. This finding justified restricting the intervention to an upper myomectomy. Additionally, fetal viability was continuously assessed intraoperatively with sequential and periodic transvaginal ultrasound examinations, and intraoperative progesterone was given as a preventive measure.

According to a large multicentric study conducted by Zhao et al, it is mandatory to check and document the fetal heart rate before, during and after the surgery.

Typically, surgical intervention is postponed until after delivery, with a preference for medical management and watchful waiting. However, Sakinci et al recently reported findings from a tertiary care study indicating no statistically significant differences in preoperative and postoperative hemoglobin levels or blood transfusion requirements (p >0.05) between women who underwent caesarean section (CS) with myomectomy and those with no myoma who had only CS. Despite this, both surgery time and length of hospitalization were notably increased in the CS myomectomy group (p =0.001 for both). The average myoma diameter measured 8.3 ± 4.1 cm, and there was a statistically significant positive relationship between myoma size and duration of hospital stay (p =0.01).

The same research study also showed a statistically significant and inverse correlation between the size of the myoma and the delivery week (p = 0.035).

Uterine leiomyomas exhibit a non-linear pattern of dimensional changes throughout pregnancy and the postpartum period. Specifically, leiomyoma size increases by an average of 6.90% per week, with a 6.12% weekly increase (p<0.001) observed from the first to the second trimester and a weekly growth rate of 3.10%±0.39% between the second and third trimesters (p<0.05). Thus, the rate of growth is significantly greater in the first trimester compared to later stages of gestation. Conversely, a notable reduction in leiomyoma size occurs during the third trimester and up to 6 weeks postpartum, with a mean decrease of 44.25%±36.05% (p<0.05).

These papers showed the importance of an accurate US examination of the size, location, number and distance of uterine myomas from the placenta before the surgical approach.

When uterine myomas are identified during pregnancy, management is typically conservative rather than surgical because of the heightened risks of significant hemorrhage, pregnancy loss, preterm delivery, and uterine rupture. Nevertheless, surgical removal of the myoma (myomectomy) should be contemplated when the mass is complicated—for example, by torsion of a pedunculated myoma—or when persistent symptoms do not improve with conservative measures. Surgical intervention should be kept as brief as possible to safeguard the pregnancy, and may also be conducted laparoscopically, using an open Hasson technique, especially at lower gestational ages (first and early second trimesters), with smaller masses, or when the myoma is not intramural. This minimally invasive strategy is associated with improved outcomes, including reduced maternal morbidity and a higher likelihood of vaginal delivery. The present case, however, illustrates a common selection bias in which laparotomy is chosen over laparoscopy for larger, multiple, intramural, or critically positioned myomas, scenarios which are often linked with increased cesarean section rates due to substantial myomas, such as those adherent to the posterior wall of the uterine isthmus, potentially blocking vaginal delivery.
