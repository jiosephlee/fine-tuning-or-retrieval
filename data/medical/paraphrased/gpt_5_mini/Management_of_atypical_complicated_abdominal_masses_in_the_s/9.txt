Title: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

Rosalba De Nola, Emma Bassi, Cataldo Paletta, Mario Vicino

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her 40s, with no prior pregnancies, presented in her second trimester with abdominal pain and shortness of breath. Ultrasound identified two sizable abdominal masses: an upper mass spanning the entire left subphrenic area and extending to the fundus of the uterus, and a lower mass occupying the pouch of Douglas. The patient had a laparotomy, during which the lower (caudal) mass was not removed but left within the pelvis. Subsequent antenatal care and an elective caesarean section led to favorable outcomes for both mother and infant. When pregnant patients present with large symptomatic masses, thorough assessment via bimanual examination and appropriate, pregnancy-safe imaging such as ultrasound and non-contrast MRI is crucial. Surgery should be reserved for situations where there is a concern for malignancy or the development of clinical complications, with the primary goals being the preservation of pregnancy and avoidance of unnecessary intervention. The decision regarding the mode of delivery should be based on the characteristics and locations of uterine masses.

Background:

Evaluating and treating atypical or symptomatic abdominal masses in pregnancy poses notable clinical difficulties. Diagnostic modalities are generally restricted to physical examination, ultrasonography (US) with color Doppler, and non-contrast magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Preoperative US is especially useful in detecting symptomatic uterine leiomyomas situated on the anterior aspect of the pregnant uterus, whether they are submucosal or pedunculated. MRI surpasses other imaging methods in diagnostic accuracy for cases involving multiple, large, intramural, or posteriorly located leiomyomas, as it enables precise measurement of the distance from the myoma to the uterine cavity.

The utility of serum tumor markers in pregnancy is still contentious. For instance, CA-125 concentrations naturally increase during the first trimester and later return to baseline levels. Thus, single measurements are not sufficient for diagnosis, while serial assessments may reveal patterns valuable for distinguishing among possible diagnoses and for ongoing monitoring. Effective management mandates thorough multidisciplinary evaluation to appropriately weigh obstetric risks against the uncommon and intricate nature of these cases.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 40s with obesity (body mass index 33) and a G1P0A1 obstetric history presented to our clinic at 21 weeks gestation (second trimester) complaining of sudden, intense abdominal pain localized to both the upper left quadrant and the pelvic region (Visual Analogue Scale score of 5 at each site), accompanied by mild shortness of breath. Routine screening during the first and second trimesters indicated low risk for aneuploidies—findings corroborated by non-invasive prenatal testing—and a normal fetal morphology.

Initially, she presented to the emergency department for comprehensive assessment by multiple specialties. Her oxygen saturation was within normal limits (median 97%), and thoracic physical exam showed a heart rate of 74 bpm and blood pressure of 100/60 mm Hg, both within normal ranges. The electrocardiogram showed no abnormalities. Subsequently, she was admitted to the Obstetrical and Gynaecological Division. On examination, her abdomen appeared more distended than anticipated for her stage of pregnancy. A tender, taut-elastic mass measuring 18 cm was palpable in the entire left upper quadrant; there were no clinical indications of ascites.

The bimanual vaginal examination showed the presence of a fixed mass in the lesser pelvis of about 9 cm, rounded and hard, that partially bulged at the posterior fornix.

Analgesic management, consisting of intravenous paracetamol 1000 mg administered twice daily and placement in a semi-orthostatic position—which was presumed to minimize diaphragmatic pressure from both the gravid uterus and sizeable abdominal masses—resulted in mild symptom improvement without the need for supplemental oxygen or ventilatory assistance. Pain intensity on the Visual Analogue Scale decreased from 5 to 1.

During her hospitalization, the patient experienced a low-grade fever. A nasopharyngeal swab PCR for severe acute respiratory syndrome Coronavirus 2 returned negative; however, urine cultures identified a Klebsiella pneumoniae infection. Laboratory evaluation revealed iron deficiency anemia, with hemoglobin at 9.9 g/L (reference range 12.0–16.6 g/L) and serum ferritin at 25 mcg/L (normal values 15–300 mcg/L). C reactive protein was mildly elevated at 1.39 mg/dL (normal range 0–0.3 mg/dL). Leukocyte analysis showed leukocytosis, with total white blood cells at 11.29×10^3 /uL (reference range 4–10×10^3 /uL) and neutrophils at 9.27×10^3 /uL (reference range 2.8–5.5×10^3 /uL), findings that are consistent with physiological changes in pregnancy.

Investigations:

Ultrasound assessment revealed two sizable, single-chamber cystic lesions with smooth borders and peripheral blood flow. The superior lesion measured 18×12×7 cm, reaching from the left subphrenic area to the top of the uterus. The inferior lesion measured 9.5×8×8 cm and appeared attached to a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine fibroid exhibiting cystic changes (58×71 mm).

Laboratory tests for tumor markers (CA 19–9, CA 15–3, CEA, β2-microglobulin, and LDH) were all within standard ranges; however, the CA-125 value was raised at 41.8 U/mL (reference range <35 U/mL) and demonstrated an upward trajectory from a previous value of 36 U/mL two weeks earlier. Accordingly, there was a mild increase noted in the serum level of this marker. As a result, the patient was evaluated with MRI without contrast, which revealed masses with thickened walls (1.4 cm), heterogeneous fluid content on T1 and T2 fat-suppressed sequences, and areas of high signal on T1-T2 weighted imaging corresponding to solid components (see figure 1; only T2 fat-sat images provided). The interface between the lower mass and adjacent structures was indistinct, and it appeared to be continuous with the previously noted posterior leiomyoma seen on ultrasound. No lymphadenopathy was detected on the MRI examination.

Differential diagnosis:

On ultrasound assessment, the cystic lesions resembled two large decidualized endometriomas, with the more caudal cyst noted to be attached to a 58×71 mm intramural-subserosal leiomyoma located on the posterior uterine wall.

Magnetic resonance imaging revealed a pair of round, unilocular cysts in the adnexa, each displaying prominent blood vessels and heterogeneous internal fluid (refer to figure 1); the lower mass appeared adherent to a posterior uterine myoma with cystic degeneration measuring 58×71 mm in the intramural-subserosal region.

Because of the physical space occupied by the gravid uterus and the large size of the masses, no connecting stalk or vessel supplying the tumor was seen in the upper lesion, which supported consideration of an ovarian origin—particularly endometrioma—given the mildly elevated CA-125 in the second trimester of pregnancy (see discussion).

Imaging characteristics on ultrasound and MRI led to a differential of decidualised endometriomas versus cystic degeneration within leiomyomas: the modest CA-125 increase was more consistent with endometrioma in the upper cyst, while the lower (caudal) lesion’s continuity with uterine tissue indicated a probable myomatous nature. The patient’s dyspnoea was thought to result from physical compression by the large abdominal masses, as there was no indication of cardiopulmonary pathology.

Treatment:

Given the progression of her symptoms—including abdominal pain from the mass and dyspnoea attributable to restrictive lung disease caused by the expanding abdominal tumors—alongside the need for definitive diagnosis, concerns about possible complications such as rupture, torsion, and infection, and anticipation of further uterine expansion with fetal development, the patient consented to and subsequently underwent a laparotomy via a longitudinal xiphoid-to-pubic incision at 21 weeks and 6 days’ gestation.

First, we collected two samples of the free peritoneal fluid for cultural and cytological purposes. Then, we explored the abdominal cavity, verifying the presence of fetal cardiac activity.

Unexpectedly, the cranial mass was identified as a large pedunculated, atypical leiomyoma of the uterus exhibiting cystic features and a tense elastic consistency, with pronounced vascularity. The mass was initially twisted and leaking, which could account for the patient’s febrile episodes during hospitalization (see figure 2). Consequently, the surgical team opted to gently remove the mass at its uterine attachment using a double clamp technique (see figure 2). After transecting the stalk, the exposed base was meticulously secured with a single absorbable suture. The caudal mass, arising from the uterine torus, was left undisturbed to reduce potential obstetric complications. This mass was noted to be immobile, with a smooth external surface and a soft consistency resembling a sizeable cystic intramural-subserosal leiomyoma.

The operation was completed in 2 hours and 25 minutes. Importantly, prophylactic progesterone was administered at a dose of 100 mg intramuscularly starting during the surgery, with daily 100 mg intramuscular injections continued until the patient was discharged. Thereafter, the patient received intramuscular hydroxyprogesterone caproate (341 mg, 1 ampoule intramuscularly every 3 days as specified in the product instructions) up to the scheduled delivery. Cefazolin 2 g was given intravenously for intraoperative antibiotic prophylaxis, followed by postoperative administration of cefazolin 1 g intravenously three times daily plus clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily during the hospital admission, in accordance with the urine culture sensitivity profile (Klebsiella pneumoniae, demonstrating high susceptibility to cephalosporins and clarithromycin).

Outcome and follow-up:

Postoperatively, the patient experienced worsening anemia (Hb 7.5 g/L, HCT 24.3%) and continued iron deficiency, necessitating intravenous administration of ferric carboxymaltose (1 g) as well as two units of blood transfused, resulting in improvement (Hb 10.0 g/L, HCT 30.4%). She was discharged uneventfully 10 days after surgery and commenced close obstetric surveillance every 3 weeks, which documented fetal growth (86th percentile per the WHO fetal growth chart calculator, accessible at https://srhr.org/fetalgrowthcalculator/#/) and monitoring of the amniotic fluid index.

Cultural and cytological analyses of the peritoneal fluid returned negative results. Pathology identified benign uterine leiomyomas and zones of ischemic necrosis adjacent to the twisted pedicle.

The low-lying myoma likely would have significantly impeded descent of the fetal head during the expulsive stage of labor. For this reason, a scheduled caesarean section was performed at 37 weeks of gestation through the existing surgical scar, thereby reducing the risk of uterine scar rupture and achieving a favorable neonatal outcome.

At present, the patient experiences no myoma-associated symptoms, despite a modest decrease in the remaining pelvic myoma’s size to 7.5 cm. She has been breastfeeding successfully, but, given the partial postpartum reduction of the residual leiomyomas, lack of clinical symptoms, and having achieved her desired motherhood, she prefers not to plan any further surgical intervention at this time. The infant is developing appropriately.

Discussion:

Myomas can often be misdiagnosed as adnexal masses: the incidence during pregnancy is 0.3–10.1% and 1–6%, respectively .13

In this case, the first assessment identified abdominal masses resembling two large decidualized endometriomas, aside from the lower solid portion of the pelvic mass, which on both ultrasound and MRI measured 58×71 mm and resembled a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma with cystic degeneration. Given the lack of established protocols for managing pelvic masses during pregnancy, the multidisciplinary team evaluated that planned surgical intervention might reduce the likelihood of preterm birth.

Throughout pregnancy, serum CA-125 levels commonly exhibit two elevations: one occurring in the first trimester (median 23 U/mL, p<0.00001) and another in the third trimester (median 21 U/mL, p<0.001), both notably higher than those observed in the second trimester (median 14.0 U/mL). In contrast, this patient demonstrated a CA-125 measurement of 41.8 U/mL, which exceeds the typical median for the second trimester.

CA-125 serves primarily as a tumor marker for ovarian carcinoma, with a conventional threshold of ≥35 U/mL, particularly among women older than 50 years, in whom it demonstrates enhanced area under the curve, positive predictive value, sensitivity, and specificity. Nonetheless, elevated CA-125 levels may also be observed in various non-malignant conditions, including endometriosis and adenomyosis (levels exceeding 19 U/mL), pelvic inflammatory disease, menstruation, early and late stages of pregnancy (median concentrations of 23 and 21 U/mL, respectively), miscarriage risk, as well as in the presence of myomas (with a cut-off below 19 U/mL).57

The main factors guiding the clinical approach included the identification of large, highly vascularized lesions containing both solid and cystic areas, which posed an imminent risk of complications such as rupture, torsion, or infection. Additionally, the patient's symptoms—progressively intensifying pain and worsening shortness of breath—the potential for further enlargement of the mass increasing the likelihood of hypoxia, necrosis, and degenerative changes, mild elevation of tumor marker levels (CA-125 at 41.8 U/mL), and limited available space for ongoing physiological uterine expansion were also considered.

Given the potential neoplastic origin and indeterminate nature of the masses, the patient subsequently received a non-contrast MRI to improve diagnostic clarity following the ultrasound assessment. Current guidelines advise against gadolinium-enhanced MRI in pregnancy except when absolutely indicated, such as in the context of rheumatologic, inflammatory, or infiltrative dermatological conditions, or heightened risk for stillbirth or neonatal mortality. As a result, the multidisciplinary team proceeded with a non-contrast MRI, taking care to utilize an MRI with a magnetic field strength not exceeding 1.5 Tesla, which is generally regarded as safe beyond the first trimester.

In this case, a team consisting of surgeons, gynecologists, and obstetricians decided to carry out a longitudinal laparotomic procedure after securing formal written informed consent from the patient. Intraoperatively, the upper lesion was found to have a pedicle, which posed a considerable risk of a classic, acute complication. As a result, the surgical intervention was confined to performing only the upper myomectomy. In addition, ongoing serial and intermittent transvaginal ultrasonography was used during the operation to assess fetal viability, accompanied by the preventative use of intraoperative progesterone.

According to a large multicentric study conducted by Zhao et al, it is mandatory to check and document the fetal heart rate before, during and after the surgery.

Typically, surgical intervention is postponed until after delivery, favoring conservative and medical management. Nonetheless, a recent study by Sakinci et al performed in a tertiary center found no statistically significant differences in preoperative or postoperative hemoglobin levels, or blood transfusion requirements (p >0.05), when comparing women who had caesarean section (CS) with myomectomy to those undergoing CS alone without fibroids. However, both hospital stay and surgical time were notably longer in the CS plus myomectomy group (p = 0.001 for each). The average myoma diameter was 8.3 ± 4.1 cm, and there was a statistically significant positive correlation between fibroid size and length of hospitalization (p = 0.01).

The same research study also showed a statistically significant and inverse correlation between the size of the myoma and the delivery week (p = 0.035).

Uterine leiomyomas exhibit a non-linear pattern of size variation throughout gestation and the postpartum period. Specifically, leiomyomas enlarge at a rate of 6.90% weekly: 6.12% (p<0.001) between the first and second trimesters, and 3.10%±0.39% between the second and third trimesters (p<0.05). Consequently, the most pronounced growth occurs in the first trimester, with subsequent gestational periods showing reduced growth rates. Conversely, a significant reduction in myoma size is observed in the third trimester and 6 weeks postpartum, with an average decrease of 44.25%±36.05% (p<0.05).

These papers showed the importance of an accurate US examination of the size, location, number and distance of uterine myomas from the placenta before the surgical approach.

For uterine myomas identified during pregnancy, conservative management is generally favored over surgical intervention, as surgery carries heightened risks such as significant hemorrhage, miscarriage, preterm delivery, and uterine rupture. Nonetheless, myomectomy may become necessary in situations involving complicated lesions, such as torsion of a pedunculated myoma, or when ongoing symptoms persist without improvement under conservative measures. When indicated, surgical intervention should be kept as brief as feasible to protect the ongoing pregnancy; laparoscopy, especially with the open Hasson technique, can be considered for cases involving early gestational age (first and early second trimester), smaller tumor size, and suitable myoma location (non-intramural). This minimally invasive strategy is associated with lower maternal morbidity and an increased likelihood of vaginal delivery. However, as demonstrated in this case, a tendency exists to select laparotomy over laparoscopy when faced with larger, multiple, intramural, or critically positioned myomas. Such decisions are often influenced by the potential for these masses to increase the cesarean section rate, particularly when a large myoma is attached to the posterior isthmic uterine wall, creating a mechanical barrier to vaginal birth.
