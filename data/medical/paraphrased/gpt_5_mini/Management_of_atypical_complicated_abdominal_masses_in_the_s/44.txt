Title: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

Rosalba De Nola, Emma Bassi, Cataldo Paletta, Mario Vicino

Abstract:

Summary: A nulliparous woman in her 40s presented in her second trimester with abdominal pain and shortness of breath. Ultrasound imaging identified two substantial masses within the abdomen: one superior lesion that filled the entire left subphrenic region and extended toward the uterine fundus, and another inferior mass occupying the pouch of Douglas. The patient underwent laparotomy, leaving the lower lesion within the pelvic cavity. She continued to receive routine obstetric monitoring and later had a planned caesarean section, which resulted in optimal maternal and neonatal outcomes. For pregnant patients with large, symptomatic masses, thorough assessment—utilizing bimanual examination and safe imaging methods such as ultrasound and non-contrast MRI—is vital. Surgery should be reserved for those with suspected malignancy or complications, prioritizing fetal preservation and avoidance of unnecessary procedures. Decisions regarding delivery should be based on the size, quantity, and location of uterine masses.

Background:

The assessment and treatment of unusual or symptomatic abdominal masses in pregnancy are complex clinical issues. Diagnostic tools are restricted to physical examination, ultrasound (US) with color Doppler, and non-contrast magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Preoperative US is especially effective for locating symptomatic uterine leiomyomas along the anterior aspect of the pregnant uterus, regardless of whether they are submucosal or pedunculated in nature. In contrast, MRI delivers more precise information, particularly for numerous, large, intramural, or posterior leiomyomas, as it enables exact evaluation of the separation between the myoma and the uterine cavity.

The application of serum tumor markers for diagnosis during pregnancy is controversial. For example, CA-125 levels physiologically increase in the first trimester, then return to baseline levels later. Consequently, a single measurement lacks diagnostic value, whereas serial assessments may reveal important trends that assist with differential diagnosis and monitoring. Ideal management demands thorough multidisciplinary evaluation of risks, in order to weigh obstetric considerations against the uncommon and intricate nature of these cases.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 40s with obesity (BMI 33), gravida 1 para 0 abortus 1, presented during her second trimester (gestational age 21 weeks) reporting severe, sudden abdominal pain localized to the upper left quadrant and pelvic area (Visual Analogue Scale=5 at both locations), accompanied by mild shortness of breath. Routine screening for aneuploidies during the first and second trimesters had indicated low risk (also corroborated by non-invasive prenatal testing) and the fetal morphology was normal.

She was initially evaluated in the emergency department by a multidisciplinary team. Her oxygen saturation was within normal limits (median 97%). Examination of her thorax showed a median heart rate of 74 bpm and blood pressure at 100/60 mm Hg. The ECG results were unremarkable. Subsequently, she was moved to the Obstetrical and Gynecological Division. The abdomen appeared more distended than typical for this gestational stage, featuring a painful swelling and a firm, elastic mass spanning the upper left quadrant, measuring 18 cm, without signs indicative of ascites.

On bimanual vaginal assessment, a fixed, rounded, hard mass approximately 9 cm was noted in the lesser pelvis, partially protruding into the posterior fornix.

Treatment with intravenous paracetamol (1000 mg twice daily) combined with a semi-orthostatic posture—presumed to reduce diaphragmatic pressure from both the gravid uterus and large abdominal masses—resulted in partial symptom relief. There was a reduction in pain from 5 to 1 on the Visual Analogue Scale, and respiratory support was not required.

While hospitalized, the patient experienced a mild fever. Polymerase chain reaction testing for severe acute respiratory syndrome Coronavirus 2 via nasopharyngeal swab returned negative; however, Klebsiella pneumoniae was isolated from urine. Laboratory tests demonstrated iron deficiency anemia, with hemoglobin at 9.9 g/L (reference range 12.0–16.6 g/L) and serum ferritin of 25 mcg/L (reference range 15–300 mcg/L). There was mild elevation of C reactive protein (1.39 mg/dL, normal 0–0.3 mg/dL), and leukocytosis was noted with white blood cells at 11.29×10^3/uL (normal 4–10×10^3/uL), neutrophils at 9.27×10^3/uL (normal 2.8–5.5×10^3/uL), consistent with changes typical in pregnancy.

Investigations:

Ultrasound revealed two sizable unilocular cystic lesions with smooth borders and peripheral blood flow. The upper lesion measured 18×12×7 cm, stretching from the left subphrenic area down to the uterine fundus. The lower lesion was 9.5×8×8 cm and appeared to be attached to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma that exhibited cystic degeneration (58×71 mm).

Assessment of tumour markers (CA 19–9, CA 15–3, CEA, β2-microglobulin, and LDH) showed values within the normal range. However, CA-125 was elevated at 41.8 U/mL (normal <35 U/mL), representing an upward trend from 36 U/mL measured two weeks previously. Thus, there was a modest rise in the serum level of CA-125. Subsequently, a non-contrast MRI was performed, which revealed the masses had thick walls (1.4 cm), inhomogeneous fluid content on T1 and T2 fat-saturated sequences, and localized hyperintense solid regions on both T1 and T2 weighted imaging (refer to figure 1; T2 fat-sat sequences only are displayed). The boundary of the lower mass was indistinct, with apparent continuity with the posterior leiomyoma visualized on the prior ultrasound. MRI did not detect any lymphadenopathy.

Differential diagnosis:

On ultrasonographic assessment, the cystic lesions resembled two large decidualised endometriomas, with the more caudal cyst appearing to be attached to an intramural-subserosal leiomyoma located on the posterior uterine wall, measuring 58×71 mm.

Magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated two oval, unilocular adnexal cysts exhibiting hypertrophied vasculature and heterogenous fluid (refer to figure 1). The lower mass appeared contiguous with a posterior uterine intramural-subserosal myoma featuring cystic degeneration, also measuring 58×71 mm.

Given the ‘steric bulk’ presented by the gravid uterus alongside the considerable size of the masses, no connecting stalk or vascular supply was identified for the superior lesion, thereby directing consideration towards ovarian masses, particularly endometriomas, supported by modest CA-125 positivity during the second trimester (see discussion).

Evaluation of sonographic and MRI findings prompted a differential diagnosis including decidualised endometriomas and leiomyomas with cystic degeneration. Mildly increased CA-125 levels supported endometrioma for the upper cyst, whereas tissue continuity with the uterus indicated a leiomyomatous origin for the caudal lesion. The patient's dyspnoea was attributed to substantial abdominal mass effect, with no signs of pulmonary or cardiac impairment.

Treatment:

Owing to the deterioration of clinical status—including abdominal pain attributable to the mass effect and dyspnoea stemming from restrictive lung disease caused by the size of the abdominal tumors—along with the imperative for definitive diagnostic clarification, concern regarding possible complications such as rupture, torsion, or infection, and the potential for further uterine enlargement parallel to fetal growth, the patient consented to and underwent a laparotomy at 21 weeks plus 6 days. The procedure was performed via a longitudinal xiphoid-to-pubic incision.

Initially, two peritoneal fluid samples were obtained for both microbiological and cytological examination. The abdominal cavity was then assessed, confirming ongoing fetal cardiac activity.

Unexpectedly, the cranial mass was identified as a large, pedunculated, cystic, atypical, taut-elastic uterine leiomyoma characterized by significant vascularity. The mass appeared initially twisted and exhibited leakage, findings potentially related to the patient's fever during her hospital admission (see figure 2). Consequently, the surgical team elected to remove the mass from its uterine base with careful technique utilizing a double clamp (see figure 2). After severing the stalk, the exposed area was meticulously closed using a single absorbable suture. The caudal mass, originating at the uterine torus, was retained to minimize risks to the pregnancy. This lesion was immobile with a smooth surface and soft consistency, resembling a sizable cystic intramural-subserosal leiomyoma.

The operative procedure was completed in 2 hours and 25 minutes. The patient commenced progesterone prophylaxis with 100 mg administered intramuscularly during the operation, followed by a daily intramuscular dose of 100 mg until discharge. Subsequently, intramuscular hydroxyprogesterone caproate was administered (341 mg in a single vial every 3 days as per package instructions) and continued up to the scheduled delivery. Intraoperative antibiotic prophylaxis consisted of intravenous cefazolin 2 g; this was succeeded by postoperative antibiotics with cefazolin 1 g intravenously three times a day and clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily throughout hospitalization, guided by urine culture sensitivity results (Klebsiella pneumoniae demonstrated high sensitivity to cephalosporins and clarithromycin).

Outcome and follow-up:

Following surgery, the patient's anaemia worsened (Hb 7.5 g/L, HCT 24.3%) and iron deficiency persisted, necessitating intravenous administration of ferric carboxymaltose (1 g) and two blood transfusions, which led to improvement (Hb 10.0 g/L, HCT 30.4%). Ten days after the operation, the patient was discharged without complications and enrolled in a rigorous obstetric follow-up every 3 weeks, which revealed ongoing fetal growth (86th percentile per WHO fetal growth chart calculator, accessible at https://srhr.org/fetalgrowthcalculator/#/) and normal amniotic fluid index.

Analysis of peritoneal fluid returned negative results for both cultural and cytological assessments. Histopathological evaluation showed benign uterine leiomyomas, with regions of ischaemic necrosis adjacent to the twisted pedicle.

The caudally positioned myoma posed a significant impediment to the passage of the fetal head during the expulsive phase of delivery. Consequently, an elective caesarean section was performed at the previous surgical scar when the fetus attained 37 weeks of gestation, thereby reducing the likelihood of uterine scar rupture and achieving an excellent neonatal outcome.

At present, the patient does not experience symptoms attributable to the myoma, although the remaining pelvic myoma has decreased slightly in size to 7.5 cm. She is breastfeeding successfully but wishes to defer further surgery, owing to partial postpartum regression of the leiomyomas, absence of symptoms, and having accomplished her motherhood goals. The infant is developing normally.

Discussion:

Myomas can often be misdiagnosed as adnexal masses: the incidence during pregnancy is 0.3–10.1% and 1–6%, respectively .13

In this instance, the initial assessment revealed abdominal masses resembling two large, decidualised endometriomas. The only exception was the solid caudal portion of the pelvic mass, which appeared to correspond to a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma with cystic changes, measuring 58×71 mm, as seen on both ultrasound and MRI. Given the lack of established protocols for treating pelvic masses during pregnancy, the multidisciplinary team determined that elective surgery might reduce the risk of preterm labor.

Throughout pregnancy, serum CA-125 commonly exhibits two peaks: one during the first trimester (median 23 U/mL, p<0.00001), and another in the third trimester (median 21 U/mL, p<0.001), with lower levels observed in the second trimester (median 14.0 U/mL). Conversely, this patient demonstrated a CA-125 concentration of 41.8 U/mL, surpassing the median value typical for the second trimester.

CA-125 is predominantly employed as a tumor marker for ovarian carcinoma (current standard threshold ≥35 U/mL), particularly in women older than 50, where diagnostic accuracy, positive predictive value, sensitivity, and specificity are enhanced. However, elevated CA-125 levels can also be associated with numerous benign conditions, including endometriosis and adenomyosis (>19 U/mL), pelvic inflammatory disease, menstruation, early or late-stage pregnancy (median 23 and 21 U/mL, respectively), risk of miscarriage, and myoma (cut-off <19 U/mL).57

The main factors guiding the clinical management included the identification of large, highly vascular masses featuring both solid and cystic components, which posed an immediate risk for complications such as rupture, torsion, or infection; the patient’s deteriorating symptoms of increasing pain and shortness of breath; concerns regarding potential mass enlargement leading to heightened risks of hypoxia, necrosis, and degeneration; mildly elevated tumor markers (CA-125 at 41.8 U/mL); and limitations of pelvic space that might hinder further normal uterine growth.

Given the possible neoplastic nature of the lesions and uncertainty surrounding their origin, an MRI without contrast was performed following ultrasound to improve diagnostic clarity. Current guidelines advise against the use of gadolinium-based contrast MRI during pregnancy—except when strictly necessary for particular conditions like severe rheumatological, inflammatory, or infiltrative skin disorders, or when there is a risk of stillbirth or neonatal demise. Accordingly, the multidisciplinary team elected for a non-contrast MRI, utilizing a field strength not exceeding 1.5-Tesla, which is deemed reasonably safe after the first trimester.

In this case, the multidisciplinary team—including surgeons, gynecologists, and obstetricians—decided to proceed with a longitudinal laparotomy after obtaining explicit written informed consent from the patient. During the intraoperative assessment, the upper lesion was found to possess a pedicle, raising the likelihood of developing an acute, conventional complication. This finding justified restricting the intervention to an upper myomectomy. Additionally, continuous and periodic transvaginal ultrasound examinations were employed to evaluate fetal viability throughout the procedure, with prophylactic intraoperative progesterone administered to support pregnancy.

A large multicenter study by Zhao et al emphasizes the necessity of evaluating and documenting fetal heart rate before, during, and after operative procedures.

Typically, surgical intervention is postponed until after delivery, with a preference for conservative medical management. Nevertheless, Sakinci et al recently reported from a tertiary center that no statistically significant differences were observed between women undergoing caesarean section (CS) combined with myomectomy and those undergoing CS alone without myoma regarding both preoperative and postoperative hemoglobin levels or blood transfusion requirements (p >0.05). However, both the length of hospitalization and duration of surgery were markedly elevated among the group receiving CS with myomectomy (p = 0.001 and p = 0.001, respectively). The mean myoma diameter was 8.3 ± 4.1 cm, and a positive, statistically significant correlation was noted between myoma size and length of hospital stay (p = 0.01).

The same investigation also identified a statistically significant negative relationship between myoma size and gestational age at delivery (p = 0.035).

Uterine leiomyomas demonstrate a non-linear progression in size throughout pregnancy and the postpartum period. Specifically, myomas increase in size by an average of 6.90% per week overall, with an increment of 6.12% (p<0.001) occurring between the first and second trimesters, and 3.10%±0.39% growth from the second to third trimesters (p<0.05). As such, the rate of leiomyoma enlargement is notably greater during the first trimester than at subsequent gestational stages. Conversely, a substantial reduction in myoma size is observed in the third trimester and up to 6 weeks post-delivery (mean decrease 44.25%±36.05%, p<0.05).

These studies underscore the critical need for thorough ultrasonographic assessment of myoma size, location, number, and proximity to the placenta prior to planning surgical intervention.

When uterine myomas are detected during pregnancy, conservative management is generally favored over surgery, due to associated risks such as major hemorrhage, pregnancy loss, preterm delivery, and uterine rupture. Nonetheless, surgical removal of the myoma should be contemplated for complicated cases, such as torsion of a pedunculated leiomyoma or in situations where persistent symptoms do not improve with conservative therapy. Operative time should be minimized to help maintain the pregnancy, and laparoscopy—preferably using the open Hasson technique—may be chosen for cases with lower gestational age (first and early second trimesters), smaller mass size, and select myoma locations (excluding intramural). This technique is associated with improved maternal outcomes and the potential for vaginal delivery. Nevertheless, the case discussed here highlights a frequent selection bias favoring laparotomy over laparoscopy when confronting larger, multiple, intramural, or complexly situated myomas, which can be associated with an increased rate of cesarean section, especially when a large myoma is adherent to the posterior wall at the isthmic region, potentially obstructing vaginal delivery.
