Title: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

Rosalba De Nola, Emma Bassi, Cataldo Paletta, Mario Vicino

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her 40s, who had not previously given birth, presented during her second trimester with abdominal discomfort and shortness of breath. Ultrasound identified two substantial abdominal masses: the superior mass occupied the entire left subphrenic region and extended to the fundus of the uterus, while the inferior mass filled the pouch of Douglas. The patient underwent a laparotomy, during which the lower pelvic mass was left in place. She continued with standard obstetric surveillance and underwent a scheduled caesarean section, resulting in favorable maternal and neonatal outcomes. For large symptomatic masses discovered during pregnancy, thorough assessment with bimanual examination and safe imaging techniques, such as ultrasonography and non-contrast MRI, is critical. Surgery should be reserved for cases where there is suspicion of malignancy or if clinical complications arise, with a primary goal of preserving the pregnancy and preventing unnecessary intervention. The decision regarding delivery method should be based on the masses’ size, quantity, and anatomical distribution.

Background:

The evaluation and treatment of atypical or symptomatic abdominal masses in pregnant patients pose considerable clinical difficulties. Available diagnostic methods include physical assessment, ultrasound (US) with color Doppler, and non-contrast MRI. Preoperative US is especially effective in detecting symptomatic uterine leiomyomas located on the anterior aspect of the gravid uterus, whether submucosal or pedunculated. MRI proves to be more accurate for diagnosing multiple, large, intramural, or posterior leiomyomas, as it provides detailed information regarding the separation between the fibroid and the uterine cavity.

The utility of serum tumor markers in pregnancy remains controversial. Specifically, CA-125 levels are known to physiologically increase during the first trimester and later return to baseline. Thus, single measurements are not sufficient for diagnosis; however, serial monitoring may reveal patterns valuable for distinguishing diagnoses and tracking progress. Proper management calls for thorough multidisciplinary risk evaluation in order to carefully weigh obstetric risks against the rarity and complexity inherent to these clinical cases.

Case presentation:

An obese woman in her 40s, with a BMI of 33 and gravidity of G1P0A1, presented during her second trimester (21 weeks gestation) experiencing intense, sudden abdominal pain in the upper left quadrant and pelvis (Visual Analogue Scale score 5 for each site), accompanied by mild shortness of breath. Both her first and second trimester screenings showed low risk for aneuploidy, confirmed with non-invasive prenatal testing, and fetal structural assessment was normal.

She initially underwent comprehensive multidisciplinary evaluation in the emergency department, where oxygen saturation was within normal limits (median 97%), thoracic physical examination revealed heart rate at a median of 74 bpm, and blood pressure measured at a median of 100/60 mm Hg. ECG findings were unremarkable. Subsequently, she was transferred to the Obstetrics and Gynecology Division. The abdominal examination noted enlargement exceeding expectations for her gestational age; a tender, taut-elastic swelling occupied the entire upper left quadrant, measuring 18 cm in diameter, with no clinical evidence of ascites.

Bimanual vaginal assessment identified a non-mobile, rounded, firm mass in the lesser pelvis measuring approximately 9 cm, protruding partially towards the posterior fornix.

Symptom management included intravenous paracetamol at 1000 mg twice daily and positioning in a semi-upright posture to potentially mitigate diaphragmatic compression from the gravid uterus and large abdominal masses, resulting in slight symptom improvement; pain score reduced from 5 to 1 on the Visual Analogue Scale, without the need for supplemental oxygen or ventilatory support.

During her hospitalization, she developed a mild fever. A nasopharyngeal swab for severe acute respiratory syndrome Coronavirus 2 PCR returned negative, while urine culture identified Klebsiella pneumoniae infection. Laboratory studies revealed iron deficiency anemia (haemoglobin 9.9 g/L [normal range 12.0–16.6 g/L], serum ferritin 25 mcg/L [normal range 15–300 mcg/L]), a modest elevation in C-reactive protein (1.39 mg/dL [normal range 0–0.3 mg/dL]), and increased neutrophil count (white blood cells 11.29×10^3 /uL [normal range 4–10×10^3 /uL], neutrophils 9.27×10^3 /uL [normal range 2.8–5.5×10^3 /uL]), consistent with changes typically seen in pregnancy.

Investigations:

Ultrasound revealed two substantial, unilocular cystic lesions with smooth borders and vascularisation along the periphery. The superior lesion measured 18×12×7 cm and stretched from the left subphrenic region down to the uterine fundus. The inferior lesion, measuring 9.5×8×8 cm, appeared attached to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma undergoing cystic degeneration (dimensions: 58×71 mm).

Analysis of tumour markers, including CA 19–9, CA 15–3, CEA, β2-microglobulin, and LDH, were all within the normal range. In contrast, CA-125 was raised at 41.8 U/mL (reference <35 U/mL), which represented a gradual increase from 36 U/mL recorded two weeks earlier. Thus, there was a mild upward trend in this marker’s serum concentration. Accordingly, the patient was referred for an MRI scan performed without contrast. MRI showed thick-walled cysts (wall thickness of 1.4 cm), inhomogeneous fluid on T1 and T2 fat-saturated sequences, and discrete areas of hyperintense solid tissue on both T1- and T2-weighted images (see figure 1; only T2 fat-saturated images were displayed). The lower lesion’s boundary was indistinct, appearing contiguous with the posterior leiomyoma previously identified by ultrasound. No lymphadenopathy was seen on MRI.

Differential diagnosis:

On ultrasound examination, the cystic lesions resembled two large decidualised endometriomas. The inferior cyst appeared attached to a posterior intramural-subserosal leiomyoma of the uterus, which measured 58×71 mm.

Magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated two round, single-chamber adnexal cysts with thickened vessels and uneven fluid content (refer to figure 1). The lower lesion seemed adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration and measuring 58×71 mm.

Because the size and positioning of the gravid uterus and the substantial masses created physical obstacles, neither a connecting stalk nor a vascular supply to the upper lesion could be identified, which supported a diagnosis of ovarian pathology, particularly endometriomas, given the mild elevation in CA-125 observed during the second trimester (see discussion).

Considering the findings from ultrasonography and MRI, the possible diagnoses included decidualised endometriomas and leiomyomas undergoing cystic degeneration. The slight increase in CA-125 suggested an endometrioma for the superior cyst, while the apparent continuity with uterine tissue indicated a myomatous origin for the inferior mass. The patient's dyspnoea was attributed to mechanical pressure from the extensive abdominal growths, in the absence of pulmonary or cardiac impairment.

Treatment:

Due to the deterioration of her clinical status, including abdominal pain associated with the mass and dyspnoea resulting from restrictive lung disease attributed to the size of the abdominal tumors, as well as the need for definitive diagnostic clarification, concern regarding potential complications (such as rupture, torsion, and infection), and anticipated uterine expansion corresponding with fetal growth, the patient underwent a laparotomy at 21 weeks and 6 days. The procedure involved a longitudinal xiphoid-to-pubic incision, following her informed consent.

Initially, two samples of free peritoneal fluid were obtained for culture and cytological evaluation. Subsequently, the surgical exploration of the abdominal cavity was performed, which confirmed persistent fetal cardiac activity.

Unexpectedly, the cranial mass was identified as a sizable, pedunculated, atypical cystic and tense-elastic uterine leiomyoma, exhibiting pronounced vascularization, and noted to be initially twisted and oozing, possibly accounting for the patient’s fever during hospitalization (refer to figure 2). The surgical team elected to resect this mass by carefully detaching it from its uterine base using a double clamp technique (see figure 2). After transecting the stalk, the exposed root was meticulously closed with a single absorbable suture. The caudal mass, originating at the uterine torus, was left untouched to avoid increasing obstetric risk. This lesion was immobile, displayed a smooth surface, and possessed a soft consistency, consistent with a large cystic intramural-subserosal leiomyoma.

The operative time totaled 2 hours and 25 minutes. Of note, the patient began receiving prophylactic progesterone 100 mg intramuscularly during surgery, which was then continued at the same dose intramuscularly daily until discharge. Afterward, intramuscular caproate hydroxyprogesterone (341 mg, one vial intramuscularly every 3 days, as per package instructions) was administered until the scheduled delivery. Intraoperative antibiotic prophylaxis was provided with cefazolin 2 g intravenously, and this was followed postoperatively by cefazolin 1 g intravenously three times daily plus clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily during her inpatient stay, in accordance with urine culture antibiogram results (Klebsiella pneumoniae highly sensitive to cephalosporins and clarithromycin).

Outcome and follow-up:

Following the operation, the patient's anemia worsened (Hb 7.5 g/L, HCT 24.3%), and ongoing iron deficiency necessitated intravenous iron administration (ferric carboxymaltose 1 g) and two successful blood transfusions (resulting in Hb 10.0 g/L, HCT 30.4%). She was discharged 10 days post-surgery without complications and began intensive obstetric monitoring every 3 weeks, which demonstrated fetal growth at the 86th percentile (based on the WHO fetal growth chart calculator, https://srhr.org/fetalgrowthcalculator/#/) and evaluation of amniotic fluid index.

Analysis of peritoneal fluid yielded negative results for both cultures and cytology. Histopathological assessment identified benign uterine leiomyomas and regions of ischemic necrosis adjacent to the twisted pedicle.

The distal myoma posed a significant risk of obstructing fetal descent during the expulsive stage of labor; therefore, an elective caesarean section was conducted on the previous surgical scar upon the fetus reaching 37 weeks of gestation. This approach reduced the likelihood of uterine scar rupture and resulted in a favorable neonatal outcome.

Currently, the patient does not experience symptoms attributable to myoma, although the size of the remaining pelvic myoma has modestly decreased to 7.5 cm. She is successfully breastfeeding and has chosen not to schedule further surgical intervention at this time, considering the partial postpartum regression of the residual leiomyomas, lack of symptoms, and her satisfaction with achieving motherhood. The infant continues to develop appropriately.

Discussion:

Myomas can often be misdiagnosed as adnexal masses: the incidence during pregnancy is 0.3–10.1% and 1–6%, respectively .13

In this case, the initial assessment identified abdominal masses resembling two large decidualised endometriomas, except for a caudal solid portion of the pelvic mass, which appeared consistent with a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration, measuring 58×71 mm as seen on both ultrasound and MRI. Given the lack of specific management protocols for pelvic masses during pregnancy, the multidisciplinary team determined that planned surgical intervention might lower the likelihood of preterm labor.

Throughout pregnancy, serum CA-125 typically demonstrates two elevations: the first during the initial trimester (median 23 U/mL, p<0.00001), and the second during the third trimester (median 21 U/mL, p<0.001), both higher than in the second trimester (median 14.0 U/mL); however, in this patient, the CA-125 level was 41.8 U/mL, surpassing the median observed during the second trimester.

CA-125 primarily serves as a tumor marker for ovarian malignancy (commonly, a cut-off ≥35 U/mL is used), particularly in women older than 50 years, where it exhibits improved diagnostic performance (higher area under the curve, positive predictive value, sensitivity, and specificity). Nevertheless, elevated CA-125 can be associated with several benign conditions, including endometriosis and adenomyosis (>19 U/mL), pelvic inflammatory disease, menstruation, early or late stages of pregnancy (medians of 23 and 21 U/mL, respectively), threatened miscarriage, and uterine myomas (with a cut-off <19 U/mL).57

The clinical decision was primarily guided by several factors: the identification of large, vascularized masses containing both solid and cystic elements, which posed a real risk for complications such as rupture, torsion, or infection; the patient's worsening symptoms, including increased pain and dyspnea; the potential for the masses to increase in size, thereby elevating the likelihood of hypoxia, necrosis, and degeneration; mild elevation of tumor markers (CA-125 measured at 41.8 U/mL); and the restriction of space, which could impede normal uterine growth.

Given the possibility that the masses were neoplastic and their uncertain etiology, an MRI was performed without contrast following the ultrasound to enhance diagnostic accuracy. In accordance with current guidelines, gadolinium-enhanced MRI is generally contraindicated during pregnancy unless absolutely indicated by severe rheumatological, inflammatory, or infiltrative dermatological conditions, or in circumstances involving risk of stillbirth or neonatal demise. For these reasons, the multidisciplinary team elected to use a non-contrast MRI, ensuring the use of a device with no more than 1.5-T field strength, considered relatively safe after the first trimester.

In this case, the multidisciplinary team—including surgeons, gynecologists, and obstetricians—chose to pursue longitudinal laparotomic surgery following the acquisition of formal written informed consent from the patient. During intraoperative evaluation, the superior myoma was found to possess a pedicle, which posed considerable risk for an acute classic complication. Consequently, the team decided to restrict the intervention to an upper myomectomy. Additionally, fetal viability was assessed throughout the procedure using serial and periodic transvaginal ultrasonography, complemented by the prophylactic administration of progesterone intraoperatively.

A large multicenter investigation by Zhao et al highlighted the necessity of evaluating and documenting fetal heart rate before, during, and after surgical procedures.

Surgical intervention is typically deferred until postpartum, favoring medical management and observation. Notably, Sakinci et al's recent study at a tertiary institution found no statistically significant differences in preoperative and postoperative hemoglobin levels or blood transfusion requirements (p >0.05) between women undergoing cesarean section (CS) with myomectomy versus those without myoma who had only CS. However, the length of hospitalization and operative time were markedly greater in the CS with myomectomy cohort (both p =0.001). The average myoma diameter was 8.3 ± 4.1cm, and a significant positive correlation was observed between myoma size and hospital stay (p =0.01).

The same study revealed a significant inverse association between myoma size and the gestational week at delivery (p = 0.035).

Uterine leiomyomas exhibit a non-linear pattern in their size alterations throughout pregnancy and the postpartum period. Specifically, leiomyoma dimensions expand by 6.90% weekly: 6.12% growth (p<0.001) was observed from the first to second trimester, and 3.10%±0.39% increase occurred from the second to third trimester (p<0.05). Accordingly, the rate of leiomyoma enlargement is substantially greater during the initial trimester compared to subsequent gestational stages. Conversely, in the third trimester and during the 6 weeks following childbirth, the size of myomas was noted to decline significantly (average reduction 44.25%±36.05%, p<0.05).

These studies emphasize the necessity for thorough ultrasound evaluation to determine the size, position, number, and proximity of uterine myomas relative to the placenta prior to any surgical intervention.

For uterine myomas detected during pregnancy, conservative management is generally favored over surgical procedures because of potential complications such as severe hemorrhage, miscarriage, preterm delivery, and uterine rupture. Nonetheless, myomectomy should be contemplated for cases involving complicated masses—such as torsion of a pedunculated myoma—or persistent symptoms that do not respond to conservative measures. Surgical intervention should be as brief as possible to safeguard the pregnancy and may be performed laparoscopically, ideally utilizing the open Hasson technique in instances of lower gestational age (first and early second trimester), smaller mass size, and specific locations (excluding intramural myomas). This method ensures improved maternal outcomes and facilitates vaginal delivery. Despite this, the current case highlights a frequently encountered selection bias, wherein laparotomy is chosen over laparoscopic surgery for larger, multiple, intramural, or critically positioned myomas, which may be linked to an increased rate of cesarean section. This is due to the presence of a sizable myoma adherent to the posterior wall at the isthmic region of the uterus, potentially hindering vaginal delivery.
