Title: Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood

Hans Vaish, Vipin Vaish, Shalini Suri, Vasudha Tomar

Abstract:

Summary: An older male child presented with repeated pleural effusions on the right side, chest discomfort, and breathing difficulty, which were transiently relieved following aspiration of the effusion. The aspirated pleural fluid consistently exhibited a dark reddish color. Given the recurrence of effusions, a right-sided intercostal drain was inserted. Review of history identified a recent blunt abdominal injury caused by impact from a bicycle handlebar. Chest computed tomography revealed patchy consolidation in the right lung, evidence of hydropneumothorax, and a multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right paraesophageal area toward the pancreatic tail. Amylase and lipase concentrations were elevated in both blood and pleural fluid samples. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography demonstrated pancreatopleural fistulas on both sides, with more extensive involvement on the right. Management consisted of keeping the patient nil per os, providing total parenteral nutrition, and administering Octreotide. Within 10 days, fluid output diminished, the intercostal tube was discontinued, and the child was subsequently discharged.

Background:

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) represents a very uncommon condition characterized by repeated, large-volume, hematic pleural effusions. This complication can occur in the setting of acute or chronic pancreatitis, as well as following pancreatic injury. PPF poses diagnostic challenges since affected individuals typically present with respiratory symptoms attributable to recurrent pleural collections, yet the underlying disease originates from the pancreas, often leading to delayed recognition as investigations tend to focus on thoracic causes. The pediatric literature documents only 47 cases of PPF, in contrast to over 300 cases reported in the adult population.

Case presentation:

A pediatric patient presented to the outpatient clinic with his mother, reporting a one-week history of dyspnea and right-sided chest pain. The mother described a progressive worsening of respiratory distress during the preceding seven days. There was no history of consanguinity. Notably, the mother recalled a history of Koch’s contact, as the child's grandfather had received treatment for pulmonary Koch's disease. The child's nutritional history showed frequent ingestion of processed, high-calorie foods and minimal consumption of fresh produce or balanced meals. The child weighed 44 kg, measured 112 cm in height, and had a BMI of 35. Upon examination, his respiratory rate was 20/min, with mild tachypnea observed. Chest movement on the right was diminished. Percussion yielded a stony dull note in the right subscapular, right infra-axillary, and axillary regions. Vocal fremitus on the right was markedly diminished compared to the left. Breath sounds were reduced in the same right-sided regions. There were no signs of pallor, cyanosis, icterus, clubbing, or lymphadenopathy. The abdomen was soft, with no evidence of hepatosplenomegaly. The patient was alert and oriented to time, place, and person. Cardiovascular assessment revealed tachycardia with no murmurs. Oxygen saturation was between 88% and 89% on ambient air, and the heart rate was 110 bpm. Given the tachypnea and respiratory distress, the child was admitted to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit (PICU) and commenced on mask oxygen at 5 l/min. A chest radiograph was requested, and empirical antibiotics (piperacillin-tazobactam) were initiated following blood culture collection. Chest X-ray demonstrated a right-sided pleural effusion and blunting of the right costophrenic angle (figure 1; Table 2). Bedside lung ultrasound confirmed a pleural effusion of approximately one liter (figure 2; Table 2). Under sterile conditions, right-sided pleural fluid was aspirated, yielding roughly one liter of dark red fluid. Following thoracocentesis, the tachypnea resolved and oxygen saturation on room air improved to 95%–98%. Chest pain subsided and oxygen supplementation was no longer required within 24 hours. The child was transferred to the ward the following day and subsequently discharged. The aspirated pleural fluid was sent for analysis.

Six days later, the patient was readmitted with recurrence of right-sided chest pain, tachypnea, and breathing difficulty. He was transferred again to the PICU, where chest radiography revealed another right-sided pleural effusion. A repeat thoracocentesis extracted around one liter of similar dark red fluid. Pleural fluid adenosine deaminase (ADA) was slightly elevated (Table 1) and, coupled with a positive Koch’s contact history, empirical antitubercular therapy was started.

The patient was admitted for a third time five days later, presenting again with right-sided chest pain and dyspnea. On revisiting his history, the child revealed that he had sustained blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle a month prior. Contrast-enhanced chest CT was performed before considering intercostal drain (ICD) placement. CT findings included patchy consolidation in the right lung, right-sided hydropneumothorax, and an elongated, multiloculated, hypodense collection extending from the right pleural space, tracking from the right paraesophageal area to the upper left retroperitoneum up to the pancreatic tail (Table 2). An ICD was inserted, draining approximately one liter of dark red pleural fluid (figure 3). These imaging results, combined with the dark red pleural aspirate and abdominal trauma history, suggested a diagnosis of pancreatico-pleural fistula (PPF). MRCP was conducted the same day, revealing pancreatitis with a tubular cystic collection in the distal pancreatic body and tail, extending posteriorly into the retroperitoneum and tracking superiorly through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity. A narrow connection with the left pleural cavity was also observed. The collection communicated with the distal pancreatic duct, which was narrowed proximally. MRCP supported the diagnosis of PPF (figures 4-6; Table 1). Amylase and lipase levels in both serum and pleural fluid were assessed, with pleural fluid amylase reaching 6118 U/L and lipase at 7211 U/L (Table 2), confirming PPF. Antitubercular treatment was discontinued, and the patient was placed on nil per os and total parenteral nutrition. Differential diagnosis:

The patient presented with pleural effusion and chest pain, and had a documented episode of abdominal trauma one month prior. We excluded causes of pleural effusion other than post-traumatic pancreatitis. The child lacked any history of fever, chronic cough, or weight loss. While there was a family history of Koch’s contact and pleural fluid ADA was slightly elevated, GeneXpert for MTB came back negative. Therefore, pulmonary Koch’s was considered unlikely. Absence of fever and a lack of neutrophilic exudate in the pleural fluid made empyema improbable. Although malignancies such as lung cancer, leukemia, lymphoma, and metastatic tumors can result in pleural fluid high in amylase, there were no signs of fever or weight loss, and both the blood profile and peripheral smear were unremarkable, excluding malignancy. Cytological examination of the pleural fluid also showed no malignant cells. Amylase-rich pleural fluid can occur in Boerhaave’s syndrome (esophageal perforation); however, this diagnosis was dismissed as the child maintained normal bowel movements and there was no history of vomiting or chest pain. Acquired diaphragmatic hernia was excluded since imaging showed no evidence of abdominal organs within the thoracic cavity. Hepatic hydrothorax was eliminated as liver imaging and liver function tests were within normal limits, and there was no history of chronic liver disease. Renal pathology, including hydronephrosis, was excluded based on normal renal imaging and renal function assessments.

Treatment:

The patient received humidified oxygen via mask for respiratory support in the PICU. Thoracocentesis guided by ultrasound was performed on two occasions, each yielding approximately one liter of blood-tinged pleural fluid. Empirical antitubercular therapy was initiated but discontinued upon definitive diagnosis by MRCP. During the third hospitalization, an intercostal drain (ICD) was placed on the right side under aseptic technique, again draining roughly one liter of blood-stained pleural fluid into the ICD bag. As PPF is a rare condition in children and lacks established therapeutic protocols, management was guided by published case reports. The initial approach was conservative: the patient was kept nil per oral and commenced on Octreotide infusion at a dosage of 30 µg/kg/day (range: 10–40 µg/kg/day). Total parenteral nutrition and pancreatic enzyme supplementation were also provided. Over the next ten days, the ICD output gradually diminished and eventually ceased. The ICD was then removed, and the patient was discharged from hospital care.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following his release from the hospital, the patient has not exhibited any symptoms. He continues to see me for scheduled monthly follow-ups. There have not been any additional incidents of recurrent pleural effusions since he left the hospital (S17). He has adjusted his way of life, adopting a balanced diet and engaging in consistent physical activity as recommended. The patient has resumed normal routines and is regularly attending school. He is able to participate in vigorous activities such as running and playing without experiencing limitations.

Discussion:

PPF represents an exceedingly uncommon complication of pancreatic disease among children. To date, only 47 paediatric cases have been described in the literature. This entity arises in association with both acute and chronic pancreatitis or following trauma in paediatric patients. The precise incidence among paediatric populations remains unclear, although PPF complicates approximately 0.4% of pancreatitis cases in general. Genetic abnormalities, particularly mutations in serine peptidase inhibitor Kazal type 1 (SPINK1) and PRSS1, are important contributors, implicated in up to 30% of paediatric chronic pancreatitis cases. Non-genetic factors such as blunt abdominal trauma also play a significant role, comprising 32% of a 25-case series reviewed by Yang et al. There remain idiopathic cases which may arise from undetected genetic variants or ductal anomalies. 

Pancreatic fluid may dissect from a disrupted duct or pseudocyst via natural openings in the diaphragm—typically the aortic or oesophageal hiatus—or more rarely through direct erosion into the pleural space, resulting in the formation of a fistula connecting the pancreas to the pleural cavity. Because patients typically present with thoracic complaints, evaluation often focuses on pulmonary pathology, while the true origin is intra-abdominal. Of 21 reports identified in the literature, 68% of patients had primary respiratory manifestations, including large, persistent unilateral or bilateral pleural effusions, breathlessness, or cough. Fewer than 20% displayed abdominal symptoms such as upper abdominal pain, emesis, or weight loss, and 24% had systemic features like fever. Shang et al. (Frontiers in Paediatrics, 2023) described an 8-year-old with PPF presenting solely with a left-sided chest bulge persisting for two weeks; initial symptoms like chest wall bulging are exceptionally rare. In that instance, progressive accumulation of pleural fluid followed abdominal trauma, with adaptive respiratory compensation from the unaffected lung resulting in the absence of usual respiratory complaints such as cough or dyspnoea. Additionally, the patient intentionally withheld details of abdominal trauma, possibly due to experiences of bullying at school. Evaluation for haemorrhagic pleural effusion focused on tuberculosis and malignancy, before an alternative diagnosis was reached. 

Similarly, our patient experienced recurring pulmonary symptoms, specifically chest pain and repeated right-sided pleural effusions. A thorough history, repeated on several occasions, eventually elicited evidence of prior abdominal trauma, while MRCP was instrumental in identifying pancreatic pathology. Thus, PPF can easily masquerade as a primary pulmonary disease, and the correct diagnosis may be missed unless detailed clinical history and appropriate imaging are employed. Fluid accumulation in PPF is attributable to disruption of the pancreatic duct or pseudocyst either by external trauma or infection, resulting in leakage of pancreatic fluid. If ductal rupture is anterior, fluid enters the abdominal cavity, producing ascites; posterior rupture results in extravasation into the retroperitoneum, and fluid subsequently traverses upwards via the aortic or oesophageal hiatus, penetrating the pleura to cause a pleural effusion. An internal pancreatic fistula acts as a conduit for pancreatic secretions into the thoracic cavity. 

In adults, chronic pancreatitis is predominantly associated with alcohol use, which underlies most PPF cases. Uchiyama et al. reviewed 113 cases from Japanese sources and reported that 99% were related to chronic alcoholic pancreatitis, with 68% presenting primarily with chest symptoms. By contrast, hereditary factors account for most cases of paediatric chronic pancreatitis, including mutations of the PRSS1 gene (notably R122H and N291), the CFTR gene, the SPINK1 gene, and chymotrypsin C gene. A comprehensive family history and consideration of hereditary pancreatitis are warranted when pancreatitis in a child recurs or persists. Diagnosis of PPF is typically delayed, as initial investigations centre on the respiratory system. Pleural fluid amylase concentrations ≥1000 IU/L are diagnostic for PPF. It is essential to rule out tuberculosis, oesophageal perforation, and malignancy of the lungs or pancreas, as elevated pleural fluid amylase may also be seen in these conditions. Differentiation relies on the identification of pancreatic-type amylase in the pleural fluid for PPF; in contrast, other conditions often show salivary-type amylase. Typing pleural fluid amylase is thus a useful diagnostic step. MRCP provides 70%–80% sensitivity for detecting fistulas and evaluating ductal anatomy, whereas CT scans are effective in demonstrating calcifications, pseudocysts, and ductal dilation, but only reveal the fistula in 33%–47% of cases.
