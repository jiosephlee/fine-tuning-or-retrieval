Title: Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood

Hans Vaish, Vipin Vaish, Shalini Suri, Vasudha Tomar

Abstract:

A late childhood male presented with repeated right-sided pleural effusions, chest pain, and respiratory difficulty, all of which showed temporary improvement following aspiration. The pleural fluid consistently appeared dark red in color. Because of ongoing effusions, a right intercostal drain was inserted. His history included recent abdominal trauma involving impact with a bicycle handlebar. Chest computed tomography revealed areas of consolidation in the right lung, hydropneumothorax, and a multiloculated, hypodense collection extending from the right paraesophageal region to the pancreatic tail. Elevated amylase and lipase levels were found in both serum and pleural fluid samples. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography identified bilateral pancreatopleural fistulas, with greater involvement on the right side than the left. Management consisted of nil per os, total parenteral nutrition, and Octreotide. Drainage progressively declined over 10 days, permitting chest tube removal and eventual discharge of the patient.

Background:

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is a very uncommon condition characterized by recurrent, large-volume, often blood-tinged pleural effusions. It can develop as a complication of acute or chronic pancreatitis, as well as pancreatic injury. The condition poses a diagnostic challenge, as patients typically present with symptoms of respiratory compromise due to persistent pleural effusions; nevertheless, the underlying problem originates in the pancreas, often resulting in diagnostic delays since investigations are initially focused on thoracic causes. In pediatric patients, only 47 cases of PPF have been documented, while the adult population accounts for over 300 reported cases.

Case presentation:

A pediatric patient presented to the outpatient clinic, accompanied by his mother, with a one-week history of dyspnea and right-sided chest pain. According to the mother, the respiratory symptoms had worsened gradually over seven days. There was no parental consanguinity. The mother reported a significant history of exposure to Koch’s disease, as the child’s grandfather had previously been treated for pulmonary tuberculosis. Dietary review indicated frequent intake of processed, high-calorie foods, with minimal consumption of fresh fruits, vegetables, and balanced homemade meals. The child's weight was 44 kg, height 112 cm, and his BMI was 35.

On physical examination, his respiratory rate was 20 per minute, with mild tachypnea observed. Chest movement on the right side was diminished. Percussion was stony dull in the right subscapular, right infra-axillary, and axillary areas. Vocal fremitus was much reduced on the right compared to the left. Breath sounds were also decreased in those right-sided regions. There were no signs of pallor, cyanosis, jaundice, clubbing, or lymphadenopathy. The abdomen was soft, non-tender, and there was no hepatosplenomegaly. The patient was alert and oriented. Cardiovascular assessment showed tachycardia without murmurs. Pulse oximetry revealed an oxygen saturation of 88%–89% on ambient air, with a heart rate of 110 per minute.

Due to his tachypnea and respiratory distress, the child was admitted to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit and provided with oxygen via face mask at 5 L/min. A chest radiograph was ordered, and broad-spectrum empiric antibiotics (piperacillin-tazobactam) were initiated after sending blood cultures. The chest X-ray demonstrated a right-sided pleural effusion with blunting of the right costophrenic angle (figure 1) (Table 2). Point-of-care lung ultrasound revealed an estimated pleural fluid volume of approximately one litre (figure 2) (Table 2). Under strict aseptic conditions, thoracentesis of the right pleural space was performed, draining about one litre of dark red fluid. Following thoracentesis, the tachypnea resolved, and his oxygen saturation improved to 95%–98% on room air. The child’s chest pain abated, and oxygen therapy was discontinued within 24 hours. He was transferred to the general ward the next day and subsequently discharged. The aspirated pleural fluid was sent for laboratory analysis.

Six days later, the patient was readmitted with recurrent right-sided chest pain, tachypnea, and shortness of breath. He was returned to the PICU, where chest radiography again confirmed a right-sided pleural effusion. Repeat thoracentesis was carried out, aspirating about one litre of similar dark red pleural fluid. Pleural fluid adenosine deaminase (ADA) was found to be mildly elevated (Table 1), and due to the positive contact history for Koch’s, empirical antitubercular therapy was begun.

However, five days after this episode, the child was hospitalized a third time with comparable symptoms of right-sided chest pain and dyspnea. Upon further questioning, the child revealed that he had suffered blunt abdominal trauma to the abdomen from a bicycle handle one month prior. Before placing an intercostal chest drain (ICD), a contrast-enhanced chest CT was ordered, which showed patchy air space consolidation in the right lung, right-sided hydropneumothorax, and an elongated, lobulated, multiloculated hypodense collection originating in the right pleural space and tracking from the right paraesophageal region down to the upper left retroperitoneum, extending to the pancreatic tail (Table 2). An ICD was inserted, draining approximately one litre of dark red pleural fluid (figure 3). The combination of CT findings, the appearance of the pleural fluid, and the history of abdominal trauma suggested a diagnosis of pancreaticopleural fistula (PPF).

MRCP was promptly performed, revealing pancreatitis with a tubular cystic collection in the distal pancreas (body and tail), extending posteriorly through the retroperitoneum and ascending through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural space. A thin communication to the left pleural cavity was also noted. The collection communicated with the distal pancreatic duct, which showed proximal narrowing at the communication site. MRCP findings supported the diagnosis of PPF (figures 4–6) (Table 1). Amylase and lipase levels were measured in both serum and pleural fluid. Pleural analysis demonstrated markedly increased amylase (6118 U/L) and lipase (7211 U/L) concentrations (Table 2), corroborating the diagnosis of PPF. Differential diagnosis:

The patient arrived with pleural effusion and chest pain, following a documented episode of abdominal trauma occurring one month earlier. Aside from post-traumatic pancreatitis, alternative etiologies for pleural effusion were excluded. This pediatric patient had no history suggestive of fever, prolonged cough, or significant weight loss. Although there was a reported familial exposure to tuberculosis (Koch's contact) and a slight elevation in pleural fluid ADA, GeneXpert testing for MTB was negative, leading to exclusion of pulmonary tuberculosis. Empyema was also excluded based on the absence of fever and lack of neutrophilic exudate within the pleural fluid. While malignancy—including lung cancer, leukemia, lymphoma, and metastatic neoplasms—may cause pleural fluid elevated in amylase, the lack of fever, absence of weight loss, unremarkable blood work, and normal peripheral blood smear did not support this diagnosis. Pleural fluid cytology similarly demonstrated no malignant cells. Boerhaave's syndrome (esophageal perforation), another cause of amylase-rich pleural fluid, was excluded as the patient maintained normal bowel movements and lacked chest pain or vomiting history. Imaging studies did not show abdominal organs within the chest, thereby excluding acquired diaphragmatic hernia. Hepatic hydrothorax was ruled out due to normal liver function assessments, unremarkable liver imaging, and absence of a chronic liver disease history. Furthermore, renal pathology and hydronephrosis were not considered as renal imaging and function tests were normal.

Treatment:

In the PICU, humidified oxygen was administered via face mask for respiratory support. Ultrasound-guided thoracocentesis was performed twice, and approximately one liter of pleural fluid with a blood-stained appearance was withdrawn during each procedure. Empirical antitubercular therapy was initiated, but discontinued once the diagnosis was established via MRCP. Upon the patient’s third hospital admission, an intercostal drain (ICD) was placed on the right side under aseptic technique, and again about one liter of blood-stained pleural fluid was collected in the ICD bag. Due to the rarity of PPF in paediatric patients and the absence of specific treatment guidelines, management relied on previous case reports. Initially, conservative therapy was chosen; the child was kept nil per os and started on octreotide infusion at a dose of 30 µg/kg/day (range, 10–40 µg/kg/day). Total parenteral nutrition and pancreatic enzyme supplementation were initiated. Over the next ten days, the volume of fluid from the ICD progressively decreased until it ceased. The ICD was then removed, and the patient was subsequently discharged.

Outcome and follow-up:

Since being discharged, the child has remained asymptomatic. He continues with routine monthly follow-up visits. There have been no additional occurrences of pleural effusions since leaving the hospital (S17). He has implemented lifestyle changes, now maintaining a balanced diet and exercising regularly as recommended. He has resumed his normal daily routine and consistently attends school. He is able to participate in vigorous activities such as running and playing without any issues.

Discussion:

Panpleural fistula (PPF) represents a rare and seldom-seen complication of pancreatic disorders within paediatric patients. To date, only 47 cases of PPF have been reported in the paediatric demographic. This condition may present in both acute and chronic pancreatitis, as well as following trauma in children. Its true frequency in paediatric patients remains undetermined; however, PPF is associated with roughly 0.4% of pancreatitis cases overall. Mutations in Serine peptidase inhibitor Kazal type 1 (SPINK1) and PRSS1 genes are significant, contributing to as much as 30% of paediatric chronic pancreatitis. Additionally, blunt trauma to the abdomen stands out as a considerable non-genetic cause, as highlighted by Yang et al., who found it responsible for 32% of their 25-child cohort. Instances labeled idiopathic may reflect yet-undetected genetic or ductal anomalies.

Pancreatic fluid may escape from either a ruptured duct or pseudocyst and traverse through natural diaphragmatic openings, most commonly the aortic or oesophageal hiatuses, or less frequently via direct erosion across the diaphragm, establishing a fistulous route into the pleural space. Such cases are frequently overlooked because initial manifestations are thoracic in nature, leading clinicians to focus on finding pulmonary disease, when the actual problem arises from the abdomen.

A literature review identified 21 pertinent reports, with respiratory presentations observed in 68% of cases—these included massive, recurring unilateral or bilateral pleural effusions, dyspnoea, and cough. Fewer than 20% demonstrated abdominal complaints such as epigastric discomfort, nausea, and weight reduction, while 24% exhibited systemic symptoms including fever. Zi Yin Shang's 2023 report in Frontiers in Paediatrics described PPF in an 8-year-old male presenting only with a left-sided chest bulge for two weeks, underscoring the rarity of chest wall protrusion as an initial sign. In that case, progressive development of pleural effusion followed traumatic injury to the abdomen, with compensatory lung function permitting adaptation without standard respiratory symptoms like cough or breathlessness. The child, affected by school bullying, deliberately concealed the trauma history. Initial evaluations centered on tuberculosis and malignancy for hemorrhagic pleural fluid, but the correct diagnosis later emerged.

Similarly, our case involved a child with repeated pulmonary manifestations—chest pain and recurring right-sided pleural effusions. After multiple detailed history assessments and MRCP findings, abdominal trauma was revealed and pointed toward pancreatic pathology. Therefore, PPF may present a 'pulmonary mask,' misleading clinicians unless comprehensive history-taking and appropriate imaging are pursued.

Fluid accumulation in PPF stems from external mechanical injury or bacterial infection leading to rupture of the pancreatic duct or a pseudocyst, allowing pancreatic juice leakage. Rupture in the pancreas's anterior aspect results in ascites, while posterior rupture causes fluid to migrate retroperitoneally, ascend via the path of least resistance to the mediastinum through the aortic or oesophageal hiatus, ultimately breaking through the pleura to form a pleural effusion—a situation analogous to a leaky pipe channeling pancreatic secretions into the chest.

In adults, alcoholism is the dominant cause of chronic pancreatitis resulting in PPF. Uchiyama et al., via an extensive review of Japanese literature, found 113 cases—99% attributed to chronic alcoholic pancreatitis, predominantly with chest symptoms in 68%. By contrast, paediatric chronic pancreatitis is largely hereditary, with PRSS1, cystic fibrosis transmembrane regulator (CFTR), SPINK1, and chymotrypsin C gene mutations well-recognized etiologies. Notable PRSS1 mutations include R122H and N291 in exons 3 and 2, respectively. For children with persistent or recurrent pancreatitis, obtaining a detailed family history and considering hereditary pancreatitis is advised.

Diagnosis is often delayed as most cases undergo extensive pulmonary workups prior to the suspicion of pancreatic involvement. Pleural fluid amylase ≥1000 IU/L is diagnostic for PPF. Differential diagnoses include tuberculosis, esophageal rupture, and neoplasms in the lung or pancreas, all of which can elevate pleural fluid amylase. Pancreatic-type amylase distinguishes PPF from such conditions, which usually feature salivary-type amylase; amylase typing thus augments the diagnostic process for suspected PPF. MRCP detects the fistula and ductal abnormalities with 70–80% sensitivity. CT reliably demonstrates calcifications, pseudocysts, or duct dilation, though visualizes the fistula itself in 33–47% of instances. Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) remains the primary modality for therapeutic planning, with fistula visualization rates in paediatric patients ranging between 38% and 78%.
