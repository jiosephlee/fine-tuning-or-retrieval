Title: Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood

Hans Vaish, Vipin Vaish, Shalini Suri, Vasudha Tomar

Abstract:

Summary: An older boy presented with multiple episodes of right-sided pleural effusions accompanied by chest pain and difficulty breathing, with transient improvement following fluid aspiration. The pleural fluid was consistently dark red in appearance. Given ongoing effusions, a right intercostal drain was inserted. Further history revealed recent abdominal injury caused by a bicycle handle. Chest computed tomography demonstrated right-sided patchy consolidation, hydropneumothorax, and a multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right paraesophageal area toward the pancreatic tail. Both serum and pleural fluid amylase and lipase levels were elevated. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography identified bilateral pancreatopleural fistulas, with more involvement on the right side. Management included withholding oral intake, providing total parenteral nutrition, and administering Octreotide. Over 10 days, drainage lessened, the chest tube was withdrawn, and the patient was discharged.

Background:

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is a highly uncommon condition characterized by repeated, substantial, and blood-tinged pleural effusions. It is seen in individuals with acute pancreatitis, chronic pancreatitis, or pancreatic trauma. Managing PPF can be challenging because patients typically exhibit symptoms of respiratory compromise secondary to recurrent pleural effusions, while the underlying issue originates from the pancreas. This often results in delayed recognition, as clinicians tend to focus on identifying pulmonary causes. In the pediatric population, there have been only 47 documented cases of PPF, in contrast to more than 300 reported cases in adults.

Case presentation:

A pediatric patient presented to the outpatient clinic, accompanied by his mother, with a history of breathing difficulties and right-sided chest pain persisting for one week. According to the mother, his respiratory symptoms had gradually worsened over the course of seven days. There was no report of consanguinity. The mother disclosed a history of exposure to tuberculosis (Koch’s contact) through the child’s grandfather, who previously received adequate treatment for pulmonary tuberculosis. The child’s dietary habits included frequent intake of processed, calorie-dense foods, and he had limited consumption of fruits, vegetables, and nutritionally balanced home-cooked meals. The child weighed 44 kg, measured 112 cm in height, with a body mass index of 35.

On physical assessment, he exhibited a respiratory rate of 20/min, indicating mild tachypnea. Diminished chest movement was noted on the right side. Percussion demonstrated stony dullness in the right subscapular, infra-axillary, and axillary regions. Vocal fremitus was markedly diminished on the right compared to the left. Decreased breath sounds were evident in the right subscapular, infra-axillary, and axillary zones. No signs of pallor, cyanosis, icterus, clubbing, or lymphadenopathy were detected. The abdomen was soft without organomegaly. The child remained alert and oriented to person, time, and place. Abdominal exam revealed no hepatosplenomegaly. Cardiovascular assessment showed tachycardia without murmurs. Oxygen saturation measured 88%–89% on room air with a pulse of 110/min.

In light of his tachypnea and respiratory distress, he was admitted to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit and started on oxygen therapy via mask at a rate of 5 L/min. A chest radiograph was obtained and empiric antibiotic therapy (piperacillin-tazobactam) was initiated after collecting blood cultures. The chest X-ray showed a right-sided pleural effusion with blunting of the right costophrenic angle (figure 1) (Table 2). Bedside lung ultrasound confirmed approximately one litre of pleural fluid (figure 2) (Table 2). Right-sided thoracocentesis was performed under aseptic conditions, yielding around one litre of dark red pleural fluid. Following the procedure, the child’s tachypnea resolved and room air oxygen saturation improved to 95%–98%. His chest pain lessened, and oxygen support was discontinued within 24 hours. He was transferred to the general ward the following day and subsequently discharged. The aspirated pleural fluid was sent for laboratory analysis.

Six days post-discharge, the child was re-admitted presenting with recurrent right-sided chest pain, tachypnea, and breathing difficulties. He was once again transferred to the PICU, where repeat chest X-ray demonstrated a recurrent right pleural effusion. Repeat thoracocentesis extracted another litre of dark red pleural fluid. Analysis showed slightly increased pleural fluid adenosine deaminase (ADA) (Table 1), and given the positive TB exposure history, empirical antitubercular therapy was commenced.

Five days later, the child experienced a third admission for similar complaints of right-sided chest pain and dyspnea. A more detailed history elicited from the patient revealed that he had experienced blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handlebar approximately one month prior. Before considering intercostal drainage (ICD), a contrast-enhanced CT chest scan was ordered, which demonstrated patchy right lung air space opacities, a right hydropneumothorax, and a multiloculated, elongated, hypodense collection stretching from the right pleural space at the right paraesophageal region into the upper left retroperitoneum reaching the tail of the pancreas (Table 2). An ICD was inserted, evacuating approximately one litre of dark red pleural fluid (figure 3). The CT findings, in conjunction with the appearance of the pleural aspirate and the traumatic abdominal history, raised suspicion for a pancreaticopleural fistula (PPF).

Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) was performed on the same day, revealing pancreatitis and a tubular cystic collection involving the distal pancreatic body and tail, which extended into the retroperitoneum and superiorly through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity. There was also a narrow connection to the left pleural cavity and communication with the distal pancreatic duct, which was noted to be narrowed proximally. MRCP findings were consistent with a diagnosis of PPF (figures 4-6) (Table 1). Amylase and lipase levels in serum and pleural fluid were obtained; pleural fluid analysis revealed markedly elevated amylase (6118 U/L) and lipase (7211 U/L)

Differential diagnosis:

The patient arrived with symptoms of pleural effusion and chest pain, with a documented episode of abdominal trauma occurring one month prior. Apart from considering post-traumatic pancreatitis, alternative etiologies of pleural effusion were excluded. The child lacked any history of fever, persistent cough, or weight loss. Although there was a family history of Koch’s contact and the pleural fluid ADA was mildly raised, the GeneXpert test for MTB was negative, thereby excluding pulmonary Koch’s. There were no signs of fever nor evidence of a neutrophilic exudate in the pleural fluid, ruling out empyema. While malignancies such as lung cancer, leukemia, lymphoma, or metastatic tumors may present with amylase-rich pleural fluid, normal blood work, normal peripheral smear, and absence of fever or weight loss argued against malignancy; pleural fluid cytology was also negative for malignant cells. Amylase-rich pleural fluid can also be associated with Boerhaave’s syndrome (esophageal perforation), but this was excluded since the child had normal bowel movements and no history of chest pain or vomiting. Acquired diaphragmatic hernia was not considered as imaging did not show any abdominal organs within the thoracic cavity. Hepatic hydrothorax was eliminated due to normal liver function and imaging results, as well as a lack of history of chronic liver disease. Normal renal imaging and renal function tests excluded renal pathology or hydronephrosis.

Treatment:

In the PICU, the patient received humidified oxygen via mask for respiratory support. Ultrasound-guided thoracocentesis was performed twice, with approximately one liter of blood-tinged pleural fluid obtained each time. Antitubercular therapy was initiated empirically but discontinued once MRCP provided diagnostic confirmation. Upon the third hospital admission, a right-sided intercostal drain (ICD) was placed under strict aseptic conditions, with roughly one liter of blood-stained pleural fluid again evacuated into the ICD collection bag. As pancreatic pleural fistula (PPF) is infrequent in children and established treatment protocols are absent, management relies on published case reports. Conservative management was selected initially; the patient was made nil per os and commenced on Octreotide infusion at a dosage of 30 µg/kg/day (range: 10–40 µg/kg/day). Total parenteral nutrition and supplementation with pancreatic enzymes were also started. Over ten days, the fluid output from the ICD progressively diminished and ultimately ceased. The ICD was then removed, and the patient was subsequently discharged.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following hospital discharge, the child has remained asymptomatic. He continues to see me for scheduled monthly follow-ups. Since leaving the hospital, there have been no further incidents of recurrent pleural effusions (S17). Lifestyle adjustments have been made, with the patient now adhering to a balanced diet and engaging in regular physical activity per recommendations. He has resumed his routine activities and is consistently attending school. He is able to perform vigorous activities, such as running and playing, without experiencing any problems.

Discussion:

PPF represents an exceedingly rare complication of pancreatic disorders within the pediatric demographic. To date, only 47 cases of PPF have been documented in children. It can emerge in association with acute or chronic pancreatitis, or as a result of pediatric trauma. The precise rate of occurrence in the pediatric population remains uncertain, though it contributes to roughly 0.4% of pancreatitis cases overall. Mutations such as Serine peptidase inhibitor Kazal type 1 (SPINK1) and PRSS1 are significant, accounting for up to 30% of cases in childhood chronic pancreatitis. Non-genetic factors such as blunt trauma are also substantial triggers, making up 32% of the 25 pediatric cases studied by Yang et al. Idiopathic instances continue to be reported and may be linked to under-diagnosed genetic or ductal abnormalities. Pancreatic fluid may escape from a disrupted duct or pseudocyst, moving through natural openings in the diaphragm—most frequently via the aortic or esophageal hiatus, though direct transdiaphragmatic erosion is less common—forming a fistulous connection to the pleural cavity.

Many of these cases are overlooked, as patients primarily present with chest complaints, often prompting clinicians to focus investigations on pulmonary diseases while overlooking the abdominal origin. Through a review of literature, 21 pertinent articles were identified, wherein 68% of patients exhibited mainly respiratory complaints such as large, recurrent pleural effusions (unilateral or bilateral), shortness of breath, or cough. Fewer than 20% showed abdominal manifestations like epigastric discomfort, vomiting, or unintended weight loss. Constitutional symptoms such as fever appeared in 24% of cases. The case described by Zi Yin Shang in Frontiers in Paediatrics 2023 concerned an 8-year-old presenting solely with a protruding left chest for two weeks—a highly unusual initial symptom. In this situation, the pleural effusion likely developed gradually after abdominal trauma, with respiratory adaptation enabling compensation and absence of classic symptoms such as cough or dyspnea. The child concealed the history of trauma due to bullying, complicating diagnosis, and was initially evaluated with tuberculosis and oncological panels due to the bloody effusion, until a different diagnosis was made.

Similarly, our patient presented with recurring pulmonary symptoms—chest pain and repeated right-sided pleural fluid collections. Detailed, repeated history-taking eventually uncovered previous abdominal trauma, with MRCP findings pointing to pancreatic pathology. This syndrome may thus mimic pulmonary conditions, potentially diverting clinicians toward incorrect diagnoses unless thorough histories and relevant imaging are performed.

Fluid accumulation in PPF occurs due to external trauma or infection-induced ductal or pseudocyst rupture, resulting in pancreatic fluid leakage. Rupture at the anterior pancreas allows fluid to flow into the abdominal cavity, leading to ascites, whereas posterior rupture causes fluid to accumulate in the retroperitoneal space, track upward through the aortic or esophageal hiatus into the mediastinum, and ultimately through the pleura to create a pleural effusion. An internal pancreatic fistula behaves as a conduit, diverting pancreatic secretions into the thoracic cavity. In adults, chronic pancreatitis resulting in PPF is primarily attributed to alcohol. Uchiyama et al’s comprehensive review in Japan reported 113 cases, with 99% linked to chronic alcoholic pancreatitis and 68% presenting chest symptoms.

In contrast, genetic factors are the predominant cause of chronic pancreatitis in children. Well-characterized hereditary forms involve mutations such as those in the PRSS1 gene, CF transmembrane regulator gene, SPINK1 gene, and chymotrypsin C gene, with PRSS1 mutations R122H and N291 (exons 3 and 2) being especially noteworthy. For children with recurrent or chronic pancreatitis, a detailed family history should be undertaken, considering the possibility of hereditary pancreatitis.

Diagnosis is frequently delayed, as extensive pulmonary evaluations often precede suspicion of a pancreatic origin. Pleural fluid amylase levels equal to or exceeding 1000 IU/L are considered pathognomonic for PPF. Differential diagnoses such as tuberculosis, esophageal rupture, and lung or pancreatic neoplasms should also be considered, as they may elevate pleural amylase. PPF is distinguished by pancreatic-type amylase in the pleural fluid; in contrast, other conditions yielding increased levels usually have salivary-type amylase. Typing of pleural amylase may aid diagnostic evaluation in suspected cases. MRCP demonstrates a 70–80% sensitivity for fistula visualization and assessment of duct anatomy. While CT imaging is effective at showing pancreatic calcification, pseudocysts, or duct dilation, direct visualization of the fistula occurs in only 33–47% of cases. Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) remains the gold standard for intervention planning, with fistula detection rates in children
