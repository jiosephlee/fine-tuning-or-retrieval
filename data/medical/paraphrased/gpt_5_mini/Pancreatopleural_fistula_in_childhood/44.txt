Title: Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood

Hans Vaish, Vipin Vaish, Shalini Suri, Vasudha Tomar

Abstract:

Summary: A preadolescent male experienced multiple episodes of right-sided pleural effusions, accompanied by chest discomfort and difficulty breathing, which resolved temporarily following thoracentesis. The aspirated pleural fluid was persistently dark red in appearance. In light of ongoing effusions, a right-sided intercostal drain was inserted. Further history identified recent abdominal injury related to impact from a bicycle handle. Chest CT revealed areas of consolidation on the right, presence of hydropneumothorax, and a multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right paraesophageal area to the pancreatic tail. Both serum and pleural fluid analyses showed elevated amylase and lipase concentrations. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography demonstrated bilateral pancreatopleural fistulas, with the right side more prominently involved. Management included keeping the patient nil by mouth, initiation of total parenteral nutrition, and administration of Octreotide. Within 10 days, pleural drainage diminished, the chest tube was withdrawn, and the patient was discharged.

Background:

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is a particularly uncommon condition that manifests as recurrent, large, and often hemorrhagic pleural effusions. It has been documented in the settings of acute or chronic pancreatitis as well as pancreatic trauma. The diagnosis can be challenging since patients commonly experience respiratory distress from repeated pleural effusions, while the underlying issue actually originates in the pancreas. This often results in delayed recognition as investigations initially focus on identifying thoracic causes. To date, the literature describes only 47 cases of PPF in children, compared to over 300 reported in the adult population.

Case presentation:

A young patient presented to the outpatient clinic, accompanied by his mother, with a 1-week history of breathing difficulties and right-sided chest pain. The mother reported that the child’s respiratory distress had been progressively worsening over the past 7 days. There was no family history of consanguinity. Notably, the child’s grandfather had previously been treated for pulmonary tuberculosis (Koch's), and the mother acknowledged household exposure. Dietary history revealed regular intake of processed, high-calorie foods, while consumption of fresh produce and balanced homemade meals was limited. The patient’s weight measured 44 kg, height was 112 cm, resulting in a body mass index of 35. On physical assessment, the respiratory rate was 20 breaths per minute, demonstrating mild tachypnoea. Chest wall expansion was diminished on the right side. Percussion yielded stony dullness in the right subscapular, infra-axillary, and axillary zones. Tactile vocal fremitus was markedly reduced on the right compared to the left. Breath sounds were decreased in the same right-sided regions. No pallor, cyanosis, icterus, digital clubbing, or lymphadenopathy was detected. Abdominal examination revealed a soft abdomen without evidence of hepatosplenomegaly. Neurologically, the child was alert and oriented to time, place, and person. Cardiac evaluation revealed tachycardia, but no murmurs were appreciated. On room air, oxygen saturation ranged from 88%–89%, with a heart rate of 110 beats/minute. Given the tachypnoea and respiratory compromise, the patient was transferred to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit and administered supplemental oxygen via facemask at a rate of 5 L/min. A chest X-ray was obtained, and empiric antibiotic therapy with piperacillin-tazobactam was initiated following blood culture collection. Imaging demonstrated a right pleural effusion with blunting of the costophrenic angle (figure 1) (Table 2). Bedside lung ultrasonography estimated the effusion volume at approximately one litre (figure 2) (Table 2). Under sterile conditions, thoracocentesis was performed, and about one litre of dark red pleural fluid was aspirated. Following the procedure, tachypnoea resolved, oxygen saturation on ambient air improved to 95%–98%, chest pain lessened, and the patient discontinued oxygen within 24 hours. The following day, he was transferred to the general ward and subsequently discharged. The pleural fluid was submitted for laboratory analysis.

However, the child was re-admitted 6 days later with recurrent symptoms of right-sided chest pain, tachypnoea, and respiratory distress. Upon return to the PICU, chest radiography once more confirmed right-sided pleural effusion. Repeat thoracocentesis removed another litre of similar dark red pleural fluid. Pleural fluid adenosine deaminase (ADA) was found to be mildly elevated (Table 1), and given the positive contact history for tuberculosis, empiric antitubercular therapy was initiated. After 5 more days, the child was again admitted with identical symptoms. On reassessing the patient’s history, it was revealed that he had sustained blunt trauma to the abdomen from a bicycle handle one month previously. A contrast-enhanced chest CT was obtained prior to planned intercostal drain (ICD) placement. CT imaging indicated patchy consolidation within the right lung, right hydropneumothorax, and a complex lobulated hypodense collection in the right pleural space, tracking from the right paraoesophageal region to the left upper retroperitoneum near the pancreatic tail (Table 2). An ICD was placed, yielding approximately one litre of dark red fluid (figure 3). The CT results, the recurrent dark red pleural aspirate, and the history of abdominal trauma raised suspicion for a pancreaticopleural fistula (PPF). An MRCP performed the same day demonstrated evidence of pancreatitis, with a tubular cystic collection arising from the distal pancreatic body and tail, extending posteriorly into the retroperitoneum, and tracking superiorly through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity. There was also a narrow communication seen with the left pleural space. The collection was communicating with the distal pancreatic duct, with proximal duct narrowing noted. The MRCP supported the diagnosis of PPF (figures 4-6) (table 1). Serum and pleural fluid samples were sent for amylase and lipase quantification. Pleural fluid analysis showed markedly raised amylase (6118 U/L) and lipase (7211 U/L) levels (table 2), corroborating PPF. Differential diagnosis:

The patient exhibited pleural effusion and chest pain, with a clear history of abdominal trauma occurring one month prior. After considering post-traumatic pancreatitis, alternative etiologies for pleural effusion were excluded. The child lacked symptoms such as fever, chronic cough, or weight loss. Although there was a family history of Koch’s contact and pleural fluid ADA showed mild elevation, GeneXpert for MTB returned negative, effectively excluding pulmonary Koch’s. No fever was present, nor was there a neutrophilic exudate in the pleural fluid, ruling out empyema. While amylase-rich pleural fluid can be found in malignancies such as lung cancer, leukemia, lymphoma, and metastatic tumors, malignancy was dismissed due to absence of fever and weight loss, along with a normal blood profile and normal peripheral smear. Pleural fluid cytology also failed to detect any malignant cells. Boerhaave’s syndrome (esophageal perforation) was considered, given its association with amylase-rich pleural fluid, but excluded since the child had regular bowel movements and no reported chest pain or vomiting. Acquired diaphragmatic hernia was not considered likely, as imaging did not show abdominal organs in the thoracic cavity. Hepatic hydrothorax was ruled out, with normal liver function tests, unremarkable liver imaging, and no history of chronic liver disease. Renal causes, including hydronephrosis, were eliminated owing to normal renal imaging and renal function studies.

Treatment:

The patient received humidified oxygen via mask as respiratory support in the PICU. Ultrasound-guided thoracocentesis was performed on two occasions, with approximately one liter of blood-tinged pleural fluid withdrawn each time. Antitubercular medications were commenced empirically, but discontinued once the diagnosis was confirmed using MRCP. Upon the third hospital admission, an intercostal drain (ICD) was inserted on the right side under strict aseptic conditions, again yielding about one liter of blood-stained pleural fluid collected in the ICD bag. Given that PPF is a rare occurrence in pediatric patients with no established treatment protocols, management relies on published case reports. Conservative measures were initially selected; the child was kept nil per oral and commenced on octreotide infusion at a dose of 30 µg/kg/day (range 10–40 µg/kg/day). Total parenteral nutrition and pancreatic enzyme supplementation were initiated. Over a period of 10 days, the amount of fluid draining from the ICD gradually diminished and ultimately ceased. The ICD was then removed and the patient was subsequently discharged.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following hospital discharge, the patient has remained asymptomatic. He continues to attend monthly follow-up appointments with me. Since leaving the hospital, there have been no additional incidents of recurrent pleural effusions (S17). He has implemented recommended lifestyle adjustments, including a balanced diet and consistent physical activity. He has resumed his usual routines and is back to attending school consistently. He is able to participate in vigorous activities such as running and playing without limitation.

Discussion:

PPF is a highly uncommon complication of pancreatic disease seen in children. To date, only 47 pediatric cases have been documented. It can occur secondary to both acute and chronic pancreatitis or following traumatic injury in pediatric patients. The precise frequency among children is undetermined, but PPF complicates about 0.4% of pancreatitis cases overall. Mutations in the serine peptidase inhibitor Kazal type 1 (SPINK1) and PRSS1 genes are found in up to 30% of pediatric chronic pancreatitis cases. Blunt trauma to the abdomen is a significant non-genetic cause, accounting for 32% in the 25-child cohort evaluated by Yang et al. Idiopathic cases are still reported, likely reflecting undetected genetic or ductal abnormalities. Pancreatic fluid may flow from a disrupted duct or pseudocyst through anatomical diaphragmatic openings—typically the aortic or esophageal hiatus, and less frequently via direct erosion through the diaphragm—forming a fistula into the pleural space. A majority of cases go undetected initially because children frequently present with thoracic complaints, prompting an extensive search for pulmonary causes, while the true pathology resides in the abdomen. Review of the literature identified 21 relevant reports, showing that 68% of patients exhibited primarily respiratory symptoms—such as large or recurring unilateral or bilateral pleural effusions, breathlessness, and cough. Fewer than 20% displayed abdominal signs like epigastric pain, vomiting, or weight loss; constitutional symptoms (e.g., fever) were seen in 24% of cases. Zi Yin Shang (Frontiers in Paediatrics 2023) described an 8-year-old boy with PPF presenting solely with a protruding left chest for two weeks—a presentation noted to be quite rare. In this patient, pleural effusion gradually developed after abdominal trauma, with sufficient function from the unaffected lung allowing adaptation, thereby masking typical respiratory complaints such as cough or breathlessness. Moreover, the child had experienced bullying and intentionally withheld his abdominal trauma history. He was evaluated for tuberculosis and malignancy due to hemorrhagic pleural effusion, but ultimately the diagnosis diverged. Similarly, in our case, the child experienced recurrent chest pain and repeated right-sided pleural fluid collections. The history of abdominal injury surfaced only after repeated inquiry, and MRCP findings supported a diagnosis related to pancreatic disease. Thus, PPF often masquerades as a pulmonary disorder, potentially misleading clinicians unless a thorough history and appropriate imaging are obtained. 

PPF fluid accumulation is caused by external trauma or infection-induced rupture of the pancreatic duct or pseudocyst, resulting in pancreatic fluid leakage. Anterior duct ruptures release fluid into the peritoneal space, leading to ascites; posterior ruptures allow pancreatic secretions to flow into the retroperitoneum, ascend through the least resistant path, enter the mediastinum via the aortic or esophageal hiatus, and breach the pleura, causing pleural effusion. An internal fistula acts as a conduit, channeling pancreatic secretions into the chest cavity. In adults, chronic pancreatitis due to alcohol is the predominant underlying cause for PPF. Uchiyama et al's comprehensive countrywide review in Japan identified 113 cases, with 99% attributable to chronic alcoholic pancreatitis and chest symptoms present in 68% of subjects. Conversely, in the pediatric population, hereditary factors are more significant in chronic pancreatitis causation. Known genetic drivers include mutations in PRSS1, CFTR, SPINK1, and chymotrypsin C genes, notably the R122H and N291 variants within PRSS1 exon 3 and exon 2, respectively. When pancreatitis recurs or becomes chronic in children, detailed family history and genetic testing should be undertaken to evaluate for hereditary pancreatitis.

Diagnosis is often delayed since extensive pulmonary investigation usually precedes consideration of a pancreatic etiology. Pleural fluid amylase concentrations ≥1000 IU/L are diagnostic of PPF. However, conditions such as tuberculosis, esophageal perforation, and lung or pancreatic tumors must be ruled out, as they can also elevate pleural fluid amylase. The presence of pancreatic-type amylase distinguishes PPF, while other conditions typically involve salivary-type amylase; therefore, pleural fluid amylase subtyping should be included in the diagnostic approach. MRCP provides 70–80% sensitivity for visualizing fistulas and duct anatomy; CT can show pancreatic calcifications, pseudocysts, or ductal dilation, but directly depicts the fistula in only 33–47% of cases. Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) remains the definitive modality for therapeutic planning, with fistula visualization rates in children ranging from 38% to 78%.
