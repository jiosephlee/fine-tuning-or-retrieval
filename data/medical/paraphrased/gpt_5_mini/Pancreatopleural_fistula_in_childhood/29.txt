Title: Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood

Hans Vaish, Vipin Vaish, Shalini Suri, Vasudha Tomar

Abstract:

Summary: An older child presented with repeated right-sided pleural effusions, accompanied by chest pain and difficulty breathing, which showed transient relief following aspiration. The aspirated pleural fluid was persistently dark red in color. Owing to ongoing effusions, a right intercostal drain was inserted. On review of his history, there was a recent incident of abdominal trauma caused by a bicycle handle. Chest computed tomography demonstrated patchy right-sided consolidation, hydropneumothorax, and a multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right paraesophageal area toward the pancreatic tail. Amylase and lipase levels were elevated in both serum and pleural fluid samples. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography established the presence of bilateral pancreatopleural fistulas (right side greater than left). Management consisted of nil per oral, administration of total parenteral nutrition, and Octreotide. Within 10 days, pleural drainage reduced, allowing removal of the chest tube, and the patient was subsequently discharged.

Background:

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is an exceptionally uncommon condition, characterized by repeated, substantial pleural effusions containing blood. This finding has been seen in patients with acute or chronic pancreatitis and following pancreatic injury. Diagnosing PPF can be challenging because it manifests primarily with respiratory symptoms arising from persistent pleural fluid accumulation, while the underlying issue originates in the pancreas. This discrepancy often results in delayed identification, as the focus typically remains on detecting thoracic causes. In the pediatric population, only 47 instances of PPF have been documented, in contrast to over 300 adult cases.

Case presentation:

A child presented to the outpatient clinic with his mother, reporting a one-week history of breathing difficulties and right-sided chest pain. According to the mother, his symptoms had become progressively worse over the past seven days. There was no record of consanguinity. The mother mentioned exposure to Koch’s, as the child's grandfather had been diagnosed with pulmonary Koch’s and had received appropriate therapy. Dietary evaluation showed the child frequently consumed processed, high-calorie foods, and had inadequate intake of fresh fruits, vegetables, or balanced home-prepared meals. His weight was 44 kg and height 112 cm, with a calculated BMI of 35. Clinical assessment revealed a respiratory rate of 20/min, consistent with mild tachypnoea. Chest excursion was reduced on the right side. Percussion revealed stony dullness in the right subscapular, right infra-axillary, and axillary areas. There was a marked reduction in vocal fremitus on the right compared to the left. Decreased breath sounds were also identified in the same right-sided regions. Examination did not reveal pallor, cyanosis, icterus, clubbing, or lymphadenopathy. The abdomen was soft, non-tender, with no evidence of hepatosplenomegaly. The child was alert and oriented. Cardiovascular examination indicated tachycardia in the absence of murmurs. Room air oxygen saturation was between 88% and 89%, with a heart rate of 110/min. Due to respiratory distress and tachypnoea, the patient was admitted to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit and commenced on oxygen via mask at 5 l/min. A chest radiograph was performed, and empiric antibiotic therapy with piperacillin-tazobactam was started following collection of blood cultures. Imaging showed a right pleural effusion with blunting of the right costophrenic angle (figure 1, Table 2). Bedside lung ultrasound estimated the effusion at approximately one litre (figure 2, Table 2). Right-sided thoracentesis was performed under strict aseptic technique, yielding about one litre of dark red pleural fluid. Post-procedure, the patient’s tachypnoea resolved, and oxygen saturation on room air improved to 95%–98%. The child’s chest pain diminished, and he was weaned from oxygen within 24 hours. He was transferred to the ward the following day and subsequently discharged. The aspirated pleural fluid was submitted for analysis. 

However, the child was readmitted six days later with recurrence of right-sided chest pain, tachypnoea, and dyspnoea. He was again transferred to the PICU, where repeat chest X-ray showed a right-sided pleural effusion. Another thoracentesis was conducted, draining roughly one litre of dark red fluid. Pleural fluid adenosine deaminase (ADA) was mildly increased (Table 1), and given the prior Koch’s exposure, antitubercular therapy was started empirically. 

Within five days, the patient was admitted for a third time, exhibiting similar symptoms of right-sided chest pain and respiratory difficulty. Further history-taking revealed he had experienced blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handlebar a month earlier. A contrast-enhanced CT scan of the chest was performed prior to intercostal drain (ICD) insertion. The scan demonstrated patchy air space consolidation in the right lung with right-sided hydropneumothorax, and a lobulated, multiloculated hypodense collection tracking from the right pleural space at the right paraoesophageal level to the left-sided upper retroperitoneum extending to the tail of the pancreas (Table 2). An ICD was placed, draining approximately one litre of dark red pleural fluid (figure 3). These imaging results, combined with the patient’s history of abdominal trauma and the appearance of the pleural aspirate, raised suspicion for a pancreaticopleural fistula (PPF). 

Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) was performed the same day, revealing features of pancreatitis with a tubular cystic collection in the distal body and tail of the pancreas extending posteriorly into the retroperitoneum and superiorly through the oesophageal hiatus into the right pleural space. A narrow tract was also identified extending into the left pleural cavity. The collection was noted to communicate with the distal pancreatic duct, with upstream duct narrowing. The MRCP findings were indicative of PPF (figures 4–6; Table 1). Levels of amylase and lipase in serum and pleural fluid were measured, with pleural fluid demonstrating markedly elevated amylase (6118 U/L) and lipase (7211 U/L) concentrations (Table 2), corroborating the diagnosis of PPF. Differential diagnosis:

The patient exhibited pleural effusion accompanied by chest pain, and had a documented episode of abdominal trauma one month prior. All etiologies for pleural effusion aside from post-traumatic pancreatitis were excluded. This child lacked a history of fever, prolonged cough, or unintentional weight loss. Although there was a documented family contact with Koch’s disease and pleural fluid ADA levels were slightly raised, GeneXpert for MTB was negative, leading to the exclusion of pulmonary Koch’s disease. The absence of fever and a neutrophilic exudate in the pleural fluid excluded empyema. While malignancies such as lung cancer, leukemia, lymphoma, and metastatic tumors may present with amylase-rich pleural fluid, the lack of fever, weight loss, normal blood work, and a normal peripheral smear excluded neoplastic processes. Pleural fluid cytology did not demonstrate malignant cells. Boerhaave’s syndrome (esophageal perforation) was considered; however, it was ruled out due to normal bowel movements, absence of chest pain and vomiting. Acquired diaphragmatic hernia was excluded since chest imaging did not reveal abdominal organs within the thoracic cavity. Hepatic hydrothorax was dismissed as liver function tests and liver imaging were normal, and there was no history suggestive of chronic liver disease. Finally, normal renal imaging and kidney function tests excluded renal causes such as hydronephrosis.

Treatment:

In the pediatric intensive care unit, the patient received respiratory support with humidified oxygen delivered via mask. Ultrasound-guided thoracocentesis was performed on two separate occasions, with each procedure yielding approximately one liter of blood-tinged pleural fluid. Antitubercular medications were initiated empirically but discontinued upon confirmation of the diagnosis with MRCP. During a third hospital admission, an intercostal drain (ICD) was placed on the right side under strict aseptic technique, resulting in drainage of another liter of blood-stained pleural fluid into the ICD collection bag. Pleuro-pancreatic fistula (PPF) is an infrequent and unusual condition in children and currently has no established management protocols. Therapeutic approaches are guided by available case reports. Conservative management was selected initially; the child was kept nil per oral and commenced on an Octreotide infusion at a dose of 30 µg/kg/day (range: 10–40 µg/kg/day). Total parenteral nutrition and supplementation with pancreatic enzymes were initiated. Over a period of 10 days, drainage from the ICD diminished progressively and ultimately ceased. The ICD was then removed, and the patient was discharged from the hospital.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following his hospital discharge, the child has experienced no further symptoms. He continues to see me for monthly follow-up appointments. Since leaving the hospital, he has not had any additional episodes of recurrent pleural effusion (S17). He has adopted recommended lifestyle changes, maintaining a balanced diet and engaging in regular physical activity. The patient has resumed his usual routines, including consistent school attendance. He is able to participate in physically demanding activities such as running and playing without limitations.

Discussion:

Panpleural fistula (PPF) represents an extremely rare complication associated with pancreatic disorders in children, with only 47 cases described in the literature to date. PPF may occur in the context of either acute or chronic pancreatitis, or following pediatric abdominal trauma. The precise frequency in children remains uncertain, although it complicates roughly 0.4% of pancreatitis cases in general. Genetic mutations—including serine peptidase inhibitor Kazal type 1 (SPINK1) and PRSS1—contribute substantially, being implicated in up to 30% of pediatric chronic pancreatitis cases. Blunt abdominal trauma is another significant non-genetic factor, accounting for 32% within a 25-case series reviewed by Yang et al. Idiopathic presentations persist, possibly due to unrecognized genetic or ductal anomalies.

Pancreatic fluid may enter the pleural space by leaking from a disrupted duct or pseudocyst, passing through natural openings in the diaphragm—most commonly the aortic or esophageal hiatus—or by direct transdiaphragmatic erosion, thereby creating a fistulous pathway into the pleural cavity. Many cases are overlooked since patients often present with thoracic complaints, prompting evaluation for pulmonary disease while the primary pathology resides in the abdomen. Literature review identified 21 pertinent publications, wherein 68% of cases presented primarily with respiratory signs, such as large and recurrent unilateral or bilateral pleural effusions, shortness of breath, and cough. Fewer than 20% reported abdominal manifestations—including epigastric discomfort, emesis, or weight loss—and 24% showed constitutional features like fever. A report by Zi Yin Shang in Frontiers in Paediatrics (2023) highlighted a case of PPF in an 8-year-old boy, whose sole symptom was a bulging left chest over two weeks. Initial presentation with chest bulging is exceedingly uncommon. In the described case, the pleural effusion likely accumulated gradually following abdominal trauma. Compensatory adaptation by the healthy lung allowed for an absence of typical respiratory complaints such as cough or dyspnoea. Furthermore, the child experienced school bullying and intentionally withheld the history of abdominal trauma. Evaluation for hemorrhagic pleural effusion focused on tuberculosis and malignancy, but diagnosis ultimately diverged. Similarly, our patient exhibited recurrent pulmonary manifestations in the form of chest pain and repeated right-sided pleural fluid collections. Disclosure of abdominal trauma was achieved only after repeated interviewing, and MRCP findings directed us toward a pancreatic origin. Therefore, this condition frequently masquerades as a pulmonary disease, potentially leading clinicians away from the correct diagnosis unless detailed history and appropriate imaging are pursued.

PPF occurs when external forces or infection cause rupture of the pancreatic duct or a pseudocyst, resulting in leakage of pancreatic secretions. Should the rupture occur anteriorly, pancreatic fluid enters the peritoneal cavity, leading to ascites; posterior ruptures result in retroperitoneal leakage, which may track along paths of least resistance into the mediastinum via the aortic or esophageal hiatus, then penetrate the pleura to create a pleural effusion. The internal fistula functions analogously to a leaking pipe, channeling pancreatic fluids into the thoracic compartment.

In adults, chronic pancreatitis—and consequently PPF—is overwhelmingly caused by alcohol. The most extensive review to date, conducted by Uchiyama et al, documented 113 cases within Japanese literature, establishing that 99% were attributable to chronic alcohol-induced pancreatitis, with chest symptoms dominating presentation in 68% of cases. Conversely, genetic etiologies predominate in pediatric chronic pancreatitis. Mutations in PRSS1, CF transmembrane regulator, SPINK1, and chymotrypsin C genes are recognized causes of hereditary pancreatitis; specifically, PRSS1 exon 3 (R122H) and exon 2 (N291) mutations are particularly notable. When pediatric pancreatitis becomes recurrent or chronic, thorough family history and consideration of hereditary causes are essential.

Diagnosis of PPF is frequently delayed, as the majority of cases undergo comprehensive pulmonary investigation prior to the recognition of pancreatic etiology. A pleural fluid amylase concentration of ≥1000 IU/L is considered diagnostic for PPF. Differential diagnosis must include tuberculosis, esophageal perforation, and malignancies of the lung or pancreas, given their association with elevated pleural fluid amylase. Pancreatic-type amylase differentiates PPF from other causes, while those conditions typically exhibit salivary-type amylase. Typing of pleural fluid amylase may assist in identifying PPF.
