Title: Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood

Hans Vaish, Vipin Vaish, Shalini Suri, Vasudha Tomar

Abstract:

A male child in late childhood developed repeated right-sided pleural effusions accompanied by chest pain and difficulty breathing, with transient relief following fluid aspiration. The pleural fluid consistently appeared dark red. Persistent effusions led to the placement of a right intercostal drain. History revealed a recent abdominal injury caused by a bicycle handle. Chest CT demonstrated patchy consolidation in the right lung, hydropneumothorax, and a multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right paraesophageal area to the pancreatic tail. Elevated levels of amylase and lipase were found in both serum and pleural fluid. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography identified bilateral pancreatopleural fistulas, predominantly on the right. Management included nil per oral, total parenteral nutrition, and administration of Octreotide. Over the course of ten days, the amount of drainage gradually reduced, allowing for removal of the chest tube and discharge of the patient.

Background:

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) represents a very uncommon condition, characterized by repeated, large-volume pleural effusions that may be blood-tinged. PPF occurs in the context of acute pancreatitis, chronic pancreatitis, or pancreatic injury. Clinical presentation can be challenging, as patients commonly exhibit symptoms of respiratory distress caused by recurrent pleural collections; yet, the underlying problem is pancreatic in origin. This often postpones diagnosis because the clinical focus initially centers on possible thoracic causes. In pediatric populations, only 47 instances of PPF have been documented, while more than 300 cases have been described in adults.

Case presentation:

A pediatric patient was brought to the outpatient clinic by his mother due to complaints of respiratory difficulty and right-sided chest pain persisting for one week. According to the mother, the child’s respiratory symptoms had gradually worsened over a period of seven days. There was no consanguinity in the family. She mentioned that the child had been exposed to tuberculosis through his grandfather, who was treated for pulmonary tuberculosis (Koch’s). Dietary assessment indicated a pattern of frequent intake of processed, high-calorie foods, with minimal consumption of fresh produce and balanced home-cooked meals. The patient weighed 44 kg, had a height of 112 cm, and a body mass index of 35. On physical exam, he had a respiratory rate of 20/min with signs of mild tachypnea, and decreased chest movement on the right side. Percussion revealed stony dullness in the right subscapular, infra-axillary, and axillary zones. There was marked reduction in vocal fremitus on the right in comparison to the left. Breath sounds were diminished over the right subscapular, right infra-axillary, and axillary areas. There were no findings of pallor, cyanosis, jaundice, digital clubbing, or lymphadenopathy. The abdominal examination was unremarkable, with no hepatosplenomegaly detected. The child was alert and appropriately oriented. Cardiovascular exam demonstrated tachycardia but no murmurs. On ambient air, oxygen saturation was 88%–89% and the heart rate was 110/min. Given the tachypnea and evidence of respiratory compromise, the patient was transferred to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit and administered oxygen via face mask at 5 l/min. A chest X-ray was performed, and empirical intravenous antibiotics (piperacillin-tazobactam) were commenced following collection of blood cultures. Imaging revealed a right-sided pleural effusion with obliteration of the right costophrenic angle (figure 1; Table 2). Bedside thoracic ultrasound estimated approximately one liter of pleural fluid (figure 2; Table 2). Under sterile technique, a right-sided thoracocentesis was performed, evacuating about one liter of dark red fluid. The patient’s tachypnea subsided post-procedure and oxygen saturation on room air improved to 95%–98%. Chest pain also lessened, and oxygen therapy was discontinued within 24 hours. He was subsequently transferred to the general pediatric ward the next day and later discharged. The aspirated pleural fluid was sent for further diagnostic analysis. 

However, six days after discharge, the patient was readmitted with recurrent symptoms: right-sided chest pain, tachypnea, and breathing difficulty. He was again placed in the PICU, where repeat chest X-ray again identified a right pleural effusion. Thoracocentesis was repeated, yielding about one liter of similarly dark red pleural fluid. Pleural fluid Adenosine Deaminase (ADA) was mildly elevated (Table 1), and due to the positive tuberculosis contact history, empiric antitubercular therapy was initiated. 

Five days later, the child was admitted for a third time with persistent right-sided chest pain and shortness of breath. The case history was reviewed in greater detail, and the child disclosed having experienced blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handlebar approximately one month prior. Before placing an intercostal drain (ICD), a contrast-enhanced CT scan of the chest was obtained, revealing patchy airspace consolidation in the right lung, a right hydropneumothorax, and an elongated, lobulated multiloculated hypodense collection tracking from the right pleural space at the right paraesophageal area down to the upper left retroperitoneum to the region of the pancreatic tail (Table 2). An ICD was inserted, and approximately one liter of dark red pleural fluid was drained (figure 3). The CT appearance, combined with the dark red pleural fluid and trauma history, indicated the likelihood of a pancreaticopleural fistula (PPF). Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) performed the same day identified findings of pancreatitis with a tubular cystic collection at the distal body and tail of the pancreas extending posteriorly within the retroperitoneum and ascending superiorly through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity. There was also a narrow communication to the left pleural cavity. The collection appeared to connect with the distal pancreatic duct, which was narrowed just proximal to this point. MRCP suggested a diagnosis of PPF (figures 4–6; Table 1). Differential diagnosis:

The patient exhibited pleural effusion and chest pain and had a clear history of abdominal trauma one month prior. After excluding other potential etiologies for pleural effusion, post-traumatic pancreatitis remained as a probable cause. The child lacked any history of fever, persistent cough, or weight loss. Although there was a positive family history of Koch's exposure and pleural fluid ADA showed a slight increase, the GeneXpert test for MTB was negative, thereby eliminating pulmonary Koch's from consideration. The absence of fever and the lack of neutrophilic exudate in the pleural fluid excluded empyema. While certain malignancies (such as lung cancer, leukemia, lymphoma, or metastatic tumors) may present with pleural fluid containing high amylase levels, malignancy was deemed unlikely due to the patient’s lack of fever and weight loss, normal complete blood count, unremarkable peripheral smear, and pleural fluid cytology findings showing no malignant cells. Amylase-rich pleural fluid can also occur with Boerhaave’s syndrome (esophageal perforation); this was discounted since the child was having regular bowel movements and reported no chest pain or vomiting. Acquired diaphragmatic hernia was not supported, as chest imaging did not show any abdominal organs within the thoracic cavity. Hepatic hydrothorax was excluded on the basis of normal liver function tests, unremarkable liver imaging, and absence of chronic liver disease history. Lastly, renal causes or hydronephrosis were ruled out due to normal renal imaging and function results.

Treatment:

In the PICU, the patient received respiratory support using humidified oxygen delivered via mask. Ultrasound-guided thoracocentesis was performed twice, with approximately one litre of blood-tinged pleural fluid withdrawn each session. Antitubercular medications were initiated empirically, but discontinued once the diagnosis was confirmed through MRCP. During the patient's third hospitalization, an intercostal drain (ICD) was placed on the right side under aseptic conditions, yielding another litre of blood-stained pleural fluid collected in the ICD bag. Given the rarity of PPF in paediatric patients and the absence of established treatment protocols, therapeutic decisions were based on individual case reports. A conservative management strategy was implemented initially; the child was made nil per oral and started on Octreotide infusion at 30 µg/kg/day (range: 10–40 µg/kg/day). Total parenteral nutrition as well as pancreatic enzyme supplementation were commenced. Over the next 10 days, drainage from the ICD progressively decreased and eventually ceased. The ICD was removed, and the patient was subsequently discharged from the hospital.

Outcome and follow-up:

Since leaving the hospital, the child has remained asymptomatic. He attends scheduled monthly follow-up appointments with me. There have been no further occurrences of recurrent pleural effusions post-discharge (S17). The patient has adopted lifestyle changes, maintaining a balanced diet and engaging in regular physical activity as recommended. He has resumed his routine, including regular school attendance, and is able to participate in vigorous activities such as running and playing without limitation.

Discussion:

Panpleuroperitoneal fistula (PPF) represents a highly uncommon complication associated with pancreatic pathology in children. To date, only 47 paediatric cases have been described in the literature. PPF may occur in the context of either acute or chronic pancreatitis, or following paediatric abdominal trauma. The precise incidence in this age group remains undetermined; however, PPF accounts for approximately 0.4% of all pancreatitis complications. Genetic factors, specifically serine peptidase inhibitor Kazal type 1 (SPINK1) and PRSS1 mutations, play a significant role, contributing to 30% of paediatric chronic pancreatitis cases. Non-genetic factors, such as blunt abdominal trauma, are also relevant, as highlighted in the study by Yang et al., in which such injuries comprised 32% among their cohort of 25 patients. There are ongoing reports of idiopathic cases, potentially attributable to undetected genetic or anatomical ductal abnormalities. 

Pancreatic fluid may dissect from a ruptured pancreatic duct or pseudocyst through anatomical diaphragmatic pathways—primarily the aortic or oesophageal hiatus—or rarely by direct diaphragmatic erosion, resulting in a fistulous tract to the pleural cavity. A majority of such cases are overlooked initially since they present predominantly with thoracic symptoms; diagnostic efforts frequently focus on the respiratory system, although the origin is intra-abdominal. From a review of the literature encompassing 21 relevant reports, 68% of cases featured predominantly respiratory symptoms, such as large, recurrent, unilateral or bilateral pleural effusions, breathlessness, and cough. Less than one-fifth presented with gastrointestinal manifestations, including epigastric pain, emesis, and weight loss, while constitutional symptoms (fever) were documented in 24%. Zi Yin Shang (Frontiers in Paediatrics 2023) described a unique case in an 8-year-old with a bulging left chest for two weeks; initial presentation with chest bulging is exceedingly rare. In that case, pleural fluid developed gradually after abdominal trauma; compensation by the contralateral lung facilitated adaptation, thus the absence of classic respiratory symptoms. Additionally, psychosocial factors led the child to conceal the history of abdominal injury. Work-up focused initially on tuberculosis and malignancy due to haemorrhagic effusion, but eventual diagnosis diverged. Our case similarly demonstrated recurrent thoracic symptoms—chest pain and repeated right-sided pleural effusion—with abdominal trauma disclosed only after detailed history-taking and MRCP pinpointing the pancreatic source. This underscores the potential of PPF to masquerade as a primary pulmonary disorder, necessitating thorough history and appropriate imaging to avoid misdiagnosis. 

Fluid accumulation in PPF results from external trauma or infection causing rupture of the pancreatic duct or pseudocyst, leading to pancreatic secretions leaking. Anterior duct rupture produces ascites by leaking into the peritoneal cavity, while posterior rupture allows fluid to traverse the retroperitoneum and ascend through the diaphragmatic hiatus into the mediastinum, eventually rupturing through the pleura to cause effusion—effectively creating an internal fistula which reroutes pancreatic exocrine output to the chest. In adults, chronic pancreatitis is most often attributable to alcohol abuse, predominating as the cause for PPF. The comprehensive Japanese review by Uchiyama et al. documented 113 cases, revealing 99% linked to alcoholic chronic pancreatitis; chest symptoms were predominant in 68%. Contrastingly, paediatric chronic pancreatitis is typically rooted in genetic mutations—such as PRSS1, CF transmembrane regulator, SPINK1, and chymotrypsin C genes—with specific mutations (R122H and N291 in exons 3 and 2 of PRSS1) frequently implicated. Recurrence or chronicity of pancreatitis in children should prompt a full family history to assess for hereditary causes. 

Diagnostic delays are common, as most children undergo extensive evaluation for respiratory causes before pancreatic origins are considered. A pleural fluid amylase level ≥1000 IU/L is pathognomonic for PPF. Differential diagnosis should include tuberculosis, oesophageal perforation, and malignancies of the lung or pancreas, as these also produce elevated pleural amylase. Distinguishing pancreatic from salivary type amylase in pleural fluid aids differentiation; the latter is elevated in conditions discussed previously. Amylase typing may therefore form part of the investigative process. MRCP demonstrates 70–80% sensitivity for fistula and duct visualisation, whereas CT is best for identifying calcifications, pseudocysts, or ductal dilation but shows fistulas in only 33–47%. ERCP remains the gold standard for therapy planning, with paediatric fistula visualisation rates of 38–78%.
