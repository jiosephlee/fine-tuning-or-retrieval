Title: Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood

Hans Vaish, Vipin Vaish, Shalini Suri, Vasudha Tomar

Abstract:

Summary: A late childhood male developed recurrent right pleural effusions accompanied by chest pain and respiratory compromise, with transient improvement following thoracocentesis. The drained pleural fluid consistently appeared dark red in color. Recurrent collections led to placement of a right intercostal drainage tube. Further history revealed recent blunt abdominal injury caused by a bicycle handlebar. Chest computed tomography demonstrated patchy consolidation in the right lung, hydropneumothorax, and a multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right paraesophageal space toward the pancreatic tail. Elevated levels of amylase and lipase were found in both serum and pleural fluid samples. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography revealed pancreatopleural fistulas bilaterally, with greater involvement on the right. Management included instituting nil per oral, total parenteral nutrition, and administration of Octreotide. After 10 days, drainage lessened, the chest drain was withdrawn, and the patient was subsequently discharged.

Background:

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is a very uncommon condition characterized by recurrent, large, and sometimes hemorrhagic pleural effusions. It has been identified in association with acute or chronic pancreatitis as well as pancreatic trauma. The condition can be challenging to recognize because its clinical manifestations involve respiratory compromise secondary to recurrent pleural collections, leading clinicians to focus on the thoracic region, while the primary disorder originates in the pancreas. To date, there have been only 47 cases documented in the pediatric population, compared to over 300 reported cases in adults.

Case presentation:

A pediatric patient was brought to the outpatient clinic by his mother, presenting with a one-week history of dyspnea and right-sided chest discomfort. According to the mother, his symptoms had progressively worsened over the prior 7 days. There was no report of parental consanguinity. The mother mentioned that the child had a history of contact with his grandfather, who had been treated for pulmonary tuberculosis (Koch's). Dietary review revealed the child frequently consumed processed, calorie-dense foods, while having low intake of fruits, vegetables, and balanced home-cooked meals. His weight measured 44 kg, height was 112 cm, and his BMI calculated at 35.

On physical examination, the child demonstrated a respiratory rate of 20/min with mild tachypnea. Right-sided chest wall expansion was reduced. Percussion revealed stony dullness over the right subscapular, infra-axillary, and axillary areas. Palpation showed markedly decreased vocal fremitus on the right compared to the left. Auscultation found breath sounds diminished in the same right thoracic regions. He did not exhibit pallor, cyanosis, jaundice, digital clubbing, or lymphadenopathy. The abdomen was soft without hepatosplenomegaly. The child was alert and oriented to person, time, and place. Cardiovascular assessment noted tachycardia but was otherwise unremarkable. Oxygen saturation measured 88%–89% on room air, with a pulse rate of 110/min.

Given the respiratory distress and tachypnea, the patient was admitted to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit (PICU) and placed on 5 l/min supplemental oxygen via mask. A chest X-ray was performed, and empirical piperacillin-tazobactam was initiated after obtaining blood cultures. The radiograph indicated a right-sided pleural effusion with blunted right costophrenic angle (figure 1) (Table 2). Point-of-care lung ultrasound estimated the effusion volume at approximately one litre (figure 2) (Table 2). Thoracentesis was performed under aseptic conditions, aspirating about one litre of dark red pleural fluid. After the procedure, tachypnea resolved and room air saturations improved to 95%–98%. Chest pain subsided and oxygen was discontinued within 24 hours. He was transferred to the ward the following day and later discharged. The aspirated pleural fluid was sent for laboratory analysis.

Six days later, the child was readmitted for recurrence of right-sided chest pain, tachypnea, and breathing difficulty. He was returned to the PICU, where imaging again confirmed right-sided pleural effusion. Repeat thoracentesis yielded another litre of dark red fluid. Pleural fluid adenosine deaminase (ADA) was mildly elevated (Table 1), and owing to a positive history of TB contact, empirical anti-tubercular therapy was started.

After five days, the patient was hospitalized for a third time with similar right-sided pleuritic pain and dyspnea. Reevaluation elicited a history of blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle one month earlier. Before placement of an intercostal drainage (ICD) tube, a contrast-enhanced chest CT was obtained, showing patchy air-space consolidation in the right lung, right-sided hydropneumothorax, and an elongated, multiloculated, hypodense collection extending from the right pleura along the right paraesophageal region into the upper left retroperitoneum as far as the pancreatic tail (Table 2). An ICD was inserted, and about one litre of dark red pleural fluid was drained (figure 3). The imaging findings, persistent hemorrhagic pleural fluid, and trauma history indicated a possible pancreaticopleural fistula (PPF).

MRCP performed on the same day demonstrated pancreatitis with a tubular cystic collection at the distal body and tail of the pancreas, extending posteriorly into the retroperitoneum and then ascending through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity; a narrow channel into the left pleural cavity was also observed. The collection communicated with the distal pancreatic duct, which was narrowed proximally to the connection site. These MRCP results (figures 4-6) (Table 1) supported the diagnosis of PPF. Serum and pleural fluid specimens were analyzed for amylase and lipase, which revealed substantially elevated levels in pleural fluid—amylase: 6118 U/L, lipase: 7211 U/L (Table 2)—further substantiating PPF.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient exhibited pleural effusion accompanied by chest pain, following a documented episode of abdominal trauma occurring one month prior. All etiologies of pleural effusion besides post-traumatic pancreatitis were excluded. The child did not report fever, prolonged cough, or weight loss. Although there was a familial history of Koch’s exposure and the pleural fluid ADA was slightly raised, the GeneXpert test for MTB returned negative, thereby excluding pulmonary Koch’s. The absence of fever and lack of neutrophilic exudate in the pleural fluid eliminated the possibility of empyema. Amylase-enriched pleural fluid can be associated with malignancies such as lung cancer, leukemia, lymphoma, and metastatic tumors; however, no fever or weight loss was observed, blood counts and peripheral smear appeared normal, and pleural fluid cytology revealed no malignant cells, thus malignancy was excluded. Boerhaave’s syndrome, which involves esophageal perforation and can present with amylase-rich pleural fluid, was also ruled out since the child had normal bowel movements and lacked history of vomiting or chest pain. Acquired diaphragmatic hernia was excluded because imaging did not demonstrate abdominal organs within the thoracic cavity. Hepatic hydrothorax was excluded on the basis of normal liver function tests and imaging, along with the absence of a history of chronic liver disease. Additionally, normal renal imaging and function studies ruled out renal pathology, including hydronephrosis.

Treatment:

The patient received respiratory support in the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit (PICU) through humidified oxygen delivered via mask. Ultrasound-guided thoracocentesis was performed on two occasions, each time extracting approximately one litre of pleural fluid with a bloody appearance. Empirical antitubercular therapy was initiated, but discontinued after confirmation of diagnosis by MRCP. During the third hospital admission, an intercostal drain (ICD) was placed on the right side under sterile conditions, and about one litre of blood-tinged pleural fluid was collected in the ICD bag. Pleuro-pancreatic fistula (PPF) is rare in children and lacks standardized treatment protocols. Management is primarily guided by individual case reports. Conservative therapy was undertaken initially, and the patient was made nil per oral while starting an Octreotide infusion at a dose of 30 µg/kg/day (range: 10–40 µg/kg/day). Total parenteral nutrition and supplementation of pancreatic enzymes were provided. Over the ensuing 10 days, fluid drainage through the ICD diminished and ultimately ceased. The ICD was subsequently withdrawn, and the patient was discharged home.

Outcome and follow-up:

Since leaving the hospital, the patient has not experienced any symptoms. He continues to see me for monthly follow-up appointments. Following his discharge, there have been no further occurrences of recurrent pleural effusions (S17). He has implemented lifestyle changes, including adherence to a balanced diet and engaging in regular physical activity, as recommended. He has resumed his usual routine and is attending school consistently. The child is able to participate in vigorous activities such as running and playing without limitation.

Discussion:

PPF represents a very uncommon and rare complication of pancreatic disease in children. To date, only 47 cases of paediatric PPF have been reported. It can arise following either acute or chronic pancreatitis, as well as after abdominal trauma in paediatric patients. The true incidence among children remains unclear; however, PPF complicates approximately 0.4% of pancreatitis cases overall. Mutations such as serine peptidase inhibitor Kazal type 1 (SPINK1) and PRSS1 are notable, being present in up to 30% of chronic paediatric pancreatitis. Blunt abdominal trauma is another notable non-genetic precipitant and was identified in 32% of the 25-patient series evaluated by Yang et al. Idiopathic occurrences still present, possibly linked to undetected genetic or ductal abnormalities. Pancreatic secretions may escape from a ruptured duct or pseudocyst, tracking through diaphragmatic hiatuses—primarily the aortic or oesophageal—or through direct erosion of the diaphragm, thereby establishing a fistulous passage to the pleural space. Many cases are overlooked due to predominant thoracic presentations, with clinical focus and investigations centred on respiratory pathology, while the actual disease is of abdominal origin. A review of the literature revealed 21 pertinent publications, indicating that 68% of cases presented mainly with respiratory symptoms, including large or recurrent unilateral or bilateral pleural effusions, breathlessness, and cough. Fewer than 20% exhibited abdominal signs such as epigastric discomfort, vomiting, and weight loss. Constitutional symptoms such as fever occurred in 24%. Zi Yin Shang’s 2023 report in Frontiers in Paediatrics included an 8-year-old boy whose sole symptom was a left-sided chest bulge for two weeks—such presentations are particularly rare. In this case, pleural fluid accumulation likely developed gradually after abdominal trauma, and respiratory adaptation by the unaffected lung masked classic symptoms like cough or dyspnoea. Additionally, the child concealed the trauma history due to school bullying. He underwent investigations for tuberculosis and malignancy because of his haemorrhagic pleural fluid, though ultimately a different diagnosis was reached. In our patient, recurrent respiratory complaints consisted of chest pain and repeated right-sided pleural effusions. Multiple detailed history-taking sessions eventually elicited the abdominal trauma; MRCP led to identification of pancreatic pathology. Thus, this entity often presents with a ‘Pulmonary mask’, misleading clinicians toward purely respiratory diagnoses unless thorough history and appropriate imaging are undertaken.

Fluid accumulation in PPF results either from external trauma or infection, causing rupture of a pancreatic duct or pseudocyst with subsequent pancreatic fluid leakage. If disruption is anterior, fluid collects in the peritoneal cavity, resulting in ascites; posterior rupture allows leakage into the retroperitoneum, where pancreatic secretions follow the least resistance upwards, pass through the aortic or oesophageal hiatus, and penetrate the pleura to produce an effusion. An internal pancreatic fistula functions as a conduit, diverting pancreatic fluids into the thoracic cavity. In adults, chronic pancreatitis attributed to alcohol is the predominant precursor to PPF. Uchiyama et al reported the largest single-country series, detailing 113 cases in Japan, with 99% related to alcohol-induced chronic pancreatitis, and thoracic symptoms dominating in 68%. In contrast, paediatric chronic pancreatitis arises mostly from genetic factors. Pathogenic mutations include those affecting PRSS1, CFTR, SPINK1, and chymotrypsin C genes, with representative PRSS1 mutations at R122H and N291 (exons 3 and 2). For children with recurrent or chronic pancreatitis, comprehensive family history and consideration of hereditary disease are advised.

Delayed diagnosis is frequent since most patients undergo exhaustive respiratory evaluation prior to suspicion of pancreatic origin. Pleural fluid amylase concentrations of ≥1000 IU/L are diagnostic for PPF. Other disorders, such as tuberculosis, oesophageal rupture, and lung or pancreatic cancers, may also elevate pleural amylase and must be excluded. The detection of pancreatic-type amylase within pleural fluid differentiates PPF from these, while other causes show salivary-type amylase elevation; amylase typing can be a useful part of the diagnostic process in suspected PPF cases. MRCP has a sensitivity between 70% and 80% in visualising fistula and ductal structures, while CT is effective in revealing pancreatic calcification, pseudocysts, or ductal dilation but directly shows the fistula in only 33%–47% of cases. Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) remains the gold standard for therapeutic decisions, with paediatric fistula visualisation rates ranging from 38% to 78%.
