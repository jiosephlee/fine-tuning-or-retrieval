Title: Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood

Hans Vaish, Vipin Vaish, Shalini Suri, Vasudha Tomar

Abstract:

Summary: An older child presented with repeated right-sided pleural effusions, accompanied by chest pain and breathing difficulty, which resolved temporarily after thoracentesis. The aspirated pleural fluid was repeatedly described as dark red. Because of the ongoing effusions, a right intercostal drain was inserted. On further questioning, there was a history of recent blunt abdominal injury sustained from impact with a bicycle handle. Chest computed tomography revealed patchy consolidation in the right lung, hydropneumothorax, and a multiloculated hypodense area extending from the right paraesophageal region to the pancreatic tail. Both serum and pleural fluid amylase and lipase levels were found to be elevated. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography demonstrated the presence of bilateral pancreatopleural fistulas, with the right side more affected than the left. Management included keeping the patient nil per os, administering total parenteral nutrition, and giving Octreotide. Over the subsequent ten days, drainage from the chest tube declined, the tube was removed, and the patient was discharged home.

Background:

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is a very uncommon condition that manifests as recurrent, large-volume pleural effusions that are often blood-tinged. It typically arises in the setting of acute or chronic pancreatitis or following pancreatic injury. Diagnosing PPF can be challenging, as patients often exhibit symptoms of respiratory distress related to the recurring pleural fluid collections, causing clinicians to focus on thoracic causes and potentially overlook the pancreatic origin of the problem. To date, there have been only 47 documented pediatric cases of PPF, compared to over 300 adult cases reported in the literature.

Case presentation:

A pediatric patient was brought to the outpatient clinic by his mother, who reported that he had experienced difficulty breathing and right-sided chest pain for one week. According to the mother, these symptoms had progressively worsened over seven days. There was no family history of consanguinity. She also reported that the child had been exposed to Koch’s contact via his grandfather, who had pulmonary tuberculosis and had been treated properly. The child's diet was characterized by frequent consumption of processed, high-calorie foods, with limited intake of fresh fruits, vegetables, and nutritionally balanced home-cooked meals. The patient weighed 44 kg, stood 112 cm tall, and had a body mass index of 35. On assessment, he exhibited a respiratory rate of 20 per minute, indicative of mild tachypnea. Examination revealed diminished chest expansion on the right side. Percussion produced a stony dull sound over the right subscapular, right infra-axillary, and axillary regions. Vocal fremitus was notably reduced on the right compared to the left. Breath sounds were also diminished in the aforementioned right thoracic regions. There was no evidence of pallor, cyanosis, icterus, digital clubbing, or lymphadenopathy. The abdomen was soft with no signs of hepatosplenomegaly. The child was alert and appropriately oriented. Cardiac evaluation showed tachycardia without any murmurs. Room air oxygen saturation was 88%–89%, with a heart rate of 110 per minute. Due to ongoing tachypnea and respiratory distress, he was transferred to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit, where he received oxygen via mask at 5 liters per minute. Empiric antibiotic therapy with piperacillin-tazobactam was initiated following blood cultures, and a chest X-ray was obtained. Imaging revealed a right-sided pleural effusion with blunting of the right costophrenic angle (figure 1) (Table 2). Bedside lung ultrasonography confirmed a pleural effusion of approximately one liter (figure 2) (Table 2). Thoracentesis of the right pleural cavity, performed under aseptic technique, yielded about one liter of dark red fluid. Respiratory rate improved and oxygen saturation returned to 95%–98% on room air post-procedure. Chest pain resolved and oxygen supplementation was discontinued within 24 hours. The patient was transferred to the general ward the next day and subsequently discharged. The pleural fluid specimen was sent for laboratory analysis. 

However, the child was readmitted after 6 days with recurrent right-sided chest pain, tachypnea, and dyspnea. He was again placed in the PICU, where repeat chest X-ray demonstrated another right-sided pleural effusion. Thoracentesis was repeated, extracting roughly one liter of dark red pleural fluid. Pleural fluid adenosine deaminase (ADA) was slightly increased (Table 1), and with the history of Koch's exposure, empiric antitubercular therapy was started. Five days later, the patient was hospitalized for a third episode, again presenting with right-sided chest discomfort and breathlessness. A more detailed history at this point revealed a blunt abdominal injury from a bicycle handle sustained a month earlier. Before inserting an intercostal drain (ICD), a contrast-enhanced CT chest scan was performed, showing patchy consolidations in the right lung, right-sided hydropneumothorax, and a long, lobulated, multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right pleural space through the right paraoesophageal region, down to the upper left retroperitoneum and the tail of the pancreas (Table 2). After ICD placement, about one liter of dark red pleural fluid was evacuated (figure 3). In light of the CT findings, nature of the pleural fluid, and trauma history, a pancreaticopleural fistula (PPF) was suspected. MRCP, conducted on the same day, identified pancreatitis with a tubular cystic collection extending from the distal body and tail of the pancreas posteriorly within the retroperitoneum, then superiorly through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural space. There was also evidence of a narrow tract connecting with the left pleural cavity. The collection communicated with the distal pancreatic duct, which exhibited a proximal narrowing. MRCP supported a PPF diagnosis (figures 4-6) (Table 1). Serum and pleural fluid amylase and lipase were measured, revealing markedly elevated levels in pleural fluid: amylase at 6118 U/L and lipase at 7211 U/L (Table 2), further corroborating the PPF diagnosis. Antitubercular medications were discontinued, and the child was started on nothing by mouth and received total parenteral nutrition. An octreotide infusion was initiated at 30 µg

Differential diagnosis:

The patient exhibited pleural effusion and chest discomfort, with a clear prior episode of abdominal trauma one month earlier. After considering and excluding alternative etiologies for pleural effusion apart from post-traumatic pancreatitis, we noted that the child had no record of fever, prolonged cough, or weight reduction. Although there was a familial history of exposure to Koch’s, and the pleural fluid showed a mildly increased ADA level, GeneXpert testing for MTB yielded negative results, thereby excluding pulmonary Koch’s. Furthermore, the absence of fever and lack of neutrophilic exudate in the pleural fluid eliminated empyema as a possibility. Certain malignancies, including pulmonary, leukemic, lymphomatous, and metastatic tumors, may produce amylase-enriched pleural effusions; nevertheless, the patient did not experience fever or weight loss, had a normal hematological profile, and normal findings on peripheral smear analysis, collectively arguing against malignancy. Cytological examination of pleural fluid similarly detected no malignant cells. The potential for Boerhaave’s syndrome (esophageal perforation), another source of amylase-rich effusion, was negated as the patient maintained regular bowel habits without episodes of vomiting or additional chest pain. Imaging revealed no abdominal contents within the thoracic cavity, ruling out acquired diaphragmatic hernia. Normal liver function studies and hepatic imaging, as well as an absence of previous chronic liver disease, discounted hepatic hydrothorax. Lastly, renal disease and hydronephrosis were excluded on the basis of unremarkable renal imaging and normal renal function tests.

Treatment:

The patient received humidified oxygen via mask in the PICU for respiratory support. Thoracocentesis was performed under ultrasound guidance on two occasions, with approximately one litre of blood-tinged pleural fluid withdrawn each time. Empirical antitubercular therapy was initiated, but discontinued once MRCP established the diagnosis. During the third hospital admission, an intercostal drain (ICD) was inserted on the right side under strict aseptic conditions, again yielding around one litre of blood-tinged pleural effusion in the drain bag. As pancreatico-pleural fistula (PPF) is both infrequent and rarely encountered in paediatrics, standardized management protocols are lacking; therapy decisions were informed by individual case reports. Conservative management was initially selected: the patient was made nil per os and commenced on an Octreotide infusion at a dose of 30 µg/kg/day (range: 10–40 µg/kg/day). Total parenteral nutrition and supplementation with pancreatic enzymes were also provided. Over the following 10 days, drainage from the ICD progressively lessened and ultimately ceased. The ICD was subsequently removed and the patient was discharged.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following hospital discharge, the patient has remained asymptomatic. He continues to attend scheduled monthly follow-up appointments with me. Since leaving the hospital, there have been no additional instances of recurrent pleural effusions (S17). The child has made lifestyle adjustments, adhering to a balanced diet and engaging in regular physical activity as recommended. He has resumed normal daily routines and is consistently going to school. He is able to participate in vigorous activities such as running and playing without limitation.

Discussion:

PPF is an exceptionally rare complication of pancreatic disorders in children. To date, only 47 cases have been reported in paediatric patients. It can arise in both acute and chronic pancreatitis, as well as following traumatic injury in children. The true prevalence in the paediatric population remains uncertain, though it accounts for approximately 0.4% of pancreatitis cases overall. Mutations in genes such as serine peptidase inhibitor Kazal type 1 (SPINK1) and PRSS1 are frequently implicated, representing as much as 30% of paediatric chronic pancreatitis cases. Non-genetic factors, such as blunt abdominal trauma, are also significant and constituted 32% of a 25-patient series analyzed by Yang et al. There continues to be a portion of cases considered idiopathic, potentially due to unrecognized genetic mutations or anatomical ductal variants.

In PPF, pancreatic fluid may escape from a compromised duct or pseudocyst and pass into the thoracic cavity via natural diaphragmatic passages, commonly the aortic or oesophageal hiatus, or less frequently by eroding directly through the diaphragm and forming a fistula with the pleural space. These cases are frequently overlooked because children typically present with respiratory symptoms, prompting extensive pulmonary evaluations while the underlying cause is intra-abdominal. Literature review uncovered 21 pertinent cases, with 68% manifesting mainly with pulmonary signs such as large, recurrent unilateral or bilateral pleural effusions, breathlessness, and cough. Fewer than 20% presented with abdominal symptoms like epigastric pain, vomiting, or weight loss, while 24% showed systemic signs such as fever. One case described by Zi Yin Shang in Frontiers in Paediatrics 2023 featured an 8-year-old boy who exhibited only a prominent left chest for two weeks. Presenting with chest wall protrusion as the initial sign is exceedingly uncommon. In that patient, it appears the pleural effusion slowly accumulated following abdominal trauma. The unaffected lung’s compensatory capacity masked usual respiratory symptoms. Furthermore, a history of school bullying led the child to hide the traumatic event. Initial workup included evaluation for tuberculosis and malignancy related to hemorrhagic pleural effusion, before an alternate diagnosis was determined.

In our patient, recurrent right-sided pleural effusions and chest pain were prominent. The history of trauma became evident only after multiple detailed interviews and findings on MRCP suggested pancreatic involvement. Consequently, PPF may masquerade as a primary thoracic disorder, potentially leading clinicians astray if a comprehensive history is not obtained and appropriate imaging is not performed. Fluid buildup in PPF results from trauma or bacterial infection causing rupture of the pancreatic duct or a pseudocyst, permitting pancreatic secretions to leak. If the anterior pancreas ruptures, fluid enters the abdominal cavity causing ascites; if posterior, fluid tracks retroperitoneally into the mediastinum via the aortic or esophageal hiatus and then into the pleural space, generating effusion—a pancreatic fistula acts as a conduit, channeling these secretions into the chest.

In adults, chronic pancreatitis and subsequent PPF are most frequently associated with alcohol consumption. Uchiyama et al’s large-scale review of Japanese reports documented 113 PPF cases, revealing that 99% stemmed from chronic alcoholic pancreatitis, and respiratory complaints were primary in 68% of these. In paediatrics, hereditary factors predominate. Pathogenic mutations such as those in PRSS1, the CFTR gene, SPINK1, and chymotrypsin C are established causes of inherited pancreatitis, with PRSS1 R122H and N291 mutations (exons 3 and 2) being particularly notable. For paediatric patients with recurrent or chronic pancreatitis, thorough family history and evaluation for hereditary pancreatitis is warranted.

Diagnosis of PPF is commonly delayed due to the focus on respiratory investigations before considering a pancreatic source. A pleural fluid amylase of ≥1000 IU/L is diagnostic for PPF. However, elevated amylase can be seen in tuberculosis, esophageal perforation, and malignancies of the lung or pancreas, necessitating their exclusion. Pancreatic-type amylase distinguishes PPF, whereas other conditions typically elevate salivary amylase. Thus, pleural fluid amylase fractionation can help confirm the diagnosis. MRCP has a 70%–80% sensitivity for visualizing the fistulous tract and ductal anatomy, while CT imaging can reliably detect calcifications, pseudocysts, or duct dilation, yet only identifies the actual fistula in 33%–47% of cases. Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) remains the definitive method for therapeutic decision-making, with fistula visualization rates ranging from 38% to 78% in paediatric series.
