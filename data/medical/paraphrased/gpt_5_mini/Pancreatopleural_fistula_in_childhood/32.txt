Title: Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood

Hans Vaish, Vipin Vaish, Shalini Suri, Vasudha Tomar

Abstract:

Summary: An older child presented with repeated right-sided pleural effusions accompanied by chest pain and difficulty breathing, with temporary relief following aspiration of the fluid. The pleural fluid maintained a dark red color on each occasion. A right intercostal drain was inserted because of the persistence of the effusions. His medical history was notable for recent abdominal injury caused by a bicycle handlebar. Chest computed tomography revealed areas of right pulmonary consolidation, hydropneumothorax, and a multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right paraesophageal area toward the pancreatic tail. Both serum and pleural fluid levels of amylase and lipase were found to be elevated. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography showed bilateral pancreatopleural fistulas, with the right side affected more severely than the left. Management included keeping the patient NPO, administering total parenteral nutrition, and initiating treatment with Octreotide. Over the next 10 days, pleural drainage decreased, the chest tube was withdrawn, and the child was discharged.

Background:

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is a very uncommon condition characterized by repeated, large-volume, blood-tinged pleural effusions. It occurs in the context of acute pancreatitis, chronic pancreatitis, or pancreatic injury. The clinical presentation can be perplexing, as it manifests primarily with symptoms of respiratory compromise caused by persistent pleural fluid accumulation, yet the underlying cause originates in the pancreas. This often leads to a delay in diagnosis because the focus is typically placed on searching for thoracic abnormalities. To date, only 47 cases of PPF have been documented in the pediatric population, while adult cases number over 300.

Case presentation:

A pediatric patient presented to the outpatient clinic with his mother, reporting a week-long history of dyspnea and right-sided chest pain. According to the mother, the respiratory symptoms had progressively worsened over the last seven days. There was no reported family history of consanguinity. She noted a history of tuberculosis exposure (“Koch’s contact”) from the child’s grandfather, who had been appropriately treated for pulmonary tuberculosis. Dietary history indicated frequent intake of processed, calorie-dense foods with scant consumption of fruits, vegetables, and nutritionally balanced homemade meals. The child weighed 44 kg and was 112 cm tall, resulting in a body mass index of 35.

On physical exam, he exhibited a respiratory rate of 20 breaths per minute, consistent with mild tachypnea. Chest examination found diminished movement on the right, and percussion revealed stony dullness in the right subscapular, infra-axillary, and axillary regions. Tactile vocal fremitus was significantly reduced on the right in comparison to the left. Auscultation demonstrated decreased breath sounds in the same right-sided areas. No signs of pallor, cyanosis, icterus, clubbing, or lymphadenopathy were observed. The abdomen was soft and non-tender, without evidence of hepatosplenomegaly. Neurologically, the patient was alert and oriented. Cardiovascular evaluation showed tachycardia but no murmurs. Oxygen saturation on ambient air was measured at 88–89%, and heart rate at 110/min. Given the presence of tachypnea and respiratory distress, the patient was transferred to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit and initiated on supplemental oxygen at 5 L/min via mask. A chest radiograph was obtained, and empirical antibiotics (piperacillin-tazobactam) were started after collecting blood cultures.

The chest X-ray revealed a right-sided pleural effusion with blunting of the right costophrenic angle (figure 1) (Table 2). Bedside lung ultrasonography showed an estimated pleural effusion volume of approximately one liter (figure 2) (Table 2). Under aseptic conditions, right-sided thoracentesis was performed, yielding about one liter of dark red pleural fluid. Post-procedure, the child’s tachypnea resolved, and oxygen saturation improved to 95–98% on room air. Chest pain subsided and oxygen therapy was discontinued within 24 hours. The patient was transferred to the general ward the following day and subsequently discharged. Pleural fluid aspirate was sent for diagnostic evaluation.

However, six days later, the patient was readmitted with recurrent right-sided chest pain, tachypnea, and breathing difficulty. He was again admitted to the PICU. Repeat chest X-ray confirmed a recurrent right-sided pleural effusion. Another thoracentesis was done, again yielding approximately one liter of dark red fluid. Pleural fluid adenosine deaminase (ADA) was mildly elevated (Table 1), and given the history of tuberculosis contact, empirical antitubercular treatment was initiated.

The patient returned for a third admission five days later with similar symptoms of right-sided chest pain and respiratory distress. On revisiting the history, the child disclosed he had sustained a blunt abdominal injury from a bicycle handle one month previously. Before placing an intercostal drain (ICD), a contrast-enhanced chest CT was performed. The CT scan demonstrated patchy airspace opacities in the right lung with right hydropneumothorax and an elongated, multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right pleural space, following the right paraesophageal area to the upper left retroperitoneum, reaching the tail of the pancreas (Table 2). An ICD was placed, resulting in drainage of about one liter of dark red fluid (figure 3). This clinical scenario—persistent hemorrhagic effusion, imaging findings, and the context of prior abdominal trauma—suggested a pancreaticopleural fistula (PPF). MRCP was arranged the same day, revealing evidence of pancreatitis with a tubular cystic collection at the distal pancreas body and tail, which extended into the retroperitoneum and upward through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity. A narrow tract to the left pleural cavity was also identified, and the collection communicated with the distal pancreatic duct, with narrowing noted proximally. MRCP supported the diagnosis of PPF (figures 4-6) (Table 1). Serum and pleural fluid samples were tested for amylase and lipase.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient exhibited pleural effusion and chest pain, with a confirmed episode of abdominal trauma occurring 1 month prior. All etiologies for pleural effusion aside from post-traumatic pancreatitis were excluded. The child had no prior history of fever, persistent cough, or weight loss. Although there was a family history of contact with Koch's and the pleural fluid adenosine deaminase (ADA) was mildly elevated, GeneXpert testing for Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB) returned negative. Thus, pulmonary Koch's was excluded. There was neither fever nor any evidence of neutrophilic exudate in the pleural fluid, thereby eliminating empyema. While malignancies—such as lung cancer, leukemia, lymphoma, and metastatic tumors—may be associated with pleural fluid containing high amylase, these were dismissed due to absence of fever, lack of weight loss, a normal hematological profile, and an unremarkable peripheral blood smear. Pleural fluid cytology was also negative for malignant cells. Amylase-rich pleural fluid may be present in Boerhaave’s syndrome (esophageal perforation); however, this was excluded as the child had regular bowel movements and did not report chest pain or episodes of vomiting. Acquired diaphragmatic hernia was ruled out given that imaging did not show abdominal organs in the thoracic cavity. Hepatic hydrothorax was excluded based on normal liver function tests and liver imaging, as well as no evidence or history of chronic liver disease. Both renal imaging and renal function were normal, ruling out renal disease and hydronephrosis.

Treatment:

In the pediatric intensive care unit, the patient received humidified oxygen via face mask for respiratory support. Thoracocentesis, guided by ultrasound, was performed on two occasions, yielding approximately one liter of blood-tinged pleural fluid each time. Empirical antitubercular therapy was initiated but discontinued after confirming the diagnosis using MRCP. During the third hospital admission, a right-sided intercostal drain (ICD) was placed under aseptic conditions, with another one liter of hemorrhagic pleural fluid drained into the ICD collection bag. Given that pancreaticopleural fistula (PPF) is rare in pediatric patients and lacks established management protocols, treatment was informed by case report literature. A conservative approach was adopted: oral intake was withheld, and the patient was started on an octreotide infusion at 30 µg/kg/day (within a range of 10–40 µg/kg/day). Total parenteral nutrition was provided along with pancreatic enzyme supplementation. Over the ensuing 10 days, the volume of pleural fluid drainage from the ICD gradually diminished and eventually ceased. The ICD was then removed, and the child was subsequently discharged.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following his release from the hospital, the patient has remained asymptomatic. He continues to see me for routine monthly follow-up visits. Since leaving the hospital, there have been no further instances of pleural effusion recurrence (S17). He has implemented recommended lifestyle modifications, including adopting a balanced diet and engaging in regular physical activity. The patient has resumed his usual routines and is back to regular school attendance. He is able to participate in vigorous activities, such as running and playing, without limitation.

Discussion:

PPF represents a highly uncommon and seldom-seen complication associated with pancreatic disorders among children. To date, there are only 47 reported instances of paediatric PPF. This entity may arise in both acute and chronic pancreatitis or following traumatic injuries in children. The precise prevalence is unknown in paediatric patients, but it affects roughly 0.4% of pancreatitis cases overall. Genetic mutations, including serine peptidase inhibitor Kazal type 1 (SPINK1) and PRSS1, play a significant role, with up to 30% of paediatric chronic pancreatitis attributed to these alterations. Blunt trauma to the abdomen constitutes another notable non-genetic precipitant, accounting for 32% in a 25-patient series analyzed by Yang et al. There remain idiopathic presentations, possibly linked to yet-unrecognized ductal or genetic variants. Pancreatic secretions may enter the pleural cavity via abnormal routes from a disrupted pancreatic duct or pseudocyst, passing through diaphragmatic hiatuses (typically aortic or oesophageal, less often via direct diaphragmatic erosion) and forming a fistulous communication to the pleura. Diagnosis is frequently missed, as most presentations manifest with thoracic complaints and clinicians focus diagnostic efforts on pulmonary causes, whereas the origin is abdominal. Upon review of published reports, 21 were pertinent, with 68% of cases primarily presenting respiratory manifestations such as large, recurrent unilateral or bilateral pleural effusions, breathlessness, and cough. Fewer than 20% displayed abdominal features like epigastric pain, emesis, and weight loss, while 24% experienced systemic symptoms, including fever. Zi Yin Shang’s report in Frontiers in Paediatrics 2023 included a case in which an 8-year-old presented solely with a two-week history of a bulging left chest, a manifestation rare for PPF. In this scenario, the effusion likely evolved progressively after trauma, with respiratory adaptation by the healthy lung preventing typical symptoms like cough and dyspnoea. Furthermore, the child had concealed a history of abdominal trauma due to school bullying. Evaluation included workup for tuberculosis and malignancy due to haemorrhagic pleural fluid, although the diagnosis diverged. In our own experience, the patient had repeated pulmonary symptoms—chest pain and recurrent right-sided pleural effusions—with trauma history only uncovered after repeated interviews, and MRCP suggested pancreatic pathology. Consequently, PPF can masquerade as a primary pulmonary disorder, potentially misleading clinicians until detailed history and proper imaging expose the underlying cause. Fluid accumulation in PPF occurs secondary to trauma or infection, which causes rupture of the pancreatic duct or pseudocyst, permitting leakage of pancreatic fluid. When the anterior pancreas ruptures, fluid enters the peritoneal cavity causing ascites; rupture posteriorly allows fluid to accumulate in the retroperitoneal space and ascend into the mediastinum via the aortic or oesophageal hiatus, breaching the pleura and causing a pleural effusion. An internal fistula thereby acts as a conduit for pancreatic secretions into the chest. Adult chronic pancreatitis is overwhelmingly alcohol-related in PPF, as illustrated by Uchiyama et al’s comprehensive Japanese review of 113 cases, of which 99% involved alcohol, with chest symptoms predominating in 68%. In contrast, genetic factors dominate chronic pancreatitis in children. Mutations in PRSS1, CFTR, SPINK1, and chymotrypsin C are recognized causes of hereditary pancreatitis, notably the PRSS1 exon 3 R122H and exon 2 N291 mutations. When paediatric pancreatitis recurs or appears chronic, thorough familial assessment and consideration of hereditary forms is warranted. Diagnostic delays are common as patients undergo extended pulmonary investigation before pancreatic involvement is suspected. Pleural fluid amylase ≥1000 IU/L is diagnostic for PPF; however, differential diagnosis must consider tuberculosis, oesophageal rupture, and malignancy, which can also cause high pleural fluid amylase. The presence of pancreatic-type amylase differentiates PPF, while other conditions exhibit salivary-type amylase; therefore, pleural amylase typing may aid diagnosis. MRCP is sensitive in 70%–80% of cases for fistula and duct anatomy visualization. CT readily identifies calcifications, pseudocysts, or ductal dilation but depicts the fistula itself in only 33%–47% of cases. Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) remains definitive for therapeutic evaluation, with fistula visualization rates in children ranging from 38% to 78%. PPF management encompasses medical, endoscopic, and surgical approaches. Owing to its rarity, standard treatment protocols are lacking.
