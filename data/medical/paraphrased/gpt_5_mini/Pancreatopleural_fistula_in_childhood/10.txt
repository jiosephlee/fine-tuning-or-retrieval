Title: Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood

Hans Vaish, Vipin Vaish, Shalini Suri, Vasudha Tomar

Abstract:

Summary: A preadolescent boy presented with multiple episodes of right-sided pleural effusion, chest discomfort, and shortness of breath, which showed temporary relief after thoracentesis. The pleural aspirate consistently appeared dark red in color. Due to the recurrent nature of the effusions, a right-sided intercostal chest drain was inserted. Further inquiry uncovered a history of recent abdominal trauma resulting from a bicycle handlebar injury. Chest CT imaging demonstrated patchy consolidation in the right lung, hydropneumothorax, and a multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right paraesophageal area toward the pancreatic tail. Laboratory investigations revealed raised levels of amylase and lipase in both the serum and pleural fluid. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography established the diagnosis of bilateral pancreatopleural fistulas, with the right side being more significantly involved than the left. Management included initiating nil per os, administration of total parenteral nutrition, and use of Octreotide. Over the ensuing 10 days, drainage from the chest tube diminished, leading to its removal, and the child was subsequently discharged.

Background:

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is a very uncommon condition characterized by recurrent, large, and sometimes hemorrhagic pleural effusions. It has been identified in the setting of acute and chronic pancreatitis as well as pancreatic trauma. The clinical presentation can be misleading, as patients often exhibit respiratory compromise from repeated pleural effusions, though the underlying problem originates in the pancreas. This often leads to a delayed diagnosis, with initial investigations directed toward thoracic causes. In the pediatric population, only 47 cases have been described, compared to over 300 reported cases in adults.

Case presentation:

A pediatric patient presented to the outpatient clinic with his mother, reporting a one-week history of breathing difficulty and right-sided chest discomfort. According to his mother, his symptoms had worsened steadily over seven days. There was no report of parental consanguinity. The mother mentioned a history of exposure to pulmonary tuberculosis, as the child’s grandfather had been appropriately treated for Koch’s. The child’s dietary pattern consisted largely of processed and calorically dense foods, with scant consumption of fresh produce and nutritionally balanced homemade meals. His anthropometric assessment revealed a weight of 44 kg, height of 112 cm, and a BMI of 35. On clinical evaluation, his respiratory rate was 20 per minute, and he was mildly tachypneic. There was observable restriction of chest wall movement on the right side. Percussion over the right subscapular, infra-axillary, and axillary areas produced stony dullness. Palpation detected markedly diminished vocal fremitus on the right relative to the left. Auscultation disclosed decreased breath sounds over the same regions on the right. There were no clinical signs of pallor, cyanosis, jaundice, digital clubbing, or lymphadenopathy. His abdomen was soft, and no hepatosplenomegaly was present. Neurologically, he was alert and oriented to person, place, and time. Cardiovascular examination showed tachycardia without audible murmurs. Oxygen saturation on ambient air was between 88% and 89%, with a pulse of 110 per minute. In light of the tachypnea and respiratory distress, the patient was transferred to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit and started on oxygen at 5 liters per minute via face mask. A chest radiograph was ordered, and empirical piperacillin-tazobactam therapy was initiated after collecting blood cultures. Radiography demonstrated a right-sided pleural effusion with blunted right costophrenic angle (figure 1) (Table 2). Point-of-care lung ultrasonography identified approximately one liter of pleural fluid (figure 2) (Table 2). Under sterile conditions, right-sided thoracentesis was performed, yielding roughly one liter of dark red fluid. The patient's tachypnea subsided post-drainage, and his oxygenation on room air improved to 95%–98%. His chest pain abated, and oxygen supplementation was discontinued within a day. He was transferred to the ward the following day and later discharged. Pleural fluid was sent for further laboratory evaluation. 

However, six days later, the child was readmitted with similar right-sided chest pain, tachypnea, and dyspnea. He returned to the PICU, and a repeat chest radiograph again demonstrated right-sided pleural effusion. Repeat thoracentesis evacuated another liter of dark red pleural fluid. Pleural fluid adenosine deaminase (ADA) was found to be slightly increased (Table 1), and in light of the known Koch’s contact, empirical antitubercular therapy was started. Nonetheless, the patient was hospitalized a third time after five days, again with right-sided chest pain and breathing difficulty. Upon reevaluation, he reported sustaining blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle a month earlier. Prior to intercostal drainage tube placement, a contrast-enhanced chest CT was performed, which showed patchy consolidations in the right lung, right-sided hydropneumothorax, and a multiloculated, elongated hypodense collection extending from the right pleural space and tracking from the right paraesophageal region to the upper left retroperitoneum to the pancreatic tail (Table 2). An intercostal drain was inserted, evacuating around one liter of dark red pleural fluid (figure 3). These imaging results, together with the fluid’s appearance and the reported trauma, suggested a pancreaticopleural fistula (PPF). MRCP was performed the same day, revealing findings consistent with pancreatitis and a tubular cystic collection involving the distal body and tail of pancreas, extending retroperitoneally and then through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural space, as well as a narrow connection to the left pleural cavity. There was communication between the collection and the distal pancreatic duct, with proximal ductal narrowing. These MRCP findings indicated a PPF (figures 4-6) (Table 1). Both serum and pleural fluid amylase and lipase levels were obtained. The analysis of pleural fluid showed substantially raised amylase (6118 U/L) and lipase (7211 U/L) levels (Table 2), further supporting the PPF diagnosis. Differential diagnosis:

The patient exhibited pleural effusion and chest pain, with a clear account of abdominal trauma occurring one month prior. Apart from post-traumatic pancreatitis, alternative etiologies for pleural effusion were excluded. There was no history of fever, chronic cough, or weight loss in this child. Although there was a family history of contact with tuberculosis (Koch’s), and the pleural fluid ADA was only mildly raised, GeneXpert testing for MTB returned negative results; therefore, pulmonary tuberculosis was excluded. The absence of fever and neutrophilic exudate in the pleural fluid also excluded empyema. Malignancies—including pulmonary, leukemia, lymphoma, and metastatic neoplasms—can present with pleural fluid high in amylase, but these were considered unlikely given the lack of fever, weight loss, as well as normal blood work and peripheral smear. Pleural fluid cytology failed to demonstrate malignant cells. Boerhaave’s syndrome (esophageal rupture) was dismissed as the child continued to pass stools normally and reported no episodes of chest pain related to vomiting. Imaging did not show abdominal organs in the thoracic cavity, ruling out acquired diaphragmatic hernia. Hepatic hydrothorax was considered unlikely due to normal liver function studies and imaging, along with no history of chronic liver disease. Lastly, unremarkable renal imaging and function made renal pathology or hydronephrosis unlikely.

Treatment:

In the PICU, the patient received humidified oxygen administered via face mask. Thoracocentesis was performed twice under ultrasound guidance, with approximately one liter of hemorrhagic pleural fluid obtained during each procedure. Antitubercular therapy was initiated empirically but discontinued following diagnostic confirmation using MRCP. Upon the third hospital admission, an intercostal drain (ICD) was placed on the right side under sterile conditions, once more yielding about one liter of blood-tinged pleural fluid collected into the ICD reservoir. Given that pancreaticopleural fistula (PPF) is a rare occurrence among pediatric patients and established management protocols are lacking, treatment decisions relied on prior case reports. Conservative management was initiated, keeping the patient nil per oral and starting an Octreotide infusion at a dose of 30 µg/kg/day (dose range: 10–40 µg/kg/day). The patient was also provided with total parenteral nutrition and pancreatic enzyme supplementation. Over a period of 10 days, drainage from the ICD progressively diminished until it ceased completely. The ICD was subsequently removed, and the patient was discharged home.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following hospital discharge, the child has remained asymptomatic. He attends scheduled monthly follow-up visits with me. Since leaving the hospital, there have been no additional occurrences of recurrent pleural effusions (S17). The patient has adopted lifestyle changes, now maintaining a balanced diet and engaging in regular physical activity as recommended. He has resumed his normal daily routine and consistently attends school. He is able to participate in vigorous activities, such as running and playing, without limitations.

Discussion:

Pancreaticopleural fistula (PPF) is an exceptionally uncommon and rare complication arising from pancreatic disorders in children. To date, only 47 pediatric PPF cases have been recorded in the literature. PPF may develop in association with either acute or chronic pancreatitis, as well as following abdominal trauma in pediatric patients. The precise frequency in the pediatric age group remains undetermined; however, PPF occurs in approximately 0.4% of pancreatitis cases overall. Genetic mutations—including serine peptidase inhibitor Kazal type 1 (SPINK1) and PRSS1—significantly contribute to pediatric chronic pancreatitis, being present in as many as 30% of cases. In the non-hereditary subset, blunt force abdominal trauma is a notable inciting factor, making up 32% of a 25-patient series examined by Yang et al. Cases continue to arise without an identifiable etiology—referred to as idiopathic—which may reflect currently unrecognized genetic or ductal anomalies.

Pancreatic secretions may escape via a disrupted duct or pseudocyst, tracking through natural diaphragmatic openings—most often the aortic or esophageal hiatus—or, less frequently, through direct erosion of the diaphragm, leading to a fistulous link between the pancreas and pleural space. Many of these cases evade timely detection, since initial manifestations are primarily thoracic, directing clinicians towards investigating pulmonary causes while the pathology resides in the abdomen. Literature review identified 21 pertinent cases, revealing that in 68% of these, major symptoms were respiratory, such as sizable, recurrent, unilateral or bilateral pleural effusions, shortness of breath, and cough. Fewer than 20% of patients described abdominal complaints such as pain in the epigastric region, nausea, or unintentional weight loss. Around 24% experienced general symptoms such as fever.

A case described by Zi Yin Shang in *Frontiers in Pediatrics* (2023) involved an 8-year-old male presenting solely with a progressively enlarging left-sided chest over two weeks—a rare initial sign for PPF. In that scenario, the pleural effusion presumably accumulated gradually after the child suffered abdominal trauma, and compensatory function in the unaffected lung allowed for gradual adaptation, masking typical signs like cough or breathlessness. The patient also concealed a trauma history, likely influenced by school bullying. Initial evaluation pursued infectious and malignancy workups due to hemorrhagic pleural fluid, only for subsequent findings to redirect the diagnosis.

In our own patient, recurrent right-sided pleural effusions and chest pain marked the clinical course. The antecedent abdominal injury was disclosed only upon repeated history-taking, with magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) clarifying the pancreatic etiology. Accordingly, PPF often presents with misleadingly respiratory features—a “pulmonary mask”—potentially diverting care teams unless thorough clinical histories and directed imaging are pursued. Fluid accumulation in PPF ensues following trauma or infection causing rupture of the pancreatic duct or a pseudocyst, leaking pancreatic fluid. Should the rupture occur anteriorly, the effusion collects intra-abdominally as ascites; with posterior rupture, it moves retroperitoneally, following the path of least resistance upward, traversing the mediastinum via diaphragmatic openings, penetrating the pleura to produce pleural effusions. Such internal pancreatic fistulas act akin to leaking pipes, diverting pancreatic secretions into the thorax.

Whereas in adults, alcohol use is the principal cause of chronic pancreatitis and secondary PPF, a comprehensive Japanese review by Uchiyama et al identified 113 cases, with chronic alcohol-related pancreatitis accounting for 99% and thoracic symptoms dominant in 68% of instances. Conversely, pediatric chronic pancreatitis largely stems from genetic factors. Well-known inherited contributors are PRSS1 gene mutations (notably R122H in exon 3 and N291 in exon 2), SPINK1, CFTR, and chymotrypsin C gene mutations. Whenever pancreatitis recurs or appears chronic in a child, a detailed family history is warranted, and hereditary pancreatitis should be considered.

The diagnostic process is commonly protracted, as most cases are initially subjected to in-depth pulmonary assessments before a pancreatic cause is entertained. The detection of pleural fluid amylase at levels ≥1,000 IU/L is diagnostic of PPF. However, similar elevations may be observed with tuberculosis, esophageal rupture, or malignancies of the lungs or pancreas, hence such causes should be excluded. The presence of pancreatic-type amylase in the pleural effusion distinguishes PPF from these conditions, which usually feature salivary-type amylase; thus, amylase isoenzyme typing should be included in the diagnostic approach to suspected PPF.
