Title: Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood

Hans Vaish, Vipin Vaish, Shalini Suri, Vasudha Tomar

Abstract:

A preadolescent male exhibited multiple episodes of right-sided pleural effusion, accompanied by thoracic pain and difficulty breathing. These symptoms were alleviated temporarily through aspiration. The pleural fluid was consistently described as dark red. Recurrent effusions necessitated insertion of a right intercostal drain. Medical history disclosed recent blunt abdominal trauma involving a bicycle handlebar. Chest computed tomography identified areas of right pulmonary consolidation, hydropneumothorax, and a multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right paraesophageal area towards the pancreatic tail. Amylase and lipase levels were elevated in both serum and pleural fluid samples. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography revealed bilateral pancreatopleural fistulas, with greater involvement on the right. Management included bowel rest, total parenteral nutrition, and administration of Octreotide. Within ten days, drainage declined, allowing removal of the chest tube and subsequent discharge of the patient.

Background:

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is a very uncommon condition characterized by recurrent, large-volume, and occasionally hemorrhagic pleural effusions. This complication can arise in the setting of acute or chronic pancreatitis, as well as pancreatic injury. Diagnosing PPF can be challenging since it often manifests with symptoms of respiratory compromise due to persistent pleural fluid accumulation, diverting clinical attention toward thoracic causes and delaying identification of the pancreatic origin. In the pediatric population, only 47 instances have been documented, in contrast to over 300 reported adult cases.

Case presentation:

A pediatric patient presented to the outpatient clinic with his mother, reporting one week of respiratory difficulty and right-sided chest pain. According to his mother, his symptoms had progressively worsened over seven days. There was no consanguinity in the family history. The mother reported a prior exposure to tuberculosis (Koch’s contact) from the child’s grandfather, who had previously received treatment for pulmonary tuberculosis. Diet history revealed frequent intake of processed, high-calorie foods with minimal consumption of fresh produce or balanced homemade meals. The child weighed 44 kg, stood 112 cm tall, and had a body mass index of 35. On physical exam, his respiratory rate was 20/min, with mild tachypnea observed. The right side of his chest exhibited diminished movement, and percussion produced stony dullness over the right subscapular, infra-axillary, and axillary areas. Vocal fremitus was markedly decreased on the right compared to the left, and breath sounds were reduced in the same regions. There was no evidence of pallor, cyanosis, jaundice, digital clubbing, or lymphadenopathy. The abdominal examination was unremarkable with no liver or spleen enlargement, and the patient was alert and oriented. Cardiovascular assessment revealed tachycardia but no murmurs. Oxygen saturation was 88%–89% on room air, and the pulse rate was 110/min. Due to the child’s tachypnea and respiratory distress, he was admitted to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit and received oxygen via facemask at 5 L/min. A chest radiograph was performed, and after collecting blood cultures, empirical antibiotics (piperacillin-tazobactam) were initiated. Imaging demonstrated a right-sided pleural effusion with obliteration of the right costophrenic angle (figure 1) (Table 2). Point-of-care lung ultrasound confirmed a pleural effusion of approximately one liter (figure 2) (Table 2). Right-sided thoracocentesis was performed under aseptic technique, aspirating about one liter of dark red pleural fluid. The child’s tachypnea resolved following the procedure, with oxygen saturation on room air improving to 95%–98%. Chest pain lessened, and he was off supplemental oxygen within 24 hours, after which he was transferred to the ward and subsequently discharged. The pleural fluid was submitted for analysis.

However, the child was readmitted six days later with recurrent right-sided chest pain, tachypnea, and respiratory difficulty. He was again transferred to the PICU, where chest radiography demonstrated another right-sided pleural effusion. Repeat thoracocentesis yielded approximately one liter of dark red pleural fluid. Pleural fluid adenosine deaminase (ADA) was mildly elevated (Table 1), and given the prior history of Koch’s contact, empirical antitubercular treatment was started. The child was admitted a third time five days later for the same symptoms of right-sided chest pain and dyspnea. A more thorough history at this stage revealed he had experienced blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle one month earlier. Before placing an intercostal drain (ICD), a contrast-enhanced CT scan of the chest was obtained. Imaging showed patchy consolidation in the right lung, right-sided hydropneumothorax, and a multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right pleural space, tracking alongside the esophagus to the left-sided retroperitoneum near the pancreatic tail (Table 2). An ICD was inserted, yielding approximately one liter of dark red pleural fluid (figure 3). The imaging findings, coupled with the dark red pleural aspirate and history of abdominal trauma, suggested the diagnosis of a pancreaticopleural fistula (PPF). Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) performed the same day revealed pancreatitis with a tubular cystic collection arising from the distal pancreatic body and tail, extending posteriorly in the retroperitoneum and superiorly via the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity, with a narrow connection also visible to the left pleural cavity. The collection was seen communicating with the distal pancreatic duct, which was narrowed upstream from the site of communication. MRCP findings were consistent with a PPF (figures 4–6) (Table 1). Serum and pleural fluid amylase and lipase assays were ordered. Pleural fluid results indicated amylase 6118 U/L and lipase 7211 U/L (Table 2), strongly supporting the PPF diagnosis. Antitubercular therapy was discontinued, and the patient was made nil per os and commenced on total parenteral nutrition. Octreotide infusion was initiated at 30 µg/kg/day (range 10–40 µg/kg/day). Differential diagnosis:

The patient exhibited pleural effusion accompanied by chest pain, and had a documented episode of abdominal trauma one month prior. Aside from post-traumatic pancreatitis, alternative etiologies for pleural effusion were excluded. The child did not report fever, persistent cough, or weight loss. Although there was a family history of contact with Koch’s disease and pleural fluid ADA was slightly raised, GeneXpert testing for MTB yielded negative results, thereby excluding pulmonary Koch’s. Empyema was considered unlikely given the absence of fever and the lack of neutrophilic exudate in the pleural fluid. Malignancies such as lung cancer, leukaemia, lymphoma, or metastatic lesions may lead to amylase-rich pleural fluid, but normal blood tests, absence of fever or weight loss, normal peripheral smear, and negative pleural fluid cytology for malignant cells argued against malignancy. Boerhaave’s syndrome (esophageal perforation) was discounted since the child had regular bowel movements and no history of chest pain or vomiting. Acquired diaphragmatic hernia was excluded, as imaging did not reveal abdominal organs within the thoracic cavity. Hepatic hydrothorax was ruled out due to normal liver function tests, normal hepatic imaging, and no indication of chronic liver disease. Normal renal imaging and function eliminated renal pathology or hydronephrosis as causes.

Treatment:

In the PICU, humidified oxygen was administered via mask for respiratory support. The patient underwent ultrasound-guided thoracocentesis on two occasions, with approximately one litre of blood-tinged pleural fluid extracted each time. Antitubercular medication was initiated empirically, but discontinued upon confirmation of the diagnosis with MRCP. During the third hospitalization, a right-sided intercostal drain (ICD) was placed under strict aseptic conditions, again yielding about one litre of blood-stained pleural fluid collected in the ICD bag. Due to the rarity and infrequency of PPF in children, established treatment protocols are lacking; management is typically informed by published case reports. Initial therapy was conservative: the child was kept nil per oral and received octreotide infusion at a dose of 30 µg/kg/day (range 10–40 µg/kg/day), along with total parenteral nutrition and pancreatic enzyme supplementation. Over a ten-day period, the volume of fluid draining from the ICD progressively diminished, eventually ceasing completely. The ICD was subsequently withdrawn, and the patient was discharged.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following hospital discharge, the patient has not experienced any symptoms. He attends monthly follow-up appointments. There have been no further occurrences of pleural effusions since his release (S17). The patient has adopted recommended lifestyle changes, maintaining a balanced diet and consistent exercise routine. He has resumed normal daily life, is going to school consistently, and is able to participate in vigorous activities such as running and playing without restriction.

Discussion:

PPF represents a highly unusual and rare consequence of pancreatic disease in children. To date, only 47 paediatric cases of PPF have been reported. It may be seen following either acute or chronic pancreatitis, or as a result of paediatric trauma. The precise frequency in children is not known; however, PPF is found to complicate approximately 0.4% of all pancreatitis cases. Genetic mutations in serine peptidase inhibitor Kazal type 1 (SPINK1) and PRSS1 are especially notable, being implicated in up to 30% of paediatric chronic pancreatitis. Additionally, blunt abdominal trauma is a significant acquired trigger, accounting for 32% of the 25-case cohort studied by Yang et al. Idiopathic presentations persist, potentially owing to yet-undetected genetic or ductal abnormalities. The movement of pancreatic fluid occurs when disruption of the pancreatic duct or pseudocyst allows tracking through diaphragmatic hiatuses—commonly the aortic or oesophageal hiatus, less often through direct erosion into the pleura—creating a fistulous connection with the pleural cavity.

Many cases are overlooked, as patients tend to present mainly with thoracic complaints, and initial efforts are frequently focused on diagnosing respiratory conditions, even though the true origin is abdominal. The literature review identified 21 relevant publications, within which 68% of cases manifested chiefly as respiratory signs, including large and recurrent pleural effusions (unilateral or bilateral), shortness of breath, and cough. Fewer than 20% had gastrointestinal symptoms such as epigastric pain, vomiting, and weight loss; 24% presented with constitutional signs like fever. Shang et al. (Frontiers in Paediatrics 2023) documented an 8-year-old with PPF whose sole presenting symptom for two weeks was left-sided chest protrusion. Such presentations are exceedingly rare. In that instance, pleural effusion likely developed steadily after abdominal trauma, and the functioning contralateral lung compensated, so usual respiratory symptoms like cough and dyspnoea were absent. Furthermore, this child, who experienced bullying at school, intentionally withheld the trauma history. Diagnostic work-up initially focused on tuberculosis and malignancy due to the haemorrhagic pleural fluid, but ultimately proceeded in a different diagnostic direction. In the present case, the child similarly developed repeated pulmonary symptoms, including chest pain and recurring right-sided pleural fluid accumulation. The history of trauma was eventually elicited after repeated interviews with the patient, and MRCP findings pointed towards a pancreatic origin.

Thus, PPF may obscure the underlying disease as a ‘pulmonary mask,’ possibly leading clinicians astray unless a comprehensive history and appropriate imaging are obtained. The accumulation of fluid in PPF results from external trauma or infection, causing rupture of the pancreatic duct or pseudocyst and leakage of pancreatic fluid. Should the rupture occur anteriorly, fluid enters the abdominal cavity, producing ascites; a posterior rupture leads fluid into the retroperitoneal space, which ascends along the path of least resistance through the aortic or oesophageal hiatus, entering the mediastinum, then traversing the pleura to cause pleural effusion. The internal pancreatic fistula effectively diverts secretions into the thoracic space, mimicking a leaky conduit.

Among adults, alcohol is the chief cause of chronic pancreatitis leading to PPF. Uchiyama et al. performed an extensive national review, cataloguing 113 cases in Japanese literature. Their analysis showed that 99% were linked to chronic alcoholic pancreatitis, with chest symptoms predominant in 68%. In children, genetic etiologies are more common, with mutations in genes such as PRSS1, CF transmembrane regulator, SPINK1, and chymotrypsin C frequently responsible for hereditary pancreatitis. Representative mutations include R122H and N291 in exons 3 and 2 of PRSS1, respectively. Where pancreatitis in children is recurrent or persistent, a detailed family history should be taken and hereditary pancreatitis considered.

Diagnosis is frequently delayed, as initial investigations typically focus on pulmonary causes before a pancreatic etiology is recognized. A pleural fluid amylase ≥1000 IU/L is diagnostic for PPF. However, conditions such as tuberculosis, oesophageal rupture, and malignancies of the lung or pancreas also produce elevated pleural fluid amylase, so these must be excluded. Pancreatic isoenzyme amylase in the pleural fluid distinguishes PPF; other conditions yielding high amylase usually exhibit salivary isoenzyme. Typing of pleural fluid amylase is a useful component of the diagnostic algorithm in suspected PPF. MRCP demonstrates 70%–80% sensitivity for visualizing the fistula and ductal structures.
