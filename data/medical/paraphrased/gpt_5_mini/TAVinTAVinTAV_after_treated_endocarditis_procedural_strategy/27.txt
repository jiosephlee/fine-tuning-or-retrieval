Title: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

Evgeny Vladimirovich Merkulov, Goar Kimovna Arutyunyan, Andrey Sergeevich Tereshchenko, Dmitriy V Pevzner, Iiliya Tarasovich Zyuryaev, Mariya Alekseevna Terenicheva, Olga Nickolaevna Ganeeva, Alexey Vladimirovich Knigin

Abstract:

Summary: Due to the range of technical difficulties inherent in transcatheter valve-in-transcatheter valve approaches, therapeutic decisions must be individualized for each patient. Here, we discuss a case involving a male in his late 70s, whose clinical course included two previous transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) procedures and a history of infective endocarditis, later complicated by severe structural deterioration of the aortic prosthesis that necessitated a third transcatheter intervention. The patient was first managed with a Lotus valve in 2014 and underwent a valve-in-valve procedure using an Evolut prosthesis in 2022, which was subsequently compromised by endocarditis-driven degeneration and pronounced restenosis. In 2024, a third prosthetic valve, an Acurate neo2, was placed employing a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV approach. As TAVI indications continue to broaden and survival among recipients improves, complex procedures involving multiple prostheses are likely to increase, emphasizing the importance of advanced procedural planning, imaging protocols, and ongoing follow-up. One year after the most recent intervention, the patient displays no symptoms and maintains proper valve function. This case demonstrates the possibility of performing a third TAVI following managed prosthetic valve endocarditis, aided by CT-guided planning and a 1-year outcome assessment, which is seldom documented in current literature.

Background:

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) has become a standard therapeutic option for individuals with severe aortic stenosis, encompassing a wide spectrum of operative risk categories. With rising numbers of TAVI procedures and extended survival, structural valve degeneration and the necessity for repeat interventions have become increasingly prevalent in clinical settings.

In response, transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV) techniques have been adopted as practical approaches for managing dysfunctional transcatheter prostheses. Nevertheless, these procedures involve distinct anatomical and technical hurdles, such as the potential for coronary artery occlusion and diminished effective orifice area, which may result in severe prosthesis–patient mismatch. These complexities are heightened in sequential reintervention scenarios, notably transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV), where multiple superimposed valve frames require careful evaluation.

Current data concerning repeated transcatheter valve procedures are sparse, especially in patients who have experienced prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). In such intricate cases, meticulous preprocedural imaging, comprehensive risk assessment tailored to the individual, and multidisciplinary consultation are crucial for determining procedural feasibility and optimizing outcomes.

The present case underscores the significance of CT-based procedural planning and targeted transcatheter intervention in a patient with several previous TAVI procedures and a history of treated PVE, drawing attention to important factors in lifelong management of structural valve disorders.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his late 70s was admitted to the Academician E.I. Chazov National Medical Research Center presenting with shortness of breath, a spastic feeling on the left side of the chest during physical activity (specifically while walking 200–300 m), as well as dizziness and fatigue.

His past medical history included arterial hypertension, three-vessel coronary artery disease, and several percutaneous coronary interventions: the right coronary artery in 2009, left anterior descending (LAD) artery in 2010, a repeat LAD intervention in 2012, and the circumflex artery in 2014. He also had third-degree atrioventricular block treated with pacemaker implantation in 2014, type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and multiple episodes of gastrointestinal bleeding starting in 2022.

In 2014, progressive degeneration and significant stenosis of the aortic valve led to the transcatheter implantation of a 27 mm Lotus valve (Boston Scientific). By December 2021, the patient’s exertional dyspnoea worsened, and echocardiography confirmed severe bioprosthetic valve stenosis. By early 2022, the mean gradient had increased to 83 mm Hg, and left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) dropped to 42%, down from 54% noted in December.

In May 2022, a TAV-in-TAV procedure was performed using a 23 mm Evolut valve (Medtronic). This was complicated by a pulsatile femoral hematoma and pseudoaneurysm, necessitating open vascular intervention.

After the Evolut TAV-in-TAV procedure, the patient’s left ventricular EF improved from 42% to 54%, indicating an early positive haemodynamic outcome. Guideline-directed heart failure management included beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, and diuretics as clinically warranted.

Three months afterwards, in September 2022, the patient was readmitted to the hospital due to ongoing fever and worsening symptoms of heart failure. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) demonstrated marked restenosis of the aortic valve prosthesis, with a mean gradient of 35 mm Hg. Blood cultures identified Enterococcus faecalis, leading to a diagnosis of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). The patient underwent a complete regimen of targeted antibiotics for E. faecalis PVE, consisting of six weeks of intravenous ampicillin-based therapy, followed by oral treatment in line with recommendations from infectious disease specialists. By the time of the third TAVI, antimicrobial therapy had been concluded, and there was no ongoing long-term suppressive antibiotic use. Clinical evaluations, repeated blood cultures, transoesophageal echocardiography, and multislice CT (MSCT) demonstrated infection resolution with no evidence of ongoing endocarditis.

Following antimicrobial treatment and some improvement in symptoms, a follow-up visit in January 2024 found deterioration of the prosthetic valve, including severe aortic stenosis and moderate-to-severe regurgitation. The mean gradient across the valve was 49 mm Hg, and left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) had returned to 66%.

A decision for repeat intervention was reached. At admission in June 2024, TTE revealed ongoing grade 2–3 intraprosthetic aortic regurgitation, a persistent mean transvalvular gradient of 49 mm Hg, and a significantly reduced aortic valve area (AVA) of 0.5 cm², which is indicative of severe prosthetic valve dysfunction. AVA calculations were done using the continuity equation, utilizing left ventricular outflow tract measurements and Doppler velocity-time integrals. Visualization confirmed two overlapping bioprosthetic valves.

MSCT demonstrated a minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm in the Evolut prosthesis, verifying severe haemodynamic compromise and informing the plan for a third transcatheter valve implantation (figure 1). Preprocedural MSCT assessment was utilized to evaluate anatomical suitability and potential risk for coronary artery obstruction. The analysis entailed measuring coronary ostial height, sinus of Valsalva size, valve-to-coronary distance, and examining the interaction of frames between the previously deployed transcatheter valves. Special consideration was given to the spatial relationship between the projected neo-skirt and the coronary ostia, as well as to ensuring the feasibility of a high implantation approach to safeguard both coronary flow and future coronary access. This information influenced the choice of valve, implantation depth, and procedural strategy, making it possible to proceed without coronary protection.

Prior to the reintervention, transoesophageal echocardiography was repeated and excluded ongoing endocarditis or paravalvular issues.

Treatment:

Considering the patient's intricate triple-stented anatomy, history of infective endocarditis, and prohibitive surgical risk, the third TAVI was executed as a computed tomography-guided, strategy-focused intervention emphasizing protection of the coronary arteries, proper commissural alignment, and maximization of the remaining effective orifice area.

The intervention was carried out under local anesthesia utilizing left radial and left femoral arterial access. Severe aortic regurgitation was confirmed via aortography with a pigtail catheter (figure 2).

Through left femoral access, a Safari guidewire was advanced into the left ventricle. The aortic valve was pre-dilated using a 22/40 mm balloon catheter during rapid ventricular pacing at 180 beats per minute. Concurrent aortography verified ongoing unobstructed coronary flow (figure 3).

A self-expanding bioprosthetic valve (Acurate neo2, size S) was placed at the aortic valve site. Alignment of the device with the Evolut valve leaflets, as determined by preprocedural CT simulation, was performed to optimize the effective orifice area and minimize the likelihood of patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM).

Further post-dilation was accomplished with a 20/40 mm balloon during rapid pacing to enhance valve deployment. Final aortography confirmed the valve's accurate positioning, maintained coronary perfusion, and absence of perivalvular or transvalvular regurgitation (figure 4).

Attention to commissural alignment during valve placement, guided by preprocedural CT imaging and fluoroscopic markers, was intended to facilitate coronary access and decrease procedural complexity in future interventions.

Outcome and follow-up:

Procedural efficacy and valve function were evaluated utilizing the Valve Academic Research Consortium-3 (VARC-3) criteria, encompassing assessment of haemodynamic performance, detection of residual regurgitation, and identification of complications associated with the procedure.

Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) carried out immediately following the intervention on 7 June 2024 demonstrated correct positioning of the third transcatheter prosthesis. The leaflet motion was preserved, allowing full opening, and no aortic regurgitation was detected (grade 0). Nonetheless, haemodynamic measurements revealed a notable transvalvular pressure gradient, with a mean value of 67 mm Hg. The calculated aortic valve area (AVA) measured 0.9 cm² (indexed AVA 0.4 cm²/m²) and Doppler velocity index (DVI) was 0.24, findings indicative of severe patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM).

TTE performed one month after implantation showed considerable improvement, with the mean gradient decreased to 27 mm Hg and AVA increased to 1.1 cm². Evidence of left ventricular remodelling was seen as chamber volumes diminished. No residual regurgitation was present, and prosthesis function persisted in the range consistent with moderate stenosis.

After 3.5 months, the patient had no symptoms and demonstrated satisfactory exercise capacity. There were no indications of heart failure or recurrence of infection. The valve prosthesis continued to perform well, with maintained improvement in haemodynamic variables.

At the one-year follow-up (June 2025), clinical status remained stable and the patient reported no shortness of breath or signs of cardiac decompensation. TTE indicated a mean gradient of 26 mm Hg, AVA of 1.1 cm², and ejection fraction (EF) of 65%. No complications related to the valve, regurgitation, or recurrence of endocarditis were observed.

Collectively, these outcomes support the durability of the procedure and point toward lasting functional gains achieved with the TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV approach for this patient at elevated risk.

Discussion:

This case underscores both the complexity of clinical decision-making and the procedural intricacies encountered when undertaking a third TAVI in a patient who previously underwent TAV-in-TAV interventions and has a background of PVE.

Although TAVI is a well-established therapy for aortic stenosis, its application in patients with a prior history of endocarditis remains contentious. The 2025 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines for valvular heart disease and infective endocarditis management continue to recommend surgical valve replacement as the preferred treatment modality for PVE, especially when complicated by heart failure or persistent infection. Nonetheless, the guidelines stress the importance of tailoring the approach to the individual, with consideration of infection status, operative risk, and anatomical viability.

In 2014, TAVI was selected as the primary intervention due to the patient’s elevated surgical risk and extensive comorbid conditions, rendering surgical replacement less favourable. Upon development of structural valve dysfunction, a transcatheter repeat procedure was performed in 2022 to restore valve function and mitigate the dangers associated with open-heart surgery. After successful resolution of PVE and with no evidence of ongoing infection, a third transcatheter procedure was chosen in 2024 owing to the patient’s extremely high surgical risk and anatomical complexity stemming from the existing valve frames. Pre-procedural CT imaging supported the feasibility of maintaining coronary flow and securing adequate haemodynamic outcomes with the intended approach.

The failure of the prosthetic valve in this patient appears to have resulted from several contributing factors. Although the episode of infective endocarditis may have caused leaflet damage and led to mechanical obstruction, ongoing structural deterioration of the transcatheter valve prostheses was a major component. Crucially, after resolution of infection and the decision that continued suppressive antibiotics were unnecessary, management of the dysfunctional prosthetic valve followed protocols similar to those for patients without active or persistent endocarditis. Accordingly, procedural choices were determined mainly by anatomical suitability, the severity of haemodynamic compromise, and the overall surgical risk, rather than the patient's prior infectious episode.

For this particular case, the patient had finished the required course of antibiotics, exhibited no clinical or imaging evidence of active infection, and underwent comprehensive preprocedure assessment, including TEE and MSCT, both demonstrating an absence of vegetations or paravalvular abnormalities. In addition, the EuroSCORE II was calculated at 27.36%, and the patient had several comorbidities—COPD, diabetes, and a previous gastrointestinal bleed—as well as two prior transcatheter prostheses with extensive stent integration into the aortic wall. These factors made conventional surgery extremely risky and technically challenging. The deep-seated fixation of the earlier valves meant surgical correction would probably entail complete replacement of the aortic root and ascending aorta.

Multiple published reports have documented TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV interventions,37 describing distinct anatomical and technical obstacles encountered during these procedures. Our case contributes additional insight to this scarce literature, particularly due to the unusual circumstance of valve deterioration following endocarditis and sustained valve functionality at the 1-year mark.

This case highlights the increasing need for comprehensive lifetime management plans in structural valve disease. With each successive transcatheter procedure, factors such as multiple overlapping valve frames, commissural orientation, access to coronary arteries, and preservation of effective orifice area become progressively more complicated. Early consideration of long-term procedural sequences and careful planning are crucial, starting from the initial intervention—especially in younger individuals or those at elevated risk.

In this patient, the structural valve deterioration featured leaflet fibrosis, resulting from previous degeneration, along with likely mechanical limitation post-infection. The medical team elected to deploy a third valve—a self-expanding Acurate neo2 prosthesis—guided by preoperative CT imaging and 3D modeling, which verified procedural feasibility and satisfactory clearance of the coronaries (see figures 1, 5, and 7). The third transcatheter valve was intentionally implanted at a high position. Positioning the device at the level of the Evolut valve leaflets enabled securing the existing leaflets against the stent frame, thereby avoiding additional narrowing of the effective orifice area due to inward leaflet folding. This technique also reduced the risk of creating a new skirt below the coronary ostia, which facilitated both the maintenance of coronary blood flow and potential future coronary interventions. Decisions were informed by thorough preprocedure CT evaluation and played a pivotal role in achieving favorable hemodynamics in this triple-stented configuration. Although implanting three transcatheter valves is inherently complex, this methodology proved to be both technically viable and reproducible, provided there is comprehensive preprocedure imaging and an established implantation plan.

The Acurate neo2 valve was chosen due to its supra-annular leaflet placement, comparatively short frame, and favorable hemodynamics in restricted anatomies. Additionally, its capacity for commissural alignment was particularly important in this patient to safeguard future access to the coronary arteries.

One significant technical issue in TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedures involves the potential for compromised coronary access as a result of multiple overlapping stent frames. Even when commissural alignment is achieved, the increased mesh from successive valves may still complicate subsequent coronary interventions. This underscores the importance of thorough procedural planning in individuals with existing coronary disease or those for whom future coronary access may be critical. Preprocedural CT allowed for assessment of the intended landing zone, evaluation of frame interactions, and estimation of valve-to-coronary distances; however, comprehensive three-dimensional expansion analysis with specialized software was not conducted.

Resolution of the transvalvular gradient following the third valve placement was likely due to improved commissural alignment, successful post-dilatation, and partial mechanical expansion of the previous Evolut frame, resulting in better leaflet mobility and central flow characteristics. Although deliberate frame fracture was not performed, high-pressure balloon post-dilation probably facilitated further radial expansion of the Evolut frame, thus improving valve performance.

The intervention was carried out with institutional authorization for off-label usage of the Acurate neo2 prosthesis. Shared decision-making involved both the patient and his family, and the case underwent discussion in a multidisciplinary heart team meeting, which included interventional cardiologists, cardiac surgeons, imaging experts, and anesthesiologists.

In the event of early failure of the current valve, therapeutic options would be severely restricted. Surgical removal of the prosthesis would be highly complex and associated with considerable risk. Novel catheter-based approaches, such as bioprosthetic valve fracture or leaflet laceration procedures (for example, bioprosthetic or native aortic scallop intentional laceration to prevent iatrogenic coronary artery obstruction [BASILICA]), may be considered, but their effectiveness and safety in the context of a triple-stented anatomy remain unclear.
