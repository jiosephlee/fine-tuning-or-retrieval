Title: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

Evgeny Vladimirovich Merkulov, Goar Kimovna Arutyunyan, Andrey Sergeevich Tereshchenko, Dmitriy V. Pevzner, Iiliya Tarasovich Zyuryaev, Mariya Alekseevna Terenicheva, Olga Nickolaevna Ganeeva, Alexey Vladimirovich Knigin

Abstract:

Summary: Due to the range of technical complexities involved in transcatheter valve-in-transcatheter valve (TAV-in-TAV) procedures, therapeutic approaches must be customized for each individual case. We report the case of a male patient in his late 70s with a complicated medical background, including two prior transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) procedures and a history of infective endocarditis, who subsequently experienced severe dysfunction of his aortic prosthesis, necessitating a third transcatheter treatment. The patient initially received a Lotus valve in 2014, followed by a valve-in-valve procedure utilizing an Evolut device in 2022, which was complicated by degeneration due to endocarditis and marked restenosis. In 2024, an Acurate neo2 prosthesis was deployed through a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV strategy. As TAVI indications broaden and treated patients’ survival extends, complex interventions involving multiple transcatheter prostheses may become increasingly prevalent, underscoring the importance of enhanced procedural planning, advanced imaging modalities, and diligent long-term monitoring. One year after his third procedure, the patient continues to show no symptoms and the valve prosthesis remains functional. This case demonstrates that a third TAVI is a feasible option even following prosthetic valve endocarditis, using CT-based procedural planning and providing one-year follow-up, which is rarely documented in published reports.

Background:

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) is now a well-recognized therapy for individuals with severe aortic stenosis spanning various levels of surgical risk. With the ongoing rise in TAVI procedures and enhanced long-term survival, cases of structural valve deterioration and subsequent requirement for additional interventions are being seen more frequently in practice.

In this context, transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV) procedures have become a practical option for treating failed transcatheter heart valves. Nonetheless, these interventions carry distinct anatomical and technical difficulties, notably the possibility of coronary artery obstruction and a decrease in the effective orifice area, which may lead to significant prosthesis-patient mismatch. Such issues become even more pronounced during successive reinterventions, such as transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV), where the presence of several overlapping stent frames demands meticulous consideration.

There is a scarcity of data on repeated transcatheter valve procedures, especially among individuals who have previously experienced prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). In these complicated cases, comprehensive preprocedural imaging, tailored risk evaluation, and collaborative multidisciplinary planning are critical for assessing procedural suitability and achieving optimal results.

This case demonstrates how CT-guided planning and a strategy-based transcatheter intervention approach were utilized in a patient with a history of multiple TAVI procedures and managed prosthetic valve endocarditis, emphasizing important factors for long-term structural valve disease management.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his late seventies was hospitalized at the Academician E.I. Chazov National Medical Research Center following complaints of shortness of breath, a spasmodic feeling in the left chest provoked by exertion such as walking 200–300 meters, along with dizziness and tiredness.

His past medical history included arterial hypertension, three-vessel coronary artery disease, and several percutaneous coronary interventions: the right coronary artery in 2009, the left anterior descending (LAD) artery in 2010, a repeat intervention on the LAD in 2012, and the circumflex artery in 2014. Additional conditions comprised third-degree atrioventricular block managed with pacemaker implantation in 2014, type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding episodes beginning in 2022.

In 2014, the patient received a 27 mm Lotus valve (Boston Scientific) via transcatheter approach due to worsening aortic valve deterioration and marked stenosis. By December 2021, there was notable progression of exertional dyspnoea, and echocardiography revealed marked bioprosthetic valve stenosis. By early 2022, the mean gradient had risen to 83 mm Hg, and the ejection fraction had further dropped to 42%, compared to 54% recorded in December.

In May 2022, the patient received a TAV-in-TAV procedure utilizing a 23 mm Evolut valve (Medtronic). During the intervention, a pulsatile femoral hematoma and pseudoaneurysm developed, necessitating open vascular surgical management.

After the Evolut TAV-in-TAV procedure, the patient’s left ventricular ejection fraction increased from 42% to 54%, reflecting a positive initial haemodynamic effect. He received standard heart failure therapy according to guidelines, which included beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, and diuretics as appropriate.

Three months later, in September 2022, the patient was readmitted with ongoing fever and worsening symptoms of heart failure. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) revealed significant restenosis of the aortic valve prosthesis, with a mean gradient of 35 mm Hg. Blood cultures grew Enterococcus faecalis, leading to a diagnosis of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). The patient underwent a complete course of targeted antibiotic therapy for E. faecalis PVE, consisting of six weeks of intravenous ampicillin-based antibiotics, followed by oral therapy as directed by infectious disease specialists. By the time the third TAVI was performed, the antimicrobial therapy had been concluded, and the patient was not receiving ongoing suppressive antibiotics. Clinical evaluation, serial blood cultures, transoesophageal echocardiography, and multislice CT (MSCT) indicated that the infection had resolved with no signs of active endocarditis.

Following the conclusion of antibiotic treatment and some improvement in symptoms, reassessment in January 2024 demonstrated declining function of the prosthetic valve, characterized by severe aortic stenosis and moderate-to-severe regurgitation. The mean transvalvular gradient measured 49 mm Hg, and left ventricular ejection fraction had improved to 66%.

It was determined that another intervention was necessary. Upon admission in June 2024, transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) revealed ongoing grade 2–3 aortic regurgitation within the prosthesis, a mean transvalvular pressure gradient of 49 mm Hg, and a significantly diminished aortic valve area (AVA) measuring 0.5 cm², findings indicative of severe dysfunction of the prosthetic valve. The AVA calculation employed the continuity equation using left ventricular outflow tract data and Doppler-derived velocity-time integral measurements. Imaging identified two overlapping bioprosthetic valves.

MSCT demonstrated that the narrowest diameter within the Evolut prosthesis measured 13.7 mm, indicating significant haemodynamic impairment and informing the strategy for a third transcatheter valve implantation (see figure 1). The preprocedural MSCT was utilized to evaluate anatomic suitability and estimate the likelihood of coronary artery obstruction. This assessment involved examining coronary ostial height, the dimensions of the sinus of Valsalva, the distance between the valve and coronary arteries, and the interface among the frames of the previously placed transcatheter valves. Special focus was given to the spatial relationship between the anticipated neo-skirt and the coronary ostia, as well as the practicality of high-positioned implantation to maintain coronary perfusion and ensure access for potential future interventions. These anatomical insights influenced choices regarding the type of valve, depth of placement, and overall procedural plan, enabling the procedure to be completed without the need for coronary protection measures.

Transoesophageal echocardiography was performed prior to the reintervention and confirmed the absence of active endocarditis or paravalvular complications.

Treatment:

In light of the patient’s intricate anatomy involving three prior stents, previous history of infective endocarditis, and high risk associated with surgery, the team scheduled a third TAVI procedure guided by CT imaging. The focus was placed on safeguarding coronary flow, achieving proper commissural alignment, and maximizing the remaining effective orifice area.

The procedure was performed under local anaesthesia using left radial and left femoral arterial access. Aortography with a pigtail catheter confirmed severe aortic regurgitation (figure 2).

A Safari guidewire was advanced into the left ventricle through left femoral artery access. The aortic valve underwent balloon pre-dilatation utilizing a 22/40 mm catheter while rapid ventricular pacing at 180 beats per minute was maintained. Concurrent aortography demonstrated that coronary perfusion was preserved, as shown in figure 3.

An Acurate neo2 self-expanding bioprosthetic valve (size S) was placed in the aortic location. The device’s positioning was coordinated with the Evolut valve leaflets, as determined by preprocedural CT modeling, in order to optimize the effective orifice area and minimize the likelihood of patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM).

A 20/40 mm balloon was utilized for post-dilation during rapid pacing in order to achieve optimal valve expansion. Subsequent aortography demonstrated appropriate valve placement, maintained coronary blood flow, and no evidence of perivalvular or transvalvular regurgitation (figure 4).

Valve deployment was performed with attention to commissural alignment, utilizing preprocedural CT scans and fluoroscopic markers to maintain coronary accessibility and minimize potential difficulties with future interventions.

Outcome and follow-up:

Procedural outcomes and prosthetic valve function were evaluated using the Valve Academic Research Consortium-3 guidelines, encompassing assessments of haemodynamic performance, detection of any residual regurgitation, and monitoring for procedure-associated complications.

A transthoracic echocardiogram obtained immediately following the procedure on 7 June 2024 verified appropriate placement of the third transcatheter valve. The valve leaflets demonstrated full mobility and excursion, and there was no detectable aortic regurgitation (grade 0). Despite this, haemodynamic assessment revealed marked transvalvular gradients, with a mean value of 67 mm Hg. The aortic valve area (AVA) was measured at 0.9 cm² (indexed AVA 0.4 cm²/m²), and the Doppler velocity index (DVI) was 0.24—findings that are indicative of severe patient–prosthesis mismatch (PPM).

At 1 month post-implantation, transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated significant progress: the mean transvalvular gradient measured 27 mm Hg, and the aortic valve area had expanded to 1.1 cm². Evidence of left ventricular remodeling was present, indicated by decreased ventricular volumes. No residual regurgitation was detected, and the prosthetic valve continued to perform stably, classified within moderate stenosis parameters.

At 3.5 months follow-up, the patient continued to be free of symptoms and demonstrated adequate exercise capacity. There was no evidence of heart failure or recurrence of infection. The prosthetic device continued to function well, with persistent enhancement in haemodynamic measures.

At one year following the implantation procedure (June 2025), the patient exhibited ongoing clinical stability and maintained an active lifestyle, with no shortness of breath or indications of heart failure. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed a mean transvalvular gradient of 26 mm Hg, an aortic valve area of 1.1 cm², and a left ventricular ejection fraction of 65%. There were no detected valve-related adverse events, regurgitation, or signs of repeat endocarditis.

These findings confirm the durability of the result and suggest a sustained functional benefit of the TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV intervention in this high-risk patient.

Discussion:

This case highlights the decision-making process and technical challenges of performing a third TAVI in a patient with prior TAV-in-TAV procedures and a history of PVE.

Although TAVI is now a well-recognized therapy for aortic stenosis, its application in individuals with a history of endocarditis is still debated. The 2025 guidelines from the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) for treating valvular heart disease and infective endocarditis advocate for surgical valve replacement as the preferred approach in prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE), especially when the condition is associated with heart failure or persistent infection. Nevertheless, these guidelines highlight the need for personalised management based on infection status, operative risk, and anatomical suitability.

TAVI was initially selected as the treatment approach in 2014 due to the patient’s elevated surgical risk and numerous comorbidities, which rendered traditional surgical valve replacement an unsuitable option at the time. When structural valve deterioration developed, a repeat transcatheter intervention was chosen in 2022 in order to restore valve function while minimizing the hazards associated with open-heart surgery. After the successful management of PVE and in the absence of ongoing infection, a third transcatheter procedure was undertaken in 2024 owing to extremely high surgical risk, complex anatomical features from existing valve frames, and CT evidence supporting the feasibility of a strategy that maintained coronary patency and achieved satisfactory haemodynamic outcomes.

The underlying cause of this patient's prosthetic valve dysfunction was probably due to multiple factors. Although the past occurrence of infective endocarditis could have resulted in leaflet damage and ensuing mechanical impairment, ongoing structural deterioration of the transcatheter valves was also a major contributor. Notably, after the infection was deemed to be cleared and indefinite antibiotic suppression was deemed unnecessary, the approach to addressing prosthetic valve malfunction was similar to the management in patients without current or persistent endocarditis. Thus, choices regarding intervention were mainly influenced by anatomical suitability, the degree of haemodynamic compromise, and the surgical risk profile, rather than being determined solely by the antecedent infection.

In this scenario, the patient had finished a course of antimicrobials, exhibited no signs of active infection either clinically or radiographically, and underwent comprehensive preprocedural assessments including both TEE and MSCT, which demonstrated no evidence of vegetations or paravalvular issues. Additionally, the patient’s EuroSCORE II was calculated at 27.36%, and she had multiple significant comorbid conditions, such as COPD, diabetes, and a history of gastrointestinal bleeding, along with two previously implanted transcatheter valves that were extensively incorporated into the aortic wall. These factors collectively made open surgical intervention especially risky and technically challenging. Due to the secure integration of the earlier valves, surgical treatment would have probably involved replacement of the entire aortic root and ascending aorta.

Multiple reports have described TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV interventions,37 each illustrating distinct anatomical and technical obstacles. Our case contributes to this sparse literature, presenting the distinctive scenario of degeneration following endocarditis and maintained valve performance at one-year follow-up.

This case highlights the increasing relevance of comprehensive, lifelong management plans in treating structural valve disease. Each successive transcatheter reintervention introduces additional complexities, including factors like layered stent frames, commissural orientation, the ability to reaccess coronary arteries, and preserving sufficient effective orifice area. Proactive, long-term procedural planning and early discussions about potential future interventions are essential—especially for younger individuals or those considered high-risk—even prior to the initial procedure.

In this case, leaflet fibrosis resulting from previous degeneration and likely mechanical limitation following infection contributed to the patient's structural valve deterioration. The clinical team decided to implant a third valve—a self-expanding Acurate neo2 prosthesis—after preprocedural CT and 3D modeling confirmed procedural feasibility and sufficient clearance of the coronary arteries (see figures 1, 5, and 7). The third transcatheter valve was intentionally implanted at a high position. By positioning the device at the height of the Evolut valve leaflets, the pre-existing leaflets were secured against the stent frame, preventing further decrease in effective orifice area that could result from leaflet inward folding. Furthermore, this high implantation minimized creation of a neo-skirt beneath the coronary ostia, aiding in the maintenance of coronary blood flow and ensuring potential future coronary access. This technique was informed by detailed preprocedural CT evaluation and played a pivotal role in attaining satisfactory haemodynamic performance in the context of triple-valve stenting. While the use of three transcatheter valves introduces significant procedural complexity, the described method was technically viable and reproducible when meticulous preprocedural imaging and a well-planned implantation strategy were employed.

The Acurate neo2 was chosen because it features supra-annular leaflet placement, a comparatively short frame, and demonstrates beneficial haemodynamic characteristics in constricted anatomies. The valve’s architecture facilitates commissural alignment, an important factor in this scenario to maintain potential coronary access going forward.

An important technical issue associated with TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedures is the potential for compromised coronary access resulting from several superimposed stent frames. Even when commissural alignment is pursued, the overall accumulation of mesh can complicate subsequent coronary procedures. This underscores the importance of thorough procedural planning, particularly in patients with pre-existing coronary artery disease or in whom preserved coronary access is likely to be necessary. Preprocedural CT provided assessment of the landing site, allowed evaluation of frame interaction, and offered estimations of the distance between the valve and coronaries; however, comprehensive three-dimensional expansion assessment utilizing specialized software was not performed.

The resolution of the transvalvular gradient following the third device implantation is likely due to improved commissural alignment, successful post-dilation, and partial mechanical widening of the previous Evolut stent frame, which led to better leaflet mobility and more favorable central flow patterns. Although no deliberate frame fracture was attempted, performing high-pressure balloon post-dilation probably facilitated additional radial expansion of the Evolut, resulting in improved valvular performance.

The intervention was carried out with institutional authorization for off-label application of the Acurate neo2 valve. The decision-making process was collaborative, engaging both the patient and his family, and the case was presented at a multidisciplinary heart team meeting comprising interventional cardiologists, cardiac surgeons, imaging experts, and anesthesiologists.

If the existing prosthesis were to fail ahead of the expected timeframe, available therapies would be highly restricted. Surgical removal would continue to pose significant technical challenges and carry substantial risk. Novel transcatheter approaches like bioprosthetic valve fracturing or leaflet laceration—for instance, bioprosthetic or native aortic scallop intentional laceration to prevent iatrogenic coronary artery obstruction (BASILICA)—could potentially be options, but the practicality and safety of these interventions in scenarios involving three stented layers are not yet established.
