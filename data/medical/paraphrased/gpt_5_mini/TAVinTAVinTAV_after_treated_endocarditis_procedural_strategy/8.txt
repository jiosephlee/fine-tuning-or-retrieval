Title: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

Evgeny V. Merkulov, Goar K. Arutyunyan, Andrey S. Tereshchenko, Dmitriy V. Pevzner, Iiliya T. Zyuryaev, Mariya A. Terenicheva, Olga N. Ganeeva, Alexey V. Knigin

Abstract:

Summary: Due to the range of technical complexities inherent in transcatheter valve-in-transcatheter valve (TAV-in-TAV) procedures, the management approach should be individualized for each patient. We report the case of a male in his late seventies with a complicated medical background, which included two previous transcatheter aortic valve implantations (TAVI) and a history of infective endocarditis, who subsequently experienced significant deterioration of the aortic prosthesis and required a third transcatheter intervention. The patient initially received a Lotus valve in 2014, underwent a TAV-in-TAV procedure with an Evolut device in 2022, and then suffered endocarditis-associated degeneration with marked restenosis. In 2024, a third transcatheter prosthetic valve (Acurate neo2) was placed using a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV technique. As indications for TAVI continue to broaden and patient life expectancy increases, the incidence of complex procedures involving multiple prosthetic valves is likely to rise, underscoring the importance of enhancing procedural planning, imaging approaches, and long-term follow-up. At the 1-year follow-up point, the patient remained symptom-free and the valve function was maintained. This case demonstrates the practicality of performing a third TAVI following previously treated prosthetic valve endocarditis, utilizing CT-guided planning and supported by 1-year follow-up, a scenario that is still infrequently described in the existing literature.

Background:

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) is now a well-recognized therapeutic approach for individuals with severe aortic stenosis, regardless of their surgical risk category. With the expanding use of TAVI and the associated improvement in patient longevity, clinicians are more frequently facing cases of structural valve degeneration and the subsequent requirement for additional procedures.

Within this context, transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV) interventions have become a feasible approach for treating degenerated transcatheter bioprostheses. Nevertheless, these procedures carry distinct anatomical and procedural difficulties, including an elevated risk of coronary artery occlusion and diminished effective orifice area, potentially leading to pronounced prosthesis-patient mismatch. These complications are further intensified with repeated procedures such as transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV), where the presence of multiple superimposed valve stents necessitates meticulous assessment.

There is a paucity of data concerning repeated transcatheter valve interventions, especially among individuals who have previously experienced prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). In these challenging cases, comprehensive imaging prior to the procedure, tailored evaluation of patient risks, and collaborative decision-making across specialties are critical to assess procedural suitability and enhance outcome success.

This report demonstrates the importance of CT-guided procedural planning and a strategy-based transcatheter approach in a patient with a history of multiple TAVI operations and managed prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE), emphasizing crucial factors for long-term care in structural valve disorders.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his late 70s was hospitalized at the Academician E.I. Chazov National Medical Research Center following complaints of shortness of breath, a spasmodic feeling on the left side of his chest upon physical activity (specifically when walking 200–300 m), as well as experiencing dizziness and tiredness.

His past medical history included arterial hypertension and three-vessel coronary artery disease, with a history of several percutaneous coronary interventions: right coronary artery in 2009, left anterior descending (LAD) artery in 2010, repeat LAD intervention in 2012, and circumflex artery in 2014. He also had third-degree atrioventricular block managed with pacemaker implantation in 2014, along with comorbid conditions such as type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding episodes beginning in 2022.

In 2014, the patient received a 27 mm Lotus valve (Boston Scientific) via transcatheter approach due to worsening aortic valve disease and significant stenosis. By December 2021, there was a marked deterioration in exertional dyspnoea, with echocardiography indicating advanced stenosis of the bioprosthetic valve. In early 2022, the mean transvalvular gradient had risen to 83 mm Hg, accompanied by a further decrease in ejection fraction (EF) to 42%, compared to 54% recorded in December.

In May 2022, the patient received a TAV-in-TAV procedure utilizing a 23 mm Evolut valve (Medtronic). Following the intervention, the patient experienced a pulsatile femoral hematoma and pseudoaneurysm, necessitating open surgical repair of the vascular injury.

After receiving the Evolut TAV-in-TAV, the patient's left ventricular ejection fraction increased from 42% to 54%, suggesting an early positive haemodynamic effect. Guideline-recommended medical management for heart failure was provided, including beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, and diuretics when appropriate.

Three months later, in September 2022, the patient was readmitted due to ongoing fever and an exacerbation of heart failure symptoms. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) revealed significant restenosis of the aortic valve prosthesis, with a mean gradient of 35 mm Hg. Blood cultures identified Enterococcus faecalis, leading to a diagnosis of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). The patient underwent a complete course of pathogen-directed antimicrobial therapy for E. faecalis-related PVE, which included six weeks of intravenous ampicillin-based treatment, followed by oral antibiotics as advised by infectious disease specialists. Before the third TAVI, antimicrobial therapy had concluded, and the patient was not on ongoing suppressive antibiotics. Clinical evaluation, multiple blood cultures, transoesophageal echocardiography, and multislice CT (MSCT) demonstrated infection resolution without indications of active endocarditis.

Following the conclusion of antimicrobial treatment and some improvement in symptoms, a follow-up examination in January 2024 showed deterioration in prosthetic valve performance, characterized by severe aortic stenosis and moderate-to-severe regurgitation. The mean transvalvular gradient measured 49 mm Hg, while left ventricular ejection fraction had improved to 66%.

It was decided to undertake a repeat intervention. Upon admission in June 2024, transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) revealed ongoing moderate to moderately severe intraprosthetic aortic regurgitation, a mean transvalvular gradient of 49 mm Hg, and a significantly decreased aortic valve area (AVA) measuring 0.5 cm², findings indicative of severe malfunction of the prosthetic valve. The AVA was determined using the continuity equation, derived from measurements of the left ventricular outflow tract and Doppler-obtained velocity-time integrals. Visualization showed the presence of two overlapping bioprosthetic valves.

MSCT identified a minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm within the Evolut prosthesis, indicating significant haemodynamic impairment and providing essential information for planning a third transcatheter valve procedure (figure 1). The preprocedural MSCT evaluation assessed anatomical suitability and the potential for coronary artery obstruction by examining coronary ostial height, sinus of Valsalva measurements, valve-to-coronary distances, and the interaction between the structures of the previously implanted transcatheter valves. Special consideration was given to the predicted position of the neo-skirt in relation to the coronary ostia, as well as the possibility of high implantation to maintain coronary perfusion and future coronary accessibility. These assessments informed the choice of valve, depth of implantation, and procedural approach, allowing the procedure to proceed without coronary protection.

Transoesophageal echocardiography was performed prior to the reintervention and confirmed the absence of active endocarditis or paravalvular complications.

Treatment:

Due to the patient’s intricate anatomy involving three prior stents, a history of infective endocarditis, and a surgical risk considered prohibitive, the third TAVI was designed as a strategy-focused procedure informed by CT imaging. The approach prioritized coronary protection, proper commissural alignment, and maximization of the remaining effective orifice area.

The procedure was performed under local anaesthesia using left radial and left femoral arterial access. Aortography with a pigtail catheter confirmed severe aortic regurgitation (figure 2).

A Safari guidewire was advanced into the left ventricle through the left femoral approach. The aortic valve was pre-dilated using a 22/40 mm balloon catheter during rapid ventricular pacing at 180 beats per minute. Concurrent aortography verified that coronary perfusion was maintained (figure 3).

An Acurate neo2 self-expanding bioprosthetic valve (size S) was deployed in the aortic position. Utilizing preprocedural CT-based modeling, the valve was positioned in alignment with the Evolut valve leaflets to optimize the effective orifice area and minimize the likelihood of patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM).

A 20/40 mm balloon was utilized for post-dilation while rapid pacing was maintained to ensure optimal expansion of the valve. The concluding aortogram verified accurate placement, maintained coronary flow, and showed no evidence of either perivalvular or transvalvular regurgitation (figure 4).

During valve implantation, careful attention was paid to commissural alignment in order to maintain coronary access and minimize procedural challenges in the future, utilizing guidance from preprocedural CT scans and fluoroscopic reference points.

Outcome and follow-up:

Procedural outcomes and prosthetic valve function were evaluated based on the Valve Academic Research Consortium-3 guidelines, incorporating assessments of haemodynamic performance, detection of any remaining regurgitation, and identification of procedure-associated complications.

A transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) carried out immediately post-procedure on 7 June 2024 verified proper placement of the third transcatheter valve. The leaflets demonstrated full mobility with unobstructed opening, and there was no aortic regurgitation detected (grade 0). Nonetheless, hemodynamic assessment revealed elevated transvalvular gradients, with a mean gradient measured at 67 mm Hg. The aortic valve area (AVA) was calculated at 0.9 cm² (with an indexed AVA of 0.4 cm²/m²), and the Doppler velocity index (DVI) was 0.24, findings indicative of severe prosthesis–patient mismatch (PPM).

At one month post-implantation, transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) demonstrated significant progress: the mean gradient measured 27 mm Hg, and the aortic valve area (AVA) had expanded to 1.1 cm². Evidence of left ventricular remodeling was present, as indicated by decreased chamber volumes. No residual regurgitation was detected, and the prosthetic valve continued to function steadily, maintaining moderate stenosis.

At 3.5 months follow-up, the patient continued to exhibit no symptoms and demonstrated adequate exercise capacity. There was no evidence of heart failure or return of infection. The prosthetic valve continued to function well, and haemodynamic measures remained improved.

One year after the implantation in June 2025, the patient continued to be clinically stable and active, exhibiting no dyspnea or indications of decompensation. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed a mean transvalvular gradient of 26 mm Hg, an aortic valve area of 1.1 cm², and an ejection fraction of 65%. There were no valve-associated complications, regurgitation, or signs of recurrent endocarditis detected.

These findings confirm the durability of the result and suggest a sustained functional benefit of the TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV intervention in this high-risk patient.

Discussion:

This case highlights the decision-making process and technical challenges of performing a third TAVI in a patient with prior TAV-in-TAV procedures and a history of PVE.

Although transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) is now a recognized therapy for aortic stenosis, its application in individuals with a history of endocarditis is still debated. The 2025 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines for treating valvular heart disease and infective endocarditis advocate for surgical valve replacement in prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE), especially when patients present with heart failure or persistent infection. Nonetheless, the guidelines stress the importance of tailoring treatment to each patient, considering infection status, surgical risk, and anatomical considerations.

TAVI was initially selected as the preferred approach in 2014 due to the patient’s elevated operative risk and significant comorbid conditions, rendering traditional surgical valve replacement inadvisable at the time. When the patient later experienced structural valve deterioration, a transcatheter reintervention was carried out in 2022 to restore valvular function without subjecting the patient to the hazards of open-heart surgery. After successful management of PVE and in the absence of ongoing infection, a third transcatheter procedure was chosen in 2024, guided by unacceptably high surgical risk, anatomical complexities related to existing valve structures, and CT evidence supporting the technical feasibility of an approach that would preserve coronary perfusion and provide satisfactory haemodynamic outcomes.

In this case, the failure of the prosthetic valve was probably due to multiple factors. Although the episode of infective endocarditis may have caused damage to the leaflet and led to subsequent mechanical limitation, ongoing structural deterioration of the transcatheter valve devices was also a major contributor. Crucially, after the infection had been considered eradicated and there was no need for continued antibiotic suppression, the approach to managing the prosthetic valve malfunction became consistent with care for patients who do not have active or persistent endocarditis. As a result, procedural planning was determined primarily by considerations of anatomical suitability, the severity of haemodynamic compromise, and the patient's operative risk, rather than solely the previous occurrence of infection.

In this scenario, the patient had completed a full course of antibiotics and demonstrated no evidence of ongoing infection, either clinically or on imaging. Preprocedural assessments, which included both transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) and multislice computed tomography (MSCT), ruled out vegetations or any paravalvular issues. The patient’s European System for Cardiac Operative Risk Evaluation (EuroSCORE II) was 27.36%, and their medical history was notable for serious comorbid conditions such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), diabetes mellitus, and prior episodes of gastrointestinal bleeding. Additionally, the patient had two previously placed transcatheter prosthetic valves with substantial stent ingrowth into the aortic wall, making conventional surgery extremely risky and technically complex. Due to the extensive anchoring of the earlier prostheses, surgical management would probably have required complete replacement of both the aortic root and the ascending aorta.

Multiple case reports describing TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV interventions have been documented,^37 emphasizing a range of anatomical and technical difficulties. This case contributes further to the scarce literature, particularly given the distinct scenario of degeneration following endocarditis and sustained valve performance at the one-year follow-up.

This case highlights the increasing significance of adopting long-term management approaches in structural valve pathology. As additional transcatheter interventions are performed, factors including stacked valve frames, precise commissural orientation, potential for coronary access, and preservation of adequate effective orifice area present added challenges. Proactive long-term planning and early conversations regarding procedural options are essential, beginning with the initial procedure—especially for younger patients or those at elevated risk.

The patient's structural valve deterioration was characterized by leaflet fibrosis resulting from previous degeneration and likely mechanical limitation post-infection. The clinical team opted to place a third valve—a self-expanding Acurate neo2 prosthesis—following preprocedural CT and three-dimensional modeling that confirmed procedural viability and sufficient distance from the coronary arteries (see figures 1, 5, and 7). A high implantation of this third transcatheter valve was intentionally selected as a procedural maneuver. Positioning the device at the same level as the Evolut valve's leaflets enabled the pre-existing leaflets to be secured against the stent frame, which helped avert a further decrease in the effective orifice area due to potential inward leaflet collapse. Furthermore, this method limited the development of a neo-skirt beneath the coronary ostia, aiding in the preservation of both coronary perfusion and possible future coronary interventions. The preprocedural CT evaluation was instrumental in directing this approach, which was crucial for achieving suitable haemodynamics in a setting of triple valve stenting. Despite the challenges of deploying three transcatheter valves, this method was both technically achievable and reproducible, provided that preprocedural imaging was thorough and an established implantation plan was followed.

The Acurate neo2 valve was chosen because its supra-annular leaflet configuration, comparatively short frame, and advantageous haemodynamics make it suitable for use in narrow anatomies. The device’s construction facilitates commissural alignment, an important factor in this instance to maintain potential access to the coronary arteries in the future.

An important technical issue in TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV cases is the potential for compromised coronary access as a result of several superimposed stent frames. While attempts were made to achieve commissural alignment, the aggregated mesh could still hinder subsequent coronary procedures. This underscores the importance of careful procedural planning for individuals with existing coronary disease or in whom maintaining coronary access is likely to be important in the future. Preprocedural CT facilitated assessment of the landing zone, analysis of stent frame relationships, and estimation of valve-to-coronary distances, though a detailed three-dimensional expansion analysis using specialized software was not conducted.

The resolution of the transvalvular gradient following the third valve placement is likely due to improved commissural orientation, successful post-dilation, and partial mechanical widening of the previously implanted Evolut frame, which enhanced leaflet mobility and central hemodynamics. Although no deliberate frame fracture was undertaken, post-dilation with a high-pressure balloon probably facilitated additional radial expansion of the Evolut, leading to better valve performance.

The intervention was carried out with institutional consent for the off-label application of the Acurate neo2 prosthesis. The patient and his family participated in shared decision-making, and the case underwent evaluation by a multidisciplinary heart team comprised of interventional cardiologists, cardiac surgeons, imaging experts, and anesthesiologists.

If the present prosthesis were to fail earlier than expected, therapeutic choices would be highly constrained. Removal of the device through surgery would continue to pose considerable technical challenges and significant risk. Novel transcatheter techniques, including methods such as bioprosthetic valve fracture or leaflet laceration (for example, bioprosthetic or native aortic scallop intentional laceration to prevent iatrogenic coronary artery obstruction [BASILICA]), could be potential options, though it is unclear whether these approaches are safe or feasible in situations involving triple-stented anatomy.
