Title: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

Evgeny Vladimirovich Merkulov, Goar Kimovna Arutyunyan, Andrey Sergeevich Tereshchenko, Dmitriy V Pevzner, Iiliya Tarasovich Zyuryaev, Mariya Alekseevna Terenicheva, Olga Nickolaevna Ganeeva, Alexey Vladimirovich Knigin

Abstract:

Summary: Due to the variety of technical considerations involved in transcatheter valve-in-transcatheter valve interventions, management decisions for each patient should be individualized. We describe a male patient in his late 70s with a complicated medical history, including two previous transcatheter aortic valve implantations (TAVI) and a diagnosis of infective endocarditis, who subsequently experienced significant deterioration of his aortic prosthesis necessitating a third transcatheter procedure. He initially received a Lotus valve in 2014, underwent a TAV-in-TAV with an Evolut prosthesis in 2022, and then developed endocarditis-related deterioration and severe restenosis. In 2024, an Acurate neo2 prosthetic valve was placed through a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV approach. As indications for TAVI continue to broaden and patient longevity increases, these intricate multi-prosthesis procedures are likely to become more common, underscoring the importance of enhanced procedural planning, optimized imaging, and comprehensive follow-up. At one-year post-procedure, the patient is asymptomatic with sustained valve function. This report demonstrates the practicality of a third TAVI following prosthetic valve endocarditis, facilitated by CT-based planning and supported by one-year outcome, which is infrequently documented in the available literature.

Background:

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) is now a well-established therapeutic approach for individuals with severe aortic stenosis, encompassing a wide spectrum of surgical risk categories. With rising numbers of patients receiving TAVI and improvements in long-term survival, clinicians are increasingly encountering cases of structural valve deterioration necessitating repeat procedures.

Transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV) interventions have emerged as a practical option for addressing malfunctioning transcatheter prostheses. Nonetheless, these procedures present unique anatomical and procedural complexities, such as the heightened risk of coronary artery occlusion and a smaller effective orifice area, which may result in significant prosthesis-patient mismatch. These issues become even more pronounced during successive reinterventions, for example, transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV), where the cumulative layering of valve frames necessitates meticulous evaluation.

The available data on sequential transcatheter valve reinterventions are sparse, especially among patients with prior episodes of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). In such high-complexity cases, comprehensive preprocedural imaging, tailored risk evaluation, and collaborative multidisciplinary deliberation are critical to assessing procedural suitability and maximizing outcomes.

The present case demonstrates the use of computed tomography (CT)-based planning and a strategic transcatheter approach in a patient who had undergone multiple prior TAVI interventions and received treatment for PVE, underscoring important aspects of managing structural valve disease throughout a patient’s lifetime.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his late seventies was admitted to the Academician E.I. Chazov National Medical Research Center after presenting with shortness of breath, a spasmodic sensation on the left side of his chest occurring during physical activity (while walking 200–300 m), as well as dizziness and fatigue.

His past medical history included arterial hypertension, three-vessel coronary artery disease, and multiple percutaneous coronary interventions: right coronary artery in 2009, left anterior descending (LAD) artery in 2010, recurrent LAD intervention in 2012, and circumflex artery in 2014. Additional conditions comprised third-degree atrioventricular block managed with pacemaker implantation in 2014, type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding episodes since 2022.

In 2014, advancing aortic valve degeneration and marked stenosis necessitated transcatheter placement of a 27 mm Lotus valve (Boston Scientific). By December 2021, worsening exertional dyspnoea and echocardiographic assessment revealed significant bioprosthetic valve stenosis. In early 2022, the mean gradient had risen to 83 mm Hg and the ejection fraction had dropped to 42% (from 54% noted in December).

The patient underwent TAV-in-TAV with a 23 mm Evolut valve (Medtronic) in May 2022. This intervention was complicated by the onset of a pulsatile femoral hematoma and pseudoaneurysm, requiring surgical intervention with open vascular repair.

After the Evolut TAV-in-TAV procedure, his left ventricular ejection fraction improved from 42% to 54%, reflecting a positive early hemodynamic effect. The patient received guideline-based medical management for heart failure, including beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, and diuretics as needed.

Three months later, in September 2022, the patient was admitted once more due to ongoing fever and deteriorating symptoms of heart failure. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) demonstrated significant restenosis of the aortic valve prosthesis, with a mean gradient measuring 35 mm Hg. Blood cultures identified Enterococcus faecalis, establishing the diagnosis of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). Targeted antimicrobial therapy for PVE attributed to E. faecalis was administered, consisting of 6 weeks of intravenous ampicillin-based treatment, followed by oral antibiotics as advised by infectious disease specialists. Upon undergoing the third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI), the antimicrobial regimen was completed, and long-term suppressive antibiotics were not required. Clinical evaluation, repeat blood cultures, transoesophageal echocardiography, and multislice CT (MSCT) demonstrated resolution of infection, with no signs indicative of ongoing endocarditis.

Following the completion of antimicrobial treatment and partial improvement in symptoms, subsequent follow-up in January 2024 indicated further decline in prosthetic valve function, manifesting as severe aortic stenosis with moderate-to-severe regurgitation. The mean transvalvular gradient was recorded at 49 mm Hg, while the left ventricular ejection fraction had improved to 66%.

The clinical team opted for reintervention. Upon hospitalisation in June 2024, TTE revealed persistent grade 2–3 intraprosthetic aortic regurgitation, a mean gradient of 49 mm Hg across the valve, and a significantly diminished aortic valve area (AVA) of 0.5 cm², all consistent with advanced prosthetic valve dysfunction. The AVA was derived via the continuity equation, utilizing measurements from the left ventricular outflow tract and Doppler velocity-time integrals. Imaging detected two overlapping bioprosthetic valves.

MSCT disclosed a minimum internal lumen diameter of 13.7 mm within the Evolut prosthesis, verifying substantial haemodynamic impairment and informing procedural plans for a third transcatheter valve placement (figure 1). Preprocedural MSCT evaluation was conducted to review anatomical suitability and assess risk for coronary artery obstruction, including assessment of coronary ostial height, sinus of Valsalva measurements, valve-to-coronary distance, and interplay between the previously implanted transcatheter valve frames. Careful consideration was given to the projected neo-skirt in relation to the coronary ostia, alongside the practicality of elevated implantation to maintain coronary flow and permit future coronary intervention. These data influenced choice of valve, implantation depth, and procedural approach, enabling the procedure without the need for coronary protection.

Transoesophageal echocardiography was also performed ahead of the reintervention and verified the absence of active endocarditis or paravalvular issues.

Treatment:

Considering the patient's intricate anatomy with three prior stents, history of infective endocarditis, and significant surgical risk, the third TAVI procedure was designed using CT guidance with a focus on coronary safeguarding, commissural orientation, and maximizing residual effective orifice area.

The intervention utilized local anesthesia, accessing the arteries via left radial and left femoral approaches. Severe aortic regurgitation was confirmed with aortography performed through a pigtail catheter (figure 2).

A Safari guidewire was introduced into the left ventricle through the left femoral route. The aortic valve was pre-dilated with a 22/40 mm balloon during rapid ventricular pacing at 180 bpm. Concurrent aortographic imaging verified that coronary flow remained unobstructed (figure 3).

An Acurate neo2 self-expanding bioprosthesis, size S, was deployed in the aortic location. Preprocedural CT modeling enabled alignment of the device with the leaflets of the Evolut valve, aiming to maximize the effective orifice area and minimize the likelihood of patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM).

Valve post-dilation was carried out with a 20/40 mm balloon during rapid pacing to enhance expansion. Final aortography demonstrated proper device placement, maintained coronary circulation, and showed no evidence of perivalvular or transvalvular regurgitation (figure 4).

Throughout deployment, commissural orientation was prioritized to facilitate future coronary access and lower procedural complexity, informed by CT imaging and fluoroscopic anatomical reference points.

Outcome and follow-up:

The evaluation of procedural outcome and prosthetic valve function was carried out using the Valve Academic Research Consortium-3 criteria, encompassing assessment of haemodynamic performance, residual regurgitation, and any procedure-associated complications.

Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) conducted immediately post-procedure on 7 June 2024 verified successful placement of the third transcatheter valve. The leaflets demonstrated normal mobility with full excursion, and there was no detectable aortic regurgitation (graded as 0). Despite this, haemodynamic measurements revealed elevated transvalvular gradients, with a mean value of 67 mm Hg. The aortic valve area (AVA) calculated was 0.9 cm² (indexed AVA 0.4 cm²/m²), and the Doppler velocity index (DVI) measured 0.24, indicative of severe prosthesis–patient mismatch (PPM).

At the 1-month follow-up, TTE indicated substantial haemodynamic improvement: the mean gradient decreased to 27 mm Hg and AVA had risen to 1.1 cm². Evidence of left ventricular remodeling was seen, marked by a decrease in chamber dimensions. Valve competence remained, with no detectable residual regurgitation, and overall function was preserved within a range indicative of moderate stenosis.

After 3.5 months, the patient continued to report no symptoms and demonstrated preserved exercise capacity. There were no manifestations of heart failure or recurrence of infection. The bioprosthesis sustained optimal function with continued enhancement in haemodynamic parameters.

At the 1-year assessment following implantation (June 2025), the patient remained clinically stable and active, with no exertional dyspnoea or signs of clinical deterioration. TTE demonstrated a mean pressure gradient of 26 mm Hg, AVA of 1.1 cm², and an ejection fraction (EF) of 65%. There were no occurrences of valve-related complications, regurgitant lesions, or recurrent endocarditis.

These results support the long-term durability and ongoing functional improvement following TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV implantation in this high-risk individual.

Discussion:

This report underscores both the clinical reasoning and procedural complexities associated with executing a third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) in a patient who previously underwent two TAV-in-TAV interventions and has a background of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE).

Although TAVI is a well-accepted therapeutic option for aortic stenosis, its application in individuals with a history of endocarditis remains debated. The 2025 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines for treating valvular heart disease and infective endocarditis recommend surgical valve replacement as the preferred approach in PVE, especially where heart failure or refractory infection is present. Nonetheless, the guidelines advise that management decisions should be tailored to the patient, factoring in infection status, operative risk, and anatomical suitability.

TAVI was initially selected in 2014 due to the patient’s elevated surgical risk and significant comorbid conditions, rendering traditional surgery less appropriate. When structural valve degeneration emerged, a transcatheter redo approach was adopted in 2022 to reestablish valve functionality and circumvent the hazards of open surgery. After effective management of PVE and without evidence of ongoing infection, proceeding with a third transcatheter procedure in 2024 was guided by the patient’s unacceptably high surgical risk, intricate anatomical issues stemming from previous valve implants, and CT assessment indicating that this plan would maintain coronary patency and deliver satisfactory haemodynamics.

The causes of prosthetic valve dysfunction in this patient were likely varied. Although an episode of infective endocarditis may have led to leaflet damage with subsequent mechanical limitation, ongoing structural deterioration of the transcatheter valves was also a major factor. Notably, after resolution of the infection with no requirement for chronic suppressive antibiotics, the approach to managing the valve dysfunction mirrored that used in patients without current or recurrent endocarditis. As a result, the primary considerations for procedural planning focused on anatomical suitability, the severity of haemodynamic impairment, and overall surgical risk, rather than relying solely on the previous infectious history.

In this instance, the patient completed antimicrobial treatment, exhibited no clinical or imaging indications of persistent infection, and underwent an extensive preprocedure assessment, including both TEE and MSCT, which confirmed an absence of vegetations or paravalvular pathology. Additionally, the patient's EuroSCORE II was 27.36%, and there were multiple comorbidities (COPD, diabetes, a history of gastrointestinal bleeding), along with two previously placed transcatheter prosthetic valves that had become extensively incorporated into the aortic wall. These factors combined to make open surgery exceptionally high risk and technically challenging. The firm anchoring of the prior valves meant that a surgical approach would probably have required complete replacement of the aortic root and ascending aorta.

Multiple reports documenting TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV interventions have been described,37 illustrating diverse anatomical and procedural obstacles. Our report contributes additional data to this small collection, distinguished by the setting of degeneration following endocarditis and sustained valve performance at the one-year mark.

This case highlights the increasing significance of lifelong management approaches in structural valve pathology. Each successive transcatheter procedure introduces greater complexity regarding issues such as stent frame accumulation, proper commissural alignment, maintaining coronary access, and preserving adequate effective orifice area. Thorough preplanning and early discussions about potential procedural paths are essential, beginning with the initial intervention—especially for younger patients or those at elevated risk.

In this patient, structural valve failure was characterized by leaflet fibrosis stemming from previous degeneration and likely mechanical restriction resulting from infection. The care team opted for implantation of a third prosthesis—the self-expanding Acurate neo2—after preprocedural CT imaging and 3D modelling confirmed feasibility and sufficient coronary clearance (see figures 1, 5, and 7). The third valve was intentionally positioned high during implantation. Placement at the level of the existing Evolut valve leaflets enabled fixation of prior leaflets against the stent frame, thereby averting further reductions in the effective orifice area associated with inward folding of leaflets. Additionally, this technique minimized creation of a neo-skirt beneath the coronary ostia, helping preserve coronary flow and enabling future coronary access. Preprocedural CT evaluation shaped this approach and was crucial in securing acceptable haemodynamic performance in the triple-stented configuration. Despite the procedural intricacy inherent in a triple transcatheter valve setup, the strategy proved both technically achievable and replicable with comprehensive imaging and predefined implantation protocols.

The Acurate neo2 valve was chosen due to its supra-annular leaflet configuration, comparatively low frame profile, and advantageous haemodynamic characteristics suited for constricted anatomies. Its structural features facilitate commissural alignment, an important factor in this case to ensure potential access to coronary arteries in the future.

A major technical issue with TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV interventions is the possibility of compromised coronary access caused by multiple overlapping stent structures. Despite efforts to achieve optimal commissural alignment, the accumulation of metallic mesh can create obstacles for subsequent coronary procedures. This underscores the importance of thorough procedural planning for patients with existing coronary disease or those who may require coronary access later on. Preprocedural computed tomography allowed determination of the landing area, assessment of stent frame interaction, and estimation of the distance between the valve and coronary ostia, although comprehensive three-dimensional expansion analysis was not performed with specialized software.

The resolution of the transvalvular gradient following implantation of the third valve likely resulted from a combination of precise commissural alignment, successful post-dilation, and partial mechanical expansion of the pre-existing Evolut frame, enhancing leaflet mobility and improving central hemodynamics. Although deliberate stent frame fracture was not executed, the use of high-pressure balloon post-dilation probably contributed to further radial opening of the Evolut frame, optimizing valve performance.

The procedure was carried out under institutional approval for the off-label use of the Acurate neo2 device. The decision-making process included discussions between the patient, his family, and review in a multidisciplinary heart team conference comprising interventional cardiologists, cardiac surgeons, imaging experts, and anaesthesiologists.

If the current prosthesis were to fail prematurely, therapeutic options would be severely restricted. Surgical removal would present significant technical challenges and high risk. New percutaneous approaches such as bioprosthetic valve fracture or leaflet laceration—including techniques like bioprosthetic or native aortic scallop intentional laceration to avert iatrogenic coronary artery obstruction (BASILICA)—could be contemplated, although their practicality and safety in a triple stent scenario remain indeterminate.
