Title: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

Evgeny Vladimirovich Merkulov, Goar Kimovna Arutyunyan, Andrey Sergeevich Tereshchenko, Dmitriy V. Pevzner, Iiliya Tarasovich Zyuryaev, Mariya Alekseevna Terenicheva, Olga Nickolaevna Ganeeva, Alexey Vladimirovich Knigin

Abstract:

Summary: Due to the variety of technical difficulties inherent in transcatheter valve-in-transcatheter valve interventions, therapeutic approaches must be individualized for each patient. We describe the case of a male patient in his late 70s with a complex history comprising two previous transcatheter aortic valve implantations (TAVI) and an episode of infective endocarditis, who subsequently experienced significant prosthetic aortic valve degeneration requiring a third transcatheter procedure. The patient was initially managed with a Lotus valve in 2014, underwent a TAV-in-TAV using an Evolut device in 2022, and then developed severe restenosis secondary to endocarditis-related degeneration. In 2024, a third transcatheter valve (Acurate neo2) was deployed utilizing the TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV approach. As the use of TAVI expands and patient survival improves, the frequency of such complex multi-valve interventions is likely to rise, emphasizing the importance of advanced procedural planning, optimized imaging, and comprehensive long-term follow-up. At one year post-procedure, the patient is symptom-free with intact prosthetic valve performance. This report demonstrates the practicality of a third TAVI after successfully treated prosthetic valve endocarditis, planned with CT imaging and followed for one year—a scenario infrequently detailed in existing literature.

Background:

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) is now a recognized therapeutic approach for individuals with severe aortic stenosis, regardless of their surgical risk category. With the expanding population of TAVI recipients and enhancements in long-term survival, clinicians are seeing a higher incidence of structural valve degeneration and the associated necessity for subsequent procedures.

In this context, performing a transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV) procedure has become an established approach for treating failed transcatheter valves. Nevertheless, these procedures entail unique anatomical and technical difficulties, such as an elevated risk of coronary artery blockage and the potential for a diminished effective orifice area, which may lead to significant prosthesis-patient mismatch. These issues become even more pronounced with successive reinterventions, like transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV), where the presence of multiple superimposed valve stent frames poses additional considerations.

Data on repeat transcatheter valve interventions are sparse, especially for individuals with prior prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). In these challenging cases, comprehensive imaging before the procedure, tailored risk evaluation, and collaborative multidisciplinary planning are crucial to assess the appropriateness and enhance the results of the intervention.

This case demonstrates the use of CT-guided procedural planning and targeted transcatheter intervention in a patient with a history of multiple TAVI procedures and previously managed PVE, underscoring important factors for the long-term care of structural valve pathology.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his late seventies was hospitalized at the Academician E.I. Chazov National Medical Research Center, reporting shortness of breath, a spastic feeling on the left side of his chest provoked by physical activity (specifically while walking 200–300 m), along with dizziness and general fatigue.

His past medical background included arterial hypertension, significant three-vessel coronary artery disease, and a series of percutaneous coronary interventions: right coronary artery in 2009; left anterior descending (LAD) artery in 2010; a repeat LAD procedure in 2012; and circumflex artery intervention in 2014. Other comorbidities comprised third-degree atrioventricular block necessitating pacemaker implantation in 2014, type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and recurring gastrointestinal hemorrhages since 2022.

In 2014, the patient received a 27 mm Lotus valve (Boston Scientific) via transcatheter approach because of worsening aortic valve degeneration and critical stenosis. By December 2021, there was an escalation of exertional dyspnoea, with echocardiography indicating significant stenosis of the bioprosthetic valve. In early 2022, the mean transvalvular gradient rose to 83 mm Hg, and the ejection fraction (EF) dropped further to 42%, down from 54% noted in December.

In May 2022, the patient received a TAV-in-TAV procedure with placement of a 23 mm Evolut valve (Medtronic). The intervention was complicated by the onset of a pulsatile hematoma and pseudoaneurysm in the femoral region, necessitating open surgical vascular repair.

After undergoing the Evolut TAV-in-TAV procedure, the patient’s left ventricular ejection fraction increased from 42% to 54%, reflecting a positive initial haemodynamic outcome. Heart failure management was continued according to established guidelines, comprising beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, and diuretics as appropriate.

Three months later, in September 2022, the patient was readmitted due to ongoing fever and exacerbation of heart failure signs. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) revealed significant restenosis of the aortic valve prosthesis, with a mean gradient measured at 35 mm Hg. Blood cultures identified Enterococcus faecalis, leading to a diagnosis of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). The patient underwent a complete regimen of pathogen-directed antimicrobial therapy targeting E. faecalis PVE, which included 6 weeks of intravenous ampicillin-based antibiotics, subsequently transitioning to oral therapy as advised by infectious disease consultants. By the time of the third TAVI procedure, the antimicrobial regimen was concluded, and no chronic suppressive antibiotics were prescribed. Comprehensive evaluation, including serial blood cultures, transoesophageal echocardiography, and multislice computed tomography (MSCT), confirmed that the infection had resolved, with no indications of persistent endocarditis.

Following the conclusion of antibiotic treatment and some improvement in symptoms, assessment in January 2024 showed deteriorating function of the prosthetic valve, characterized by severe aortic stenosis and moderate to severe regurgitation. The measured mean gradient was 49 mm Hg, and the left ventricular ejection fraction had improved to 66%.

A plan was established to undertake a repeat intervention. Upon admission in June 2024, transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) identified ongoing moderate-to-severe (grade 2–3) intraprosthetic aortic regurgitation, a mean transvalvular gradient of 49 mm Hg, and a significantly diminished aortic valve area (AVA) measuring 0.5 cm², findings that indicated severe dysfunction of the prosthetic valve. The AVA was determined via the continuity equation, utilizing measurements from the left ventricular outflow tract and velocity-time integrals obtained from Doppler imaging. Imaging revealed two superimposed bioprosthetic valves.

Multislice computed tomography (MSCT) demonstrated that the Evolut valve prosthesis had a minimal lumen diameter of 13.7 mm, indicative of significant haemodynamic impairment and informing the approach for a planned third transcatheter valve procedure (see figure 1). The preprocedural MSCT assessment was utilized to determine anatomical suitability and evaluate the potential for coronary artery obstruction. This review encompassed measurements of the height of the coronary ostia, dimensions of the sinus of Valsalva, valve-to-coronary distances, and the interface between the structural frames of previously implanted transcatheter valves. Special focus was given to the spatial relationship between the expected neo-skirt of the valve and the coronary ostia, as well as the possibility of high implantation to maintain coronary perfusion and ensure access for any future coronary interventions. These anatomical insights influenced the choice of valve, the target implantation depth, and overall procedural planning, enabling the intervention to proceed without the need for coronary protection measures.

Transoesophageal echocardiography was performed prior to the reintervention and confirmed the absence of active endocarditis or paravalvular complications.

Treatment:

Considering the patient’s intricate anatomy involving three stents, history of infective endocarditis, and high risk associated with surgery, the third TAVI was scheduled using CT guidance and a strategy-focused approach, prioritizing safeguarding coronary perfusion, achieving proper commissural alignment, and maximizing the remaining effective orifice area.

The procedure was performed under local anaesthesia using left radial and left femoral arterial access. Aortography with a pigtail catheter confirmed severe aortic regurgitation (figure 2).

A Safari guidewire was advanced into the left ventricle through access obtained from the left femoral artery. The aortic valve underwent balloon pre-dilatation using a 22/40 mm catheter while the patient was paced at 180 beats per minute for rapid ventricular pacing. Concurrent aortography demonstrated that coronary perfusion was not compromised (figure 3).

An Acurate neo2 self-expanding bioprosthetic valve (size S) was placed in the aortic position. Preprocedural CT modeling was used to align the device with the leaflets of the Evolut valve, aiming to optimize the effective orifice area and minimize the likelihood of patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM).

A 20/40 mm balloon was utilized for post-dilation while rapid pacing was maintained to ensure optimal valve deployment. Subsequent aortography verified appropriate valve placement, sustained coronary blood flow, and no evidence of either perivalvular or transvalvular regurgitation (see figure 4).

During valve implantation, attention was paid to commissural alignment to maintain coronary access and minimize the complexity of subsequent interventions, using guidance from pre-procedural CT scans and fluoroscopic reference points.

Outcome and follow-up:

Assessment of procedural success and function of the prosthetic valve was performed based on the Valve Academic Research Consortium-3 guidelines, incorporating analysis of haemodynamic parameters, detection of any persistent regurgitation, and identification of complications associated with the procedure.

A transthoracic echocardiogram obtained immediately post-procedure on 7 June 2024 verified proper placement of the third transcatheter valve. The prosthetic leaflets demonstrated full mobility and opening, with no aortic regurgitation observed (grade 0). Despite this, haemodynamic assessment revealed markedly elevated transvalvular gradients, with a mean gradient measured at 67 mm Hg. The aortic valve area was calculated at 0.9 cm² (with an indexed area of 0.4 cm²/m²), and the Doppler velocity index was 0.24, findings that are indicative of severe prosthesis–patient mismatch.

At one month post-implantation, transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated significant progress: the mean gradient had decreased to 27 mm Hg and the aortic valve area (AVA) had expanded to 1.1 cm². Evidence of left ventricular remodeling was present, reflected by decreased chamber volumes. No residual regurgitation was identified, and valve function persisted in the range characteristic of moderate stenosis.

At 3.5 months follow-up, the patient continued to display no symptoms and demonstrated satisfactory exercise capacity. There was no evidence of heart failure or return of infection. The prosthetic device continued to function well, with haemodynamic parameters showing persistent improvement.

One year following implantation in June 2025, the patient continued to exhibit clinical stability and maintained an active lifestyle, without experiencing dyspnea or manifestations of heart failure. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed a mean gradient of 26 mm Hg, an aortic valve area of 1.1 cm², and a left ventricular ejection fraction of 65%. There were no valve-associated complications, regurgitation, or indications of recurrent endocarditis.

These findings confirm the durability of the result and suggest a sustained functional benefit of the TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV intervention in this high-risk patient.

Discussion:

This case highlights the decision-making process and technical challenges of performing a third TAVI in a patient with prior TAV-in-TAV procedures and a history of PVE.

Although TAVI is now a standard approach for treating aortic stenosis, there is ongoing debate about its appropriateness in individuals with a history of endocarditis. The 2025 guidelines from the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) for valvular heart disease and infective endocarditis recommend surgical valve replacement as the preferred option in cases of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE), especially when heart failure or persistent infection is present. Nevertheless, the guidelines highlight that care should be tailored to each patient, considering the extent of infection control, surgical risk profile, and anatomical suitability.

TAVI was initially selected in 2014 due to the patient’s elevated surgical risk and significant comorbidities, which rendered traditional surgical valve replacement unsuitable at that point. When the patient later experienced structural valve degeneration, a repeat transcatheter approach was implemented in 2022 to re-establish valve function while minimizing the hazards associated with open surgery. After effective management of PVE and with no evidence of ongoing infection, the plan for a third transcatheter procedure in 2024 was guided by continued high surgical risk, challenging anatomical features resulting from existing valve frames, and pre-procedural CT imaging that confirmed the feasibility of a technique designed to maintain coronary patency and provide satisfactory haemodynamic outcomes.

In this patient, failure of the prosthetic valve was probably due to multiple contributing factors. Although the episode of infective endocarditis may have led to leaflet damage resulting in impaired mobility, ongoing structural deterioration of the transcatheter valve prosthesis was also a major factor. Notably, after infection resolution and the absence of a need for extended suppressive antibiotics, the approach to managing prosthetic valve dysfunction mirrored that used in patients without current or persistent endocarditis. Consequently, procedural planning focused mainly on anatomical suitability, the degree of haemodynamic impairment, and overall surgical risk, rather than relying solely on the prior occurrence of infection.

In this instance, the patient had finished a course of antimicrobial treatment and displayed neither clinical nor radiologic evidence of ongoing infection. Comprehensive preprocedural assessment was conducted, including TEE and MSCT, both of which confirmed no presence of vegetations or paravalvular pathology. Additionally, the patient's EuroSCORE II was calculated at 27.36%, with notable comorbid conditions such as COPD, diabetes mellitus, and a history of gastrointestinal bleeding. The individual also had two previously implanted transcatheter prosthetic valves that were deeply incorporated into the aortic wall, factors that collectively made conventional surgery exceedingly high-risk and technically challenging. Due to the extensive anchoring of the prior valves, any surgical approach would probably have required complete replacement of both the aortic root and the ascending aorta.

Numerous published case reports have described TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV interventions,37 underscoring different procedural and anatomical difficulties encountered. This report contributes additional data to this small collection, notably presenting a scenario involving valve deterioration following endocarditis, with sustained valve performance observed at one-year follow-up.

This case highlights the increasing significance of long-term management approaches for structural valve disease. Each subsequent transcatheter intervention adds complexity in aspects such as overlapping device frames, aligning commissures, ensuring coronary access, and maintaining sufficient effective orifice area. Careful planning for future procedures and early conversations regarding possible treatment paths are essential, especially during the initial intervention, and are particularly important for younger individuals or those at elevated risk.

In this case, leaflet fibrosis resulting from previous degeneration, combined with likely mechanical restriction after infection, contributed to the patient's structural deterioration. The team decided to implant a third valve, specifically a self-expanding Acurate neo2 prosthesis. This choice was informed by preprocedural CT scans and 3D modeling, which confirmed procedural feasibility and sufficient clearance of the coronary arteries (see figures 1, 5, and 7). The decision to position the third transcatheter valve at a higher level was intentional. Deploying the device at the height of the Evolut valve’s leaflets facilitated apposition of the existing leaflets against the stent frame, helping to avoid further narrowing of the effective orifice area due to leaflet infolding. Furthermore, this implantation technique reduced the risk of creating a neo-skirt beneath the coronary ostia, thereby supporting ongoing coronary perfusion and maintaining access for potential future interventions. Preprocedural CT evaluation guided this approach, which proved critical in attaining favorable hemodynamics within the context of three overlapping stented valves. Although placing a third transcatheter valve presented technical challenges, the procedure was both feasible and repeatable when following thorough imaging assessments and a carefully planned implantation protocol.

The Acurate neo2 valve was chosen due to its supra-annular leaflet placement, comparatively short frame height, and advantageous haemodynamic properties in patients with restricted anatomical dimensions. Additionally, its structure facilitates commissural alignment—an important factor here to maintain potential access to the coronaries in the future.

One major technical challenge associated with TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV interventions is the potential for compromised coronary access resulting from several layers of overlapping stent frames. While efforts were made to achieve commissural alignment, the increased mesh density could still complicate any subsequent coronary procedures. This underscores the importance of thorough procedural preparation in individuals with existing coronary disease or in cases where future coronary access is anticipated. Preprocedural computed tomography was useful for assessing the appropriate landing area, analyzing frame interactions, and estimating valve-to-coronary separations, though comprehensive three-dimensional expansion assessment using specialized software was not conducted.

The resolution of the transvalvular gradient following the third valve implantation is likely due to improved commissural alignment, successful post-dilation, and partial mechanical widening of the existing Evolut frame, which led to enhanced leaflet mobility and more central blood flow. Although deliberate frame fracture was not carried out, post-dilation using a high-pressure balloon probably promoted additional radial expansion of the Evolut frame, resulting in better valve performance.

The intervention took place with institutional authorization for the off-label application of the Acurate neo2 prosthesis. A collaborative approach was taken, engaging both the patient and his family in decision-making, and the case underwent assessment during a multidisciplinary heart team conference attended by interventional cardiologists, cardiac surgeons, imaging experts, and anesthesiologists.

If the existing prosthesis were to fail earlier than expected, therapeutic alternatives would be very restricted. Removal of the device surgically would continue to pose substantial technical challenges and significant risk. Newer catheter-based interventions, including bioprosthetic valve fracture or intentional leaflet laceration procedures such as bioprosthetic or native aortic scallop intentional laceration to prevent iatrogenic coronary artery obstruction (BASILICA), could be contemplated. However, the practicality and safety of these approaches in cases involving three stented layers have not been clearly established.
