Title: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

Evgeny Vladimirovich Merkulov, Goar Kimovna Arutyunyan, Andrey Sergeevich Tereshchenko, Dmitriy V Pevzner, Iiliya Tarasovich Zyuryaev, Mariya Alekseevna Terenicheva, Olga Nickolaevna Ganeeva, Alexey Vladimirovich Knigin

Abstract:

Summary: Due to the various technical complexities inherent to transcatheter valve-in-transcatheter valve procedures, the therapeutic approach must be customized for each patient. We describe a man in his late 70s with a complicated medical background, notable for two previous transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) procedures and an episode of infective endocarditis, who subsequently experienced significant aortic prosthesis deterioration necessitating a third transcatheter intervention. His initial management involved implantation of a Lotus valve in 2014, followed in 2022 by TAV-in-TAV placement with an Evolut prosthesis, after which degeneration from endocarditis resulted in severe restenosis. In 2024, an additional (third) prosthetic valve (Acurate neo2) was delivered via a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedure. As TAVI indications broaden and patient survival extends, complex procedures involving multiple prostheses are likely to occur more frequently, underscoring the importance of enhanced procedural planning, advanced imaging methods, and ongoing long-term monitoring. At one year post-procedure, the patient continues to be asymptomatic with stable prosthetic function. This report demonstrates the viability of a third TAVI following treated prosthetic valve endocarditis, facilitated by CT-guided planning and one-year follow-up—an approach rarely documented to date.

Background:

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) is now a recognized therapeutic approach for individuals with severe aortic stenosis, regardless of their surgical risk classification. With a rising number of TAVI procedures being performed and enhancements in long-term patient survival, clinicians are seeing a higher incidence of structural valve degeneration and subsequent requirements for repeat interventions.

Within this context, performing transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV) procedures has become an accepted approach to address malfunctioning transcatheter valves. Nonetheless, these interventions present unique anatomical and procedural complexities, including heightened risks such as coronary artery obstruction and diminished effective orifice area, which can lead to significant prosthesis-patient mismatch. These difficulties become even more pronounced with repeated procedures—such as transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV)—where the presence of multiple, superimposed valve frames must be meticulously evaluated.

There is a scarcity of data on repeated transcatheter valve procedures, especially among individuals with prior prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). In these challenging cases, comprehensive imaging before the procedure, personalized risk evaluation, and collaborative decision-making across multiple specialties are crucial to assess procedure suitability and enhance clinical results.

This case demonstrates the importance of CT-guided procedural planning and a strategic approach to transcatheter intervention in a patient with a history of multiple TAVI procedures and managed prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE), emphasizing crucial factors in the long-term care of structural valve pathology.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his late 70s was hospitalized at the Academician E.I. Chazov National Medical Research Center due to complaints of shortness of breath, a spasmodic feeling on the left side of his chest associated with physical activity (noted while walking 200–300 m), as well as dizziness and fatigue.

His past medical history included arterial hypertension, triple-vessel coronary artery disease, and several percutaneous coronary interventions: one in 2009 targeting the right coronary artery, another in 2010 for the left anterior descending (LAD) artery, a repeat LAD procedure in 2012, and a circumflex artery intervention in 2014. Further comorbidities comprised third-degree atrioventricular block necessitating pacemaker placement in 2014, type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), as well as recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding episodes beginning in 2022.

In 2014, as a result of ongoing aortic valve deterioration accompanied by critical stenosis, the patient received a 27 mm Lotus valve (Boston Scientific) via transcatheter procedure. By December 2021, there was notable worsening of exertional dyspnoea, and echocardiography demonstrated findings indicative of severe stenosis of the bioprosthetic valve. Early in 2022, the mean transvalvular gradient had risen to 83 mm Hg, while ejection fraction (EF) had further decreased to 42%, compared to 54% in December.

In May 2022, the patient received a transcatheter aortic valve-in-valve procedure using a 23 mm Evolut valve (Medtronic). The intervention was complicated by the onset of a pulsatile hematoma and pseudoaneurysm at the femoral site, necessitating open surgical vascular repair.

After the Evolut TAV-in-TAV procedure, the patient's left ventricular ejection fraction increased from 42% to 54%, reflecting an early positive haemodynamic effect. Heart failure management included guideline-recommended medications such as beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, and diuretics as appropriate.

Three months later, in September 2022, the patient was readmitted to the hospital due to ongoing fever and aggravated symptoms of heart failure. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) revealed severe restenosis of the aortic valve prosthesis, with a mean transvalvular gradient of 35 mm Hg. Blood cultures identified Enterococcus faecalis, leading to a diagnosis of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). The patient completed a comprehensive course of pathogen-specific antimicrobial therapy for E. faecalis PVE, consisting of 6 weeks of intravenous ampicillin-based treatment, succeeded by oral therapy as directed by the infectious disease team. At the point of the third TAVI intervention, the antimicrobial regimen had concluded, and there was no ongoing long-term suppressive antibiotic therapy. Clinical evaluation, repeated blood cultures, transoesophageal echocardiography, and multislice CT (MSCT) all demonstrated that the infection had resolved, without signs indicative of active endocarditis.

Following the conclusion of antimicrobial treatment and some improvement in symptoms, a follow-up evaluation in January 2024 showed deterioration in prosthetic valve performance, characterized by severe aortic stenosis and moderate-to-severe regurgitation. The measured mean gradient was 49 mm Hg, and the left ventricular ejection fraction had returned to 66%.

A plan for reintervention was established. Upon her admission in June 2024, transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) demonstrated ongoing grade 2–3 intraprosthetic aortic regurgitation, a mean transvalvular gradient of 49 mm Hg, and a significantly diminished aortic valve area (AVA) measuring 0.5 cm², findings indicative of severe dysfunction of the prosthetic valve. The AVA determination utilized the continuity equation, employing left ventricular outflow tract measurements and Doppler velocity-time integrals. Imaging revealed the presence of two superimposed bioprosthetic valves.

MSCT demonstrated that the Evolut prosthesis had a minimal lumen diameter of 13.7 mm, indicating significant haemodynamic impairment and informing the strategy for performing a third transcatheter valve procedure (see figure 1). Prior to the intervention, MSCT was utilized to determine the anatomical suitability of the procedure and to evaluate the potential for coronary artery obstruction. The assessment involved measuring the coronary ostial height, sinus of Valsalva dimensions, valve-to-coronary distances, and examining how the frames of the previously placed transcatheter valves interacted. Special emphasis was placed on the predicted relationship between the neo-skirt and the coronary ostia, as well as the potential for high implantation to maintain coronary perfusion and ensure future coronary access. These evaluations influenced the choice of valve, depth of implantation, and procedural approach, enabling the procedure to be conducted without the need for coronary protection.

Transoesophageal echocardiography was performed prior to the reintervention and confirmed the absence of active endocarditis or paravalvular complications.

Treatment:

Due to the patient's intricate anatomy involving three stents, history of infective endocarditis, and excessively high surgical risk, a third TAVI was scheduled employing a CT-based, strategically tailored approach focused on safeguarding the coronary arteries, ensuring commissural alignment, and maximizing the remaining effective orifice area.

The procedure was performed under local anaesthesia using left radial and left femoral arterial access. Aortography with a pigtail catheter confirmed severe aortic regurgitation (figure 2).

A Safari guidewire was advanced into the left ventricle through the left femoral route. The aortic valve underwent balloon pre-dilatation using a 22/40 mm catheter while rapid ventricular pacing at 180 beats per minute was applied. Concurrent aortography demonstrated that coronary perfusion was not compromised (figure 3).

An Acurate neo2 self-expanding bioprosthetic valve, size S, was deployed in the aortic position. Alignment of the device with the leaflets of the Evolut valve was guided by preprocedural CT modeling, aiming to optimize the effective orifice area and minimize the likelihood of patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM).

A 20/40 mm balloon was utilized for post-dilation under rapid pacing to achieve optimal expansion of the valve. Subsequent aortography demonstrated the valve was appropriately positioned, coronary flow was maintained, and there was no evidence of perivalvular or transvalvular regurgitation (figure 4).

Commissural positioning was considered during valve implantation to maintain coronary accessibility and minimize future procedural challenges, using pre-procedural CT scans and fluoroscopic reference points for guidance.

Outcome and follow-up:

Assessment of procedural success and prosthetic valve functionality was conducted based on the Valve Academic Research Consortium-3 standards, which encompass analysis of haemodynamic parameters, detection of any remaining regurgitation, and identification of complications associated with the procedure.

A transthoracic echocardiogram conducted directly following the procedure on 7 June 2024 verified correct placement of the third transcatheter valve. The leaflets displayed normal mobility and fully opened, with no signs of aortic regurgitation (grade 0). Nevertheless, haemodynamic assessment revealed notable transvalvular gradients, with a mean gradient measuring 67 mm Hg. The aortic valve area was calculated at 0.9 cm² (indexed AVA 0.4 cm²/m²), and the Doppler velocity index (DVI) measured 0.24—findings indicative of severe prosthesis–patient mismatch (PPM).

At 1 month post-implantation, transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated significant progress, revealing a mean gradient of 27 mm Hg and an aortic valve area expanded to 1.1 cm². Remodeling of the left ventricle was evident, indicated by decreased chamber volumes. No residual regurgitation was detected, and the prosthetic valve continued to function within the moderate stenosis range.

At 3.5 months post-procedure, the patient continued to exhibit no symptoms and demonstrated satisfactory exercise capacity. There was no evidence of heart failure or repeat infection. The prosthetic device continued to function effectively, with persistent enhancement of haemodynamic measurements.

One year following the implantation in June 2025, the patient continued to be clinically stable and physically active, reporting no dyspnea or indications of decompensation. Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated a mean gradient of 26 mm Hg, an aortic valve area of 1.1 cm², and an ejection fraction of 65%. There were no signs of valve-related complications, regurgitation, or recurrent endocarditis detected.

These findings confirm the durability of the result and suggest a sustained functional benefit of the TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV intervention in this high-risk patient.

Discussion:

This case highlights the decision-making process and technical challenges of performing a third TAVI in a patient with prior TAV-in-TAV procedures and a history of PVE.

Although TAVI is now a recognized therapeutic option for aortic stenosis, its application in individuals with a history of endocarditis remains debated. The 2025 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines on valvular heart disease and infective endocarditis advise surgical valve replacement as the preferred approach in PVE, especially when there is associated heart failure or persistent infection. Nonetheless, the guidelines highlight the importance of tailoring treatment to each patient, considering factors such as infection management, operative risk, and the practicality of the anatomy.

TAVI was initially selected as the preferred approach in 2014 due to the patient’s elevated operative risk and presence of several comorbid conditions, rendering traditional surgical valve replacement less suitable at that point. With later onset of structural valve degeneration, a repeat transcatheter procedure was carried out in 2022 to re-establish valve function while circumventing the hazards associated with open-heart surgery. After PVE was effectively managed and with no signs of ongoing infection, a third transcatheter procedure was undertaken in 2024. This decision reflected the patient’s unacceptably high surgical risk, challenging anatomy owing to prior valve prostheses, and CT evidence supporting the feasibility of a strategy that would both maintain coronary patency and deliver satisfactory haemodynamic results.

The cause of prosthetic valve dysfunction in this case was probably due to multiple factors. The prior episode of infective endocarditis might have caused leaflet damage leading to impaired motion, but ongoing structural deterioration of the transcatheter valves was also a major contributing factor. Notably, after the infection had been deemed cleared and the patient did not need continued suppressive antibiotic treatment, the approach to treating the failing prosthesis was the same as for individuals without current or persistent endocarditis. As a result, procedural choices were mainly determined by the patient’s anatomy, the severity of the haemodynamic compromise, and the operative risk, rather than by the previous infection alone.

In this instance, the patient had completed a course of antibiotics and displayed neither clinical evidence nor imaging findings indicative of ongoing infection. Comprehensive preprocedural assessments were performed, including TEE and MSCT, both of which verified the lack of vegetations or paravalvular pathology. Additionally, the patient’s EuroSCORE II was 27.36%, reflecting considerable operative risk, with relevant comorbid conditions such as COPD, diabetes mellitus, and a history of gastrointestinal bleeding. The patient also had two previously placed transcatheter valve prostheses, both with substantial stent incorporation into the aortic wall. These factors together made conventional surgery exceedingly risky and technically challenging. Due to the secure fixation of the previous valves, an operative approach would likely have required complete replacement of the aortic root and ascending aorta.

Multiple reports documenting TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV interventions have been described in the literature,37 illustrating a range of anatomical and technical difficulties encountered. This case contributes further to the scarce evidence available, presenting a distinctive scenario involving degeneration after endocarditis and maintained valve function observed at one-year follow-up.

This case highlights the increasing significance of comprehensive, lifelong management approaches for structural valve disease. With every subsequent transcatheter intervention, factors including frame overlap, commissural alignment, coronary access, and remaining effective orifice area add further complexity. Proactive, long-range planning and early discussions regarding future procedural options are essential, especially during the initial intervention, and are particularly important in younger individuals or those considered high risk.

In this case, the patient's structural valve deterioration included leaflet fibrosis due to prior degenerative changes and likely mechanical limitation after infection. The clinicians elected to implant a third valve—a self-expanding Acurate neo2 device—after preprocedural CT scans and 3D modeling confirmed both procedural feasibility and sufficient clearance from the coronary arteries (see figures 1, 5, and 7). A high-positioned deployment of the third transcatheter valve was purposefully selected. Placing the device at the level of the existing Evolut valve leaflets enabled the immobilization of the previous leaflets against the stent frame, effectively avoiding additional reduction in the effective orifice area that can result from leaflet infolding. Moreover, this technique reduced the likelihood of neo-skirt development below the coronary ostia, thereby helping to maintain coronary perfusion and access for potential future interventions. The procedural plan was based on preprocedural CT evaluation and played a crucial role in attaining a satisfactory haemodynamic outcome despite the presence of three superimposed transcatheter valves. Although the configuration was complex, utilizing a carefully planned implantation protocol informed by advanced preprocedural imaging allowed for successful and reproducible results in triple valve-in-valve scenarios.

The Acurate neo2 was chosen due to its supra-annular leaflet placement, comparatively short frame, and optimal haemodynamics in restricted anatomies. The device's architecture facilitates commissural alignment, an important factor in this case to maintain the potential for future coronary interventions.

A major technical issue with TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV interventions is the potential for compromised coronary access as a result of several overlapping stent structures. Despite efforts to achieve commissural alignment, the aggregate stent material can still hinder subsequent coronary procedures. This underscores the importance of thorough procedural strategy, especially in individuals with established coronary pathology or in whom maintaining access is anticipated to be crucial. Preprocedural CT scanning was utilized to assess the implantation site, analyze stent frame relationships, and estimate distances between the valve and coronary ostia; however, comprehensive three-dimensional expansion modeling with specialized software was not conducted.

The resolution of the transvalvular gradient following the third device implantation is likely due to improved commissural alignment, successful post-dilation, and partial mechanical expansion of the pre-existing Evolut valve frame, leading to better leaflet mobility and more favorable central flow patterns. Although a deliberate frame fracture was not carried out, post-dilation with a high-pressure balloon probably aided additional radial enlargement of the Evolut frame, thereby augmenting valve performance.

The intervention was carried out with institutional authorization for the off-label application of the Acurate neo2 valve. The patient and his family actively participated in the shared decision-making process, and the case underwent discussion by a multidisciplinary heart team comprising interventional cardiologists, cardiac surgeons, imaging experts, and anesthesiologists.

If the existing prosthesis were to fail ahead of schedule, available therapeutic alternatives would be highly restricted. Surgical removal would continue to pose considerable technical challenges and carry substantial risk. Innovative catheter-based interventions, including bioprosthetic valve fracture or intentional leaflet laceration procedures—such as bioprosthetic or native aortic scallop intentional laceration to prevent iatrogenic coronary artery obstruction (BASILICA)—could be potential options, although it remains unclear whether these approaches are practical and safe in the context of triple-stented anatomy.
