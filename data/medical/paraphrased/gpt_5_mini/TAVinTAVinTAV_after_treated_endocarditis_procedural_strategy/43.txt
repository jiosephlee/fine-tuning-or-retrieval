Title: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

Evgeny Vladimirovich Merkulov, Goar Kimovna Arutyunyan, Andrey Sergeevich Tereshchenko, Dmitriy V Pevzner, Iiliya Tarasovich Zyuryaev, Mariya Alekseevna Terenicheva, Olga Nickolaevna Ganeeva, Alexey Vladimirovich Knigin

Abstract:

Summary: Owing to the wide range of technical complexities inherent in transcatheter valve-in-transcatheter valve procedures, therapeutic approaches must be customized for each patient. Here, we report the case of a man in his late 70s with a challenging medical background, which included two prior transcatheter aortic valve implantations (TAVI) and an episode of infective endocarditis, who subsequently experienced significant deterioration of the aortic prosthesis necessitating a third transcatheter procedure. The patient’s initial treatment involved placement of a Lotus valve in 2014, followed by TAV-in-TAV with an Evolut prosthesis in 2022. Subsequent degeneration related to endocarditis resulted in severe restenosis. In 2024, an Acurate neo2 valve was implanted as a third prosthesis using a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV approach. As TAVI indications continue to broaden and patient life expectancy increases, the incidence of complex interventions involving multiple prostheses is likely to rise, underscoring the importance of advanced procedural planning, optimized imaging methods, and long-term monitoring. At the 1-year follow-up, the patient remains free of symptoms and valve function is preserved. This report demonstrates that a third TAVI procedure after managed prosthetic valve endocarditis is achievable, aided by CT-based planning and continued follow-up, an outcome seldom described in published literature.

Background:

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) is now widely recognized as an effective therapy for patients with severe aortic stenosis, encompassing those with varied levels of surgical risk. With a growing patient population undergoing TAVI and improving longevity, clinicians are increasingly encountering structural valve deterioration and subsequent requirements for reintervention.

In response, transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV) procedures have become a feasible approach for addressing malfunctioning transcatheter valve prostheses. Nonetheless, these procedures present unique anatomical and procedural complexities, such as heightened risk for coronary artery obstruction and a diminished effective orifice area that may lead to significant prosthesis-patient mismatch. These issues are even more pronounced during successive reinterventions, like transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV), in which clinicians must account for the effects of multiple superimposed valve frames.

Data supporting repeated transcatheter valve interventions is scarce, especially in cases involving previous prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). In these intricate situations, thorough preprocedural imaging, tailored risk evaluation, and collaborative multidisciplinary deliberation are vital to assessing procedural feasibility and achieving optimal outcomes.

The present case underscores the importance of computed tomography-based planning and a strategic approach to transcatheter intervention in a patient with several prior TAVI implants and previously managed PVE, emphasizing critical elements for long-term management of structural valve pathology.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his late seventies was hospitalized at the Academician E.I. Chazov National Medical Research Center due to the onset of dyspnoea, a spastic discomfort localized to the left side of the chest during physical activity (noted when walking 200–300 meters), as well as dizziness and general fatigue.

His past medical history included arterial hypertension, coronary artery disease affecting three vessels, and several percutaneous coronary interventions: right coronary artery in 2009, left anterior descending (LAD) artery in 2010, repeat LAD procedure in 2012, and intervention on the circumflex artery in 2014. He also had third-degree atrioventricular block necessitating pacemaker placement (2014), type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and repeated gastrointestinal bleeding episodes beginning in 2022.

In 2014, progressive aortic valve deterioration with severe stenosis led to a transcatheter placement of a 27 mm Lotus valve (Boston Scientific). By December 2021, the patient’s exertional dyspnoea intensified, and echocardiography demonstrated pronounced bioprosthetic valve stenosis. In early 2022, the mean transvalvular gradient rose to 83 mm Hg, with ejection fraction decreasing further to 42% (compared to 54% measured in December).

The patient underwent TAV-in-TAV replacement with a 23 mm Evolut valve (Medtronic) in May 2022. This intervention was complicated by a pulsatile femoral haematoma and the formation of a pseudoaneurysm, which required surgical vascular repair.

Subsequent to the Evolut TAV-in-TAV procedure, left ventricular ejection fraction improved from 42% to 54%, suggesting a positive early haemodynamic effect. The patient was treated with heart failure therapies per established guidelines, including beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, and diuretics as clinically appropriate.

Three months later, in September 2022, the patient was readmitted due to ongoing fever and aggravation of heart failure symptoms. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) demonstrated pronounced restenosis of the aortic valve prosthesis, with a mean gradient measured at 35 mm Hg. Blood culture results identified Enterococcus faecalis, leading to a diagnosis of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). Management consisted of a complete regimen of targeted antibiotics for E. faecalis PVE, specifically including 6 weeks of intravenous ampicillin-based therapy and subsequent oral treatment as advised by infectious disease specialists. By the time the third TAVI procedure took place, the antimicrobial course had concluded, and no maintenance suppressive antibiotic therapy was being administered. Clinical evaluation, serial blood cultures, transoesophageal echocardiography, and multislice CT (MSCT) confirmed infection resolution, with no signs indicating persistent endocarditis.

Upon completion of antimicrobial treatment and partial symptomatic improvement, follow-up in January 2024 indicated deterioration in prosthetic valve performance, presenting with severe aortic stenosis and moderate-to-severe regurgitation. The mean gradient had increased to 49 mm Hg, and left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) had recovered to 66%.

A decision was made to pursue further intervention. During admission in June 2024, TTE revealed ongoing grade 2–3 intraprosthetic aortic regurgitation, a mean transvalvular gradient of 49 mm Hg, and a notably diminished aortic valve area (AVA) of 0.5 cm², consistent with advanced prosthetic valve malfunction. The AVA was determined via the continuity equation, utilizing left ventricular outflow tract parameters and Doppler-derived velocity-time integrals. Two overlapping bioprosthetic valves were noted on imaging.

MSCT showed a minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm within the Evolut prosthesis, confirming significant hemodynamic compromise and informing procedural planning for a third transcatheter valve implantation (see figure 1). Preprocedural MSCT was applied to evaluate anatomical suitability and assess the risk of coronary artery obstruction. This included review of coronary ostial height, measurements of the sinus of Valsalva, valve-to-coronary distance, and the structural interplay of previously implanted transcatheter valve frames. Emphasis was placed on the relationship between the prospective neo-skirt and coronary ostia, as well as the potential for high implantation to maintain coronary perfusion and allow future coronary access. The information obtained guided both valve selection and implantation depth, enabling completion of the procedure without need for coronary protection.

Transoesophageal echocardiography was conducted prior to this reintervention and confirmed no evidence of active endocarditis or paravalvular issues.

Treatment:

Due to the patient’s intricate triple-stented anatomy, history of infective endocarditis, and unsuitable surgical risk profile, the third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) was designed as a computed tomography (CT)-guided, strategic procedure prioritizing coronary protection, commissural orientation, and maximizing the remaining effective orifice area.

The intervention took place under local anesthesia, employing left radial and left femoral arterial access. Aortography performed with a pigtail catheter demonstrated marked aortic regurgitation (figure 2).

Through left femoral access, a Safari guidewire was advanced into the left ventricle. The aortic valve was pre-dilated using a 22/40 mm balloon catheter while rapid ventricular pacing was set at 180 beats per minute. Concurrent aortography verified that coronary flow was not impeded (figure 3).

An Acurate neo2 self-expanding bioprosthetic valve (size S) was deployed at the aortic site. Utilizing preprocedural CT modeling, the device was positioned to align with the leaflet plane of the previously implanted Evolut valve, thereby optimizing effective orifice area and minimizing the chance of patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM).

Further balloon dilation was carried out using a 20/40 mm balloon during rapid pacing to ensure optimal expansion of the implanted valve. The concluding aortography established accurate placement, maintained coronary perfusion, and absence of both perivalvular and transvalvular regurgitation (figure 4).

Commissural orientation was considered during valve placement to safeguard coronary access and simplify future procedures, guided by CT imaging acquired before the intervention as well as fluoroscopic reference points.

Outcome and follow-up:

Procedural outcomes and prosthetic valve function were evaluated following the Valve Academic Research Consortium-3 guidelines, which encompassed assessment of haemodynamic status, detection of any residual regurgitation, and identification of procedure-related adverse events.

A transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) conducted immediately post-procedure on 7 June 2024 demonstrated appropriate placement of the third transcatheter valve. The valve cusps exhibited normal mobility and full opening, with no detectable aortic regurgitation (grade 0). Nevertheless, haemodynamic measurements revealed elevated transvalvular gradients, with a mean gradient of 67 mm Hg. The aortic valve area (AVA) measured 0.9 cm² (indexed AVA 0.4 cm²/m²), and the Doppler velocity index (DVI) was 0.24, indicative of severe patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM).

At the 1-month follow-up, TTE findings showed significant improvement; the mean gradient had decreased to 27 mm Hg and the AVA increased to 1.1 cm². There was evidence of left ventricular remodelling, reflected by reduced chamber volumes. No residual regurgitation was present, and valve performance remained consistent within a moderately stenotic classification.

By 3.5 months post-procedure, the patient continued to be asymptomatic and exhibited satisfactory exercise capacity. There were no clinical signs of heart failure or recurrence of infection. The valve prosthesis continued to function well, with persistent enhancement of haemodynamic values.

At 1 year after implantation (June 2025), the patient remained stable and physically active, without shortness of breath or features of heart failure. TTE recorded a mean gradient of 26 mm Hg, AVA of 1.1 cm², and an ejection fraction (EF) of 65%. There was an absence of valve-related adverse events, regurgitation, and recurrent endocarditis.

The collective data illustrate sustained procedural durability and continued functional improvement with the TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV approach in this high-risk individual.

Discussion:

This case illustrates the clinical reasoning and procedural complexities associated with conducting a third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) in a patient with previous TAV-in-TAV interventions and a history of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE).

Although TAVI has been widely adopted as a therapy for aortic stenosis, its application in individuals with prior endocarditis remains debated. The 2025 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines for valvular heart disease and infective endocarditis recommend surgical valve replacement for PVE, especially when complicated by heart failure or uncontrolled infection. Nevertheless, these guidelines underscore the necessity for tailored management, considering factors such as infection eradication, operative risk, and anatomical suitability.

The decision to use TAVI as the primary approach in 2014 stemmed from the patient's elevated surgical risk and multiple comorbidities, rendering traditional surgical valve replacement unsuitable at that point. Upon subsequent development of structural valve degeneration, a repeat transcatheter procedure was elected in 2022 to restore valve function and avoid the hazards associated with open-heart surgery. After PVE was effectively treated and with no signs of ongoing infection, proceeding with a third transcatheter procedure in 2024 was predicated on extremely high surgical risk, intricate anatomical issues arising from the previously implanted valve frames, and CT evidence supporting the possibility of maintaining coronary perfusion and acceptable haemodynamics.

The underlying causes of prosthetic valve malfunction in this patient appeared to be multiple. Although the occurrence of infective endocarditis may have resulted in leaflet damage with consequent mechanical limitation, ongoing deterioration of the transcatheter valve structures also contributed considerably. Notably, after resolution of the infection and in the absence of a need for long-term suppressive antibiotics, the approach to treating prosthetic valve dysfunction mirrored that used in cases without current endocarditis. Thus, procedural choices were primarily determined by anatomical suitability, severity of haemodynamic impairment, and operative risk, rather than solely the prior infectious history.

In this scenario, the patient had completed the prescribed antimicrobial regimen, lacked any clinical or imaging evidence of persistent infection, and underwent comprehensive preprocedural assessment, including TEE and MSCT, which both excluded the presence of vegetations or paravalvular issues. Additionally, the patient had a EuroSCORE II of 27.36%, along with substantial comorbid conditions—COPD, diabetes, and a history of gastrointestinal hemorrhage—and two previously placed transcatheter prostheses with extensive integration of stents into the aortic wall. These factors made open surgical repair particularly precarious and technically demanding. Due to the deep anchoring of the prior devices, surgical management would probably have required replacement of the entire aortic root and ascending aorta.

Multiple case reports documenting TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV interventions have been described,37 illustrating a range of anatomical and procedural difficulties. The present case contributes further to this scarce literature, distinguished by the context of degeneration following endocarditis and sustained valve functionality at the 1-year mark.

This case illustrates the increasing relevance of comprehensive lifetime management plans for structural valve disease. Each subsequent transcatheter procedure introduces greater challenges, such as frame stacking, commissural alignment, maintaining coronary accessibility, and preserving residual effective orifice area. Establishing long-term strategies and initiating early discussions regarding possible procedural pathways are essential, especially at the initial intervention stage—most notably in younger individuals or those at elevated risk.

The structural deterioration encountered in this patient was characterized by leaflet fibrosis due to prior valve degeneration, alongside potential mechanical restriction secondary to infection. The care team elected to implant a third valve—a self-expanding Acurate neo2 prosthesis—based on findings from preprocedural CT and three-dimensional modeling, which confirmed procedural viability and sufficient coronary clearance (figures 1, 5, and 7). Placement of the third transcatheter valve was intentionally performed at a high position. Deploying the prosthesis at the level of the existing Evolut valve leaflets secured the prior leaflets to the stent frame, effectively preventing further narrowing of the effective orifice area from leaflet folding inwards. Moreover, this method reduced the development of a neo-skirt beneath the coronary ostia, thereby supporting continued coronary blood flow and facilitating future coronary access. The decision for this implantation strategy was informed by preprocedural CT findings and played a crucial role in attaining satisfactory haemodynamics within a triple-stented configuration. Although a triple-layered transcatheter valve anatomy presents significant technical challenges, this method was achievable and reproducible, provided thorough preprocedural imaging and a clearly defined implantation plan.

The Acurate neo2 valve was chosen due to its supra-annular leaflet orientation, modest frame height, and advantageous haemodynamic profile in constricted anatomies. Its structure facilitates commissural alignment, which was especially important in this case to maintain potential for coronary access in the future.

One major technical issue with TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV interventions is the heightened risk of compromised coronary access caused by overlapping stent frames. Although efforts were made to achieve commissural alignment, the accumulation of mesh layers could still impede future coronary procedures. This underscores the importance of thorough procedural planning in patients with existing coronary pathology or for whom maintaining access is critical. Preprocedural computed tomography (CT) assisted in determining the landing zone, assessing interactions between device frames, and estimating the distances from the valve to coronary ostia, although dedicated three-dimensional expansion software was not utilized.

The elimination of the transvalvular gradient following the third device implantation is attributable to optimized commissural orientation, effective post-dilatation, and partial mechanical expansion of the earlier Evolut frame, thereby enhancing leaflet mobility and central flow. Although frame fracture was not purposefully performed, the application of high-pressure balloon post-dilation likely resulted in further radial enlargement of the Evolut frame, thus improving prosthesis performance.

This intervention was undertaken with institutional authorization for off-label use of the Acurate neo2 device. The decision process involved collaboration with the patient and his family, and the case received multidisciplinary evaluation through a heart team conference comprising interventional cardiologists, cardiac surgeons, imaging experts, and anesthesiologists.

If early structural failure of the current prosthesis occurs, therapeutic possibilities would be severely limited. Surgical removal would continue to pose significant technical challenges and high risk. Novel catheter-based approaches, such as bioprosthetic valve fracture or leaflet laceration (for instance, bioprosthetic or native aortic scallop intentional laceration to prevent iatrogenic coronary artery obstruction (BASILICA)), may be contemplated; however, their practicality and safety in the context of triple-stented anatomies remain unclear.
