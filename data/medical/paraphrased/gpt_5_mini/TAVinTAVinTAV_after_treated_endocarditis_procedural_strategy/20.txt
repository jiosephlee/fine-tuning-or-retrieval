Title: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

Evgeny Vladimirovich Merkulov, Goar Kimovna Arutyunyan, Andrey Sergeevich Tereshchenko, Dmitriy V Pevzner, Iiliya Tarasovich Zyuryaev, Mariya Alekseevna Terenicheva, Olga Nickolaevna Ganeeva, Alexey Vladimirovich Knigin

Abstract:

Summary: Due to the various technical challenges inherent in transcatheter valve-in-transcatheter valve procedures, management must be individualized for each patient scenario. We describe the case of a male patient in his late seventies with a complex background, including two previous transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) procedures as well as a history of infective endocarditis, who subsequently experienced significant degeneration of the aortic valve prosthesis necessitating a third transcatheter procedure. The patient initially received a Lotus valve in 2014, followed by a TAV-in-TAV using an Evolut device in 2022, which was complicated by endocarditis and subsequent prosthetic deterioration with marked restenosis. In 2024, an Acurate neo2 valve was positioned using a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV approach. As TAVI use broadens and survival among patients increases, such complex, multi-valve interventions are likely to be encountered more frequently, underscoring the importance of enhanced procedural preparation, advanced imaging methodologies, and diligent long-term monitoring. At 1-year post-procedure, the patient remains symptom-free with sustained valve performance. This report demonstrates that performing a third TAVI following successfully managed prosthetic valve endocarditis is feasible, facilitated by computed tomography-guided planning and 1-year observation, a scenario that is seldom documented in existing literature.

Background:

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) is now widely recognized as a treatment option for individuals with severe aortic stenosis, regardless of their surgical risk category. With an increasing volume of TAVI procedures and improved long-term outcomes, clinicians are encountering structural valve deterioration and the subsequent need for additional interventions more frequently.

In these circumstances, performing a transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV) is a practical approach for addressing failed transcatheter prostheses. Nevertheless, this type of reintervention presents unique anatomical and technical complexities, including a heightened possibility of obstructing the coronary arteries and diminished effective orifice area, which may lead to pronounced prosthesis-patient mismatch. Such difficulties are further compounded in cases of repeated reinterventions, as seen with transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV), where the presence of multiple superimposed valve frames requires careful management.

Data on consecutive transcatheter valve procedures is still scarce, especially among patients who have experienced prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). In these intricate cases, comprehensive preprocedural imaging, personalized evaluation of risks, and collaborative, multidisciplinary planning are critical to assess procedure viability and optimize outcomes.

This case demonstrates the application of CT-based procedural planning and deliberate transcatheter intervention for a patient who underwent multiple TAVI procedures and had previously treated PVE, underscoring important factors for the ongoing management of structural valve disease throughout a patient’s lifetime.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his late seventies was hospitalized at the Academician E.I. Chazov National Medical Research Center after experiencing shortness of breath, a constricting feeling in the left side of the chest triggered by physical activity (walking 200–300 m), dizziness, and fatigue.

His past medical history included arterial hypertension, triple-vessel coronary artery disease, and multiple percutaneous coronary interventions: right coronary artery in 2009, left anterior descending (LAD) artery in 2010, repeat LAD in 2012, and circumflex artery in 2014. Other significant conditions were third-degree atrioventricular block necessitating pacemaker placement (2014), type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding episodes since 2022.

In 2014, as his aortic valve disease worsened to severe stenosis, he received a transcatheter implantation of a 27 mm Lotus valve (Boston Scientific). By December 2021, he reported increased exertional breathlessness, and echocardiography revealed marked stenosis of the bioprosthetic valve. In early 2022, the mean gradient measured 83 mm Hg, and left ventricular ejection fraction had decreased to 42% from 54% in December.

In May 2022, a TAV-in-TAV procedure was performed with placement of a 23 mm Evolut valve (Medtronic). This intervention was complicated by the formation of a pulsatile femoral haematoma and pseudoaneurysm, requiring surgical vascular repair.

Post-Evolut TAV-in-TAV, his left ventricular ejection fraction improved to 54% from 42%, showing an early beneficial haemodynamic effect. He was subsequently treated according to heart failure guidelines with beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, and diuretics where indicated.

Three months later, in September 2022, the patient was admitted once more due to ongoing fever and exacerbation of heart failure manifestations. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) demonstrated marked restenosis of the aortic valve prosthesis, with a mean gradient of 35 mm Hg. Blood cultures yielded Enterococcus faecalis, prompting a diagnosis of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). The patient underwent a complete regimen of pathogen-directed antimicrobial treatment for E. faecalis-associated PVE, consisting of 6 weeks of intravenous ampicillin-based therapy, followed by oral therapy as advised by the infectious disease team. When the third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) was scheduled, the antimicrobial course had concluded and no ongoing suppressive antibiotic regimen was employed. Clinical review, serial blood cultures, transoesophageal echocardiography, and multislice computed tomography (MSCT) confirmed eradication of infection with no signs suggestive of active endocarditis.

After completion of antibiotics and partial improvement of symptoms, follow-up evaluation in January 2024 identified deteriorating function of the prosthetic valve, manifesting as severe aortic stenosis with moderate-to-severe regurgitation. The mean transvalvular gradient was 49 mm Hg and the left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) had normalized to 66%.

A decision was taken to perform another intervention. Upon admission in June 2024, TTE revealed persistent grade 2–3 intraprosthetic aortic regurgitation, a mean gradient across the valve of 49 mm Hg, and a severely reduced aortic valve area (AVA) measured at 0.5 cm², indicating advanced prosthetic valve dysfunction. Calculation of the AVA employed the continuity equation, incorporating measurements from the left ventricular outflow tract and Doppler-derived velocity-time integrals. Imaging identified two overlapping bioprosthetic valves.

MSCT showed a minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm within the Evolut prosthesis, confirming significant haemodynamic impairment and informing preprocedural planning for a third transcatheter valve placement (figure 1). Preprocedural MSCT was utilized to evaluate anatomical suitability and estimate the potential risk of coronary obstruction. This assessment included measurements of coronary ostial height, sinus of Valsalva size, valve-to-coronary distance, and analysis of the interaction between the frames of previously implanted transcatheter valves. Special attention was devoted to the expected position of the neo-skirt relative to the coronary ostia and the feasibility of a high implantation strategy to maintain coronary flow and ensure access for future coronary procedures. These anatomical insights informed the selection of valve type, determination of implantation depth, and the procedural approach, thereby allowing the intervention to proceed without the need for coronary protection.

Transoesophageal echocardiography was carried out prior to the repeat intervention and verified the absence of ongoing endocarditis or paravalvular pathology.

Treatment:

Due to the patient's intricate anatomy with three prior stents, a history of infective endocarditis, and an unacceptably high risk for open surgery, a third TAVI was scheduled as a computed tomography-directed intervention, prioritizing strategies for coronary protection, commissural alignment, and optimization of the remaining effective orifice area.

The intervention took place under local anesthesia via left radial and left femoral arterial access points. Severe aortic regurgitation was verified using a pigtail catheter during aortography (figure 2).

A Safari guidewire was advanced into the left ventricle through the left femoral route. The aortic valve underwent balloon pre-dilation using a 22/40 mm catheter, performed under rapid ventricular pacing at 180 beats per minute. Concurrent aortography demonstrated unobstructed coronary flow (figure 3).

A self-expanding bioprosthetic valve (Acurate neo2, size S) was deployed at the aortic valve site. Its positioning was coordinated with the leaflets of the preexisting Evolut valve, as planned from preprocedural CT imaging, with the goal of maximizing the effective orifice area and minimizing the likelihood of patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM).

A 20/40 mm balloon was used for post-dilation under continued rapid pacing to enhance expansion of the new valve. Final aortographic evaluation indicated correct device placement, maintained coronary perfusion, and absence of perivalvular or transvalvular regurgitation (figure 4).

During valve implantation, commissural alignment was meticulously planned to facilitate future coronary interventions and decrease the complexity of possible subsequent procedures, utilizing findings from preprocedural CT scans and intraoperative fluoroscopy.

Outcome and follow-up:

The outcome of the procedure and function of the prosthetic valve were evaluated in accordance with the Valve Academic Research Consortium-3 guidelines, encompassing assessment of haemodynamic parameters, detection of residual regurgitation, and identification of procedural complications.

A transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) performed immediately post-procedure on 7 June 2024 demonstrated that the third transcatheter valve had been successfully implanted. The valve’s leaflets were freely moving, opening completely, and no aortic regurgitation was observed (grade 0). Nevertheless, haemodynamic measurements revealed substantial transvalvular gradients, with a mean gradient of 67 mm Hg. The aortic valve area (AVA) was determined to be 0.9 cm² (indexed AVA 0.4 cm²/m²), while the Doppler velocity index (DVI) measured 0.24, which is indicative of severe prosthesis–patient mismatch (PPM).

One month following the procedure, TTE displayed considerable improvement: the mean gradient had fallen to 27 mm Hg, and AVA had risen to 1.1 cm². Evidence of left ventricular remodelling was noted, including reduced chamber volumes. No residual regurgitation was present, and the prosthetic valve remained stable, functioning within a range consistent with moderate stenosis.

At 3.5 months after valve implantation, the patient continued to be asymptomatic and demonstrated robust exercise capacity. There were no manifestations of heart failure or recurrent infection. The prosthetic valve sustained effective function, and improvements in haemodynamic variables persisted.

By the 1-year mark post implantation (June 2025), the patient continued to be clinically stable, physically active, and without dyspnoea or signs of decompensated heart failure. TTE findings included a mean gradient of 26 mm Hg, AVA of 1.1 cm², and an ejection fraction (EF) of 65%. No prosthesis-related complications, valve regurgitation, or recurrence of endocarditis were detected.

Collectively, these results demonstrate lasting efficacy and indicate ongoing clinical benefit from the TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedure in this high-risk patient.

Discussion:

This case illustrates the complexities and considerations involved in performing a third TAVI for a patient previously treated with two TAV-in-TAV interventions and a history of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE).

Although TAVI is a well-established option for managing aortic stenosis, its application in individuals with past endocarditis remains debated. The 2025 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines for valvular heart disease and infective endocarditis recommend surgical valve replacement for PVE, particularly when complicated by heart failure or persistent infection. Nonetheless, the guidelines underscore the need for tailored management, assessing factors such as infection control, operative risk, and anatomical suitability.

The initial decision to pursue TAVI in 2014 was prompted by the patient’s elevated surgical risk and extensive comorbidities, rendering surgical valve replacement less viable at the time. With the onset of structural valve degeneration, a repeat transcatheter approach was adopted in 2022 to restore valve function while avoiding the hazards associated with surgery. After successful PVE therapy and with no evidence of active infection, the rationale for proceeding with a third transcatheter procedure in 2024 centered on substantial surgical risk, intricate anatomical challenges due to prior valve frames, and CT-confirmed suitability for maintaining coronary patency and achieving favorable haemodynamics.

The failure of the prosthetic valve in this individual was presumably due to several contributing factors. Although the episode of infective endocarditis may have led to leaflet damage and subsequent mechanical limitation, ongoing structural deterioration of the transcatheter prostheses was also a major element. Notably, once infection was deemed resolved and there was no indication for extended suppressive antibiotic therapy, the approach to managing prosthetic valve dysfunction became consistent with that of patients who do not have active endocarditis. Consequently, procedural decisions were influenced chiefly by anatomical suitability, haemodynamic compromise, and operative risk, rather than solely by prior infectious history.

For this patient, antimicrobial treatment had been completed, there were no signs—clinical or radiographic—of current infection, and a comprehensive assessment prior to intervention had been performed, including TEE and MSCT, which both confirmed no vegetations or paravalvular abnormalities. Additionally, the patient’s EuroSCORE II was calculated at 27.36%, with substantial comorbid conditions (COPD, diabetes, previous gastrointestinal hemorrhage) as well as two prior transcatheter valves featuring significant stent integration into the aortic wall, all of which made surgical repair extremely risky and technically challenging. Due to the extensive fixation of existing prosthetic valves, an operative solution would likely have involved complete replacement of the aortic root and ascending aorta.

Multiple case reports detailing TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedures have been documented,37 demonstrating distinct anatomical and technical challenges encountered. Our report further expands this scarce literature, presenting a case distinguished by degeneration secondary to endocarditis and stable valve performance after 1 year.

This case highlights the increasing necessity for lifelong management approaches in structural valve pathology. Each subsequent transcatheter intervention introduces greater complexity, including issues like layered stent frames, commissural orientation, coronary access, and preservation of residual effective orifice area. It is crucial to engage in comprehensive planning and proactive discussions regarding procedural pathways from the initial treatment, especially in patients who are younger or at higher risk.

The patient exhibited structural deterioration characterized by leaflet fibrosis, attributed both to prior degeneration and mechanical constraint following infective episodes. The clinical team elected to implant a third valve—a self-expanding Acurate neo2 prosthesis—using information from preprocedural CT scans and 3D modelling, which confirmed both technical viability and sufficient clearance of the coronaries (figures 1, 5 and 7). The team intentionally positioned the third valve at a high level. Deploying the device at the height of the Evolut valve leaflets served to secure the existing leaflets against the stent frame, which helped to prevent further decrease of the effective orifice area from leaflet invagination. Additionally, this high placement reduced the creation of a neo-skirt beneath the coronary ostia, supporting maintained coronary perfusion and future coronary accessibility. The approach was directed by preprocedural CT evaluation and was essential to obtain satisfactory haemodynamics in the context of a triple-stented structure. Despite the inherent complexity, implantation of three transcatheter valves was achievable and could be reliably performed when guided by extensive preprocedural imaging and an established deployment strategy.

The Acurate neo2 valve was chosen for this procedure owing to its supra-annular leaflet position, modest frame height, and advantageous haemodynamic characteristics in narrow anatomies. Its structural features facilitate commissural alignment, which was particularly important in this scenario to ensure potential coronary re-access in the future.

A major technical challenge in TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV interventions is the likelihood of compromised coronary access, resulting from the presence of multiple stent layers. Despite attempts at commissural alignment, the accumulation of meshwork can still hinder future coronary procedures. This underscores the necessity for thorough procedural preparation, especially for patients with existing coronary disease or those who may require coronary interventions subsequently. Preprocedural CT imaging enabled the determination of landing zone dimensions, assessment of frame interactions, and estimation of the distances between the valve and coronary arteries, although comprehensive three-dimensional expansion analysis via specialised software was not conducted.

Resolution of the transvalvular gradient following the third valve implantation was likely due to optimized commissural positioning, successful post-dilatation, and partial mechanical expansion of the previously placed Evolut frame, which collectively improved leaflet function and promoted central flow. While deliberate fracture of the frame was not pursued, high-pressure balloon post-dilatation probably aided in further radial expansion of the Evolut device, contributing to enhanced valve operation.

The intervention was conducted under institutional authorization for compassionate use of the Acurate neo2 device beyond its approved indications. The decision process included shared discussions with the patient and his relatives, and the case underwent multidisciplinary heart team evaluation, involving interventional cardiologists, cardiac surgeons, imaging experts, and anaesthesiologists.

In the event of premature dysfunction of the newly implanted prosthesis, therapeutic choices would be exceedingly restricted. Surgical removal would present considerable technical difficulty and significant risk. Novel transcatheter approaches, such as bioprosthetic valve fracture or intentional leaflet laceration—for instance, bioprosthetic or native aortic scallop intentional laceration to prevent iatrogenic coronary artery obstruction (BASILICA)—might be contemplated, though the applicability and safety of these methods in triple-stented settings remain largely undefined.
