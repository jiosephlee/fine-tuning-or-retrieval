Title: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

Evgeny Vladimirovich Merkulov, Goar Kimovna Arutyunyan, Andrey Sergeevich Tereshchenko, Dmitriy V Pevzner, Iiliya Tarasovich Zyuryaev, Mariya Alekseevna Terenicheva, Olga Nickolaevna Ganeeva, Alexey Vladimirovich Knigin

Abstract:

Summary: Owing to the varied technical challenges inherent in transcatheter valve-in-transcatheter valve procedures, treatment approaches should be individualized for each patient. This report details the case of a male in his late 70s with a complicated clinical background that includes two previous transcatheter aortic valve implantations (TAVI) and infective endocarditis, who later experienced severe deterioration of the aortic prosthesis necessitating a third transcatheter intervention. The initial procedure involved a Lotus valve implantation in 2014, followed by a TAV-in-TAV procedure with an Evolut device in 2022, after which valve degeneration and significant restenosis due to endocarditis were observed. In 2024, a third prosthetic device (Acurate neo2) was placed using a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV approach. As TAVI indications broaden and patient survival improves, the incidence of intricate multi-prosthesis cases is expected to rise, emphasizing the importance of enhanced procedural planning, imaging methodologies, and prolonged surveillance. At one year post-procedure, the patient remains symptom-free with stable valve performance. This case demonstrates the possibility of performing a third TAVI following management of prosthetic valve endocarditis, facilitated by CT-based planning and one-year follow-up, an approach rarely documented in current literature.

Background:

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) is now widely recognized as a standard therapy for individuals with severe aortic stenosis, irrespective of their surgical risk classification. With a rising number of TAVI procedures being performed and greater longevity among recipients, clinicians are more frequently encountering cases of structural valve degeneration, necessitating repeat interventions.

In response, the transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV) technique has developed as an effective approach for addressing degenerated transcatheter valves. Nevertheless, these procedures introduce distinct anatomical and procedural difficulties, such as an elevated risk of coronary artery obstruction and the potential for diminished effective orifice area leading to significant prosthesis-patient mismatch. These complexities become more pronounced with additional reinterventions, like transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV), where consideration of multiple superimposed valve stents is necessary.

Currently, data on successive transcatheter valve interventions are scarce, especially for individuals with a background of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). In such intricate scenarios, comprehensive preprocedural imaging, tailored risk analysis, and collaborative decision-making among specialists are crucial for assessing the appropriateness of the intervention and achieving optimal results.

The present case demonstrates the importance of CT-based planning and a strategic, team-based transcatheter approach in a patient who previously underwent multiple TAVI procedures and had managed PVE, emphasizing critical aspects for the lifelong care of structural valve pathology.

Case presentation:

A man in his late 70s was admitted to the Academician E.I. Chazov National Medical Research Center after reporting shortness of breath, a spastic feeling on the left side of his chest during exertion (such as walking 200–300 m), dizziness, and fatigue.

His past medical history included arterial hypertension, three-vessel coronary artery disease, and multiple percutaneous coronary interventions: in 2009 for the right coronary artery, in 2010 for the left anterior descending (LAD) artery, a repeat procedure in the LAD in 2012, and intervention in the circumflex artery in 2014. He also had comorbid third-degree atrioventricular block which led to pacemaker implantation in 2014, type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding since 2022.

In 2014, due to worsening aortic valve disease and severe stenosis, the patient underwent transcatheter placement of a 27 mm Lotus valve (Boston Scientific). By December 2021, his exertional dyspnoea had intensified, and echocardiogram showed significant bioprosthetic valve stenosis. By early 2022, the mean gradient had risen to 83 mm Hg, and the left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) had decreased from 54% in December to 42%.

In May 2022, he received a TAV-in-TAV procedure with insertion of a 23 mm Evolut valve (Medtronic). This intervention was complicated by the formation of a pulsatile femoral haematoma and pseudoaneurysm, necessitating open vascular repair.

After the Evolut TAV-in-TAV, the patient’s left ventricular EF rose from 42% to 54%, signifying an encouraging early haemodynamic outcome. Guideline-based management for heart failure was initiated, including beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, and diuretics as required.

Three months later, in September 2022, the patient was readmitted with ongoing fever and exacerbation of heart failure manifestations. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) demonstrated pronounced restenosis of the aortic valve prosthesis, yielding a mean gradient of 35 mm Hg. Blood cultures identified Enterococcus faecalis, leading to a diagnosis of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). The patient underwent a complete course of antimicrobial therapy tailored to E. faecalis PVE, consisting of 6 weeks of intravenous ampicillin-based treatment, succeeded by oral medication in line with infectious disease specialist advice. At the time of the subsequent transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI), antimicrobial therapy had concluded, and the patient was not on prolonged suppressive antibiotic treatment. Clinical evaluation, serial blood cultures, transoesophageal echocardiography, and multislice CT (MSCT) all confirmed eradication of the infection, showing no signs of active endocarditis.

Upon finishing the antimicrobial regimen and with partial improvement of symptoms, January 2024 follow-up documented declining function of the prosthetic valve with pronounced aortic stenosis and moderate-to-severe regurgitation. The mean transvalvular gradient reached 49 mm Hg, and left ventricular ejection fraction had improved to 66%.

A decision was reached to undertake further intervention. When the patient was admitted in June 2024, TTE revealed persistent grade 2–3 intraprosthetic aortic regurgitation, a mean gradient of 49 mm Hg across the valve, and a substantially diminished aortic valve area (AVA) of 0.5 cm², indicative of severe prosthetic valve malfunction. The AVA determination utilized the continuity equation, incorporating left ventricular outflow tract data and Doppler-based velocity-time integrals. Two overlapping bioprosthetic valves were observed.

MSCT identified a minimal lumen diameter of 13.7 mm within the Evolut prosthesis, corroborating significant haemodynamic impairment and informing the procedural plan for a third transcatheter valve placement (figure 1). Preprocedural MSCT assisted in evaluating anatomical suitability and risk of coronary artery obstruction, with assessment focusing on coronary ostial height, sinus of Valsalva size, valve-to-coronary distance, and the interplay of frames from previous valve implants. Detailed analysis considered the spatial relationship between the predicted neo-skirt and coronary ostia, alongside the possibility of a high implant position to secure coronary flow and permit future coronary access. These considerations shaped the choice of valve, implant position, and approach, allowing the procedure to proceed without coronary protection.

Transoesophageal echocardiography, conducted prior to reintervention, confirmed the absence of active endocarditis and ruled out paravalvular complications.

Treatment:

Owing to the patient’s intricate anatomy with three prior stents, history of infective endocarditis, and unsuitable surgical profile, a third TAVI was scheduled utilizing a CT-guided approach, focusing on coronary preservation, precise commissural positioning, and maximizing the remaining effective orifice area.

The intervention was conducted under local anesthesia, employing left radial and left femoral arterial access points. Aortography through a pigtail catheter demonstrated pronounced aortic regurgitation (figure 2).

A Safari guidewire was advanced into the left ventricle from the left femoral route. The aortic valve underwent balloon pre-dilation using a 22/40 mm catheter during rapid ventricular pacing at 180 beats per minute. Concurrent aortography verified unimpeded coronary flow (figure 3).

An Acurate neo2 self-expanding bioprosthetic valve (size S) was placed at the aortic site. Alignment of the device with the existing Evolut valve leaflets was achieved per CT-based preprocedural planning, aiming to optimize orifice area and minimize patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM).

The valve was subsequently post-dilated with a 20/40 mm balloon under continued rapid pacing to ensure optimal deployment. Follow-up aortography indicated proper valve placement, preserved coronary circulation, and absence of perivalvular or transvalvular regurgitation (figure 4).

Attention to commissural alignment during valve insertion was maintained, employing preprocedural CT scans and fluoroscopic guidance to retain coronary accessibility and simplify potential future interventions.

Outcome and follow-up:

Procedural efficacy and prosthetic valve performance were evaluated in accordance with the Valve Academic Research Consortium-3 guidelines, encompassing assessment of haemodynamic profile, presence of residual regurgitation, and procedure-associated complications.

Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) conducted immediately post-procedure on 7 June 2024 demonstrated successful implantation of the third transcatheter valve. The valve leaflets were well mobile and fully opening, with aortic regurgitation graded as 0. Nevertheless, haemodynamic data revealed markedly elevated transvalvular gradients; mean gradient measured at 67 mm Hg. The aortic valve area (AVA) was calculated to be 0.9 cm² (indexed AVA 0.4 cm²/m²), with Doppler velocity index (DVI) at 0.24, indicative of severe prosthesis–patient mismatch (PPM).

One month following valve placement, TTE revealed substantial improvement. The mean gradient decreased to 27 mm Hg and AVA increased to 1.1 cm². Evidence of left ventricular remodelling was present with reduced chamber volumes. No residual regurgitation was identified, and the prosthetic valve operated stably within a moderately stenotic range.

By 3.5 months post-implantation, the patient remained free of symptoms and demonstrated robust exercise tolerance. There were no manifestations of heart failure or recurrence of infection. The prosthetic valve sustained favorable function, with persistent enhancement of haemodynamic measures.

At the 1-year follow-up (June 2025), the patient continued to be clinically stable, active, and without dyspnoea or evidence of decompensation. TTE findings included a mean gradient of 26 mm Hg, AVA of 1.1 cm², and an ejection fraction of 65%. There were no prosthesis-associated complications, regurgitation, or recurrence of endocarditis observed.

Overall, these results demonstrate the durability of the intervention and imply sustained functional benefit from the TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV strategy in this high-risk individual.

Discussion:

This case illustrates both the complexities in decision-making and the procedural difficulties associated with performing a third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) in a patient with a history of previous TAV-in-TAV procedures and prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE).

Although TAVI is now a well-established therapy for aortic stenosis, its application in individuals with a prior history of endocarditis is still debated. The 2025 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines for valvular heart disease and infective endocarditis advocate for surgical valve replacement as the preferred approach in PVE, particularly when cases are complicated by heart failure or persistent infection. Nevertheless, the guidelines stress the importance of individualised management that takes into account infection control, the patient’s operative risk, and anatomical suitability for intervention.

In 2014, TAVI was initially chosen due to the patient’s elevated surgical risk and significant comorbid conditions, making traditional surgical valve replacement an unsuitable option. With subsequent onset of structural valve deterioration, a repeat transcatheter intervention was selected in 2022 to re-establish valve function and avoid the hazards associated with open surgery. After PVE was successfully managed and without evidence of ongoing infection, the decision in 2024 to undertake a third transcatheter procedure was informed by unacceptable surgical risk, the complexity posed by the presence of prior valve frames, and computed tomography demonstrating that maintaining coronary perfusion and achieving suitable haemodynamic outcomes via this strategy was feasible.

The underlying causes of prosthetic valve dysfunction in this patient appeared to be multiple. Although the episode of infective endocarditis may have resulted in damage to the valve leaflet and led to mechanical limitation, ongoing structural deterioration of the transcatheter prostheses was also a prominent contributing factor. Notably, after it was determined that the infection had resolved and continued antibiotic therapy was unnecessary, the approach to managing the prosthetic valve disorder followed the standard protocol used for patients without current or recurrent endocarditis. Thus, decisions regarding the procedure were based predominantly on anatomical suitability, the degree of haemodynamic impairment, and the patient's operative risk, rather than being influenced solely by the previous infection.

For this patient, antimicrobial treatment had been completed, with no evidence of persistent infection either clinically or on imaging, and an extensive preprocedural work-up—including TEE and MSCT—confirmed that there were no vegetations or paravalvular issues present. Additionally, the EuroSCORE II was calculated at 27.36%, and the patient had considerable comorbidities (including COPD, diabetes, and a history of gastrointestinal bleeding), together with two prior transcatheter prosthetic valves exhibiting substantial stent incorporation into the aortic wall. These factors collectively made conventional surgery exceptionally risky and technically challenging. Due to the significant anchoring of the previous prostheses, a surgical approach would likely require replacement of the entire aortic root and ascending aorta.

Multiple reports describing TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedures have been documented,37 illustrating a range of anatomical and technical difficulties. Our report further contributes to this sparse literature, particularly given the distinctive scenario of valve deterioration following endocarditis and maintained valve function at one year.

This case illustrates the increasing significance of lifelong management approaches for structural valve disorders. Each subsequent transcatheter intervention introduces added complexity regarding issues such as overlapping frames, aligning commissures, accessing the coronaries, and maintaining sufficient residual orifice area. Comprehensive planning and early conversations about procedural direction are essential—even from the initial intervention—especially in younger individuals or those at elevated risk.

In this patient, valve degeneration consisted of leaflet fibrosis secondary to earlier deterioration and likely mechanical restriction due to previous infection. The clinical team opted to implant a third valve—a self-expanding Acurate neo2 prosthesis—supported by preprocedural CT and three-dimensional modeling that confirmed its practicality and adequate clearance for the coronaries (see figures 1, 5, and 7). Purposeful high positioning of the third transcatheter valve was chosen as a procedural tactic. Placement of the device at the level of the Evolut valve leaflets enabled stabilization of the existing leaflets against the stent frame, which prevented additional reduction of the effective orifice area from leaflet folding inward. Moreover, this approach reduced the creation of a neo-skirt beneath the coronary openings and helped maintain coronary blood flow and ensure future access. The decision was informed by preprocedural CT evaluation and was crucial for obtaining satisfactory haemodynamic performance in an anatomy with three stented valves. Although the triple transcatheter valve scenario was intricate, the technique demonstrated technical feasibility and consistency when relying on detailed preprocedural imaging and a predetermined implantation plan.

The Acurate neo2 was chosen due to its supra-annular leaflet configuration, modest frame height, and advantageous haemodynamic profile, particularly in narrower anatomies. Its architecture facilitates commissural alignment, which was an important factor in this case to maintain possible future access to the coronary arteries.

One major technical challenge associated with TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedures is the potential compromise of coronary access caused by the superimposition of multiple stent frames. Even though commissural alignment was pursued, the increased mesh complexity might still hinder future coronary procedures. This underscores the importance of thorough procedural planning for patients with pre-existing coronary disease or for whom access may be required subsequently. Preprocedural CT allowed determination of the landing area, assessment of frame interaction, and estimation of the distance between the valve and coronaries; however, comprehensive three-dimensional expansion analysis with specialized software was not conducted.

Resolution of the transvalvular gradient following the third device implantation is likely attributable to the optimal commissural alignment, successful post-dilatation, and partial mechanical expansion of the previously placed Evolut frame, which improved leaflet movement and central flow. Although frame fracture was not deliberately performed, the application of high-pressure balloon post-dilatation probably enhanced the radial expansion of the Evolut, thereby further improving valve performance.

Institutional approval was obtained for the off-label utilization of the Acurate neo2 prosthesis during the procedure. The patient and his family participated in shared decision-making, and the case was discussed at a multidisciplinary heart team meeting consisting of interventional cardiologists, cardiac surgeons, imaging specialists, and anesthesiologists.

If this prosthesis fails unexpectedly, available therapies would be severely limited. Surgical explantation would be technically complex and entail significant risk. Novel catheter-based strategies such as bioprosthetic valve fracture or leaflet laceration—for instance, bioprosthetic or native aortic scallop intentional laceration to prevent iatrogenic coronary artery obstruction (BASILICA)—may be contemplated; nevertheless, their safety and efficacy in situations involving triple-stent layers remain unclear.
