Title: Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery

Rosanne St Bernard, Ishac Nazy, Theodore Earl Warkentin

Abstract:

Summary: An elderly male patient received heparin anticoagulation during cardiac surgery; afterwards, his platelet count progressively declined, reaching 50×10⁹/L by postoperative day 5 (POD5), which led to an evaluation for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibodies. Brain imaging identified a parietal stroke. A screening immunoassay for HIT antibodies returned a strongly positive result. The serotonin-release assay showed an atypical pattern, with marked serotonin release at 0, 0.1, and 0.3 U/mL heparin that was only partially inhibited by a monoclonal antibody blocking the platelet Fc receptor. The patient’s anticoagulation regimen was switched to argatroban in combination with high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin. Ongoing hypercoagulability necessitated transition to fondaparinux, after which the platelet count gradually recovered (155×10⁹/L by POD62) and neurological deficits resolved. Further laboratory analysis detected HIT antibodies with heparin-independent platelet-activating effects, consistent with autoimmune HIT (aHIT). In addition to severe thrombocytopenia (nadir platelet count, 13×10⁹/L), unique features of this aHIT case included an unusually early postoperative presentation (prior to POD5) likely attributable to preoperative heparin exposure, as well as an antibody profile with partial resistance to inhibition by Fc receptor blockade.

Background:

Unfractionated heparin (UFH) has served as the predominant anticoagulant administered intraoperatively throughout the approximately 70-year span of cardiac surgery involving cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB). This preference is attributed to several benefits, such as the suppression of haemostatic activation induced by artificial surfaces, clearance mechanisms independent of both renal and hepatic pathways, availability of intraoperative monitoring via activated clotting time, and affordability. Nevertheless, UFH carries a significant risk of heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT), an immunologically mediated complication. The incidence of HIT following cardiac surgery is estimated to range between 0.5% and 1.0%, influenced by the specific postoperative heparin regimen (UFH or low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH)) and its duration. This condition is particularly important clinically due to its strong predisposition toward thrombosis, with an approximate 15-fold increase in relative risk.

Traditionally, HIT has been understood as a condition marked by pronounced platelet activation via clustering of the platelet Fc receptor, which occurs due to immune complexes composed of heparin-dependent immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibodies targeting platelet factor 4 (PF4) when it is bound to the polyanion heparin. More recently, it has become apparent that certain patients exhibit atypical and sometimes severe manifestations of HIT caused by antibodies that can activate platelets even in the absence of heparin; this particular form has been referred to as ‘autoimmune HIT’ (aHIT) (for review). Here, we present a case of aHIT characterized by exceptionally marked and persistent thrombocytopenia, complicated further by thrombotic stroke, following cardiac surgery. This case reveals novel aspects of aHIT by illustrating an exceptionally severe serological presentation, including partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade. It also underscores the complexities involved in diagnosing early postoperative thrombocytopenia, as the patient’s notably rapid onset of aHIT was attributed to preoperative exposure to UFH. The patient ultimately achieved a good recovery.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his 70s, with a 2-year history of exertional shortness of breath and chronic atrial fibrillation (managed with apixaban 5 mg twice daily), was noted to have a previously unrecognized systolic murmur. He was referred for diagnostic cardiac catheterisation; however, his apixaban was not discontinued prior to the procedure. To accommodate this, the cardiologist opted for a transradial artery access and administered a reduced dose of periprocedural anticoagulation (2000 U of unfractionated heparin). Angiography revealed a 60% narrowing in the obtuse marginal artery, moderate severity aortic stenosis, and an enlarged ascending aorta. The patient was subsequently planned for cardiac surgery in one week.

Cardiac surgery was conducted utilizing cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), with anticoagulation maintained through administration of 45,000 U of unfractionated heparin (UFH). The procedures performed included replacement of the aortic valve with a #27 bioprosthesis, substitution of the ascending aorta using a #30 Gelweave graft, a single coronary artery bypass graft (CABG) to the obtuse marginal coronary artery employing a saphenous vein graft, and excision of the left atrial appendage. The operation progressed smoothly, with no significant hemorrhage or requirement for blood transfusion, and the patient was successfully weaned from CPB, maintaining stable haemodynamics.

The platelet count immediately before surgery was 210×10^9/L (reference range 150–400×10^9/L). On postoperative day 1 (POD1), this value dropped to 155×10^9/L, which constituted only a modest early reduction, as is often seen following cardiac surgery. Standard postoperative thromboprophylaxis with LMWH was initiated on POD1, with 5000 units of dalteparin administered subcutaneously, and the dose was increased to 7500 units on POD2, POD3, and POD4. However, the platelet count steadily decreased from POD1 through POD5, reaching a nadir of 50×10^9/L (figure 1). Clinically, the patient remained stable apart from the development of postoperative delirium. On POD5, dalteparin was discontinued, and prophylactic-dose fondaparinux (2.5 mg) was started, with the first dose administered at 18:00.

Despite the absence of focal neurological deficits, the worsening delirium and a further reduction in platelet count to 26×10^9/L on POD6 (confirmed with a repeat value of 24×10^9/L) led to a CT scan of the brain. Imaging demonstrated an acute infarction in the left parietal lobe, presenting as a hypodense region measuring 3.2 cm × 1.8 cm with cortical involvement.

Investigations:

On postoperative day 5, the complete blood count demonstrated a platelet count of 50×10^9/L and a haemoglobin level of 97 g/L (reference range 135–180 g/L), which matched the haemoglobin measurements from the previous three days, indicating anticipated postoperative blood loss rather than persistent hemorrhage. The white blood cell count was 7.8×10^9/L (reference range 4.0–11.0×10^9/L), with a differential within normal limits. Reticulocyte count was within normal parameters at 73×10^9/L (reference range 10–100×10^9/L). Haptoglobin was raised at 3.19 g/L (reference range 0.32–1.97 g/L), suggesting no evidence of active haemolysis. The serum vitamin B12 level normalized.

Coagulation panel revealed a normal prothrombin time with an international normalized ratio of 1.1 (reference range [RR]: 0.9–1.1), and a partial thromboplastin time (PTT) of 34 seconds (RR: 25–37 seconds), both within normal limits. Fibrinogen was found to be elevated at 6.59 g/L (RR: 2.00–3.93 g/L), reflecting a typical response in the immediate postoperative phase due to fibrinogen’s role as an acute phase reactant, which commonly results in marked elevation following surgery. On postoperative day 5 (POD5), a blood sample was forwarded to a reference laboratory for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibody analysis.

Other blood work included normal chemistry (urea, creatinine, alanine transaminase and bilirubin). Blood and urine cultures were drawn. A chest X-ray showed no major abnormalities.

Examination of the peripheral blood smear on postoperative day 7 verified thrombocytopenia, noting a lack of platelet aggregates, and no detection of red cell fragments (schistocytes) or ‘toxic’ changes in white blood cells. The initial screening test for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT)—a PF4/polyanion ELISA—demonstrated a markedly elevated result at 2.2 optical density (OD) units (reference range <0.4 OD units), whereas the definitive platelet activation assay—the serotonin-release assay (SRA)—produced an ‘indeterminate’ outcome (addressed further below).

Differential diagnosis:

Typical causes of significant thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery, such as cardiogenic shock, critical illness, and infection, were considered improbable based on both clinical presentation and laboratory data (hemodynamic parameters were stable; blood and urine cultures were negative). Uncommon causes, including post-transfusion purpura (since no blood products were administered), post-surgical thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (with haptoglobin within the normal range and no schistocytes initially observed), and drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia, were also deemed unlikely, as the decrease in platelet count occurred too soon in relation to the timing of newly introduced postoperative medications. The medications given exclusively during surgery (vancomycin, cefazolin, and tranexamic acid) and those administered in the postoperative period (furosemide, metoprolol, pantoprazole, and midodrine) were not temporally associated with the onset of thrombocytopenia.

While the decline in platelet count developed earlier than would be expected from the intraoperative UFH used during CPB, later investigation uncovered that UFH had also been given during preoperative cardiac catheterisation. Combined with the presence of thrombotic stroke and a strongly positive PF4/polyanion ELISA, HIT was considered the most likely diagnosis, presumed to have been induced by the UFH received during preoperative catheterisation. Additional information about the 'indeterminate' SRA was solicited from the McMaster Platelet Immunology Laboratory, which had performed the test.

The SRA findings underwent a thorough evaluation. In line with the presence of platelet-activating HIT antibodies, the patient's serum collected on postoperative day 5 triggered pronounced serotonin release at both 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL UFH (97% and 99%, respectively; reference range <20% release). Additionally, no serotonin release (0%) occurred at a supratherapeutic UFH concentration (100 U/mL); the lack of platelet activation at such elevated heparin levels is characteristic of HIT and signifies the inability to form PF4-immune complexes under these conditions. Nonetheless, two noteworthy aspects of the SRA emerged. First, the patient’s serum induced substantial serotonin release in the absence of UFH (96% release at 0 U/mL heparin; RR <20%), which is typical of autoimmune HIT (aHIT) but not seen in classic heparin-dependent HIT. Second, when an Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody (IV.3) was introduced, it did not fully suppress the serotonin release observed with the patient’s serum at 0.1 U/mL UFH: 76% serotonin release persisted in the presence of IV.3, instead of the expected reduction to under 20% with standard HIT antibodies. This led to the SRA result initially being labeled as ‘indeterminate’. The underlying reason was that the patient did have aHIT, but the HIT antibodies demonstrated exceptional reactivity, resulting in only partial inhibition with IV.3, rather than the complete suppression usually observed. As far as we are aware, this unusual SRA pattern has not been described before.

Treatment:

Fondaparinux at prophylactic doses was stopped, and therapy was initiated with argatroban, a direct thrombin inhibitor, at 0.75 mcg/kg/min, aiming for a PTT between 55 and 85 seconds. While fondaparinux at therapeutic doses is recognized as an effective off-label anticoagulant for HIT, the managing hematologist opted for argatroban due to its shorter half-life, providing a safer profile in the context of possible hemorrhage following recent stroke and profound thrombocytopenia. Additionally, high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin was administered: initially 100 g daily for two consecutive days, with a third 100 g dose added after platelet counts failed to rise after the first two days of IVIg therapy. Repeated measurements of fibrinogen and d-dimer were conducted to indirectly monitor the effectiveness of anticoagulation, as increasing fibrinogen and decreasing d-dimer values would support the therapeutic management of HIT-related hypercoagulable states.

During the initial therapeutic period, there was a steady decline in fibrinogen levels observed from postoperative day (POD) 6 to POD17, reaching a lowest point of 2.49 g/L. The d-dimer concentration peaked near POD12, and peripheral blood smears revealed the presence of red blood cell fragments (schistocytes) between POD9 and POD16. These three laboratory abnormalities were indicative of significant, advancing consumptive coagulopathy linked to HIT. Additionally, the patient's platelet count showed only a slight elevation following administration of high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IV Ig), rising from 13×10^9/L on POD7 to a maximum interim value of 54×10^9/L by POD14. This response represented a substantially smaller increase in platelet count than is typically observed after high-dose IV Ig in patients with autoimmune HIT (aHIT; for review).

On postoperative day 14, anticoagulation was transitioned from argatroban to fondaparinux at a therapeutic dose of 10 mg administered subcutaneously once daily. This modification took into account the simpler dosing regimen of fondaparinux, which requires once-daily subcutaneous injections as opposed to the PTT-dependent intravenous infusion needed for argatroban. Additionally, there was concern that argatroban may not have been sufficiently managing the hypercoagulable state associated with HIT, given the previously described decreasing fibrinogen, increasing d-dimer, and emergence of schistocytes observed during argatroban therapy. Because fondaparinux exerts its effect as an indirect, antithrombin (AT)-dependent inhibitor of factor Xa, 1000 units of AT concentrate were administered before starting fondaparinux, as the patient's AT level before the infusion was slightly decreased at 0.70 U/mL (reference range 0.80–1.40 U/mL).

Outcome and follow-up:

The platelet count returned to normal levels over time (155×10^9/L on POD62 and 202×10^9/L by POD87), and the patient achieved full neurological recovery. Fondaparinux was continued through POD65, after which apixaban was restarted at an oral dose of 5 mg twice daily for ongoing anticoagulation. Serial SRA assays remained strongly positive for several months, with subsequent tests displaying a more typical aHIT pattern (figure 2). The final blood sample, collected at POD124 after normalization of the platelet count, demonstrated findings compatible with primarily heparin-dependent HIT antibodies: there was 15% serotonin release in the absence of UFH and 100% release with UFH at a concentration of 0.3 U/mL.

Discussion:

The patient exhibited several unusual clinical, laboratory, and serologic findings, all of which were ultimately explained by a diagnosis of severe aHIT. Initially, his HIT-associated thrombocytopenia manifested earlier than the typical window for HIT, which generally occurs between postoperative days 5 and 10 (POD5–POD10) in individuals post–cardiac surgery. Fewer than 10% of cardiac surgery patients who develop HIT experience a platelet decline attributable to HIT prior to POD5, a pattern associated with recent preoperative heparin exposure, which was also observed in this patient.

Additionally, the patient's platelet count decreased to a notably low nadir of 13×10^9/L, accompanied by decreasing fibrinogen, elevated d-dimer concentrations, and the presence of schistocytes on the blood smear while receiving argatroban therapy. These manifestations of marked HIT-induced hypercoagulability are characteristic of aHIT.

Third, unlike the rapid improvement in platelet counts often observed in aHIT patients following high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin therapy, our patient’s thrombocytopenia responded only slowly, suggesting an exceptionally severe form of aHIT. Serologically, this was supported by the finding that monoclonal antibody IV.3 only partially inhibited platelet activation in vitro—a serological pattern that, to our knowledge, has not been previously documented in aHIT. Recognition of this atypical serologic profile may be important to prevent missing the diagnosis of aHIT and its associated clinical risks. Despite this, the diagnosis of aHIT was established in this patient for several reasons. First, analysis of sera collected later during the extended course of platelet recovery revealed SRA reactivity patterns more aligned with classical aHIT. Second, the final tested sample demonstrated a heparin-dependent SRA profile typical of HIT, indicative of declining aHIT antibody levels. Third, there were no alternative diagnoses that sufficiently explained the unusual clinical presentation. Fortunately, the patient experienced a complete neurological recovery without any lingering deficits.
