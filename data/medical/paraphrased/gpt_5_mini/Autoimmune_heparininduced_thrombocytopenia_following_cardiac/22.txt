Title: Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery

Rosanne St Bernard, Ishac Nazy, Theodore Earl Warkentin

Abstract:

Summary: An elderly male patient received heparin anticoagulation during cardiac surgery. In the postoperative period, his platelet count progressively declined, reaching 50×10⁹/L by postoperative day 5 (POD5), which led to evaluation for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibodies. Brain imaging revealed a parietal stroke. An initial immunoassay for HIT antibodies returned a strong positive. The platelet activation assay (serotonin-release assay) demonstrated an unusual pattern—marked serotonin release at 0, 0.1, and 0.3 U/mL heparin that was only partially suppressed by a platelet Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody. Anticoagulation therapy was switched to argatroban in combination with high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin. Ongoing signs of hypercoagulability led to a subsequent change to fondaparinux, after which the platelet count gradually improved (reaching 155×10⁹/L by POD62), and neurological deficits resolved. Further laboratory testing identified HIT antibodies exhibiting heparin-independent platelet-activating characteristics, consistent with autoimmune HIT (aHIT). In addition to severe thrombocytopenia (lowest platelet count, 13×10⁹/L), noteworthy features in this aHIT case include onset early in the postoperative course (prior to POD5) attributable to preoperative heparin exposure, and an unusual antibody laboratory profile demonstrating partial resistance to Fc receptor inhibition.

Background:

Over the past seventy years, unfractionated heparin (UFH) has been almost exclusively utilized as the intraoperative anticoagulant during heart surgery involving cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB). This preference is attributed to several benefits, such as suppression of haemostasis caused by contact with artificial surfaces, elimination independent of renal or hepatic function, intraoperative monitoring via activated clotting time, and affordability. Nevertheless, UFH is associated with a significant immune-mediated adverse event known as heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT). HIT occurs relatively frequently following cardiac procedures, with an estimated incidence ranging from 0.5% to 1.0%, which is influenced by both the length and type of postoperative heparin thromboprophylaxis (UFH versus low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH)). It is particularly notable for its robust correlation with thrombotic events, conferring an approximate 15-fold increased risk of thrombosis.

Traditionally, HIT has been regarded as a condition characterized by potent platelet activation via clustering of platelet Fc receptors, which is instigated by immune complexes involving heparin-dependent immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibodies that target platelet factor 4 (PF4) when it is complexed with polyanionic heparin. More recently, it has been recognized that a subset of patients experiences atypical, frequently severe clinical manifestations caused by HIT antibodies capable of activating platelets independently of heparin; this variant is termed ‘autoimmune HIT’ (aHIT) (for review). The present report discusses a case of aHIT with exceptionally profound and prolonged thrombocytopenia, which was further complicated by thrombotic stroke after cardiac surgery. The case affords new perspectives on the range of aHIT by illustrating a distinctly severe serological profile with only partial susceptibility to Fc receptor blockade. Additionally, it underscores the complexities involved in diagnosing postoperative thrombocytopenia early, as the patient’s anomalously early presentation of aHIT was attributable to preoperative UFH exposure. Ultimately, the patient experienced a positive outcome.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his seventies, who had experienced exertional shortness of breath for two years and had a history of persistent atrial fibrillation (managed with oral apixaban 5 mg twice daily), was noted to have a previously unrecognized systolic murmur. He underwent diagnostic cardiac catheterization without interrupting his apixaban therapy beforehand. Consequently, the cardiologist opted for a transradial arterial approach and administered a reduced intraprocedural anticoagulant dose (just 2000 U of unfractionated heparin). Angiography identified 60% stenosis of the obtuse marginal branch, moderate aortic stenosis, and an enlarged ascending aorta; the patient was subsequently scheduled for cardiac surgery in one week.

Cardiac surgery was carried out utilizing cardiopulmonary bypass, with anticoagulation maintained by 45,000 U of UFH. The operative procedures comprised aortic valve replacement with a #27 bioprosthesis, substitution of the ascending aorta using a #30 Gelweave graft, coronary artery bypass grafting to the obtuse marginal branch using a saphenous vein, and excision of the left atrial appendage. The surgery was straightforward, with no significant intraoperative bleeding or necessity for blood transfusions, and the patient was weaned from bypass with stable hemodynamics.

The platelet count immediately prior to surgery measured 210×10^9/L (reference range 150–400). On the first postoperative day (POD1), it dropped to 155×10^9/L, a modest decrease often observed following cardiac surgery. Standard thromboprophylactic management with low molecular weight heparin (LMWH) was initiated on POD1, starting with 5000 U of dalteparin administered subcutaneously, then increased to 7500 U on POD2, POD3, and POD4. Contrary to expectations, the platelet count continued to fall steadily from POD1 through POD5, reaching a nadir of 50×10^9/L (see figure 1). Apart from postoperative delirium, the patient’s recovery was unremarkable. Dalteparin was omitted on POD5; instead, prophylactic fondaparinux (2.5 mg) was started, the initial dose administered at 18:00 on POD5.

Despite the absence of localized neurological deficits, the escalation of postoperative delirium and further platelet count reduction on POD6 to 26×10^9/L (with a repeat count at 24×10^9/L) warranted a CT scan of the brain. Imaging demonstrated an acute infarct, identified as an area of hypodensity measuring 3.2 cm by 1.8 cm in the left parietal lobe, extending into the cerebral cortex.

Investigations:

The complete blood count performed on POD5 revealed a platelet level of 50×10^9/L, with an associated haemoglobin of 97 g/L (reference range 135–180 g/L), consistent with prior haemoglobin measurements over the preceding three days, indicative of anticipated early postoperative blood loss without evidence of continued bleeding. The white blood cell count measured 7.8×10^9/L (reference range 4.0–11.0×10^9/L), and the differential was within normal limits. Reticulocyte count was noncontributory at 73×10^9/L (reference range 10–100×10^9/L). Haptoglobin was elevated at 3.19 g/L (reference range 0.32–1.97 g/L), supporting the absence of significant haemolysis. Serum vitamin B12 concentrations normalized.

Assessment of coagulation parameters demonstrated a normal prothrombin time with a corresponding INR of 1.1 (reference range 0.9–1.1), a partial thromboplastin time (PTT) of 34 seconds (reference range 25–37 seconds), and a fibrinogen level elevated at 6.59 g/L (reference range 2.00–3.93 g/L), reflecting the expected postoperative acute phase response, as fibrinogen tends to rise markedly during this period. On POD5, a specimen was forwarded to a reference lab for HIT antibody analysis.

Additional laboratory results included normal values for serum urea, creatinine, alanine transaminase, and bilirubin. Cultures of blood and urine were obtained. Chest radiography demonstrated no significant abnormalities.

Examination of the peripheral blood smear on POD7 corroborated thrombocytopenia (with no platelet aggregation), and showed neither schistocytes nor ‘toxic’ alterations in white blood cells. Initial HIT screening using the PF4/polyanion ELISA was markedly positive at 2.2 optical density units (reference range <0.4 OD units), whereas the confirmatory serotonin-release assay (SRA) returned an ‘indeterminate’ result, to be discussed further.

Differential diagnosis:

Typical causes of marked thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery, such as cardiogenic shock or severe illness and infection, were considered improbable based on the patient’s stable haemodynamic status and negative blood and urine cultures. Less frequent etiologies like post-transfusion purpura were discounted since the patient had not received blood transfusions. Post-surgical thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura was also deemed unlikely due to normal haptoglobin levels and the absence of schistocytes initially. Drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia was considered improbable as well, as the reduction in platelet count occurred before exposure to any new postoperative medications—specifically those administered only during the operation (vancomycin, cefazolin, tranexamic acid) or initiated postoperatively (furosemide, metoprolol, pantoprazole, midodrine).

Although the timing of thrombocytopenia appeared too early to attribute to unfractionated heparin (UFH) given intraoperatively during cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), further investigation revealed that UFH had also been administered intra-procedurally during preoperative cardiac catheterization. In conjunction with the occurrence of thrombotic stroke and a strongly positive PF4/polyanion ELISA, heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) was considered the most likely diagnosis, probably initiated by the UFH provided at preoperative cardiac catheterization. Additional information about the ‘indeterminate’ SRA was requested from the McMaster Platelet Immunology Laboratory.

A thorough evaluation of the SRA findings was undertaken. The patient’s POD5 serum exhibited robust serotonin release at both 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL UFH concentrations (97% and 99%, respectively; RR<20% release), consistent with platelet-activating HIT antibodies. In contrast, at a high UFH concentration (100 U/mL), no serotonin release was observed (0%), a phenomenon characteristic of HIT and indicative of the failure to form PF4-containing immune complexes at these elevated UFH levels. Nonetheless, two additional aspects of the SRA results were observed. First, the patient’s serum produced pronounced serotonin release even in the absence of UFH (96% release at 0 U/mL heparin; RR<20%), a finding typically associated with aHIT rather than conventional heparin-dependent HIT. Second, introducing the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody (IV.3) did not fully suppress serotonin release in the presence of patient serum at 0.1 U/mL UFH: serotonin release remained at 76% with IV.3, whereas inhibition to less than 20% release is usually expected with HIT antibodies. This observation originally led to labeling the SRA result as 'indeterminate.' The eventual conclusion was that the patient did have aHIT, with highly reactive HIT antibodies, resulting in only partial suppression by IV.3 compared to the typical response. To date, such an unusual SRA reactivity pattern has not been previously described.

Treatment:

Fondaparinux at prophylactic dosing was stopped, and the patient was started on argatroban at therapeutic doses—specifically 0.75 mcg/kg/min—with a goal to maintain the PTT between 55 and 85 s. While therapeutic-dose fondaparinux is recognized as an effective ‘off-label’ option for HIT, the hematologist opted for argatroban due to its shorter half-life, which provides increased flexibility in managing possible bleeding, particularly in light of the patient’s recent stroke and notably severe thrombocytopenia. Additionally, high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin was administered: initially, 100 g on two successive days, then a third dose of 100 g, in response to the absence of a rise in platelet count after the first two days of IVIG treatment. Fibrinogen and d-dimer levels were repeatedly measured to indirectly assess anticoagulation effectiveness, with an increase in fibrinogen and a decrease in d-dimer interpreted as evidence that the hypercoagulable state associated with HIT was being controlled.

During the initial course of therapy, fibrinogen concentrations progressively declined between POD6 and POD17, reaching a low of 2.49 g/L, while d-dimer levels peaked around POD12. Additionally, peripheral blood smears revealed the presence of schistocytes from POD9 to POD16. These combined observations were indicative of significant, evolving HIT-associated consumptive coagulopathy. Furthermore, following high-dose intravenous Ig administration, the patient’s platelet count showed only a modest elevation—from 13×10^9/L on POD7 prior to intravenous Ig to a temporary maximum of 54×10^9/L on POD14. This increase was notably less than the typical rise in platelet count observed in patients with aHIT receiving high-dose intravenous Ig (as previously reviewed).

On postoperative day 14, the patient’s anticoagulation regimen was changed from argatroban to fondaparinux at a therapeutic dose of 10 mg administered subcutaneously once daily. The rationale for switching included fondaparinux’s convenient once-daily subcutaneous administration, in contrast to the intravenous argatroban dosing that requires PTT adjustments, and concerns that argatroban may not have adequately controlled HIT-related hypercoagulability, as evidenced by decreased fibrinogen, increased d-dimer levels, and the development of schistocytes during argatroban therapy. Since fondaparinux acts as an indirect, antithrombin-dependent factor Xa inhibitor, the patient received supplemental antithrombin concentrate (1000 U) prior to initiating fondaparinux; the antithrombin level before infusion was slightly below the reference range, measuring 0.70 U/mL (RR 0.80–1.40 U/mL).

Outcome and follow-up:

The platelet count progressively returned to the normal range (155×10^9/L on POD62 and 202×10^9/L on POD87), with the patient also experiencing full neurological restoration. Fondaparinux therapy continued through POD65, after which apixaban was reinitiated at a dosage of 5 mg orally twice daily for ongoing anticoagulation. Successive SRA assays remained markedly positive for multiple months, and several later SRA evaluations demonstrated a profile more typical of aHIT (see figure 2); the final blood sample, drawn at POD124 after normalization of platelet counts, indicated predominantly heparin-dependent HIT antibodies, showing 15% serotonin release in the absence of UFH, contrasted by 100% release when UFH was present at 0.3 U/mL.

Discussion:

This patient exhibited several unusual clinical, laboratory, and serological findings, each ultimately explained by the diagnosis of severe aHIT. Initially, his HIT-induced thrombocytopenia manifested prior to the typical onset timeframe for HIT, which ranges from postoperative day (POD) 5 to POD10 in individuals undergoing cardiac surgery. Less than 10% of cardiac surgery patients who develop HIT experience a platelet drop before POD5, a pattern often associated with preoperative heparin exposure, which was confirmed in this case.

Additionally, the patient’s platelet count decreased to an atypically low minimum of 13×10^9/L, accompanied by decreasing fibrinogen concentrations, elevated d-dimer levels, and schistocytes observed while on argatroban anticoagulation. These findings of pronounced HIT-related hypercoagulability are characteristic of aHIT.

Thirdly, our patient did not demonstrate the rapid improvement in thrombocytopenia commonly observed following administration of high-dose intravenous Ig to individuals with aHIT; this suggests that the patient may have experienced an unusually severe variant of the disorder. Specifically, serological findings indicative of a particularly severe form of aHIT included only partial suppression of platelet activation in vitro when monoclonal antibody IV.3 was present. To our knowledge, this distinctive serological pattern has not previously been documented in cases of aHIT, and recognition of it could be critical in preventing an overlooked diagnosis of aHIT, which could result in negative clinical outcomes. Nevertheless, the diagnosis of aHIT was ultimately established for our patient based on at least three factors. First, analysis of sera obtained during the extended period of platelet count normalization revealed some SRA reactivity patterns more typical for aHIT. Second, the final sample analyzed exhibited a heparin-dependent SRA response characteristic of conventional HIT, indicating a decrease in aHIT-specific antibodies. Third, no alternative diagnosis could account for the patient’s atypical clinical trajectory. Encouragingly, the patient experienced a complete clinical recovery, with no lasting neurological deficits.
