Title: Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery

Rosanne St Bernard, Ishac Nazy, Theodore Earl Warkentin

Abstract:

Summary: An older male patient received cardiac surgery utilizing heparin for anticoagulation. Following the procedure, his platelet count progressively decreased, reaching 50×10⁹/L on postoperative day 5 (POD5), which prompted investigation for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibodies; brain imaging detected a parietal stroke. HIT antibody screening with immunoassay was strongly positive, while the serotonin-release assay for platelet activation displayed an unusual pattern: marked serotonin release at 0, 0.1, and 0.3 U/mL heparin with only partial suppression by a platelet Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody. Anticoagulation therapy was transitioned to argatroban along with high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin. Ongoing hypercoagulability led to initiation of fondaparinux, and the platelet count gradually improved (155×10⁹/L by POD62); neurological deficits resolved. Subsequent testing verified HIT antibodies that could activate platelets independently of heparin, identifying this case as autoimmune HIT (aHIT). In addition to severe thrombocytopenia (lowest platelet count of 13×10⁹/L), notable features included early onset postoperatively (before POD5) related to prior heparin exposure before surgery, as well as a distinctive laboratory aHIT antibody profile demonstrating partial resistance to Fc receptor inhibition.

Background:

For the past seven decades, unfractionated heparin (UFH) has been the primary anticoagulant used intraoperatively in heart surgeries requiring cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), primarily due to its multiple benefits, such as preventing haemostatic reactions to artificial surfaces, clearance independent of renal or hepatic function, real-time intraoperative anticoagulant assessment through activated clotting time, and cost-effectiveness. Nevertheless, UFH is associated with a significant immunologic complication: heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT). Following cardiac surgery, HIT occurs with a reported incidence of 0.5%–1.0%, contingent upon the duration and choice of postoperative heparin for thromboprophylaxis (comparing UFH versus low-molecular-weight heparin [LMWH]), and is notable for its high correlation with thrombotic events, demonstrating an approximate 15-fold increase in risk.

Traditionally, HIT has been characterized by robust platelet activation via Fc receptor engagement, instigated by heparin-dependent immune complexes involving immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibodies targeting the positively charged protein platelet factor 4 (PF4) once bound to polyanionic heparin. Recent advances, however, have illuminated the existence of patients experiencing atypical and often pronounced clinical manifestations due to HIT antibodies that activate platelets independently of heparin; this atypical variant is termed ‘autoimmune HIT’ (aHIT) (see review). Herein, we present a case of aHIT marked by extremely severe and persistent thrombocytopenia, further complicated by thrombotic stroke after cardiac surgery. This case enriches understanding of the aHIT spectrum by revealing a distinctive serological profile with partial resistance to Fc receptor inhibition. It also underscores diagnostic complexities encountered with postoperative thrombocytopenia, as the patient’s unusually rapid onset of aHIT was attributed to preoperative UFH exposure. Despite these challenges, the patient ultimately achieved a positive clinical outcome.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his seventies, with a two-year history of exertional shortness of breath and chronic atrial fibrillation (maintained on oral apixaban 5 mg twice daily), was noted to have a previously unrecognized systolic murmur. He was referred for diagnostic cardiac catheterization, but the apixaban was not discontinued prior to the procedure; as a result, the cardiologist opted for a transradial artery access and utilized a lower-than-standard intraprocedural anticoagulant dose (only 2000 U of UFH). Angiography revealed a 60% narrowing of the obtuse marginal coronary artery, moderate aortic stenosis, and dilation of the ascending aorta. Consequently, he was scheduled for cardiac surgery the following week.

The heart surgery was performed utilizing cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), with anticoagulation managed using 45,000 U of UFH. The operative intervention comprised aortic valve replacement with a #27 bioprosthetic valve, ascending aorta replacement with a #30 Gelweave prosthetic graft, a single coronary artery bypass graft (saphenous vein graft to the obtuse marginal artery), and excision of the left atrial appendage. The procedure was completed without complications, significant bleeding, or transfusion requirements, and the patient was weaned off CPB without difficulty, demonstrating stable hemodynamics.

The patient's platelet count immediately prior to surgery measured 210×10^9/L (reference range: 150–400×10^9/L). On postoperative day 1 (POD1), the platelet level dropped to 155×10^9/L, indicating a modest initial decrease in platelets typical after cardiac procedures. Standard postoperative thromboprophylaxis was initiated on POD1, using low molecular weight heparin (LMWH) with 5000 U of dalteparin given subcutaneously; higher doses of 7500 U were administered on POD2, POD3, and POD4. However, between POD1 and POD5, the platelet count continued to decline, reaching 50×10^9/L (see figure 1). Aside from postoperative delirium, the patient remained clinically stable. Dalteparin was withheld on POD5, and instead, fondaparinux at a prophylactic dose of 2.5 mg was started (first dose at 18:00 on POD5).

Despite the absence of specific neurological deficits, progression of postoperative delirium and further decrease in platelet count to 26×10^9/L (repeat: 24×10^9/L) on POD6 led to a CT scan of the brain. Imaging identified a new cerebral infarction, characterized by a hypodense area measuring 3.2 cm×1.8 cm in the left parietal lobe, with involvement extending to the cerebral cortex.

Investigations:

On postoperative day 5, the complete blood count revealed a platelet count of 50×10^9/L, with a concurrent haemoglobin level of 97 g/L (reference range 135–180 g/L), which aligned with the haemoglobin levels measured over the previous three days and reflected anticipated perioperative blood loss without evidence of active bleeding. The white blood cell count was measured at 7.8×10^9/L (reference range 4.0–11.0×10^9/L), and the differential was within normal limits. Reticulocyte count was 73×10^9/L (reference range 10–100×10^9/L), which was not remarkable. Haptoglobin was found to be elevated at 3.19 g/L (reference range 0.32–1.97 g/L), supporting the lack of significant haemolysis. The serum vitamin B12 concentration was normalized.

Assessment of coagulation parameters revealed normal prothrombin time, a corresponding international normalized ratio of 1.1 (reference range 0.9–1.1), and a normal partial thromboplastin time of 34 seconds (reference range 25–37 seconds). Fibrinogen was elevated at 6.59 g/L (reference range 2.00–3.93 g/L), consistent with the postoperative phase, as it functions as an acute phase protein and typically rises substantially after surgery. On POD5, a sample was sent to a reference laboratory for testing of HIT antibodies.

Other laboratory results showed normal values for chemistry tests, including urea, creatinine, alanine transaminase, and bilirubin. Blood and urine cultures were collected. A chest radiograph demonstrated no significant findings.

Examination of a peripheral blood smear taken on POD7 verified thrombocytopenia, with no evidence of platelet aggregates, red cell fragments (schistocytes), or abnormal white cell morphology consistent with toxicity. The HIT screening immunoassay (PF4/polyanion ELISA) was markedly positive at 2.2 optical density (OD) units (reference range <0.4 OD units); however, the serotonin-release assay (SRA), which serves as a confirmatory platelet activation test, produced an 'indeterminate' result, which is elaborated upon in subsequent sections.

Differential diagnosis:

Typical causes of marked thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery, such as cardiogenic shock, severe illness, or infection, were considered unlikely based on clinical presentation and laboratory data (stable haemodynamics; negative results from blood and urine cultures). Similarly, uncommon etiologies—including post-transfusion purpura (as no transfusions had been administered), postsurgical thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (with normal haptoglobin and initially absent schistocytes), and drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia—were also deemed improbable, given that the drop in platelet count happened too early in relation to any new medications. These medications included those administered only during surgery (vancomycin, cefazolin, tranexamic acid) and those initiated in the postoperative period (furosemide, metoprolol, pantoprazole, and midodrine).

Even though the decline in platelets appeared earlier than would be expected from intraoperative UFH given during CPB, further investigation revealed that UFH had been administered during a preoperative cardiac catheterization. This history, combined with a thrombotic stroke event and a strongly positive PF4/polyanion ELISA, supported a presumptive diagnosis of HIT, probably precipitated by the UFH during the preoperative procedure. The laboratory at McMaster Platelet Immunology Laboratory was contacted for additional clarification on the reported ‘indeterminate’ SRA results.

The SRA findings underwent careful evaluation. Supporting the presence of platelet-activating HIT antibodies, the patient’s serum collected on postoperative day 5 induced pronounced serotonin release at both 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL of UFH (97% and 99%, respectively; with a reference range of less than 20% release). Furthermore, there was no detectable serotonin release (0%) at a supratherapeutic UFH concentration of 100 U/mL; this lack of platelet activation at very high heparin doses is a hallmark of HIT, indicating that PF4-immune complexes cannot form at such elevated UFH concentrations. Nevertheless, two additional observations were made in the SRA analysis. First, the patient’s serum alone—without added UFH—elicited substantial serotonin release (96% at 0 U/mL heparin; reference range <20%), a pattern typical of aHIT rather than conventional heparin-dependent HIT. Second, introducing the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody (IV.3) failed to fully suppress serotonin release initiated by patient serum at 0.1 U/mL UFH: serotonin release remained at 76% with IV.3 present, rather than falling below 20% as expected with HIT antibody-mediated reactions. Consequently, this led to an initial classification of the SRA outcome as ‘indeterminate.’ However, this was attributed to a diagnosis of aHIT with the patient’s HIT antibodies demonstrating unusually robust reactivity, leading to only partial reduction in serotonin release by IV.3. To date, such an abnormal SRA profile has not been previously documented in the literature.

Treatment:

Prophylactic fondaparinux was stopped, and argatroban, a direct thrombin inhibitor, was started at a therapeutic dose of 0.75 mcg/kg/min, aiming for a PTT between 55 and 85 seconds. While fondaparinux at therapeutic doses can serve as an effective off-label anticoagulant for HIT, the hematologist chose argatroban due to its shorter half-life, prioritizing ease of management should bleeding develop, especially given the patient's recent stroke and marked thrombocytopenia. Additionally, high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin was administered: 100 g daily over two consecutive days, and a third 100 g dose was given when platelet counts did not rise after the initial two infusions. To indirectly monitor anticoagulation efficacy, serial measurements of fibrinogen and d-dimer were obtained, with increasing fibrinogen and decreasing d-dimer suggesting that HIT-related hypercoagulability was being controlled.

During the initial phase of therapy, fibrinogen levels steadily declined from postoperative day 6 (POD6) to postoperative day 17 (POD17), reaching their lowest point at 2.49 g/L. D-dimer levels peaked around POD12, and peripheral blood smears revealed schistocytes from POD9 through POD16. These three laboratory findings were indicative of a severe and worsening consumptive coagulopathy associated with HIT. Additionally, after administration of high-dose intravenous Ig, the patient demonstrated only a modest rise in platelet count, increasing from 13×10^9/L on POD7 (before Ig treatment) to a transient peak of 54×10^9/L by POD14. This increase was substantially lower than the typical platelet count response observed following high-dose intravenous Ig therapy in cases of aHIT (referenced in literature).

On postoperative day 14, the patient’s anticoagulation was transitioned from argatroban to fondaparinux at a therapeutic dose of 10 mg administered subcutaneously once daily. The rationale for selecting fondaparinux included its simpler dosing regimen, requiring only once-daily subcutaneous injection, compared to argatroban, which necessitates continuous intravenous infusion adjusted by PTT. Additionally, there was concern that argatroban may not adequately manage the hypercoagulable state associated with HIT, as reflected by decreasing fibrinogen, elevated d-dimer values, and the emergence of schistocytes during argatroban therapy. Since fondaparinux functions as an indirect, antithrombin-dependent inhibitor of factor Xa, the patient received supplemental antithrombin concentrate (1000 U) before initiating fondaparinux; the measured antithrombin level prior to infusion was slightly below normal at 0.70 U/mL (reference range 0.80–1.40 U/mL).

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient’s platelet count progressively returned to normal levels, reaching 155×10^9/L on POD62 and 202×10^9/L by POD87, along with full neurological restoration. Fondaparinux was continued until POD65, after which apixaban was restarted at 5 mg orally twice daily for ongoing anticoagulation. Consecutive SRA tests remained strongly positive over several months, with later SRA results demonstrating a pattern more typical of aHIT (figure 2). The most recent sample, collected at POD124 after platelet normalization, indicated mainly heparin-dependent HIT antibodies, demonstrating 15% serotonin release in the absence of UFH and 100% release when UFH at 0.3 U/mL was present.

Discussion:

The patient demonstrated several unusual clinical, laboratory, and serological findings, all of which were ultimately consistent with a diagnosis of severe aHIT. Initially, the decrease in platelet count associated with HIT began outside the typical timeframe, which for patients post-cardiac surgery generally spans from postoperative day (POD) 5 to POD10. Only approximately 10% of individuals undergoing cardiac surgery who develop HIT will exhibit a platelet decrease before POD5, a pattern related to recent heparin exposure prior to surgery, as was observed in this case.

Additionally, the patient’s platelet count reached an exceptionally low nadir of 13×10^9/L, accompanied by declining fibrinogen, elevated d-dimer concentrations, and the presence of schistocytes during treatment with argatroban. These manifestations of pronounced hypercoagulability related to HIT are characteristic features pointing toward a diagnosis of aHIT.

Thirdly, our patient failed to demonstrate the rapid improvement in thrombocytopenia that is commonly observed with administration of high-dose intravenous Ig in cases of aHIT; this suggests the presence of an unusually severe manifestation of the disorder. In fact, serological findings indicated an atypically severe subtype of aHIT, evidenced by only partial inhibition of platelet activation in vitro when monoclonal antibody IV.3 was present. To our knowledge, this distinctive serological pattern has not previously been described in aHIT, and recognizing it could prevent diagnostic oversight and subsequent adverse outcomes. Nonetheless, a diagnosis of aHIT was eventually established in our patient, based on several factors. First, subsequent serum samples collected during the extended period of platelet count normalization exhibited SRA reactivity profiles consistent with aHIT. Second, the final specimen tested produced a heparin-dependent SRA pattern typical of classic HIT, indicative of declining aHIT antibody levels. Third, no alternate diagnosis adequately accounted for the patient’s exceptional clinical trajectory. Fortunately, the patient experienced a full recovery without any lingering neurological deficits.
