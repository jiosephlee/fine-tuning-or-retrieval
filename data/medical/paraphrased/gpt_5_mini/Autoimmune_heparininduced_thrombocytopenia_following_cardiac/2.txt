Title: Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery

Rosanne St Bernard, Ishac Nazy, Theodore Earl Warkentin

Abstract:

Summary: An older male patient received heparin for anticoagulation during cardiac surgery. After surgery, his platelet levels progressively decreased, dropping to 50×10⁹/L by postoperative day 5 (POD5), leading clinicians to evaluate for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibodies; imaging of the brain revealed a parietal infarct. Testing with a HIT antibody screening immunoassay showed a markedly positive result, while the platelet activation assay (serotonin-release assay) demonstrated an unusual pattern: pronounced serotonin release at 0, 0.1, and 0.3 U/mL heparin concentrations, with only incomplete suppression upon addition of a platelet Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody. The anticoagulant regimen was adjusted to argatroban along with high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin. Because of ongoing hypercoagulable state, the patient was later transitioned to fondaparinux, which was followed by a gradual improvement in platelet numbers (reaching 155×10⁹/L by POD62); his neurological symptoms resolved. Further laboratory testing established the presence of HIT antibodies that could activate platelets independently of heparin, consistent with autoimmune HIT (aHIT). Key features of this aHIT case, in addition to marked thrombocytopenia (platelet nadir, 13×10⁹/L), included a notably early postoperative presentation (prior to POD5) due to pre-surgery heparin administration, and an uncommon laboratory antibody pattern showing only partial inhibition by Fc receptor blockade.

Background:

For nearly seven decades, unfractionated heparin (UFH) has served as the primary intraoperative anticoagulant in heart surgeries utilizing cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB). Its popularity stems from several key advantages: UFH effectively inhibits haemostasis triggered by artificial surfaces, is cleared independently of renal or hepatic function, enables intraoperative monitoring through activated clotting time, and is relatively inexpensive. Nevertheless, UFH carries a significant risk: the development of heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT), an immune-mediated complication. HIT occurs with a frequency of about 0.5%–1.0% in patients undergoing cardiac surgery, varying according to the type and length of postoperative thromboprophylaxis (whether using UFH or low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH)), and is marked by a pronounced association with thrombosis, conferring an approximate 15-fold increase in risk.

Traditionally, HIT is understood as a condition driven by robust platelet activation via clustering of Fc receptors on platelets, initiated when immune complexes form between heparin, immunoglobulin G (IgG) class antibodies, and the cationic protein platelet factor 4 (PF4), which binds to polyanionic heparin. Recently, it has been recognized that some cases diverge from this conventional course; in particular, a subset of patients develops HIT antibodies capable of activating platelets independently of heparin, leading to atypical and often severe manifestations. This variant, termed ‘autoimmune HIT’ (aHIT), has been the subject of recent reviews. Herein, we present a case of aHIT characterized by marked, prolonged thrombocytopenia and complicated by thrombotic stroke following cardiac surgery. This case yields new perspectives on aHIT, illustrating a particularly severe serological presentation marked by partial resistance to blockade of Fc receptors. Furthermore, it underlines the complexities involved in diagnosing thrombocytopenia in the early postoperative period; in this instance, the unusually prompt onset of aHIT was attributable to preoperative UFH exposure. The patient ultimately recovered successfully.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his seventies, who had experienced exertional dyspnoea for two years and had a history of chronic atrial fibrillation (managed with oral apixaban 5 mg twice daily), was identified with a previously unrecognized systolic murmur. He underwent diagnostic cardiac catheterisation without discontinuing his apixaban before the procedure; as a result, the cardiologist opted for a transradial artery approach and administered a lower-than-standard intraprocedural anticoagulant dose (limited to 2000 U of UFH). Angiography revealed a 60% stenosis in the obtuse marginal coronary artery, moderate aortic stenosis, and dilation of the ascending aorta, leading to a plan for cardiac surgery to be conducted a week later.

The cardiac surgery utilized CPB, and anticoagulation was managed with 45 000 U of UFH. The procedure comprised aortic valve replacement using a #27 bioprosthesis, replacement of the ascending aorta with a #30 Gelweave graft, a single coronary artery bypass utilizing a saphenous vein graft to the obtuse marginal coronary artery, and excision of the left atrial appendage. The operation was carried out smoothly, with no significant blood loss or need for transfusion, and the patient was weaned from CPB with stable and favourable haemodynamic status.

The platelet count immediately prior to surgery was 210×10^9/L (reference range (RR): 150–400). On the first postoperative day (POD1), the platelet count dropped to 155×10^9/L, indicating only a modest early reduction typically observed following cardiac surgery. Standard postoperative thromboprophylaxis with LMWH was initiated on POD1, with 5000 U dalteparin administered subcutaneously, followed by increased doses of 7500 U on POD2, POD3, and POD4. Unexpectedly, the platelet count steadily decreased between POD1 and POD5, reaching 50×10^9/L (see figure 1). Apart from experiencing postoperative delirium, the patient exhibited no other significant complications. Dalteparin was withheld on POD5, and prophylactic fondaparinux at a dose of 2.5 mg was introduced (first administered on POD5 at 18:00).

Despite the absence of localized neurological deficits, a worsening of postoperative delirium and further reduction in platelet count to 26×10^9/L (with a repeated value of 24×10^9/L) on POD6 led to a CT scan of the brain. Imaging identified an acute infarct within the left parietal lobe, displaying a hypodense area measuring 3.2 cm×1.8 cm extending into the cerebral cortex.

Investigations:

On POD5, the full blood count revealed a platelet level of 50×10^9/L. The haemoglobin was measured at 97 g/L (reference range [RR] 135–180 g/L), a value consistent with the preceding three days, aligning with anticipated early postoperative blood loss in the absence of ongoing bleeding. The white blood cell count was 7.8×10^9/L (RR 4.0–11.0×10^9/L) with a normal differential. Reticulocyte count fell within expected limits at 73×10^9/L (RR 10–100×10^9/L). Haptoglobin levels were increased at 3.19 g/L (RR 0.32–1.97 g/L), suggesting no significant haemolysis. Serum vitamin B12 concentration was within the normal range.

Assessment of coagulation parameters indicated a normal prothrombin time and international normalised ratio of 1.1 (RR 0.9–1.1), as well as a normal partial thromboplastin time (PTT) of 34 seconds (RR 25–37 s). Fibrinogen was markedly elevated at 6.59 g/L (RR 2.00–3.93 g/L), which is consistent with the acute-phase response typically seen in the early postoperative period. On POD5, a blood specimen was forwarded to a reference laboratory for the assessment of HIT antibodies.

Additional laboratory evaluation showed unremarkable chemistry values, including urea, creatinine, alanine transaminase, and bilirubin. Blood and urine cultures were collected, and chest radiography revealed no significant findings.

Microscopic review of the peripheral blood smear from POD7 demonstrated thrombocytopenia, with no platelet clumping and an absence of schistocytes or abnormal (‘toxic’) features in white blood cells. The screening test for HIT—the PF4/polyanion ELISA—was notably positive, yielding an optical density (OD) of 2.2 (RR <0.4 OD units), whereas the confirmatory functional assay, the serotonin-release assay (SRA), produced an ‘indeterminate’ outcome (details discussed elsewhere).

Differential diagnosis:

Frequently cited causes of significant thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery—namely cardiogenic shock/critical illness and infection—were considered improbable based on both clinical observations and laboratory data (such as stable haemodynamic status, and negative results from blood and urine cultures). Less common etiologies, including post-transfusion purpura (not applicable, as no transfusions had been performed), post-surgical thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (given normal haptoglobin levels and the initial absence of schistocytes), and drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia, were also deemed unlikely since the decline in platelet count occurred too soon to be associated with any new perioperative medications; drugs administered intraoperatively (vancomycin, cefazolin, tranexamic acid) and in the immediate postoperative period (furosemide, metoprolol, pantoprazole, midodrine) had not been started early enough to account for the timing of thrombocytopenia.

While the rapid decline in platelets was also too premature to be accounted for by UFH used intraoperatively during CPB, further investigation revealed that UFH had been administered intraprocedurally during preoperative heart catheterization. In conjunction with the development of thrombotic stroke and a strongly positive PF4/polyanion ELISA result, heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) was considered the most likely diagnosis, presumably precipitated by UFH exposure during preoperative catheterization. To further clarify the results from the ‘indeterminate’ SRA, additional information was solicited from the McMaster Platelet Immunology Laboratory.

The SRA findings underwent a thorough evaluation. Aligned with the detection of platelet-activating HIT antibodies, the patient’s serum collected on POD5 elicited pronounced serotonin release at both 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL UFH (97% and 99%, respectively; RR<20% release), whereas exposure to supratherapeutic UFH levels (100 U/mL) resulted in no detectable serotonin release (0%). This absence of platelet activation at high heparin concentrations is indicative of HIT and is attributed to the inability to form PF4-immune complexes when UFH is present in excess. Nevertheless, two noteworthy observations emerged from the SRA data. First, the patient’s serum induced a marked serotonin release even in the absence of UFH (96% release at 0 U/mL heparin; RR<20%), a pattern typically seen in aHIT rather than traditional heparin-dependent HIT. Second, introducing the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody (IV.3) did not fully suppress serotonin release provoked by the patient's serum with 0.1 U/mL UFH: serotonin release persisted at 76% despite the presence of IV.3, as opposed to the expected suppression to below 20% typically observed with HIT antibodies. This finding initially led to classifying the SRA result as ‘indeterminate.’ The underlying reason appeared to be that while the patient indeed had aHIT, their HIT antibodies demonstrated exceptional reactivity, resulting in only partial inhibition by IV.3. To the best of our knowledge, this unique SRA response pattern has not been documented in previous reports.

Treatment:

The patient’s prophylactic fondaparinux regimen was halted, and therapy with argatroban—a direct thrombin inhibitor administered at a therapeutic dose of 0.75 mcg/kg/min—was initiated, with the aim of maintaining a PTT between 55 and 85 s. Although therapeutic doses of fondaparinux can serve as an effective ‘off-label’ anticoagulant in cases of HIT, the attending haematologist opted for argatroban because of its comparatively short half-life, which offered greater flexibility should hemorrhagic complications arise, particularly in light of the recent cerebrovascular event and marked thrombocytopenia. Furthermore, high-dose intravenous Ig was administered, with an initial 100 g dose on each of two consecutive days, and a subsequent 100 g dose, due to the absence of a platelet response after the first two days of intravenous Ig. Additionally, repeated assessments of fibrinogen and d-dimer levels were undertaken as surrogate measures for the efficacy of anticoagulation; specifically, increasing fibrinogen and decreasing d-dimer concentrations would support successful suppression of the HIT-related hypercoagulable state.

During the initial phase of therapy, there was a marked reduction in fibrinogen concentrations observed between POD6 and POD17, reaching a minimum of 2.49 g/L. D-dimer values peaked near POD12, and peripheral blood smears revealed the presence of schistocytes from POD9 through POD16. Collectively, these findings indicated a significant, progressive consumptive coagulopathy related to severe HIT. Additionally, after initiation of high-dose intravenous Ig, the patient experienced only a slight elevation in platelet count, rising from 13×10$^9$/L on POD7 prior to intravenous Ig administration to a maximum of 54×10$^9$/L on POD14. This increment is considerably less pronounced than the platelet response typically observed in patients with aHIT following high-dose intravenous Ig treatment (for review).

On postoperative day 14 (POD14), the anticoagulant regimen was modified from argatroban to fondaparinux at a therapeutic dose of 10 mg administered subcutaneously once per day. The rationale behind this adjustment included the more convenient dosing schedule of fondaparinux—given once daily via subcutaneous injection—contrasted with the need for continuous intravenous infusion and frequent partial thromboplastin time (PTT) monitoring required by argatroban. Additionally, there were concerns that argatroban might not be adequately addressing the hypercoagulability associated with HIT, as evidenced by the declining fibrinogen concentrations, rising d-dimer values, and presence of schistocytes during argatroban treatment. Because fondaparinux operates as an indirect factor Xa inhibitor dependent on antithrombin (AT) activity, a supplemental dose of AT concentrate (1000 U) was administered before initiating fondaparinux. Notably, the patient's AT level prior to infusion was mildly decreased at 0.70 U/mL (reference range 0.80–1.40 U/mL).

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient's platelet count steadily returned to normal levels (155×10^9/L by POD62 and 202×10^9/L by POD87), and neurological function fully recovered. Fondaparinux treatment was maintained up to POD65, after which apixaban was reintroduced at a dosage of 5 mg orally twice daily for ongoing anticoagulation. Repeated SRA tests continued to be strongly positive for several months, and later SRA profiles displayed findings more typical of aHIT (see figure 2). The final tested blood sample, collected at POD124 after normalization of platelet counts, demonstrated mostly heparin-dependent HIT antibodies, with serotonin release measured at 15% in the absence of UFH and 100% release when UFH was present at a concentration of 0.3 U/mL.

Discussion:

The patient exhibited several unusual clinical, laboratory, and serological findings, all of which were ultimately explained by a diagnosis of severe aHIT. Initially, the patient developed HIT-associated thrombocytopenia earlier than typically expected; in cardiac surgery patients, HIT usually emerges between POD5 and POD10. A decline in platelet count before POD5 occurs in only approximately 10% of post-cardiac surgery HIT cases, a pattern associated with recent preoperative exposure to heparin—a detail present in this case.

Additionally, the patient’s platelet count dropped to a markedly low nadir of 13×10^9/L, which was accompanied by decreasing fibrinogen, increasing d-dimer concentrations, and schistocytosis during argatroban anticoagulant therapy. This constellation of findings, reflecting profound HIT-related hypercoagulability, is characteristic of aHIT.

Third, unlike the typical rapid resolution of thrombocytopenia observed after administration of high-dose intravenous Ig in patients with aHIT, our patient’s platelet count recovered only sluggishly, indicating an especially severe manifestation of the condition. Notably, serological analysis revealed a uniquely severe aHIT subtype, as demonstrated by only partial inhibition of platelet activation in vitro when monoclonal antibody IV.3 was present; to our knowledge, such a serological pattern has not been previously documented in aHIT cases. Recognition of this distinct profile is important, as it may prevent diagnostic oversight of aHIT and avoid subsequent negative clinical outcomes. Despite these atypical findings, the aHIT diagnosis was ultimately substantiated based on several factors. First, serum samples collected later during the protracted platelet count normalization exhibited SRA reactivity patterns more commonly associated with aHIT. Second, the final sample analyzed displayed a heparin-dependent SRA response profile characteristic of conventional HIT, indicating a reduction in aHIT antibody levels. Third, alternative explanations for the patient's unusual clinical progression were lacking. Encouragingly, the patient ultimately achieved full clinical recovery without lingering neurological deficits.
