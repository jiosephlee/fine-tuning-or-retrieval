Title: Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery

Rosanne St Bernard, Ishac Nazy, Theodore Earl Warkentin

Abstract:

Summary: An older male patient received heparin anticoagulation during cardiac surgery and subsequently experienced a progressive decline in platelet counts, reaching 50×10⁹/L by postoperative day 5 (POD5). This decrease raised suspicion for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT), leading to antibody testing; neuroimaging identified a parietal stroke. The initial HIT antibody screening immunoassay was strongly positive, whereas the serotonin-release assay produced an unusual pattern—demonstrating marked serotonin release at 0, 0.1, and 0.3 U/mL heparin concentrations, with only partial suppression by a monoclonal antibody targeting the platelet Fc receptor. Anticoagulant therapy was changed to argatroban in conjunction with high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin. Due to ongoing hypercoagulability, therapy was further modified to fondaparinux, after which platelet counts gradually improved (155×10⁹/L, POD62), and neurological deficits resolved. Further laboratory analyses verified the presence of HIT antibodies exhibiting heparin-independent platelet-activating activity, confirming a diagnosis of autoimmune HIT (aHIT). Notable features in this instance of aHIT, in addition to severe thrombocytopenia (platelet count reaching a minimum of 13×10⁹/L), were the early onset following surgery (prior to POD5), attributed to preoperative heparin exposure, as well as a distinctive antibody profile in laboratory studies, revealing partial resistance to Fc receptor inhibition.

Background:

Over the past seven decades, unfractionated heparin (UFH) has remained the predominant anticoagulant employed intraoperatively in heart surgeries utilizing cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB). This long-standing preference is attributed to UFH’s multiple benefits, such as its effective suppression of artificial surface-induced haemostatic activation, clearance mechanisms that are independent of both renal and hepatic function, the feasibility of intraoperative monitoring via activated clotting time, and its economical cost. Nevertheless, UFH carries a notable risk of inducing an immune-mediated complication known as heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT). The incidence of HIT following cardiac surgery is relatively high, estimated at 0.5%–1.0%, varying with the duration and choice of postoperative heparin thromboprophylaxis (whether UFH or low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH)). HIT is particularly significant for its pronounced association with thrombotic events, conferring an approximate 15-fold increase in thrombosis risk.

Traditionally, HIT has been understood as a syndrome characterized by potent platelet activation mediated by immune complexes formed between heparin-dependent immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibodies and the cationic protein, platelet factor 4 (PF4), complexed with the polyanionic heparin. This process involves clustering of platelet Fc receptors, initiating pathological platelet activation. However, emerging evidence has demonstrated that a subset of patients experience a non-classical and often more severe clinical course, attributable to HIT antibodies capable of activating platelets independently of heparin; this variant has been termed ‘autoimmune HIT’ (aHIT) (see review for details). In this paper, we present a case of aHIT marked by exceptionally severe and persistent thrombocytopenia, complicated by a thrombotic stroke, in the context of cardiac surgery. The case affords new perspectives on the aHIT spectrum by illustrating a distinctly severe serological profile, showing partial insensitivity to Fc receptor inhibition. Additionally, this report underscores the complexities inherent in diagnosing early postoperative thrombocytopenia, as the patient’s unusually rapid onset of aHIT was attributable to prior exposure to UFH before surgery. Despite the gravity of these complications, the patient ultimately achieved a positive recovery.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his seventies, with a two-year history of exertional dyspnoea and chronic atrial fibrillation (managed with oral apixaban 5 mg twice daily), was newly identified to have a systolic murmur during examination. Diagnostic heart catheterisation was performed, although the patient did not discontinue apixaban beforehand. To mitigate bleeding risk, the interventional cardiologist opted for a transradial artery approach and administered a reduced intraprocedural anticoagulant dose (restricted to 2000 U of UFH). Coronary angiography demonstrated 60% stenosis in the obtuse marginal artery, moderate aortic stenosis, and an enlarged ascending aorta, for which he was scheduled for cardiac surgery the following week.

Cardiac surgery was subsequently undertaken utilizing CPB, with a total of 45 000 U of UFH administered for anticoagulation. The surgical interventions comprised aortic valve replacement using a #27 bioprosthesis, ascending aorta replacement with a #30 Gelweave graft, single-vessel coronary artery bypass employing a saphenous vein graft to the obtuse marginal artery, and excision of the left atrial appendage. The operative course was straightforward, marked by minimal bleeding, no transfusion requirements, and smooth weaning from CPB with stable haemodynamic parameters.

The patient's platelet count immediately before surgery measured 210×10^9/L, which is within the normal reference interval (150–400). On the first postoperative day (POD1), the count decreased to 155×10^9/L, indicating only a mild early reduction typical for patients undergoing cardiac surgery. Standard postoperative thromboprophylaxis using LMWH was initiated on POD1, starting with 5000 U of dalteparin delivered subcutaneously, followed by increased doses of 7500 U on POD2, POD3, and POD4. Contrary to expectations, the platelet count continued to fall steadily from POD1 through POD5, ultimately reaching 50×10^9/L (see figure 1). Aside from experiencing postoperative delirium, the patient exhibited no other complications. Dalteparin administration was withheld on POD5, and in its place, prophylactic fondaparinux at a dose of 2.5 mg was started (first administered at 18:00 on POD5).

Despite the absence of localizing neurological signs, the persistence and worsening of postoperative delirium, in conjunction with a further drop in platelet count to 26×10^9/L on POD6 (subsequently confirmed at 24×10^9/L), led to the decision to obtain a CT scan of the brain. Imaging identified an acute cerebral infarction, characterized by a hypodense area measuring 3.2 cm×1.8 cm within the left parietal region, extending to involve the cerebral cortex.

Investigations:

On postoperative day 5 (POD5), the complete blood count revealed a platelet count of 50×10^9/L, with an associated haemoglobin level of 97 g/L (reference range [RR] 135–180 g/L), mirroring the haemoglobin measurements recorded over the previous three days. These findings are in keeping with expected early postoperative blood losses, and there was no indication of continued hemorrhage. The white blood cell count measured 7.8×10^9/L (RR 4.0–11.0×10^9/L), with a normal differential. Reticulocyte count was within the normal interval at 73×10^9/L (RR 10–100×10^9/L). Haptoglobin concentration was raised at 3.19 g/L (RR 0.32–1.97 g/L), supporting the absence of active haemolysis. The serum vitamin B12 level had normalized.

Assessment of coagulation parameters showed a normal prothrombin time, yielding an international normalized ratio of 1.1 (RR 0.9–1.1), and the partial thromboplastin time (PTT) was also within normal limits at 34 s (RR 25–37 s). Fibrinogen levels were notably increased at 6.59 g/L (RR 2.00–3.93 g/L), which is characteristic of the immediate postoperative phase, as fibrinogen—an acute phase reactant—often peaks during this period. On POD5, a sample was submitted to a reference center for HIT antibody testing.

Further laboratory investigations demonstrated normal chemistry profiles (including urea, creatinine, alanine transaminase, and bilirubin). Both blood and urine cultures were collected, and chest radiography revealed no significant abnormalities.

Examination of the peripheral blood smear on POD7 corroborated the presence of thrombocytopenia, showing no platelet clumping and no evidence of schistocytes or pathologic white cell morphologies. Screening immunoassay for HIT via the PF4/polyanion ELISA demonstrated a strong positive result at 2.2 optical density (OD) units (RR <0.4 OD units). However, the serotonin-release assay (SRA), serving as the confirmatory platelet activation test, produced an ‘indeterminate’ outcome, the implications of which are addressed in a later section.

Differential diagnosis:

Typical causes of significant thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery—such as cardiogenic shock or critical illness and infection—appeared improbable in light of the patient's clinical presentation and laboratory data (haemodynamic parameters were within normal limits; both blood and urine cultures were negative). Other rare etiologies, including post-transfusion purpura (the patient did not receive blood transfusions), post-surgical thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (normal haptoglobin levels, absence of schistocytes initially), and drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia, were also considered unlikely, since the decline in platelet count developed too rapidly to be attributed to new postoperative medications. These included drugs administered exclusively on the day of surgery (vancomycin, cefazolin, and tranexamic acid) or those introduced postoperatively (furosemide, metoprolol, pantoprazole, and midodrine).

Despite the fact that the reduction in platelet count occurred too quickly to be linked to intraoperative UFH exposure during CPB, further investigation uncovered that UFH had been used during a preoperative heart catheterisation procedure. Considering this, together with the occurrence of thrombotic stroke and a strongly positive PF4/polyanion ELISA, HIT emerged as the most plausible diagnosis, presumably induced by the UFH given prior to surgery during catheterisation. Additional clarification on the ‘indeterminate’ SRA was sought from the reporting facility, the McMaster Platelet Immunology Laboratory.

The results of the SRA were subjected to thorough analysis. In agreement with the presence of platelet-activating HIT antibodies, the patient’s serum collected on POD5 demonstrated pronounced serotonin release at both 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL UFH concentrations (97% and 99% release, respectively; reference range [RR] <20%), whereas at a supratherapeutic UFH level (100 U/mL), there was a complete absence of serotonin release (0%). This lack of platelet activation at high concentrations of heparin is typical of HIT, attributable to the inability of immune complexes containing PF4 to assemble at such elevated UFH levels. Nevertheless, two notable observations emerged from the SRA findings. First, a robust serotonin release was observed when the patient’s serum was tested without UFH present (96% serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin; RR <20%), a response that is typically associated with aHIT rather than the traditional heparin-dependent HIT. Second, the inclusion of the monoclonal antibody IV.3, which blocks the Fc receptor, did not fully suppress the serotonin release induced by the patient's serum at 0.1 U/mL UFH. Instead, serotonin release remained elevated at 76% in the presence of IV.3, far above the expected suppression to below 20% usually associated with classic HIT antibodies. Consequently, the SRA result was initially categorized as ‘indeterminate’. The underlying cause, however, was that the patient indeed had aHIT, and the HIT antibodies exhibited an unusual degree of reactivity, leading to only partial inhibition by IV.3. To the best of our knowledge, this distinctive SRA profile has not previously been documented.

Treatment:

Fondaparinux at a prophylactic dose was stopped, and the patient was started on therapeutic-dose argatroban, a direct thrombin inhibitor, administered at 0.75 mcg/kg/min, with a target PTT of 55–85 s. While therapeutic dosing of fondaparinux is an effective 'off-label' option for managing HIT, the consulting haematologist opted for argatroban because of its shorter half-life, which would be advantageous should bleeding occur, especially considering the patient’s recent stroke and pronounced thrombocytopenia. Additionally, high-dose intravenous Ig was administered—initially 100 g for two consecutive days, and a third dose of 100 g was given when there was no appreciable rise in platelet count after the first 2 days of therapy. To indirectly monitor the efficacy of anticoagulation, serial measurements of fibrinogen and d-dimer were obtained, since an increase in fibrinogen and a decrease in d-dimer would indicate improved anticoagulant management of HIT-related hypercoagulability.

During the initial phase of treatment, a continuous reduction in fibrinogen concentrations was observed between POD6 and POD17, reaching a lowest point of 2.49 g/L. Additionally, d-dimer values peaked near POD12, and peripheral blood smears revealed the presence of schistocytes from POD9 through POD16. Collectively, these indicators pointed to a severe and advancing HIT-associated consumptive coagulopathy. Furthermore, despite administration of high-dose intravenous Ig, the patient's platelet count showed only a limited increase, rising from 13×10^9/L on POD7 (prior to intravenous Ig) to a maximum of 54×10^9/L by POD14. This increment is notably less pronounced than the typical rise in platelet count generally reported after high-dose intravenous Ig treatment for patients with aHIT (for review).

On postoperative day 14, the patient’s anticoagulation therapy was transitioned from argatroban to fondaparinux at a therapeutic dose of 10 mg administered subcutaneously once per day. Factors influencing the selection of fondaparinux included its convenient once-daily subcutaneous administration, in contrast to the need for intravenous delivery and PTT-based dose adjustments with argatroban. Another rationale for switching was concern about inadequate management of HIT-related hypercoagulability with argatroban, suggested by previously noted reductions in fibrinogen, elevations in d-dimer, and emergence of schistocytes during argatroban therapy. Recognizing that fondaparinux acts as an indirect, antithrombin (AT)-dependent inhibitor of factor Xa, a 1000-unit dose of AT concentrate was administered before fondaparinux initiation due to a mildly decreased baseline AT level of 0.70 U/mL (normal range 0.80–1.40 U/mL).

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient's platelet levels returned to the normal range over time (155×10^9/L at POD62 and 202×10^9/L at POD87), accompanied by a full restoration of neurological function. Fondaparinux therapy continued up to POD65, after which apixaban was reinstated at an oral dose of 5 mg twice daily for ongoing anticoagulation management. Repeated SRA assays stayed highly positive for several months, and subsequent assessments revealed SRA patterns increasingly typical of aHIT (figure 2). The most recent blood sample analyzed, drawn at POD124 after normalization of platelet count, demonstrated findings indicative of primarily heparin-dependent HIT antibodies: serotonin release was 15% without UFH but reached 100% when UFH was present at a concentration of 0.3 U/mL.

Discussion:

The patient exhibited multiple atypical characteristics across clinical presentation, laboratory values, and serological markers, each ultimately explainable by the diagnosis of severe aHIT. Notably, the thrombocytopenia related to HIT emerged earlier than what is typically observed, occurring prior to the conventional timeframe of POD5 to POD10 seen in post-cardiac surgery patients. It is uncommon—approximately 10%—for individuals who have undergone cardiac surgery and subsequently develop HIT to experience a platelet count reduction before POD5, a situation associated with recent heparin administration before surgery, which was also confirmed in this case.

Additionally, the patient’s platelet count dropped to an exceptionally low nadir of 13×10^9/L, accompanied by decreased fibrinogen levels, elevated d-dimer values, and evidence of schistocytosis during ongoing argatroban anticoagulation. Such manifestations, reflecting profound HIT-related hypercoagulability, are consistent with a diagnosis of aHIT.

Third, unlike the typical rapid improvement in thrombocytopenia seen in aHIT patients treated with high-dose intravenous Ig, our patient’s response was less pronounced, suggesting a particularly severe presentation of aHIT. Notably, serological analysis revealed a uniquely severe aHIT subtype, evidenced by only partial inhibition of platelet activation in vitro in the presence of monoclonal antibody IV.3. To our knowledge, this distinctive serological pattern has not been previously described in association with aHIT, and recognizing it may help prevent underdiagnosis of aHIT and its potential negative outcomes. Ultimately, the diagnosis of aHIT in our patient was substantiated based on three key points. First, follow-up serum samples obtained during the patient’s prolonged platelet count recovery period displayed SRA reactivity profiles that are more typically associated with aHIT. Second, the final serum sample demonstrated a heparin-dependent SRA pattern characteristic of conventional HIT, indicative of declining aHIT antibody levels. Third, no other credible diagnoses were identified that could explain the patient’s atypical clinical trajectory. Encouragingly, the patient experienced full clinical recovery and had no persistent neurological deficits.
