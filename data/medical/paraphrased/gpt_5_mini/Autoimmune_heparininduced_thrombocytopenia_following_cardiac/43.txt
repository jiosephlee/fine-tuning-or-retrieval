Title: Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery

Rosanne St Bernard, Ishac Nazy, Theodore Earl Warkentin

Abstract:

Summary: An older male patient received heparin anticoagulation during cardiac surgery, after which his platelet count progressively declined, dropping to 50×10⁹/L by postoperative day 5 (POD5); this decrease led to an evaluation for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibodies. Brain imaging revealed a parietal lobe stroke. HIT antibody screening by immunoassay was strongly positive, while the platelet activation (serotonin-release) assay exhibited an unusual pattern—marked serotonin release at 0, 0.1, and 0.3 U/mL heparin, with only partial inhibition by an Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody. Argatroban anticoagulation was initiated along with high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin. Ongoing hypercoagulability resulted in a transition to fondaparinux, subsequently leading to progressive improvement in platelet counts (155×10⁹/L by POD62), and neurological deficits resolved. Subsequent laboratory analysis verified HIT antibodies with the ability to activate platelets independent of heparin, consistent with autoimmune HIT (aHIT). Beyond severe thrombocytopenia (lowest platelet count, 13×10⁹/L), distinguishing features in this aHIT case included its early onset following surgery (before POD5) due to antecedent heparin exposure, as well as a distinctive laboratory aHIT antibody pattern showing incomplete inhibition by Fc receptor blockade.

Background:

For more than seven decades, unfractionated heparin (UFH) has been utilized almost exclusively as the intraoperative anticoagulant during cardiac procedures involving cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), owing to several key benefits such as prevention of haemostasis induced by contact with artificial surfaces, clearance independent of renal or hepatic function, capability for intraoperative monitoring via activated clotting time, and affordability. Nevertheless, UFH carries the significant risk of provoking heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT), an immune-mediated complication. HIT arises fairly frequently after cardiac surgery, with reported incidence ranging between 0.5% and 1.0%, subject to the choice and duration of postoperative heparin thromboprophylaxis (UFH compared to low-molecular-weight heparin [LMWH]), and is notable for its pronounced link to thrombosis, with a relative risk approximating 15.

Traditionally, HIT has been regarded as a condition marked by robust platelet activation via clustering of platelet Fc receptors. This results from the formation of immune complexes containing IgG class antibodies targeting platelet factor 4 (PF4)—a cationic protein—when it interacts with the polyanionic molecule heparin. More recently, it has become apparent that some patients exhibit atypical, frequently severe clinical manifestations owing to HIT antibodies capable of activating platelets independent of heparin; this distinct subset has been described as ‘autoimmune HIT’ (aHIT) (see review). The current report details a case of aHIT characterized by pronounced, persistent thrombocytopenia complicated by thrombotic stroke following cardiac surgery. This case offers new perspectives on the range of aHIT, demonstrating an unusually severe serological presentation with only partial resistance to blockade of Fc receptors. Furthermore, the case underscores the complexities involved in diagnosing early postoperative thrombocytopenia, given that the patient's strikingly rapid onset of aHIT was attributable to prior UFH exposure before surgery. In the end, the patient recovered well.

Case presentation:

A male in his 70s, with a two-year history of exertional shortness of breath and chronic atrial fibrillation (managed with oral apixaban 5 mg twice daily), was discovered to have a new systolic heart murmur. He was referred for diagnostic cardiac catheterisation, but his apixaban had not been discontinued prior to the procedure. Therefore, the cardiologist chose a transradial artery approach and administered a lower than typical intraprocedural anticoagulation dose (limited to 2000 U of UFH). Coronary angiography revealed a 60% narrowing in the obtuse marginal coronary artery, moderate aortic stenosis, and an enlarged ascending aorta; cardiac surgery was planned for one week later.

The patient underwent cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), utilizing 45,000 U of UFH for anticoagulation. Surgical interventions included aortic valve replacement using a #27 bioprosthetic valve, substitution of the ascending aorta with a #30 Gelweave graft, a single coronary artery bypass to the obtuse marginal coronary artery using a saphenous vein graft, and excision of the left atrial appendage. The operation was completed without complications, significant blood loss, or requirement for transfusion, and separation from CPB was achieved easily, with stable hemodynamics.

Just prior to surgery, the patient's platelet count measured 210×10^9/L (normal range 150–400). On the first postoperative day (POD1), this dropped to 155×10^9/L, indicating a modest decrease in platelet numbers commonly observed following cardiac operations. Standard thromboprophylaxis with low molecular weight heparin (LMWH) was initiated on POD1, with a 5000 U dose of dalteparin given subcutaneously, and subsequently increased to 7500 U on POD2, POD3, and POD4. Unexpectedly, from POD1 through POD5, the platelet count steadily decreased, reaching 50×10^9/L (see figure 1). The patient remained stable apart from experiencing postoperative delirium. Dalteparin was withheld on POD5, and a prophylactic dose of fondaparinux (2.5 mg) was started, first administered at 18:00 on POD5.

Despite the absence of localized neurological deficits, the worsening delirium combined with a further platelet count reduction to 26×10^9/L (confirmed at 24×10^9/L) on POD6 warranted a CT scan of the brain. Imaging revealed an acute infarction in the left parietal lobe, with the area of hypodensity measuring 3.2 cm×1.8 cm and extending to the cerebral cortex.

Investigations:

On postoperative day 5, the complete blood count revealed a platelet level of 50×10^9/L and a haemoglobin concentration of 97 g/L (reference range 135–180 g/L). This haemoglobin result was comparable to values measured over the previous three days, aligning with anticipated early postoperative blood loss in the absence of ongoing hemorrhage. The corresponding white blood cell count was 7.8×10^9/L (reference range 4.0–11.0×10^9/L) with a normal differential. Reticulocyte count was within the normal range at 73×10^9/L (reference range 10–100×10^9/L). Haptoglobin was increased at 3.19 g/L (reference range 0.32–1.97 g/L), a finding that does not suggest active haemolysis. Serum vitamin B12 was reported as normal.

Coagulation parameters indicated a prothrombin time within normal limits, corresponding to an INR of 1.1 (reference range 0.9–1.1), a partial thromboplastin time of 34 seconds (reference range 25–37 seconds), and a raised fibrinogen level of 6.59 g/L (reference range 2.00–3.93 g/L). The elevated fibrinogen was attributed to the acute phase response commonly observed in the early period following surgery. A blood sample was obtained on postoperative day 5 and referred to a specialized laboratory for HIT antibody analysis.

Additional laboratory evaluations showed that electrolytes, renal function (urea, creatinine), alanine transaminase, and bilirubin levels were within reference ranges. Both blood and urine cultures were collected. Chest radiography did not reveal significant pathology.

The peripheral blood smear reviewed on postoperative day 7 verified the low platelet count without evidence of platelet aggregation, red cell fragmentation (such as schistocytes), or significant white blood cell morphological abnormalities. Screening for HIT using the PF4/polyanion ELISA produced a markedly positive result at 2.2 optical density units (reference range <0.4 OD units). However, the confirmatory platelet activation assay—the serotonin-release assay (SRA)—was interpreted as 'indeterminate,' with further discussion provided subsequently.

Differential diagnosis:

Typical causes of marked thrombocytopenia after cardiac surgery, such as cardiogenic shock or critical illness and infection, appeared improbable, considering the patient’s stable haemodynamics and negative blood and urine culture results. Less frequent etiologies for post-cardiac surgery thrombocytopenia were also deemed unlikely: post-transfusion purpura was excluded since the patient did not receive any blood products; post-surgical thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura was unsupported by normal haptoglobin and initial absence of schistocytes; drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia was improbable, as the decrease in platelet count manifested too early for it to be attributable to either medications administered solely on the day of surgery (vancomycin, cefazolin, tranexamic acid) or postoperative agents (furosemide, metoprolol, pantoprazole, midodrine).

Even though the onset of thrombocytopenia was too rapid to be caused by UFH exposure during intraoperative cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), further history taking revealed that the patient had received intraprocedural UFH during preoperative cardiac catheterisation. Combined with the presence of a thrombotic stroke and a strongly positive PF4/polyanion ELISA, heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) was considered the most likely diagnosis, presumably precipitated by UFH given during preoperative cardiac catheterisation. Additional clarification on the ‘indeterminate’ serotonin release assay (SRA) findings was requested from the reporting site, the McMaster Platelet Immunology Laboratory.

A thorough evaluation of the SRA findings was performed. Indicating the existence of platelet-activating HIT antibodies, the patient's serum from postoperative day 5 produced robust serotonin release at both 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL UFH concentrations (97% and 99% release, respectively; reference range <20%), and showed no serotonin release (0%) at an extremely high concentration of UFH (100 U/mL). The lack of platelet activation at excessive heparin levels is a hallmark of HIT, signifying that immune complexes containing PF4 cannot form under these conditions. Nevertheless, two other notable aspects of the SRA findings were observed. Firstly, the patient’s serum elicited pronounced serotonin release even when no UFH was present (96% release at 0 U/mL heparin; reference range <20%), which is a distinctive marker of aHIT but is not associated with conventional heparin-dependent HIT. Secondly, application of the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody IV.3 did not fully suppress the serotonin release seen with patient serum at 0.1 U/mL UFH; serotonin release remained at 76% despite IV.3, rather than being reduced to below 20% as typically happens with HIT antibodies. This finding initially led to classifying the SRA outcome as 'indeterminate'. The underlying reason is that the patient indeed had aHIT, but their HIT antibodies displayed unusually strong reactivity, so the suppression usually achieved by IV.3 was only partial. As far as we are aware, this uncommon SRA response pattern has not previously been described.

Treatment:

Fondaparinux at prophylactic dosing was stopped, and argatroban, a direct thrombin inhibitor, was initiated at a therapeutic rate of 0.75 mcg/kg/min, targeting a PTT of 55–85 seconds. Although fondaparinux administered at therapeutic doses is recognized as an effective off-label anticoagulant for HIT, the haematologist managing the case opted for argatroban due to its shorter half-life, to mitigate bleeding risk in light of the recent stroke and unusually profound thrombocytopenia. Additionally, high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (Ig) therapy was administered—initially 100 g daily for two days, followed by a third 100 g dose—because platelet counts did not rise after the initial two days of IV Ig treatment. Repeated fibrinogen and d-dimer measurements were obtained to indirectly assess anticoagulant effectiveness, as increases in fibrinogen and decreases in d-dimer would indicate successful control of HIT-associated hypercoagulability.

During the initial phase of therapy, fibrinogen concentrations progressively declined between POD6 and POD17, reaching their lowest at 2.49 g/L. D-dimer levels peaked around POD12, and peripheral blood smears identified schistocytes—red cell fragments—from POD9 to POD16. Collectively, these three indicators supported the diagnosis of a severe and progressive HIT-related consumptive coagulopathy. Additionally, following administration of high-dose intravenous Ig, the patient’s platelet count showed only a minimal increase, rising from 13×10^9/L on POD7 to a temporary maximum of 54×10^9/L on POD14. This increment was notably less than the typical platelet count response observed after high-dose intravenous Ig treatment in cases of aHIT (for review).

On postoperative day 14, the patient’s anticoagulation regimen was changed from argatroban to fondaparinux at a therapeutic dose of 10 mg administered subcutaneously once per day. Factors influencing this switch included the simpler administration protocol of fondaparinux, which requires only once-daily subcutaneous injection compared to the intravenous, PTT-adjusted dosing necessary for argatroban, and concerns about possible inadequate suppression of HIT-related hypercoagulable state by argatroban. This concern stemmed from previously noted laboratory trends of decreasing fibrinogen, increasing d-dimer, and the presence of schistocytes during argatroban treatment. Since fondaparinux acts as an indirect antithrombin (AT)-dependent factor Xa inhibitor, a dose of AT concentrate (1000 U) was administered before initiating fondaparinux, as the patient’s antithrombin level prior to infusion was slightly below normal at 0.70 U/mL (reference range 0.80–1.40 U/mL).

Outcome and follow-up:

The platelet count steadily returned to within normal limits, measuring 155×10^9/L on POD62 and 202×10^9/L on POD87; neurologically, the patient also experienced full recovery. Fondaparinux therapy continued until POD65, after which apixaban was restarted at 5 mg orally twice daily for ongoing anticoagulation. Serial serotonin release assays (SRA) continued to show strongly positive results for several months, with subsequent tests displaying a more typical SRA pattern consistent with aHIT (see figure 2). The final SRA performed, using a blood sample collected on POD124 after normalization of the platelet count, demonstrated findings indicative of mainly heparin-dependent HIT antibodies, evidenced by 15% serotonin release without UFH and 100% release when UFH at 0.3 U/mL was present.

Discussion:

The patient exhibited multiple uncommon clinical, laboratory, and serologic characteristics, all traceable to the diagnosis of severe autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (aHIT). Initially, his HIT-associated thrombocytopenia manifested earlier than the typical timeframe for HIT onset in cardiac surgery patients, which generally occurs between postoperative day 5 (POD5) and postoperative day 10 (POD10). Approximately 10% of individuals who undergo cardiac surgery and subsequently develop HIT experience a platelet count decline before POD5, a pattern associated with preoperative heparin use, as was observed in this patient.

Additionally, the patient’s platelet count decreased to a notably low nadir of 13×10^9/L, accompanied by reduced fibrinogen concentrations, elevated d-dimer values, and the presence of schistocytes during anticoagulation with argatroban. Such manifestations of pronounced HIT-related hypercoagulability are indicative of aHIT.

Third, our patient failed to demonstrate the rapid resolution of thrombocytopenia that is typically seen in individuals with aHIT treated with high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin; this suggests the presence of an especially severe variant of the condition. Supporting this, serological testing revealed only partial inhibition of platelet activation in vitro when monoclonal antibody IV.3 was added—an atypical finding that, to our knowledge, represents a newly identified serological profile not previously documented in cases of aHIT. Recognition of this atypical presentation may prevent missed diagnoses and associated negative outcomes. Despite this, the diagnosis of aHIT was ultimately established in our case, based on several factors. First, testing serum obtained later during the extended recovery of platelet counts revealed some SRA (serotonin release assay) activity patterns consistent with aHIT. Second, the final sample demonstrated a heparin-dependent SRA response typical of classic HIT, suggesting decreasing levels of aHIT antibodies over time. Third, no alternative diagnosis accounted for the patient's distinctive clinical evolution. Encouragingly, the patient achieved full clinical recovery without persistent neurological deficits.
