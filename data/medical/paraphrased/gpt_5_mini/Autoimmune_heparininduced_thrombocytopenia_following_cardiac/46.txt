Title: Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery

Rosanne St Bernard, Ishac Nazy, Theodore Earl Warkentin

Abstract:

Summary: An older male patient received heparin for anticoagulation during cardiac surgery; after surgery, his platelet count progressively declined, dropping to 50×10⁹/L by the fifth postoperative day (POD5), which led to testing for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibodies. Brain imaging detected a parietal lobe infarction. HIT antibody screening via immunoassay produced a strong positive, and the serotonin-release assay (platelet activation test) displayed an atypical result: robust serotonin release at heparin concentrations of 0, 0.1, and 0.3 U/mL, with only partial suppression upon addition of a platelet Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody. Anticoagulation therapy was switched to argatroban together with high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin. Ongoing hypercoagulability led to a subsequent change to fondaparinux, after which platelet counts gradually improved (to 155×10⁹/L on POD62), and neurological deficits resolved. Laboratory studies confirmed the presence of HIT antibodies capable of activating platelets without heparin, consistent with autoimmune HIT (aHIT). In addition to marked thrombocytopenia (lowest platelet count, 13×10⁹/L), unique features of this aHIT case included an unusually early onset after surgery (prior to POD5) due to prior heparin exposure, and an uncommon laboratory antibody signature demonstrating only partial inhibition by Fc receptor blockade.

Background:

For nearly seven decades, unfractionated heparin (UFH) has been the primary anticoagulant used intraoperatively during heart surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), owing to several benefits such as suppression of haemostasis induced by contact with artificial surfaces, elimination independent of renal or hepatic pathways, real-time monitoring through activated clotting time, and affordability. Nonetheless, UFH carries a significant risk: an immune-mediated complication known as heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT). HIT is relatively frequent after cardiac operations, with an incidence ranging from 0.5% to 1.0%, depending on the specific regimen and duration of postoperative heparin thromboprophylaxis (UFH versus low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH)). It is distinguished by its notable association with thrombosis, conferring an approximate 15-fold increased risk.

Traditionally, HIT has been understood as a condition driven by potent platelet activation, secondary to clustering of platelet Fc receptors due to immune complexes formed from heparin-dependent IgG antibodies targeting platelet factor 4 (PF4) when bound to heparin. More recently, evidence has accumulated that in some patients, HIT antibodies can activate platelets independently of heparin, leading to atypical—and often more severe—clinical manifestations. This variant is termed ‘autoimmune HIT’ (aHIT) (see review). Here, we report a case of aHIT characterized by pronounced and persistent thrombocytopenia, compounded by thrombotic stroke, in the postoperative period following cardiac surgery. This report illustrates a uniquely severe serological form of aHIT, displaying partial resistance to Fc receptor inhibition, and draws attention to the diagnostic complexity of early postoperative thrombocytopenia—the patient’s unusually rapid onset of aHIT was attributed to prior UFH exposure before surgery. The patient ultimately experienced a positive clinical outcome.

Case presentation:

A male in his 70s, who had experienced exertional dyspnea for two years and had a history of chronic atrial fibrillation (managed with apixaban 5 mg orally twice daily), was recently identified to have an unrecognized systolic murmur. When he underwent diagnostic cardiac catheterization, he did not pause his apixaban beforehand. As a result, the cardiologist opted for a transradial artery access and administered a reduced intraprocedural anticoagulant dose (UFH at 2000 U). Angiography demonstrated 60% stenosis of the obtuse marginal coronary artery, moderate aortic stenosis, and an enlarged ascending aorta. Cardiac surgery was scheduled for the following week.

Cardiac surgery was completed utilizing cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), with anticoagulation achieved using 45,000 U of UFH. The operative procedures entailed replacement of the aortic valve with a #27 bioprosthesis; substitution of the ascending aorta using a #30 Gelweave graft; single coronary artery bypass grafting to the obtuse marginal artery with a saphenous vein graft; and excision of the left atrial appendage. The surgery was uneventful, with no significant blood loss or transfusion required, and the patient was separated from bypass without difficulty and had stable haemodynamics.

The platelet count immediately prior to surgery measured 210×10^9/L (reference range 150–400). On the first postoperative day (POD1), this value dropped to 155×10^9/L, indicating only a slight reduction commonly seen after cardiac surgery. Standard postoperative thromboprophylaxis using LMWH was initiated on POD1, beginning with 5000 U dalteparin administered subcutaneously. The dosing was increased to 7500 U on POD2, POD3, and POD4. Unexpectedly, from POD1 through POD5, the platelet count continued to decline, reaching 50×10^9/L (figure 1). Aside from postoperative delirium, the patient remained clinically stable. Dalteparin was withheld on POD5, and a prophylactic dose of fondaparinux (2.5 mg) was introduced, with the initial dose given at 18:00 on POD5.

Despite the absence of localized neurological signs, the worsening delirium in conjunction with a further decrease in platelets on POD6 to 26×10^9/L (repeated result 24×10^9/L) led to a CT scan of the brain. Imaging demonstrated an acute cerebral infarction, with a hypodense region measuring 3.2 cm×1.8 cm located within the left parietal lobe, extending into the cerebral cortex.

Investigations:

On postoperative day 5 (POD5), the complete blood count revealed a platelet count of 50×10^9/L, accompanied by a haemoglobin level of 97 g/L (reference range 135–180 g/L), which was comparable to haemoglobin measurements from the prior three days, aligning with anticipated early postoperative blood loss and no evidence of continued bleeding. The white blood cell count measured 7.8×10^9/L (reference range 4.0–11.0×10^9/L), with a normal differential. Reticulocyte count was within normal limits at 73×10^9/L (reference range 10–100×10^9/L). Haptoglobin was raised at 3.19 g/L (reference range 0.32–1.97 g/L), indicating no significant haemolysis. Serum vitamin B12 level had normalized.

Coagulation testing demonstrated a normal prothrombin time, yielding an international normalized ratio of 1.1 (reference range 0.9–1.1), a normal partial thromboplastin time of 34 s (reference range 25–37 s), and increased fibrinogen at 6.59 g/L (reference range 2.00–3.93 g/L), typical of the early postoperative period since fibrinogen, an acute phase reactant, is expected to be substantially elevated shortly after surgery. A blood sample obtained on POD5 was submitted to a reference laboratory for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibody analysis.

Additional laboratory studies showed unremarkable chemistry values (urea, creatinine, alanine transaminase, and bilirubin). Blood and urine cultures were obtained. The chest radiograph was unremarkable.

Examination of the peripheral blood smear on POD7 verified thrombocytopenia, with no platelet clumping and lacking both schistocytes (red cell fragments) and abnormal or ‘toxic’ white cell features. The screening HIT immunoassay (PF4/polyanion ELISA) was markedly positive, measuring 2.2 optical density (OD) units (reference range <0.4 OD units), whereas the confirmatory platelet activation test (serotonin-release assay, SRA) gave an ‘indeterminate’ outcome (addressed later).

Differential diagnosis:

Typical causes of marked thrombocytopenia after cardiac surgery, including cardiogenic shock, critical illness, or infection, were deemed improbable due to the patient's stable hemodynamics and negative results on blood and urine cultures. Less common etiologies, such as post-transfusion purpura (with no history of transfusion), postoperative thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (supported by normal haptoglobin levels and initially absent schistocytes), and drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia were also unlikely because the timeline of platelet count decline did not correlate with newly started perioperative medications. The medications introduced only on the day of surgery (vancomycin, cefazolin, and tranexamic acid) and those administered during the postoperative phase (furosemide, metoprolol, pantoprazole, and midodrine) were therefore considered unlikely causes.

While the rapid decrease in platelet count appeared inconsistent with the timing of unfractionated heparin (UFH) used intraoperatively during cardiopulmonary bypass, further review revealed that UFH had been given during preoperative cardiac catheterization. Given the combination of thrombotic stroke and a strongly positive PF4/polyanion ELISA result, heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) was considered the leading diagnosis, most likely incited by the preoperative UFH exposure. Additional clarification regarding the ‘indeterminate’ SRA results was sought from the McMaster Platelet Immunology Laboratory, which performed the assay.

The SRA findings underwent thorough evaluation. Indicative of platelet-activating HIT antibodies, the patient’s serum obtained on postoperative day 5 elicited marked serotonin release at 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL UFH, registering 97% and 99% respectively (reference range <20%), along with an absence of serotonin release (0%) when exposed to a supratherapeutic UFH concentration (100 U/mL); failure to trigger platelet activation at elevated heparin levels is typical of HIT and reflects the inability of PF4 immune complexes to form under these conditions. Nonetheless, two additional aspects were observed in the SRA data. First, the patient’s serum prompted robust serotonin release without UFH (96% at 0 U/mL heparin; RR <20%), a hallmark of autoimmune HIT (aHIT) rather than classic heparin-dependent HIT. Second, when the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody (IV.3) was added, complete inhibition of serotonin release did not occur with patient serum at 0.1 U/mL UFH; instead, serotonin release remained at 76% in the presence of IV.3, not dropping below 20% as is typically expected in HIT cases. This led to an initial classification of the SRA as ‘indeterminate’. Ultimately, it was determined that the patient had aHIT, with highly reactive HIT antibodies that produced only partial inhibition by IV.3, diverging from typical HIT antibody behavior. This particular SRA response pattern appears to be novel, as it has not previously been documented in the literature.

Treatment:

Prophylactic fondaparinux was stopped, and the patient began therapeutic argatroban, a direct thrombin inhibitor, at 0.75 mcg/kg/min, aiming for a PTT between 55–85 s. While therapeutic doses of fondaparinux can be used off-label to treat HIT effectively, the hematologist opted for argatroban due to its shorter half-life, which would be preferable should bleeding develop, considering the patient’s recent stroke and pronounced thrombocytopenia. Additionally, high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin was administered: 100 g on each of two consecutive days, followed by a third 100 g dose. This escalation was in response to the absence of platelet count rise after the initial two days of IVIg treatment. Serial fibrinogen and d-dimer measurements were performed to indirectly assess anticoagulant effectiveness, with increased fibrinogen and decreased d-dimer suggesting control of HIT-related hypercoagulability by anticoagulation.

During the initial phase of therapy, there was a gradual reduction in fibrinogen levels from postoperative day 6 (POD6) through postoperative day 17 (POD17), reaching a lowest point of 2.49 g/L. D-dimer concentrations peaked around POD12, and peripheral blood smears identified schistocytes between POD9 and POD16. Collectively, these findings indicated a pronounced, advancing consumptive coagulopathy secondary to HIT. In addition, the patient demonstrated only a slight rise in platelet count after high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin administration, increasing from a pre-treatment level of 13×10^9/L (POD7) to a temporary maximum of 54×10^9/L (POD14). This represents a significantly less pronounced platelet recovery compared to the typical response observed in aHIT patients treated with high-dose intravenous Ig (for review).

On postoperative day 14, the patient’s anticoagulation therapy was changed from argatroban to therapeutic-dose fondaparinux, administered at 10 mg once daily via subcutaneous injection. The rationale for transitioning to fondaparinux included its simpler dosing schedule—requiring only once-daily subcutaneous administration compared to argatroban’s PTT-adjusted intravenous regimen—and concerns regarding argatroban’s potentially inadequate suppression of hypercoagulability related to HIT, evidenced by declining fibrinogen, increasing d-dimer, and the emergence of schistocytes during argatroban therapy. Since fondaparinux acts as an indirect, antithrombin-dependent inhibitor of factor Xa, AT concentrate (1000 U) was administered prior to initiating fondaparinux because the patient’s antithrombin level before infusion was mildly decreased at 0.70 U/mL (reference range 0.80–1.40 U/mL).

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient's platelet levels steadily returned to normal, measuring 155×10^9/L on POD62 and 202×10^9/L by POD87; he also achieved full neurological recovery. Fondaparinux treatment was continued up to POD65, after which apixaban was restarted at a dose of 5 mg orally twice daily for ongoing anticoagulation. Serial SRA testing remained markedly positive over several months, with subsequent SRA assays displaying a pattern more typical of aHIT (see figure 2). The final blood sample, drawn on POD124 after platelet counts had normalized, demonstrated findings indicative of mainly heparin-dependent HIT antibodies: serotonin release was 15% without UFH but increased to 100% when UFH at 0.3 U/mL was present.

Discussion:

The patient exhibited several uncommon clinical, laboratory, and serological findings, all of which were ultimately explained by a diagnosis of severe autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (aHIT). Initially, the HIT-related drop in platelet count began prior to the typical time frame for HIT in patients following cardiac surgery, which generally occurs between postoperative day 5 (POD5) and postoperative day 10 (POD10). Approximately 10% of cardiac surgery patients who develop HIT experience a decrease in platelets before POD5, a pattern associated with recent preoperative heparin administration, as observed in this case.

Additionally, the patient reached an atypically low platelet nadir of 13×10^9/L, accompanied by declining fibrinogen levels, elevated d-dimer concentrations, and the presence of schistocytes while on argatroban anticoagulation. These manifestations, characteristic of severe HIT-related hypercoagulability, serve as evidence for a diagnosis of aHIT.

Thirdly, our patient failed to display the rapid resolution of thrombocytopenia often seen when patients with aHIT are treated with high-dose intravenous Ig, indicating a particularly severe variant of this condition. In fact, serological findings suggesting a rare, severe subtype of aHIT were observed—namely, only partial suppression of platelet activation in vitro with monoclonal antibody IV.3. To our knowledge, this new serological pattern has not previously been described in the context of aHIT, and recognizing it may help prevent missed diagnoses, which could lead to negative clinical outcomes. Despite this, the diagnosis of aHIT was ultimately established based on at least three factors. First, testing with serum collected later during the extended period of platelet count recovery demonstrated some SRA reactivity patterns more in line with aHIT. Second, the final sample tested exhibited a heparin-dependent SRA profile consistent with classical HIT, suggesting a reduction of aHIT antibodies. Third, no alternative diagnosis could sufficiently account for the patient’s atypical clinical presentation. Fortunately, the patient experienced a full clinical recovery, with no lasting neurological deficits.
