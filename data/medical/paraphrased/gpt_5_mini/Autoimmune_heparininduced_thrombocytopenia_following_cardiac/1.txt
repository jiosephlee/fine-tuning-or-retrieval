Title: Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery

Rosanne St Bernard, Ishac Nazy, Theodore Earl Warkentin

Abstract:

An older male patient, following cardiac surgery conducted under heparin anticoagulation, experienced a progressive decline in platelet counts, which dropped to 50×10⁹/L by postoperative day 5 (POD5). This decline prompted assessment for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibodies. Brain imaging detected a parietal lobe stroke. The initial immunoassay for HIT antibodies returned a strong positive, while the serotonin-release assay displayed an unusual pattern: marked serotonin release occurred at 0, 0.1, and 0.3 U/mL heparin concentrations, with only partial inhibition observed upon the addition of a platelet Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody. Anticoagulant therapy was transitioned to argatroban combined with high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin. Ongoing signs of hypercoagulability necessitated further modification to fondaparinux, after which the platelet count increased incrementally (155×10⁹/L by POD62), and neurological symptoms subsided. Further laboratory testing identified HIT antibodies possessing platelet-activating activity independent of heparin, consistent with autoimmune HIT (aHIT). Distinctive features of this aHIT case included both marked thrombocytopenia (platelet nadir at 13×10⁹/L) and an atypically early presentation (prior to POD5), likely triggered by heparin administration before surgery, alongside a unique antibody profile exhibiting partial resistance to inhibition via Fc receptor blockade.

Background:

Over the past 70 years, unfractionated heparin (UFH) has served as the primary anticoagulant administered during heart surgeries involving cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB). Its continued use is largely attributable to multiple advantages: effective suppression of haemostatic activation on artificial surfaces, elimination that does not rely on renal or hepatic pathways, the availability of intraoperative anticoagulation monitoring through activated clotting time, and affordability. Despite these benefits, a major complication associated with UFH is heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT), a well-characterized immune-mediated reaction. After cardiac surgery, HIT occurs in approximately 0.5%–1.0% of patients, with the risk varying according to the duration and specific type of postoperative heparin-based thromboprophylaxis (UFH compared to low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH)). Of particular clinical significance is the marked increase in thrombotic events associated with HIT, reflected by an approximate 15-fold elevated relative risk.

Traditionally, HIT has been characterized as a syndrome of pronounced platelet activation resulting from the engagement of platelet Fc receptors by immune complexes formed from immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibodies. These antibodies specifically target platelet factor 4 (PF4) when complexed with polyanionic heparin. Recently, it has become apparent that a subset of patients may exhibit atypical, often more severe clinical manifestations, due to HIT antibodies that possess the ability to activate platelets independently of heparin exposure. This variant has been designated ‘autoimmune HIT’ (aHIT) (see review). In this paper, we present a case of aHIT demonstrating profound and sustained thrombocytopenia, further complicated by the occurrence of a thrombotic stroke after cardiac surgery. The described case contributes important new perspectives to the understanding of aHIT, as it shows an exceptionally severe serological profile characterized by only partial inhibition with Fc receptor blockade. Moreover, this report underscores the complexities inherent in diagnosing early postoperative thrombocytopenia, since the rapid development of aHIT in this patient was attributable to antecedent exposure to UFH prior to surgery. The patient ultimately achieved a positive clinical outcome.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his 70s, who had experienced exertional dyspnoea for two years and had a longstanding history of atrial fibrillation (managed with oral apixaban 5 mg twice daily), was newly identified as having a systolic murmur during examination. Prior to diagnostic heart catheterisation, he had not discontinued his apixaban regimen. Given this, the interventional cardiologist chose a transradial artery approach for catheterisation, administering a reduced intraprocedural anticoagulant dose (specifically, 2000 U of UFH). Angiographic evaluation demonstrated a 60% stenosis of the obtuse marginal coronary artery, moderate aortic stenosis, and an enlarged ascending aorta; cardiac surgery was planned for the following week.

The patient subsequently underwent cardiac surgery utilizing CPB, with anticoagulation maintained by 45 000 U of UFH. The operative procedures comprised replacement of the aortic valve with a #27 bioprosthesis, ascending aorta replacement using a #30 Gelweave graft, single bypass grafting of the obtuse marginal coronary artery with a saphenous vein graft, and excision of the left atrial appendage. The surgical course was straightforward, with minimal bleeding and no requirement for blood transfusion; the patient was weaned from bypass with stable and optimal haemodynamics.

The patient's platelet count immediately prior to surgery measured 210×10^9/L (reference range (RR) 150–400). On postoperative day 1 (POD1), this dropped to 155×10^9/L, indicating a relatively slight decrease for the context of cardiac surgery. Standard postoperative thromboprophylaxis with LMWH was initiated on POD1 using 5000 U of dalteparin via subcutaneous injection. Higher doses (7500 U) were subsequently administered on POD2, POD3, and POD4. Surprisingly, the platelet count continued to decrease steadily from POD1 to POD5, reaching 50×10^9/L (see figure 1). Clinically, the patient was stable aside from experiencing postoperative delirium. Dalteparin was withheld on POD5, and instead, fondaparinux at a prophylactic dose (2.5 mg) was started (initial dose given on POD5 at 18:00).

Despite no overt focal neurological deficits, the worsening postoperative delirium—together with a further reduction in platelet count to 26×10^9/L (confirmed at 24×10^9/L)—led to brain CT imaging on POD6. The scan showed an acute cerebral infarct, characterized by a hypodense area measuring 3.2 cm×1.8 cm in the left parietal lobe, extending through the cerebral cortex.

Investigations:

On postoperative day 5 (POD5), the complete blood count showed a platelet count of 50×10^9/L, accompanied by a haemoglobin measurement of 97 g/L (reference range [RR] 135–180 g/L), which was comparable to values recorded over the prior three days. This stable trend in haemoglobin levels aligns with anticipated early surgical blood loss and indicates no evidence of active bleeding. The corresponding white blood cell count measured 7.8×10^9/L (RR 4.0–11.0×10^9/L), with a normal differential profile. The reticulocyte count, recorded at 73×10^9/L (RR 10–100×10^9/L), was within the expected range. Haptoglobin was elevated at 3.19 g/L (RR 0.32–1.97 g/L), supporting the absence of significant haemolysis. The patient's serum vitamin B12 was restored to normal concentrations.

Assessment of coagulation parameters revealed a normal prothrombin time and an international normalised ratio of 1.1 (RR 0.9–1.1). The partial thromboplastin time (PTT) was also within normal limits at 34 s (RR 25–37 s). Fibrinogen levels, however, were notably raised at 6.59 g/L (RR 2.00–3.93 g/L), which is expected during the acute postoperative phase due to its function as an acute phase reactant. A sample obtained on POD5 was submitted to a reference laboratory for HIT antibody testing.

Further laboratory investigations, including renal and liver biochemistry (urea, creatinine, alanine transaminase, and bilirubin), were all within normal parameters. Blood and urine cultures were obtained, and chest radiography showed no significant pathological findings.

Examination of the peripheral blood film on POD7 confirmed ongoing thrombocytopenia, with no evidence of platelet aggregates, red cell fragments (schistocytes), or abnormal changes in white blood cells. HIT screening using the PF4/polyanion enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) demonstrated a marked positive result at 2.2 optical density (OD) units (RR <0.4 OD units), while the confirmatory serotonin-release assay (SRA) produced an ‘indeterminate’ outcome, to be discussed further below.

Differential diagnosis:

Frequent causes of significant thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery, including cardiogenic shock, critical illness, and infection, appeared improbable based on the observed clinical course and laboratory data (hemodynamics remained stable; cultures from blood and urine showed no evidence of infection). In addition, less common etiologies such as post-transfusion purpura (in the absence of any transfused blood products), post-operative thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (supported by normal haptoglobin levels and a lack of schistocytes initially), and drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia (the decline in platelets occurred too promptly for recently introduced postoperative medications, which included vancomycin, cefazolin, and tranexamic acid on the day of surgery, and furosemide, metoprolol, pantoprazole, and midodrine in the following period), were also considered improbable.

While the precipitous drop in platelets occurred too early to implicate the intraoperative UFH exposure during CPB, subsequent investigation revealed that UFH had been administered during a preoperative heart catheterization procedure. Coupled with the patient’s thrombotic stroke and the strong positive result of the PF4/polyanion ELISA, HIT was considered the most likely diagnosis, most probably induced by the UFH received during preoperative catheterization. Additional clarification about the ‘indeterminate’ SRA result was sought from the reporting institution, the McMaster Platelet Immunology Laboratory.

The SRA findings were subjected to a detailed reassessment. In alignment with the presence of platelet-activating HIT antibodies, the patient’s postoperative day 5 (POD5) serum induced robust serotonin release at both 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL UFH (97% and 99%, respectively; reference range [RR] <20% release). Conversely, no serotonin release (0%) was observed when exposed to a supratherapeutic concentration (100 U/mL) of UFH, which is typical for HIT and is attributed to the incapacity of PF4-immune complexes to form at such elevated UFH concentrations. Notably, two additional observations emerged from the SRA analysis. Firstly, the patient’s serum elicited significant serotonin release in the complete absence of UFH (96% release at 0 U/mL heparin; RR <20%), a finding that is indicative of aHIT rather than conventional heparin-dependent HIT. Secondly, introducing the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody IV.3 did not fully suppress serotonin release in the presence of the patient’s serum with 0.1 U/mL UFH: a release of 76% persisted despite IV.3, as opposed to the anticipated suppression to below 20% seen with classic HIT antibodies. This atypical response led to an initial classification of the SRA result as ‘indeterminate.’ The interpretation, however, was that the patient was indeed affected by aHIT, but possessed unusually reactive HIT antibodies, resulting in only partial inhibition by IV.3. To our knowledge, such an unconventional SRA profile has not been previously documented.

Treatment:

Prophylactic-dose fondaparinux was stopped, and the patient was started on therapeutic-dose argatroban, a direct thrombin inhibitor, at a rate of 0.75 mcg/kg/min with a target PTT of 55–85 s. While therapeutic dosing of fondaparinux is recognized as an effective ‘off-label’ anticoagulation strategy for HIT, the hematologist managing the case opted for argatroban, which has a shorter half-life, to better address potential bleeding complications, especially considering the patient's recent stroke and pronounced thrombocytopenia. Furthermore, high-dose intravenous Ig was administered—initially 100 g daily for two days and subsequently a third 100 g infusion—after observing no significant platelet count increase during the first two days of treatment. Repeated measurements of fibrinogen and d-dimer levels were performed to indirectly monitor the effectiveness of anticoagulation therapy, with anticipated trends being rising fibrinogen and declining d-dimer values as indicators of adequate control of HIT-related hypercoagulability.

The patient's initial management period was marked by a steady reduction in fibrinogen concentrations between POD6 and POD17, with the lowest recorded value being 2.49 g/L. D-dimer values peaked near POD12, and peripheral blood smears revealed the presence of schistocytes from POD9 through POD16. Collectively, these observations indicated a severe, advancing consumptive coagulopathy linked to HIT. In addition, despite high-dose intravenous Ig therapy, there was only a slight elevation in the platelet count, rising from 13×10^9/L on POD7 prior to Ig administration to a transient maximum of 54×10^9/L on POD14. This increment was substantially less than what is typically observed in patients with aHIT following high-dose intravenous Ig, as described in the literature.

On postoperative day 14 (POD14), the patient’s anticoagulation regimen was changed from argatroban to fondaparinux at a therapeutic dose of 10 mg administered subcutaneously once daily. The rationale for selecting fondaparinux included its straightforward dosing schedule—requiring only once-daily subcutaneous injections—unlike argatroban, which necessitates continuous intravenous infusion with PTT-based dose adjustments. Additionally, there were concerns that argatroban might not have been adequately managing the patient’s HIT-associated hypercoagulable state, as evidenced by persistently declining fibrinogen levels, increasing d-dimer concentrations, and the detection of schistocytes during argatroban therapy. Since fondaparinux exerts its anticoagulant effect as an indirect antithrombin (AT)-dependent inhibitor of factor Xa, 1000 U of AT concentrate was administered prior to initiating fondaparinux due to a slightly decreased pre-infusion AT level of 0.70 U/mL (reference range 0.80–1.40 U/mL).

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient's platelet levels gradually returned to the normal range (155×10^9/L on POD62 and 202×10^9/L on POD87), and the patient experienced full neurological recovery. Fondaparinux therapy was continued until POD65, after which apixaban was restarted at 5 mg orally twice daily for ongoing anticoagulation management. Serial SRA tests remained strongly positive for several months, with subsequent SRA results demonstrating a profile more typical of aHIT (figure 2). The most recent blood sample, collected at POD124 after platelet normalization, indicated primarily heparin-dependent HIT antibodies—showing 15% serotonin release without UFH, but 100% release when UFH was present at 0.3 U/mL.

Discussion:

This patient exhibited multiple uncommon clinical, laboratory, and serological characteristics, all of which were ultimately consistent with a diagnosis of severe aHIT. To begin with, the development of HIT-associated thrombocytopenia in this individual occurred earlier than the typical timeframe for HIT—usually POD5 to POD10 following cardiac surgery. Fewer than 10% of cardiac surgery patients who manifest HIT experience a HIT-related decrease in platelet count prior to POD5, a scenario that is often associated with recent exposure to heparin before surgery, as was observed in our patient.

In addition, this patient’s platelet count declined to an exceptionally low nadir of $13 \times 10^9/\mathrm{L}$, accompanied by decreased fibrinogen, increased d-dimer levels, and the presence of schistocytes while on argatroban anticoagulation. Such markers of marked HIT-induced hypercoagulability are characteristic indicators of aHIT.

Third, unlike the rapid improvement in thrombocytopenia commonly observed with administration of high-dose intravenous Ig in cases of aHIT, our patient’s platelet count did not recover swiftly, suggesting a particularly severe variant of the condition. Notably, the serological profile—characterized by only partial inhibition of platelet activation in vitro in the presence of the monoclonal antibody IV.3—indicates an unusually severe subtype of aHIT. To our knowledge, this unique serological response has not been previously described in the context of aHIT, and recognition of this pattern may prevent missed diagnoses that could result in unfavorable outcomes. Despite the atypical presentation, the diagnosis of aHIT was eventually established in our case, supported by several key findings. First, analysis of follow-up sera obtained during the extended phase of platelet count normalization revealed some SRA reactivity patterns more aligned with classic presentations of aHIT. Second, the final sample examined demonstrated a heparin-dependent SRA response typical of classic HIT, suggesting a decline in aHIT antibody activity. Third, alternative explanations for the patient’s unusual clinical progression were lacking. Ultimately, the patient experienced a complete clinical recovery without persistent neurological deficits.
