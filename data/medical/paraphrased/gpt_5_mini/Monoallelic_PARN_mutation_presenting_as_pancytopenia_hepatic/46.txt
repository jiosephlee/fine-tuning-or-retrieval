Title: Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

Jinal Soni, Karun Saathveeg Sam, Vinus Taneja, Pooja Khosla

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late twenties presented with progressive fatigue and generalized weakness lasting five years. Physical assessment noted premature signs of aging, anemia, neuropathy, and enlargement of the liver and spleen. Laboratory findings demonstrated pancytopenia, dimorphic anemia, marked vitamin B12 deficiency, along with hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. Although nutritional deficits were addressed, the presence of cytopenia, early greying of hair, osteoporosis, as well as fibrosis of liver and lungs suggested the possibility of a genetic condition. Clinical exome sequencing detected a monoallelic PARN missense mutation (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg), which was reported as a Variant of Uncertain Significance. Germline pathogenic variants in PARN have been linked to dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), and to telomere-associated pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure 4. The alignment of clinical and genetic findings in this individual was consistent with a diagnosis of telomere biology disorder (TBD). This report underscores the need to consider TBDs in young adults with unexplained multisystem involvement, even in the absence of traditional mucocutaneous manifestations. Early identification is essential for genetic counseling, ongoing surveillance, and evaluating bone marrow transplantation in cases with progressive disease.

Background:

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) are uncommon syndromes characterized by accelerated aging, resulting from inherited mutations in genes associated with telomere function, which produce unusually short or impaired telomeres. The principal tissues impacted are those with high cellular turnover, such as bone marrow, epidermis, mucous membranes, hair follicles, and liver cells. TBD encompasses a variety of clinical presentations, with dyskeratosis congenita (DC) serving as the archetypal disorder. Additional forms include Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome.

PARN mutations are associated with unique clinical features among these disorders, with two pathogenic alleles resulting in autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), whereas single allele mutations are more frequently linked to telomere-associated pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4).

We report a female in her late twenties presenting with persistent fatigue and involvement of multiple organ systems, ultimately found to have a telomere biology disorder.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late twenties was referred to our tertiary care facility due to a five-year progression of worsening generalized weakness and early onset fatigue. During this period, her functional abilities deteriorated, limiting her participation in daily tasks relative to individuals her age. In the preceding three months, her weakness intensified further, hindering her capacity for self-care and necessitating help. Additional symptoms included shortness of breath on exertion, occasional chest pain, weight loss, and loss of appetite.

On physical assessment, her haemodynamic status was stable. Significant observations consisted of pallor, icterus, early greying of hair, hyperpigmentation of the knuckles, and a bald tongue exhibiting atrophy of the lingual papillae.

The musculoskeletal evaluation demonstrated thoracic scoliosis along with atrophy of the thenar muscles on both sides. Abdominal assessment revealed an enlarged liver palpable 5 cm beneath the right costal margin, with a span of 18 cm. The respiratory examination identified diminished breath sounds in both infra-scapular areas. Findings from the cardiovascular examination were within normal limits.

Investigations:

Initial laboratory testing identified pancytopenia accompanied by a reduced reticulocyte percentage (0.39%). The peripheral blood smear displayed dimorphic red blood cells, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and neutrophils with hypersegmented nuclei—indicative of dysfunctional erythropoiesis. Additional serum analysis revealed profound deficiencies of vitamin B12 and folate. Iron panel showed increased levels of serum iron and ferritin, supporting a diagnosis of ineffective erythropoiesis (table 1).

Assessment of bone marrow demonstrated trilineage hematopoietic activity with marked erythroid proliferation and heightened iron content, while no malignancy, dysplastic changes, or infiltrative processes were identified; cytogenetic testing returned normal results.

Haemolysis markers included raised lactate dehydrogenase and elevated indirect bilirubin. Direct antiglobulin testing (DAT) was positive for IgG (2+), whereas the indirect test yielded a negative result. In spite of these findings, the subdued reticulocyte response along with the bone marrow characteristics favored ineffective erythropoiesis as the predominant mechanism, rather than destruction mediated by immune processes in the peripheral blood.

In order to assess for gastrointestinal malabsorption, an upper GI endoscopy was undertaken, revealing a grossly normal appearance. Histological evaluation of duodenal tissue identified mild chronic gastritis with a diminished number of parietal cells, a finding that may underlie the patient's B12 malabsorption through decreased intrinsic factor secretion. Despite this, the clinical manifestations appeared too pronounced to be attributed solely to nutritional deficiency.

Given evidence of liver impairment, additional diagnostic procedures were pursued. Abdominal ultrasonography demonstrated both hepatomegaly and splenomegaly. Transient elastography indicated grade II hepatic steatosis, with a controlled attenuation parameter measured at 295 dB/m, and features consistent with cirrhosis, as the liver stiffness value was 18.1 kPa. There was an elevation in inflammatory markers. Autoimmune testing, including antinuclear antibody and autoantibodies relevant to autoimmune hepatitis, yielded negative results. Evaluation for infectious etiologies—encompassing viral hepatitis, HIV, cytomegalovirus, and scrub typhus—was predominantly negative, with the exception of a positive Epstein-Barr virus IgG (see table 2).

Positron emission tomography–CT was utilized to assess for hidden infiltrative or inflammatory conditions. The imaging revealed uneven ¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose accumulation within both axial and appendicular bone marrow compartments. There were widespread ground-glass opacities, numerous fibrotic regions, and subpleural atelectatic changes throughout both lungs. Additional findings included hepatosplenomegaly and ascites. No signs suggestive of malignancy or granulomatous disease were observed (figures 1 and 2).

Other notable results consisted of secondary hyperparathyroidism, electrolyte imbalances such as hypocalcaemia and hypokalaemia, vitamin D deficiency, and osteoporosis verified by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry. These abnormalities accounted for her repeated episodes of bone pain and fractures (see table 3). Electromyography along with nerve conduction studies demonstrated an asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy characterized by chronic neurogenic alterations in both proximal and distal muscle groups.

Considering the combination of cytopenia, hepatic dysfunction, premature greying, osteoporosis, and neuropathy, a syndromic or genetic cause was considered likely.

Clinical exome sequencing revealed a heterozygous missense mutation in the PARN gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg, exon 8). This missense change affects a highly conserved amino acid and is predicted to negatively impact protein function. Nonetheless, its contribution to the disease has not been established and thus it has been categorized as a ‘Variant of Uncertain Significance’. While the pathogenic nature of this particular variant is unclear, PARN involvement combined with the patient’s presentation aligns with the features of the TBD spectrum.

Differential diagnosis:

The combination of pancytopenia, involuntary weight loss, premature greying, and fatigue prompted evaluation for hematologic, nutritional, and autoimmune etiologies. The substantially decreased reticulocyte count indicated impaired erythropoiesis, which led to initial investigation of nutrient deficiencies and bone marrow failure syndromes.

Early identification of profound vitamin B12 deficiency explained several clinical findings and was supported by bone marrow signs of ineffective erythropoiesis. Despite correcting these deficits, the overall systemic dysfunction persisted and remained out of proportion to what would be expected.

An immune-mediated cause was briefly considered, given a positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) and elevated inflammatory indices. Nonetheless, the absence of haemolysis, negative autoimmune panels, and lack of typical systemic manifestations argued against autoimmune cytopenia or connective tissue disorder.

The cause of hepatic fibrosis (HF) was indeterminate, as both infectious and autoimmune origins were ruled out. The presence of premature aging signs, chronic cytopenia, and fibrosis affecting both the liver and lungs in a young individual raised the likelihood of an unrecognized genetic condition. Despite no reported family history, the consideration of a hereditary syndrome persisted, as a de novo mutation could not be excluded.

This raised the possibility of a telomere biology disorder capable of resembling acquired diseases and exhibiting heterogeneous penetrance. While uncommon, such disorders may not show the typical mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita, but rather present more discreetly with bone marrow insufficiency, fibrotic changes in organs, and signs of premature ageing.

Genetic testing confirmed this suspicion by revealing a disease-causing heterozygous variant in the PARN gene, which accounted for her spectrum of clinical manifestations.

Treatment:

The patient received intravenous cyanocobalamin at a dose of 1000 µg once daily for five days, then transitioned to weekly administration over four weeks, and subsequently continued on monthly maintenance therapy to address marked deficiency.

Additional supportive measures comprised whole blood transfusions for symptomatic anemia, oral folic acid at 1 mg daily, and vitamin D at 60 000 IU weekly along with 1000 mg elemental calcium daily for osteoporosis management. Electrolyte disturbances were managed with routine supplementation.

The patient participated in physiotherapy sessions, and after confirmation of a TBD diagnosis, her family was provided with genetic counseling.

Outcome and follow-up:

After deficiencies were addressed and supportive measures implemented, the patient experienced relief of her symptoms. At her 2-week follow-up appointment, hematologic parameters remained within stable ranges, her fatigue lessened, and there were no emerging clinical issues.

She remains under routine outpatient monitoring to assess hepatic and pulmonary status, as well as to observe for any advancement of bone marrow or systemic disease involvement.

Discussion:

Telomeres are repetitive DNA sequences located at the ends of chromosomes that serve to protect chromosomal stability by preventing degradation. During repeated cellular divisions, telomeres progressively shorten until they reach a threshold length, at which point cellular aging or programmed cell death is initiated. In normal physiology, telomerase—a ribonucleoprotein enzyme comprising telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and telomerase RNA component (TERC)—counteracts this shortening by extending telomeres. Nevertheless, disease-causing mutations affecting the genes responsible for the telomerase complex components disrupt the enzyme's activity, resulting in accelerated telomere shortening and giving rise to a set of inherited disorders known as TBD.

Pathogenic variants in DKC1 inherited in an X-linked recessive manner were the first to connect dyskeratosis congenita (DC) with notably shortened telomeres, establishing DC as the archetype for telomere biology disorders (TBDs). DC is typically characterized by a mucocutaneous triad: oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular pigmentation of the skin, or by the presence of two of these features along with bone marrow failure syndrome (BMFS). Additional well-recognized manifestations comprise idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), hepatic fibrosis (HF), and an elevated risk for both hematological and solid tumors. Severe TBD subtypes with onset during childhood—including Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome—also arise from genetic variants affecting genes associated with DC.

TBDs can manifest as isolated mucocutaneous symptoms, involvement of specific organs, or haematological abnormalities, and may appear in adult patients who do not display the classic DC triad. The underlying hereditary defect is frequently concealed by disorders such as aplastic anaemia, bone marrow failure syndromes (BMFS), idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), or hepatic failure (HF), as well as gastrointestinal disorders. Due to the absence of the traditional triad and delayed onset of symptoms, affected individuals are often incorrectly diagnosed, particularly when evaluated outside of specialized centres. Monoallelic mutations in TERT, TERC, and RTEL1 are most commonly found in TBD cases, which frequently present as IPF or BMFS.

The PARN gene, a member of a well-conserved exoribonuclease family, functions to regulate gene expression by catalyzing the deadenylation process, thereby reducing the length of the mRNA poly(A) tail. High-throughput sequencing of the TERC RNA's 3'-terminal revealed that PARN is essential for excising oligo(A) tails added after transcription, which mark nuclear RNAs for degradation; this step is necessary for the proper maturation of the TERC's 3' end.

PARN is categorized within the 20% of genes that exhibit the lowest tolerance for rare, disruptive mutations, reinforcing its vital function and the principle of haploinsufficiency in individuals carrying these variants. Pathogenic germline alterations in PARN are established contributors to dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), as well as the wider DC/TBD spectrum. Cases of HH syndrome have been attributed to biallelic mutations in PARN. Single germline pathogenic alleles have been associated with telomere-related PFBMFT4. The association between PARN and IPF was initially noted following the discovery of new monoallelic variants; additional investigations identified rare monoallelic PARN variants in five out of 262 sporadic IPF patients subjected to whole-exome sequencing, suggesting a new relationship now documented in over 65 published cases.1517 Nonetheless, the complete spectrum of clinical manifestations related to PARN mutations is not fully delineated, owing to the scarcity of cases described to date.

Of particular interest, the patient possessed a heterozygous missense alteration in PARN, c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg), impacting a residue that is highly conserved within the R3H domain (amino acids 178–245). Analysis with five different in silico missense prediction algorithms indicated that four—LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, and SIFT—forecast this variant to have detrimental functional consequences. According to ACMG/AMP guidelines for variant interpretation, this mutation fulfills several supporting criteria: PM1, as the change occurs in a critical, well-characterized functional region (R3H domain) and no benign variants are known at this site; PM2, since the variant is either absent or exceedingly infrequent in population databases; PP3, given consistent predictions of deleterious impact from several computational tools; and PP2, due to the rarity of benign missense variants in PARN and the established pathogenicity mechanism involving missense changes in this gene. While its pathogenic significance is still uncertain, the association with TBDs is bolstered by PARN’s known involvement and similarities with other missense variants in PARN, such as p.Lys59Arg (ClinVar ID: VCV000424063.2) and p.Ala383Val (ClinVar ID: VCV000180661.1), which ClinVar classifies as ‘likely pathogenic’.

TBDs can exhibit significant variability due to involvement of multiple genes, diverse modes of inheritance, incomplete penetrance, and the possibility of somatic genetic rescue affecting individual organ systems to different extents. Furthermore, environmental exposures and lifestyle choices may alter the replicative abilities across organs. In our patient's case, such intricacies may account for the phenotype seen, despite only identifying a monoallelic PARN variant.

Monoallelic mutations in PARN are more often linked to idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) and are less associated with primary hematological disorders. However, the presence of cytopenia in this patient suggests the potential existence of an underlying TBD that could make the bone marrow more vulnerable to hematopoietic challenges like profound vitamin B12 deficiency. Shortened telomeres impair the proliferative function of hematopoietic stem cells, thereby accelerating cellular aging and raising the risk for bone marrow failure syndromes (BMFS). Although the cytopenia observed was most likely due to a significant deficiency of vitamin B12, the detected PARN variant highlights the necessity for sustained clinical and hematological surveillance to monitor for any signs of developing marrow failure phenotype in this individual with a genetic predisposition.

Cirrhosis related to telomere biology disorders (TBD) has historically been underestimated, but recent data indicate it may be more common than previously thought. A retrospective study from the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry, utilizing transient elastography, reported that 88.8% of adults with pathogenic mutations in TERC or TERT exhibited at least moderate hepatic fibrosis (HF), and 33.3% had cirrhosis. Additionally, a similar case described in the literature involved a patient presenting with pancytopenia and HF, who possessed monoallelic variants in PARN (c.1613C>G) and TERT (c.2476G>A). In comparison, our patient carried only a single monoallelic PARN mutation, yet demonstrated both hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. While monoallelic PARN mutations are typically linked with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), the effect on telomerase activity may contribute to HF observed with TERT or TERC mutations. These observations highlight a broader clinical presentation for PARN-related TBDs, emphasizing that HF should be considered a feature of its phenotype.

The definitive diagnosis of suspected TBD relies on genetic analysis utilizing next-generation sequencing and on assessment of telomere length via flow cytometry-based fluorescence in situ hybridisation assays. Management remains largely supportive, focusing on addressing symptom burden, requirements for organ transplantation, and reduction of risk factors; haematopoietic stem cell transplantation offers a possible cure in cases presenting with haematologic involvement. Although limited preclinical and early clinical investigations indicate that androgens such as danazol and nandrolone may promote telomere extension, the durability of these effects over time has not been established.

The patient exhibited symptoms consistent with TBD, HF, and IPF. After a thorough assessment ruling out typical etiologies, whole-exome sequencing revealed the pathogenic gene responsible, and a comprehensive review of the literature expanded our knowledge of this uncommon clinical scenario. Management was primarily supportive, and her relatives received genetic counselling. She was informed about her diagnosis and acknowledged that definitive treatment was unavailable. During her follow-up two weeks later, hematologic parameters were unchanged, and no additional evidence of worsening clinical status was observed.
