Title: Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

Jinal Soni, Karun Saathveeg Sam, Vinus Taneja, Pooja Khosla

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late twenties presented with a five-year history of worsening fatigue and generalized weakness. Physical examination demonstrated features of premature aging, anemia, neuropathy, and enlargement of both the liver and spleen. Laboratory evaluation revealed pancytopenia, dimorphic anemia, marked deficiency of vitamin B12, as well as evidence of hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. Although nutritional deficiencies were addressed, the combination of persistent cytopenias, early greying, osteoporosis, and fibrotic involvement of the liver and lungs prompted consideration of an inherited disorder. Clinical exome analysis identified a heterozygous missense variant in PARN (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg), which was categorized as a Variant of Uncertain Significance. Pathogenic germline mutations in PARN have been linked to dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), as well as to telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure 4. The observed phenotype in conjunction with genetic findings supported the diagnosis of a telomere biology disorder (TBD). This case underscores the necessity of considering TBDs in younger individuals with otherwise unexplained, multisystem involvement, even in the absence of classic mucocutaneous manifestations. Early identification is vital for appropriate genetic counselling, monitoring, and evaluation for possible bone marrow transplantation in progressive cases.

Background:

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) constitute uncommon syndromes characterized by accelerated aging due to inherited defects in genes involved with telomere function, resulting in telomeres that are either excessively short or defective. The main cells impacted are those with high turnover, including hematopoietic cells in the bone marrow, epithelial cells of the skin, mucosal surfaces, hair follicles, and hepatocytes. TBD encompasses a variety of clinical presentations, with dyskeratosis congenita (DC) serving as the classic example. Additional conditions falling within this category are Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome.

Within this spectrum of disorders, mutations in PARN are associated with unique clinical presentations; individuals with biallelic PARN variants develop autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), whereas those with monoallelic variants are typically linked to telomere-associated pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4).

We report the case of a female in her late twenties who presented with chronic fatigue and symptoms affecting multiple organ systems, and was subsequently found to have a telomere biology disorder (TBD).

Case presentation:

A female patient in her late twenties was referred to our tertiary centre after experiencing a 5-year progression of generalized weakness and early onset fatigue. During this period, her level of functioning deteriorated, diminishing her capacity for day-to-day tasks when compared to individuals her age. In the preceding 3 months, the severity of her weakness escalated, interfering with self-care and necessitating help. Additional symptoms included exertional shortness of breath, episodic chest pain, weight loss, and diminished appetite.

Upon physical assessment, her haemodynamic parameters were within normal limits. Significant clinical features comprised pallor, icterus, early onset greying of hair, hyperpigmentation of the knuckles, and a smooth tongue lacking lingual papillae.

Evaluation of the musculoskeletal system demonstrated thoracic scoliosis along with atrophy of both thenar eminences. Abdominal assessment identified an enlarged liver palpable 5 cm beneath the right costal margin with a total span of 18 cm. Respiratory examination noted diminished breath sounds bilaterally in the infra-scapular regions. Cardiovascular findings were unremarkable.

Investigations:

Initial laboratory workup indicated pancytopenia accompanied by a decreased reticulocyte percentage (0.39%). Examination of the peripheral blood smear identified the presence of dimorphic red blood cells, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and hypersegmented neutrophils, findings that imply ineffective erythropoiesis. Serum assays demonstrated profound deficiencies in both vitamin B12 and folate. Iron panel showed raised levels of serum iron and ferritin, consistent with ineffective erythropoiesis (table 1).

Bone marrow assessment showed trilineage hematopoiesis with marked erythroid hyperplasia and increased iron reserves, lacking features of neoplastic, dysplastic, or infiltrative pathology; cytogenetic studies returned normal results.

Indicators of hemolysis included increased lactate dehydrogenase and indirect hyperbilirubinemia. The direct antiglobulin test (DAT) yielded a 2+ positivity for IgG, while the indirect test was negative. Nonetheless, the diminished reticulocyte production and marrow findings indicated ineffective erythropoiesis rather than immune-mediated peripheral destruction.

To investigate possible gastrointestinal malabsorption, an upper GI endoscopy was conducted and demonstrated no gross abnormalities. Histopathological analysis of a duodenal biopsy identified mild chronic gastritis with a reduction in parietal cell numbers, which might account for impaired intrinsic factor secretion and subsequent B12 malabsorption. Nevertheless, the clinical presentation was more severe than would be expected from nutritional deficiency alone.

Due to evidence of hepatic dysfunction, additional imaging and evaluation were pursued. Abdominal ultrasonography identified hepatosplenomegaly. Transient elastography indicated grade II hepatic steatosis, with a controlled attenuation parameter of 295 dB/m, and evidence of cirrhosis, with a liver stiffness measurement of 18.1 kPa. Elevated inflammatory markers were observed. Conversely, autoimmune testing—including antinuclear and autoimmune hepatitis antibodies—returned negative results. Evaluation for infectious causes such as viral hepatitis, HIV, cytomegalovirus, and scrub typhus was notable only for Epstein-Barr virus IgG positivity (see table 2).

Positron emission tomography–CT was utilized to assess for underlying infiltrative or inflammatory disorders. The scan demonstrated uneven uptake of ¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose within the bone marrow of both axial and appendicular regions. Both lungs exhibited widespread ground-glass opacities accompanied by numerous fibrotic alterations and subpleural atelectasis. Additionally, hepatosplenomegaly and ascites were observed, although there were no signs indicative of active malignancy or granulomatous disease (figures 1 and 2).

Other notable results were secondary hyperparathyroidism, electrolyte imbalances (hypocalcemia and hypokalemia), vitamin D deficiency, and osteoporosis as demonstrated by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, which accounted for her persistent bone pain and repeated fractures (table 3). Electromyography combined with nerve conduction testing identified an asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy, displaying chronic neurogenic alterations affecting both proximal and distal muscle groups.

The aggregation of features, including cytopenia, impaired liver function, early onset greying of hair, osteoporosis, and neuropathy, prompted consideration of a syndromic or genetic cause.

Exome sequencing revealed a heterozygous missense mutation in the PARN gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg, located in exon 8). This variant affects a residue that is highly conserved and is thought to negatively impact protein function. Despite this, its contribution to the patient’s disease could not be confirmed, resulting in its classification as a ‘Variant of Uncertain Significance’. While the pathogenic potential of this specific change is unclear, both the PARN gene and the patient’s clinical presentation align with the range of TBD manifestations.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient's symptoms—pancytopenia, weight loss, premature greying, and fatigue—prompted evaluation for possible hematologic, nutritional, and autoimmune conditions. A significantly decreased reticulocyte count suggested compromised erythropoiesis, thus initially directing attention toward potential nutrient depletion and bone marrow failure disorders.

A profound vitamin B12 deficiency was promptly established and explained multiple clinical aspects, corroborated by bone marrow findings of inadequate erythropoiesis. Nonetheless, after addressing the deficiency, the patient’s systemic impairment persisted and remained notably disproportionate.

An immune-mediated etiology was briefly contemplated due to the presence of a positive DAT and increased inflammatory parameters. Nevertheless, the lack of hemolysis, negative autoimmune laboratory results, and absence of other systemic manifestations did not support an autoimmune cytopenia or a connective tissue disorder.

Hepatic fibrosis (HF) was idiopathic, as both autoimmune and infectious origins had been ruled out. The distinctive presentation of early-onset ageing signs, ongoing cytopenia, and fibrosis affecting both pulmonary and hepatic tissues in a young individual directed consideration toward a rare genetic disease. Despite the lack of familial precedent, the possibility of a de novo genetic alteration could not be dismissed.

This suggested a diagnosis of a telomere biology disorder capable of resembling acquired diseases, exhibiting diverse levels of penetrance. Although uncommon, such disorders may not display the typical mucocutaneous triad seen in dyskeratosis congenita, instead presenting more discreetly as marrow failure, organ fibrosis, and signs consistent with premature ageing.

Genetic testing confirmed this suspicion by revealing a disease-causing heterozygous mutation in the PARN gene, which offered a comprehensive rationale for the spectrum of clinical findings observed.

Treatment:

The patient received intravenous cyanocobalamin at a dose of 1000 µg per day for 5 days, transitioned to weekly administration over 4 weeks, and subsequently continued with monthly maintenance to address pronounced deficiency.

Supportive measures involved whole blood transfusions to alleviate symptomatic anaemia, daily oral folic acid (1 mg), and weekly vitamin D (60 000 IU) in addition to elemental calcium (1000 mg daily) for osteoporosis management. Electrolyte disturbances were treated with conventional supplementation.

Physiotherapy was provided, and genetic counselling was offered to her family following the identification of a TBD.

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient experienced relief of symptoms after her deficiencies were addressed and supportive treatment was provided. At her two-week follow-up, laboratory evaluations demonstrated stable blood counts, her fatigue had lessened, and there were no additional clinical issues noted.

She remains under ongoing outpatient monitoring to assess hepatic and pulmonary function, as well as to observe for any advancement of bone marrow or systemic disease.

Discussion:

Telomeres, composed of repetitive nucleotide sequences at chromosome termini, function to maintain genome stability by protecting chromosomes from erosion. During normal cell division, telomeres progressively shorten until they reach a threshold that induces cellular senescence or programmed cell death. The maintenance of telomere length under physiological circumstances depends on telomerase, a ribonucleoprotein comprised of telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and the telomerase RNA component (TERC), which restores telomere length through extension. Nevertheless, disease-causing mutations affecting the genes coding for telomerase components disrupt its activity, causing accelerated telomere shortening and ultimately giving rise to a spectrum of inherited disorders known as TBD.

Pathogenic variants in DKC1 with X-linked recessive inheritance were the initial genetic alterations connecting dyskeratosis congenita (DC) to significantly reduced telomere length, thereby establishing DC as the canonical telomere biology disorder (TBD). The classic clinical diagnosis of DC relies on the presence of a triad consisting of oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular pigmentation of the skin, or having two of these signs in addition to bone marrow failure syndrome (BMFS). Additional recognised features comprise idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), hepatic fibrosis (HF), and an elevated susceptibility to both haematologic and solid tumours. Severe, early-onset forms of TBD, such as Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome, are also caused by mutations in genes associated with DC.

TBDs can manifest as isolated involvement of the skin and mucosa, specific organs, or hematologic abnormalities, and may occur in adults absent the characteristic DC triad. Frequently, the underlying genetic abnormality is concealed by disorders like aplastic anemia, bone marrow failure syndromes (BMFS), idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), heart failure (HF), or gastrointestinal pathologies. In the absence of the classic triad and because the clinical onset is delayed, patients are commonly misdiagnosed, particularly outside specialized medical facilities. Monoallelic mutations in TERT, TERC, and RTEL1 are commonly detected in TBDs, usually presenting as either IPF or BMFS.

PARN, a member of a well-preserved group of exoribonucleases, shortens mRNA poly(A) tails through deadenylation, thereby influencing gene expression. Extensive sequencing of TERC RNA at its 3-prime end revealed that PARN is essential for eliminating oligo(A) tails added post-transcriptionally, which otherwise mark nuclear RNAs for breakdown; this activity is necessary for proper maturation of the TERC’s 3-prime end.

PARN is among the top 20% of genes most intolerant to rare, loss-of-function mutations, underscoring its vital importance and supporting haploinsufficiency as a mechanism in carriers. Germline pathogenic PARN alterations are established contributors to dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive type 6 (DKCB6), as well as the broader DC/TBD disease spectrum. Cases of HH syndrome have been documented in patients with biallelic PARN mutations. Monoallelic germline pathogenic PARN variants have been associated with telomere-related PFBMFT4. The association between PARN and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) was first suggested following discovery of new monoallelic variants, and later investigations identified rare monoallelic PARN variants in five out of 262 individuals with sporadic IPF subjected to whole-exome sequencing, solidifying a recently recognized link that has now been described in more than 65 cases in published reports.1517 Nonetheless, the full array of clinical manifestations tied to PARN alterations remains unclear due to the small number of cases recorded to date.

Of note, our patient possessed a heterozygous missense variant in PARN, c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg), impacting a highly conserved amino acid within the R3H domain (residues 178–245). According to four out of five in silico missense prediction platforms (LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, and SIFT), the variant is expected to cause a harmful functional effect. Assessment with the ACMG/AMP variant classification guidelines indicates that this variant fulfills several supporting criteria: PM1—the alteration is located in a critical and well-defined functional domain (R3H domain) where benign variation has not been observed; PM2—the variant is absent or occurs extremely infrequently in population databases; PP3—multiple computational tools anticipate a detrimental impact on the gene or gene product; PP2—PARN is a gene with infrequent benign missense variants and for which missense variants commonly cause disease. While its clinical interpretation remains uncertain, the possible connection to TBDs is reinforced by PARN’s established biological function, as well as similarities to other missense variants in PARN, including p.Lys59Arg (ClinVar ID: VCV000424063.2) and p.Ala383Val (ClinVar ID: VCV000180661.1), which ClinVar categorizes as ‘likely pathogenic.’

TBDs can involve multiple genes, exhibit different inheritance patterns, show incomplete penetrance, and feature somatic genetic rescue, all of which may affect specific organ systems in varied ways. Furthermore, both environmental and lifestyle influences can differently impact the regenerative abilities of these systems. In our patient's case, such complexities may account for the clinical presentation, even though only a heterozygous PARN mutation was detected.

Monoallelic PARN mutations typically present with IPF rather than primary hematological abnormalities; however, our patient’s cytopenia suggests a potential underlying TBD, potentially heightening bone marrow vulnerability to hematopoietic stressors like severe vitamin B12 deficiency. Shortened telomeres limit hematopoietic stem cell renewal, causing early cell senescence and increased susceptibility to bone marrow failure syndromes (BMFS). Although the patient’s cytopenia was most likely due to profound vitamin B12 deficiency, identification of the PARN variant emphasizes the need for continuous clinical and hematological evaluation for signs of progressive marrow failure in this genetically susceptible individual.

Cirrhosis associated with telomere biology disorders (TBD) has historically been underdiagnosed; however, recent data indicate its incidence may be higher than previously thought. A retrospective study from the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry utilizing transient elastography demonstrated that 88.8% of adult individuals harboring pathogenic TERC or TERT mutations exhibited at least moderate hepatic fibrosis (HF), with 33.3% showing evidence of cirrhosis. Similarly, one published report noted a case presenting with pancytopenia and HF in a patient carrying monoallelic mutations in both PARN (c.1613C>G) and TERT (c.2476G>A). In distinction, the case described here involved an individual with only a heterozygous PARN mutation who nonetheless manifested both hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. While monoallelic PARN mutations are most often linked to idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), potential downstream effects on telomerase activity might explain the occurrence of HF more commonly seen in the context of TERC or TERT alterations. Collectively, these observations broaden the recognized clinical manifestations of PARN-related TBDs and highlight the necessity of considering hepatic fibrosis as part of its clinical presentation.

The definitive diagnosis of suspected TBD relies on genetic analysis via next-generation sequencing as well as flow cytometry-based fluorescence in situ hybridization assays to assess telomere length. Management is primarily supportive and individualized based on symptom burden, requirements for organ transplantation, and modification of risk factors. Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation may offer a curative option for those exhibiting hematologic involvement. Although limited preclinical and early clinical trials indicate that androgens such as danazol and nandrolone may promote telomere lengthening, the duration and sustainability of these effects have not been established.

The patient exhibited symptoms characteristic of TBD, HF, and IPF. After thorough assessment to rule out prevalent etiologies, whole-exome sequencing pinpointed the responsible gene. A comprehensive review of the literature expanded our insight into this uncommon presentation. Supportive care was administered, and her relatives received genetic counseling. She was informed about her diagnosis and recognized that definitive treatments were unavailable. At the follow-up appointment two weeks later, her hematologic parameters were unchanged, with no evidence of further clinical decline.
