Title: Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

Jinal Soni, Karun Saathveeg Sam, Vinus Taneja, Pooja Khosla

Abstract:

Summary: A female in her late 20s reported a 5-year duration of worsening fatigue and widespread weakness. Physical examination demonstrated premature signs of ageing, anaemia, neuropathy, and enlargement of the liver and spleen. Laboratory evaluation revealed pancytopenia, dimorphic anaemia, a pronounced deficiency in vitamin B12, hepatic fibrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis. Although her nutritional deficiencies were addressed, the presence of cytopenia, early hair greying, osteoporosis, and both hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis prompted consideration of an underlying genetic etiology. Clinical exome sequencing detected a monoallelic PARN missense variant (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg), designated as a Variant of Uncertain Significance. Pathogenic germline alterations in PARN have been linked to dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), as well as telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure 4. The alignment of clinical and genetic findings in this patient indicated a telomere biology disorder (TBD). This report underscores the need to evaluate for TBDs in younger adults presenting with unexplained, multisystem involvement, even in the absence of classic mucocutaneous manifestations. Prompt identification is vital for appropriate genetic counselling, ongoing surveillance, and consideration of bone marrow transplantation in cases with progressive disease.

Background:

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) represent a group of uncommon premature-aging conditions resulting from inherited mutations in telomere-associated genes, which cause telomeres to be unusually short or defective. These disorders mainly impact tissues with rapidly replicating cells, such as the bone marrow, skin, mucous membranes, hair follicles, and liver cells. TBD encompasses a variety of clinical presentations, with dyskeratosis congenita (DC) serving as the classic example. Additional related conditions include Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome.

Within this category of conditions, mutations in PARN exhibit a unique clinical presentation: individuals with two mutated alleles develop autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), whereas single allele mutations are frequently associated with telomere-associated pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4).

Herein, we report a female patient in her late twenties presenting with persistent fatigue and symptoms affecting multiple organ systems, who was subsequently found to have a TBD.

Case presentation:

A female patient in her late twenties was referred to our tertiary facility due to a five-year progression of generalized weakness and early onset fatigue. During this period, her physical functioning deteriorated, resulting in diminished ability to complete routine tasks relative to individuals of her age group. In the most recent three months, her symptoms intensified, significantly impacting her independence in self-care and necessitating help from others. Additional symptoms included shortness of breath on exertion, episodic chest discomfort, unintentional weight loss, and decreased appetite.

Upon physical assessment, her vital signs were within normal limits. Key observations included pallor, jaundice, early onset grey hair, hyperpigmentation over the knuckles, and a smooth tongue with loss of lingual papillae.

Evaluation of the musculoskeletal system revealed thoracic scoliosis and wasting of both thenar eminences. Abdominal examination demonstrated enlargement of the liver, palpable 5 cm below the right costal margin with a span of 18 cm. On respiratory assessment, breath sounds were diminished in the areas beneath both scapulae. No abnormalities were detected on cardiovascular examination.

Investigations:

The initial labs indicated pancytopenia accompanied by a diminished reticulocyte percentage (0.39%). The peripheral blood smear revealed dimorphic red blood cells, basophilic stippling, the presence of Cabot rings, and neutrophils with increased segmentation—features indicative of ineffective erythropoiesis. Serum assays demonstrated marked deficiencies of both vitamin B12 and folate. Iron panel showed raised serum iron and ferritin levels, in keeping with ineffective erythropoiesis (see table 1).

Bone marrow assessment showed active haematopoiesis across all three cell lines with a proliferation of erythroid cells and increased iron deposition; there was no evidence to suggest malignancy, abnormal cell development, or an infiltrative process, and cytogenetic studies were unremarkable.

Haemolysis was indicated by high levels of lactate dehydrogenase and indirect hyperbilirubinaemia. The direct antiglobulin test (DAT) came back positive for IgG at a strength of 2+, while the indirect test was negative. Nonetheless, the inadequate reticulocyte production and bone marrow findings favored a diagnosis of ineffective erythropoiesis over immune-mediated peripheral destruction.

An upper gastrointestinal endoscopy was conducted to investigate possible gastrointestinal malabsorption and revealed normal findings upon gross examination. Duodenal biopsy demonstrated mild chronic gastritis with a decreased number of parietal cells, suggesting potential interference with intrinsic factor production and subsequent B12 malabsorption. Nevertheless, the extent of the patient's clinical presentation seemed excessive relative to deficiencies expected from nutrition alone.

Given evidence of liver dysfunction, additional imaging and evaluation were undertaken. Abdominal ultrasound detected both hepatomegaly and splenomegaly. Transient elastography indicated grade II hepatic steatosis with a controlled attenuation parameter of 295 dB/m and cirrhosis characterized by a liver stiffness of 18.1 kPa. Inflammatory markers were elevated. Tests for autoimmune disorders, including antinuclear and autoimmune hepatitis antibodies, returned negative results. Screening for infectious causes such as viral hepatitis, HIV, Cytomegalovirus, and scrub typhus yielded no significant findings, except that Epstein-Barr virus IgG was positive (see table 2).

Positron emission tomography–CT was utilized to assess for hidden infiltrative or inflammatory disease. The imaging revealed uneven uptake of ¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose throughout the axial and appendicular bone marrow. Both lungs demonstrated widespread ground-glass opacity accompanied by several fibrotic and subpleural atelectatic alterations. Hepatosplenomegaly and ascites were also observed; however, there were no indications of obvious malignancy or granulomatous process (figures 1 and 2).

Other notable observations were secondary hyperparathyroidism, disturbances in electrolytes such as hypocalcaemia and hypokalaemia, vitamin D deficiency, and osteoporosis verified by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, which accounted for her ongoing bone pain and repeated fractures (see table 3). Electromyography and nerve conduction testing showed asymmetrical sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy with chronic neurogenic alterations in both proximal and distal muscle groups.

Considering the overall presentation—including cytopenia, liver impairment, early greying of hair, osteoporosis, and neuropathy—a syndromic or genetic cause was considered likely.

Clinical exome sequencing revealed a heterozygous missense variant in the PARN gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg, exon 8). This missense change affects a highly conserved amino acid and is expected to impair protein function. Nonetheless, its contribution to the disease cannot be determined at this time, and thus it has been classified as a ‘Variant of Uncertain Significance’. While the pathogenicity of this particular variant has not been established, both the gene involvement and the patient’s phenotype align with features observed in TBD.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient’s symptoms, including pancytopenia, weight loss, premature greying, and fatigue, prompted evaluation for possible haematologic, nutritional, and autoimmune etiologies. The significantly decreased reticulocyte count suggested impaired red blood cell production, initially indicating the possibility of nutrient deficiencies or bone marrow failure syndromes.

Early identification of severe vitamin B12 deficiency provided an explanation for several clinical manifestations, which was further corroborated by bone marrow findings of ineffective erythropoiesis. Nevertheless, following correction of the identified deficiencies, the persistent and disproportionate systemic symptoms could not be fully explained.

Consideration was given to an immune-related etiology due to a positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) and raised inflammatory markers. However, the lack of haemolysis, negative autoimmune serologies, and absence of additional systemic signs were inconsistent with a diagnosis of autoimmune cytopenia or connective tissue disorder.

The origin of hepatic fibrosis (HF) remained unclear after ruling out autoimmune and infectious etiologies. The combination of signs indicating early aging, chronic cytopenia, and fibrosis affecting both the lungs and liver in a young adult led clinicians to consider an underlying, unidentified genetic disorder. While no familial history was reported, which initially lessened the likelihood of an inherited condition, the prospect of a de novo mutation was still regarded as plausible.

As a result, a hypothesis was formulated regarding a telomere biology disorder that could imitate acquired diseases and exhibit heterogeneous penetrance. While uncommon, it might not demonstrate the typical mucocutaneous triad seen in dyskeratosis congenita (DC), and instead present in a more understated manner with bone marrow failure, fibrotic changes in organs, and signs indicative of premature aging.

Genetic testing supported this hypothesis by revealing a disease-causing heterozygous mutation in the PARN gene, offering a cohesive interpretation for her diverse clinical manifestations.

Treatment:

Intravenous cyanocobalamin at a dose of 1000 µg was administered each day for 5 days, then once weekly for 4 weeks, and subsequently continued on a monthly maintenance schedule to address the marked deficiency.

Supportive care included transfusion of whole blood for anaemia with clinical symptoms, oral folic acid at 1 mg per day, as well as vitamin D (60 000 IU weekly) along with elemental calcium (1000 mg daily) to manage osteoporosis. Electrolyte disturbances were corrected using standard supplementation protocols.

Physiotherapy was initiated, and genetic counselling was provided to the patient’s family after the diagnosis of a TBD.

Outcome and follow-up:

After addressing her deficiencies and providing supportive management, the patient experienced relief of her symptoms. At the 2-week follow-up visit, her hematological parameters remained consistent, she reported reduced fatigue, and there were no additional clinical issues identified.

She remains under routine outpatient monitoring to assess liver and lung function and to evaluate for any advancement of bone marrow or systemic disease involvement.

Discussion:

Telomeres consist of repetitive DNA sequences at the termini of chromosomes, serving to protect genomic stability by preventing chromosomal deterioration. During successive cell divisions, these telomeric regions progressively shorten until they reach a threshold length that initiates cell senescence or programmed cell death. Normally, telomerase—a ribonucleoprotein made up of telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and telomerase RNA component (TERC)—acts to sustain telomere length through its elongating activity. Pathogenic mutations in genes coding for telomerase components compromise this enzyme’s function, causing early telomere shortening and giving rise to a spectrum of hereditary disorders termed telomere biology disorders (TBD).

Mutations in DKC1 inherited in an X-linked recessive pattern were the first to demonstrate the association between dyskeratosis congenita (DC) and significantly shortened telomeres, positioning DC as the archetype of telomere biology disorders (TBDs). DC is typically diagnosed based on the mucocutaneous triad: oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation, or by the presence of any two of these signs alongside bone marrow failure syndrome (BMFS). Additional recognised clinical presentations include idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), hepatic fibrosis (HF), as well as an elevated risk for both haematological and solid tumours. Variants in genes involved in DC are also responsible for severe, early-onset TBD phenotypes such as Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome.

Telomere biology disorders can present with only mucocutaneous, organ-specific, or hematological symptoms, and may appear in adults absent the hallmark triad seen in dyskeratosis congenita. Frequently, the underlying inherited abnormality is concealed by conditions including aplastic anemia, bone marrow failure syndromes, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis, and gastrointestinal disorders. Because the classic triad is not present and symptom onset may be delayed, patients are commonly misdiagnosed, particularly outside of specialized medical centers. Monoallelic mutations in TERT, TERC, and RTEL1 are commonly detected in TBDs, usually presenting as idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis or bone marrow failure syndromes.

The PARN gene, a member of a well-preserved group of exoribonucleases, functions by reducing the poly(A) tail length of mRNAs via deadenylation, influencing the regulation of gene expression. Through extensive sequencing of the 3′ terminal of TERC RNA, it was demonstrated that PARN is necessary to eliminate oligo(A) tails added after transcription, which mark nuclear RNAs for degradation, and is crucial for proper maturation of the TERC's 3′ end.

PARN is classified among the 20% of genes least capable of tolerating rare, disruptive variants, highlighting both its critical function and the principle of haploinsufficiency in individuals carrying such changes. Germline pathogenic variants in PARN have been established as causative for dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), as well as the broader spectrum of DC/TBD conditions. Cases of HH syndrome have involved biallelic PARN mutations, while monoallelic germline pathogenic variants are associated with telomere-related PFBMFT4. The connection between PARN and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) was initially identified through the discovery of novel monoallelic variants, and later investigations found monoallelic rare variants in PARN in five of 262 sporadic IPF patients who underwent whole-exome sequencing, reinforcing a growing association now described in more than 65 cases across published reports.1517 Despite this, the full clinical spectrum attributable to PARN mutations is still not well delineated, as only a limited number of cases have been documented.

Importantly, our patient possessed a heterozygous missense mutation in PARN, c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg), which alters a highly conserved amino acid within the R3H domain (amino acids 178–245). According to four out of five computational prediction platforms (LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, and SIFT), this variant is expected to disrupt protein function. Assessment with ACMG/AMP criteria indicates multiple supporting points: PM1—the variant is located in a key, established functional region (the R3H domain) lacking any benign variants; PM2—the alteration is either absent or very infrequently seen in population data; PP3—several in silico algorithms suggest a detrimental impact on the gene or its product; PP2—PARN exhibits low benign missense variant frequency, and pathogenic missense changes are a recognized cause of disease in this gene. While the clinical impact is currently unresolved, its relevance to TBDs is suggested both by the established function of PARN and by comparison to other missense alterations—such as p.Lys59Arg (ClinVar ID: VCV000424063.2) and p.Ala383Val (ClinVar ID: VCV000180661.1)—which are classified as 'likely pathogenic' in ClinVar.

TBDs are influenced by multiple genes with varying modes of inheritance, incomplete penetrance, and somatic genetic rescue, which can lead to differing impacts on organ systems. Furthermore, environmental and lifestyle factors may differently affect the proliferative abilities of various organs. For our patient, these intricate factors could contribute to the clinical presentation observed, even though only a single PARN allele variant was found.

While single-allele PARN mutations are typically linked to IPF and not primary blood disorders, the cytopenia found in our patient suggests a possible underlying TBD that might increase bone marrow vulnerability to stresses such as pronounced vitamin B12 deficiency. Reduced telomere length limits the regenerative capacity of haematopoietic stem cells, promoting premature cellular aging and elevating the risk for BMFS. Although the patient’s cytopenia is most likely due to a marked deficiency in vitamin B12, the detection of a PARN variant highlights the necessity for continuous clinical and hematologic evaluation to monitor for any signs of emerging marrow failure in a genetically at-risk individual.

Cirrhosis in TBD has frequently gone unnoticed; nonetheless, recent data indicate it may occur more often than previously believed. A retrospective study from the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry, employing transient elastography, demonstrated that 88.8% of adults with disease-causing TERC or TERT mutations exhibited at least moderate HF, while 33.3% presented with cirrhosis. The literature reports a similar patient with pancytopenia and HF who possessed single-allele variants in both PARN (c.1613C>G) and TERT (c.2476G>A). Conversely, our patient carried only a monoallelic PARN mutation, yet manifested both liver and lung fibrosis. While monoallelic PARN is generally linked to IPF, its downstream effects on telomerase may explain the HF usually associated with TERT or TERC mutations. Collectively, these observations suggest a broadened clinical range for PARN-related TBDs and highlight the need to recognize HF within its phenotype.

Definitive diagnosis of suspected TBD relies on genetic analysis via next-generation sequencing and assessment of telomere length using flow cytometry-based fluorescence in situ hybridization assays. Management remains largely supportive and is individualized based on clinical severity, the requirement for organ transplantation, and modification of risk factors. Haematopoietic stem cell transplantation offers a curative possibility for those presenting with haematological involvement. Although limited preclinical data and small-scale clinical trials indicate that androgens such as danazol and nandrolone may promote telomere elongation, their prolonged effects are not yet established.

The patient exhibited symptoms consistent with TBD, HF, and IPF. A thorough assessment ruled out more frequently encountered etiologies, and whole-exome sequencing revealed the responsible genetic mutation. Comprehensive review of existing literature provided further insight into this uncommon clinical scenario. Supportive measures were initiated, and her family received genetic counseling. She acknowledged the specifics of her diagnosis and recognized that curative therapies were unavailable. At her two-week review, hematological parameters stayed within normal limits, and there was no evidence of worsening clinical status.
