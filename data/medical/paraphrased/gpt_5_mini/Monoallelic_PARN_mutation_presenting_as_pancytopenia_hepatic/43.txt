Title: Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

Jinal Soni, Karun Saathveeg Sam, Vinus Taneja, Pooja Khosla

Abstract:

Summary: A female patient in her late twenties presented with a 5-year history characterized by increasing fatigue and generalized weakness. Physical examination identified premature aging features, anaemia, neuropathic changes, and hepatosplenomegaly. Laboratory findings demonstrated pancytopenia, dimorphic anaemia, profound vitamin B12 deficiency, in addition to evidence of hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. Even after nutritional deficiencies were addressed, the combination of cytopenias, early-onset greying, osteoporosis, and fibrotic involvement of the liver and lungs suggested a possible genetic etiology. Clinical exome sequencing revealed a heterozygous missense variant in PARN (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg), categorized as a Variant of Uncertain Significance. Pathogenic germline PARN mutations have previously been linked to dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), as well as telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure 4. In this patient, the relationship between clinical features and genetic findings was consistent with a diagnosis of a telomere biology disorder (TBD). This case underlines the need to consider TBDs in young adults with unexplained multi-organ involvement, even in the absence of classic mucocutaneous signs. Early diagnosis is critical for informing genetic counseling, ongoing surveillance, and evaluation for bone marrow transplantation in cases with disease progression.

Background:

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) represent uncommon syndromes characterized by premature ageing, resulting from inherited mutations in genes associated with telomere maintenance, which cause excessively short or malfunctioning telomeres. These abnormalities chiefly impact tissues with high cellular turnover, including bone marrow, skin, mucosal surfaces, hair follicles, and liver cells. TBD encompasses a spectrum of clinical presentations, with dyskeratosis congenita (DC) considered the classic example. Additional syndromes include Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome.

Of the numerous genetic alterations associated with these conditions, mutations in PARN exhibit a unique clinical presentation; individuals with two mutated alleles develop autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), whereas those with a single mutated allele are typically predisposed to telomere-mediated pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4).

Here, we report a female patient in her late twenties who experienced extended fatigue and symptoms affecting multiple organ systems, ultimately receiving a diagnosis of a TBD.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late twenties was referred to our tertiary care facility due to a five-year progression of generalized weakness and increasing early-onset fatigue. During this period, her functional status gradually deteriorated, resulting in diminished capability to manage routine tasks relative to others her age. Over the preceding three months, her symptoms intensified, limiting her capacity for self-care and necessitating support from others. She further described experiencing shortness of breath on exertion, occasional chest pain, unintended weight loss, and a decreased appetite.

Upon assessment, the patient demonstrated stable haemodynamic parameters. Significant clinical observations comprised pallor, icterus, early onset of hair greying, hyperpigmentation over the knuckles, and a bald tongue characterized by lingual papillae atrophy.

Musculoskeletal evaluation identified thoracic scoliosis alongside atrophy of the thenar muscles on both hands. Abdominal examination revealed an enlarged liver, palpable 5 cm beneath the right costal margin, with a span of 18 cm. Examination of the respiratory system showed diminished breath sounds bilaterally in the infra-scapular areas. The cardiovascular assessment yielded normal findings.

Investigations:

Initial laboratory tests demonstrated pancytopenia accompanied by a decreased reticulocyte count (0.39%). Examination of the peripheral blood smear revealed dimorphic red blood cells, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and neutrophils with hypersegmentation, all indicative of ineffective erythropoiesis. Analysis of serum showed marked deficiencies in both vitamin B12 and folate. Iron profile demonstrated elevated serum iron and ferritin, which is characteristic of ineffective erythropoiesis (table 1).

Evaluation of the bone marrow showed trilineage hematopoiesis, erythroid hyperplasia, and increased iron reserves, with no signs of malignancy, dysplastic changes, or infiltration; cytogenetic assessment returned normal findings.

Markers for hemolysis were raised lactate dehydrogenase and indirect hyperbilirubinemia. The direct antiglobulin test (DAT) was positive for IgG (2+), whereas the indirect test was negative. Nonetheless, the diminished reticulocyte response and bone marrow findings favored ineffective erythropoiesis over peripheral immune-mediated red cell destruction.

To investigate possible gastrointestinal malabsorption, an upper GI endoscopy was conducted and found to be grossly unremarkable. Biopsy of the duodenum revealed mild chronic gastritis with a decreased number of parietal cells, which may have led to B12 malabsorption through diminished intrinsic factor synthesis. Nonetheless, the clinical manifestations were considered more severe than would be expected from nutritional deficiency alone.

Given evidence of hepatic impairment, additional imaging and evaluation were undertaken. Abdominal ultrasonography detected both hepatomegaly and splenomegaly. Transient elastography demonstrated grade II hepatic steatosis (controlled attenuation parameter: 295 dB/m) along with cirrhosis (liver stiffness measurement: 18.1 kPa). Inflammatory markers were elevated. Despite this, tests for autoantibodies such as antinuclear and autoimmune hepatitis antibodies were negative. Screening for infectious etiologies including viral hepatitis, HIV, Cytomegalovirus, and scrub typhus did not identify significant abnormalities aside from Epstein-Barr virus IgG positivity (see table 2).

Positron emission tomography–CT was utilized to assess for hidden infiltrative or inflammatory disease. The imaging demonstrated uneven uptake of ¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose throughout the bone marrow in both axial and appendicular regions. Both lungs exhibited widespread ground-glass opacities accompanied by numerous fibrotic areas and subpleural atelectasis. Additional findings included enlargement of the liver and spleen as well as the presence of ascites, but no clear signs of malignancy or granulomatous conditions were identified (see figures 1 and 2).

Further evaluations demonstrated secondary hyperparathyroidism, disturbances in electrolytes (namely hypocalcaemia and hypokalaemia), vitamin D deficiency, and osteoporosis which was verified through dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry. These abnormalities accounted for her repeated episodes of bone pain and fractures (see table 3). Electromyography and nerve conduction testing indicated an asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy, accompanied by chronic neurogenic changes affecting both proximal and distal muscle groups.

Considering the aggregate of symptoms—cytopenia, liver dysfunction, early onset greying, osteoporosis, and neuropathy—a syndromic or genetic cause was considered likely.

Clinical exome sequencing revealed a heterozygous missense mutation in the PARN gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg, located in exon 8). This variant affects a highly conserved amino acid and is predicted to adversely impact protein function. Nonetheless, its contribution to the disease cannot be definitively determined and it has been classified as a ‘Variant of Uncertain Significance’. Despite uncertainty regarding the pathogenicity of this particular variant, the association of PARN and the patient's clinical presentation align with features typical of TBD.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient exhibited pancytopenia, unintended weight loss, premature greying, and fatigue, prompting evaluation for hematologic, nutritional, and autoimmune etiologies. The profoundly decreased reticulocyte count indicated impaired erythropoiesis, which initially led to the investigation of potential nutrient deficiencies and bone marrow failure syndromes.

Severe vitamin B12 deficiency was promptly identified, providing an explanation for multiple clinical manifestations, as corroborated by bone marrow findings consistent with ineffective erythropoiesis. Nonetheless, despite correction of the vitamin deficiency, the ongoing and disproportionate systemic abnormalities persisted.

Given a positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) and increased inflammatory markers, an immune-mediated process was considered. However, the lack of hemolysis, negative autoimmune panel, and absence of characteristic systemic signs made autoimmune cytopenia or a connective tissue disorder unlikely.

Hepatic fibrosis (HF) did not have an identifiable cause, as both autoimmune and infectious etiologies were ruled out. The combination of premature ageing signs, ongoing cytopenia, and fibrotic changes affecting both the lungs and liver in a young adult pointed clinicians toward consideration of an obscure genetic disorder. Even though there was no family history to support a hereditary condition, the possibility of a de novo mutation could not be excluded.

This supported the consideration of a telomere biology disorder capable of resembling acquired diseases, exhibiting variable penetrance and, although uncommon, potentially presenting without the typical mucocutaneous triad seen in DC. Instead, it may appear insidiously with bone marrow failure, organ fibrosis, and signs of premature aging.

Genetic testing confirmed this suspicion by detecting a disease-causing heterozygous mutation in the PARN gene, accounting for her spectrum of clinical manifestations.

Treatment:

Intravenous cyanocobalamin was administered at a dose of 1000 µg per day for five days, then switched to weekly dosing over four weeks, with subsequent monthly maintenance to address significant deficiency.

Additional supportive care comprised whole blood transfusions to manage symptomatic anaemia, oral folic acid at 1 mg daily, vitamin D at 60 000 IU weekly, and elemental calcium at 1000 mg daily for osteoporosis. Electrolyte disturbances were managed with routine supplementation.

Physiotherapy was provided, and genetic counselling was arranged for her family after the diagnosis of a TBD.

Outcome and follow-up:

After addressing her deficiencies and providing supportive therapy, the patient experienced relief of symptoms. At a two-week follow-up appointment, her hematologic parameters remained steady, she reported decreased fatigue, and no additional clinical issues were observed.

She remains under ongoing outpatient monitoring to assess hepatic and pulmonary status, as well as to track any changes related to bone marrow or broader systemic disease involvement.

Discussion:

Telomeres are repetitive nucleotide sequences located at chromosome termini that serve to protect genetic material from erosion. During successive cell divisions, these telomeric regions progressively diminish in length and, upon reaching a threshold, initiate either cell senescence or programmed cell death. Normally, telomerase, a ribonucleoprotein enzyme comprising telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and the telomerase RNA component (TERC), counteracts this shortening by extending telomeres. Mutations in genes responsible for encoding telomerase subunits disrupt its activity, causing premature reduction in telomere length and giving rise to an inherited spectrum of disorders known as telomere biology diseases (TBD).

The identification of X-linked recessive mutations in DKC1 was the initial discovery linking dyskeratosis congenita (DC) with significantly reduced telomere length, establishing DC as the archetypal telomere biology disorder (TBD). DC is traditionally characterized by a mucocutaneous triad: reticular skin pigmentation, nail dystrophy, and oral leukoplakia; diagnosis may also be made if two of these findings are present in addition to bone marrow failure syndrome (BMFS). Additional recognized clinical features include idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), heart failure (HF), and an elevated risk for both hematologic and solid malignancies. More severe forms of childhood-onset TBDs—such as Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome—are also associated with mutations in genes involved in DC.

TBDs can appear with solely mucocutaneous, organ-specific, or hematologic features, and may emerge in adults absent the classic DC triad. The underlying hereditary abnormality is often concealed by disorders including aplastic anemia, bone marrow failure syndromes, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, or hepatic failure and gastrointestinal conditions. Due to the absence of the traditional triad and the tendency for delayed symptom onset, these individuals are commonly misdiagnosed, particularly outside of specialized facilities. Monoallelic mutations in TERT, TERC, and RTEL1 are routinely detected in TBDs, frequently manifesting as idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis or bone marrow failure syndromes.

The PARN gene, a member of a well-preserved exoribonuclease family, functions to regulate gene expression by catalyzing the deadenylation process, thereby reducing the poly(A) tail length of mRNA. Extensive sequencing of the 3′ terminal region of TERC RNA indicated that PARN is essential for the elimination of oligo(A) tails added post-transcriptionally, which mark nuclear RNAs for degradation and are necessary for the 3′-end maturation of TERC.

PARN is classified within the top 20% of genes exhibiting low tolerance for uncommon, disruptive mutations, underscoring its critical biological function and the theory of haploinsufficiency in individuals carrying such variants. Pathogenic germline mutations in PARN are known etiologies of dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), and the wider DC/TBD spectrum. Cases of HH syndrome have been linked to biallelic PARN alterations. In addition, monoallelic germline pathogenic variants in PARN have been associated with telomere-related PFBMFT4. The relationship between PARN and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) was initially established by the detection of novel monoallelic variants; subsequent research identified rare monoallelic PARN variants in five out of 262 patients with sporadic IPF who had undergone whole-exome sequencing, further substantiating an emerging connection now described in more than 65 reported cases in the literature.1517 Nonetheless, the complete clinical spectrum resulting from PARN mutations remains incompletely characterized, as the number of documented cases is still relatively small.

Of particular interest, our patient possessed a heterozygous missense mutation in PARN, c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg), altering a highly conserved amino acid within the R3H domain (residues 178–245). According to four out of five in silico prediction platforms (LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, and SIFT), the variant is anticipated to disrupt protein function. Assessment according to ACMG/AMP variant classification guidelines indicates this variant fulfills multiple supportive criteria: PM1—located within a functionally significant domain (the R3H domain) with no documented benign variants; PM2—not observed or exceedingly rare in population datasets; PP3—several computational methods suggest a damaging impact on the gene or gene product; PP2—the PARN gene exhibits a low frequency of benign missense variants, and pathogenic missense changes are commonly implicated in disease. Despite uncertainty regarding its clinical significance, its possible connection to TBDs is suggested by PARN’s well-established role and similarities to other PARN missense variants, such as p.Lys59Arg (ClinVar ID: VCV000424063.2) and p.Ala383Val (ClinVar ID: VCV000180661.1), both listed as ‘likely pathogenic’ in the ClinVar database.

TBDs can be influenced by multiple genes, varying patterns of inheritance, incomplete penetrance, and somatic genetic rescue, which may impact organ systems differently. Environmental exposures and lifestyle choices also contribute variably to the replicative abilities across organ systems. In our patient, these factors may account for the clinical presentation, even though only a single PARN allele variant was detected.

Monoallelic PARN mutations typically are linked to idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) rather than to primary hematological abnormalities. However, the cytopenia present in this patient raises suspicion for an underlying TBD that could increase marrow vulnerability to hematopoietic stressors, such as marked vitamin B12 deficiency. Shortened telomeres diminish the replicative capacity of hematopoietic stem cells, resulting in accelerated cellular aging and a heightened risk for bone marrow failure syndromes (BMFS). Although the observed cytopenia in this case was most likely due to significant vitamin B12 deficiency, the discovery of a PARN variant highlights the necessity for continued clinical and hematological surveillance for signs of evolving marrow failure in this genetically at-risk patient.

Cirrhosis has frequently gone unrecognized in Telomere Biology Disorders (TBD), though new data indicate it might occur more commonly than once thought. In a retrospective study from the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry, transient elastography revealed that 88.8% of adult individuals with pathogenic TERC or TERT variants demonstrated at least moderate hepatic fibrosis (HF), and 33.3% had cirrhosis. Similarly, another published case detailed an individual with pancytopenia and HF, carrying single allele variants in both PARN (c.1613C>G) and TERT (c.2476G>A). By comparison, our patient carried a single monoallelic PARN variant yet presented with both hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. While monoallelic PARN mutations are most often linked with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), the subsequent effects on telomerase activity could contribute to the development of HF as typically associated with TERC or TERT mutations. Collectively, these observations point toward a widening phenotype for PARN-related TBDs and highlight the necessity of recognizing hepatic fibrosis within its clinical presentation.

The definitive diagnosis of suspected TBD relies on genetic analysis using next-generation sequencing, as well as flow cytometry fluorescence in situ hybridisation to assess telomere length. Management is primarily supportive, individualized according to symptom burden, indications for organ transplantation, and reduction of contributing risk factors. Haematopoietic stem cell transplantation offers a curative approach for those exhibiting haematologic involvement. Although androgens such as danazol and nandrolone have demonstrated potential to extend telomere length in preclinical and limited clinical trials, the durability of these effects has yet to be determined.

The patient exhibited symptoms consistent with TBD, HF, and IPF. A comprehensive workup ruled out more frequent etiologies, and whole-exome sequencing revealed the causative genetic variant. A thorough review of the literature provided further insight into this uncommon clinical manifestation. Her care centered on supportive measures, and her relatives received genetic counselling. She comprehended the details of her diagnosis and acknowledged that definitive therapies were not possible. Upon reassessment two weeks later, her hematological parameters were unchanged, and she demonstrated no additional evidence of worsening clinical status.
