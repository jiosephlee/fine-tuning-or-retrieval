Title: Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

Jinal Soni, Karun Saathveeg Sam, Vinus Taneja, Pooja Khosla

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late twenties presented with a 5-year history marked by worsening fatigue and widespread muscle weakness. On physical examination, she exhibited features of premature aging, anaemia, neuropathy, and enlargement of both the liver and spleen. Laboratory assessments revealed pancytopenia, dimorphic anaemia, marked vitamin B12 deficiency, hepatic fibrosis, as well as pulmonary fibrosis. Although her nutritional deficits were corrected, the combination of persistent cytopenias, premature hair greying, osteoporosis, and fibrotic changes in the liver and lungs suggested the presence of an underlying genetic disorder. Clinical exome sequencing detected a heterozygous missense mutation in the PARN gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg), which is considered a Variant of Uncertain Significance. Pathogenic germline variants in PARN are linked to dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), and to telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure 4. In this patient, alignment of clinical findings with genetic data was consistent with a diagnosis of a telomere biology disorder (TBD). This report underscores the need to consider TBDs in younger adults who present with unexplained, multisystem involvement, even in the absence of classic mucocutaneous manifestations. Early identification is essential for enabling appropriate genetic counseling, close monitoring, and timely discussion of bone marrow transplantation should the disorder progress.

Background:

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) comprise a group of uncommon syndromes characterized by accelerated aging, resulting from inherited abnormalities in genes associated with telomere function, which cause telomeres to become unusually short or impaired. Cells that undergo frequent division—such as those in the bone marrow, epidermis, mucosal surfaces, hair follicles, and liver—are primarily involved. TBDs present with various clinical manifestations, with dyskeratosis congenita (DC) serving as the classic example. Additional disorders in this group include Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome.

Within this spectrum of disorders, mutations in PARN present a characteristic clinical picture; individuals with biallelic PARN mutations develop autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), whereas those with single allele variants typically exhibit telomere-associated pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4).

We report the case of a female patient in her late twenties experiencing persistent fatigue and manifestations involving multiple organ systems, who was subsequently found to have a telomere biology disorder (TBD).

Case presentation:

A woman in her late twenties was evaluated at our tertiary care facility due to a five-year progression of increasing generalized weakness and early onset fatigue. During this period, she experienced a decline in her functional abilities, limiting her daily activity compared to individuals her age. In the preceding three months, her weakness had intensified, affecting her self-care and resulting in the need for help. Additionally, she described exertional shortness of breath, occasional chest pain, unintended weight loss, and reduced appetite.

On physical assessment, the patient maintained stable haemodynamics. Significant observations comprised pallor, icterus, early onset of hair greying, hyperpigmentation over the knuckles, and a smooth tongue with loss of lingual papillae.

Musculoskeletal evaluation identified thoracic scoliosis along with atrophy of both thenar regions. The abdominal exam demonstrated hepatomegaly extending 5 cm beneath the right costal margin with a span of 18 cm. Respiratory assessment noted diminished breath sounds bilaterally in the infra-scapular areas. Examination of the cardiovascular system did not reveal any abnormalities.

Investigations:

The initial lab assessment identified pancytopenia accompanied by a decreased reticulocyte percentage (0.39%). Examination of the peripheral blood smear revealed the presence of dimorphic erythrocytes, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and hypersegmented neutrophils, findings indicative of defective erythropoiesis. Measurement of serum levels demonstrated marked deficiency of vitamin B12 and folate. Iron panel showed elevated concentrations of serum iron and ferritin, supporting the diagnosis of ineffective erythropoiesis (table 1).

Analysis of the bone marrow showed trilineage haematopoiesis with pronounced erythroid proliferation and increased iron reserves, without evidence to suggest malignancy, dysplasia, or infiltration; cytogenetic testing returned normal results.

Evidence of haemolysis was provided by increased lactate dehydrogenase and indirect hyperbilirubinaemia. The direct antiglobulin test (DAT) yielded a positive result for IgG (2+), but the indirect antiglobulin test was negative. Nonetheless, the insufficient reticulocyte production and bone marrow characteristics favored a diagnosis of ineffective erythropoiesis over peripheral immune-mediated red blood cell destruction.

In order to investigate gastrointestinal malabsorption, an upper gastrointestinal endoscopy was carried out and found to be unremarkable in terms of gross appearance. Duodenal biopsy demonstrated mild chronic gastritis accompanied by decreased parietal cell numbers, which may have contributed to impaired vitamin B12 absorption due to diminished intrinsic factor production. Nonetheless, the extent of the patient’s clinical manifestations was greater than could be explained solely by nutritional deficiency.

Due to evidence of hepatic dysfunction, additional imaging and evaluation were undertaken. Abdominal ultrasound identified both hepatomegaly and splenomegaly. Transient elastography indicated grade II hepatic steatosis (controlled attenuation parameter: 295 dB/m) as well as cirrhosis (liver stiffness: 18.1 kPa). Elevated inflammatory markers were observed. However, testing for autoimmune diseases, including antinuclear antibodies and autoimmune hepatitis antibodies, yielded negative results. Screening for infections such as viral hepatitis, HIV, Cytomegalovirus, and scrub typhus showed no significant findings other than positive Epstein-Barr virus IgG (table 2).

Positron emission tomography–CT was utilized to assess for hidden infiltrative or inflammatory conditions. The imaging revealed uneven uptake of ¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose within both the axial and appendicular bone marrow. Both lungs demonstrated widespread ground-glass opacification accompanied by numerous fibrotic areas and subpleural atelectasis. Additional findings included enlargement of the liver and spleen as well as the presence of ascites, but there was no sign of definite malignancy or granulomatous pathology (figures 1 and 2).

Other notable results were secondary hyperparathyroidism, electrolyte imbalances such as hypocalcaemia and hypokalaemia, vitamin D deficiency, and osteoporosis as demonstrated by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, which accounted for her repeated episodes of bone pain and fractures (see table 3). Electromyography together with nerve conduction studies indicated an asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy, showing chronic neurogenic alterations in both proximal and distal muscle groups.

Considering the collection of abnormalities—cytopenia, liver impairment, early-onset greying of hair, osteoporosis, and neuropathy—a genetic or syndromic cause was considered likely.

Clinical exome sequencing revealed a heterozygous missense mutation in the PARN gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg, located in exon 8). This missense change affects a highly conserved amino acid and is anticipated to impair protein function. Nevertheless, the clinical significance of this variant has not been established; thus, it is classified as a ‘Variant of Uncertain Significance’. While the pathogenic potential of this particular mutation is undetermined, both the presence of the PARN variant and the patient’s phenotype align with the clinical manifestations associated with TBD.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient's symptoms, including pancytopenia, weight loss, premature greying, and fatigue, prompted consideration of hematologic, nutritional, and autoimmune etiologies. The significantly decreased reticulocyte count suggested impaired erythropoiesis, which led clinicians to initially suspect nutrient deficiencies or bone marrow failure syndromes.

An early diagnosis of profound vitamin B12 deficiency explained several findings and was corroborated by bone marrow evidence of ineffective erythropoiesis. Nevertheless, despite correction of these deficiencies, persistent and disproportionate systemic dysfunction remained.

Given a positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) and raised inflammatory markers, an immune-related process was entertained temporarily. However, the absence of hemolysis, negative results for autoimmune serologies, and a lack of broader systemic manifestations argued against autoimmune cytopenia or a connective tissue disorder.

The underlying cause of hepatic fibrosis (HF) was not identified, as both infectious and autoimmune origins had been ruled out. The distinct combination of signs including early-onset ageing, continuous cytopenia, and fibrosis affecting both pulmonary and hepatic tissues in a young adult pointed clinicians toward considering a rare genetic condition. While the lack of relevant familial background seemed to negate a hereditary syndrome at first, the potential for a spontaneous (de novo) mutation could not be dismissed.

This prompted consideration of a telomere biology disorder (TBD) capable of imitating acquired diseases and exhibiting diverse penetrance. While uncommon, such a disorder may not display the typical mucocutaneous triad associated with dyskeratosis congenita (DC), but rather present with more understated symptoms like bone marrow failure, organ fibrosis, and signs indicative of premature ageing.

Genetic testing confirmed this by revealing a disease-causing single-allele mutation in the PARN gene, thereby offering a comprehensive rationale for her varied clinical manifestations.

Treatment:

The patient received intravenous cyanocobalamin at a dose of 1000 µg per day for five days, transitioned to weekly administration for four weeks, and subsequently placed on monthly maintenance therapy to address marked deficiency.

Additional supportive measures consisted of whole blood transfusions to manage symptomatic anemia, daily oral folic acid at 1 mg, vitamin D at 60,000 IU weekly, and elemental calcium 1,000 mg daily for treatment of osteoporosis. Any electrolyte disturbances were managed with standard replacement protocols.

Physiotherapy was initiated, and her family was provided genetic counseling after a diagnosis of a TBD was established.

Outcome and follow-up:

After correcting her deficiencies and providing supportive management, the patient experienced relief of symptoms. At the 2-week follow-up visit, her hematologic parameters remained stable, she reported reduced fatigue, and no additional clinical issues were noted.

She remains under ongoing outpatient monitoring to assess liver and lung function, as well as to track any advancement of bone marrow or systemic disease involvement.

Discussion:

Telomeres are repetitive DNA elements located at the termini of chromosomes, serving to protect chromosome ends from deterioration and maintaining genomic stability. During successive cell divisions, telomeres progressively shorten, and when they reach a threshold length, cellular senescence or programmed cell death is initiated. Normally, telomerase—a ribonucleoprotein complex comprised of telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and telomerase RNA component (TERC)—is responsible for sustaining telomere length by adding DNA repeats. Mutations affecting genes that encode components of the telomerase complex disrupt its activity, resulting in accelerated telomere shortening and culminating in a spectrum of hereditary disorders known as TBD.

The association between DC and significantly shortened telomeres was first identified through X-linked recessive pathogenic variants in DKC1, establishing DC as the archetype of telomere biology disorders (TBDs). Traditionally, DC is characterized by a mucocutaneous triad comprising oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation, or alternatively, by the presence of any two of these signs in conjunction with bone marrow failure syndrome (BMFS). Additional recognized features of DC include idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), hepatic fibrosis (HF), and heightened susceptibility to both haematological and solid tumors. Furthermore, severe early-onset TBDs—such as Hoyeraal–Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome—are also caused by mutations in genes related to DC.

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) can appear with single mucocutaneous symptoms, involvement of specific organs, or blood-related findings, and may develop in adults absent the classical dyskeratosis congenita (DC) triad. Frequently, underlying genetic abnormalities are obscured by coexisting conditions such as aplastic anaemia, bone marrow failure syndromes (BMFS), idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), hepatic fibrosis (HF), or gastrointestinal disorders. In the absence of hallmark triad features and with late onset of symptoms, misdiagnosis is common, especially outside of specialized medical centers. Monoallelic mutations in TERT, TERC, and RTEL1 are usually detected in TBDs, with presentation often resembling IPF or BMFS.

The PARN gene, a member of a well-preserved group of exoribonucleases, reduces mRNA poly(A) tail length via deadenylation, thereby influencing gene regulation. High-throughput sequencing of the 3-prime end of TERC RNA demonstrated that PARN is essential for eliminating oligo(A) tails added post-transcriptionally, which mark nuclear RNAs for breakdown and are necessary for the proper maturation of the TERC 3-prime terminus.

PARN ranks among the top 20% of genes most intolerant to rare, disruptive mutations, highlighting its critical function and supporting haploinsufficiency in heterozygous individuals. Germline pathogenic mutations in PARN are established contributors to dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), as well as the broader DC/TBD disease spectrum. Cases of HH syndrome have been observed with biallelic PARN alterations. Monoallelic germline pathogenic PARN variants have also been associated with telomere-related PFBMFT4. The association between PARN and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) was first suggested following the discovery of new monoallelic variants, and later research identified rare monoallelic PARN variants in five out of 262 patients with sporadic IPF through whole-exome sequencing, creating a growing body of evidence now described in over 65 cases in the literature.1517 Nonetheless, a comprehensive understanding of the clinical manifestations linked to PARN mutations remains elusive because of the relatively small number of documented cases.

Of particular interest, our patient possesses a single missense variant in PARN, c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg), which alters a highly conserved amino acid within the R3H domain (residues 178–245). Four out of five in silico missense prediction tools (LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, and SIFT) indicate that this variant likely has a detrimental impact on protein function. According to ACMG/AMP guidelines for variant interpretation, several criteria are satisfied by this variant: PM1—the substitution is located in a crucial, well-defined functional domain (R3H) with no known benign variants at this site; PM2—it is either absent or exceptionally rare in population databases; PP3—multiple computational programs predict a harmful effect on the gene or its product; PP2—PARN exhibits a low frequency of benign missense changes and missense mutations in this gene frequently cause disease. Although the clinical significance of this variant is unresolved, its possible connection to TBDs is underscored by the known function of PARN and similarities to other missense changes in the gene—such as p.Lys59Arg (ClinVar ID: VCV000424063.2) and p.Ala383Val (ClinVar ID: VCV000180661.1)—both classified as ‘likely pathogenic’ in ClinVar.

TBDs can be influenced by multiple genes, various modes of inheritance, incomplete penetrance, and somatic genetic rescue, each impacting organ systems differently. Environmental and lifestyle influences further contribute to variability in the replication capacity among organ systems. For our patient, these factors may account for the clinical presentation, notwithstanding the identification of only a single monoallelic PARN variant.

While monoallelic PARN mutations are typically linked to IPF rather than primary hematological abnormalities, the presence of cytopenia in this patient suggests a possible underlying TBD that could increase bone marrow vulnerability to hematopoietic stressors, such as profound vitamin B12 deficiency. Telomere attrition impairs the proliferative ability of hematopoietic stem cells, precipitating premature cellular aging and heightening the risk for BMFS. Although the cytopenia noted in this case was most likely due to pronounced vitamin B12 deficiency, detection of a PARN variant highlights the necessity for continued clinical and hematological surveillance to monitor for potential progression toward marrow failure in this genetically susceptible patient.

Cirrhosis associated with telomere biology disorders (TBD) has frequently been overlooked, though new data indicate that its occurrence may be greater than once thought. A retrospective review conducted using the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry and transient elastography showed that 88.8% of adults with pathogenic mutations in TERC or TERT exhibited at least moderate hepatic fibrosis (HF), and 33.3% were diagnosed with cirrhosis. Similarly, a published report described a patient presenting with pancytopenia and HF who possessed monoallelic variants in both PARN (c.1613C>G) and TERT (c.2476G>A). Conversely, our case involved a patient with only a monoallelic PARN variant who nonetheless developed hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. While monoallelic PARN mutations are most often linked to idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), their downstream effect on telomerase activity could potentially contribute to the HF more typically observed with TERC or TERT alterations. These observations broaden the recognized clinical range of PARN-related TBDs and emphasize the need to include HF as a possible feature of this condition.

The definitive diagnosis of suspected TBD relies on genetic analysis utilizing next-generation sequencing in addition to flow cytometry-based fluorescence in situ hybridization assays for telomere length assessment. Current management strategies focus on supportive care, individualized according to the extent of symptoms, indications for organ transplantation, and reduction of risk factors. Haematopoietic stem cell transplantation represents a potentially curative option for individuals presenting with haematologic involvement. Although early preclinical work and limited clinical trials indicate that androgens such as danazol and nandrolone may promote telomere elongation, their prolonged impact is not yet fully established.

The patient exhibited signs consistent with TBD, HF, and IPF. After thorough assessment to rule out more frequent etiologies, whole-exome sequencing pinpointed the responsible gene, and a comprehensive review of the literature deepened our knowledge of this uncommon manifestation. Supportive care was instituted, and the patient's family received genetic counseling. She was informed about the characteristics of her disease and acknowledged that definitive treatment was not possible. At a follow-up appointment two weeks later, her blood counts were unchanged, and there were no further indications of clinical worsening.
