Title: Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

Jinal Soni, Karun Saathveeg Sam, Vinus Taneja, Pooja Khosla

Abstract:

Summary: A female in her late twenties reported a five-year duration of increasing fatigue and widespread weakness. Physical assessment demonstrated indications of premature aging, anemia, neuropathy, and enlargement of both liver and spleen. Laboratory studies revealed pancytopenia, dimorphic anemia, pronounced vitamin B12 deficiency, hepatic fibrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis. After nutritional deficiencies were treated, the combination of cytopenia, early greying, osteoporosis, hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis prompted suspicion of an underlying genetic condition. Clinical exome analysis detected a heterozygous PARN missense mutation (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg), categorized as a Variant of Uncertain Significance. Pathogenic germline variants in PARN have been linked to dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), and telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure 4. The clinical and genetic findings in this patient were consistent with a telomere biology disorder (TBD) diagnosis. This report underscores the necessity of considering TBDs in young adults presenting with unexplained multisystem involvement, regardless of the absence of classic mucocutaneous manifestations. Early identification is essential for appropriate genetic counseling, regular monitoring, and evaluation for bone marrow transplant in cases with ongoing progression.

Background:

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) represent a group of uncommon syndromes associated with premature aging, resulting from inherited mutations in genes responsible for telomere maintenance. These mutations produce telomeres that are unusually short or dysfunctional. Cell types with high proliferation rates—namely bone marrow, skin, mucosal tissue, hair, and hepatocytes—are most frequently impacted. TBDs encompass a variety of clinical presentations, with dyskeratosis congenita (DC) serving as the classic example. Additional disorders in this spectrum include Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome.

Within this category of disorders, PARN mutations are associated with a unique clinical presentation; individuals with mutations affecting both alleles develop autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), whereas single-allele mutations are more frequently linked to telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4).

Here, we report the case of a female patient in her late twenties presenting with persistent fatigue and involvement of multiple organ systems, who was eventually found to have a telomere biology disorder.

Case presentation:

A female patient in her late twenties was referred to our tertiary care facility due to a 5-year progression of generalized weakness and increasing early fatigue. Her physical function steadily deteriorated during this period, limiting her capability to carry out daily tasks when compared with individuals her age. In the past three months, her symptoms intensified significantly, resulting in marked self-care difficulties and dependence on others for assistance. Additional complaints included shortness of breath upon exertion, occasional chest discomfort, unintended weight loss, and reduced appetite.

During physical assessment, her haemodynamic parameters were within normal limits. Key observations were pallor, icterus, early greying of hair, hyperpigmentation over the knuckles, and a bald tongue with diminished lingual papillae.

Evaluation of the musculoskeletal system detected thoracic scoliosis and atrophy of both thenar eminences. On abdominal examination, the liver was enlarged, palpable 5 cm below the right costal margin, with a span of 18 cm. Respiratory assessment showed decreased breath sounds in both infra-scapular areas. The cardiovascular system showed no abnormal findings.

Investigations:

The initial laboratory assessment revealed pancytopenia accompanied by a decreased reticulocyte percentage (0.39%). Examination of the peripheral blood smear showed dimorphic red cells, basophilic stippling, the presence of Cabot rings, and neutrophils with hypersegmentation, all indicative of impaired erythropoiesis. Serum assays indicated marked deficiencies in both vitamin B12 and folate. Iron panel results showed high serum iron and ferritin levels, further supporting the diagnosis of ineffective erythropoiesis (table 1).

A bone marrow biopsy demonstrated active production of all three cell lineages, notable erythroid hyperplasia, and increased iron stores, without signs of malignancy, dysplasia, or infiltration by other processes; cytogenetic studies returned normal results.

Indicators of haemolysis consisted of raised lactate dehydrogenase and indirect hyperbilirubinaemia. The direct antiglobulin test (DAT) was positive for IgG at 2+, whereas the indirect DAT was negative. Nevertheless, the diminished reticulocyte response together with bone marrow findings aligned more closely with ineffective erythropoiesis rather than destruction secondary to an immune mechanism in the peripheral circulation.

To investigate potential gastrointestinal malabsorption, an upper gastrointestinal endoscopic examination was performed and revealed normal gross findings. Histologic analysis of the duodenal biopsy demonstrated mild chronic gastritis with a decreased number of parietal cells, which may result in diminished intrinsic factor synthesis and subsequent vitamin B12 malabsorption. Nevertheless, the extent of the patient's clinical presentation was greater than expected for a deficiency of this nature alone.

Because hepatic dysfunction was noted, additional imaging and evaluation were conducted. Abdominal ultrasonography identified hepatosplenomegaly. Transient elastography indicated grade II hepatic steatosis with a controlled attenuation parameter measured at 295 dB/m, as well as cirrhosis with liver stiffness at 18.1 kPa. Elevated levels of inflammatory markers were found. Testing for autoimmune diseases, such as antinuclear antibodies and autoimmune hepatitis markers, returned negative results. An infectious disease screening—including assays for viral hepatitis, HIV, Cytomegalovirus, and scrub typhus—did not yield any notable findings except for positive Epstein-Barr virus IgG (see table 2).

To investigate potential hidden infiltrative or inflammatory disease, a positron emission tomography–CT scan was carried out. This revealed uneven ¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose accumulation in both axial and appendicular bone marrow. Bilateral lung fields displayed widespread ground-glass opacity accompanied by several fibrotic alterations and subpleural atelectasis. Additional findings included hepatosplenomegaly and the presence of ascites. No clear signs of malignancy or granulomatous disease were identified (figures 1 and 2).

Further assessments demonstrated secondary hyperparathyroidism, abnormalities in electrolytes such as hypocalcaemia and hypokalaemia, vitamin D deficiency, and osteoporosis verified by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, providing an explanation for her repeated bone pain and fractures (see table 3). Electromyography combined with nerve conduction studies showed an asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy characterized by chronic neurogenic alterations in both proximal and distal muscle groups.

The combination of cytopenia, liver impairment, early onset greying of hair, osteoporosis, and neuropathy raised suspicion for a syndromic or genetic cause.

Clinical exome sequencing revealed a heterozygous missense mutation in the PARN gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg, exon 8). This missense alteration affects a residue that is highly conserved, with predictive analyses suggesting it is detrimental to protein function. Nevertheless, the contribution of this variant to the disease cannot be confirmed, leading to its classification as a 'Variant of Uncertain Significance'. Despite the uncertainty regarding the pathogenicity of this particular mutation, both PARN involvement and the patient's clinical presentation align with the phenotypic range observed in TBD.

Differential diagnosis:

The constellation of pancytopenia, unintentional weight loss, premature greying, and fatigue prompted evaluation for hematologic, nutritional, and autoimmune etiologies. The significantly reduced reticulocyte count suggested impaired erythropoiesis, which guided initial focus toward nutritional deficits and disorders characterized by marrow failure.

Severe vitamin B12 deficiency was quickly identified and explained multiple clinical findings, corroborated by bone marrow indications of disrupted erythropoiesis. Nonetheless, after addressing the deficiency, persistent and disproportionately severe systemic abnormalities continued.

A possible immune-related mechanism was briefly entertained, given a positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) and raised inflammatory parameters. Despite this, the lack of hemolysis, negative results on autoimmune serologies, and absence of broader systemic manifestations were inconsistent with autoimmune cytopenias or connective tissue disease.

Hepatic fibrosis (HF) presented without an identifiable cause, as autoimmune and infectious origins had been ruled out. The combination of premature aging symptoms, ongoing cytopenia, and fibrosis affecting both the liver and lungs in a young adult raised suspicion for an obscure inherited condition. While the lack of familial history seemed to contradict a genetic syndrome, the possibility of a de novo mutation could not be discounted.

This prompted consideration of a telomere biology disorder (TBD) capable of imitating acquired disorders and exhibiting diverse degrees of penetrance. Although uncommon, such cases may not display the typical mucocutaneous triad seen in dyskeratosis congenita (DC), and instead may present more insidiously with bone marrow insufficiency, tissue fibrosis, and signs of premature ageing.

Genetic testing confirmed this suspicion by detecting a pathogenic heterozygous mutation in the PARN gene, accounting for the range of her observed clinical manifestations.

Treatment:

The patient received intravenous cyanocobalamin at a dose of 1000 µg per day for five days, transitioned to weekly dosing for four weeks, and then moved to monthly maintenance administration to address a marked deficiency.

Additional supportive interventions consisted of whole blood transfusions to manage symptomatic anemia, oral folic acid at 1 mg daily, vitamin D supplementation (60 000 IU weekly), and elemental calcium (1000 mg daily) targeting osteoporosis. Standard supplementation was used to correct electrolyte disturbances.

Physiotherapy was initiated, and after a diagnosis of a TBD, the patient’s family was provided with genetic counseling.

Outcome and follow-up:

After deficiencies were corrected and supportive treatment provided, the patient experienced alleviation of symptoms. At the 2-week follow-up, her hematologic parameters remained consistent, she reported less fatigue, and there were no additional clinical issues identified.

She maintains ongoing outpatient evaluations to monitor liver and lung function and to assess for potential advancement of bone marrow or systemic disease.

Discussion:

Telomeres are repetitive nucleotide sequences located at the ends of chromosomes, serving to protect genomic stability by preventing chromosome deterioration. During successive rounds of cellular replication, telomeres progressively diminish in length until they reach a threshold that induces cell senescence or programmed cell death. Normally, telomere length is maintained by telomerase, a ribonucleoprotein enzyme complex composed of telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and the telomerase RNA component (TERC), which facilitates telomere extension. Pathogenic mutations in genes encoding the telomerase components disrupt its activity, leading to accelerated telomere shortening and ultimately resulting in a group of inherited disorders known as TBD.

Pathogenic variants in DKC1 inherited in an X-linked recessive manner were the first to demonstrate an association between dyskeratosis congenita (DC) and significantly shortened telomeres, thereby establishing DC as the prototypical telomere biology disorder (TBD). DC is traditionally characterized by the mucocutaneous triad consisting of oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation, or by the presence of two of these findings along with bone marrow failure syndrome (BMFS). Additional recognized clinical features include idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), hepatic fibrosis (HF), and an increased propensity for both hematological and solid tumors. Rare, severe subtypes of TBD with early onset, including Hoyeraal–Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome, arise from pathogenic variants in genes that are also implicated in DC.

TBDs can manifest with mucocutaneous, organ-specific, or hematological symptoms alone, and may occur in adults lacking the hallmark DC triad. In most cases, the hereditary abnormality is obscured by disorders such as aplastic anemia, BMFS, IPF, heart failure, or gastrointestinal conditions. Because the classical triad is absent and clinical onset is delayed, affected individuals are often misdiagnosed, particularly outside specialized centers. Monoallelic mutations in TERT, TERC, and RTEL1 are commonly detected in TBDs, and these disorders frequently present as IPF or BMFS.

The PARN gene, a member of the widely conserved exoribonuclease family, regulates gene expression by trimming the poly(A) tail of mRNA via deadenylation. Extensive sequencing of the 3’ end of TERC RNA revealed that PARN is necessary to eliminate oligo(A) tails added after transcription, which mark nuclear RNAs for degradation. This removal process is essential for proper 3’-end processing and maturation of TERC.

PARN is ranked among the top 20% of genes with the lowest tolerance to rare, disruptive mutations, underscoring its critical importance and supporting the principle of haploinsufficiency in affected individuals. Germline pathogenic mutations in PARN have been established as causes of dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive type 6 (DKCB6), as well as the broader DC/TBD spectrum. Cases of HH syndrome have been described in patients with biallelic PARN mutations. In contrast, monoallelic germline pathogenic mutations have been associated with telomere-related PFBMFT4. The connection between PARN and IPF was initially reported with the discovery of novel monoallelic variants; later investigations identified rare monoallelic PARN variants in 5 of 262 sporadic IPF patients subjected to whole-exome sequencing, reinforcing an association that now encompasses more than 65 reported cases in published literature.1517 Nevertheless, the full spectrum of clinical manifestations linked to PARN mutations has yet to be clarified, owing to the paucity of documented cases.

Importantly, our patient possessed a monoallelic missense mutation in PARN, c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg), which alters a highly conserved amino acid within the R3H domain (positions 178–245). Four out of five in silico missense prediction tools (LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, and SIFT) indicate that this variant likely has a harmful impact on protein function. Applying the ACMG/AMP guidelines for variant classification, this mutation fulfills several supporting criteria: PM1—the amino acid change occurs within a crucial, well-characterized functional domain (R3H domain) lacking known benign variants; PM2—the variant is either not present or exceedingly rare in population databases; PP3—numerous computational programs forecast a deleterious effect on the gene or its product; and PP2—missense mutations are a prevalent cause of disease in PARN, which itself is associated with a low frequency of benign missense changes. Though the pathogenicity of this specific variant remains unclear, it may be pertinent to TBDs, considering PARN's documented function and similar PARN missense variants—including p.Lys59Arg (ClinVar ID: VCV000424063.2) and p.Ala383Val (ClinVar ID: VCV000180661.1)—which ClinVar lists as ‘likely pathogenic’.

TBDs can be influenced by multiple genes, varying modes of inheritance, incomplete penetrance, and somatic genetic rescue, which can result in differential effects across organ systems. Furthermore, environmental exposures and lifestyle factors can impact the ability of distinct organs to replicate. In the case of our patient, these factors may account for the clinical presentation, even though only a single PARN allele variant was detected.

Monoallelic PARN mutations are predominantly linked to IPF rather than primary hematologic disorders; however, the development of cytopenia in our patient suggests a possible underlying TBD, potentially increasing vulnerability of the bone marrow to hematopoietic challenges, such as significant vitamin B12 deficiency. Shortened telomeres reduce the proliferative capacity of hematopoietic stem cells, fostering premature cellular aging and heightening the risk for BMFS. While the cytopenia in this patient was likely caused by profound vitamin B12 deficiency, the identification of a PARN variant highlights the necessity for continued clinical and hematologic surveillance to monitor for progression to marrow failure in this genetically at-risk individual.

Cirrhosis has frequently been overlooked in TBD, though recent data indicates it may occur more commonly than previously thought. In a retrospective study from the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry utilizing transient elastography, 88.8% of adult individuals with pathogenic variants in TERC or TERT demonstrated at least moderate hepatic fibrosis (HF), with 33.3% presenting with cirrhosis. Another similar report described a case featuring pancytopenia and HF in a patient possessing monoallelic mutations in both PARN (c.1613C>G) and TERT (c.2476G>A). Conversely, our case involved a patient with only a monoallelic PARN mutation, who nevertheless developed both hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. While monoallelic PARN is frequently linked to idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), its influence on telomerase activity could contribute to the HF generally associated with TERT or TERC mutations. Collectively, these observations broaden the recognized clinical manifestations of PARN-related TBD and highlight the need to include HF within its phenotype.

The definitive diagnosis of suspected TBD relies on genetic analysis utilizing next-generation sequencing, along with flow cytometry-based fluorescence in situ hybridisation assays to assess telomere length. Management is predominantly supportive and individualized, based on the severity of symptoms, the requirement for organ transplantation, and strategies to reduce risk factors. Haematopoietic stem cell transplantation offers a possible cure for individuals presenting with haematologic involvement. Although preclinical research and limited clinical trials indicate that androgens such as danazol and nandrolone may promote telomere extension, their long-term outcomes have not yet been established.

This patient exhibited signs consistent with TBD, HF, and IPF. After thorough assessment to rule out more prevalent etiologies, whole-exome sequencing revealed the pathogenic gene involved. An exhaustive review of the literature helped to broaden our knowledge of this uncommon manifestation. Supportive care was administered, and her relatives received genetic counseling. She was informed regarding her diagnosis and acknowledged that definitive treatment was not available. At her 2-week review, hematologic parameters were unchanged and no additional symptoms or evidence of disease progression were noted.
