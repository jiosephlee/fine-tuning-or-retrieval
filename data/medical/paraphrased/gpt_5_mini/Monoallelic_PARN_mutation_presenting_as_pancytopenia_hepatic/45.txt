Title: Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

Jinal Soni, Karun Saathveeg Sam, Vinus Taneja, Pooja Khosla

Abstract:

Summary: A female in her late twenties presented with five years of worsening fatigue and generalized weakness. Physical examination demonstrated features suggestive of accelerated ageing, anemia, neuropathy, and enlargement of liver and spleen. Laboratory evaluation revealed pancytopenia, dimorphic anemia, profound vitamin B12 deficiency, hepatic fibrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis. Although nutritional deficiencies were addressed, the presence of cytopenias, premature greying of hair, osteoporosis, and fibrosis of hepatic and pulmonary tissue raised suspicion for an underlying genetic disorder. Clinical exome sequencing detected a single-allele PARN missense mutation (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg), designated as a Variant of Uncertain Significance. Pathogenic germline mutations in PARN are recognized in dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive type 6 (DKCB6), as well as in telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis or bone marrow failure type 4. The correlation between clinical presentation and genetics in this patient supported the diagnosis of a telomere biology disorder (TBD). This case underscores the need to include TBDs in the differential diagnosis of young adults presenting with unexplained multisystem involvement, even in the absence of classic mucocutaneous findings. Prompt identification facilitates appropriate genetic counseling, surveillance, and consideration of bone marrow transplantation for progressive disease.

Background:

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) represent uncommon syndromes associated with accelerated ageing that result from inherited mutations in genes responsible for telomere maintenance, ultimately producing telomeres that are unusually short or not functioning properly. Cells with high proliferative activity—such as those found in the bone marrow, skin, mucosal surfaces, hair follicles, and liver—are most commonly impacted. TBDs encompass a range of clinical manifestations, with dyskeratosis congenita (DC) serving as the primary example. Additional forms of TBD include Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome.

Of the numerous mutations found within these disorders, PARN mutations display unique clinical characteristics; individuals with mutations affecting both alleles develop autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), whereas single-allele mutations more commonly contribute to telomere-associated pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4).

Here, we report on a female patient in her late twenties who experienced persistent fatigue and symptoms affecting multiple organ systems, culminating in a diagnosis of a telomere biology disorder (TBD).

Case presentation:

A female patient in her late twenties was evaluated at our tertiary care center due to a 5-year progression of generalized weakness and early onset of fatigue. Her physical abilities steadily diminished over time, making it increasingly difficult to manage daily tasks in comparison to individuals of similar age. During the past three months, her muscle weakness significantly intensified, resulting in reduced self-sufficiency and necessitating help with self-care activities. Additional symptoms included shortness of breath on exertion, occasional chest pain, unintentional weight loss, and loss of appetite.

Upon assessment, her haemodynamic status was stable. Significant observations were pallor, icterus, early onset greying of hair, hyperpigmentation over the knuckles, and a smooth tongue with loss of lingual papillae.

Musculoskeletal evaluation demonstrated thoracic scoliosis along with atrophy of both thenar eminences. On abdominal examination, hepatomegaly was detected, extending 5 cm below the right costal margin with a total span of 18 cm. The respiratory exam showed diminished breath sounds in the bilateral infra-scapular areas. Cardiovascular findings were normal.

Investigations:

The initial laboratory assessment indicated pancytopenia and a decreased reticulocyte percentage (0.39%). Examination of the peripheral blood smear showed dimorphic red cells, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and neutrophils with increased segmentation, all pointing to impaired erythropoiesis. Serum assays demonstrated profound deficiencies in vitamin B12 and folate. Iron parameters were characterized by raised serum iron and ferritin levels, supporting the diagnosis of ineffective erythropoiesis (see table 1).

Bone marrow evaluation exhibited active production of all three hematopoietic lineages, marked erythroid expansion, and augmented iron reserves, with no signs indicative of malignancy, dysplastic changes, or infiltrative pathology; cytogenetic results were within normal limits.

Indicators of hemolysis included increased lactate dehydrogenase and elevated indirect bilirubin. The direct antiglobulin test (DAT) returned a positive result for IgG (2+), while the indirect DAT was negative. Nevertheless, the suppressed reticulocyte output and bone marrow findings aligned more closely with a process of ineffective erythropoiesis as opposed to immune-mediated destruction of blood cells in the periphery.

In order to assess for gastrointestinal malabsorption, an upper GI endoscopy was conducted, demonstrating normal findings on gross examination. Duodenal histology indicated mild chronic gastritis accompanied by a decreased number of parietal cells, which may have led to compromised intrinsic factor production and resultant B12 deficiency. Nevertheless, the magnitude of clinical symptoms seemed excessive in relation to what would be expected from nutritional deficiency alone.

Concerns regarding hepatic impairment led to additional imaging and evaluation. Abdominal ultrasound identified enlargement of both liver and spleen. Transient elastography findings were consistent with grade II hepatic steatosis (controlled attenuation parameter: 295 dB/m) and established cirrhosis (liver stiffness: 18.1 kPa). Elevated inflammatory markers were also documented. Despite this, autoimmune screening, including antinuclear and autoimmune hepatitis antibodies, yielded negative results. Broad infectious testing for causes such as viral hepatitis, HIV, Cytomegalovirus, and scrub typhus was essentially non-contributory except for the detection of Epstein-Barr virus IgG positivity (table 2).

To investigate possible hidden infiltrative or inflammatory conditions, a positron emission tomography–CT scan was conducted. This revealed uneven ¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose accumulation throughout the bone marrow of both the axial and appendicular skeleton. There was widespread ground-glass opacity accompanied by numerous fibrotic and subpleural atelectatic alterations across both lungs. Additionally, hepatosplenomegaly and ascites were observed, although there were no signs suggesting clear malignancy or granulomatous disorder (see figures 1 and 2).

Other notable results consisted of secondary hyperparathyroidism, disruptions in electrolyte balance (including hypocalcaemia and hypokalaemia), deficiency of vitamin D, and osteoporosis verified by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry. These findings accounted for her repeated episodes of bone pain and fractures (table 3). Electromyography, along with nerve conduction studies, demonstrated an asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy accompanied by chronic neurogenic alterations affecting both proximal and distal muscle groups.

The combination of cytopenia, liver dysfunction, early onset greying of hair, osteoporosis, and neuropathy suggested the possibility of a syndromic or genetic origin.

Clinical exome sequencing revealed a heterozygous missense variant in the PARN gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg, exon 8). This variant results in a change at a highly conserved amino acid position and is anticipated to adversely affect protein function. Nevertheless, its significance in the context of the patient’s disease remains undetermined; thus, it has been designated as a 'Variant of Uncertain Significance'. While the pathogenic status of this particular variant is unclear, the association of PARN and the patient’s clinical presentation align with the features of TBD.

Differential diagnosis:

The constellation of pancytopenia, weight loss, early onset greying, and fatigue in this patient prompted evaluation for hematologic, nutritional, and autoimmune etiologies. A significantly reduced reticulocyte count indicated impaired erythropoiesis, prompting initial consideration of possible nutrient deficiencies or bone marrow failure syndromes.

Severe vitamin B12 deficiency was quickly identified and accounted for multiple clinical manifestations, with bone marrow findings corroborating ineffective erythropoiesis. Nevertheless, the patient’s widespread dysfunction persisted at a level that was not explained by the corrected deficiencies.

A transient suspicion of immune-mediated disease emerged due to a positive DAT and raised inflammatory markers. However, the lack of hemolysis, negative results for autoimmune serologies, and absence of systemic symptoms did not support a diagnosis of autoimmune cytopenia or connective tissue disease.

Hepatic fibrosis (HF) presented without an identifiable cause, as both infectious and autoimmune origins had been ruled out. The presence of premature ageing signs, ongoing cytopenia, and fibrotic changes affecting both the lungs and liver in a young adult raised the likelihood of an underlying genetic disorder that had not yet been recognized. Despite a lack of familial cases suggesting inheritance, the possibility of a de novo mutation could not be dismissed.

This prompted consideration of a telomere biology disorder (TBD) capable of imitating acquired disorders and demonstrating inconsistent penetrance. While uncommon, it might not show the typical mucocutaneous triad seen in dyskeratosis congenita (DC) and may present more inconspicuously with bone marrow failure, organ fibrosis, and signs of premature aging.

Genetic testing confirmed this suspicion by revealing a disease-causing heterozygous mutation in the PARN gene, offering a cohesive rationale for her diverse clinical manifestations.

Treatment:

The patient received intravenous cyanocobalamin at a dose of 1000 µg per day over 5 days, transitioned to weekly administrations for 4 weeks, and subsequently maintained monthly dosing to address severe deficiency.

Supportive care involved whole blood transfusions for management of symptomatic anemia, daily oral folic acid at 1 mg, weekly vitamin D at 60 000 IU, and elemental calcium at 1000 mg daily for treatment of osteoporosis. Electrolyte disturbances were managed with conventional supplementation.

Physiotherapy was initiated, and genetic counselling was provided to her family after the diagnosis of a TBD.

Outcome and follow-up:

After deficiencies were corrected and supportive treatment provided, the patient experienced a reduction in symptoms. At the 2-week follow-up visit, her hematologic parameters remained consistent, her fatigue was alleviated, and there were no additional clinical issues noted.

She is still undergoing routine outpatient monitoring to assess hepatic and pulmonary function and to observe for changes in bone marrow or systemic disease involvement.

Discussion:

Telomeres are repetitive DNA sequences located at the ends of chromosomes, which function to protect chromosomes from deterioration and maintain genomic stability. During cell division, these telomeric regions progressively shorten until they reach a threshold that initiates cellular senescence or programmed cell death. Normally, telomere length is sustained by the activity of telomerase, a ribonucleoprotein enzyme made up of telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and the telomerase RNA component (TERC), which extends telomeres. Nevertheless, pathogenic mutations in genes coding for telomerase components compromise its enzymatic action, causing accelerated telomere shortening. This leads to a spectrum of hereditary disorders collectively known as TBD.

Pathogenic variants in DKC1 inherited in an X-linked recessive pattern were the first to connect dyskeratosis congenita (DC) with significantly shortened telomeres, positioning DC as the classic example of a telomere biology disorder (TBD). DC is typically characterized by the mucocutaneous triad consisting of oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation, or by the presence of two of these signs in conjunction with bone marrow failure syndrome (BMFS). Additional documented features include idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), heart failure (HF), and elevated risk for both hematological and solid tumor malignancies. Severe childhood-onset TBD variants—namely Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome—also stem from mutations in genes associated with DC.

TBDs can appear with involvement limited to mucocutaneous tissues, specific organs, or hematologic abnormalities, and may emerge in adults absent the characteristic DC triad. Often, the underlying hereditary anomaly is obscured by concurrent conditions, including aplastic anemia, bone marrow failure syndromes (BMFS), idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), heart failure (HF), or gastrointestinal disorders. Because of the lack of classical symptoms and late clinical onset, affected individuals are commonly misdiagnosed, particularly if evaluated outside of specialized facilities. Monoallelic mutations in TERT, TERC, and RTEL1 are most frequently detected in TBDs, commonly presenting as IPF or BMFS.

The PARN gene is a member of a well-preserved group of exoribonucleases and functions to reduce mRNA poly(A) tail length by deadenylation, thereby influencing gene regulation. Comprehensive sequencing of the 3’ terminal region of TERC RNA has demonstrated that PARN is essential for eliminating oligo(A) tails added after transcription, which mark nuclear RNAs for decay; this process is necessary for the proper maturation of the TERC’s 3’ end.

PARN ranks among the 20% of genes exhibiting minimal tolerance to infrequent, disruptive mutations, underscoring its critical function and reinforcing the idea of haploinsufficiency in affected individuals. Inherited pathogenic variants in PARN are established contributors to dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), as well as the broader classification of DC/TBD. Cases of HH syndrome have been associated with biallelic PARN mutations. Telomere-related PFBMFT4 has been linked to monoallelic pathogenic PARN variants in the germline. The connection between PARN and IPF was initially revealed following the detection of novel monoallelic variants, and subsequent investigations identified rare monoallelic PARN variants in five out of 262 sporadic IPF patients subjected to whole-exome sequencing, thus recognizing a growing association that has now been noted in over 65 published cases.1517 Nevertheless, due to the small number of documented instances, the full spectrum of clinical manifestations associated with PARN mutations remains inadequately characterized.

Of particular interest, our patient possesses a heterozygous missense alteration in PARN, c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg), which impacts a highly conserved amino acid within the R3H domain (residues 178–245). Four out of five computational missense prediction programs (LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, and SIFT) indicate that this variant is likely detrimental to protein function. According to ACMG/AMP guidelines for variant interpretation, several supporting criteria are fulfilled: PM1—the amino acid change is located within a key, well-established functional region (R3H domain) where benign variation has not been described; PM2—the variant is absent or extremely infrequent in population data; PP3—multiple in silico algorithms predict harmful consequences for gene or protein function; PP2—the PARN gene exhibits a low frequency of benign missense changes, with missense variants frequently implicated in disease pathogenesis. Although the clinical significance of this variant is currently unresolved, its possible association with TBDs is substantiated by the functional role of PARN and similarities with other PARN missense variants—including p.Lys59Arg (ClinVar ID: VCV000424063.2) and p.Ala383Val (ClinVar ID: VCV000180661.1)—that have been listed as ‘likely pathogenic’ in the ClinVar database.

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) involve multiple genes, exhibit varying inheritance patterns, and are characterized by incomplete penetrance and somatic genetic rescue, each of which can impact organ systems differently. Moreover, environmental exposures and lifestyle choices may alter the proliferative ability of distinct organs. These intricate factors may account for our patient's clinical presentation, even though only a single-allele PARN mutation was detected.

Monoallelic PARN variants are typically linked to idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) rather than primary hematologic abnormalities; however, the cytopenia observed in this patient suggests an underlying TBD, which could predispose the bone marrow to heightened vulnerability during hematopoietic stress, such as profound vitamin B12 deficiency. Reduced telomere length restricts hematopoietic stem cell proliferation, leading to premature cellular aging and an increased likelihood of bone marrow failure syndromes (BMFS). Although the patient's cytopenia most probably resulted from marked vitamin B12 deficiency, the discovery of a PARN mutation highlights the need for sustained clinical and hematologic surveillance to identify any emerging marrow failure characteristics in this genetically susceptible individual.

Cirrhosis related to telomere biology disorders (TBD) has frequently been overlooked; nonetheless, recent data indicate that its occurrence may be higher than once thought. A retrospective study conducted through the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry utilizing transient elastography demonstrated that 88.8% of adult individuals with pathogenic mutations in TERC or TERT exhibited at least moderate hepatic fibrosis (HF), with 33.3% progressing to cirrhosis. Another report in the literature detailed a patient presenting with pancytopenia and HF, who carried monoallelic variants in both PARN (c.1613C>G) and TERT (c.2476G>A). In comparison, our case involved a patient with only a monoallelic PARN mutation who nonetheless developed hepatic as well as pulmonary fibrosis. While monoallelic PARN mutations are typically linked to idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), their downstream effects on telomerase activity may explain the presence of HF generally associated with TERT or TERC alterations. Collectively, these observations broaden the known clinical manifestations of PARN-related TBDs and highlight the significance of recognizing hepatic fibrosis within its clinical presentation.

The definitive diagnosis of suspected telomere biology disorders (TBD) involves genetic analysis via next-generation sequencing, as well as assessment of telomere length using flow cytometry-based fluorescence in situ hybridisation techniques. Management primarily focuses on supportive care, individualized according to the intensity of symptoms, the need for organ transplantation, and minimization of risk factors. Haematopoietic stem cell transplantation offers a curative option for those exhibiting hematologic complications. Although limited preclinical and early-phase clinical investigations have indicated that androgens such as danazol and nandrolone may promote telomere extension, the long-term implications of these therapies are still unclear.

The patient exhibited symptoms consistent with TBD, HF, and IPF. After a comprehensive assessment ruling out frequently encountered etiologies, whole-exome sequencing revealed the responsible genetic abnormality. A thorough review of relevant literature helped deepen insight into this uncommon manifestation. Supportive care was implemented, and her family received genetic counseling. She comprehended the prognosis and acknowledged the absence of curative therapies. At her follow-up visit two weeks later, her hematologic parameters were unchanged and there was no evidence of further clinical decline.
