Title: Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

Jinal Soni, Karun Saathveeg Sam, Vinus Taneja, Pooja Khosla

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late twenties presented with five years of progressively worsening fatigue and generalized weakness. Physical examination revealed evidence of accelerated ageing, anaemia, neuropathy, and enlargement of both liver and spleen. Laboratory investigations demonstrated pancytopenia, dimorphic anaemia, marked deficiency of vitamin B12, as well as findings of hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. Although her nutritional deficiencies were treated, the combined findings of multiple cytopenias, early greying of hair, osteoporosis, and fibrosis affecting both liver and lungs prompted consideration of an underlying genetic etiology. Clinical exome sequencing detected a heterozygous missense variant in PARN (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg), designated as a Variant of Uncertain Significance. Pathogenic germline PARN variants have previously been linked to dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), as well as to telomere-associated pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure 4. The alignment of clinical findings with genetic results in this patient was consistent with a diagnosis of telomere biology disorder (TBD). This report underscores the need to suspect TBDs in younger adults who present with unexplained multisystem involvement, even when typical mucocutaneous manifestations are missing. Prompt identification is vital for appropriate genetic counseling, close monitoring, and, in progressive cases, evaluation for bone marrow transplantation.

Background:

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) represent a group of uncommon premature aging conditions arising from inherited mutations in genes associated with telomere maintenance, resulting in telomeres that are unusually short or impaired in function. The cell populations most impacted are those undergoing frequent division, including bone marrow, skin, mucosal surfaces, hair follicles, and hepatocytes. TBDs manifest with a variety of clinical presentations, among which dyskeratosis congenita (DC) serves as the classic example. Additional variants comprise Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome.

Of the mutations recognized within this category of disorders, PARN variants are associated with a unique clinical presentation. Biallelic mutations result in autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), whereas monoallelic mutations are typically linked to telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4).

Here, we report the case of a female patient in her late twenties who experienced persistent fatigue and involvement of multiple organ systems, ultimately leading to the diagnosis of a telomere biology disorder (TBD).

Case presentation:

A female patient in her late twenties was referred to our tertiary facility after experiencing a gradual deterioration in generalized weakness and early onset fatigue over a period of five years. Throughout this time, her physical abilities became increasingly limited, hindering her from carrying out day-to-day tasks in comparison to others her age. Within the most recent three months, her muscle weakness had intensified, leaving her dependent on help for personal care. Additionally, she described symptoms of shortness of breath with exertion, episodes of chest discomfort, decreased appetite, and unintentional weight loss.

Upon assessment, her hemodynamic status was stable. Significant clinical signs were pallor, icterus, early graying of hair, knuckle hyperpigmentation, and a smooth, bald tongue with atrophy of the lingual papillae.

Musculoskeletal evaluation demonstrated thoracic scoliosis and atrophy of both thenar eminences. On abdominal examination, the liver was enlarged, palpable 5 cm below the right costal margin, measuring 18 cm in span. Respiratory assessment identified diminished breath sounds in both infra-scapular areas. Cardiovascular evaluation did not reveal any abnormalities.

Investigations:

The initial laboratory workup demonstrated pancytopenia accompanied by a reduced reticulocyte percentage (0.39%). Examination of the peripheral blood smear revealed dimorphic red cells, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and neutrophils with excessive segmentation—findings indicative of impaired erythropoiesis. Serum assays identified marked deficiencies in both vitamin B12 and folate. Iron panel results showed increased serum iron and ferritin, aligning with the presence of ineffective erythropoiesis (refer to table 1).

Bone marrow analysis showed haematopoiesis across all three cell lineages, pronounced erythroid hyperplasia, and expanded iron reserves, with no features of malignancy, dysplastic changes, or infiltration; cytogenetic testing returned normal results.

Indicators of haemolysis consisted of heightened lactate dehydrogenase and an increase in indirect bilirubin. The direct antiglobulin test (DAT) yielded a positive result for IgG at 2+, whereas the indirect test was negative. Nonetheless, the diminished reticulocyte count and marrow findings favored ineffective erythropoiesis over peripheral immune-driven cell destruction.

To investigate the possibility of gastrointestinal malabsorption, an upper GI endoscopy was conducted and was grossly unremarkable. Histological analysis of duodenal samples revealed mild chronic gastritis with a decrease in parietal cell numbers, which could have played a role in B12 deficiency by impairing intrinsic factor synthesis. Nevertheless, the extent of clinical manifestations was out of proportion to those expected from nutritional deficiencies alone.

Abnormal liver function raised concern for further hepatobiliary issues, prompting additional imaging and evaluation. Abdominal ultrasonography identified enlargement of both the liver and spleen. Transient elastography demonstrated grade II hepatic steatosis, as indicated by a controlled attenuation parameter of 295 dB/m, along with cirrhotic changes, with liver stiffness measured at 18.1 kPa. Elevated inflammatory markers were observed. Despite these findings, autoimmune screening—including testing for antinuclear and autoimmune hepatitis antibodies—yielded negative results. Evaluation for infectious etiologies such as viral hepatitis, HIV, Cytomegalovirus, and scrub typhus was generally negative, with the exception of a positive Epstein-Barr virus IgG result (see table 2).

Positron emission tomography–CT was utilized to investigate possible occult infiltrative or inflammatory processes. The imaging demonstrated uneven ¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose accumulation within both axial and appendicular bone marrow compartments. Both lungs exhibited widespread ground-glass opacities along with numerous fibrotic areas and subpleural atelectasis. Additional findings included hepatosplenomegaly and ascites; however, there were no signs consistent with active malignancy or granulomatous pathology (figures 1 and 2).

Other notable results consisted of secondary hyperparathyroidism, abnormalities in electrolytes (including hypocalcaemia and hypokalaemia), vitamin D deficiency, and osteoporosis, which was demonstrated by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry and accounted for her repeated episodes of bone pain and fractures (table 3). Electromyography along with nerve conduction studies identified an asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy, featuring chronic neurogenic alterations affecting both proximal and distal muscles.

The aggregate of clinical features—cytopenia, impaired hepatic function, early greying, osteoporosis, and neuropathy—prompted consideration of a syndromic or genetic cause.

Clinical exome sequencing revealed a heterozygous missense mutation in the PARN gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg, exon 8). This missense mutation affects a highly conserved amino acid and is anticipated to negatively impact protein function. Despite this, its contribution to the disease could not be determined; as a result, the variant has been classified as a 'Variant of Uncertain Significance'. While the pathogenicity of this particular mutation has not been established, both the implication of PARN and the patient’s clinical presentation align with the known manifestations of TBD.

Differential diagnosis:

The combination of pancytopenia, unintentional weight loss, premature greying, and fatigue in this patient prompted evaluation for hematological, nutritional, and autoimmune conditions. The significantly reduced reticulocyte count indicated suppressed erythropoiesis, which initially suggested possible nutrient deficiencies or bone marrow failure syndromes.

A pronounced vitamin B12 deficiency was identified early in the workup and explained several clinical findings, as corroborated by bone marrow evidence of impaired erythropoiesis. Nonetheless, despite correction of the vitamin deficiencies, the patient's multi-system impairment persisted and was out of proportion to what would be expected.

Consideration was given to an immune-mediated etiology following the detection of a positive DAT and elevated markers of inflammation. However, the absence of hemolysis, negative autoimmune laboratory tests, and lack of broader systemic manifestations made autoimmune cytopenias or connective tissue disorders unlikely.

The underlying cause of hepatic fibrosis (HF) could not be determined, with both infectious and autoimmune etiologies having been ruled out. The unusual combination of early onset aging characteristics, sustained cytopenia, and fibrosis affecting both lung and liver tissue in a young adult patient raised suspicion of an obscure genetic disorder. Despite the lack of familial history, the possibility of a de novo genetic mutation was still considered.

This prompted consideration of a telomere biology disorder (TBD) capable of resembling acquired diseases and demonstrating varying degrees of penetrance. While uncommon, such disorders might not show the typical mucocutaneous triad seen in dyskeratosis congenita (DC), instead presenting in a more insidious manner with bone marrow failure, fibrotic changes in organs, and signs of premature ageing.

Genetic testing supported this by revealing a disease-causing heterozygous mutation in the PARN gene, thus accounting for the diverse clinical manifestations observed in this patient.

Treatment:

The patient received intravenous cyanocobalamin at a dose of 1000 µg per day for five days, followed by weekly administration for four weeks, and then transitioned to monthly maintenance therapy to address the marked deficiency.

Supportive care consisted of whole blood transfusions to manage symptomatic anemia, oral folic acid (1 mg daily), and vitamin D (60 000 IU weekly) together with elemental calcium (1000 mg daily) for treatment of osteoporosis. Electrolyte disturbances were managed with routine supplementation.

The patient additionally participated in physiotherapy, and her family was provided with genetic counseling upon confirmation of a TBD diagnosis.

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient's symptoms improved after her deficiencies were addressed and supportive therapy was provided. At her two-week follow-up, blood counts remained stable, fatigue had lessened, and there were no additional clinical issues identified.

She is being monitored through regular outpatient appointments to assess hepatic and pulmonary function and to track any changes related to bone marrow or systemic disease involvement.

Discussion:

Telomeres are repetitive DNA segments located at the termini of chromosomes, functioning to maintain genomic stability by protecting these ends from deterioration. As cells divide, telomeres progressively shorten until they reach a threshold length that induces cellular aging or programmed cell death. Normally, the telomerase enzyme, a ribonucleoprotein complex comprising telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and the telomerase RNA component (TERC), extends telomeres to preserve their length. Mutations affecting genes that encode telomerase components disrupt this enzymatic activity, causing accelerated telomere shortening and giving rise to a collection of inherited disorders known as TBD.

X-linked recessive pathogenic variants in DKC1 were the first to demonstrate the association between dyskeratosis congenita (DC) and significantly shortened telomeres, positioning DC as the archetype of telomere biology disorders (TBDs). Traditionally, DC is characterized by the mucocutaneous triad: oral leukoplakia, dystrophy of the nails, and reticular pigmentation of the skin; alternatively, the presence of any two of these features along with bone marrow failure syndrome (BMFS) also defines the disorder. Additional recognized clinical manifestations include idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), hepatic fibrosis (HF), and elevated risk for both hematological and solid tumor malignancies. Several severe, childhood-onset TBD variants—including Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome—are likewise linked to mutations in genes associated with DC.

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) can exhibit isolated mucocutaneous symptoms, organ-specific manifestations, or hematological abnormalities, and may first appear in adults without the characteristic dyskeratosis congenita (DC) triad. Commonly, the inherited abnormalities are obscured by diagnoses such as aplastic anemia, bone marrow failure syndromes (BMFS), idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), or hepatic failure (HF), as well as gastrointestinal conditions. The absence of the classic triad combined with late onset often leads to frequent misdiagnosis, particularly in non-specialist settings. Monoallelic mutations in TERT, TERC, and RTEL1 are frequently detected in TBDs, with clinical presentations often including IPF or BMFS.

The PARN gene is part of a well-preserved group of exoribonucleases that modulate gene expression by trimming the poly(A) tail of mRNA via deadenylation. Extensive sequencing of TERC RNA at the 3' terminal revealed that PARN plays a crucial role in eliminating oligo(A) tails added after transcription, which mark nuclear RNAs for degradation, and is essential for proper 3'-end processing of TERC.

PARN ranks among the 20% of genes most intolerant to rare, loss-of-function mutations, underscoring its critical importance and reinforcing the principle of haploinsufficiency in individuals carrying such variants. Pathogenic germline alterations in PARN have been established as causative in dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive type 6 (DKCB6), as well as the wider spectrum of DC/TBD disorders. Cases of HH syndrome have been linked to biallelic mutations in PARN. Monoallelic germline pathogenic variants have been associated with telomere-related PFBMFT4. The connection between PARN and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) was initially demonstrated through the discovery of novel monoallelic variants. Subsequent investigations reported rare monoallelic PARN variants in five out of 262 individuals with sporadic IPF who had whole-exome sequencing, further supporting this relationship, which has now been described in over 65 cases in the published literature.1517 Despite this, the complete clinical spectrum attributable to PARN mutations remains largely undefined, given the relatively small number of documented cases.

Importantly, this patient was found to have a heterozygous missense mutation in PARN, specifically c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg), which affects a highly conserved amino acid within the R3H domain (residues 178–245). Four out of five computational tools (LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, and SIFT) predict this variant to be functionally deleterious. Applying the ACMG/AMP guidelines for variant interpretation, several criteria are met: PM1—this amino acid change occurs within a critical and well-characterized functional domain (R3H domain) where benign variants have not been observed; PM2—the variant is either not present or extremely uncommon in population genetic datasets; PP3—multiple computational algorithms suggest a harmful impact on gene or protein function; PP2—PARN is known to have a low rate of benign missense variation and missense mutations are a recognized mechanism of pathogenesis. While its clinical significance is still unclear, the variant’s possible association with TBDs is bolstered by PARN's known involvement and by comparison with other missense changes—such as p.Lys59Arg (ClinVar ID: VCV000424063.2) and p.Ala383Val (ClinVar ID: VCV000180661.1)—that are listed as ‘likely pathogenic’ in ClinVar.

In TBDs, the involvement of multiple genes, diverse patterns of inheritance, incomplete penetrance, and somatic genetic rescue can influence various organ systems differently. Furthermore, environmental exposures and individual lifestyle choices may differentially impact the replicative ability of specific tissues. In our patient, these underlying complexities could account for the clinical presentation observed, despite the detection of only a single monoallelic PARN variant.

While monoallelic PARN variants are typically linked to IPF, rather than primary hematological abnormalities, the occurrence of cytopenia in this case suggests the possibility of a latent TBD that may increase the vulnerability of the bone marrow to stressors such as profound vitamin B12 deficiency. Telomere attrition reduces the proliferative lifespan of hematopoietic stem cells, thereby promoting premature cellular senescence and heightening the risk of BMFS. Though it is most probable that the patient’s cytopenia resulted from severe vitamin B12 deficiency, identification of the PARN variant highlights the necessity for continued clinical and hematological surveillance to detect any progression towards a bone marrow failure phenotype in this genetically susceptible patient.

Cirrhosis has historically been underdiagnosed in TBD, though new data indicates it could be more common than previously recognized. A retrospective review from the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry employing transient elastography revealed that 88.8% of adults carrying pathogenic mutations in TERC or TERT exhibited at least moderate hepatic fibrosis (HF), with cirrhosis present in 33.3%. Similarly, a published case reported a patient with pancytopenia and HF, possessing monoallelic variants in both PARN (c.1613C>G) and TERT (c.2476G>A). Conversely, our patient carried only a single PARN mutation but nonetheless developed hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. While monoallelic PARN is most often linked with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), its effects on telomerase function may explain the occurrence of HF typically associated with TERC or TERT mutations. These observations broaden the clinical scope of PARN-related TBDs and highlight the necessity of including HF in its clinical phenotype.

Definitive diagnosis of suspected telomere biology disorders (TBD) relies on genetic analysis using next-generation sequencing, as well as flow cytometry-based fluorescence in situ hybridisation to assess telomere length. Management strategies focus on supportive care, addressing symptom burden, transplantation of affected organs when indicated, and reducing modifiable risk factors. Haematopoietic stem cell transplantation may offer a curative approach for individuals presenting with haematologic involvement. Although preclinical investigations and limited clinical studies have indicated that androgens such as danazol and nandrolone may lengthen telomeres, the persistence of these effects over time is not yet established.

The patient exhibited symptoms indicative of TBD, HF, and IPF. A thorough assessment ruled out prevalent etiologies, after which whole-exome sequencing revealed the pathogenic gene. A comprehensive review of existing literature further clarified this uncommon clinical scenario. Supportive measures were administered, and her relatives received genetic counseling. The patient comprehended her diagnosis and acknowledged that curative treatments were unavailable. Two weeks after initial evaluation, her hematological parameters were unchanged and she exhibited no additional clinical decline.
