Title: Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

Jinal Soni, Karun Saathveeg Sam, Vinus Taneja, Pooja Khosla

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late twenties presented with a five-year history marked by progressively worsening fatigue and generalized weakness. Physical examination showed features suggestive of premature aging, anemia, neuropathy, and hepatosplenomegaly. Laboratory assessments revealed pancytopenia, dimorphic anemia, profound vitamin B12 deficiency, as well as evidence of hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. Although correction of nutritional deficiencies was achieved, the combination of cytopenias, early onset greying, osteoporosis, and fibrosis in both the liver and lungs suggested a possible underlying genetic etiology. Clinical exome sequencing detected a heterozygous PARN missense mutation (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg), which was interpreted as a Variant of Uncertain Significance. Pathogenic germline variants in PARN have previously been linked to dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), and to telomere-associated pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure 4. The clinical and genetic findings in this individual were consistent with a diagnosis of telomere biology disorder (TBD). This report underscores the need to consider TBDs in young adults presenting with unexplained multisystem involvement, even when typical mucocutaneous stigmata are lacking. Prompt diagnosis is essential for appropriate genetic counseling, surveillance, and timely consideration of bone marrow transplant in cases showing clinical progression.

Background:

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) represent uncommon syndromes characterized by accelerated aging, resulting from inherited mutations in genes associated with telomere maintenance, which cause telomeres to become excessively short or nonfunctional. Tissues composed of rapidly proliferating cells—such as those in the bone marrow, epidermis, mucous membranes, hair follicles, and liver—are principally involved. TBDs encompass a spectrum of clinical presentations, with dyskeratosis congenita (DC) serving as the classic example. Additional disorders in this category comprise Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome.

Within this class of disorders, mutations in PARN present a unique clinical profile: when both alleles are affected, they result in autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), whereas single-allele variants are more frequently associated with telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4).

We describe a woman in her late 20s with prolonged fatigue and multisystem involvement who was ultimately diagnosed with a TBD.

Case presentation:

A female patient in her late twenties was referred to our tertiary care facility, presenting with a 5-year progression of generalised weakness and early onset fatigue. During this period, her functional status gradually deteriorated, limiting her capacity for daily tasks in comparison to individuals of similar age. In the preceding 3 months, her weakness had intensified, affecting her ability to care for herself and necessitating help. Additionally, she experienced shortness of breath with exertion, occasional chest discomfort, unintentional weight loss, and decreased appetite.

On examination, she was haemodynamically stable. Notable findings included pallor, icterus, premature greying of hair, knuckle hyperpigmentation and a bald tongue with atrophy of lingual papillae.

On musculoskeletal assessment, thoracic scoliosis was observed along with atrophy of the thenar muscles on both sides. The abdominal evaluation demonstrated hepatomegaly, with the liver palpable 5 cm below the right costal margin and measuring 18 cm in span. Pulmonary examination identified diminished breath sounds in the infra-scapular areas bilaterally. The cardiovascular examination did not reveal any abnormalities.

Investigations:

The initial laboratory workup revealed pancytopenia accompanied by a decreased reticulocyte count (0.39%). Examination of the peripheral blood smear showed a dimorphic red cell population, the presence of basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and hypersegmented neutrophils, findings that indicate ineffective erythropoiesis. Serum assays demonstrated pronounced deficiencies in both vitamin B12 and folate. Iron panel results indicated increased levels of serum iron and ferritin, further supporting the diagnosis of ineffective erythropoiesis (see table 1).

The bone marrow evaluation demonstrated active production of all three hematopoietic lineages, notable erythroid hyperplasia, and increased iron deposition; no signs of malignancy, dysplasia, or infiltrative pathology were present. Cytogenetic studies yielded normal results.

Indicators of hemolysis were present, with raised lactate dehydrogenase and elevated unconjugated bilirubin levels. The direct antiglobulin test (DAT) returned positive for IgG at a strength of 2+, whereas the indirect antiglobulin test was negative. However, given the blunted reticulocyte response and the marrow characteristics, the overall picture pointed more towards ineffective erythropoiesis as opposed to immune-mediated red cell destruction in the periphery.

An upper gastrointestinal endoscopy was carried out to assess for malabsorption, and no gross abnormalities were noted. Histological examination of the duodenal tissue revealed mild chronic gastritis and a decrease in parietal cell numbers, which may have led to diminished intrinsic factor synthesis and subsequent vitamin B12 malabsorption. Nonetheless, the degree of clinical symptoms seemed excessive compared to what would be expected from nutritional deficiency by itself.

Further investigation was undertaken due to evidence of hepatic dysfunction. Abdominal ultrasonography identified both hepatomegaly and splenomegaly. Transient elastography demonstrated grade II hepatic steatosis, with a controlled attenuation parameter measured at 295 dB/m, and findings consistent with cirrhosis, as indicated by liver stiffness of 18.1 kPa. Elevated inflammatory markers were noted. Nonetheless, autoimmune panels, including antinuclear and autoimmune hepatitis antibodies, returned negative results. Testing for infectious causes, such as viral hepatitis, HIV, Cytomegalovirus, and scrub typhus, did not reveal any significant findings, with the exception of a positive Epstein-Barr virus IgG result (see table 2).

Positron emission tomography–CT was conducted to investigate potential hidden infiltrative or inflammatory processes. The imaging revealed uneven ¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose uptake throughout both axial and appendicular bone marrow. Additionally, both lungs demonstrated widespread ground-glass opacities with several areas of fibrosis and subpleural atelectasis. Hepatosplenomegaly and ascites were also present. However, there were no signs of clear malignancy or granulomatous disease (see figures 1 and 2).

Other notable results showed the presence of secondary hyperparathyroidism, electrolyte imbalances such as hypocalcaemia and hypokalaemia, along with vitamin D deficiency. Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry confirmed osteoporosis, providing an explanation for her repeated episodes of bone pain and fractures (table 3). Electromyography and nerve conduction assessments indicated an asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy, characterized by chronic neurogenic alterations in both proximal and distal muscle groups.

Given the constellation of findings—cytopenia, hepatic dysfunction, premature greying, osteoporosis and neuropathy—a syndromic or genetic aetiology was suspected.

Clinical exome sequencing revealed a heterozygous missense mutation in the PARN gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg, located in exon 8). This mutation affects an amino acid residue that is highly conserved and is predicted to negatively impact protein function. Nevertheless, its precise contribution to the disease could not be determined, and as such, it is classified as a ‘Variant of Uncertain Significance.’ Despite the uncertain pathogenicity of this particular variant, both the patient’s clinical presentation and PARN gene involvement align with the spectrum observed in telomere biology disorders (TBD).

Differential diagnosis:

The combination of pancytopenia, unintended weight loss, early onset greying of hair, and fatigue in this patient prompted evaluation for hematologic, autoimmune, and nutritional etiologies. The significantly decreased reticulocyte count suggested impaired erythropoiesis, guiding the initial differential towards possible nutritional deficiencies and bone marrow failure syndromes.

Severe deficiency of vitamin B12 was identified promptly and explained multiple clinical findings, as corroborated by bone marrow findings indicative of ineffective erythropoiesis. Nonetheless, despite supplementation and resolution of these deficiencies, the systemic abnormalities persisted and were more pronounced than expected.

A transient consideration was given to an immune-mediated etiology in light of the positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) and increased inflammatory markers. However, the lack of hemolysis, negative results on autoimmune serologies, and no evidence of systemic involvement made autoimmune cytopenia or connective tissue disease unlikely.

There was no identifiable cause for hepatic fibrosis (HF), as both infectious and autoimmune origins were ruled out. The combination of early-onset aging characteristics, ongoing cytopenias, and fibrosis affecting both hepatic and pulmonary tissues in a young adult raised concern for an unrecognized genetic condition. Despite an initial lack of familial occurrence suggesting otherwise, the potential for a de novo genetic mutation could not be dismissed.

This raised the suspicion of a telomere biology disorder (TBD) capable of resembling acquired diseases and exhibiting heterogeneous penetrance. Although uncommon, it might not display the characteristic mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita (DC), instead presenting in a more indolent fashion with bone marrow failure, fibrosis of organs, and signs consistent with premature aging.

This was substantiated by genetic analysis, which identified a pathogenic monoallelic variant in the PARN gene, providing a unifying explanation for the constellation of her clinical features.

Treatment:

The patient was treated with intravenous cyanocobalamin 1000 µg daily for 5 days, then weekly for 4 weeks, followed by monthly maintenance to correct severe deficiency.

Supportive care involved administering whole blood transfusions to manage symptomatic anemia, along with oral folic acid at a dose of 1 mg per day. Vitamin D was provided at 60,000 IU weekly, combined with 1,000 mg of elemental calcium daily to address osteoporosis. Standard supplementation was used to correct any electrolyte disturbances.

She also underwent physiotherapy, and her family received genetic counselling following diagnosis of a TBD.

Outcome and follow-up:

After her deficiencies were addressed and supportive measures provided, the patient experienced alleviation of symptoms. At the two-week follow-up visit, her hematologic values remained steady, her fatigue had lessened, and there were no additional clinical issues observed.

She continues to receive regular outpatient follow-up for surveillance of hepatic and pulmonary function and to monitor the progression of bone marrow or systemic involvement.

Discussion:

Telomeres are repetitive nucleotide sequences located at the termini of chromosomes, functioning to maintain genomic stability by protecting chromosomal ends from deterioration. During successive cycles of cellular replication, these telomeric sequences progressively shorten, eventually reaching a threshold that induces cellular senescence or programmed cell death. Normally, telomere length is sustained by telomerase, a ribonucleoprotein enzyme composed of telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and the telomerase RNA component (TERC), which enables telomere extension. Nevertheless, deleterious mutations in genes encoding telomerase complex components compromise telomerase activity, resulting in accelerated telomere shortening and giving rise to a spectrum of inherited disorders collectively referred to as TBD.

Pathogenic variants in DKC1 exhibiting X-linked recessive inheritance initially demonstrated the association between dyskeratosis congenita (DC) and significantly shortened telomeres, establishing DC as the archetypal telomere biology disorder (TBD). Traditionally, DC is diagnosed based on the presence of the mucocutaneous triad: oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation, or by two of these signs in combination with bone marrow failure syndrome (BMFS). Additional recognized features include idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), hepatic fibrosis (HF), and a heightened risk for both hematologic and solid tumors. Severe, early-onset TBD subtypes—including Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome—are likewise caused by mutations in genes associated with DC.

TBDs can manifest as isolated mucocutaneous findings, involvement of specific organs, or haematological abnormalities, and may become apparent in adulthood in the absence of the characteristic DC triad. Frequently, underlying inherited abnormalities are obscured by overlapping conditions such as aplastic anaemia, bone marrow failure syndromes (BMFS), idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), or hepatic fibrosis (HF), as well as gastrointestinal disorders. The absence of the classic triad and a delayed onset of symptoms often lead to misdiagnosis, particularly outside of specialised medical facilities. Monoallelic mutations in TERT, TERC, and RTEL1 are commonly associated with TBDs, usually presenting as IPF or BMFS.

The PARN gene is part of a well-conserved group of exoribonucleases and functions to shorten the poly(A) tails on mRNAs via deadenylation, thereby influencing gene expression. High-throughput sequencing of the 3’ end of TERC RNA has demonstrated that PARN is necessary for trimming post-transcriptionally added oligo(A) tails, which mark nuclear RNAs for degradation, an essential step in the 3’ end processing of TERC.

PARN ranks among the 20% of genes with the lowest tolerance to rare, disruptive mutations, underscoring its critical function and supporting the idea of haploinsufficiency in heterozygous carriers. Germline pathogenic mutations in PARN have been identified as causes of dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), as well as the broader DC/TBD spectrum. Cases of HH syndrome have involved biallelic pathogenic variants in PARN. Monoallelic germline mutations have been associated with telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis with bone marrow failure, type 4 (PFBMFT4). The association between PARN and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) was first noted with the detection of novel monoallelic mutations, and later research found rare monoallelic PARN variants in five out of 262 sporadic IPF patients who had undergone whole-exome sequencing. This link has now been described in more than 65 reported cases in the literature.1517 Nonetheless, the full spectrum of clinical manifestations due to PARN mutations remains unclear, largely because of the small number of documented cases.

Importantly, our patient carried a heterozygous missense alteration in PARN, specifically c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg), which affects a highly conserved amino acid within the R3H domain (residues 178–245). Four out of five in silico tools for missense prediction (LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, and SIFT) indicate that this variant is functionally damaging. Assessment according to ACMG/AMP criteria highlights several supporting points: PM1—the change occurs within the critical, well-characterized R3H domain where benign variations have not been documented; PM2—the variant is extremely rare or not observed in population databases; PP3—multiple computational analyses predict a deleterious effect on gene function; PP2—the PARN gene displays a low frequency of benign missense changes, with disease commonly resulting from such variants. Despite the current uncertainty surrounding the clinical impact of this variant, its possible connection to TBDs is underpinned by the gene’s known biological function and by comparable missense changes in PARN—such as p.Lys59Arg (ClinVar ID: VCV000424063.2) and p.Ala383Val (ClinVar ID: VCV000180661.1)—that are classified as ‘likely pathogenic’ in ClinVar.

Various genes, diverse patterns of inheritance, incomplete penetrance, and somatic genetic rescue can each uniquely impact the organs involved in telomere biology disorders (TBDs). Environmental exposures and lifestyle choices may also distinctly alter the replicative function across different tissues. For our patient, such intricate factors might account for the clinical presentation seen, even though only a single PARN allele variant was detected.

While single-copy PARN mutations are more frequently linked to IPF than to primary hematological symptoms, the development of cytopenia in our patient suggests the potential for an underlying TBD that could increase the bone marrow’s vulnerability to haematopoietic challenges such as profound vitamin B12 deficiency. Shortening of telomeres reduces the proliferative capacity of haematopoietic stem cells, resulting in premature cellular senescence and elevating the risk of BMFS. Although the cytopenia in this case was most likely due to significant vitamin B12 deficiency, detection of a PARN variant highlights the need for continual clinical and hematologic follow-up to monitor for the emergence of marrow failure features in this individual with genetic susceptibility.

Cirrhosis associated with TBD has frequently gone unrecognized, though recent data indicate it may occur more frequently than previously believed. A retrospective study from the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry utilizing transient elastography revealed that 88.8% of adult patients with pathogenic variants in TERC or TERT exhibited at least moderate hepatic fibrosis (HF), while 33.3% had progressed to cirrhosis. Another report described an individual with pancytopenia and hepatic fibrosis who possessed heterozygous mutations in both PARN (c.1613C>G) and TERT (c.2476G>A). In contrast, the patient described here had only a heterozygous PARN variant, yet nonetheless manifested both liver and lung fibrosis. While monoallelic PARN mutations are most often linked to idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), their effect on telomerase activity may explain the hepatic fibrosis more commonly associated with TERT or TERC alterations. Collectively, these observations broaden the recognized clinical manifestations of PARN-related TBDs and highlight the need to include hepatic fibrosis within its clinical phenotype.

The definitive diagnosis of suspected TBD relies on genetic analysis utilizing next-generation sequencing, alongside flow cytometry fluorescence in situ hybridization assays for telomere length assessment. Management strategies focus primarily on supportive care, addressing symptom burden, organ transplant requirements, and minimizing risk factors. Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation may offer a curative option for individuals presenting with hematological involvement. Preliminary evidence from preclinical and limited clinical studies indicates that androgens such as danazol and nandrolone might induce telomere elongation; however, the durability of these effects over the long term has yet to be established.

The patient exhibited signs consistent with TBD, HF, and IPF. After thorough assessment to rule out other frequently encountered etiologies, whole-exome sequencing revealed the pathogenic gene involved, and an in-depth literature review offered further insight into this uncommon clinical manifestation. Supportive measures formed the cornerstone of her management, and her family received genetic counseling. She comprehended her diagnosis and recognized that definitive therapeutic interventions were not possible. At the two-week review, her hematological parameters were unchanged, with no new evidence of disease progression.
