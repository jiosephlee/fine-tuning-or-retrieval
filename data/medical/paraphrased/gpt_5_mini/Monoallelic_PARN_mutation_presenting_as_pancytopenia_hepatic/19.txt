Title: Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

Jinal Soni, Karun Saathveeg Sam, Vinus Taneja, Pooja Khosla

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late twenties reported a five-year progression of fatigue and generalized weakness. Physical examination was notable for findings consistent with premature aging, anemia, neuropathy, and hepatosplenomegaly. Laboratory results demonstrated pancytopenia, dimorphic anemia, profound vitamin B12 deficiency, as well as evidence of hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. Although her nutritional deficits were addressed, the combination of cytopenias, early greying, osteoporosis, hepatic fibrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis suggested a possible underlying genetic condition. Clinical exome sequencing revealed a heterozygous missense variant in PARN (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg), categorized as a Variant of Uncertain Significance. Pathogenic germline variants in PARN are implicated in dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), as well as in telomere-associated pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure 4. Correlation of clinical and genetic findings in this patient supported a diagnosis consistent with a telomere biology disorder (TBD). This report underscores the need to evaluate for TBDs in young adults presenting with unexplained multisystem involvement, even in the absence of classic mucocutaneous signs. Timely identification is essential to inform genetic counselling, monitoring, and consideration of hematopoietic stem cell transplantation for progressive disease.

Background:

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) represent uncommon syndromes characterized by early-onset aging due to inherited mutations in genes associated with telomere function, resulting in excessively short or defective telomeres. Cell populations with high proliferative activity, such as those in bone marrow, skin, mucosal surfaces, hair, and liver cells, are particularly susceptible. The spectrum of TBD encompasses a variety of clinical manifestations, with dyskeratosis congenita (DC) serving as the prototypical disorder. Additional conditions within this category include Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome.

Within this collection of disorders, PARN mutations display a unique clinical presentation; individuals with two affected alleles develop autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), whereas single affected alleles are typically associated with telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4).

Here, we report on a female patient in her late twenties exhibiting chronic fatigue and symptoms affecting multiple organ systems, who was eventually found to have a telomere biology disorder (TBD).

Case presentation:

A woman in her late twenties was referred to our tertiary facility after experiencing generalized weakness and early fatigue that had gradually deteriorated over five years. During this period, she noticed a marked decrease in her capacity to carry out everyday tasks relative to others her age. In the most recent three months, her symptoms intensified further, significantly limiting her independence in self-care and necessitating support. Additional complaints included shortness of breath on exertion, occasional chest pain, unintentional weight loss, and a decreased appetite.

Upon assessment, the patient maintained haemodynamic stability. Significant physical signs comprised pallor, icterus, early greying of hair, hyperpigmentation over the knuckles, and a smooth, bald tongue with lingual papillae atrophy.

Musculoskeletal evaluation demonstrated thoracic scoliosis along with atrophy of the thenar eminences bilaterally. Abdominal assessment identified hepatomegaly, measuring 5 cm below the right costal margin with a total liver span of 18 cm. Examination of the respiratory system indicated diminished breath sounds in both infra-scapular areas. No abnormalities were detected during cardiovascular examination.

Investigations:

Initial laboratory testing identified pancytopenia accompanied by a decreased reticulocyte count (0.39%). Examination of the peripheral blood smear revealed dimorphic erythrocytes, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and hypersegmented neutrophils—findings indicative of impaired erythropoiesis. Assessment of serum levels demonstrated marked deficiencies in both vitamin B12 and folate. Iron panel results showed increased serum iron and ferritin levels, supporting the presence of ineffective erythropoiesis (see table 1).

Evaluation of the bone marrow revealed trilineage hematopoiesis along with erythroid hyperplasia and increased iron deposition, with no signs of malignancy, dysplasia, or infiltrative pathology; cytogenetic testing yielded normal results.

Indicators of hemolysis included raised lactate dehydrogenase and indirect hyperbilirubinemia. The direct antiglobulin test (DAT) returned positive for IgG (2+), whereas the indirect antiglobulin test was negative. Nevertheless, the low reticulocyte count and bone marrow findings favored a diagnosis of ineffective erythropoiesis rather than immune-mediated peripheral destruction.

To assess for possible gastrointestinal malabsorption, the patient underwent an upper gastrointestinal endoscopy, which was grossly unremarkable. Biopsies taken from the duodenum demonstrated mild chronic gastritis and a decrease in parietal cells, findings that may have contributed to vitamin B12 deficiency due to diminished intrinsic factor synthesis. Nonetheless, the clinical manifestations appeared more pronounced than would be expected from nutritional deficiency alone.

Due to evidence of liver dysfunction, additional imaging and evaluations were conducted. Abdominal ultrasonography identified both hepatomegaly and splenomegaly. Transient elastography findings included grade II hepatic steatosis (controlled attenuation parameter: 295 dB/m) and cirrhosis (liver stiffness: 18.1 kPa). Inflammatory markers were elevated. Autoimmune panels—consisting of antinuclear antibody and autoimmune hepatitis-specific antibodies—yielded negative results. Broad infectious disease testing for viral hepatitis, HIV, Cytomegalovirus, and scrub typhus returned unremarkable results, except for a positive IgG antibody to Epstein-Barr virus (see table 2).

Positron emission tomography–CT was conducted to assess for hidden infiltrative or inflammatory processes. The imaging revealed uneven ¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose accumulation within both the axial and appendicular bone marrow. Additionally, there were widespread ground-glass opacities observed, accompanied by numerous fibrotic areas and subpleural atelectasis throughout both lungs. Hepatosplenomegaly and ascites were also present, though no clear signs of malignancy or granulomatous disease were identified (figures 1 and 2).

Other notable results comprised secondary hyperparathyroidism, electrolyte imbalances such as hypocalcaemia and hypokalaemia, deficiency in vitamin D, and osteoporosis, which was validated by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry. These findings accounted for her persistent bone pain and recurrent fractures (table 3). Electromyography along with nerve conduction studies indicated an asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy, characterized by chronic neurogenic alterations in both proximal and distal muscle groups.

The combination of cytopenia, hepatic impairment, early onset greying, osteoporosis, and neuropathy led to suspicion of a syndromic or genetic origin.

Clinical exome sequencing revealed a heterozygous missense mutation in the PARN gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg, located in exon 8). This missense change affects a highly conserved amino acid and is predicted to negatively impact protein function. Nonetheless, its contribution to the disease has not been determined, and thus it has been classified as a ‘Variant of Uncertain Significance’. Despite the ambiguity regarding the pathogenicity of this particular variant, the patient's phenotype and the gene implicated align with the clinical spectrum associated with TBD.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient presented with pancytopenia, unexplained weight loss, early greying of hair, and fatigue, prompting evaluation for hematologic, nutritional, and autoimmune etiologies. The discovery of a substantially decreased reticulocyte count indicated impaired erythropoiesis, initially directing focus toward nutritional deficits and bone marrow failure syndromes.

A pronounced vitamin B12 deficiency was rapidly identified, accounting for several clinical findings, and corroborated by bone marrow demonstration of ineffective erythropoiesis. Nevertheless, correction of these deficits failed to fully resolve the widespread systemic dysfunction, which remained unusually severe and persistent.

An immune-related mechanism was considered transiently because of a positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) and raised inflammatory markers. However, in the absence of hemolysis, negative autoimmune serological tests, and no evidence of systemic features, autoimmune cytopenias or connective tissue disorders were deemed unlikely.

Hepatic fibrosis (HF) was without an identifiable cause, as autoimmune and infectious etiologies had been ruled out. The combination of signs indicative of early ageing, sustained cytopenia, and fibrotic changes affecting both pulmonary and hepatic tissues in a young individual raised concerns for a previously unrecognized inherited condition. Even though there was no familial precedent to support a genetic syndrome, the possibility of a de novo genetic alteration could not be excluded.

This prompted the consideration of a telomere biology disorder (TBD) that may resemble acquired diseases and exhibit differing levels of penetrance. While uncommon, such disorders may not display the characteristic mucocutaneous triad typically associated with dyskeratosis congenita (DC), but may instead present more insidiously with bone marrow failure, organ fibrosis, and signs of premature ageing.

Genetic testing confirmed this suspicion by revealing a pathogenic heterozygous mutation in the PARN gene, offering a comprehensive explanation for the range of clinical manifestations observed in this patient.

Treatment:

The patient received intravenous cyanocobalamin at a dose of 1000 µg each day for 5 days, subsequently once weekly for 4 weeks, and then transitioned to monthly administration for ongoing correction of profound deficiency.

Additional supportive care comprised whole blood transfusions to address symptomatic anaemia, oral folic acid (1 mg per day), vitamin D (60 000 IU weekly), and elemental calcium (1000 mg daily) to manage osteoporosis. Electrolyte disturbances were addressed using routine supplementation.

Physiotherapy was also initiated, and genetic counselling was provided to her family after the diagnosis of a TBD.

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient experienced relief of symptoms after addressing her deficiencies and receiving supportive management. At the follow-up visit two weeks later, her hematologic parameters remained steady, she reported reduced fatigue, and there were no additional clinical issues identified.

She remains under ongoing outpatient observation to assess hepatic and pulmonary status and to track any advancement of bone marrow or systemic disease.

Discussion:

Telomeres are repetitive DNA segments located at the ends of chromosomes that help maintain genomic stability by protecting chromosomes from deterioration. During normal cell division, these telomeric sequences gradually decrease in length until a threshold is reached, prompting either cell senescence or programmed cell death. Normally, telomerase—a ribonucleoprotein that comprises telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and telomerase RNA component (TERC)—ensures telomere maintenance by lengthening these regions. Nevertheless, mutations in the genes responsible for components of the telomerase complex disrupt the enzyme’s activity, causing accelerated telomere shortening and leading to a spectrum of inherited disorders known as TBD.

X-linked recessive mutations in DKC1 were the first to demonstrate a connection between dyskeratosis congenita (DC) and significantly shortened telomeres, positioning DC as the archetype for telomere biology disorders (TBDs). The traditional clinical diagnosis of DC relies on the presence of the mucocutaneous triad—oral leukoplakia, dystrophic nails, and reticulated skin hyperpigmentation—or two of these findings combined with bone marrow failure syndrome (BMFS). Additional commonly observed manifestations are idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), hepatic fibrosis (HF), and a heightened susceptibility to both hematologic and solid tumors. Several severe forms of early-onset TBDs, including Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome, also arise from mutations in genes associated with DC.

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) can manifest as isolated involvement of mucocutaneous tissues, specific organs, or hematologic abnormalities, and they may occur in adulthood without exhibiting the classical dyskeratosis congenita (DC) triad. Frequently, the underlying hereditary defect is concealed by disorders such as aplastic anemia, bone marrow failure syndromes (BMFS), idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), or hepatic failure (HF), as well as gastrointestinal conditions. In the absence of the classical triad and with delayed symptom onset, these individuals are commonly misdiagnosed, particularly outside of specialized healthcare settings. Monoallelic mutations in the TERT, TERC, and RTEL1 genes are usually observed in TBDs and often manifest clinically as IPF or BMFS.

The PARN gene, part of a well-conserved group of exoribonucleases, functions by trimming the poly(A) tails on mRNAs via deadenylation, thereby controlling gene expression. High-throughput sequencing of the 3' end of TERC RNA demonstrated that PARN is necessary to eliminate oligo(A) tails added post-transcriptionally to nuclear RNAs earmarked for degradation, an essential step in achieving proper 3' end maturation of TERC.

PARN is among the top 20% of genes exhibiting low tolerance to rare and disruptive mutations, underscoring its crucial function and supporting the idea of haploinsufficiency in individuals carrying such variants. Germline pathogenic mutations in PARN are established causes of dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive type 6 (DKCB6), as well as the wider DC/TBD clinical spectrum. Cases of HH syndrome have been associated with biallelic PARN mutations. Monoallelic germline pathogenic PARN variants have also been linked to telomere-related PFBMFT4. The initial association between PARN and IPF was observed following identification of new monoallelic variants; further investigations identified monoallelic rare variants in PARN in five out of 262 patients with sporadic IPF who underwent whole-exome sequencing, contributing to a growing connection now described in more than 65 published cases.1517 Despite these reports, the complete clinical spectrum linked to PARN mutations is still not fully defined owing to the scarcity of documented cases.

Importantly, our patient carried a monoallelic missense variant in PARN, c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg), impacting a highly conserved amino acid within the R3H domain (spanning residues 178–245). According to four out of five in silico missense prediction algorithms (LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, and SIFT), this variant is expected to result in a harmful functional effect. Assessment using ACMG/AMP variant classification guidelines indicated that several supporting criteria are satisfied: PM1—the altered residue is situated within a critical and established functional region (R3H domain) where benign variation has not been observed; PM2—the variant is either not present or extremely uncommon in population datasets; PP3—multiple bioinformatic prediction tools indicate a deleterious impact on the gene or gene product; PP2—PARN is characterized by a low frequency of benign missense variation, with missense mutations frequently implicated in disease. While its clinical significance is uncertain, its potential importance in TBDs is underscored by the established function of PARN and similarities with other PARN missense variants, such as p.Lys59Arg (ClinVar ID: VCV000424063.2) and p.Ala383Val (ClinVar ID: VCV000180661.1), which are designated as ‘likely pathogenic’ in ClinVar.

Several genetic factors—including multiple genes, differing inheritance patterns, incomplete penetrance, and somatic genetic rescue—can exert variable effects on organ involvement in telomere biology disorders (TBDs). Furthermore, environmental influences and lifestyle choices can impact the proliferative abilities of various tissues to differing degrees. In relation to our case, such intricacies could account for the clinical features observed, despite detection of only a single PARN allele variant.

Monoallelic PARN variants are generally associated with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) rather than with primary hematological presentations; however, the cytopenia in this patient suggests a possible underlying TBD, which might increase the susceptibility of bone marrow to hematopoietic challenges such as severe vitamin B12 deficiency. Shortened telomeres decrease the proliferative potential of hematopoietic stem cells, promoting accelerated cell aging and elevating the likelihood of bone marrow failure syndromes (BMFS). Although it is probable that the cytopenia in this patient resulted from significant vitamin B12 deficiency, the presence of the PARN variant emphasizes the necessity of continued clinical and hematological follow-up to monitor for any developing features of marrow failure in this genetically susceptible patient.

Cirrhosis in telomere biology disorders (TBD) has historically been under-recognized; nonetheless, recent data indicate its frequency may be higher than previously thought. A retrospective study from the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry utilizing transient elastography identified that 88.8% of adult individuals harboring pathogenic mutations in TERC or TERT exhibited at least moderate hepatic fibrosis (HF), with 33.3% developing cirrhosis. Another report described a case involving a patient presenting with pancytopenia and hepatic fibrosis, who had monoallelic mutations in both PARN (c.1613C>G) and TERT (c.2476G>A). Conversely, the patient we describe possessed a monoallelic mutation in PARN alone and yet manifested both hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. While monoallelic PARN mutations are often linked to idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), their effect on telomerase activity could contribute to hepatic fibrosis, similar to the presentation associated with TERT or TERC mutations. Collectively, these observations broaden the clinical spectrum associated with PARN-related TBDs and highlight the necessity of recognizing hepatic fibrosis as part of its clinical phenotype.

The definitive diagnosis of suspected telomere biology disorders (TBD) relies on genetic analysis utilizing next-generation sequencing, as well as fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) techniques with flow cytometry to assess telomere length. Present management is primarily supportive and individualized according to the extent of symptoms, necessity for organ transplantation, and modification of risk factors. Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation offers the possibility of cure for individuals exhibiting hematologic involvement. Although preliminary preclinical data and limited clinical reports indicate that androgens such as danazol and nandrolone may lengthen telomeres, the durability of these effects over the long term has yet to be determined.

The patient exhibited symptoms consistent with TBD, HF, and IPF. After conducting a thorough assessment to rule out typical etiologies, whole-exome sequencing revealed the underlying genetic defect. A comprehensive review of the literature expanded our knowledge of this uncommon manifestation. Supportive care was initiated, along with genetic counselling for her relatives. She demonstrated insight into her diagnosis and acknowledged the absence of curative interventions. At the two-week follow-up, her hematologic parameters were unchanged, and there were no additional indications of clinical worsening.
