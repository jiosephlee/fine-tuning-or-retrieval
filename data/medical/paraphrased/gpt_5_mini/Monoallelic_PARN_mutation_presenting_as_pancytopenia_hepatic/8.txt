Title: Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

Jinal Soni, Karun Saathveeg Sam, Vinus Taneja, Pooja Khosla

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late twenties was evaluated for a five-year progression of fatigue and generalized weakness. Physical assessment demonstrated features indicative of accelerated ageing, anaemia, neuropathy, and hepatosplenomegaly. Laboratory analyses revealed pancytopenia, dimorphic anaemia, marked vitamin B12 deficiency, hepatic fibrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis. While nutritional deficiencies were addressed, the combination of cytopenias, early hair greying, osteoporosis, and evidence of hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis prompted suspicion for an underlying genetic disorder. Clinical exome sequencing detected a heterozygous PARN missense mutation (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg), categorized as a Variant of Uncertain Significance. Pathogenic germline alterations in PARN have been linked to dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), as well as to telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure 4. Integration of clinical findings and genetic data in this individual was consistent with a telomere biology disorder (TBD). This case underscores the need to evaluate for TBDs in young adults presenting with unexplained multisystem involvement, even in the absence of hallmark mucocutaneous signs. Prompt diagnosis plays a vital role in informing genetic counseling, ongoing monitoring, and the potential for bone marrow transplantation in cases with disease progression.

Background:

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) represent a group of rare syndromes characterized by accelerated ageing due to inherited mutations in genes responsible for telomere maintenance, resulting in telomeres that are either unusually short or dysfunctional. The primary cellular targets are those with high proliferative rates, such as those found in the bone marrow, skin, mucosal tissues, hair follicles, and liver (hepatocytes). TBDs manifest through a diverse spectrum of clinical presentations, with dyskeratosis congenita (DC) serving as the classical example. Additional conditions within this category comprise Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome.

Within this category of disorders, PARN mutations are notable for their unique clinical presentations: individuals with mutations in both alleles develop autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), whereas those with a single mutated allele are more frequently associated with telomere-associated pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4).

Here, we report on a female patient in her late twenties who presented with chronic fatigue and signs of multisystem disease, eventually receiving a diagnosis of a TBD.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late twenties arrived at our tertiary care center, reporting a 5-year progression of increasing generalized weakness and early onset of fatigue. Her ability to carry out everyday tasks had gradually diminished during this period, and she struggled to keep up with her peers. In the preceding three months, her symptoms intensified to such an extent that she became dependent on others for self-care. Additionally, she described symptoms of shortness of breath upon exertion, occasional chest pain, unintended weight loss, and a loss of appetite.

Upon assessment, she maintained stable haemodynamics. Significant clinical observations were pallor, icterus, early onset greying of hair, hyperpigmentation over the knuckles, and a smooth, bald tongue due to loss of lingual papillae.

The musculoskeletal evaluation identified thoracic scoliosis along with atrophy of the thenar muscles on both hands. Abdominal assessment demonstrated hepatomegaly, with the liver palpable 5 cm below the right costal margin and measuring 18 cm in span. On respiratory examination, breath sounds were diminished in the infra-scapular regions bilaterally. The cardiovascular system revealed no abnormalities.

Investigations:

The initial laboratory assessment indicated pancytopenia along with a decreased reticulocyte count (0.39%). Examination of the peripheral blood smear showed the presence of dimorphic erythrocytes, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and hypersegmented neutrophils, all findings indicative of ineffective erythropoiesis. Serum analysis further demonstrated marked deficiencies in vitamin B12 and folate. Iron studies revealed increased levels of serum iron and ferritin, findings compatible with ongoing ineffective erythropoiesis (see table 1).

Bone marrow analysis demonstrated trilineage haematopoiesis accompanied by erythroid hyperplasia and augmented iron reserves, without any signs of malignant transformation, dysplasia, or infiltrative processes; additionally, cytogenetic testing returned normal results.

Evaluation for haemolysis showed elevated levels of lactate dehydrogenase and indirect hyperbilirubinaemia. The direct antiglobulin test (DAT) was positive for IgG (2+), whereas the indirect antiglobulin test was negative. Nevertheless, the diminished reticulocyte response and marrow examination findings favored a diagnosis of ineffective erythropoiesis over that of peripheral immune-mediated haemolysis.

To assess for potential malabsorption in the gastrointestinal tract, an upper gastrointestinal endoscopy was conducted and revealed no obvious macroscopic abnormalities. Histological analysis of a duodenal biopsy demonstrated mild chronic gastritis accompanied by a decreased number of parietal cells, which may underlie cobalamin (B12) malabsorption due to impaired intrinsic factor secretion. Nevertheless, the clinical manifestations appeared excessively severe relative to what would be expected from nutritional deficiency by itself.

Given signs of hepatic impairment, additional diagnostic imaging and laboratory investigations were pursued. Abdominal ultrasonography identified enlargement of both the liver and spleen (hepatosplenomegaly). Transient elastography revealed evidence of grade II hepatic steatosis (controlled attenuation parameter: 295 dB/m) as well as cirrhosis (liver stiffness: 18.1 kPa). Markers of systemic inflammation were elevated. Autoimmune panels, including antinuclear antibodies and serologic tests for autoimmune hepatitis, were negative. Evaluation for infectious etiologies such as viral hepatitis, HIV, Cytomegalovirus, and scrub typhus yielded no significant findings, aside from a positive Epstein-Barr virus IgG result (table 2).

To assess for hidden infiltrative or inflammatory disease, positron emission tomography–CT was utilized. The imaging revealed irregular ¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose accumulation within both the axial and appendicular bone marrow. Additionally, both lungs demonstrated widespread ground-glass opacities accompanied by numerous fibrotic alterations and subpleural areas of atelectasis. Further findings included enlargement of the liver and spleen as well as the presence of ascites, while no clear signs of malignancy or granulomatous processes were observed (see figures 1 and 2).

Further evaluation identified secondary hyperparathyroidism, abnormalities in electrolyte balance such as hypocalcaemia and hypokalaemia, a deficiency in vitamin D, and osteoporosis as evidenced by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry; these findings accounted for the patient's repeated episodes of bone pain and fractures (table 3). Electromyographic assessment and nerve conduction studies demonstrated an asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy, accompanied by chronic neurogenic alterations in both proximal and distal musculature.

In light of this complex clinical picture—including cytopenia, hepatic dysfunction, premature greying, osteoporosis, and neuropathy—a genetic or syndromic origin was considered likely.

Clinical exome sequencing revealed a single-allele missense mutation in the PARN gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg, exon 8). This missense mutation affects a residue that is highly conserved across species, and computational predictions suggest it is likely to impair protein function. Nonetheless, its exact contribution to the patient’s phenotype could not be determined, leading to its classification as a ‘Variant of Uncertain Significance’. Despite the ambiguous pathogenic status of this particular mutation, both the association of PARN and the patient's observed clinical presentation align with features commonly observed within the TBD spectrum.

Differential diagnosis:

The combination of pancytopenia, unintended weight loss, early onset greying of hair, and fatigue prompted evaluation for haematological, nutritional, and autoimmune causes. The presence of a significantly reduced reticulocyte count was suggestive of defective erythropoiesis, directing attention initially towards possibilities such as nutrient deficiencies and marrow failure syndromes.

Early investigations revealed profound vitamin B12 deficiency, which explained several of the clinical and laboratory abnormalities, including bone marrow findings consistent with ineffective erythropoiesis. Nevertheless, after repletion of the identified deficiencies, the patient continued to experience systemic symptoms that were both persistent and disproportionate to the initial findings.

An immune-mediated etiology was considered for a short period because of a positive DAT in conjunction with increased inflammatory markers. However, the lack of laboratory or clinical evidence of haemolysis, the absence of supportive autoimmune serology, and no features suggestive of connective tissue disease or systemic autoimmune disorder weighed against a diagnosis of autoimmune cytopenia.

The underlying cause of hepatic fibrosis (HF) remained unidentified, as both autoimmune and infectious origins were ruled out. The uncommon combination of early-onset ageing characteristics, ongoing cytopenia, and fibrotic changes in both the liver and lungs in a young adult prompted clinicians to consider an obscure genetic disorder. Despite the initial lack of familial history, which seemed inconsistent with an inherited syndrome, the prospect of a de novo mutation could not be excluded.

This prompted the proposal of a TBD capable of imitating acquired disorders and displaying inconsistent penetrance. Although uncommon, such a disorder might not exhibit the traditional mucocutaneous triad characteristic of DC, and could instead appear with less obvious signs such as bone marrow failure, fibrotic changes in organs, and symptoms of premature ageing.

Genetic testing confirmed this suspicion, revealing a disease-causing monoallelic mutation in the PARN gene, thereby offering a comprehensive rationale for the range of her clinical manifestations.

Treatment:

The patient received intravenous cyanocobalamin at a dosage of 1000 µg per day for 5 days, transitioned to weekly administration over a period of 4 weeks, and subsequently continued with monthly doses for maintenance to address the marked deficiency.

Additional management consisted of whole blood transfusions to manage symptomatic anaemia, oral folic acid (1 mg daily), and vitamin D (60 000 IU weekly) in combination with elemental calcium (1000 mg daily) to treat osteoporosis. Disturbances in electrolyte levels were managed with conventional supplementation protocols.

Physiotherapy was initiated as part of her rehabilitation, and, following the identification of a TBD, her family participated in genetic counselling.

Outcome and follow-up:

After addressing the deficiencies and providing supportive treatment, the patient experienced relief from her symptoms. At the 2-week follow-up appointment, her blood parameters remained stable, her fatigue had lessened, and no additional clinical issues were identified.

She is still undergoing routine outpatient monitoring to assess hepatic and pulmonary function and to observe for any advancement of bone marrow or systemic disease.

Discussion:

Telomeres, composed of repetitive DNA sequences at chromosome termini, play a crucial role in safeguarding genome stability by protecting chromosomal ends from deterioration. During successive rounds of cell division, these telomeric sequences progressively shorten until they reach a threshold length that initiates cellular senescence or programmed cell death. Under normal physiological circumstances, telomere length is sustained by the enzyme telomerase—a ribonucleoprotein complex comprising telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and telomerase RNA component (TERC)—which facilitates the addition of telomeric repeats. Mutations in genes responsible for the telomerase complex, however, compromise its activity, leading to accelerated telomere shortening and ultimately giving rise to a spectrum of inherited disorders collectively known as TBD.

The discovery that X-linked recessive mutations in DKC1 are responsible for DC provided the initial evidence connecting DC to significantly shortened telomeres, establishing DC as the archetypal TBD. The classical clinical criteria for DC include the mucocutaneous triad of oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation, or alternatively, the presence of two of these clinical signs accompanied by bone marrow failure syndrome (BMFS). Additional recognised symptoms encompass idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), HF, and a heightened susceptibility to both haematological and solid cancers. Variants in genes associated with DC are also causative for severe, early-onset TBD forms such as HH syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome.

TBDs can appear with only mucocutaneous symptoms, organ involvement, or haematological abnormalities, and may not exhibit the characteristic DC triad, especially when manifesting in adulthood. Often, the underlying genetic abnormality is obscured by diagnoses such as aplastic anaemia, BMFS, IPF, HF, or various gastrointestinal disorders. In the absence of the classic triad and because clinical symptoms may appear later, misdiagnosis is common, particularly outside of specialized medical centres. TBDs are frequently associated with monoallelic mutations in TERT, TERC, and RTEL1, which tend to present clinically as IPF or BMFS.

The PARN gene, a member of a well-conserved group of exoribonucleases, functions to modulate gene expression by reducing the length of mRNA poly(A) tails via deadenylation. Comprehensive sequencing of the 3-prime end of TERC RNA has revealed that PARN is essential for eliminating oligo(A) tails added post-transcriptionally, which mark nuclear RNAs for degradation. This removal process is critical for the proper maturation of the 3-prime end of TERC.

PARN ranks within the bottom 20% of genes in terms of tolerance to rare, deleterious mutations, underscoring its vital function and supporting the notion of haploinsufficiency among individuals carrying such variants. Pathogenic germline mutations in PARN are established contributors to dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), as well as the more extensive DC/TBD spectrum. Cases of HH syndrome have been linked to biallelic mutations in PARN. Additionally, monoallelic germline pathogenic variants have been associated with telomere-related PFBMFT4. The initial evidence connecting PARN to IPF stemmed from the discovery of new monoallelic variants, and subsequent analyses identified monoallelic rare PARN variants in five out of 262 sporadic IPF patients undergoing whole-exome sequencing. This finding has substantiated a growing relationship, with more than 65 documented instances described in the literature.1517 Nevertheless, because only a small number of cases have been reported, the full clinical spectrum associated with PARN mutations remains largely uncharacterized.

Significantly, the patient carried a monoallelic missense mutation in PARN, specifically c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg), which impacts a highly conserved amino acid located within the R3H domain (residues 178–245). Four out of five computational tools for predicting missense variant impact (LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, and SIFT) suggest this variant is functionally deleterious. According to the ACMG/AMP guidelines for variant interpretation, the variant satisfies several lines of evidence: PM1—it affects a crucial and well-characterized functional region (R3H domain) with no recorded benign variants; PM2—it is either absent from or exceedingly rare in population variant databases; PP3—numerous in silico models consistently predict a damaging effect; and PP2—the PARN gene generally shows a low frequency of harmless missense variation, with pathogenic missense alterations being a recognized cause of disease. Although its pathogenicity remains indeterminate, the variant’s potential connection to TBDs is underlined by PARN’s established biological function and similarity to other PARN missense mutations—such as p.Lys59Arg (ClinVar ID: VCV000424063.2) and p.Ala383Val (ClinVar ID: VCV000180661.1)—which ClinVar classifies as ‘likely pathogenic’.

The manifestation of TBDs across organ systems can be variably shaped by multiple genetic factors, diverse inheritance patterns, incomplete penetrance, and somatic genetic rescue. Moreover, environmental exposures and lifestyle choices can differently impact the regenerative ability of individual organs. In the present case, these intricate mechanisms may underlie the clinical presentation, even though only a single pathogenic allele in PARN was detected.

Monoallelic PARN mutations typically correlate more with IPF than with primary haematological abnormalities; however, the occurrence of cytopenia in this patient suggests a latent TBD, potentially increasing the vulnerability of bone marrow to haematopoietic challenges such as significant vitamin B12 deficiency. Shortened telomeres impair the proliferative capacity of haematopoietic stem cells, precipitating premature cellular ageing and heightened susceptibility to BMFS. Although the patient’s cytopenia is most plausibly a consequence of marked vitamin B12 deficiency, the identification of a PARN variant highlights the necessity for persistent clinical and haematological follow-up to detect any progression toward marrow failure in this genetically predisposed patient.

Cirrhosis in the context of TBD has historically been overlooked; nonetheless, recent data indicate it may occur more frequently than previously recognized. A retrospective study utilizing transient elastography, conducted by the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry, identified that 88.8% of adults carrying pathogenic mutations in either TERC or TERT presented with at least moderate HF, and cirrhosis was observed in 33.3% of cases. Similarly, there is a published report of a patient exhibiting pancytopenia and HF, who possessed monoallelic variants in both PARN (c.1613C>G) and TERT (c.2476G>A). By comparison, our patient possessed a single heterozygous mutation in PARN yet developed both hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. While monoallelic PARN mutations are typically linked to IPF, their effects on telomerase activity might explain the HF more commonly attributed to TERT or TERC mutations. Collectively, these observations broaden the recognized clinical manifestations of PARN-associated TBDs and highlight the need to regard HF as a potential component of the disease phenotype.

The definitive approach for confirming suspected TBD relies on genetic analyses utilizing next-generation sequencing, along with flow cytometry-based fluorescence in situ hybridisation to assess telomere length. Current management primarily focuses on supportive care, individualized based on the severity of symptoms, requirements for organ transplantation, and addressing modifiable risk factors. Haematopoietic stem cell transplantation may offer a curative option for those presenting with haematologic symptoms. Although preclinical research and limited clinical trials indicate that androgens such as danazol and nandrolone may promote telomere extension, the long-term outcomes of such therapies are not yet established.

The patient exhibited clinical signs consistent with TBD, HF, and IPF. A comprehensive assessment ruled out more frequent etiologies, after which whole-exome sequencing revealed the responsible genetic mutation. An in-depth literature review further contextualized this atypical case. Management was primarily supportive, and the patient's family received genetic counseling. The patient demonstrated an awareness of her diagnosis and acknowledged that curative treatments were not available. On review two weeks later, her hematological parameters were unchanged, and her clinical condition had not worsened.
