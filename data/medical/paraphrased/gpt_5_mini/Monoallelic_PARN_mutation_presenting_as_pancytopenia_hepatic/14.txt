Title: Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

Jinal Soni, Karun Saathveeg Sam, Vinus Taneja, Pooja Khosla

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late 20s presented with a five-year course of worsening fatigue and diffuse weakness. Physical examination demonstrated features of early-onset ageing, anaemia, neuropathy, and enlargement of the liver and spleen. Laboratory testing revealed pancytopenia, dimorphic anaemia, profound vitamin B12 deficiency, hepatic fibrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis. Although nutritional deficits were corrected, the presence of persistent cytopenia, premature hair greying, osteoporosis, along with hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis suggested the possibility of an inherited disorder. Clinical exome analysis identified a heterozygous missense mutation in PARN (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg), categorized as a Variant of Uncertain Significance. Germline pathogenic changes in PARN have been linked to dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), as well as telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure 4. The combination of clinical presentation and genetic findings in this case was consistent with a telomere biology disorder (TBD). This case underscores the need to consider TBDs in young adults presenting with otherwise unexplained, multi-organ involvement—even in the absence of classic mucocutaneous signs. Prompt diagnosis is vital to inform genetic counselling, monitoring, and consideration of bone marrow transplantation in progressive disease.

Background:

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) represent a group of uncommon premature ageing conditions resulting from Mendelian mutations in genes associated with telomere maintenance, resulting in excessively short or malfunctioning telomeres. These defects primarily impact cell types with high turnover, such as those in the bone marrow, skin, mucosal linings, hair follicles, and liver (hepatocytes). TBD encompasses a spectrum of clinical presentations, with dyskeratosis congenita (DC) serving as the classic example. Additional forms include Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome.

Within this category of disorders, PARN mutations are associated with a unique clinical presentation; individuals with biallelic alterations develop autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), whereas heterozygous mutations are more commonly linked to telomere-associated pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4).

We describe a woman in her late 20s with prolonged fatigue and multisystem involvement who was ultimately diagnosed with a TBD.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late twenties was evaluated at our tertiary care facility due to a five-year progression of generalized weakness and increasing fatigue with minimal activity. During this period, her overall functional abilities decreased, making it harder for her to manage everyday tasks relative to individuals her age. In the preceding three months, her symptoms intensified, significantly limiting her ability to care for herself and necessitating help from others. Additional complaints included shortness of breath with exertion, episodic chest discomfort, unintentional weight loss, and loss of appetite.

On examination, she was haemodynamically stable. Notable findings included pallor, icterus, premature greying of hair, knuckle hyperpigmentation and a bald tongue with atrophy of lingual papillae.

On musculoskeletal assessment, thoracic scoliosis and atrophy of both thenar eminences were noted. Examination of the abdomen found an enlarged liver, palpable 5 cm beneath the right costal margin with a total span of 18 cm. Assessment of the respiratory system demonstrated diminished breath sounds over both infra-scapular areas. Cardiovascular evaluation revealed no abnormal findings.

Investigations:

The initial lab workup revealed pancytopenia accompanied by a decreased reticulocyte count (0.39%). Examination of the peripheral blood smear identified dimorphic red blood cells, basophilic stippling, the presence of Cabot rings, and neutrophils with hypersegmentation, findings indicative of ineffective erythropoiesis. Serum testing demonstrated marked deficiencies in both vitamin B12 and folate. Iron panel showed increased levels of both serum iron and ferritin, supporting the diagnosis of ineffective erythropoiesis (table 1).

Bone marrow assessment showed active production of all three cell lines, with a predominance of erythroid precursors and increased iron reserves; there were no signs indicative of malignancy, dysplasia, or infiltration, and cytogenetic studies returned normal results.

Haemolysis was suggested by increased lactate dehydrogenase levels and raised unconjugated bilirubin. The direct antiglobulin test (DAT) showed a 2+ positivity for IgG, while the indirect antiglobulin test was negative. Nevertheless, both the diminished reticulocyte count and the marrow findings pointed more towards ineffective erythropoiesis, rather than immune-mediated haemolysis in the periphery.

An upper gastrointestinal endoscopy was undertaken to assess for gastrointestinal malabsorption, revealing no notable abnormalities on gross inspection. Histologic examination of a duodenal biopsy demonstrated mild chronic gastritis along with a decreased number of parietal cells, a potential factor in vitamin B12 malabsorption due to diminished intrinsic factor synthesis. Nevertheless, the extent of clinical symptoms seemed excessive when compared to what would be expected from nutritional deficiency by itself.

The onset of hepatic dysfunction led to additional imaging studies and evaluations. Abdominal ultrasound demonstrated both hepatomegaly and splenomegaly. Transient elastography indicated grade II hepatic steatosis with a controlled attenuation parameter of 295 dB/m, along with cirrhosis as shown by liver stiffness measuring 18.1 kPa. Elevated levels of inflammatory markers were noted. Nevertheless, autoimmune markers—including antinuclear antibody and autoimmune hepatitis antibodies—were negative. Screening for infections such as viral hepatitis, HIV, Cytomegalovirus, and scrub typhus yielded no significant findings, except for a positive IgG to Epstein-Barr virus (see table 2).

A positron emission tomography–CT scan was conducted to investigate possible hidden infiltrative or inflammatory disorders. The imaging demonstrated uneven ¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose uptake throughout the bone marrow of both the axial and appendicular skeleton. Both lungs exhibited widespread ground-glass opacity, as well as multiple areas of fibrosis and subpleural atelectasis. Additionally, hepatosplenomegaly and ascites were present. There were no findings suggestive of frank malignancy or granulomatous disease (figures 1 and 2).

Further evaluation identified secondary hyperparathyroidism, electrolyte imbalances such as hypocalcaemia and hypokalaemia, a deficiency of vitamin D, and osteoporosis demonstrated via dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, which accounted for her repeated episodes of bone pain and fractures (see table 3). Electromyography along with nerve conduction studies indicated an asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy, with longstanding neurogenic alterations observed in both proximal and distal muscle groups.

Given the constellation of findings—cytopenia, hepatic dysfunction, premature greying, osteoporosis and neuropathy—a syndromic or genetic aetiology was suspected.

Clinical exome analysis revealed a heterozygous missense mutation in the PARN gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg, located in exon 8). This variant changes an amino acid that is highly conserved and is predicted to impair protein function. Nevertheless, its association with the disease has not been established, leading to its classification as a ‘Variant of Uncertain Significance’. While the disease-causing potential of this particular variant has not been confirmed, both the presence of the PARN alteration and the patient’s presentation align with features commonly observed in the spectrum of TBD.

Differential diagnosis:

The occurrence of pancytopenia, unintentional weight loss, early greying of hair, and persistent fatigue in the patient prompted consideration of hematologic, nutritional, and autoimmune etiologies. The discovery of a significantly reduced reticulocyte count suggested impaired erythropoiesis, which initially directed the differential diagnosis toward causes such as nutritional deficiencies and bone marrow failure syndromes.

A marked deficiency of vitamin B12 was identified promptly and explained many of the clinical findings, which was corroborated by bone marrow findings consistent with ineffective erythropoiesis. Nonetheless, despite addressing these deficiencies, the extent and persistence of systemic impairment were greater than expected.

An immune-mediated etiology was entertained because of the positive DAT and increased inflammatory markers. However, the lack of evidence for haemolysis, negative results on autoimmune serology testing, and no systemic manifestations did not support diagnoses such as autoimmune cytopenia or connective tissue disease.

The cause of hepatic fibrosis (HF) was uncertain, as autoimmune and infectious etiologies had been ruled out. The coexistence of early-onset aging characteristics, ongoing cytopenia, and fibrotic changes in both the lungs and liver of this young adult led clinicians to consider a possible underlying genetic disorder. While no family history was evident, making a hereditary syndrome appear unlikely, the potential for a spontaneous de novo mutation could not be dismissed.

This prompted consideration of a telomere biology disorder (TBD) capable of imitating acquired diseases and displaying a broad spectrum of penetrance. Although infrequent, it might not exhibit the typical mucocutaneous triad seen in dyskeratosis congenita (DC), instead presenting more insidiously through bone marrow failure, organ fibrosis, and signs of premature aging.

This was substantiated by genetic analysis, which identified a pathogenic monoallelic variant in the PARN gene, providing a unifying explanation for the constellation of her clinical features.

Treatment:

The patient was treated with intravenous cyanocobalamin 1000 µg daily for 5 days, then weekly for 4 weeks, followed by monthly maintenance to correct severe deficiency.

Supportive care involved administering whole blood transfusions to manage symptomatic anemia, along with daily oral folic acid at 1 mg. For osteoporosis, vitamin D was provided at a dose of 60,000 IU each week and elemental calcium at 1,000 mg daily. Standard electrolyte supplementation was used to address any imbalances.

She also underwent physiotherapy, and her family received genetic counselling following diagnosis of a TBD.

Outcome and follow-up:

After addressing her deficiencies and providing supportive management, the patient experienced relief of her symptoms. At the 2-week follow-up visit, her hematologic parameters remained steady, she reported less fatigue, and no additional clinical issues were noted.

She continues to receive regular outpatient follow-up for surveillance of hepatic and pulmonary function and to monitor the progression of bone marrow or systemic involvement.

Discussion:

Telomeres are repetitive DNA elements located at the termini of chromosomes that help maintain genome stability by protecting chromosomes from damage. During successive rounds of cell division, telomeres progressively shorten until they reach a threshold length, at which point cells either enter senescence or undergo apoptosis. Normally, telomerase—a ribonucleoprotein composed of telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and the telomerase RNA component (TERC)—sustains telomere length by adding repeats to chromosome ends. Mutations in genes coding for components of the telomerase machinery compromise the enzyme’s activity, resulting in accelerated telomere loss, which underlies a spectrum of hereditary disorders known as telomere biology disorders (TBD).

Pathogenic X-linked recessive mutations in DKC1 were the first to connect dyskeratosis congenita (DC) with significantly shortened telomeres, thereby identifying DC as the archetypal telomere biology disorder (TBD). DC is characteristically described by the presence of the mucocutaneous triad—oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and a reticular pattern of skin pigmentation—or by the occurrence of any two of these findings in conjunction with bone marrow failure syndrome (BMFS). Additional recognized clinical features include idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), hepatic fibrosis (HF), as well as a higher susceptibility to both haematological and solid tumors. Several severe telomere biology disorder subtypes presenting in childhood, such as Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome, are also associated with variants in genes involved in DC.

Telomere biology disorders can manifest as isolated involvement of mucocutaneous tissues, specific organs, or hematologic abnormalities, and may first appear in adulthood in the absence of the classic dyskeratosis congenita triad. Frequently, the underlying hereditary defect is obscured by presentations resembling aplastic anemia, bone marrow failure syndromes, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, heart failure, or gastrointestinal conditions. In the absence of hallmark features and because of delayed onset of symptoms, affected individuals are commonly misdiagnosed, particularly outside of specialized medical centers. Monoallelic mutations in TERT, TERC, and RTEL1 genes are most often detected in cases of TBDs, typically manifesting clinically as idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis or bone marrow failure syndromes.

The PARN gene, a member of a well-conserved exoribonuclease family, functions in the modulation of gene expression by reducing mRNA poly(A) tail length via deadenylation. Deep sequencing of the 3′ end of TERC RNA demonstrated that PARN is essential for eliminating post-transcriptionally added oligo(A) tails that mark nuclear RNAs for degradation, an essential step in the maturation of TERC’s 3′ end.

PARN ranks within the 20% of genes that are least accepting of rare, disruptive mutations, reinforcing its critical biological function and supporting the hypothesis of haploinsufficiency in heterozygous individuals. Pathogenic germline mutations in PARN have been established as causes of dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive type 6 (DKCB6), as well as the broader spectrum associated with DC/TBD. Biallelic variants in PARN have also been identified in patients with HH syndrome. Monoallelic germline pathogenic mutations have been linked to telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis bone marrow failure syndrome 4 (PFBMFT4). The connection between PARN and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) was initially demonstrated through the identification of new monoallelic variants, and later reports described rare heterozygous PARN variants in 5 of 262 individuals with sporadic IPF analyzed by whole-exome sequencing, an association now noted in more than 65 published cases.1517 Nonetheless, the complete clinical spectrum related to PARN mutations is still not well characterized, primarily due to the small number of cases described to date.

Importantly, this patient was found to carry a heterozygous missense mutation in PARN, c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg), which alters a highly conserved amino acid within the R3H domain (residues 178–245). This variant is predicted to be damaging by four out of five computational prediction tools (LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, and SIFT). According to ACMG/AMP guidelines for variant assessment, it fulfills several criteria: PM1—the change is located in a key and well-established functional domain (R3H domain) that lacks benign variation; PM2—the variant is either absent or present at an extremely low frequency in population databases; PP3—numerous in silico models suggest a deleterious impact on gene function; and PP2—PARN generally exhibits a low frequency of benign missense variants, with missense changes recognized as a typical cause of disease. Although the clinical implications of this specific variant are not yet established, its possible association with telomere biology disorders is indicated by the known function of the gene and by similarity to other PARN missense variants—namely p.Lys59Arg (ClinVar ID: VCV000424063.2) and p.Ala383Val (ClinVar ID: VCV000180661.1)—which have been classified as ‘likely pathogenic’ in the ClinVar database.

A range of genes, various patterns of inheritance, incomplete penetrance, and somatic genetic rescue can each influence distinct organ systems in telomere biology disorders (TBDs) in different ways. Furthermore, both environmental and lifestyle influences can affect the proliferative potential of specific organ systems. In the case of our patient, these layers of complexity might account for the clinical presentation seen, even though only a single-allele PARN variant was detected.

While heterozygous PARN mutations are typically linked to IPF rather than primary hematological symptoms, the cytopenia observed in this patient suggests the potential presence of an underlying TBD, which could make the bone marrow more vulnerable to hematopoietic stressors such as marked vitamin B12 deficiency. Shortened telomeres reduce the proliferative capacity of hematopoietic stem cells, accelerating cellular aging and elevating the likelihood of BMFS. Although the patient's cytopenia was most plausibly due to pronounced vitamin B12 deficiency, identification of a PARN variant highlights the necessity for continued clinical and hematological surveillance to monitor for any progression towards marrow failure in a genetically predisposed patient.

Cirrhosis in telomere biology disorders (TBD) has historically been overlooked, though recent studies indicate it may be more widespread than previously thought. Data from the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry, employing transient elastography, demonstrated that 88.8% of adult individuals with pathogenic mutations in TERC or TERT exhibited at least moderate hepatic fibrosis (HF), and cirrhosis was identified in 33.3%. Another case report detailed an individual with both pancytopenia and hepatic fibrosis who possessed single-copy pathogenic variants in PARN (c.1613C>G) and TERT (c.2476G>A). In contrast, our case involved a patient with only a heterozygous PARN mutation who nonetheless developed hepatic as well as pulmonary fibrosis. While heterozygous mutations in PARN are often linked to idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), their consequent effect on telomerase activity might explain the presence of hepatic fibrosis, a finding more characteristic of TERT or TERC mutations. Collectively, these observations expand the recognized clinical manifestations of PARN-related TBD and highlight the necessity of including hepatic fibrosis within its disease spectrum.

Definitive diagnosis of suspected TBD relies on genetic analysis via next-generation sequencing and the assessment of telomere length using flow cytometry-based fluorescence in situ hybridization assays. Management focuses on symptomatic relief, addressing organ transplantation requirements, and minimizing risk factors; hematopoietic stem cell transplantation may offer a cure for individuals exhibiting hematologic involvement. Although limited preclinical and small-scale clinical studies have indicated that androgens such as danazol and nandrolone may promote telomere elongation, the extended outcomes of this approach have yet to be determined.

The patient exhibited symptoms consistent with TBD, HF, and IPF. After comprehensive assessment ruled out other prevalent etiologies, whole-exome sequencing pinpointed the responsible gene. A thorough review of existing literature expanded our insight into this unusual case. Supportive measures constituted the mainstay of her management, and her family received genetic counseling. She comprehended her diagnosis and acknowledged that curative treatments were not available. At a follow-up visit two weeks later, her hematological parameters had not changed, and no additional clinical decline was noted.
