Title: Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I

Ali Mohamed Mousa, Mervat Mahmoud, Ghaida Mubarak AlShuraiaan, Zouheir Ibrahim Bitar

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her early 40s reported a 7-year history of recurrent episodes of choking affecting both solid and liquid foods, as well as nighttime choking episodes occurring three to four times per week, and persistent occipital headaches over 14 years. During the COVID-19 pandemic, home oxygen monitoring revealed severe hypoxaemic events (oxygen saturation 70%–80%) in the absence of respiratory distress. Multiple laryngoscopies and endoscopic evaluations aimed at determining the cause of her choking yielded normal findings. A review of a head CT from 14 years earlier, initially performed following trauma, identified a previously unnoticed crowded foramen magnum. MRI studies of the head and neck subsequently confirmed Chiari malformation type I (CMI), with a cerebellar tonsillar herniation measuring 11 mm. Polysomnography revealed mild sleep apnoea (apnoea–hypopnoea index 6.0), mainly characterized by central events (72%). This case highlights that CMI may present with respiratory symptoms that resemble acquired central hypoventilation syndrome (Ondine’s curse), underscoring the need to consider structural brain anomalies in patients who present with unexplained respiratory symptoms.

Background:

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is characterized by inferior herniation of the cerebellar tonsils extending 5 mm or more below the foramen magnum as seen on sagittal MRI scans. Recent imaging data suggest that CMI affects about 1% of adults, yet only around 0.1% of the overall population develop symptoms. There is a slight predominance among females, with a ratio of 1.3:1, and most cases present clinically in the third decade of life.

The pathogenesis is primarily related to a congenitally reduced posterior fossa size, resulting in a discrepancy between the volume of neural tissues and the spatial confines. Yet, current research calls into question the longstanding view of Chiari malformation type I (CMI) as inherently pathological, suggesting it exists along a continuum that ranges from benign anatomical differences to those causing significant clinical concerns. Recent studies assessing posterior fossa volume have identified considerable variation across demographic groups, with no clear relationship between measurements and clinical symptoms. This evolving perspective underscores the importance of tailoring clinical decisions to each patient's symptomatology, carefully separating incidental anatomical variants from true pathological conditions that warrant surgical treatment.

Chiari syndrome is the symptomatic expression of CMI and is distinguished by a characteristic group of neurological signs. The primary symptom is sudden onset occipital headache, frequently triggered by Valsalva maneuvers, occurring in more than 80% of individuals. Adults often experience more pronounced symptoms than children, particularly in cases where syringomyelia is also present. Involvement of the lower cranial nerves has been documented in 15%–26% of cases, generally manifesting as oropharyngeal dysfunction, including palatal weakness, tongue atrophy, or cricopharyngeal achalasia.

Pulmonary symptoms are now acknowledged as a growing complication associated with CMI. Among 90 adult CMI patients evaluated prospectively, half exhibited sleep-related breathing abnormalities. A central apnoea index of 5 or higher was identified in 6.7% of participants, though pure central sleep apnoea was noted in only one individual, possibly due to brainstem compression. This respiratory dysfunction is not limited to sleep abnormalities; a case series has described instances of acute respiratory failure and severe hypoxaemia in individuals with CMI.

Respiratory impairment in these patients is attributed to compression of the brainstem, blockage of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) pathways, and disruption of centers responsible for respiratory regulation, resulting in episodic respiratory manifestations. These symptoms constitute an acquired variant of central hypoventilation syndrome, occasionally referred to as ‘Ondine’s curse’, which is distinct from the congenital type linked to PHOX2B mutations. Current recommendations highlight the relevance of this association by advising routine polysomnography for individuals with Chiari malformation type I (CMI) who exhibit signs suggestive of sleep-disordered breathing. Importantly, surgical decompression of the posterior fossa may restore normal CSF flow and alleviate respiratory symptoms, highlighting the necessity of considering CMI in cases of otherwise unexplained respiratory dysfunction.

Case presentation:

A female patient in her early 40s came to our family medicine practice with a 7-year history of unpredictable choking episodes during both eating and sleeping, involving both solid and liquid foods equally. She described these events as abrupt and alarming, frequently disrupting meals or waking her from sleep with forceful coughing and gasping, but never causing complete obstruction of her airway. Nocturnal occurrences happened three to four times weekly, with some nights having two episodes. Over time, she underwent multiple assessments, including three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper gastrointestinal endoscopies, all yielding normal findings. Despite thorough investigation, her symptoms continued unabated. The consequences for her social life were substantial; she began to avoid dining out and participating in social events because of apprehension over potential choking in public. This persistent fear and humiliation contributed to further social isolation and emotional challenges.

A more thorough review of her medical history indicated that her symptoms first appeared 14 years prior, following a minor automobile collision in which she briefly lost consciousness. Soon after the event, she experienced ongoing headaches located at the occipital region and vertex. The headaches were described as continuous, resembling a sensation of pressure, and frequently confined to a coin-sized area at the vertex. Notably, the pain did not worsen with coughing, straining, or other Valsalva maneuvers. She was diagnosed by several clinicians with nummular headache and underwent multiple local injections into the scalp, which resulted in only short-term alleviation. The most prominent symptom was significant allodynia at the vertex, where even the mere weight of her hair would provoke discomfort. Eventually, she found that trimming her hair short lessened her baseline pain from 6/10 to 3/10; however, this choice conflicted with her cultural norms, leading to additional emotional distress.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the patient started routinely checking her oxygen saturation at home with a pulse oximeter. Over a span of several months, she consistently observed asymptomatic declines in oxygen saturation to values between 70% and 80%. She frequently was unaware of these occurrences until they were detected on her device. Numerous emergency department assessments concluded that her symptoms were caused by anxiety.

A critical event transpired at an international airport. As she awaited her departure, she developed sudden onset vertigo and nausea. Medical staff at the scene recorded her oxygen saturation at 78%, resulting in her being refused permission to board. Her symptoms fully resolved within minutes following the initiation of supplemental oxygen. Further pulmonary assessment, encompassing chest radiography and pulmonary function tests, yielded unremarkable results.

Considering her longstanding occipital and vertex headaches, recurrent choking spells, and unexplained episodes of hypoxia, clinicians sought a single neurological cause to explain her symptoms. Examination of prior medical documentation indicated that a non-contrast cranial CT had been performed 14 years before, subsequent to her injury, and was interpreted as unremarkable at that time. Nonetheless, upon meticulous reevaluation, the scan demonstrated a narrowed foramen magnum as well as obliteration of cerebrospinal fluid spaces at the craniocervical junction. Differential diagnosis:

The constellation of choking affecting solids and liquids equally, unexplained hypoxia and chronic headaches prompted systematic evaluation:

Given the presence of choking symptoms, gastro-esophageal reflux disease accompanied by laryngopharyngeal reflux was first suspected. However, the diagnosis was ruled out due to normal findings on pH monitoring and endoscopic evaluation, as well as the absence of improvement following maximal acid-suppressive therapy.

Evaluation for myasthenia gravis was performed because of bulbar symptoms. However, negative antibody results, no evidence of fatigability, and the absence of diurnal fluctuation rendered this diagnosis improbable.

After episodes of hypoxia, primary pulmonary disease was thoroughly assessed. Imaging of the chest and pulmonary function studies were normal, thereby ruling out intrinsic pulmonary pathology.

Central alveolar hypoventilation syndrome (also known as acquired Ondine’s curse) was indicated by intact voluntary respiration, while involuntary respiratory control was compromised during periods of sleep or when attention was diverted. Its occurrence after head trauma differentiated it from congenital variants of the condition.

All symptoms were ultimately accounted for by brainstem pathology resulting from CMI, as it caused compression of both the respiratory centers and nuclei of the lower cranial nerves, thereby offering a single unifying diagnosis.

Discussion:

The close timing of the patient’s motor vehicle accident and the emergence of symptoms necessitates thorough evaluation. Chiari malformation type I is described in the literature as a congenital disorder, but it can also occur following head trauma or other neurological insults. Current etiological frameworks2022 acknowledge this range, differentiating primary, congenital Chiari malformations from secondary or acquired variants that arise due to traumatic events or elevated intracranial pressure.

In this patient, a CT scan acquired soon after the injury showed a constricted foramen magnum. Since there were no pre-injury images available, it is unclear if this feature was a direct result of the trauma or simply uncovered by it. The abrupt emergence of occipital headache after the incident, along with a gradual progression of bulbar and respiratory symptoms spanning 14 years, may indicate either: (1) tonsillar herniation secondary to trauma, or (2) traumatic events worsening or exposing an existing but asymptomatic congenital anomaly.

This case highlights the intricate respiratory features associated with CMI and broadens our comprehension of secondary central hypoventilation patterns seen in structural lesions of the brainstem. Differentiating between voluntary and automatic ventilation is fundamental to interpreting the respiratory pathophysiology of CMI. While voluntary respiration, governed by cortical pathways, is usually preserved in those with CMI, automatic respiration—which is managed by brainstem centers and is particularly vital during sleep—becomes impaired due to compression of the medullary region by the cerebellar tonsils. In this patient, voluntary ventilatory capacity was maintained, but the automatic respiratory drive was lost, mirroring the presentation of congenital central hypoventilation syndrome but stemming from a distinct underlying mechanism. The incident at the airport, in which transient inattention led to severe desaturation in the absence of dyspnoea, clearly demonstrates this isolated defect in automatic breathing: the patient, quite literally, ‘forgets to breathe’.

We propose that the underlying factor for the patient’s notable lack of dyspnoea during pronounced hypoxaemic episodes is a primary dysfunction localized to the brainstem. This pathology likely disrupts the essential ascending afferent neural pathways required to reach the cerebral cortex and elicit conscious perception. Breathlessness, as a component of interoception, relies on these afferent signals being processed by specific cortical regions such as the anterior insular cortex. Interruption of these neural signals within the brainstem would prevent their arrival at the cortex, thereby impeding the generation of air hunger and resulting in the patient’s perilous unawareness of their underlying physiological compromise.

The pronounced difference between the intensity of our patient’s clinical symptoms and the comparatively low AHI value (6.0) observed on polysomnography requires clarification. Owing to logistical constraints, we opted for type III polysomnography instead of type I, which does not incorporate EEG monitoring. The lack of EEG precluded precise assessment of actual sleep duration, probably resulting in a substantial undervaluation of respiratory disturbances. In the absence of EEG data, the AHI is determined using the total duration of the recording—including periods when the patient may have been awake—rather than the actual time spent asleep. Consequently, this approach introduces a methodological issue, implying that the AHI of 6.0 likely does not reflect the true extent of the patient’s sleep-disordered breathing.

Although this limitation exists, multiple findings indicate central hypoventilation: (1) central events comprise the majority (72%) despite the total AHI remaining low; (2) oxygen saturation levels dropped to 70%–80% without concurrent dyspnoea or patient recognition—both in the airport setting and during routine assessments; and (3) imaging demonstrates marked compression of the brainstem, evidenced by 11 mm of tonsillar herniation.

A comprehensive Type I polysomnography—including full EEG surveillance, end-tidal CO2 measurement, and video documentation—has been scheduled at a separate sleep facility. Nevertheless, the study has not yet been completed due to ongoing coordination of the patient’s availability, willingness to undergo the procedure, and timing at the external center. When conducted, this assessment is expected to yield a more conclusive description of sleep architecture, breathing patterns, and the extent and severity of central hypoventilation.

It should be noted that, based on current findings, central hypoventilation continues to be a strongly supported clinical suspicion but not a conclusively established diagnosis.

Our patient’s most troubling manifestation was oropharyngeal dysphagia, equally affecting ingestion of both solids and liquids, and accompanied by episodes of choking and coughing. Validated prospective swallowing evaluations revealed dysfunction in 36% of cases, with most exhibiting neurogenic features. The parallel impairment across different textures distinguishes neurogenic from mechanical dysphagia. The patient’s prominent complaint underwent repeated gastroenterological and otolaryngological assessments, yielding normal results; however, diagnosis was postponed for several years. This delay underscores a critical clinical insight: dysphagia or choking symptoms absent structural abnormalities on endoscopy should raise suspicion for a neurological cause. According to a retrospective analysis, the frequency of CMI among children with chronic dysphagia was 3.3%, significantly surpassing the 0.1%–0.77% prevalence in the general pediatric population. These findings imply that persistent dysphagia may serve as a significant marker for underlying CMI.

The patient’s experience of vertex pain that improves following hair cutting illustrates a notable, yet largely unexplored, clinical occurrence. Occipital neuralgia has been observed alongside Chiari malformation type I (CMI), potentially resulting in heightened scalp sensitivity. Its diagnostic criteria include “dysesthesia and/or allodynia evident during non-noxious stimulation of the scalp and/or hair.” Nevertheless, connections between occipital neuralgia and CMI are infrequently described in the literature, and definitive evidence for a causal relationship remains lacking.

Another possibility is the newly recognized ‘burning vertex syndrome’, characterized by scalp discomfort, sensitivity to touch of the skin, and manifestations that do not align precisely with recognized primary headache categories such as migraine, tension-type, cluster, or nummular headache. The proposed underlying mechanism may involve small nerve fibers in the scalp, and affected individuals demonstrate mixed responses to greater occipital nerve blockade. For our patient, the vertex-related complaints could indicate: (1) occipital neuralgia associated with CMI; (2) a coincident burning vertex syndrome; or (3) a distinct primary headache disorder altogether. There remains a notable lack of systematic records concerning cutaneous allodynia in patients with CMI, a gap highlighted by the fact that no validated tools to measure such symptoms in CMI individuals existed prior to the Chiari Symptom Profile. Due to this limited evidence base, we describe this observation as a notable clinical phenomenon rather than as a diagnostic criterion.

This case underscores the considerable difficulty in diagnosing CMI, especially when it manifests with unusual symptoms like central hypoventilation and choking, resulting in a delayed diagnosis spanning 14 years. The process, marked by early misdirection and a slow progression toward the accurate neurological identification, mirrors patterns frequently described in existing literature.
