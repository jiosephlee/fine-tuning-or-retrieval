Title: Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I

Ali Mohamed Mousa, Mervat Mahmoud, Ghaida Mubarak AlShuraiaan, Zouheir Ibrahim Bitar

Abstract:

Summary: A female patient in her early 40s reported a 7-year history of repeated choking episodes involving both solids and liquids, alongside nocturnal choking occurring 3–4 times per week, and a 14-year history of chronic occipital headaches. During the COVID-19 pandemic, home oxygen monitoring unexpectedly detected severe hypoxaemia (oxygen saturations ranging from 70%–80%) in the absence of respiratory discomfort. Repeated laryngoscopic and endoscopic examinations, performed as part of the evaluation for her choking, revealed no abnormalities. Retrospective review of a cranial CT scan conducted 14 years prior following unrelated trauma identified a previously unrecognized crowded foramen magnum. Subsequent MRI of the head and neck established the diagnosis of Chiari malformation type I (CMI) with cerebellar tonsillar herniation measuring 11 mm. Polysomnography indicated mild sleep apnea, with an apnoea–hypopnoea index of 6.0, showing a predominance (72%) of central apnoeic events. This report highlights that CMI may manifest with respiratory complaints resembling acquired central hypoventilation syndrome (Ondine’s curse), underscoring the necessity of evaluating for structural abnormalities of the brain in cases of otherwise unexplained respiratory dysfunction.

Background:

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is characterized by downward herniation of the cerebellar tonsils measuring at least 5 mm beneath the foramen magnum as seen on sagittal MRI imaging. Recent imaging data estimate that CMI is present in 1% of adults; however, only about 0.1% of individuals in the general population are symptomatic. There is a slight female predominance with a ratio of 1.3:1, and most cases are diagnosed in individuals in their 20s.

The underlying pathophysiology involves an underdeveloped posterior fossa, resulting in a disproportion between the size of the neural elements and the surrounding space. Nonetheless, current research questions the conventional view of Chiari malformation type I (CMI) as inherently abnormal, suggesting that it exists along a continuum ranging from benign anatomical variants to forms that produce clinical symptoms. Volumetric analyses have revealed considerable demographic differences in posterior fossa dimensions that do not reliably correspond with clinical manifestations. Consequently, this evolving perspective calls for a tailored, symptom-oriented clinical approach to distinguish between anatomical variants without symptoms and truly pathological cases that may benefit from surgical management.

Chiari syndrome refers to the clinically symptomatic form of CMI, marked by a characteristic set of neurological signs. The principal symptom is intermittent occipital headache triggered by Valsalva maneuvers, occurring in more than 80% of individuals. Adults usually exhibit more pronounced symptoms than pediatric patients, particularly when syringomyelia is also present. Involvement of the lower cranial nerves is observed in approximately 15% to 26% of cases, most often manifesting as oropharyngeal deficits including palatal weakness, atrophy of the tongue, or cricopharyngeal achalasia.

Pulmonary complications are being more frequently identified in individuals with CMI. In a prospective cohort of 90 adult CMI patients, half exhibited breathing disturbances associated with sleep. A central apnoea index of 5 or higher was detected in 6.7% of these individuals, yet isolated central sleep apnoea was noted in only a single patient, possibly indicating compression of the brainstem. These respiratory issues are not limited to abnormalities during sleep; one case series also reported acute episodes of respiratory failure and severe, potentially fatal hypoxaemia among CMI patients.

Respiratory impairment arises through mechanisms such as compression of the brainstem, obstruction of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) pathways, and disruption of the centres regulating respiration, resulting in episodic respiratory disturbances. These symptoms constitute an acquired central hypoventilation syndrome—also referred to as ‘Ondine’s curse’—which is separate from the congenital variant linked to PHOX2B gene mutations. Recently published guidelines highlight this association, recommending that patients with Chiari malformation type I (CMI) and suspected sleep-related breathing disorders undergo routine polysomnography. Significantly, decompression of the posterior fossa can reinstate physiological CSF circulation and may resolve respiratory issues, highlighting the need to consider CMI when evaluating cases of unexplained respiratory compromise.

Case presentation:

A female patient in her early forties came to our family medicine practice with a 7-year history of recurrent, unpredictable choking episodes during both meals and sleep. These incidents involved both solid foods and liquids and were described as abrupt and highly distressing, frequently disrupting meals or waking her from sleep with forceful coughing and gasping, but never leading to total airway blockage. Nocturnal occurrences were reported three to four times weekly, with occasional instances of two episodes in a single night. She had previously undergone multiple evaluations, including three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper GI endoscopies, all yielding normal results. Nevertheless, her symptoms continued unabated. The effect on her social life was profound; she refrained from dining out or attending gatherings out of concern for choking in public. This fear, coupled with embarrassment, resulted in progressively increased social isolation and psychological distress.

A thorough review of her medical history indicated that her complaints originated 14 years prior, subsequent to a minor car accident in which she experienced a brief loss of consciousness. Soon after, she began having persistent headaches located in the occipital region and at the vertex. She characterized these headaches as continuous and pressure-like, typically confined to a small, coin-sized spot on the vertex. The headaches were not worsened by actions such as coughing, straining, or performing Valsalva maneuvers. She received a diagnosis of nummular headache from several clinicians and underwent repeated local injections into the scalp, but these provided only short-lived improvement. The most notable symptom was significant allodynia at the vertex, to the extent that merely the weight of her hair would provoke pain. She eventually realized that maintaining short hair lessened her baseline pain score from 6/10 to 3/10, though this measure conflicted with cultural norms and was associated with psychological distress.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the patient started tracking her oxygen saturation levels at home with a personal pulse oximeter. Over a period of several months, she consistently recorded episodes where her oxygen saturation fell to between 70% and 80%, without accompanying symptoms. These episodes typically went unnoticed until she measured her levels. Repeated emergency department assessments ascribed her symptoms to anxiety.

A significant event took place at an international airport. As she awaited boarding, the patient developed sudden-onset vertigo and nausea. Airport medical staff noted her oxygen saturation to be 78%, resulting in her being prohibited from flying. She experienced full relief of her symptoms within a few minutes after receiving supplemental oxygen. Later assessments by a pulmonologist, which involved chest imaging and pulmonary function tests, revealed no abnormalities.

Due to the presence of longstanding headaches located in the occipital and vertex regions, along with recurrent choking incidents and unexplained episodes of low oxygen saturation, clinicians sought a single neurological explanation for her symptoms. Upon examining her historical medical documentation, a non-contrast CT head scan, conducted 14 years prior after her accident, was found to have been interpreted as unremarkable at the time. Nevertheless, upon careful retrospective examination, the imaging demonstrated evidence of a crowded foramen magnum and absence of cerebrospinal fluid around the craniocervical junction. Differential diagnosis:

The constellation of choking affecting solids and liquids equally, unexplained hypoxia and chronic headaches prompted systematic evaluation:

Gastroesophageal reflux disease with associated laryngopharyngeal reflux was initially suspected due to the presence of choking symptoms. However, this diagnosis was ruled out after pH monitoring and endoscopic evaluations returned normal findings, and the patient showed no improvement with maximal acid suppression therapy.

Myasthenia gravis was evaluated due to symptoms affecting the bulbar region. Negative antibody testing, lack of fatigability and absence of diurnal variation made this unlikely.

Primary pulmonary pathology was extensively investigated following hypoxic episodes. Normal chest imaging and pulmonary function tests excluded intrinsic lung disease.

Central alveolar hypoventilation syndrome (acquired Ondine’s curse) was highly suspected due to the patient’s intact ability to breathe voluntarily, while involuntary respiration was compromised during periods of distraction or sleep. The condition was considered acquired, secondary to head trauma, rather than congenital in origin.

Brainstem pathology secondary to CMI ultimately explained all symptoms through compression of the respiratory centres and lower cranial nerve nuclei, providing the unifying diagnosis.

Discussion:

The timing of our patient’s symptom development following the motor vehicle collision necessitates thorough evaluation. Chiari malformation type I is reported in the literature as occurring congenitally as well as arising after head trauma or other neurologic insults. Recent etiological frameworks (2022) acknowledge this range by differentiating primary (congenital) Chiari malformations from those that are secondary or acquired, attributable to trauma or elevations in intracranial pressure.

In this patient, a CT scan obtained right after the incident showed a narrowed foramen magnum. Because no imaging is available from before the trauma, it is unclear if the injury created this condition or simply brought a pre-existing abnormality to light. The rapid emergence of occipital headaches after the trauma, along with a gradual progression to bulbar and respiratory complaints over the next 14 years, could indicate either (1) herniation of the cerebellar tonsils triggered by trauma or (2) trauma accentuating or exposing an asymptomatic congenital anomaly.

This case illustrates the intricate respiratory features associated with CMI and provides additional insight into the patterns of acquired central hypoventilation found in structural brainstem disorders. Differentiating between voluntary and automatic respiration is fundamental to grasping the respiratory pathophysiology in CMI. While voluntary breathing—which relies on cortical pathways—usually remains unaffected in individuals with CMI, automatic respiration—regulated by brainstem centers and crucial during sleep—can be impaired owing to compression of medullary regions by the cerebellar tonsils. In this patient, voluntary ventilatory ability was preserved, but the capacity for automatic respiratory drive was lost, producing a clinical picture similar to congenital central hypoventilation syndrome, though arising through a distinct underlying mechanism. The airport incident, in which a brief distraction resulted in marked desaturation in the absence of dyspnoea, starkly demonstrates this isolated dysfunction of automatic respiratory control: the patient, in effect, ‘forgets to breathe’.

We propose that a primary dysfunction of the brainstem underlies the striking absence of dyspnoea in this patient during episodes of marked oxygen desaturation. Such a brainstem abnormality would plausibly impair vital afferent neural pathways that are required to ascend to the cortex for the conscious perception of respiratory discomfort. The sensation of breathlessness represents an aspect of interoception, which relies on the relay of these afferent inputs to critical brain areas such as the anterior insular cortex. If these signals are interrupted at the level of the brainstem, the cortex does not receive sufficient information to produce the sensation of air hunger, accounting for the patient’s lack of awareness of significant physiological compromise.

The notable difference observed between the patient’s pronounced clinical manifestations and the modest polysomnographic results (AHI 6.0) requires clarification. Owing to practical constraints, we utilized type III instead of type I polysomnography, which does not include EEG monitoring. The lack of EEG precluded precise determination of actual sleep duration, thereby likely resulting in underreporting of respiratory events. In the absence of EEG, the AHI is determined based on total recording time—including intervals of wakefulness—rather than solely the periods of confirmed sleep. This methodological limitation suggests that the documented AHI of 6.0 likely understates the actual burden of sleep-disordered breathing.

Nevertheless, multiple findings indicate central hypoventilation: (1) central respiratory events comprise the majority (72%) even though the total AHI remains low; (2) observed oxygen saturations decreased to 70%–80% in the absence of dyspnoea or any patient-reported symptoms (noted both at the airport and during regular assessments); and (3) imaging reveals substantial compression of the brainstem, demonstrated by an 11 mm descent of the tonsils.

A Type I polysomnographic assessment including comprehensive EEG monitoring, end-tidal CO₂ measurement, and video documentation has been scheduled at an outside sleep facility. Nevertheless, carrying out this evaluation is still awaiting the alignment of the patient's schedule, their consent to undergo the procedure, and appointment availability at the external location. Upon completion, the study is expected to yield more conclusive data regarding sleep architecture, breathing patterns, and the existence and degree of central hypoventilation.

We acknowledge that, given the current evidence, central hypoventilation remains a well-supported clinical hypothesis, rather than a definitively confirmed diagnosis.

Our patient’s most troubling symptom was oropharyngeal dysphagia, which involved equal difficulty with both solids and liquids, accompanied by episodes of choking and coughing. Prospective assessments using validated swallowing tests demonstrated dysfunction in 36% of individuals, predominantly showing neurogenic patterns. The presence of similar impairment across all consistencies is characteristic of neurogenic, rather than mechanical, dysphagia. Initially, and on several subsequent occasions, the patient’s most pronounced symptom was evaluated from both gastroenterological and otolaryngological standpoints, yielding normal results; however, the accurate diagnosis was not established for several years. This diagnostic delay serves as a valuable clinical reminder: when dysphagia or choking occurs in the absence of an evident structural abnormality on endoscopy, a neurological etiology should be considered. In a retrospective cohort of pediatric patients with persistent dysphagia, the prevalence of CMI was found to be 3.3%, which is markedly higher than the 0.1%–0.77% prevalence noted in the broader pediatric population. These findings imply that chronic dysphagia may be a significant clinical clue to underlying CMI.

The patient's description of vertex pain alleviated by haircutting is noteworthy and yet remains insufficiently explored in the literature. Occipital neuralgia, which may coexist with CMI, is known to produce heightened sensitivity of the scalp. The diagnostic criteria include "dysesthesia and/or allodynia triggered by non-painful stimulation of the scalp and/or hair." Nevertheless, reports of occipital neuralgia associated with CMI are uncommon, and definitive evidence demonstrating a direct connection is lacking.

Another possible explanation is the more recently identified ‘burning vertex syndrome’, characterized by pain at the scalp, cutaneous allodynia, and symptoms that do not correspond precisely to existing primary headache categories such as migraine, cluster, tension-type, or nummular headache. The proposed underlying mechanism may involve small fiber nerve pathways originating in the scalp, and patient responses to greater occipital nerve blocks have been inconsistent. In our case, the symptoms experienced at the vertex could indicate: (1) occipital neuralgia associated with CMI; (2) coincidental burning vertex syndrome; or (3) a separate, distinct primary headache condition. There is a notable lack of systematic reporting regarding cutaneous allodynia patterns in patients with CMI, highlighted by the fact that no validated tool existed to assess these specific symptoms prior to the development of the Chiari Symptom Profile. Because of this limited evidence base, we describe this observation as an intriguing clinical note rather than as a conclusive diagnostic marker.

This case underscores the considerable difficulty in diagnosing Chiari malformation type I (CMI) when it manifests with non-classical symptoms like central hypoventilation and episodes of choking, resulting in a protracted 14-year interval before diagnosis was established. The trajectory from initial diagnostic uncertainty to eventual identification of a neurological etiology is a commonly described narrative in existing literature.
