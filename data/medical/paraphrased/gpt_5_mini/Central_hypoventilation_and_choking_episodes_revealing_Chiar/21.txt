Title: Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I

Ali Mohamed Mousa, Mervat Mahmoud, Ghaida Mubarak AlShuraiaan, Zouheir Ibrahim Bitar

Abstract:

Summary: A female patient in her early 40s reported experiencing recurrent choking spells with both solids and liquids over a 7-year period, accompanied by nighttime choking episodes 3 to 4 times per week, as well as a 14-year history of persistent occipital headaches. Home pulse oximetry during the COVID-19 pandemic incidentally identified significant episodes of oxygen desaturation (ranging from 70% to 80%) occurring without evidence of respiratory distress. Numerous evaluations including laryngoscopy and endoscopy aimed at assessing the cause of her choking did not reveal any abnormalities. Re-examination of a 14-year-old head CT originally obtained for unrelated trauma incidentally uncovered a crowded foramen magnum that had not been previously identified. Subsequent MRI of the head and neck demonstrated a Chiari malformation type I (CMI) with 11 mm descent of the cerebellar tonsils. Polysomnography identified mild sleep apnoea, with an apnoea–hypopnoea index of 6.0, primarily consisting of central apnoeic events (72%). This report highlights that CMI may manifest with respiratory symptoms resembling acquired central hypoventilation syndrome (Ondine’s curse), underscoring the need to evaluate for structural abnormalities of the brain in individuals with otherwise unexplained respiratory impairment.

Background:

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is characterized by cerebellar tonsils extending at least 5 mm beneath the foramen magnum as seen on sagittal MRI. Recent imaging data indicate that CMI affects roughly 1% of adults; however, only about 0.1% of individuals develop symptoms. There is a slight predominance among females with a ratio of 1.3:1, and onset most frequently occurs in individuals in their third decade.

The underlying pathophysiology involves a congenitally undersized posterior fossa, resulting in a disproportion between neural elements and the space they occupy. Nevertheless, current research disputes the longstanding view that Chiari malformation type I is always a pathological condition, suggesting it exists on a continuum ranging from benign anatomical variations to malformations of clinical significance. New volumetric analyses reveal considerable differences in posterior fossa dimensions across demographics, yet these variations do not reliably predict symptoms. This evolution in perspective calls for personalized, symptom-directed management, emphasizing the need to separate incidental anatomical variants without symptoms from those warranting operative treatment.

Chiari syndrome refers to the clinical presentation associated with CMI, which is distinguished by a specific pattern of neurological signs. The primary symptom is intermittent occipital headache triggered by Valsalva maneuvers, occurring in more than 80% of affected individuals. Compared to pediatric cases, adult patients often exhibit more pronounced symptoms, particularly in the presence of concurrent syringomyelia. Involvement of the lower cranial nerves is observed in 15%–26% of cases, most commonly manifested as oropharyngeal abnormalities including weakness of the palate, atrophy of the tongue, or cricopharyngeal achalasia.

Respiratory symptoms are being acknowledged with greater frequency as a complication associated with CMI. In a prospective cohort of 90 adult patients diagnosed with CMI, half displayed evidence of sleep-related breathing abnormalities. Among these individuals, a central apnoea index of 5 or higher was detected in 6.7%, while isolated central sleep apnoea was seen in a single patient, which may be indicative of compression of the brainstem. Respiratory dysfunction linked to CMI is not limited to sleep disorders; a series of cases has reported acute respiratory failure and severe, life-threatening hypoxaemia in affected patients.

Respiratory impairment in these cases is attributed to compression of the brainstem, obstruction of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) outflow, and interference with central respiratory control areas, which collectively produce episodic breathing disturbances. Such manifestations constitute an acquired type of central hypoventilation syndrome, often referred to as 'Ondine’s curse', differing from the congenital variant linked to PHOX2B gene mutations. Contemporary recommendations highlight this association by advocating for routine polysomnography in patients with Chiari Malformation I (CMI) who are suspected to have sleep-related breathing disorders. Notably, performing posterior fossa decompression may normalize CSF flow and ameliorate respiratory symptoms, illustrating the need to identify CMI in cases of unexplained respiratory dysfunction.

Case presentation:

A female patient in her early forties visited our family medicine practice, reporting a 7-year history of recurrent choking incidents that happened unexpectedly during both mealtimes and sleep, affecting solid foods and liquids without distinction. She characterized these episodes as abrupt and distressing, frequently stopping her eating or awakening her at night with intense coughing and gasping, though they never progressed to full airway blockage. Nighttime events occurred three to four times per week, sometimes even twice in the same night. She had previously undergone extensive investigations, including three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper gastrointestinal endoscopies, all yielding normal results. Nevertheless, her symptoms continued without relief. The impact on her social life was profound; she avoided dining out and attending gatherings for fear of experiencing a choking episode in public. This persistent anxiety and humiliation contributed to heightened social isolation and increasing emotional hardship.

A thorough review of her medical history indicated that her symptoms initially appeared 14 years prior, after a minor car accident in which she briefly lost consciousness. Soon afterwards, she experienced ongoing headaches at the occipital region and vertex. She described the headaches as unrelenting and pressure-like, frequently confined to a small coin-sized spot at the vertex. The headaches did not worsen with actions such as coughing, straining, or performing Valsalva maneuvers. Several physicians diagnosed her condition as nummular headache, and she received repeated local scalp injections, which provided only short-lived alleviation. Notably, she exhibited pronounced allodynia at the vertex; even her hair resting on the area elicited discomfort. She eventually found that cutting her hair short lowered her baseline pain from 6/10 to 3/10, although this choice was at odds with cultural norms and contributed to psychological distress.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the patient initiated regular assessments of her oxygen saturation with a personal pulse oximeter at home. Over the course of several months, she noted multiple instances of asymptomatic oxygen saturation falling within the 70%–80% range. Frequently, she was unaware of these declines until she measured with her device. Numerous emergency department visits resulted in the attribution of her symptoms to anxiety.

An important event took place at an international airport. As she waited to board her flight, she developed sudden dizziness and nausea. Airport medical staff recorded her oxygen saturation at 78% and prevented her from boarding. After receiving supplemental oxygen, her symptoms subsided entirely within a few minutes. Follow-up assessment by pulmonary specialists, which included chest imaging and pulmonary function studies, showed no abnormalities.

Considering the presence of longstanding headaches in the occipital and vertex regions, in conjunction with worsening episodes of choking and unexplained low oxygen levels, an effort was made to identify a singular neurological etiology. Her prior medical documentation showed that a non-contrast head CT had been conducted 14 years earlier after her accident and was interpreted as normal at that time. Nonetheless, a thorough retrospective analysis of the imaging demonstrated crowding at the foramen magnum as well as diminished cerebrospinal fluid spaces at the craniocervical junction. Differential diagnosis:

The constellation of choking affecting solids and liquids equally, unexplained hypoxia and chronic headaches prompted systematic evaluation:

Due to the presence of choking episodes, gastro-oesophageal reflux disease accompanied by laryngopharyngeal reflux was suspected at first. However, this was ruled out because pH monitoring showed no abnormalities, endoscopic evaluations were unremarkable, and symptoms persisted despite optimal acid suppression therapy.

The possibility of myasthenia gravis was considered because of bulbar symptoms; however, negative antibody assays, no evidence of fatigability, and no daily fluctuation in symptoms reduced the likelihood of this diagnosis.

Following hypoxemic events, a thorough assessment for primary pulmonary disorders was performed. Normal results on both chest imaging and pulmonary function tests ruled out intrinsic pulmonary disease.

Central alveolar hypoventilation syndrome (acquired Ondine’s curse) was indicated by intact voluntary respiration contrasted with diminished involuntary respiratory control when the patient was distracted or asleep. The onset after head injury differentiated this from congenital variants.

Compression of the respiratory centers and lower cranial nerve nuclei caused by brainstem involvement from CMI ultimately accounted for the complete spectrum of symptoms, thereby offering a cohesive diagnosis.

Discussion:

The timing of symptom emergence following the patient’s motor vehicle accident requires thorough evaluation. Chiari malformation type I is described in the literature as occurring both congenitally and as a consequence of head trauma or various neurological insults. Current etiological classifications2022 acknowledge this continuum, differentiating primary (congenital) Chiari malformations from secondary or acquired variants that arise due to trauma or elevated intracranial pressure.

In this patient, the CT obtained directly after the injury revealed a reduced foramen magnum space. As pre-injury imaging was unavailable, it is unclear if the trauma itself was responsible for this finding or if it simply brought a preexisting condition to light. The occurrence of occipital headaches immediately after the event, with subsequent gradual onset of bulbar and respiratory manifestations spanning 14 years, may indicate either: (1) tonsillar herniation resulting directly from trauma, or (2) trauma intensifying or exposing an otherwise asymptomatic congenital anomaly.

This case highlights the intricate respiratory presentations associated with CMI and contributes to a greater comprehension of central hypoventilation resulting from structural abnormalities in the brainstem. Differentiating voluntary from automatic breathing is fundamental when evaluating the respiratory pathophysiology in CMI. The cortical circuits that govern voluntary respiration are generally unaffected in individuals with CMI. Conversely, automatic respiration—regulated by brainstem centers and critical during sleep—is impaired due to compression of the medullary region by the cerebellar tonsils. The patient in this case retained the ability to breathe voluntarily but exhibited a loss of automatic respiratory drive, paralleling the clinical picture seen in congenital central hypoventilation syndrome, though originating from a distinct underlying mechanism. The incident at the airport, where transient inattention resulted in marked oxygen desaturation without the experience of dyspnoea, serves as a striking demonstration of isolated automatic respiratory dysfunction: the patient essentially 'forgets to breathe'.

We propose that the underlying issue stems from primary dysfunction within the brainstem, resulting in the patient’s remarkable lack of breathlessness during severe oxygen desaturation events. This brainstem pathology likely interferes with essential afferent neural pathways that would otherwise transmit sensory information to the cerebral cortex and elicit conscious awareness. Breathlessness represents an aspect of interoception, which requires the proper relay and processing of afferent signals by regions such as the anterior insular cortex. If these neural transmissions are disrupted at the level of the brainstem, the cortex would not obtain the necessary sensory input, thus preventing the perception of air hunger and accounting for the patient’s hazardous unawareness of their physiological state.

The significant difference observed between the pronounced symptoms in our patient and the modest polysomnographic results (AHI 6.0) requires clarification. Owing to practical constraints, a type III polysomnographic study was utilized instead of type I, resulting in the omission of EEG monitoring. As a consequence, precise measurement of actual sleep duration was not possible, and respiratory events were likely substantially undercounted. The lack of EEG data meant that AHI calculations relied on the entire recording period, which encompasses intervals of wakefulness, rather than solely the periods of sleep. This limitation in methodology suggests that the reported AHI of 6.0 does not adequately reflect the true extent of the patient’s sleep-disordered breathing.

Although this limitation exists, multiple findings are consistent with central hypoventilation: (1) a majority of central respiratory events (72%) despite a modest total AHI; (2) observed episodes of oxygen saturation dropping to 70%–80% in the absence of dyspnoea or patient-reported symptoms (occurring both at the airport and during standard evaluations); and (3) imaging demonstrating notable compression of the brainstem, as evidenced by 11 mm of tonsillar herniation.

A Type I polysomnography incorporating comprehensive EEG, end-tidal CO2 measurement, and video monitoring has been scheduled at an outside sleep facility. The study has yet to be performed due to ongoing arrangements regarding the patient's availability, consent for the procedure, and logistical considerations at the external location. Upon completion, the results are expected to offer more precise details regarding the patient’s sleep structure, breathing patterns, and to better determine the existence and degree of central hypoventilation.

We recognize that, based on presently available data, central hypoventilation is strongly suspected from a clinical standpoint, but has not been definitively diagnosed.

Our patient’s most troubling complaint was oropharyngeal dysphagia, presenting with equal impairment when swallowing solids and liquids, and accompanied by episodes of choking and coughing. Prospective studies utilizing established tools for assessing swallowing function have demonstrated dysfunction in 36% of cases, predominantly displaying neurogenic characteristics. The uniform difficulty across all food and liquid types distinguishes neurogenic dysphagia from its mechanical counterpart. The symptom that caused the greatest concern for the patient was initially, and subsequently, examined through both gastroenterological and otolaryngological evaluations, all of which yielded unremarkable results; however, a definitive diagnosis was not reached until several years later. This prolonged diagnostic period underscores a crucial point in clinical practice: when dysphagia or choking occurs in the absence of structural abnormalities identified on endoscopy, a neurological etiology should be considered. In a retrospective analysis, the prevalence of CMI among children presenting with persistent dysphagia was found to be 3.3%, a rate significantly higher than the reported 0.1%–0.77% seen in the broader paediatric population. This data implies that ongoing dysphagia may serve as an important signal for undiagnosed CMI.

The patient’s description of vertex pain that improves following hair cutting illustrates a curious, yet sparsely explored occurrence. Occipital neuralgia is sometimes seen alongside CMI and may produce increased sensitivity of the scalp. For diagnosis, criteria include ‘dysesthesia and/or allodynia observable with benign stimulation of the scalp or hair’. Nevertheless, the relationship between occipital neuralgia and CMI is infrequently documented, and there is no definitive evidence confirming a causal connection.

Another possibility to consider is the newly characterized 'burning vertex syndrome', which manifests as pain localized to the scalp, cutaneous allodynia, and clinical features that do not align precisely with recognized primary headache types like migraine, cluster, tension-type, or nummular headache. The proposed pathophysiology may involve small fiber pathways from the scalp, and patient responses to greater occipital nerve block are inconsistent. In the present case, the vertex complaints could represent: (1) occipital neuralgia associated with CMI; (2) burning vertex syndrome as a coincidental finding; or (3) another distinct primary headache condition. Comprehensive recording of cutaneous allodynia patterns in CMI populations remains insufficient, as reflected by the lack of standardized tools for assessing specific CMI symptoms before the introduction of the Chiari Symptom Profile. In light of this limited data, we report this observation as a noteworthy clinical finding rather than an established diagnostic criterion.

This case underscores the considerable difficulty in diagnosing CMI when its clinical presentation includes unusual or deceptive symptoms like central hypoventilation and choking, resulting in a protracted 14-year interval before accurate identification. The process of diagnosis, initially marked by uncertainty and eventually culminating in the recognition of a neurological cause, is frequently documented in published reports.
