Title: Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I

Ali Mohamed Mousa, Mervat Mahmoud, Ghaida Mubarak AlShuraiaan, Zouheir Ibrahim Bitar

Abstract:

Summary: A female patient in her early 40s reported a 7-year duration of recurrent choking episodes involving both solids and liquids, frequent nighttime choking events occurring three to four times each week, and longstanding occipital headaches that persisted for 14 years. During home monitoring in the COVID-19 pandemic, she was found to have severe episodes of oxygen desaturation (70%–80%) in the absence of respiratory distress. Several laryngoscopies and endoscopies investigating the choking complaints showed no abnormalities. A review of a head CT scan performed 14 years earlier for unrelated trauma revealed a previously unrecognized crowded foramen magnum. Subsequent MRI of the head and neck identified Chiari malformation type I (CMI) with 11 mm descent of the cerebellar tonsils. Polysomnography demonstrated mild sleep apnoea (apnoea–hypopnoea index 6.0) primarily due to central events (72%). This case highlights that CMI can manifest with respiratory symptoms closely resembling acquired central hypoventilation syndrome (Ondine's curse) and underscores the need to consider structural brain abnormalities in the evaluation of patients with unexplained respiratory symptoms.

Background:

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is characterized on sagittal MRI by downward displacement of the cerebellar tonsils measuring at least 5 mm below the foramen magnum. Recent imaging data indicate that CMI is present in roughly 1% of adults; however, only about 0.1% of the population exhibit symptoms. There is a slight female preponderance with a ratio of 1.3:1, and initial manifestations usually appear in the third decade.

The underlying mechanism involves an underdeveloped posterior fossa, resulting in a discrepancy between the size of neural tissue and the capacity of the cranial space. Nonetheless, recent research has contested the notion of Chiari malformation type I (CMI) as inherently abnormal, suggesting it exists along a continuum that ranges from benign anatomical variations to severe, symptomatic defects. New volumetric analyses reveal significant differences in posterior fossa size across various populations, with no reliable association to clinical symptoms. This evolving perspective calls for tailored, symptom-focused management strategies that separate clinically silent structural variations from conditions warranting operative treatment.

Chiari syndrome refers to the clinical presentation associated with CMI, marked by a characteristic group of neurological symptoms. The primary feature is intermittent occipital headache that is triggered by Valsalva maneuvers, occurring in more than 80% of individuals. Adult patients usually exhibit more pronounced symptoms than pediatric patients, particularly when syringomyelia is present concurrently. Lower cranial nerve dysfunction is observed in 15% to 26% of cases, commonly manifesting as oropharyngeal impairment including palatal weakness, tongue atrophy, or cricopharyngeal achalasia.

Respiratory symptoms are an emerging and well-recognized complication associated with CMI. In a prospective investigation involving 90 adult patients with CMI, half of them were found to suffer from sleep-related breathing abnormalities. An index for central apnoea of 5 or higher was identified in 6.7% of the cohort, yet solely one individual experienced pure central sleep apnoea, possibly attributable to compression of the brainstem. The impact of this respiratory dysfunction goes further than merely sleep disturbances; a collection of cases has described episodes of acute respiratory failure and severe hypoxaemia in individuals with CMI.

Respiratory impairment in these cases arises from compression of the brainstem, obstruction of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) outflow, and disturbance of neural respiratory control centers, resulting in episodic respiratory manifestations. These symptoms constitute an acquired variant of central hypoventilation syndrome—sometimes referred to as ‘Ondine’s curse’—which differs from the congenital central hypoventilation syndrome associated with PHOX2B gene mutations. Current recommendations highlight this association by suggesting that polysomnography should routinely be performed in patients with Chiari malformation type I (CMI) who are suspected to have sleep-related breathing disorders. Importantly, posterior fossa decompression may restore normal CSF circulation and alleviate respiratory symptoms, highlighting the necessity of considering CMI in cases of unexplained respiratory dysfunction.

Case presentation:

A woman in her early forties sought care at our family medicine clinic, reporting a 7-year history of unpredictable recurrent choking episodes during both meals and sleep, affecting ingestion of solids and liquids alike. She characterized these episodes as abrupt and distressing, frequently disrupting meals or causing her to awaken with forceful coughing and gasping; however, they never resulted in complete airway blockage. Episodes during the night occurred three to four times weekly, and occasionally twice in a single night. Throughout the course of her illness, she underwent multiple investigations, including three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper gastrointestinal endoscopies, all yielding normal results. Despite the absence of findings on diagnostic testing, her symptoms persisted unabated. The social consequences were considerable, as she refrained from attending restaurants or social events due to apprehension about experiencing choking in public. The persistent anxiety and embarrassment contributed to increased social isolation and emotional hardship.

A comprehensive review of her medical history uncovered that her symptoms originated 14 years prior, following a minor car accident during which she briefly lost consciousness. In the aftermath, she began to experience continuous headaches located in the occipital region and at the vertex. She characterized these headaches as persistent, pressure-like, and frequently confined to a small, coin-sized area on the vertex of her scalp. Notably, coughing, straining, or other Valsalva maneuvers did not worsen her headaches. Multiple clinicians diagnosed her with nummular headache, prompting a series of localized scalp injections that produced only fleeting symptom relief. The most prominent aspect of her presentation was severe allodynia at the vertex, to the extent that the weight of her hair alone provoked pain. Eventually, she observed that cutting her hair short decreased her typical pain level from 6/10 to 3/10; however, this adjustment clashed with cultural norms and caused considerable emotional hardship.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the patient started using a pulse oximeter at home to track her oxygen saturation levels. For several months, she consistently recorded episodes in which her oxygen saturation fell into the 70%–80% range, despite remaining asymptomatic. She only became aware of these events when she measured her levels. Various emergency department visits resulted in her symptoms being ascribed to anxiety.

A significant event took place at an international airport. As she was awaiting her flight, she developed sudden dizziness accompanied by nausea. Medical staff present recorded her oxygen saturation at 78%, resulting in her being prevented from boarding. After supplemental oxygen was provided, her symptoms subsided entirely within a few minutes. Later assessments by pulmonary specialists, including chest imaging and lung function tests, showed no abnormalities.

In light of her longstanding occipital and vertex headaches, escalating choking events, and episodes of unexplained hypoxia, clinicians sought a single neurological explanation. Review of her prior medical documentation disclosed that a non-contrast CT of the brain had been obtained 14 years earlier, after her trauma, and interpreted as normal. Nonetheless, meticulous reassessment of the original imaging identified a crowded foramen magnum and diminished cerebrospinal fluid space at the craniocervical junction. Differential diagnosis:

The constellation of choking affecting solids and liquids equally, unexplained hypoxia and chronic headaches prompted systematic evaluation:

Gastroesophageal reflux disease accompanied by laryngopharyngeal reflux was first suspected due to the presence of choking episodes. However, this was ruled out by normal pH monitoring results, unremarkable endoscopic findings, and absence of improvement following maximum acid suppression therapy.

Evaluation for myasthenia gravis was pursued given bulbar symptoms. However, negative antibody results, no evidence of fatigability, and stable symptoms throughout the day argued against this diagnosis.

After episodes of hypoxemia, an in-depth workup for primary pulmonary disease was performed. The absence of abnormalities on chest imaging and pulmonary function studies ruled out lung pathology.

Central alveolar hypoventilation syndrome (acquired Ondine’s curse) was highly indicated by the presence of intact conscious respiration while automatic breathing was diminished when the patient was distracted or asleep. The post-traumatic onset separated it from congenital variants.

The patient's symptoms were ultimately attributed to brainstem involvement from CMI, which caused pressure on the respiratory centers and nuclei of the lower cranial nerves, thereby offering a comprehensive explanation for the clinical presentation.

Discussion:

Close scrutiny is required regarding the timing of our patient’s motor vehicle collision and the emergence of symptoms thereafter. Chiari malformation type I is known to occur congenitally, but it has also been reported following head injury and other neurological insults. Current etiological frameworks (2022) reflect this breadth, differentiating primary Chiari malformations, which are congenital, from secondary or acquired variants stemming from trauma or elevated intracranial pressure.

In this case, a CT scan obtained shortly after the injury showed a foramen magnum with reduced space. Since imaging before the trauma is unavailable, it is unclear if the traumatic event produced this abnormality or simply exposed an existing condition. The patient’s occipital headache appearing immediately post-accident and the gradual emergence of bulbar and respiratory issues over a 14-year period may suggest either (1) herniation of the tonsils as a result of the trauma or (2) trauma aggravating or revealing an otherwise silent congenital variant.

This case highlights the intricate respiratory features associated with CMI and contributes to our comprehension of acquired central hypoventilation syndromes stemming from structural brainstem involvement. Differentiating between voluntary and automatic respiration is vital for grasping the respiratory pathophysiology in CMI. While cortical-mediated voluntary breathing is generally preserved in individuals with CMI, automatic respiration—which is regulated by brainstem centers and is indispensable during sleep—is impaired due to compression of medullary regions by tonsillar herniation. In this instance, the patient maintained the capacity for voluntary ventilation but exhibited a loss of intrinsic respiratory drive, reflecting a clinical picture similar to congenital central hypoventilation syndrome, albeit via a distinct underlying mechanism. The incident at the airport, in which brief inattention resulted in severe desaturation without a sensation of dyspnea, starkly demonstrates the selective disruption of automatic respiration: the individual effectively “forgets to breathe.”

We propose that the patient’s striking lack of dyspnoea during marked oxygen desaturation may be attributed to an underlying dysfunction within the brainstem. This presumed brainstem pathology likely disrupts essential afferent neural pathways responsible for conveying information to the cortex, where conscious sensation arises. Breathlessness represents a form of interoception, reliant on the transmission and integration of these ascending signals by central regions such as the anterior insular cortex. If these neural pathways are interrupted within the brainstem, the cortex is deprived of the requisite input to evoke the sensation of air hunger, thereby accounting for the patient’s concerning unawareness of their physiological compromise.

The substantial difference observed between the patient’s pronounced clinical symptoms and the relatively modest findings on polysomnography (AHI 6.0) requires clarification. For logistical reasons, we opted for a type III sleep study instead of type I, which does not incorporate EEG monitoring. The lack of EEG impeded precise determination of actual sleep duration, most probably resulting in considerable undercounting of respiratory disturbances. In the absence of EEG, AHI is computed based on the entire recording period, encompassing both sleep and awake states, rather than sleep alone. Consequently, this approach is likely responsible for the reported AHI of 6.0 significantly underrepresenting the actual extent of sleep-disordered breathing.

Nonetheless, several findings are indicative of central hypoventilation: (1) central apneas accounted for 72% of events even though the total AHI was low; (2) oxygen saturation levels were observed to drop to 70%–80% in the absence of dyspnea or patient recognition (occurring both at the airport and during standard assessments); and (3) imaging demonstrated substantial brainstem compression, as evidenced by an 11 mm descent of the cerebellar tonsils.

A Type I polysomnographic assessment, including comprehensive EEG monitoring, end-tidal CO₂ measurement, and video documentation, has been scheduled at an offsite sleep clinic. The execution of this evaluation is still awaiting synchronization of patient timing, consent to undergo the study, and availability at the outside location. When accomplished, this test will allow for a more conclusive assessment of sleep architecture, breathing abnormalities, and both the identification and severity of central hypoventilation.

It is recognized that, based on existing data, central hypoventilation continues to be a robustly supported clinical consideration rather than a diagnosis conclusively established.

The patient’s most troublesome symptom was oropharyngeal dysphagia, presenting with equivalent impairment for both solid and liquid intake, accompanied by episodes of choking and coughing. In a prospective study utilizing standardized swallowing evaluation tools, 36% of participants exhibited swallowing dysfunction, predominantly demonstrating neurogenic characteristics. Neurogenic dysphagia is set apart from mechanical types by the similar difficulty experienced with all food and fluid consistencies. Despite comprehensive assessment by gastroenterology and otolaryngology, which yielded unremarkable results, the patient’s most prominent symptom underwent repeated evaluation before a definitive diagnosis was reached, resulting in a delay of several years. This postponement underscores a critical clinical point: when dysphagia or choking occurs without observable structural abnormalities via endoscopic assessment, a neurological cause should be considered. In a retrospective analysis of pediatric patients with chronic dysphagia, the rate of CMI was found to be 3.3%, substantially exceeding the reported prevalence of 0.1%–0.77% in the general pediatric population, indicating that long-standing dysphagia may be a significant marker of underlying CMI.

The patient’s description of vertex pain alleviated by hair cutting illustrates an unusual and relatively unexamined clinical occurrence. Occipital neuralgia may be found in conjunction with CMI, leading to heightened sensitivity of the scalp; established diagnostic guidelines necessitate the presence of ‘dysesthesia and/or allodynia induced by non-painful stimulation of the scalp and/or hair’. Despite this, reports documenting the relationship between occipital neuralgia and CMI are scarce, and there is no definitive evidence confirming a causal connection.

Another possible explanation is the recently identified ‘burning vertex syndrome,’ which is characterised by scalp discomfort, cutaneous allodynia, and symptoms that do not align clearly with recognized primary headache types such as migraine, tension-type, cluster, or nummular headaches. The suggested underlying pathophysiology may involve dysfunction of small fiber nerve pathways from the scalp, and patients have shown mixed responses to greater occipital nerve blockade. For our patient, the symptoms localized to the vertex could indicate: (1) occipital neuralgia secondary to CMI; (2) a coincidental manifestation of burning vertex syndrome; or (3) a distinct primary headache entity. Comprehensive data on the distribution of cutaneous allodynia in patients with CMI remain insufficient, highlighted by the prior lack of tools designed to assess such symptoms specifically in CMI prior to the introduction of the Chiari Symptom Profile. In light of the limited available evidence, we describe this presentation as a notable clinical observation, rather than ascribing to it a clear diagnostic significance.

This case illustrates the considerable difficulty in diagnosing CMI when the presentation includes uncommon or confusing symptoms, such as central hypoventilation and choking, which resulted in a 14-year delay before a definitive diagnosis was reached. The process of establishing the correct neurological diagnosis after a period of uncertainty, as described here, reflects patterns commonly reported in the literature.
