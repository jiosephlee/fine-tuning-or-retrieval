Title: Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I

Ali Mohamed Mousa, Mervat Mahmoud, Ghaida Mubarak AlShuraiaan, Zouheir Ibrahim Bitar

Abstract:

Summary: A female patient in her early 40s reported a 7-year duration of recurrent choking on both solids and liquids, with nocturnal episodes happening three to four times per week, in addition to chronic occipital headaches persisting over 14 years. During home monitoring amid the COVID-19 pandemic, she was found to have marked episodes of oxygen desaturation (70%–80%) in the absence of respiratory distress. Multiple laryngoscopic and endoscopic evaluations aimed at identifying the source of choking were unremarkable. On reassessment, a noncontrast CT of the head from 14 years earlier—originally performed for trauma—demonstrated a previously unrecognized crowded foramen magnum. Subsequent MRI of the head and neck identified Chiari malformation type I (CMI) with 11 mm cerebellar tonsillar herniation. Polysomnography revealed mild sleep apnoea (apnoea–hypopnoea index 6.0), mainly consisting of central events (72%). This report highlights that CMI may manifest with respiratory symptoms resembling acquired central hypoventilation syndrome (Ondine’s curse), underscoring the necessity of evaluating for structural lesions of the brain in patients with otherwise unexplained breathing abnormalities.

Background:

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is characterized by inferior herniation of the cerebellar tonsils by a minimum of 5 mm beneath the foramen magnum, as seen on sagittal MRI sequences. Recent imaging data indicate that CMI is present in about 1% of adults; however, clinical symptoms manifest in roughly 0.1% of individuals. There is a slight predominance in females, with a ratio of 1.3:1, and most cases become apparent in patients during their third decade.

The underlying pathophysiology involves an abnormally reduced posterior fossa size, resulting in disproportionate confinement of neural elements within limited cranial space. Despite this, current research questions the longstanding view that Chiari Malformation type I (CMI) is invariably pathological, suggesting it may range from benign anatomical differences to significant clinical malformations. New volumetric analyses highlight marked differences in posterior fossa dimensions across demographic groups, yet fail to consistently associate these variations with symptoms. Consequently, this evolving perspective calls for a tailored, symptom-focused clinical approach that separates asymptomatic structural variants from disease processes necessitating operative management.

Chiari syndrome refers to the clinical expression of CMI, marked by a unique array of neurological symptoms. The primary complaint is episodic headache in the occipital region, triggered by actions such as Valsalva manoeuvres, and observed in over 80% of individuals affected. Adults generally exhibit more pronounced symptoms than pediatric patients, particularly when syringomyelia is also present. Involvement of the lower cranial nerves occurs in 15%–26% of cases and is commonly manifested by issues with oropharyngeal function, including palatal weakness, atrophy of the tongue, or cricopharyngeal achalasia.

Respiratory symptoms are now understood to be a growing complication associated with CMI. A prospective investigation involving 90 adult CMI patients found that half exhibited sleep-related breathing disorders. Among these, 6.7% demonstrated a central apnoea index of 5 or greater, though only a single patient had pure central sleep apnoea, possibly attributable to compression of the brainstem. These respiratory abnormalities are not limited to sleep disruptions; a series of cases has reported acute respiratory failure and critical hypoxaemia in individuals with CMI.

Respiratory dysfunction in this context arises from compression of the brainstem, obstruction of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) flow, and impairment of respiratory control centers, resulting in episodic respiratory manifestations. These symptoms constitute an acquired variant of central hypoventilation syndrome, also referred to as ‘Ondine’s curse’, which differs from the congenital form linked to PHOX2B mutations. Current recommendations highlight this association by advocating for routine polysomnography in CMI patients presenting with possible sleep-related breathing disturbances. Importantly, posterior fossa decompression may restore normal CSF circulation and potentially alleviate respiratory symptoms, highlighting the need to consider CMI when evaluating unexplained respiratory compromise.

Case presentation:

A female in her early forties came to our family medicine clinic reporting a 7-year history of recurrent choking episodes that happened unpredictably during both eating and sleeping, with no difference between solids and liquids. She characterized these events as abrupt and upsetting, frequently stopping her meals or waking her from sleep with severe coughing and gasping, although she never experienced full airway blockage. She stated that nocturnal episodes occurred three to four times per week, with some nights involving two occurrences. Multiple evaluations had been performed over the years, including three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper GI endoscopies, all yielding normal findings. Her symptoms persisted despite these assessments. The effects on her social life were considerable, as she avoided restaurants and social gatherings due to concerns about choking publicly. The ongoing fear and embarrassment caused her to withdraw socially and increased her emotional distress.

A thorough assessment of her clinical history indicated that her symptoms originated 14 years prior, shortly after a minor car accident during which she briefly lost consciousness. In the aftermath, she developed ongoing headaches localized to the occipital region and the vertex. These headaches were characterized as continuous, pressure-like, and frequently limited to a small, coin-sized area at the vertex. The headaches did not worsen with activities such as coughing, straining, or performing Valsalva maneuvers. Multiple clinicians diagnosed her with nummular headache, and she received several local injections into the scalp, which provided only temporary alleviation. The most notable symptom was pronounced allodynia at the vertex; even the simple act of her hair resting in that area provoked pain. She eventually found that keeping her hair short lowered her baseline pain score from 6/10 to 3/10, though this choice conflicted with cultural norms and contributed to psychological distress.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the patient started regularly checking her oxygen levels at home with a pulse oximeter. Over the course of several months, she noted multiple instances of asymptomatic hypoxemia, with saturations falling between 70% and 80%. She typically became aware of these low readings only upon using her pulse oximeter. On several occasions, emergency assessments ascribed her complaints to anxiety.

A significant event took place at an overseas airport. As she awaited her departure, she developed sudden onset of vertigo and queasiness. Medical staff present measured her oxygen saturation at 78%, resulting in her being prevented from boarding. After receiving supplemental oxygen, her symptoms promptly resolved within a few minutes. Later, assessment by a pulmonologist, which included chest radiography and pulmonary function tests, yielded normal results.

Because the patient exhibited persistent headaches localized to the occipital region and vertex, along with worsening episodes of choking and unexplained hypoxia, the clinicians sought a neurological explanation that could account for all these symptoms. Examination of her prior medical records disclosed a head CT without contrast performed 14 years before, after her accident. Though it was initially interpreted as normal, a thorough re-examination uncovered a crowded foramen magnum and absence of cerebrospinal fluid spaces at the craniocervical junction. Differential diagnosis:

The constellation of choking affecting solids and liquids equally, unexplained hypoxia and chronic headaches prompted systematic evaluation:

Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease accompanied by laryngopharyngeal reflux was first suspected due to the presence of choking episodes. However, this was ruled out after pH monitoring and endoscopic evaluations revealed no abnormalities, and the patient did not improve with high-dose acid suppressant therapy.

Myasthenia gravis was considered given involvement of bulbar symptoms. However, negative antibody results, no evidence of fatigability, and the absence of diurnal fluctuation reduced its likelihood.

An exhaustive assessment for primary lung disorders was performed after episodes of hypoxia. Both chest imaging and pulmonary function tests were within normal limits, thereby ruling out intrinsic pulmonary disease.

Central alveolar hypoventilation syndrome (acquired Ondine’s curse) was indicated by the patient’s ability to breathe voluntarily while demonstrating deficient automatic respiratory function when distracted or asleep. The onset after head injury differentiated this case from the congenital variants.

Brainstem abnormalities resulting from CMI accounted for the entire spectrum of symptoms by exerting pressure on the respiratory centers and nuclei of the lower cranial nerves, thereby yielding a cohesive diagnostic explanation.

Discussion:

The close timing between the patient's motor vehicle collision and the emergence of symptoms is noteworthy. Chiari malformation type I has been reported as a developmental anomaly present from birth, as well as occurring after head trauma or neurological insults. Recent etiological classification systems2022 acknowledge this range, differentiating primary (congenital) Chiari malformations from secondary or acquired variants caused by injury or elevated intracranial pressure.

In this patient, post-accident CT imaging showed a congested foramen magnum. Since no pre-injury scans were available, it remains unclear if this abnormality was a consequence of the traumatic event or a pre-existing condition uncovered by the trauma. The occurrence of occipital headaches immediately following the injury, along with a subsequent gradual onset of bulbar and respiratory symptoms spanning 14 years, may be indicative of either: (1) tonsillar herniation resulting directly from trauma, or (2) trauma accentuating or disclosing a congenital anomaly that had been asymptomatic until that time.

This report illustrates the intricate respiratory presentations associated with CMI and broadens our knowledge of central hypoventilation patterns acquired through structural pathology of the brainstem. Differentiating between voluntary and automatic respiration is key to grasping the respiratory pathophysiology seen in CMI. In these cases, voluntary respiration—governed by cortical neural pathways—usually remains functional. Conversely, automatic breathing, which depends on brainstem centers and is critical during sleep, becomes impaired as medullary structures are compressed by tonsillar herniation. In our case, the patient maintained the capacity for voluntary breathing but experienced loss of automatic ventilatory drive, presenting with a phenotype akin to congenital central hypoventilation syndrome, although resulting from a distinct pathophysiology. The incident at the airport, during which a brief lapse in attention led to marked hypoxemia absent any sensation of breathlessness, provides a striking example of this isolated deficit in automatic respiratory regulation—the patient essentially ‘forgets to breathe’.

We propose that an underlying dysfunction in the brainstem may explain the striking absence of dyspnoea noted in this patient, despite severe episodes of hypoxaemia. Such a brainstem abnormality could disrupt essential afferent neural pathways responsible for transmitting signals to the cerebral cortex required for conscious perception. The sensation of breathlessness is a specific type of interoception that depends on these afferent inputs being processed by crucial cortical areas, such as the anterior insular cortex. If these neural signals are blocked or interrupted within the brainstem, the cortex would not receive adequate information to produce the subjective experience of air hunger, which could account for the patient's failure to recognize their life-threatening physiological condition.

The notable difference observed between the patient's pronounced clinical presentation and the comparatively low AHI (6.0) seen on polysomnography requires clarification. In this case, type III polysomnography was performed instead of type I, primarily due to logistical constraints. Since type III studies do not incorporate EEG monitoring, precise assessment of total sleep time was not possible, and as a result, the frequency of respiratory events may have been considerably underestimated. The lack of EEG meant that the AHI was determined using the total recording period, which also includes times when the patient was awake, rather than isolating periods of actual sleep. Consequently, this methodological shortcoming suggests that the measured AHI of 6.0 likely understates the actual extent of the patient’s sleep-disordered breathing.

Although this limitation exists, multiple findings are indicative of central hypoventilation: (1) central events constituted the majority (72%) despite a relatively low total AHI; (2) there were episodes of oxygen saturation dropping to 70%–80% unaccompanied by dyspnoea or the patient's recognition (noted both at the airport and during routine assessments); and (3) imaging demonstrated substantial brainstem compression, with 11 mm descent of the cerebellar tonsils.

A comprehensive Type I polysomnography—including full EEG monitoring, end-tidal capnography, and video documentation—has been scheduled to take place at an outside sleep facility. The study has not yet been performed due to the need for alignment between the patient's schedule, their consent to undergo the procedure, and availability at the remote site. Upon completion, this test will allow for clearer delineation of sleep stages, respiratory function, and the degree of central hypoventilation present.

At present, the diagnosis of central hypoventilation is considered a strong clinical suspicion based on available data, rather than an established fact.

Oropharyngeal dysphagia, which equally affected the intake of solids and liquids and was accompanied by episodes of choking and coughing, represented the most bothersome complaint for our patient. Prospective analyses using standardized swallowing tools revealed dysfunction in 36% of cases, with neurogenic features being most prevalent. The presence of similar impairment across all food and fluid consistencies serves to differentiate neurogenic dysphagia from mechanical forms. Although the patient's principal symptom was thoroughly and repeatedly assessed by gastroenterology and otolaryngology specialists, no abnormalities were detected; despite this, several years elapsed before an accurate diagnosis was made. The delay underscores a key clinical point: when endoscopy reveals no structural explanation for dysphagia or choking, a neurological etiology should be considered. In a retrospective review of children presenting with persistent dysphagia, the frequency of CMI was found to be 3.3%, which markedly surpasses the estimated prevalence of 0.1%–0.77% in the broader paediatric population. This observation implies that chronic dysphagia may serve as a significant clue to underlying CMI.

The patient's experience of pain localized to the vertex, which improves after hair cutting, illustrates a peculiar but largely unexplored occurrence. Occipital neuralgia, seen alongside CMI, can produce increased scalp sensitivity; its diagnostic criteria include ‘dysesthesia and/or allodynia observable during non-painful stimulation of the scalp and/or hair’. Despite this, reports connecting occipital neuralgia with CMI are uncommon, and definitive evidence confirming a causal relationship is lacking.

Another possibility is the recently recognized entity known as ‘burning vertex syndrome’, characterized by pain on the scalp, cutaneous allodynia, and clinical features that do not align cleanly with established primary headache categories such as migraine, cluster headache, tension-type headache, or nummular headache. The proposed underlying mechanism may involve small fiber neural pathways arising from the scalp, and variable clinical responses to greater occipital nerve block have been observed in affected individuals. For our patient, the vertex symptoms could be attributed to one of the following: (1) occipital neuralgia associated with CMI; (2) burning vertex syndrome manifesting independently; or (3) a distinct primary headache condition. There is currently insufficient systematic recording of cutaneous allodynia manifestations within CMI patient groups, highlighted by the historical lack of validated tools to measure these symptoms prior to the advent of the Chiari Symptom Profile. Given the limited available data, we offer this as a notable clinical finding rather than a conclusive diagnostic marker.

This case underscores the considerable difficulties in diagnosing CMI, particularly when the condition manifests with uncommon or deceptive signs like central hypoventilation and choking, resulting in a 14-year lapse before accurate identification. The course of diagnosis was marked by early uncertainty and a slow progression to a definitive neurological diagnosis—a pattern frequently described in previous reports.
