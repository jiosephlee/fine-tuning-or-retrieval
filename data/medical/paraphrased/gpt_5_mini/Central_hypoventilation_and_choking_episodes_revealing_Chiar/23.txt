Title: Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I

Ali Mohamed Mousa, Mervat Mahmoud, Ghaida Mubarak AlShuraiaan, Zouheir Ibrahim Bitar

Abstract:

Summary: A female patient in her early 40s reported a 7-year history of recurrent choking episodes involving both solids and liquids, as well as nocturnal choking that occurred 3–4 times per week, along with chronic occipital headaches over 14 years. During the COVID-19 pandemic, home monitoring identified unexpected episodes of severe oxygen desaturation (ranging from 70% to 80%) in the absence of respiratory distress. Comprehensive laryngoscopic and endoscopic evaluations for the choking episodes were unremarkable. Re-examination of a 14-year-old CT head scan, originally performed after trauma, uncovered a previously missed crowded foramen magnum. MRI imaging of the head and neck subsequently demonstrated Chiari malformation type I (CMI) with 11 mm of cerebellar tonsillar herniation. A polysomnography study revealed mild sleep apnoea, with an apnoea–hypopnoea index of 6.0, predominantly due to central apnoeic events (72%). This report highlights that CMI may manifest with respiratory complaints that resemble acquired central hypoventilation syndrome (Ondine’s curse), underlining the need to evaluate for structural central nervous system pathology in patients presenting with unexplained respiratory symptoms.

Background:

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is characterized by the downward herniation of the cerebellar tonsils measuring 5 mm or more beneath the foramen magnum on sagittal MRI. Recent imaging research indicates that CMI is present in about 1% of adults, but only roughly 0.1% of the population experiences symptoms. There is a slight female preponderance with a ratio of 1.3:1, and onset most frequently occurs in individuals in their third decade.

The underlying mechanism involves a congenitally undersized posterior fossa, resulting in disproportionate space for neural elements. Nonetheless, modern research has questioned the notion that Chiari Malformation Type I (CMI) is invariably pathologic, suggesting it represents a continuum ranging from benign anatomical variants to cases with clinical consequences. Contemporary volumetric analyses reveal marked demographic differences in posterior fossa size, often lacking consistent association with clinical manifestations. This evolving perspective underscores the importance of tailored, symptom-driven clinical management that differentiates incidental anatomical findings from pathological conditions necessitating operative treatment.

Chiari syndrome refers to the clinical presentation of CMI, marked by a specific set of neurological symptoms. The primary complaint is sudden-onset occipital headache triggered by Valsalva maneuvers, which occurs in more than 80% of patients. Adults generally exhibit more pronounced symptoms compared to pediatric cases, particularly if syringomyelia is present. Involvement of the lower cranial nerves is observed in 15%–26% of individuals, commonly manifesting as oropharyngeal deficits including palatal weakness, tongue atrophy, or cricopharyngeal achalasia.

Respiratory symptoms are now acknowledged as an emerging complication associated with CMI. In a prospective analysis involving 90 adult patients diagnosed with CMI, half exhibited sleep-associated breathing abnormalities. Among these, 6.7% demonstrated a central apnoea index of 5 or higher, while only a single patient experienced isolated central sleep apnoea, possibly indicating brainstem involvement. The respiratory issues are not confined to sleep disorders; a case series has also reported occurrences of acute respiratory failure and severe hypoxaemia in individuals with CMI.

Respiratory impairment in this context is attributed to compression of the brainstem, obstruction of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) egress, and disturbance of neural respiratory control centers, which collectively contribute to episodic respiratory manifestations. These clinical features constitute an acquired variant of central hypoventilation syndrome—sometimes referred to as ‘Ondine’s curse’—that is separate from the congenital central hypoventilation syndrome form associated with PHOX2B gene mutations. Contemporary guidelines highlight this relationship by advocating for routine polysomnographic evaluation in patients with CMI who have symptoms suggestive of sleep-disordered breathing. Notably, performing posterior fossa decompression may normalize CSF circulation and alleviate respiratory symptoms, emphasizing the need to consider CMI in patients presenting with unexplained respiratory abnormalities.

Case presentation:

A female patient in her early 40s arrived at our family medicine clinic reporting a seven-year history of repeated choking episodes that happened unpredictably during both meals and sleep, affecting the ingestion of solids and liquids equally. She characterized these events as abrupt and highly distressing, frequently interrupting her meals or waking her up at night with forceful coughing and difficulty breathing, although they had never led to a complete blockage of her airway. Nocturnal incidents occurred three to four times each week, and sometimes as many as twice in one night. Throughout this period, she underwent multiple investigations, including three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper gastrointestinal endoscopies, none of which revealed any abnormalities. Despite normal test results, her symptoms remained unchanged. These issues had a considerable impact on her social life, leading her to avoid dining out and social events for fear of choking in public. The ongoing anxiety and feelings of humiliation contributed to progressive social isolation and emotional suffering.

A more comprehensive medical history indicated that her symptoms originated 14 years prior, subsequent to a minor car accident during which she experienced a brief loss of consciousness. Soon after the incident, she began to suffer from persistent headaches in the occipital region and at the vertex. She characterized these headaches as continuous, pressure-like, and frequently confined to a coin-sized area at the vertex of her scalp. The headaches did not worsen with activities such as coughing, straining, or other Valsalva maneuvers. She was evaluated by several doctors, who diagnosed her condition as nummular headache; she received multiple localized scalp injections, which provided only short-term relief. A particularly notable symptom was pronounced allodynia at the vertex, to the extent that even her hair’s weight provoked discomfort. Ultimately, she found that trimming her hair short lowered her usual pain score from 6/10 to 3/10, though this action ran counter to cultural norms and caused her significant emotional distress.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the patient started checking her oxygen levels at home with a personal pulse oximeter. For several months, she consistently recorded episodes of oxygen saturation decreasing to between 70% and 80%, despite lacking symptoms. She typically became aware of these desaturations only after checking the oximeter. Several emergency department assessments concluded her symptoms were due to anxiety.

An important event took place at an international airport. As she awaited her flight, she suddenly became dizzy and nauseated. Medical staff at the scene recorded an oxygen saturation of 78%, resulting in her being prohibited from boarding. When supplemental oxygen was provided, her symptoms resolved entirely in a matter of minutes. Later assessments of her lungs, encompassing chest imaging and pulmonary function tests, revealed no abnormalities.

Considering her persistent occipital and vertex headaches, escalating episodes of choking, and unaccounted-for hypoxia, clinicians sought a neurological explanation that could encompass all these features. A look into her past medical documentation uncovered a head CT scan without contrast, conducted 14 years prior after her accident, which had initially been interpreted as unremarkable. Nevertheless, upon close reassessment, the scan demonstrated a crowded foramen magnum and obliteration of cerebrospinal fluid spaces at the craniocervical junction. Differential diagnosis:

The constellation of choking affecting solids and liquids equally, unexplained hypoxia and chronic headaches prompted systematic evaluation:

Laryngopharyngeal reflux associated with gastro-oesophageal reflux disease was suspected at first due to the presence of choking complaints. However, this diagnosis was ruled out based on normal results from pH monitoring and endoscopic evaluations, as well as the absence of improvement with full-dose acid suppression therapy.

Myasthenia gravis was considered as a possible diagnosis given the patient's bulbar symptoms. However, negative results for antibody assays, no evidence of fatigability, and the patient did not display diurnal symptom variation, making myasthenia gravis an improbable cause.

Comprehensive assessment for underlying pulmonary disease was conducted in response to episodes of hypoxemia. Imaging studies of the chest and pulmonary function assessments were normal, ruling out intrinsic pulmonary disorders.

Central alveolar hypoventilation syndrome (acquired Ondine's curse) was indicated by the maintenance of voluntary respiration while automatic breathing was diminished when the patient was distracted or asleep. Its onset after head injury differentiated it from congenital variants.

Brainstem involvement resulting from CMI accounted for all clinical manifestations by exerting pressure on the respiratory control centers and nuclei of the lower cranial nerves, thereby offering a comprehensive explanation for the patient's symptoms.

Discussion:

It is important to scrutinize the timing between this patient’s motor vehicle accident and the development of symptoms. Chiari malformation type I may present as an inherent congenital disorder, but it has also been reported following head injury and other neurologic insults. Current etiological frameworks in 2022 identify this range, separating primary (congenital) Chiari malformations from secondary or acquired types attributed to trauma or elevated intracranial pressure.

In this patient, a CT scan obtained right after the injury showed a crowded foramen magnum. Since no imaging was available before the trauma, it is unclear whether this anatomical appearance was induced by the injury or simply made apparent because of it. The patient experienced immediate occipital headaches following the trauma, with bulbar and respiratory symptoms emerging gradually over a 14-year period. This sequence may represent either (1) herniation of the cerebellar tonsils secondary to trauma or (2) a preexisting congenital abnormality that was worsened or revealed by the traumatic event.

This case illustrates the intricate respiratory presentations observed in CMI and contributes to our knowledge of acquired central hypoventilation patterns associated with structural abnormalities of the brainstem. Differentiating between voluntary and automatic respiration is pivotal for comprehending the pathophysiology of respiratory disturbances in CMI. While cortical circuits responsible for voluntary respiration are generally spared in patients with CMI, automatic respiration—regulated by brainstem centers and critical during sleep—is disrupted because of compression of the medulla by herniated tonsils. The patient maintained voluntary breathing capability but lost the capacity for automatic ventilatory drive, a pattern akin to congenital central hypoventilation syndrome, albeit with a distinct underlying pathogenesis. The episode at the airport, during which transient distraction resulted in significant oxygen desaturation without the sensation of dyspnoea, compellingly demonstrates the isolated impairment of automatic respiratory control; the patient, in essence, ‘forgets to breathe’.

We propose that a primary dysfunction within the brainstem is responsible for the concerning lack of dyspnoea observed in this patient during episodes of marked oxygen desaturation. Such a brainstem abnormality may interfere with essential afferent neural pathways that must reach the cortex to elicit conscious perception. The sensation of breathlessness represents a type of interoception that relies on processing these afferent signals by critical areas of the brain, such as the anterior insular cortex. If these signals are disrupted while passing through the brainstem, the cortex would not obtain the necessary information to induce the feeling of air hunger, accounting for the patient’s hazardous unawareness of their physiological compromise.

The pronounced difference between the patient’s significant clinical symptoms and the comparatively modest findings on polysomnography (AHI 6.0) requires clarification. Owing to logistical constraints, a type III polysomnography was performed instead of the more comprehensive type I, resulting in the lack of EEG recording. Without EEG, it was not possible to determine the actual duration of sleep, which likely caused a considerable underreporting of respiratory disturbances. The AHI was calculated based on the entire duration of the recording, encompassing periods when the patient was awake, rather than just the time spent asleep. As a result, this methodological issue suggests the measured AHI of 6.0 likely does not reflect the true extent of sleep-disordered breathing in this case.

Although this limitation exists, multiple findings indicate central hypoventilation: (1) central events constituted 72% of respiratory disturbances, even though the total AHI remained low; (2) oxygen saturation was observed to drop to 70%–80% on several occasions without any accompanying shortness of breath or awareness on the patient's part (both during routine interactions and at the airport); and (3) anatomical imaging revealed marked compression of the brainstem, evidenced by an 11 mm descent of the tonsils.

A Type I polysomnography, incorporating comprehensive EEG monitoring, end-tidal capnography, and video surveillance, has been scheduled at an outside sleep clinic. The study has not yet been performed, as it awaits synchronization of the patient’s availability, patient consent for the procedure, and appointment arrangements at the off-site location. Upon completion, this assessment is expected to yield clearer information about the patient’s sleep stages, breathing dynamics, and the extent and presence of central hypoventilation.

It is recognized that, based on existing data, central hypoventilation continues to be a clinically plausible diagnosis, but it has not yet been conclusively established.

Our patient’s most prominent and bothersome symptom was oropharyngeal dysphagia affecting both solid and liquid foods equally, accompanied by episodes of choking and coughing. A prospective assessment utilizing standardized swallowing evaluation tools revealed dysfunction in 36% of cases, predominantly displaying neurogenic characteristics. The comparable challenges with all food consistencies help differentiate neurogenic dysphagia from mechanical causes. Despite extensive initial and repeated investigations from gastrointestinal and otolaryngological viewpoints, which yielded normal results, the patient’s diagnosis was postponed for several years. This diagnostic delay underscores an essential clinical takeaway: the presence of dysphagia or choking without evident structural abnormalities on endoscopic examination should raise suspicion for a neurologic etiology. In a retrospective review of children presenting with persistent dysphagia, the rate of CMI was found to be 3.3%, significantly exceeding the 0.1%–0.77% prevalence reported among the broader paediatric population. These findings indicate that ongoing dysphagia may serve as a critical sign of underlying CMI.

The patient’s description of pain localized to the vertex of the scalp that improves after hair cutting illustrates a curious yet insufficiently explored clinical finding. Occipital neuralgia has been observed in conjunction with Chiari malformation type I (CMI) and may present with increased sensitivity of the scalp; criteria for diagnosis include ‘dysesthesia and/or allodynia triggered by benign stimulation of the scalp and/or hair’. Despite this, the connection between occipital neuralgia and CMI is seldom documented in the literature and there is a lack of definitive evidence supporting a causal relationship.

Another possible explanation is the recently identified ‘burning vertex syndrome’, characterized by scalp discomfort, cutaneous allodynia, and signs that do not conform precisely to recognized primary headache types such as migraine, cluster, tension-type, or nummular headaches. The proposed underlying mechanism might involve small fiber nerves arising from the scalp, and therapeutic responses to greater occipital nerve block have been inconsistent among affected individuals. For our patient, the symptoms at the vertex might reflect: (1) occipital neuralgia secondary to CMI; (2) burning vertex syndrome occurring coincidentally; or (3) a distinct primary headache disorder unrelated to either. The systematic reporting of patterns of cutaneous allodynia among individuals with CMI remains insufficient, highlighted by the lack of any dedicated tool to evaluate such symptoms in CMI patients before the Chiari Symptom Profile was developed. In view of the limited data available, we report this observation as a noteworthy clinical feature, but not as a conclusive diagnostic criterion.

This case underscores the considerable difficulties in identifying CMI, particularly when clinical manifestations are unusual or deceptive, including central hypoventilation and episodes of choking, resulting in a delayed diagnosis spanning 14 years. The pathway to accurate diagnosis involved early uncertainty and gradual clarification, a pattern commonly described in the published literature.
