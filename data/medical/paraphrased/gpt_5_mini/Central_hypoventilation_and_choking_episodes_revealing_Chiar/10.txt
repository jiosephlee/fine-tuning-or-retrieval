Title: Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I

Ali Mohamed Mousa, Mervat Mahmoud, Ghaida Mubarak AlShuraiaan, Zouheir Ibrahim Bitar

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her early forties presented with a 7-year history of repeated choking episodes involving both solids and liquids, frequent nocturnal choking (3–4 times per week), and ongoing occipital headaches for 14 years. During home monitoring undertaken amid the COVID-19 pandemic, she was found to have episodes of severe oxygen desaturation (70%–80%) but exhibited no signs of respiratory distress. Multiple laryngoscopic and endoscopic evaluations for her choking symptoms yielded normal findings. Re-examination of a 14-year-old CT scan of her head, originally performed after unrelated trauma, identified a previously unrecognized crowded foramen magnum. Subsequent MRI of the head and neck confirmed a Chiari malformation type I (CMI) with cerebellar tonsillar descent measuring 11 mm. Polysomnography revealed mild sleep apnoea (apnoea–hypopnoea index of 6.0) characterized predominantly by central events (72%). This case demonstrates that CMI may present with respiratory symptoms resembling those of acquired central hypoventilation syndrome (Ondine’s curse), highlighting the need to consider underlying structural abnormalities of the brain in patients with unexplained respiratory compromise.

Background:

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is characterized by downward herniation of the cerebellar tonsils extending 5 mm or more beneath the foramen magnum on sagittal magnetic resonance imaging. Current imaging data indicate that CMI is present in 1% of adults, yet only about 0.1% of the population experience symptoms. There is a slight female predominance with a ratio of 1.3:1, and the disorder most often manifests in individuals in their third decade.

The underlying pathophysiology involves a congenitally reduced posterior fossa size, which leads to a disproportion between neural elements and the containing space. Nonetheless, recent data dispute the longstanding view of CMI as inherently abnormal, suggesting a continuum ranging from benign anatomical differences to true pathological malformations. Contemporary volumetric analyses have revealed notable differences in posterior fossa dimensions across various populations, often without a direct link to clinical symptoms. This evolving perspective calls for tailored clinical strategies focused on symptoms, carefully differentiating between asymptomatic anatomical variations and disease processes that warrant surgical management.

Chiari syndrome refers to the symptomatic presentation associated with CMI, marked by a particular set of neurological symptoms. The most prominent feature is intermittent occipital headache triggered by Valsalva manoeuvres, occurring in more than 80% of individuals. Symptom severity in adults is generally greater than in children, particularly in the presence of syringomyelia. Involvement of the lower cranial nerves occurs in 15%–26% of cases, most often manifesting as oropharyngeal disturbances like palatal weakness, atrophy of the tongue, or cricopharyngeal achalasia.

Pulmonary complications are now more widely acknowledged as part of the clinical spectrum of CMI. In a prospective cohort involving 90 adult individuals diagnosed with CMI, half demonstrated sleep-disordered breathing. An elevated central apnoea index (≥5) was identified in 6.7% of cases, yet only a single patient exhibited isolated central sleep apnoea, which may be attributable to brainstem involvement. These respiratory issues are not confined to sleep disruptions; a separate series reported occurrences of acute respiratory failure and severe, potentially fatal hypoxaemia among patients with CMI.

Respiratory impairment in this context is attributed to factors such as compression of the brainstem, blockade of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) pathways, and interference with centers responsible for respiratory regulation, all of which may manifest as episodic breathing disturbances. These manifestations constitute an acquired variant of central hypoventilation syndrome, sometimes referred to as ‘Ondine’s curse,’ which differs from the congenital form linked to PHOX2B gene mutations. Recently published guidelines highlight this association, advocating for routine polysomnography in individuals with Chiari malformation type I (CMI) who display possible sleep-related breathing disturbances. Importantly, posterior fossa decompression surgery offers the possibility of re-establishing normal CSF flow and may reverse respiratory symptoms, emphasizing the need to consider CMI in cases of unexplained respiratory dysfunction.

Case presentation:

A woman in her early forties came to our family medicine clinic reporting a 7-year duration of repeated choking incidents that occurred randomly during both eating and sleeping, affecting the ingestion of solids and liquids equally. She characterized these events as abrupt and highly distressing, frequently interrupting her meals or awakening her at night with intense bouts of coughing and gasping, although she never experienced total airway blockage. The nocturnal episodes happened three to four times per week, and sometimes even twice within a single night. Throughout the years, she had received multiple comprehensive assessments, including three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper GI endoscopies, all yielding normal results. Nevertheless, her symptoms continued without relief. The condition had a marked social toll; she started to avoid dining out and social events out of concern for choking in public. The persistent anxiety and sense of embarrassment contributed to progressive social isolation and growing psychological distress.

A more thorough review of her medical history indicated that her symptoms started 14 years prior, following a minor car accident that resulted in a brief loss of consciousness. Soon after the incident, she began experiencing unremitting headaches in the occipital and vertex regions. She characterized the pain as a continuous, pressure-like sensation, frequently concentrated in a coin-sized area at the vertex. The headaches did not worsen with coughing, straining, or other Valsalva maneuvers. Several clinicians diagnosed her with nummular headache and administered multiple local scalp injections; however, these provided only temporary alleviation. A notable aspect of her condition was pronounced allodynia at the vertex, such that even the sensation of her hair resting on the area would provoke discomfort. She eventually found that keeping her hair short diminished her baseline pain from 6 out of 10 to 3 out of 10, though this decision conflicted with cultural norms and led to psychological distress.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the patient started using a home pulse oximeter to monitor her oxygen saturation levels. Over the course of several months, she repeatedly recorded asymptomatic desaturation events, with saturations falling into the 70%–80% range. These incidents were typically unnoticed until she reviewed the readings from her device. On several occasions, emergency assessments concluded that her symptoms were related to anxiety.

A significant event took place at an international airport. As she awaited her flight, the patient developed sudden onset vertigo accompanied by nausea. Airport medical staff measured her oxygen saturation at 78% and subsequently prevented her from boarding. After receiving supplemental oxygen, her symptoms rapidly abated and resolved entirely within a few minutes. Follow-up pulmonary assessment, which included chest imaging and pulmonary function tests, demonstrated normal results.

Due to the presence of persistent occipital and vertex headaches in conjunction with worsening episodes of choking and unexplained episodes of hypoxia, efforts were made to identify a single neurological disorder accounting for all symptoms. Historical chart review uncovered a non-contrast head CT performed 14 years prior, after her accident, which at the time was interpreted as unremarkable. Upon thorough re-examination, the CT demonstrated evidence of a crowded foramen magnum and absent cerebrospinal fluid space at the craniocervical junction. Differential diagnosis:

The constellation of choking affecting solids and liquids equally, unexplained hypoxia and chronic headaches prompted systematic evaluation:

Given the patient’s complaints of choking, gastroesophageal reflux disease accompanied by laryngopharyngeal reflux was initially suspected. However, this was ruled out based on normal findings from pH monitoring and endoscopic evaluation, as well as a failure to improve with maximal acid suppression therapy.

Myasthenia gravis was evaluated due to symptoms affecting the bulbar region. Negative antibody testing, lack of fatigability and absence of diurnal variation made this unlikely.

Primary pulmonary pathology was extensively investigated following hypoxic episodes. Normal chest imaging and pulmonary function tests excluded intrinsic lung disease.

Central alveolar hypoventilation syndrome (acquired Ondine’s curse) was highly suspected due to maintenance of voluntary respiration while automatic respiratory drive was compromised during periods of sleep or distraction. Its onset after head injury differentiated it from the congenital variants.

Brainstem pathology secondary to CMI ultimately explained all symptoms through compression of the respiratory centres and lower cranial nerve nuclei, providing the unifying diagnosis.

Discussion:

The timing of symptom emergence following our patient’s motor vehicle accident necessitates thorough evaluation. Chiari malformation type I is recognized to occur congenitally, but it can also develop after head trauma or other neurological events. Current etiological frameworks (2022) delineate this continuum by differentiating primary (congenital) Chiari malformations from secondary or acquired variants that arise due to trauma or elevated intracranial pressure.

In this patient, a CT scan obtained right after the incident showed a crowded foramen magnum. Since no imaging was available prior to the trauma, it remains unclear if this finding resulted from the injury or if the event simply uncovered a preexisting condition. The patient experienced occipital headaches immediately after the trauma, with gradual onset of bulbar and respiratory symptoms over a 14-year period. This sequence could indicate either trauma-induced tonsillar herniation or an injury that worsened or brought to light an otherwise silent congenital anomaly.

This case highlights the intricate respiratory involvement seen in CMI, contributing to a deeper comprehension of acquired central hypoventilation associated with structural lesions of the brainstem. Differentiating voluntary from automatic respiration is key to appreciating the respiratory pathophysiology in CMI. While voluntary respiration, governed by cortical circuits, is generally maintained in individuals with CMI, automatic respiration—regulated by the brainstem and vital for breathing during sleep—is compromised owing to compression of medullary regions by herniated tonsils. In the present case, the patient retained the capacity for voluntary breathing but lost the involuntary respiratory drive, producing a clinical picture similar to congenital central hypoventilation syndrome, though arising through a distinct mechanism. The incident at the airport, during which a brief lapse in attention resulted in marked oxygen desaturation absent of breathlessness, starkly demonstrates this specific impairment of involuntary control: the patient, in effect, ‘forgets to breathe’.

We propose that the patient’s notable lack of dyspnoea during episodes of marked oxygen desaturation stems from an underlying dysfunction within the brainstem. Such brainstem involvement likely impairs the essential afferent neural pathways that communicate to the cortex, which are required for conscious perception. Breathlessness is considered an aspect of interoception, relying on the brain’s interpretation of these afferent signals by regions such as the anterior insular cortex. If these neural transmissions are interrupted at the brainstem level, the cortex fails to receive the necessary information to produce the sensation of air hunger, thereby accounting for the patient’s dangerously reduced awareness of their compromised physiological state.

The significant mismatch between the patient’s pronounced clinical symptoms and the comparatively modest polysomnographic results (AHI 6.0) requires clarification. For logistical reasons, type III polysomnography was employed instead of type I; type III does not include EEG channels. As a result, we were unable to precisely determine actual sleep duration, likely resulting in substantial undercounting of respiratory disturbances. In the absence of EEG data, the AHI is determined by dividing the number of events by the total recording duration—which includes both sleep and wakefulness—rather than by actual sleep time. Consequently, this limitation of the testing approach likely means that the observed AHI of 6.0 underrepresents the true extent of sleep-related breathing pathology in this patient.

Nonetheless, there are multiple factors that indicate central hypoventilation: (1) a high proportion of central apneic events (72%) in the context of a relatively low total AHI; (2) episodes of oxygen saturation dropping to 70%–80% were recorded, yet the patient did not experience dyspnea or recognize these occurrences (noted both in airport settings and during standard assessments); and (3) imaging revealed considerable compression of the brainstem, with a tonsillar herniation measuring 11 mm.

A comprehensive sleep study (Type I polysomnography), incorporating full EEG tracing, end-tidal capnography, and video monitoring, has been scheduled at an outside sleep facility. The procedure has not yet taken place due to the need to coordinate with the patient regarding their availability and willingness, as well as aligning with the external center's scheduling constraints. Upon completion, the assessment will yield detailed information on the patient's sleep architecture, breathing patterns during sleep, and allow for a more accurate determination of central hypoventilation, including its severity.

We acknowledge that, given the current evidence, central hypoventilation remains a well-supported clinical hypothesis, rather than a definitively confirmed diagnosis.

The most troubling symptom experienced by our patient was oropharyngeal dysphagia, which affected both solids and liquids equally and was accompanied by episodes of coughing and choking. Validated swallowing assessment tools in a prospective study identified swallowing impairment in 36% of individuals, primarily demonstrating neurogenic characteristics. Difficulty swallowing materials of all consistencies is a feature that distinguishes neurogenic dysphagia from mechanical etiologies. The symptom that caused the most distress for the patient was initially, and on several occasions, assessed by both gastroenterology and otolaryngology teams, with all investigations yielding normal results; however, the correct diagnosis was not made until several years later. This delay underscores a significant clinical point: when dysphagia or choking occurs without an identifiable structural abnormality on endoscopic examination, neurologic causes should be considered. In a retrospective analysis, the prevalence of CMI among children presenting with persistent dysphagia was 3.3%, which is notably higher than the reported prevalence of 0.1%–0.77% in the general pediatric population. These findings indicate that ongoing dysphagia may serve as a significant clinical clue pointing to the presence of CMI.

The patient described vertex pain that improved following haircuts, illustrating an unusual but not well-explored observation. Occipital neuralgia, which can be seen in patients with CMI, may result in heightened scalp sensitivity. Diagnostic standards specify “dysesthesia and/or allodynia apparent during innocuous stimulation of the scalp and/or hair.” Nevertheless, reports of occipital neuralgia occurring with CMI are uncommon, and there is insufficient evidence to confirm a direct causal relationship.

A recently characterized entity, termed ‘burning vertex syndrome’, should also be contemplated. This syndrome manifests as pain localized to the scalp vertex, accompanied by cutaneous allodynia and features that do not correspond well to existing categories of primary headaches such as migraine, cluster headache, tension-type headache, or nummular headache. The proposed underlying mechanism involves small fiber nerve pathways within the scalp, and affected individuals show variable improvement following greater occipital nerve blockade. For our patient, symptoms at the vertex could be indicative of: (1) occipital neuralgia in association with CMI; (2) a coincidental occurrence of burning vertex syndrome; or (3) an independent primary headache disorder. Notably, comprehensive evaluation of patterns of cutaneous allodynia in individuals with CMI is lacking, underscored by the fact that no specific assessment tool for these symptoms was available in this population before the introduction of the Chiari Symptom Profile. Considering the current limited data, we share this observation as a noteworthy clinical finding rather than proposing it as a conclusive diagnostic criterion.

This case underscores the considerable difficulty in diagnosing CMI when it manifests with unusual or deceptive features like central hypoventilation and choking, which resulted in a 14-year lapse before the diagnosis was made. The process of misdiagnosis followed by eventual identification of the neurological condition is commonly reported in previous studies.
