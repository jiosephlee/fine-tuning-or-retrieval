Title: Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting

Margret Ann Schneider, Hans de Groot, Jan-Willem Geerts

Abstract:

Summary: Anaphylaxis represents the most dangerous manifestation of allergic reactions and constitutes a systemic event that demands immediate and effective intervention. In certain instances, symptoms reappear after appropriate treatment and full initial recovery. These biphasic or multiphasic patterns pose specific difficulties for practitioners, as documented rates of occurrence are inconsistent, associated risk factors remain poorly defined, and established protocols for management are absent from current guidelines. This concern is heightened in cases of multiphasic anaphylaxis, in which repeated episodes of anaphylaxis occur following satisfactory responses to standard epinephrine therapy. Given the infrequency of multiphasic anaphylaxis, published case reports describing the clinical approach serve as valuable resources for understanding this issue. This report details the presentation of multiphasic anaphylaxis to clarithromycin in a female patient in her 60s, with further evaluation suggesting a high probability of systemic mastocytosis, though the patient did not fulfill the formal diagnostic requirements.

Background:

Anaphylaxis constitutes a serious and—in cases of anaphylactic shock—potentially fatal hypersensitivity reaction, frequently prompting visits to the emergency department. Nevertheless, certain patients experience a biphasic or even multiphasic pattern, marked by an initial episode of anaphylaxis triggered by exposure to an allergen, a subsequent phase with resolution of symptoms, and later reappearance of manifestations despite the absence of renewed exposure to the allergen. Typically, this biphasic phenomenon is observed within 24 hours following the initial exposure, although instances of symptom recurrence as late as 78 hours afterward have been documented. Estimates of biphasic anaphylaxis incidence are highly variable, ranging from 0.4% up to 23.3%, although the majority of evidence supports a rate between 4% and 6%. Given the possibility of symptom relapse, it is common practice to recommend monitoring the patient for a period after apparent resolution of anaphylactic signs. Despite this, the criteria defining biphasic anaphylaxis can differ and management recommendations are not uniform, with recent debate regarding the utility of corticosteroids. Multiphasic anaphylaxis, characterized by repeated recurrence of symptoms, introduces additional complexity to treatment strategies and observation guidelines. The following report describes the case of a woman in her sixties who developed a multiphasic anaphylactic reaction after exposure to clarithromycin.

Case presentation:

A woman in her sixties, who had a documented beta-lactam antibiotic allergy, arrived at the emergency department (ED) exhibiting anaphylactic symptoms subsequent to ingesting oral clarithromycin 500 mg.

Her medical background comprised type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, psoriasis, and depression. She weighed 104 kg. Her current medications included metformin (500 mg twice daily), gliclazide retard (80 mg once daily), hydrochlorothiazide (25 mg once daily), omeprazole (20 mg once daily), and sertraline (100 mg once daily). She had taken her most recent dose of hydrochlorothiazide that morning, more than 12 hours prior to her arrival at the ED.

During adolescence, she experienced a mild allergic response to amoxicillin characterized solely by facial swelling, without involvement of oropharyngeal edema, rash, hypotension, or respiratory distress. She had also previously sustained a pronounced localized reaction to a wasp sting, which was not accompanied by systemic manifestations. The current episode marked her first occurrence of anaphylaxis.

Five days prior to presenting to the ED, her general practitioner prescribed clindamycin for erysipelas. After five days of treatment, she developed an unspecified rash on her abdomen. This was interpreted as a delayed allergic reaction to clindamycin; consequently, her general practitioner changed her antibiotic therapy to clarithromycin, a drug she had not taken previously.

She later arrived at the emergency department complaining of itchiness, facial flushing, increased sweating, agitation, and a sensation of throat swelling. These complaints began approximately 1 hour following ingestion of her first 500 mg dose of oral clarithromycin and intensified over time. She presented for medical evaluation 1 hour after her symptoms initially appeared. Upon arrival at the emergency department, her vital signs revealed low blood pressure (non-invasive measurement: 74/50 mm Hg), low peripheral oxygen saturation (88% on room air), and a respiratory rate of 22 breaths per minute. Initial examination identified swelling of the face, new onset of widespread erythema and urticaria affecting the whole body, along with excessive sweating and agitation. There was no evidence of tongue or throat swelling, and cardiovascular and pulmonary examinations were unremarkable. Laboratory assessment showed a blood glucose of 12.7 mmol/L, and ECG demonstrated sinus rhythm. Due to suspected severe allergic reaction, she promptly received intramuscular epinephrine (0.3 mg via auto-injector) and 2 mg of clemastine intravenously at a slow rate, in accordance with local guidelines for treating cutaneous manifestations. In addition, she was given supplemental oxygen (15 L/min via non-rebreather mask) and a 500 mL infusion of 0.9% sodium chloride for initial stabilization. After her condition improved with epinephrine administration, oxygen supplementation was discontinued, but it had to be reintroduced transiently when further episodes of anaphylaxis occurred. Clarithromycin therapy was halted and no additional doses were administered beyond the first 500 mg oral dose.

An initial favorable response to therapy was noted, with normalization of blood pressure and reduction of facial edema and erythema. The patient was transferred to the acute internal ward for heightened observation by specialized nursing staff, although no telemetry monitoring was utilized. Despite early improvement, within the first 12 hours of hospitalization the patient began experiencing repeated episodes consistent with anaphylactic shock. After the initial recurrence, the patient was moved to the intensive care unit (ICU) for closer surveillance. Each episode necessitated intramuscular epinephrine administration for resolution (figure 1); in total, there were four anaphylactic events, including the primary presentation in the ED. The first two administrations utilized auto-injectors containing 0.3 mg epinephrine intramuscularly, while the subsequent two doses were manually prepared at 0.5 mg epinephrine intramuscularly. Although auto-injectors at our facility deliver less than the recommended 0.5 mg, they are preferred in urgent scenarios—such as initial patient stabilization in the ED—or in contexts where anaphylactic emergencies are uncommon and hesitation over unfamiliar medication may delay treatment. After the first anaphylaxis recurrence, corticosteroid therapy was added due to their customary use in prevention of biphasic anaphylactic reactions. The patient received intravenous prednisone 30 mg daily for five days, along with a single intravenous dose of 8 mg dexamethasone, reflecting differing protocols between general ward and ICU management for anaphylaxis in our institution. Additionally, clemastine 2 mg was administered intravenously on two occasions to address cutaneous manifestations, and 500 mL of normal saline was given during the second episode of anaphylaxis. Notably, the severity of anaphylactic symptoms declined progressively, ranging from profound hypoxemia with cyanosis, hypotension, and severe dyspnea in the initial episode to dyspnea, recurring rash, and pruritus in the final event.

Early consultation with the allergology department was undertaken to obtain specialized therapeutic recommendations. Two possible strategies were evaluated: firstly, continuous intravenous infusion of epinephrine to preempt anaphylactic events; secondly, an expectant approach based on the apparent gradual attenuation of symptom severity, allowing observation for spontaneous resolution. Both methods carried considerable risks, particularly given the absence of guidance from the literature regarding management in multiphasic anaphylactic presentations.

Outcome and follow-up:

In the end, neither therapeutic strategy was required since the patient experienced no additional recurrence of symptoms thereafter. Following approximately 24 hours free of new anaphylactic episodes, she was moved back to a general medical ward. She was discharged from the hospital after a total of 3 days. Upon discharge, she began daily therapy with levocetirizine, an antihistamine, and was provided with an epinephrine auto-injector (0.3 mg) along with instructions on its use. There have been no subsequent anaphylactic events since that time.

Given the unusual and extended progression of her illness, further investigations into potential underlying etiologies were initiated. This evaluation identified a significant likelihood of systemic mastocytosis; however, the diagnostic criteria were not fully met (the patient exhibited two WHO minor criteria but the major criterion could not be evaluated due to insufficient bone marrow biopsy sample, refer to figure 2). Elevated tryptase concentrations were documented both during her third anaphylactic reaction and at baseline, with persistently increased levels during follow-up under the care of an allergologist, as indicated in table 1.

Tryptase measurements were omitted during the initial assessment of the early anaphylactic events, resulting in noncompliance with the guideline-recommended sampling schedule. Standard practice advises obtaining at least two, and preferably three, tryptase samples at designated time points. The protocol calls for the first sample to be drawn as soon as possible without delaying therapy, a second sample to be collected 1–2 hours (but no later than 4 hours) after symptom onset—when tryptase levels typically reach their maximum—and a third, baseline sample at least 24 hours after complete resolution of clinical signs. In this report, the first tryptase sample was obtained one hour following the third occurrence of anaphylaxis, and the subsequent sample was drawn 27 hours after the most recent anaphylactic event.

No confirmatory allergy testing was carried out due to concerns that procedures such as skin tests could provoke another severe anaphylactic reaction, which was considered disproportionate since additional exposures were easily avoidable. Additional findings supporting a diagnosis of systemic mastocytosis comprised hepatomegaly detected on abdominal ultrasound and possible neuropsychiatric manifestations, including depression and impaired memory.

Discussion:

Anaphylaxis is generally recognized as a distinct clinical syndrome, marked by abrupt onset and rapid escalation of symptoms affecting the airway, respiratory function, cardiovascular system, and/or gastrointestinal tract, and usually accompanied by skin or mucosal findings such as urticaria, angioedema, or flushing. While identifying an inciting allergen can strengthen the diagnosis, pinpointing a definite trigger is not always feasible.

More complicated cases, including biphasic and refractory anaphylaxis, lack precise definitions, although recent initiatives have sought to develop consensus terminology for these presentations. The Resuscitation Council UK describes biphasic anaphylaxis as the recurrence of symptoms hours after initial symptom resolution without renewed exposure to the allergen. Distinguishing genuine biphasic events from prolonged anaphylactic episodes that transiently respond to epinephrine, or those involving ongoing allergen absorption (such as from residual ingested food or extended-release pharmaceuticals), remains problematic. Refractory anaphylaxis, on the other hand, denotes continued anaphylaxis despite correct administration of epinephrine with absent or inadequate response.

Clinical guidelines on biphasic anaphylaxis focus predominantly on patient monitoring, yet there is still no consensus on the ideal duration of observation. Common recommendations suggest observation periods of 4 to 6 hours following symptom resolution. The Nederlands Huisartsen Genootschap (NHG) and the World Allergy Organisation guidelines indicate that about half of biphasic episodes develop within 6–12 hours of the initial reaction, but neither issues firm directives regarding monitoring length. The UK guideline published in 2021 offers an alternative with a risk-based, three-tiered monitoring framework. Standardized strategies are still lacking and are necessary to ensure a balance between the efficient use of healthcare resources for the majority with uncomplicated anaphylaxis, and the safety considerations pertinent to the smaller, harder-to-predict subgroup prone to biphasic responses.

There is a significant and concerning absence of specific guidance regarding advanced pharmacologic approaches for managing biphasic and multiphasic anaphylactic reactions. Treatment for these presentations generally aligns with conventional anaphylaxis protocols, involving rapid administration of intramuscular epinephrine along with appropriate supportive interventions. Clinical guidelines suggest a dosing regimen of epinephrine at 0.01 mg/kg body weight, with an upper limit of 0.5 mg. For adults, this recommendation is commonly rounded to a 0.5 mg dose, since most adults have a weight exceeding 50 kg. Notably, many commercially available auto-injectors—including those administered during the patient’s initial two anaphylactic events—are supplied with only 0.3 mg epinephrine, even in formulations designated for adult patients. Although epinephrine auto-injectors are indispensable for pre-hospital use, they can also provide value in clinical environments by reducing delays in administration and lessening the reluctance of healthcare staff less familiar with using vasoactive substances like epinephrine. Nevertheless, use of a 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injector in adults weighing more than 50 kg, while frequently encountered, may result in substantial underdosing. A retrospective analysis from 2025 identified that individuals initially managed with 0.3 mg of epinephrine required advanced interventions, such as subsequent epinephrine doses, at a significantly higher rate compared to those first treated with the 0.5 mg dose. Addressing obstacles to the recommended dosing—such as provider habituation to the historical 0.3 mg dose and widespread access to 0.3 mg auto-injectors—and promoting compliance with guideline-directed epinephrine administration could substantially enhance therapeutic outcomes in anaphylaxis.

Historically, corticosteroids were advocated in anaphylaxis management, partly to reduce the risk of biphasic reactions. Nonetheless, increasing data now highlight not only a lack of therapeutic benefit but also possible adverse effects, resulting in a recent trend away from routine steroid use for anaphylaxis. A 2017 systematic review of 22 studies found support for corticosteroid use in just one study, while the other 21 studies showed no reduction in biphasic anaphylaxis risk. Additionally, current studies highlight the importance of corticosteroid-related side effects, with a 2019 study reporting higher rates of ICU or hospital ward admission following prehospital corticosteroid administration. As a result, most modern guidelines recommend against regular corticosteroid use, although they continue to be used in practice.

Biphasic anaphylaxis remains an infrequent but clinically significant complication. The lack of consistent predictive factors complicates both risk assessment and decision-making for surveillance. Proposed risk factors include delayed epinephrine use, severe initial symptoms, the need for multiple doses of epinephrine, and a history of biphasic reactions. Still, reliable predictors to guide selective observation are currently unavailable.

Systemic mastocytosis is characterized by abnormal mast cell proliferation, excessive mediator release, and widespread organ inflammation, which increases susceptibility to severe anaphylactic events.1517 Yet, due to the uncommon nature of both mastocytosis and biphasic anaphylaxis, there is presently no published research specifically examining their relationship. Ongoing investigation into mast cell diseases and biphasic anaphylaxis has the potential to inform future therapeutic strategies.

Anaphylaxis-related mortality remains low, particularly in areas where rapid emergency response is available. Outcomes resulting in death from biphasic anaphylaxis are even more uncommon, despite the seemingly high-risk nature of these reactions. Nonetheless, anaphylaxis is still a frequent acute medical condition that requires immediate intervention and thorough observation. Although much research and many guidelines emphasize initial emergency treatment, continued medical supervision following the event is equally crucial. Enhanced knowledge of the progression of complicated anaphylaxis presentations, such as biphasic episodes, could facilitate the development of more individualized, patient-focused, and resource-conserving management protocols.

Because biphasic and especially multiphasic anaphylaxis occur infrequently, and due to the inherent difficulties of conducting research in acute emergencies, establishing robust evidence-based management strategies remains difficult. In these circumstances, expert consensus guidelines that offer specific management recommendations are highly useful; yet, formulating these recommendations is often hindered by limited available data. Given the scarcity of such complex cases, assembling a registry that gathers detailed patient information, potential management approaches—as demonstrated in our case report—and outcome measures, may serve as a foundational resource for creating data-informed, expert-driven guidelines.
