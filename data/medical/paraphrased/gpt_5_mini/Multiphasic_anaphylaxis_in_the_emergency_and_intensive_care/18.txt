Title: Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting

Margret Ann Schneider, Hans de Groot, Jan-Willem Geerts

Abstract:

Summary: Anaphylaxis represents the most serious form of allergic reaction, characterized by a potentially fatal systemic response that necessitates immediate and effective intervention. In some patients, symptoms can recur after apparent resolution and appropriate treatment, a phenomenon known as biphasic or even multiphasic anaphylaxis. These relapsing patterns pose particular challenges for healthcare providers, as their reported frequency is inconsistent, contributory risk factors remain uncertain, and established management protocols are not well delineated in existing guidelines. This is especially problematic in instances of multiphasic anaphylaxis, where individuals experience repeated anaphylactic episodes despite showing an initial favorable response to conventional epinephrine therapy. Owing to the infrequency of multiphasic anaphylaxis, published case reports serve as valuable references for clinical practice. Here, we describe a case involving a woman in her 60s who experienced multiphasic anaphylaxis following administration of clarithromycin; subsequent investigation suggested a high probability of underlying systemic mastocytosis, although she did not fulfill the formal diagnostic criteria for this condition.

Background:

Anaphylaxis is a severe allergic response that can be fatal, particularly in cases of anaphylactic shock, and frequently necessitates emergency medical intervention. While many cases resolve after initial management, a subset experiences biphasic or even multiphasic patterns. These patterns are defined by an initial anaphylactic episode upon exposure to an allergen, a symptom-free interval, and then a recurrence of symptoms without any further contact with the causative agent. Such recurrent reactions most often occur within 24 hours of the initial event, but there are documented cases of symptom return as late as 78 hours following exposure. Estimates of the frequency of biphasic anaphylaxis differ significantly, with reports ranging from 0.4% to 23.3%; however, the majority of evidence places incidence between 4% and 6%. Because relapse is possible, it is generally recommended that patients be observed after their symptoms resolve. Despite this, consensus on the definition of biphasic anaphylaxis is lacking, treatment recommendations are inconsistent, and recent years have seen the use of corticosteroids questioned. Multiphasic anaphylaxis, which involves several recurrences of symptoms, poses particular challenges concerning management and monitoring. In this report, we describe the case of a woman in her 60s who experienced a multiphasic anaphylactic response to clarithromycin.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 60s, with a known allergy to beta-lactam antibiotics, presented to the emergency department (ED) with anaphylaxis following oral clarithromycin 500 mg.

The patient’s past medical history was notable for type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, psoriasis, and depression. Her body weight was 104 kg. Her regular medications included metformin 500 mg twice daily, gliclazide 80 mg sustained-release once daily, hydrochlorothiazide 25 mg daily, omeprazole 20 mg daily, and sertraline 100 mg daily. The most recent dose of hydrochlorothiazide had been ingested that morning, over 12 hours prior to her arrival at the emergency department.

In adolescence, the patient had experienced a mild allergic response to amoxicillin characterized by facial swelling, but without involvement of the oropharynx, skin rash, hypotension, or respiratory symptoms. Additionally, she had once had a significant localized skin reaction to a wasp sting, but this had not been accompanied by systemic manifestations. The clinical episode described below marked her initial occurrence of anaphylaxis.

Five days prior to visiting the emergency department, the patient’s general practitioner started her on clindamycin for erysipelas. After five days of therapy, she developed an abdominal rash (with no further description provided), which was considered to be a delayed allergic response to clindamycin. Consequently, her general practitioner changed her antibiotic regimen to clarithromycin, a medication she had not taken previously.

She later arrived at the emergency department (ED) complaining of itching, flushing, increased sweating, restlessness, and a sensation of throat swelling. These symptoms began approximately one hour following ingestion of her initial oral dose of clarithromycin and progressively intensified. She presented for medical evaluation one hour after her symptoms first appeared. Upon arrival at the ED, her vital signs revealed low blood pressure and low oxygen saturation (non-invasive blood pressure measured at 74/50 mm Hg, oxygen saturation of 88% on room air, respiratory rate 22 breaths per minute). Physical examination identified facial swelling, a newly developed generalized erythema and urticaria affecting her whole body, heightened sweating, and agitation. There was no evidence of tongue or throat swelling, and both cardiac and pulmonary examinations were unremarkable. Her blood glucose level was measured at 12.7 mmol/L, and electrocardiogram (ECG) showed sinus rhythm. Immediate management for a presumed severe allergic reaction was initiated, including an intramuscular injection of 0.3 mg epinephrine via auto-injector and a slow intravenous dose of 2 mg clemastine, administered according to local protocols for skin reactions. She was also given supplemental oxygen at 15 liters per minute through a non-rebreather mask, and 500 mL of normal saline (0.9% sodium chloride) was administered for initial stabilization. Oxygen therapy could be discontinued following a favorable clinical response to epinephrine, but had to be temporarily restarted in the course of additional anaphylactic events. Clarithromycin was stopped, and no further doses were taken following the initial single oral 500 mg dose.

Initially, the patient responded well to treatment, with normalization of blood pressure and reductions in both facial swelling and erythema. She was admitted to the acute internal medicine ward, which allows for enhanced monitoring by specialized nursing staff, but lacks telemetry. Despite the favorable early response, the patient experienced multiple recurrences of symptoms consistent with anaphylactic shock during the first 12 hours post-admission. Following the initial recurrence of anaphylaxis, she was transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) for more comprehensive monitoring. Each anaphylactic episode was managed with intramuscular epinephrine administration (see figure 1), resulting in four total episodes, including her original presentation in the emergency department (ED). The first two doses of epinephrine, 0.3 mg each, were delivered via auto-injector, while the third and fourth episodes were treated with manually prepared 0.5 mg intramuscular doses. Although the auto-injectors available in the facility contain a lower dose than the guideline-recommended 0.5 mg, they are the preferred method in situations requiring rapid intervention, such as the initial stabilization in the ED, or in clinical settings where staff may have less routine experience treating anaphylaxis and treatment hesitation could delay critical therapy. 

Following the first recurrence, adjunctive corticosteroid therapy was initiated, in line with the conventional use of corticosteroids for potential prevention of biphasic anaphylaxis. The regimen included daily intravenous prednisone at a dose of 30 mg for five days, as well as a single 8 mg dose of intravenous dexamethasone, reflecting discrepancies between the anaphylaxis treatment protocols in the hospital’s general and intensive care wards. Additionally, the patient received intravenous clemastine 2 mg on two occasions for cutaneous manifestations, and was administered 500 mL of normal saline during the second anaphylactic episode. Notably, each subsequent episode showed diminished symptom severity, with initial events involving profound hypoxaemia, cyanosis, hypotension, and severe dyspnoea, progressing to milder symptoms such as dyspnoea, rash recurrence, and pruritus during the final episode.

An early consultation with the allergology department was obtained for specialized guidance. Two possible management strategies were discussed: instituting a continuous intravenous epinephrine infusion to prevent additional anaphylactic episodes proactively, or pursuing an observational approach to determine if symptoms would abate on their own, particularly given the apparent trend of decreasing severity. Each strategy was associated with significant risks, exacerbated by the absence of robust literature to guide treatment in cases of multiphasic anaphylaxis.

Outcome and follow-up:

In the end, neither therapeutic intervention was required, as the patient experienced no additional recurrence of symptoms thereafter. Roughly 24 hours after remaining free from anaphylactic episodes, she was moved to a general ward. Her hospital stay totaled 3 days before discharge. Upon discharge, she was prescribed levocetirizine, an antihistamine, to be taken regularly, and was instructed in the use of an epinephrine auto-injector (0.3 mg). She has not suffered any further anaphylactic events since then.

Given the unusual and extended course of her symptoms, further investigations were conducted to explore possible underlying etiologies. This workup suggested a significant likelihood of systemic mastocytosis, although the formal diagnostic criteria were not met (the patient fulfilled only two minor WHO criteria, with major criteria unassessable owing to insufficient bone marrow biopsy sample, see figure 2). Regrettably, tryptase concentrations were not assessed during the initial evaluation following the patient's first anaphylactic events, thus the established time points for tryptase assessment were not followed. Current guidelines advise collecting at least two, and preferably three, tryptase samples at designated times: the first as early as possible without delaying emergency management; the second at 1–2 hours (but within 4 hours) after symptom onset, coinciding with the expected peak in tryptase; and a third at least 24 hours post complete symptom resolution to ascertain the baseline level. In this reported case, the initial tryptase measurement was obtained one hour after the third episode of anaphylaxis, and the follow-up sample was collected 27 hours after the most recent anaphylactic event.

No allergen confirmation testing was pursued, as the potential for inducing another severe anaphylactic event through procedures like skin testing was considered disproportionate, especially given that future exposure could be readily prevented. Additional notable findings, consistent with systemic mastocytosis, included hepatomegaly detected on abdominal ultrasound, as well as potential neuropsychiatric manifestations such as depression and memory impairment.

Discussion:

Anaphylaxis is generally recognized as a distinct clinical entity, marked by abrupt onset and quick escalation of symptoms affecting the airway, respiratory function, circulation, and/or gastrointestinal tract, commonly with associated skin or mucosal findings like urticaria, angioedema, or flushing. Although identifying a probable allergen lends support to the diagnosis, determining the precise inciting agent is not always feasible.

More atypical forms, such as biphasic or refractory anaphylaxis, lack as clear diagnostic criteria, though recent initiatives have sought to develop standardized definitions. According to the Resuscitation Council UK, biphasic anaphylaxis describes the return of symptoms several hours after their initial resolution, without new allergen exposure. Discriminating between truly biphasic events and persistent anaphylaxis with a temporary response to epinephrine or continuing allergen absorption (such as from residual ingested food or slow-release drug formulations) is often difficult. Refractory anaphylaxis, on the other hand, is defined by unrelenting symptoms that fail to respond adequately to properly administered epinephrine.

Clinical guidelines on biphasic anaphylaxis focus chiefly on recommendations for observation. However, the most appropriate length of observation after initial symptom resolution is still debated. Conventional advice has favored monitoring for 4 to 6 hours. The guidelines from the Nederlands Huisartsen Genootschap (NHG) and the World Allergy Organisation indicate that roughly half of biphasic reactions emerge within 6–12 hours after the original incident, though neither offers explicit direction on observation periods. In contrast, the 2021 UK guideline suggests a three-tier risk-based monitoring strategy. There is a clear need for consensus on monitoring protocols in order to reconcile efficient use of medical resources for the many patients with single anaphylactic episodes against the imperative to protect the smaller, high-risk population at risk of biphasic reactions.

There is a notable and concerning scarcity of guidance regarding advanced medication approaches for biphasic and multiphasic anaphylactic reactions. The treatment of these recurrent forms of anaphylaxis generally follows conventional anaphylaxis protocols, which involve rapid administration of intramuscular epinephrine alongside suitable supportive measures. Clinical guidelines advise an epinephrine dose of 0.01 mg/kg based on body weight, not to exceed a total of 0.5 mg. In practice, this dose is frequently rounded to 0.5 mg for adult patients, since most adults surpass 50 kg in weight. It is particularly notable, however, that many epinephrine auto-injectors—such as those deployed during the initial two anaphylactic events in the case discussed—supply only 0.3 mg of the drug per injection, even when designated for adults. While auto-injectors are indispensable in community or pre-hospital scenarios, they also have practical utility in healthcare environments by facilitating immediate administration and minimizing reluctance among providers unfamiliar with vasoactive agents like epinephrine. Nonetheless, administering a 0.3 mg auto-injector, which is a common practice, risks suboptimal dosing in adults weighing over 50 kg. Evidence from a 2025 retrospective analysis indicated that individuals initially treated with 0.3 mg of epinephrine required escalated interventions, such as additional epinephrine doses, much more frequently than those who first received 0.5 mg. Addressing factors such as the ingrained preference for the traditional 0.3 mg dose, as well as the prevalent availability of 0.3 mg auto-injectors, and striving for closer adherence to established dosing recommendations, may be vital steps toward optimizing anaphylaxis care.

Historically, corticosteroids were advised in part to lower the risk of biphasic reactions. Yet, accumulating data indicate not only a lack of benefit but also potential harm, prompting a move away from steroid use in anaphylaxis. A systematic review conducted in 2017 evaluated 22 studies, with only a single study supporting corticosteroid administration and 21 studies finding no evidence that corticosteroids decrease the incidence of biphasic anaphylaxis. Additionally, recent research highlights the importance of adverse effects, including a 2019 study that identified a higher rate of ICU or hospital ward admissions following prehospital corticosteroid use. As a result, current guidelines generally recommend against the standard use of corticosteroids in anaphylaxis, though their use in actual practice continues.

Although biphasic anaphylaxis occurs infrequently, it is of substantial clinical concern. The lack of dependable predictors makes it challenging to assess risk and determine appropriate observation strategies. Potential predictors that have been proposed include delayed epinephrine administration, severe symptoms at onset, the requirement for multiple epinephrine injections, and a history of previous biphasic episodes. Nevertheless, robust predictors to guide targeted monitoring have yet to be established.

Systemic mastocytosis—characterized by abnormal mast cell proliferation, increased mediator release, and multiorgan inflammatory responses—places affected patients at higher risk for severe anaphylaxis.1517 However, as both systemic mastocytosis and biphasic anaphylaxis are uncommon, there currently appears to be no research specifically investigating the association between systemic mastocytosis and biphasic anaphylactic reactions. Further studies examining mast cell disorders in the context of biphasic anaphylaxis could enhance future clinical management.

Anaphylaxis-related deaths are uncommon, particularly in areas where emergency medical services are readily available. Fatalities linked to biphasic anaphylaxis are even less frequent, despite the potential severity of these episodes. Nevertheless, anaphylaxis persists as a frequent acute medical condition that necessitates immediate intervention and thorough observation. While research and established guidelines have traditionally emphasized the importance of initial emergency response, follow-up care and ongoing assessment are equally significant. Gaining more insight into intricate presentations of anaphylaxis, such as biphasic reactions, may enable the development of more precise, patient-focused, and efficient management strategies.

Due to the infrequency of biphasic and especially multiphasic anaphylaxis, along with the challenges of conducting research in acute emergency settings, formulating evidence-based management protocols remains difficult. In such scenarios, expert consensus guidelines that offer clear recommendations are especially useful; still, the absence of sufficient data complicates the formulation of expert-driven advice. Given how seldom these complex cases occur, the creation of a dedicated case registry—collecting detailed patient information, proposed management approaches as highlighted in our report, and outcome data—could serve as a foundational resource for forming data-supported, expert-informed recommendations.
