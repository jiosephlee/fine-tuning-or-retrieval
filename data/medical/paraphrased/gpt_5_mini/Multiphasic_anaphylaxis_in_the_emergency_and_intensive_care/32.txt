Title: Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting

Margret Ann Schneider, Hans de Groot, Jan-Willem Geerts

Abstract:

Summary: Anaphylaxis represents the most acute manifestation of allergic reactions and constitutes a medical emergency necessitating rapid and decisive intervention. In a subset of patients, symptoms may recur after initial resolution, despite receiving proper treatment and demonstrating an initial full response. Such biphasic or even multiphasic patterns present particular difficulties for healthcare providers, as incidence rates are inconsistently reported, risk factors are not well established, and contemporary management guidelines remain incomplete. This is particularly challenging in multiphasic anaphylaxis, where individuals experience repeated episodes despite successful administration of epinephrine as recommended. Because multiphasic anaphylaxis is relatively uncommon, individual case reports detailing management approaches can offer valuable perspectives on this issue. Here, we describe the case of a woman in her sixth decade who experienced multiphasic anaphylaxis following exposure to clarithromycin; further evaluation indicated a strong suspicion for systemic mastocytosis, although formal diagnostic criteria were not satisfied.

Background:

Anaphylaxis constitutes a severe allergic reaction that can be fatal, particularly in cases of anaphylactic shock, and frequently leads patients to seek emergency medical attention. Certain cases demonstrate a biphasic or even multiphasic progression, where the patient experiences an initial anaphylactic episode triggered by allergen exposure, followed by a symptom-free interval, and subsequently a recurrence of symptoms despite no further exposure to the allergen. Recurrence of symptoms is most commonly reported within the first 24 hours following the initial allergen contact, though instances of relapse up to 78 hours later have been documented. The occurrence of biphasic anaphylaxis is reported inconsistently in the literature, with rates ranging from 0.4% to 23.3%; most evidence indicates an incidence between 4% and 6%. In light of the potential for symptom relapse, observation after resolution of anaphylactic symptoms is generally recommended. Nevertheless, definitions of biphasic anaphylaxis remain variable, and management protocols differ, particularly regarding the use of corticosteroids, which has recently become a topic of debate. Multiphasic anaphylaxis, with several episodes of symptom recurrence, poses specific challenges for treatment and monitoring approaches. Here, we describe the case of a female patient in her sixties who experienced multiphasic anaphylaxis in response to clarithromycin.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 60s, known to be allergic to beta-lactam antibiotics, arrived at the emergency department (ED) experiencing anaphylaxis after ingesting oral clarithromycin 500 mg.

Her medical background included type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, psoriasis, and depression. She weighed 104 kg. Her current medications comprised metformin (500 mg twice daily), gliclazide (80 mg extended-release once daily), hydrochlorothiazide (25 mg once daily), omeprazole (20 mg once daily), and sertraline (100 mg once daily). The final dose of hydrochlorothiazide was taken that morning, more than 12 hours prior to ED arrival.

Previously, as an adolescent, she experienced a mild allergic response to amoxicillin characterized solely by facial swelling, without involvement of the oropharynx, rash, hypotension, or dyspnea. Additionally, she had shown a significant localized skin reaction to a wasp sting, but did not manifest any systemic effects. This episode marked her first experience with anaphylaxis.

Five days before presenting to the ED, her general practitioner prescribed clindamycin to treat erysipelas. After five days of clindamycin administration, she developed a rash on her abdomen (details not provided), which was considered a delayed allergic response to clindamycin. Consequently, her physician changed her antibiotic therapy to clarithromycin, a drug she had not received previously.

She later arrived at the emergency department reporting pruritus, flushing, excessive sweating, restlessness, and a sensation of throat swelling. These symptoms commenced approximately one hour following ingestion of her first oral clarithromycin dose, and gradually intensified. She presented for medical assessment one hour after initial symptom onset. Upon arrival, her vital parameters revealed low blood pressure and reduced oxygen saturation (non-invasive blood pressure: 74/50 mm Hg, peripheral oxygen saturation 88% on room air, respiratory rate 22 breaths per minute). Physical examination identified facial edema, widespread new erythema, urticaria involving the entire body, heightened sweating, and agitation. No enlargement of the tongue or pharynx was noted, and both cardiac and pulmonary auscultation were unremarkable. Her blood glucose measured 12.7 mmol/L, and her electrocardiogram demonstrated sinus rhythm. She promptly received treatment for a suspected severe allergic reaction, including a 0.3 mg intramuscular epinephrine auto-injector and a slow intravenous dose of 2 mg clemastine, per local protocol for cutaneous symptoms. She was also given supplemental oxygen (15 L via non-rebreather mask) and 500 mL normal saline (0.9% sodium chloride solution) during initial management. Oxygen supplementation was discontinued after clinical improvement with epinephrine but was intermittently required again during subsequent anaphylactic episodes. Clarithromycin was stopped, and she did not receive any further doses beyond the initial 500 mg oral administration.

Initially, the therapy yielded positive results, as blood pressure stabilized and facial edema along with erythema improved. The patient was transferred to the acute internal medicine ward, which offers increased surveillance by specialized nursing staff, though it does not provide telemetry monitoring. Nevertheless, despite the favorable initial response, the patient soon experienced repeated episodes with features consistent with anaphylactic shock during the first 12 hours following admission. After the first recurrence of anaphylactic shock, the patient was moved to the intensive care unit (ICU) for enhanced monitoring. Management of each episode involved intramuscular epinephrine administration (figure 1), with a total of four anaphylactic episodes including the original emergency department (ED) presentation. For the first two episodes, treatment consisted of auto-injectors delivering 0.3 mg of epinephrine intramuscularly, while the subsequent two episodes were managed with manually prepared doses of 0.5 mg epinephrine IM. The epinephrine auto-injectors available at this clinic deliver a lower dose than the recommended 0.5 mg, yet they are favored in urgent circumstances, such as during initial stabilization in the ED, or in situations where emergency interventions for anaphylaxis are uncommon and uncertainty regarding medication might cause delay. Following the initial recurrence of anaphylaxis, corticosteroid therapy was added, reflecting the conventional use of steroids to prevent biphasic anaphylaxis. The patient received intravenous prednisone at a daily dose of 30 mg for five days and a single intravenous dose of 8 mg dexamethasone, as per varying recommendations outlined in the hospital's ward and ICU protocols for anaphylaxis management. In addition, two intravenous doses of 2 mg clemastine were administered to address cutaneous manifestations, and 500 mL of normal saline was given during the second anaphylactic episode. Notably, the severity of symptoms diminished with each successive episode, from profound hypoxemia, cyanosis, hypotension, and severe dyspnea in the initial event to dyspnea with recurrent rash and pruritus during the last episode.

An early consultation with the allergology department was obtained for specialized management recommendations. Two alternative treatment strategies were evaluated: the first being continuous intravenous epinephrine infusion via pump to proactively address anaphylaxis, and the second being a watchful approach to determine if symptoms would naturally subside, given the gradually decreasing severity. Both options entailed considerable risks, especially as current literature provides limited guidance for the management of multiphasic anaphylaxis.

Outcome and follow-up:

In the end, neither intervention was required because the patient did not experience additional recurrence of symptoms. After remaining free of anaphylaxis for roughly 24 hours, she was moved to a regular hospital unit. She was discharged from the hospital following a total of 3 days of admission. Upon discharge, levocetirizine, an antihistamine, was initiated as part of her ongoing medication regimen, and she was provided with an epinephrine auto-injector (0.3 mg) along with training on its proper use. No new anaphylactic incidents have been reported since then.

Given the unusual and extended clinical presentation, an evaluation for possible underlying etiologies was undertaken. This assessment indicated a significant likelihood of systemic mastocytosis, though criteria for a definitive diagnosis were not fully met (only two of the WHO minor criteria were present, with major criteria unassessable due to insufficient bone marrow biopsy material; refer to figure 2). Tryptase concentrations measured during the third episode of anaphylaxis, as well as baseline levels, were found to be elevated and continued to remain high during subsequent allergy specialist follow-up, as presented in table 1.

Regrettably, serum tryptase measurements were not obtained during the initial assessment of the patient's first anaphylactic reactions, resulting in noncompliance with the recommended timing for tryptase evaluation. According to established guidelines, at least two, and preferably three, tryptase samples should be collected at prescribed intervals: the first as promptly as possible without delaying management, the second between 1–2 hours after symptom onset (but within a 4 hour window), coinciding with anticipated peak levels, and a third at least 24 hours post full symptom resolution to determine the patient's baseline tryptase concentration. In this case, the initial tryptase was obtained 1 hour following the third episode of anaphylaxis, and the subsequent measurement was collected 27 hours after the final event.

No confirmatory allergy testing was pursued, as the potential risk of precipitating further severe anaphylaxis through methods such as skin testing was considered disproportionate, especially since the implicated trigger could be relatively easily avoided. Additional notable findings compatible with systemic mastocytosis were detected, including hepatomegaly on abdominal ultrasonography, as well as possible neuropsychiatric manifestations such as depression and memory impairment.

Discussion:

Anaphylaxis is generally recognized as a distinct clinical diagnosis, marked by an abrupt onset and rapid escalation of symptoms affecting the airway, respiratory function, circulation, and/or gastrointestinal tract, often accompanied by skin or mucosal findings such as urticaria, angioedema, or flushing. Although establishing a probable allergen can strengthen the diagnosis, pinpointing an exact cause is not always feasible.

More complicated manifestations, including biphasic or refractory anaphylaxis, are not as well characterized, though there have been recent attempts to reach consensus definitions. According to the Resuscitation Council UK, biphasic anaphylaxis is defined as a resurgence of symptoms hours after apparent recovery, without further exposure to the allergen. It may be difficult to distinguish genuine biphasic episodes from prolonged anaphylaxis that temporarily responds to epinephrine or from continuous allergen uptake—such as from retained food or slow-release medication. Refractory anaphylaxis describes persistent symptoms that do not resolve or only minimally respond to appropriately administered epinephrine.

Guidelines addressing biphasic anaphylaxis chiefly focus on recommendations for clinical observation. Nevertheless, there is ongoing debate regarding the ideal duration of monitoring. Traditional practice recommends observation for 4 to 6 hours after symptoms subside. The Dutch Nederlands Huisartsen Genootschap (NHG) and World Allergy Organisation guidelines report that nearly 50% of biphasic events manifest within 6–12 hours post initial reaction, yet neither suggests a specific monitoring timeframe. Conversely, the UK guideline from 2021 introduces a risk-based, three-tiered strategy for patient observation. There is an evident need for unified monitoring protocols that appropriately manage healthcare resources for patients with uncomplicated, single-episode anaphylaxis while ensuring safety for those at risk of rare, but unpredictable, biphasic reactions.

There is a significant and concerning absence of detailed guidance on advanced pharmacological approaches for biphasic and multiphasic anaphylactic events. The therapeutic management of such cases generally parallels standard protocols for anaphylaxis, involving rapid intramuscular administration of epinephrine alongside suitable supportive interventions. Current guidelines advise an epinephrine dose of 0.01 mg/kg body weight, not to exceed a maximum of 0.5 mg. For adults, this often translates into a simplified dose of 0.5 mg, since most adults weigh more than 50 kg. Of note, many epinephrine auto-injectors—such as those utilized for the patient’s initial two anaphylactic episodes described in this report—deliver only 0.3 mg, even for adult patients. While these auto-injectors are indispensable in prehospital scenarios and may even be advantageous in healthcare environments by minimizing delays in administration and reducing hesitancy among staff unfamiliar with vasoactive medications, administration of only 0.3 mg may result in significant underdosing for adults above 50 kg. A retrospective analysis conducted in 2025 found that individuals who initially received 0.3 mg epinephrine required escalation of therapy, including additional epinephrine doses, more frequently than those who began with the 0.5 mg dose. Overcoming obstacles such as clinician reliance on the traditionally used 0.3 mg dose and the widespread availability of 0.3 mg auto-injectors, and enhancing compliance with guideline-recommended epinephrine dosing, may substantially optimize outcomes in anaphylaxis treatment.

Historically, corticosteroids were advocated to help prevent biphasic reactions in anaphylaxis. Recent research, however, suggests not only a lack of efficacy but also possible adverse outcomes, prompting a move away from their routine use. A systematic review published in 2017 analyzed 22 studies: just one study found a benefit for corticosteroids, while 21 did not show any evidence that corticosteroids reduce biphasic anaphylaxis risk. Moreover, emerging data highlight the significance of corticosteroid-related side effects; notably, a 2019 study reported an increased rate of ICU or hospital ward admission following prehospital corticosteroid administration. As a result, most updated guidelines discourage habitual corticosteroid use, although they continue to be administered in practice.

Biphasic anaphylaxis occurs infrequently but holds substantial clinical importance. The lack of dependable predictors complicates the process of stratifying risk and selecting appropriate observation periods. Proposed predictors include late initiation of epinephrine, pronounced initial symptoms, need for repeated epinephrine administration, and prior history of biphasic reactions. Nonetheless, robust predictors to facilitate focused surveillance are currently unavailable.

Systemic mastocytosis is characterized by aberrant mast cell proliferation, heightened mediator release, and inflammation across multiple organs, increasing susceptibility to severe anaphylactic episodes.1517 Despite the uncommon nature of these conditions, no studies to date have specifically investigated the connection between systemic mastocytosis and biphasic anaphylaxis. Future research focusing on mast cell disorders and biphasic reactions could enhance understanding and inform subsequent management strategies.

Anaphylaxis-related mortality remains low, particularly in areas where rapid emergency care is accessible. Even though biphasic anaphylaxis may appear particularly hazardous, fatal cases are exceptionally uncommon. Despite these reassuring figures, anaphylaxis continues to constitute a frequent medical emergency requiring immediate intervention and careful observation. Much attention in research and clinical guidelines centers on initial emergency management; nevertheless, continued care and vigilance following the initial event are equally critical. Enhanced comprehension of intricate presentations, such as biphasic anaphylaxis, could facilitate the development of more individualized and resource-conscious management strategies.

Research into biphasic and especially multiphasic anaphylaxis is hampered by the low incidence of these events and by challenges associated with studying emergencies, making it difficult to establish evidence-based protocols. In these scenarios, consensus guidelines developed by experts play a crucial role, although their formulation is restricted by limited available data. Given the infrequency of such complex presentations, compiling a registry that collects relevant patient information, proposed management strategies (such as those described in our report), and outcome data could serve as a foundation for generating robust, data-informed expert recommendations.
