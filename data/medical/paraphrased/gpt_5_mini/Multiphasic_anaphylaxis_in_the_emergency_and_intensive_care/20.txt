Title: Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting

Margret Ann Schneider, Hans de Groot, Jan-Willem Geerts

Abstract:

Summary: Anaphylaxis represents the most severe type of allergic reaction and can be fatal if not addressed swiftly and appropriately. In certain patients, symptoms may reappear after resolution, even when initial management has been sufficient. These biphasic or multiphasic reactions pose significant challenges for healthcare providers, particularly since their reported frequency varies, associated risk factors remain uncertain, and established protocols for treatment are not well-defined in current guidelines. This challenge is heightened when multiphasic anaphylaxis manifests as repeated episodes despite proper epinephrine therapy. Given the uncommon nature of multiphasic anaphylaxis, published case reports serve as valuable resources to inform clinical approach. This report describes a case involving a female in her 60s experiencing multiphasic anaphylaxis following clarithromycin exposure, with further evaluation indicating a strong possibility of systemic mastocytosis, although she did not fulfill the official diagnostic criteria.

Background:

Anaphylaxis represents a severe, and in cases involving anaphylactic shock, potentially fatal allergic response commonly seen in emergency settings. A subset of patients experiences biphasic or even multiphasic manifestations, which are defined by an initial anaphylactic episode in response to a trigger, subsequent resolution of symptoms with a period free of complaints, and later, reappearance of symptoms absent renewed allergen exposure. This pattern typically develops within 24 hours following the initial exposure; however, instances of symptom return as late as 78 hours post-exposure have been documented. The reported frequency of biphasic anaphylaxis is highly variable, ranging between 0.4% and 23.3%, although the majority of studies indicate an incidence around 4–6%. Given the risk of symptom recurrence, observation after resolution of anaphylaxis is routinely recommended. Nevertheless, the criteria used to define biphasic anaphylaxis differ among sources, and management guidelines are not uniform, particularly concerning the effectiveness of corticosteroids, which has been questioned in recent years. Multiphasic anaphylaxis, featuring multiple recurrences of symptoms, presents specific challenges regarding both management and monitoring strategies. This report describes the case of a woman in her sixth decade who exhibited a multiphasic anaphylactic response to clarithromycin.

Case presentation:

A female in her sixth decade, who has a documented hypersensitivity to beta-lactam antibiotics, arrived at the emergency department (ED) experiencing anaphylaxis after ingesting clarithromycin 500 mg orally.

Her past medical history includes type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, psoriasis, and depression. She weighed 104 kg. Her current medications consisted of metformin 500 mg twice daily, gliclazide retard 80 mg once daily, hydrochlorothiazide 25 mg once daily, omeprazole 20 mg once daily, and sertraline 100 mg once daily. She had taken her last dose of hydrochlorothiazide more than 12 hours before arriving at the ED.

During her adolescence, the patient had experienced a mild allergic reaction to amoxicillin characterized solely by facial swelling, without involvement of the oropharynx, rash, hypotension, or dyspnea. In addition, she previously developed a pronounced localized skin response to a wasp sting, though without any systemic symptoms. The current event marks her initial occurrence of anaphylaxis.

Five days prior to ED arrival, the patient’s general practitioner had prescribed clindamycin to treat erysipelas. After completing 5 days of clindamycin therapy, she noticed an unspecified rash on her abdomen. This rash was considered a delayed hypersensitivity response to clindamycin, prompting her physician to discontinue clindamycin and initiate clarithromycin, which she had not previously received.

She later arrived at the emergency department experiencing pruritus, flushing, excessive sweating, restlessness, and a sensation of throat swelling. These complaints began approximately one hour following the ingestion of her initial 500 mg oral dose of clarithromycin, with symptoms gradually intensifying. She presented to the hospital one hour after her symptoms first appeared. On admission, her vital signs revealed hypotension (blood pressure 74/50 mm Hg), hypoxemia (oxygen saturation 88% on room air), and a respiratory rate of 22 breaths per minute. Physical assessment demonstrated swelling of the face, new widespread erythema, urticaria affecting her entire body, pronounced sweating, and agitation. Examination did not reveal swelling of the tongue or throat, and both heart and lung auscultations were unremarkable. Laboratory testing indicated a blood glucose of 12.7 mmol/L, and electrocardiography showed sinus rhythm. She immediately received treatment for a presumed severe allergic reaction, including a 0.3 mg intramuscular epinephrine auto-injector and a slow intravenous administration of 2 mg clemastine per local protocols for cutaneous manifestations. Additional management consisted of high-flow oxygen (15 L via a non-rebreather mask) and a 500 mL infusion of normal saline (0.9% sodium chloride solution) for initial stabilization. Oxygen therapy was discontinued after significant improvement following epinephrine administration but had to be briefly resumed during later anaphylactic events. Clarithromycin therapy was stopped, and no further doses beyond the single initial 500 mg oral administration were given.

At first, there was a favorable reaction to the treatment; blood pressure stabilized, and both facial edema and erythema subsided. The patient was subsequently transferred to the acute internal medicine unit for closer observation by specialized nursing staff, although continuous cardiac telemetry was not available. Despite the positive initial progress, within the first 12 hours following admission, the patient experienced several recurrences of symptoms characteristic of anaphylactic shock. After the first recurrence, the patient was moved to the intensive care unit (ICU) for enhanced monitoring. Each recurrence required intramuscular administration of epinephrine for resolution (see figure 1), resulting in a total of four episodes of anaphylaxis, including the initial emergency department presentation. The initial two doses of epinephrine were delivered via auto-injector, containing 0.3 mg of intramuscular epinephrine each, and the latter two episodes were managed with individually prepared 0.5 mg intramuscular injections. Although the auto-injectors available at our facility contain a lower epinephrine dose than the recommended 0.5 mg, their use is favored in situations where rapid administration is crucial—such as during the immediate stabilization of new patients in the emergency department—or in environments where managing emergencies like anaphylaxis is not routine and delays might result from uncertainty with medication preparation. After the first anaphylaxis recurrence, corticosteroids were added to the management regimen, reflecting their traditional role in mitigating biphasic reactions. The patient received intravenous prednisone 30 mg daily for five days and a single intravenous dose of 8 mg dexamethasone, in accordance with varying protocols between the hospital’s general ward and ICU for anaphylaxis management. Additionally, 2 mg clemastine was administered intravenously on two occasions to address dermatological manifestations, and 500 mL normal saline was infused during the second anaphylactic episode. Notably, each subsequent episode was less severe, evolving from profound hypoxemia with cyanosis, hypotension, and marked dyspnea in the first event to, ultimately, milder dyspnea, recurrent rash, and pruritus during the final episode.

An early consultation with the allergology department was sought for expert therapeutic recommendations. Two alternative strategies were contemplated in this case. The first involved the continuous intravenous infusion of epinephrine to preemptively address anaphylaxis before recurrence of symptoms, whereas the second option was a conservative, wait-and-see approach, given the trend toward diminishing symptom intensity. Both approaches presented significant risks, compounded by the absence of literature to guide management in cases of multiphasic anaphylaxis.

Outcome and follow-up:

In the end, neither intervention was needed since the patient did not experience any additional recurrence of symptoms. Following approximately 24 hours without another episode of anaphylaxis, she was transferred back to a general medical ward. She was discharged from the hospital three days after admission. Upon discharge, she was prescribed levocetirizine, an antihistamine, as part of her routine medication regimen, and was supplied with a 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injector, along with instructions on its usage. No subsequent anaphylactic reactions have been reported.

Given the unusual and extended progression of symptoms, a more in-depth investigation into potential underlying etiologies was initiated. This work-up indicated a high likelihood of systemic mastocytosis; however, it did not fulfill the strict diagnostic criteria (with only two WHO minor criteria identified and no major criteria assessable because of insufficient material from the bone marrow biopsy—refer to figure 2). Regrettably, tryptase measurements were not obtained during the initial assessment of the patient's earlier anaphylactic events, which resulted in non-compliance with the recommended timing for tryptase evaluation. According to current guidelines, at least two, and preferably three, tryptase samples should be collected at designated intervals: the first sample as soon as possible without delaying emergency care, a second sample 1–2 hours (no later than 4 hours) after symptom onset, when tryptase concentrations typically reach their peak, and a third sample at least 24 hours after complete symptom resolution to determine baseline levels. In this particular case, the initial tryptase sample was collected 1 hour after the third episode of anaphylaxis, while the second sample was drawn 27 hours following the most recent recurrence.

Allergy confirmation testing was not carried out, as the risk of provoking another severe episode (for instance, via skin testing) was considered disproportionate, especially given the relative simplicity with which future exposures could be avoided. Additional relevant observations consistent with systemic mastocytosis included hepatomegaly identified on abdominal ultrasound as well as possible neuropsychiatric manifestations, such as depression and memory impairment.

Discussion:

Anaphylaxis is generally recognized as a clinical diagnosis distinguished by abrupt onset and swiftly progressing symptoms affecting the airway, breathing, circulatory system and/or gastrointestinal tract, most often accompanied by skin or mucosal findings such as urticaria, angioedema, or flushing. While the presence of a probable allergen can help confirm the diagnosis, it is not always feasible to pinpoint a specific cause.

More complicated cases, including biphasic or refractory anaphylaxis, are not as clearly delineated, although recent initiatives have worked to standardize definitions for these conditions. The Resuscitation Council UK describes biphasic anaphylaxis as a recurrence of symptoms several hours after they initially resolve, without further allergen exposure. Distinguishing genuine biphasic episodes from persistent anaphylaxis with only temporary improvement after epinephrine, or ongoing allergen absorption (such as from residual food or slow-release formulations), may be difficult. Refractory anaphylaxis, on the other hand, is defined by continuing anaphylactic symptoms with inadequate or absent response to appropriately administered epinephrine.

Clinical guidelines on biphasic anaphylaxis predominantly emphasize monitoring practices. However, there is continued debate regarding the ideal length of observation. Conventional approaches suggest monitoring for 4 to 6 hours after symptoms have resolved. The Dutch Nederlands Huisartsen Genootschap (NHG) and the World Allergy Organisation guidelines state that about half of biphasic reactions take place within 6–12 hours following the initial episode, but neither offers a definitive recommendation for monitoring duration. Conversely, the UK guideline 2021 presents a three-level, risk-based framework for surveillance. Establishing uniform monitoring protocols is necessary in order to adequately manage healthcare resources—given the large population of patients with a single anaphylactic event—while also safeguarding patients at risk for uncommon but hard-to-recognize biphasic complications.

There is a significant absence of guidance regarding advanced pharmacologic approaches for patients with biphasic and multiphasic anaphylactic reactions. The therapeutic approach to biphasic and multiphasic anaphylaxis predominantly aligns with conventional anaphylaxis protocols, emphasizing immediate intramuscular administration of epinephrine alongside comprehensive supportive care. Clinical guidelines advocate for an epinephrine dose of 0.01 mg/kg of body weight, up to a ceiling of 0.5 mg per dose. In practice, this is often rounded to 0.5 mg for adults, as most exceed the 50 kg threshold. Notably, commonly available auto-injectors, including those used to treat the initial two anaphylactic episodes in the case described, typically deliver just 0.3 mg of epinephrine, even when labeled for adult use. Although epinephrine auto-injectors remain crucial, especially outside the hospital, and retain value in healthcare settings—facilitating rapid administration and reducing hesitation among staff less familiar with vasoactive drugs—reliance on a 0.3 mg dose may result in considerable underdosing for adults over 50 kg. Retrospective analysis from 2025 revealed that individuals who were administered 0.3 mg initially required escalation of care, such as further epinephrine doses, more frequently compared to those given 0.5 mg at onset. Overcoming factors such as provider reliance on the traditional 0.3 mg dosage and the widespread presence of 0.3 mg auto-injectors, while promoting adherence to recommended dosing, has the potential to substantially enhance the management of anaphylaxis.

Historically, corticosteroids were advised in the management of anaphylaxis, partially to mitigate the risk of biphasic responses. Recent accumulating data, however, indicate not only a lack of efficacy but also the possibility of adverse outcomes, prompting a move away from routine steroid use in anaphylaxis treatment. A systematic review published in 2017 assessed 22 studies, finding only one study in favor of corticosteroid efficacy while the remaining 21 studies failed to demonstrate any reduction in biphasic anaphylaxis risk with corticosteroid intervention. Additionally, more recent investigations highlight the significance of side effects, and a 2019 study reported that prehospital administration of corticosteroids was correlated with increased admissions to intensive care or hospital wards. As a result, most modern guidelines now advise against the regular use of corticosteroids, although they continue to be employed in some clinical settings.

Although biphasic anaphylaxis is uncommon, it remains clinically important. The difficulty in identifying reliable prognostic indicators complicates the process of risk assessment and decisions regarding patient observation. Proposed risk factors include late administration of epinephrine, a more severe initial presentation, the need for multiple doses of epinephrine, and previous biphasic reactions. Nevertheless, dependable markers to guide individualized monitoring are currently lacking.

Systemic mastocytosis, characterized by the proliferation of mast cells, excessive release of mediators, and inflammation affecting multiple organ systems, increases susceptibility to severe anaphylactic episodes.1517 Despite the infrequency of both systemic mastocytosis and biphasic anaphylaxis, there is presently no research focusing specifically on the relationship between these conditions. Further investigations into mast cell disorders and biphasic anaphylaxis could yield meaningful advances in future management strategies.

Anaphylaxis-related deaths are uncommon, particularly where prompt emergency medical services are accessible. Fatalities due to biphasic anaphylaxis are even less frequently reported, despite the serious nature of these reactions. Nevertheless, anaphylaxis remains a frequent emergency that necessitates immediate intervention and vigilant observation. While much emphasis in literature and practice guidelines is placed on acute management, the importance of ongoing care and surveillance after the initial event should not be overlooked. Enhancing our knowledge of complicated anaphylaxis presentations, such as biphasic anaphylaxis, may help to develop more personalized, efficient, and focused management strategies.

Research into biphasic and especially multiphasic anaphylaxis is complicated by the rarity of these events and by the inherent challenges of conducting studies in emergency settings, making the formulation of thoroughly evidence-based approaches particularly difficult. In such contexts, expert consensus guidelines with explicit management directions are crucial; yet, the absence of robust data also limits these expert-driven recommendations. Given the infrequency of such complex courses of anaphylaxis, establishing a registry that documents cases, including key patient information, proposed management approaches—such as those outlined in this report—and outcome data, could form a solid foundation for data-informed expert consensus guidelines.
