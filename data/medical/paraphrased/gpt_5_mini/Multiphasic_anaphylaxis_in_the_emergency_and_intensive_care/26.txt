Title: Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting

Margret Ann Schneider, Hans de Groot, Jan-Willem Geerts

Abstract:

Summary: Anaphylaxis represents the most acute and dangerous manifestation of allergic reactions, characterized by rapid onset and a risk of fatal outcomes if not managed quickly and appropriately. A proportion of patients experience recurrence of anaphylactic symptoms following initial treatment and resolution, known as biphasic or multiphasic anaphylactic reactions. These patterns pose distinct challenges for healthcare providers, as the prevalence rates in the literature are inconsistent, contributing risk factors have not been fully elucidated, and current management protocols do not explicitly address such cases. This challenge is heightened in multiphasic anaphylaxis, where multiple episodes occur despite a satisfactory initial response to epinephrine. Given the infrequency of multiphasic presentations, published case reports serve as valuable resources in shaping clinical approaches to these situations. Here, we describe the case of a woman in her 60s who exhibited multiphasic anaphylaxis following clarithromycin exposure. Further evaluation suggested a high probability of underlying systemic mastocytosis; however, her findings did not fulfill the established criteria for a formal diagnosis.

Background:

Anaphylaxis represents a critical and potentially fatal allergic response, particularly in cases involving anaphylactic shock, and is frequently encountered in emergency settings. A proportion of anaphylaxis cases demonstrates biphasic or even multiphasic manifestations, whereby an initial response to an allergenic stimulus resolves, leading to a symptom-free period, followed by a recurrence of symptoms despite absence of further allergen exposure. This pattern typically occurs within the first 24 hours post-exposure, although instances of symptom return have been documented up to 78 hours later. The reported frequency of biphasic anaphylaxis is highly variable, with rates spanning from 0.4% to 23.3%; however, the majority of evidence suggests an incidence between 4% and 6%. Given the risk of symptom relapse, a period of monitoring following initial symptom resolution is routinely recommended. Nevertheless, there are inconsistencies in how biphasic anaphylaxis is defined, and treatment recommendations differ, particularly regarding the efficacy of corticosteroids, which has been debated in recent years. Multiphasic anaphylaxis, characterized by several cycles of symptom recurrence, presents unique difficulties concerning management and observation strategies. Here, we describe the case of a female patient in her 60s who experienced multiphasic anaphylaxis in response to clarithromycin.

Case presentation:

A female patient in her sixties, known to be allergic to beta-lactam antibiotics, arrived at the emergency department (ED) experiencing anaphylaxis subsequent to ingestion of clarithromycin 500 mg orally.

Her past medical history was significant for type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, psoriasis, and depression. She weighed 104 kg. Her current medications included metformin 500 mg twice daily, gliclazide 80 mg extended-release once daily, hydrochlorothiazide 25 mg once daily, omeprazole 20 mg once daily, and sertraline 100 mg once daily. She had taken her most recent dose of hydrochlorothiazide in the morning, more than 12 hours before her ED visit.

In adolescence, she had experienced a mild hypersensitivity reaction to amoxicillin, consisting solely of facial swelling without involvement of the oropharynx, no rash, hypotension, or dyspnea. Additionally, she had previously exhibited a pronounced local skin response to a wasp sting, absent systemic symptoms. The current admission marked her first occurrence of anaphylaxis.

Five days prior to coming to the ED, her general practitioner prescribed clindamycin for erysipelas. After 5 days of clindamycin treatment, she developed a rash on her abdomen (without further details provided), which was considered a delayed allergic response to clindamycin. As a result, her physician altered the antibiotic regimen to clarithromycin, a drug she had never previously taken.

She later arrived at the emergency department experiencing itchiness, flushing, excessive sweating, restlessness, and a sensation of throat swelling. These complaints began approximately one hour after taking her initial oral dose of clarithromycin and gradually intensified. She presented to the hospital one hour following the onset of these symptoms. Upon arrival, her vital signs indicated low blood pressure and reduced oxygen levels (non-invasive blood pressure 74/50 mm Hg, oxygen saturation 88% on room air, respiratory rate 22 breaths per minute). Physical examination revealed swelling of the face, newly developed widespread erythema, urticaria across the body, along with increased sweating and agitation. There was no evidence of tongue or throat swelling, and both cardiac and pulmonary auscultation were unremarkable. Her blood glucose measured 12.7 mmol/L and ECG demonstrated sinus rhythm. Immediate management for a suspected severe allergic response was initiated, consisting of 0.3 mg intramuscular epinephrine via auto-injector and a slow intravenous dose of 2 mg clemastine as per local protocols for dermatologic manifestations. She was also given supplemental oxygen at 15 L/min via non-rebreather mask and 500 mL of isotonic saline (0.9% sodium chloride) during the initial phase of stabilization. Oxygen supplementation was discontinued after her condition improved with epinephrine administration, but was required again intermittently during recurrent episodes of anaphylaxis. Clarithromycin was stopped, and no further doses beyond the initial 500 mg oral administration were given.

At first, the patient exhibited a favorable response to treatment: blood pressure stabilized, and both facial swelling and erythema diminished. She was subsequently transferred to the acute internal medicine ward for enhanced observation by specialized nursing staff, although this unit did not employ continuous telemetry monitoring. Nonetheless, despite early improvements, the patient suffered multiple recurrences of anaphylactic shock symptoms within the first 12 hours of hospitalization. After the initial recurrence, she was moved to the intensive care unit (ICU) for closer monitoring. Each anaphylactic event was managed with intramuscular epinephrine (see figure 1), totaling four episodes, including the initial presentation in the emergency department (ED). The first two administrations involved a 0.3 mg dose via auto-injector, while the subsequent two required 0.5 mg doses of epinephrine, prepared individually for intramuscular injection. Although the auto-injectors in use at this facility deliver a lower dose than the recommended 0.5 mg, they remain the preferred option for rapid intervention—especially during initial patient stabilization in the ED or in situations where management of emergencies such as anaphylaxis is uncommon and hesitation with unfamiliar medications could delay care. After the first recurrence of anaphylaxis, corticosteroids were introduced, recognizing their conventional utility in preventing biphasic anaphylactic reactions. The patient received 30 mg prednisone intravenously each day for five days as well as a single intravenous dose of 8 mg dexamethasone, reflecting differing recommendations between the hospital's general ward and ICU protocols for anaphylaxis. Additionally, to address cutaneous symptoms, she was administered intravenous clemastine at 2 mg twice, and received another 500 mL of normal saline during her second anaphylactic episode. Notably, each subsequent anaphylactic episode was less severe, progressing from pronounced hypoxemia, cyanosis, hypotension, and intense dyspnea at the outset to only dyspnea, rash recurrence, and pruritus during the final event.

Early consultation with the allergology department was sought to guide advanced management. In this scenario, two alternative management strategies were debated. The first considered a prophylactic, continuous infusion of intravenous epinephrine, aiming to prevent further episodes rather than reacting to symptoms as they emerged. The second option, given the observed gradual reduction in symptom severity, was a watchful waiting approach to determine whether symptoms would abate on their own. Both strategies entailed significant risks, particularly in the absence of robust literature to inform the management of multiphasic anaphylaxis.

Outcome and follow-up:

In the end, neither therapeutic intervention was required, as the patient experienced no further recurrence of symptoms. After remaining free of new anaphylactic episodes for about 24 hours, she was moved to a general medical ward. She was released from the hospital on the third day following admission. Upon discharge, the patient was prescribed levocetirizine, an antihistamine, as part of her regular medication regimen, and was provided with a 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injector, along with instructions on its use. She has not had additional anaphylactic events since then.

Given the unusual and extended progression of her illness, additional investigations were initiated to explore potential underlying etiologies. This evaluation indicated a significant likelihood of systemic mastocytosis; however, the diagnostic criteria for a definitive diagnosis were not fulfilled, as only two minor World Health Organization (WHO) criteria were present, and assessment of the major criterion was not possible due to an insufficient bone marrow sample (see figure 2). Tryptase measurements were not obtained during the assessment of the initial anaphylactic episodes, resulting in non-compliance with the recommended timing for tryptase sampling. Current guidelines advise collecting at least two, preferably three, tryptase samples at specified time points: the first sample should be drawn as early as possible without postponing acute management, the second between 1 and 2 hours following symptom onset (and no later than 4 hours), capturing the typical peak in tryptase, and the third sample should be collected at least 24 hours after complete symptom resolution to determine the baseline value. In this case, however, the initial tryptase sample was drawn one hour after the third episode of anaphylaxis, and the second sample was obtained 27 hours after the final recurrence.

No diagnostic allergy testing, such as skin tests, was conducted, as the potential for triggering severe anaphylaxis was considered disproportionate to the benefits, particularly since avoidance of re-exposure is relatively straightforward. Additional pertinent observations supporting a diagnosis of systemic mastocytosis included hepatomegaly detected via abdominal ultrasound, along with possible neuropsychiatric manifestations such as depression and memory impairment.

Discussion:

Anaphylaxis is recognized as a relatively well-defined clinical condition, characterized by the abrupt onset and swift escalation of symptoms affecting the airway, respiration, cardiovascular system, and/or gastrointestinal tract, typically alongside dermatological or mucosal signs such as urticaria, angioedema, or flushing. While identifying a probable allergen can aid in supporting the diagnosis, a definitive trigger cannot always be pinpointed.

Presentations like biphasic or refractory anaphylaxis are less specifically described, although recent initiatives have worked to generate consensus definitions in these cases. According to the Resuscitation Council UK, biphasic anaphylaxis refers to the recurrence of symptoms hours after initial resolution, with no additional exposure to the inciting allergen. Distinguishing between true biphasic reactions and sustained anaphylactic responses with transient improvement after epinephrine or continued allergen absorption (such as from residual food or extended-release medications) remains difficult. In contrast, refractory anaphylaxis is defined as an anaphylactic episode that persists with inadequate or absent response to correctly administered epinephrine.

Recommendations in guidelines concerning biphasic anaphylaxis primarily focus on the approach to clinical monitoring, yet the ideal duration of post-resolution observation is still debated. Standard observation times historically range from 4 to 6 hours after symptoms subside. The Dutch Nederlands Huisartsen Genootschap (NHG) and the World Allergy Organisation guidance note that around 50% of biphasic reactions arise within 6 to 12 hours after the original event, but neither suggests a specific monitoring period. Conversely, the 2021 UK guideline introduces a three-tier, risk-based monitoring strategy. Reaching agreement on observation protocols is essential to effectively balance healthcare resource allocation (given the high prevalence of uncomplicated single-episode anaphylaxis) against safety considerations for the smaller, more difficult-to-predict group at risk for biphasic reactions.

There is a notable and concerning scarcity of guidance regarding advanced pharmacological management in biphasic and multiphasic anaphylaxis. Therapeutic approaches to these anaphylaxis patterns primarily adhere to established treatment protocols for acute anaphylaxis, emphasizing rapid administration of intramuscular epinephrine alongside suitable supportive interventions. Current guidelines advise an epinephrine dose of 0.01 mg per kg of body weight, capping the maximal single dose at 0.5 mg. For adults, this is frequently translated to a 0.5 mg dose, since most individuals in this group exceed 50 kg. Nevertheless, it is noteworthy that many commercially available epinephrine auto-injectors—including those employed in our case’s initial two anaphylactic episodes—are formulated to deliver only 0.3 mg, regardless of their adult indication. While auto-injectors serve a pivotal function in prehospital care, they can also offer significant benefits within healthcare facilities by expediting therapy and reducing reluctance among staff who infrequently administer vasoactive drugs like epinephrine. Still, the use of 0.3 mg auto-injectors in adults over 50 kg can result in substantial underdosing. Evidence from a 2025 retrospective study indicates that individuals who received an initial 0.3 mg epinephrine dose more frequently required escalation measures—such as repeated epinephrine administration—compared to those given 0.5 mg initially. Efforts to address obstacles, including practitioners’ familiarity with the traditional 0.3 mg dosage and the widespread accessibility of 0.3 mg auto-injectors, as well as promoting adherence to guideline-recommended dosing, have the potential to markedly enhance outcomes in anaphylaxis management.

Historically, corticosteroids were utilized partly to prevent biphasic reactions in anaphylaxis. However, increasing data reveal not only a lack of benefit but also possible adverse effects, resulting in a current trend moving away from steroid use for anaphylaxis management. A 2017 systematic review included 22 studies; only one indicated a favorable effect of corticosteroids, while the remaining 21 found no evidence supporting corticosteroids in reducing biphasic anaphylaxis risk. Additionally, recent investigations have drawn attention to potential side effects, with a 2019 study demonstrating an elevated risk of ICU or hospital admission following prehospital corticosteroid therapy. As a result, the majority of up-to-date guidelines now recommend against the routine use of corticosteroids, even though they are still frequently prescribed in actual clinical practice.

Biphasic anaphylaxis, although uncommon, is of significant clinical importance. The lack of dependable predictors complicates efforts to stratify risk and guide monitoring strategies. Proposed risk factors have included delayed administration of epinephrine, initial presentation with severe symptoms, the requirement for multiple doses of epinephrine, and a history of biphasic reactions. Nonetheless, reliable predictors to inform targeted monitoring protocols have yet to be identified.

Systemic mastocytosis, which features increased mast cell numbers, overproduction of mediators, and inflammation involving multiple organs, is known to heighten susceptibility to severe anaphylaxis.1517 Nevertheless, due to the low prevalence of both mastocytosis and biphasic anaphylaxis, specific studies examining the connection between these entities are lacking. Additional research into mast cell disorders and their link to biphasic anaphylaxis may yield important guidance for future clinical management.

Fortunately, deaths due to anaphylaxis are uncommon, particularly in areas where emergency medical care is readily available. Even more rare are fatal cases of biphasic anaphylaxis, despite the significant risk associated with these reactions. Nonetheless, anaphylaxis continues to represent a frequent emergency requiring rapid intervention and close observation. Although research and existing guidelines predominantly emphasize the initial emergency management, ongoing care and appropriate monitoring are likewise crucial components. Improved insight into more complicated anaphylaxis presentations, such as biphasic reactions, has the potential to lead to a more focused, patient-specific, and resource-conscious approach to managing anaphylaxis.

The infrequency of biphasic and especially multiphasic anaphylaxis, combined with difficulties associated with conducting research during emergencies, pose substantial obstacles to establishing truly evidence-based management strategies. In these circumstances, consensus guidelines founded on expert opinion and providing clear recommendations are invaluable, yet the formulation of such expert-derived guidance remains limited by scarce data. Considering the low incidence of these complicated anaphylactic episodes, creating a registry of such cases—including relevant patient information, suggested management approaches as demonstrated in our case report, and outcome data—may serve as a foundation for more robust, data-informed expert guidelines.
