Title: Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting

Margret Ann Schneider, Hans de Groot, Jan-Willem Geerts

Abstract:

Summary: Anaphylaxis represents the most acute and dangerous manifestation of an allergic response, posing a risk to life and necessitating immediate, effective intervention. In a fraction of cases, symptoms recur following resolution, even after appropriate treatment and apparent clinical stabilization. These biphasic and occasionally multiphasic episodes create distinctive difficulties for healthcare providers, given the inconsistencies in reported prevalence, unclear predictive factors, and absence of definitive management guidance within existing protocols. These challenges are particularly notable in multiphasic anaphylaxis, in which patients experience repeated episodes despite an initial favorable response to epinephrine. Because multiphasic anaphylaxis is uncommon, published case reports serve as valuable resources for practical approaches to its management. Here, we describe a patient in her sixties who developed multiphasic anaphylaxis triggered by clarithromycin, with further assessment revealing a probable diagnosis of systemic mastocytosis, although she did not satisfy all the established criteria for this condition.

Background:

Anaphylaxis constitutes a serious, and in cases of anaphylactic shock, potentially fatal allergic response, frequently prompting emergency medical evaluation. While most episodes follow a monophasic course, some patients experience biphasic or multiphasic patterns, where initial symptoms appear after exposure to a trigger, subsequently abate during an asymptomatic interval, and then re-emerge in the absence of further allergen exposure. Typically, the recurrence manifests within 24 hours after the primary exposure, although instances of symptom relapse up to 78 hours have been documented. The prevalence of biphasic anaphylaxis is reported across a broad range, from 0.4% to 23.3%, although consensus data place the occurrence rate between 4% and 6%. Given the risk of symptom return, observation following resolution of anaphylactic manifestations is standard practice. Nevertheless, definitions pertaining to biphasic anaphylaxis can lack uniformity and management protocols differ, with recent debate regarding the efficacy of corticosteroid administration. Multiphasic anaphylaxis, marked by several bouts of symptom recurrence, poses unique treatment and monitoring difficulties. This report details the case of a woman in her sixth decade who exhibited a multiphasic anaphylactic reaction subsequent to clarithromycin exposure.

Case presentation:

A woman in her sixties, known to have an allergy to beta-lactam antibiotics, came to the emergency department experiencing anaphylaxis after taking clarithromycin 500 mg orally.

Her past medical history comprised type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, psoriasis, and depression. She weighed 104 kg. The medications she was currently taking included metformin 500 mg twice daily, gliclazide retard 80 mg once daily, hydrochlorothiazide 25 mg once daily, omeprazole 20 mg once daily, and sertraline 100 mg once daily. The last dose of hydrochlorothiazide had been taken that morning, more than 12 hours before her arrival at the ED.

During adolescence, the patient had a mild allergic response to amoxicillin, manifested solely by facial swelling without involvement of the oropharynx, rash, hypotension, or respiratory distress. She had also previously experienced a pronounced local reaction to a wasp sting, which did not involve systemic symptoms. This admission was her initial occurrence of anaphylaxis.

Five days prior to presenting to the ED, her general practitioner prescribed clindamycin for erysipelas. After five days of taking clindamycin, she developed a rash on her abdomen, which was not otherwise specified. This was thought to be a delayed allergic response to clindamycin, prompting her general practitioner to switch her antibiotic treatment to clarithromycin, a drug she had not previously received.

She later arrived at the emergency department with symptoms including itching, facial flushing, excessive sweating, restlessness, and a sensation of throat swelling. These complaints began one hour after she took her first oral clarithromycin dose, gradually becoming more severe. She presented for medical evaluation one hour after the onset of symptoms. On initial assessment in the emergency department, her vital signs indicated low blood pressure and decreased oxygen levels (non-invasive blood pressure at 74/50 mm Hg, oxygen saturation measured at 88% on room air, respiratory rate at 22 breaths per minute). Physical examination noted facial edema, new widespread erythema and urticaria affecting her entire body, as well as increased perspiration and agitation. There was no evidence of tongue or throat edema, and both cardiac and pulmonary auscultation were unremarkable. Laboratory testing revealed a blood glucose of 12.7 mmol/L, while electrocardiogram demonstrated sinus rhythm. She was promptly treated for a presumed severe allergic reaction, receiving 0.3 mg intramuscular epinephrine via auto-injector and a slow intravenous dose of 2 mg clemastine according to local protocols for skin manifestations. She was also administered supplemental oxygen at 15 L/min through a non-rebreather mask, and 500 mL of normal saline (0.9% sodium chloride) for stabilization. The supplemental oxygen was able to be discontinued after marked clinical improvement with epinephrine; however, it needed to be resumed temporarily for subsequent anaphylactic events. Clarithromycin therapy was immediately halted, with no further doses administered after the initial single 500 mg oral dose.

An initial positive response to therapy was noted, with normalization of blood pressure and reductions in both facial swelling and erythema. The patient was transferred to the acute internal medicine ward, offering enhanced surveillance by specialized nursing personnel, albeit without telemetry monitoring. Despite initial improvement, the patient experienced repeated episodes consistent with anaphylactic shock within the first 12 hours post-admission. Following the first recurrence, the patient was moved to the intensive care unit (ICU) for closer observation. Each episode was managed with intramuscular epinephrine (see figure 1), amounting to four total anaphylactic events including the initial presentation in the emergency department (ED). The first two administrations were by auto-injector, each delivering 0.3 mg of intramuscular epinephrine, while the subsequent two were manually prepared, providing 0.5 mg intramuscularly per dose. Although the auto-injector dosage at our facility is below the standard 0.5 mg, they are preferred for urgent scenarios—such as initial patient stabilization in the ED—or in contexts where emergency management of anaphylaxis is infrequent and unfamiliarity with medication could lead to treatment delays. After the initial recurrence of anaphylaxis, corticosteroids were introduced given their conventional use in preventing biphasic anaphylactic reactions. The patient was administered 30 mg prednisone intravenously daily over 5 days, and a single dose of 8 mg dexamethasone intravenously, reflecting differing protocols between the general ward and ICU for anaphylaxis at our institution. Additionally, two intravenous doses of 2 mg clemastine were given for cutaneous manifestations, along with 500 mL of normal saline during the second episode. Notably, symptom severity declined with each subsequent episode: the initial episode featured profound hypoxemia, cyanosis, hypotension, and severe dyspnea, while the final event involved dyspnea, rash reappearance, and pruritus.

Early consultation with the allergology department was sought for expert management guidance. Two alternative treatment strategies were evaluated. The first option was continuous intravenous epinephrine infusion to proactively address anaphylaxis rather than treating as symptoms developed. The second approach, in view of the gradual reduction in symptom severity, was to observe and see if the symptoms would subside spontaneously. Both strategies carried significant risks, exacerbated by the scarcity of literature providing direction in managing multiphasic cases.

Outcome and follow-up:

In the end, neither therapeutic strategy was required since the patient experienced no further recurrence of symptoms from that point forward. After about 24 hours without any additional anaphylactic events, she was moved back to a general care unit. She was discharged from the hospital three days after admission. On discharge, the patient was prescribed levocetirizine as a regular antihistamine and was provided with a 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injector, along with instructions for its use. She has not had any subsequent episodes of anaphylaxis.

Given the unusual and extended progression of the case, an expanded investigation into potential underlying etiologies was initiated. This evaluation indicated a high likelihood of systemic mastocytosis, but the criteria for a definitive diagnosis were not met (only two minor criteria per WHO; major criterion was not assessable due to insufficient bone marrow sample—refer to figure 2). Tryptase measurements were not obtained during the initial assessment of the patient’s earlier anaphylactic events; as a result, the timing of tryptase evaluations did not correspond with current recommendations. Guidelines advise that at least two, and ideally three, tryptase samples should be collected at particular time points: the first as soon as reasonably possible without postponing urgent management, a second at 1–2 hours (not exceeding 4 hours) from symptom onset when levels are generally highest, and a third at least 24 hours after complete symptom resolution to serve as a baseline. In the present case, the initial tryptase sample was drawn 1 hour following the third anaphylactic event, with a subsequent sample obtained 27 hours after the final episode.

No allergy provocation or confirmation testing was conducted, since the risk of inducing another severe anaphylactic reaction, such as could occur with skin testing, was judged disproportionate, particularly given the simplicity of preventing further exposure. Additional findings that support a possible diagnosis of systemic mastocytosis included hepatomegaly detected by abdominal ultrasound and possible neuropsychiatric manifestations such as depression and impaired memory.

Discussion:

Anaphylaxis is a clinical syndrome with distinct diagnostic criteria, marked by abrupt onset and rapid escalation of signs affecting the respiratory tract, cardiovascular system, gastrointestinal system, and often presents with skin or mucosal symptoms such as urticaria, angioedema, or flushing. Although detecting a probable allergen strengthens the diagnostic case, it is not always feasible to identify a definite causative agent.

More complicated cases, including biphasic or refractory anaphylaxis, lack precise definitions; however, recent consensus-building efforts have worked to clarify these categories. According to the Resuscitation Council UK, biphasic anaphylaxis entails the reappearance of symptoms several hours following their apparent resolution, without additional exposure to the allergen. It can be difficult to distinguish genuine biphasic events from prolonged anaphylaxis masked by brief responses to epinephrine or continued absorption of the allergen (such as remaining food or delayed-release medication). In contrast, refractory anaphylaxis is characterized by ongoing symptoms that do not respond—or respond inadequately—to appropriately administered epinephrine.

Current guidelines concerning biphasic anaphylaxis focus largely on clinical observation, but there remains controversy over the ideal length of monitoring. Conventionally, observation after symptom resolution has spanned from 4 to 6 hours. The Dutch Nederlands Huisartsen Genootschap (NHG) and the World Allergy Organisation guidelines highlight that about half of biphasic cases take place within 6–12 hours after the initial reaction, yet they do not recommend a specific monitoring duration. The 2021 UK guideline, on the other hand, introduces a three-tier, risk-stratified monitoring scheme. Unified approaches to observation are needed to reconcile healthcare resource allocation for the substantial population with single-episode anaphylaxis and the safety needs posed by the smaller, hard-to-predict group at risk for biphasic reactions.

There is a notable and concerning deficit in guidance regarding advanced pharmacologic approaches for biphasic and multiphasic anaphylactic episodes. The therapeutic approach to biphasic and multiphasic anaphylaxis primarily aligns with conventional anaphylaxis protocols, involving immediate intramuscular administration of epinephrine along with suitable supportive interventions. Consensus guidelines advise a dose of 0.01 mg/kg epinephrine, with a ceiling of 0.5 mg per administration. This dosage is frequently generalized to 0.5 mg for adults, as most exceed a body weight of 50 kg. Interestingly, many epinephrine auto-injectors—including those used in the initial two events in this case—only contain 0.3 mg, even in devices intended for adult use. While auto-injectors are invaluable in out-of-hospital emergencies, they may also have utility in healthcare environments by decreasing delays in therapy and alleviating reluctance among providers who infrequently administer vasoactive medications like epinephrine. Despite their widespread use, 0.3 mg auto-injectors present a risk of substantial underdosing in adults heavier than 50 kg. In a 2025 retrospective review, individuals who first received 0.3 mg epinephrine were found to require escalation of therapy, including additional epinephrine, at a significantly higher rate compared to those initially treated with 0.5 mg. Addressing factors such as clinician familiarity with the longstanding 0.3 mg standard and the predominance of 0.3 mg auto-injectors, as well as promoting compliance with current dosing recommendations, could significantly enhance outcomes in anaphylaxis management.

Historically, corticosteroids were advocated partly to mitigate biphasic reactions, but accumulating data reveal not only a lack of benefit but also possible adverse effects. This evidence has prompted a move away from steroid use in anaphylaxis. A systematic review published in 2017 assessed 22 studies, finding only one that favored corticosteroid use, while the remaining 21 provided no support for corticosteroids reducing biphasic anaphylaxis risk. Additionally, recent investigations highlight the impact of steroid-related side effects, with a 2019 study reporting an increased probability of ICU or hospital ward admission following prehospital corticosteroid administration. As a result, most current guidelines discourage routine corticosteroid use, though these agents are still employed in practice.

Biphasic anaphylaxis is uncommon but of significant clinical importance. The lack of dependable indicators complicates both risk assessment and decisions regarding observation. Proposed predictors encompass delayed epinephrine administration, severe initial manifestations, requirement for multiple epinephrine doses, and prior biphasic episodes, yet robust predictors that would support selective monitoring remain elusive.

Systemic mastocytosis is characterized by abnormal proliferation of mast cells, excessive release of mediators, and inflammation across multiple organ systems, making affected individuals susceptible to severe anaphylaxis. However, owing to the infrequency of both systemic mastocytosis and biphasic anaphylaxis, existing literature does not, to our knowledge, directly evaluate the association between these conditions. Further investigation of mast cell disorders and their relationship to biphasic anaphylaxis could enhance future treatment strategies.

Anaphylaxis-related mortality rates remain low, particularly in areas where emergency medical services are readily accessible. Fatal cases of biphasic anaphylaxis are even less common, despite their seemingly elevated risk. Nevertheless, despite these encouraging figures, anaphylaxis continues to represent a frequent emergency that requires immediate intervention and vigilant observation. While research and clinical guidelines often emphasize initial emergency response, ongoing care and patient surveillance are also crucial components of management. Improved insights into complex presentations, such as biphasic anaphylaxis, may facilitate more individualized, efficient, and resource-conscious approaches to managing these reactions.

Because biphasic and, especially, multiphasic anaphylaxis are uncommon and research in emergent settings is logistically challenging, establishing truly evidence-based management strategies is particularly difficult. Consensus recommendations crafted by experts are especially beneficial under these circumstances, yet the limited body of evidence also complicates the process of generating such guidelines. Considering the infrequency of these complicated cases, assembling a case registry detailing key patient information, proposed management approaches—as illustrated in this case report—and associated outcomes, could serve as a foundation for more robust, data-informed expert consensus recommendations.
