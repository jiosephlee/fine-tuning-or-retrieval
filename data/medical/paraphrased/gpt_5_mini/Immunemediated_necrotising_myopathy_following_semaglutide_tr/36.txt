Title: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

Brian Lam, Eleni Tiniakou, Xinhai Robert Zhang, Shervin Assassi

Abstract:

Summary: This case report presents a woman in her sixties who developed immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) following administration of semaglutide. Over a period of four months, she experienced worsening dysphagia, dysphonia, proximal muscle weakness, and elevated creatine kinase. These manifestations began after she received a single semaglutide dose. Diagnostic evaluation revealed necrotising myopathy with inflammatory infiltration on muscle biopsy. Treatment with corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) resulted in clinical improvement. Eventually, she regained her baseline level of function after discontinuing immunosuppression for several months and by avoiding glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists. This is the first documented instance of IMNM likely related to GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy, demonstrating a favorable outcome after brief immunosuppressive and IVIG treatment.

Background:

In recent years, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have seen widespread use in managing metabolic disorders such as type 2 diabetes and obesity. Among the side effects noted with this medication class, reduction in skeletal muscle mass has been reported. A recent case report documented rhabdomyolysis with necrotizing characteristics linked to tirzepatide, a GLP-1 receptor agonist frequently prescribed. In the reported instance, the patient exhibited creatine kinase (CK) levels in the thousands during active therapy, with a marked improvement in CK values observed within days following discontinuation of the medication, supporting the diagnosis of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis.

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) represents a unique form of myopathy marked by rapidly progressive proximal muscle weakness, histopathological findings of necrotic muscle fibers, and minimal lymphocytic infiltration. Autoantibodies such as anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (SRP) are closely linked to IMNM. Specifically, statins—which are frequently prescribed for metabolic conditions—have a strong association with the presence of HMGCR autoantibodies. Before the relationship between statin use and HMGCR autoantibodies was understood, a number of patients who experienced necrotising myopathy after statin therapy were often misdiagnosed with toxic myopathy or polymyositis. Among these individuals, ongoing muscle disease and elevated creatine kinase levels persisted even after discontinuing statin therapy. Subsequent clinical improvement with immunosuppressive treatment pointed toward an autoimmune mechanism, culminating in the recognition of HMGCR autoantibody as a defining feature of this disorder. It should be emphasized that approximately one-third of IMNM patients do not test positive for these autoantibodies. This seronegativity not only introduces greater complexity in reaching a diagnosis but also implies that other autoantibodies and causative factors have yet to be elucidated.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 60s, whose medical history included obesity, hepatic steatosis, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and a distant episode of deep venous thrombosis/pulmonary embolism, presented with gradually worsening proximal muscle weakness, difficulty swallowing, and changes in her voice.

Previously, she was in good health and could perform all her daily activities independently. Three years before her current decline, she was evaluated in the outpatient setting for elevated liver transaminases. At that time, her aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels were mildly increased to about double the upper limit of normal, with ALT being higher than AST. Results for alkaline phosphatase, albumin, bilirubin, and platelet count were all within normal ranges. She did not consume alcohol and was not taking any medications known to be hepatotoxic. Abdominal ultrasound demonstrated an enlarged liver with diffusely increased echotexture, most consistent with hepatic steatosis. Laboratory testing for viral hepatitis, as well as anti-nuclear, anti-mitochondrial, anti-smooth muscle, and liver-kidney microsomal antibodies, were negative. A liver biopsy revealed steatohepatitis compatible with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), showing an NAFLD Activity Score of 4 out of 8 and a stage of 0. She was given recommendations to adopt a low-calorie diet and engage in regular physical activity.

Because of ongoing hepatic steatosis and obesity 2.5 years following her initial NAFLD diagnosis, semaglutide therapy was begun. Shortly after administration of her first subcutaneous 0.25 mg dose of semaglutide, she developed nausea and vomiting. These symptoms continued, prompting hospital admission two days later for persistent nausea and vomiting, inadequate oral intake, dyspnoea, acute kidney injury, and marked metabolic acidosis. Her management included intravenous fluids and bicarbonate alongside anti-emetic medications. She required a brief transfer to the intensive care unit for non-invasive ventilatory support. Her renal function subsequently improved following hydration, obviating the need for haemofiltration or dialysis. Admission laboratory tests revealed an elevated creatine kinase (CK) of 337 U/L, although she exhibited no other related manifestations, such as myalgia, rash, or dark urine. During the same admission, she developed episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation; however, cardiac troponins remained within the normal range. She was discharged after a 5-day hospital stay. By discharge, her gastrointestinal complaints had abated, but she remained physically debilitated. Over the ensuing four months, she noted increasing weakness. She reported no previous statin use or intake of other supplements associated with IMNM, including mushrooms, red yeast rice, or other nutritional products. She further denied a family history of myopathy, any recent strenuous or prolonged physical exertion, and did not use alcohol or recreational drugs.

Four months following her first and only dose of semaglutide, her condition continued to worsen. She was unable to stand or maintain an upright sitting position without support, and for several days had experienced difficulty swallowing both liquids and solids. Consequently, she sought care at an external hospital, where her creatine kinase (CK) level was measured at 3992 U/L (reference range: 12–191 U/L) upon admission. Treatment was initiated with intravenous methylprednisolone at 1000 mg per day for 5 days, supervised by neurology via telemedicine. Subsequently, she was transferred to a tertiary center for rheumatology assessment. Upon arrival, she was afebrile, had normal blood pressure, and showed no signs of distress. Examination identified marked muscle weakness, especially in proximal muscle groups (table 1), but no rashes, skin abnormalities, pulmonary crackles, synovitis, edema, sicca symptoms, oral ulcers, or other neurological deficits were observed.

After administration of pulse methylprednisolone, oral prednisone was initiated at a dosage of 1 mg/kg per day. Because her muscle weakness persisted and she displayed pronounced symptoms, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was provided at a total dose of 2 g/kg, administered over four days. Over the following two weeks, her creatine kinase (CK) levels showed significant improvement (see figure 1). She was discharged after nearly four weeks of hospitalization. Twelve days post-discharge, she required readmission for ten days due to septic shock resulting from a pseudomonal urinary tract infection; during this admission, her CK remained within normal limits.

She underwent multiple outpatient follow-up visits, during which she demonstrated ongoing enhancements in both functional ability and general well-being, while her CK values remained consistently within the normal range. As a result, her prednisone therapy was tapered quickly and discontinued without difficulty. Taking into account her favorable clinical progress and financial considerations, she did not receive further IVIG beyond the initial administration during her hospitalization.

Her final outpatient assessment occurred 10 months post initial evaluation and commencement of treatment, at which time she had returned to her baseline level of function and had remained off immunosuppressive therapy for the preceding 8 months.

Investigations:

Her laboratory evaluation revealed an elevated creatine kinase (CK) of 1237 U/L at the time of assessment, with a maximum value of 3992 U/L recorded one week earlier at the referring facility. Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) was found to be within the normal range. Aspartate aminotransferase (AST) measured 191 U/L and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) was 165 U/L, which was interpreted in the context of increased CK and a known history of hepatic steatosis. Autoantibody testing showed negative results for anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-Sjogren’s syndrome antibodies type A (anti-SSA), anti-Smith antibodies, anti-U1 ribonucleoprotein antibodies (anti-U1 RNP), anti-double-stranded DNA, ANCA, and anti-mitochondrial antibodies. Complement levels and creatinine were both within normal limits, and no evidence of cytopenias was seen. The myositis antibody panel was negative for SRP, HMGCR, as well as Jo-1, Pl-7, Pl-12, EJ, OJ, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-y, NXP-2, and cN-1A autoantibodies. There was a low-titer positive SS-B autoantibody at 2.4 (reference range: <1.0). Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was increased at 22 mm/hour, with C-reactive protein elevated at 22.8 mg/L. Of note, high-sensitivity troponin (HS-Troponin) was raised at 62 pg/mL (reference range ≤53 pg/mL), and proBNP was elevated at 4358 pg/mL (reference range: 0–125 pg/mL).

Significant imaging studies performed included MRI of both femurs, echocardiography, cardiac MRI, liver ultrasound with Doppler, high-resolution chest CT, and abdominal MRI. The femoral MRI displayed mild, symmetrical muscular edema bilaterally, predominantly affecting the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps regions, as well as subcutaneous edema lateral to both thighs (figure 2). Echocardiographic findings revealed a left ventricular ejection fraction estimated at 65%–70% and a right ventricular systolic pressure measuring 46 mm Hg. Cardiac MRI demonstrated mild hypokinesis in the mid-anteroseptal and anterior segments, with accompanying myocardial edema and absence of definite late gadolinium enhancement, suggestive either of myocardial inflammation/early myocarditis or possibly a localized stress-induced cardiomyopathy. There was no evidence of significant myocardial scarring, infarction, or major valvular abnormality. Liver ultrasound with Doppler detected hepatosteatosis. High-resolution CT scan of the chest showed no signs of interstitial lung disease or lymph node enlargement. Abdominal MRI revealed steatotic hepatomegaly with no other abnormalities or lymphadenopathy.

Further diagnostic evaluations included a second liver biopsy and a biopsy of the thigh muscle. The liver biopsy, conducted during her hospitalization, revealed several tissue cores displaying macrovesicular steatosis with both small and large droplets affecting approximately 70% of the examined hepatic parenchyma. There was no notable portal inflammation or evidence of interface hepatitis. These pathological results corresponded with her established diagnosis of hepatosteatosis and indicated mild worsening of her chronic liver disease, yielding an NAFLD Activity Score of 5 out of 8 and stage 1. The biopsy from the right vastus lateralis muscle showed scattered areas of necrotic as well as regenerating muscle fibers (figure 3A,B), and immunohistochemical analyses identified acid phosphatase (figure 3D) and cluster of Differentiation-68 (CD68) positivity, indicating macrophage activity and the presence of myophagocytosis (figure 4B). Additionally, staining for membrane attack complex was detected on both necrotic and non-necrotic myofibers (figure 4C), suggesting an inflammatory process. Sparse CD3-positive cells, denoting T-lymphocyte presence (figure 4A), and perivascular inflammatory infiltrates (figure 3C) were also observed. Collectively, the muscle biopsy findings supported a diagnosis of IMNM.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient developed a pronounced and immediate adverse response following her first and only administration of semaglutide, indicating a possible intolerance. Initial manifestations included gastrointestinal disturbances and renal insufficiency, the latter thought to be due to hypovolemia. Although her creatine kinase (CK) was only mildly raised at first—levels not compatible with rhabdomyolysis—her clinical status worsened despite discontinuation of semaglutide. Additionally, she exhibited characteristic patterns of muscle weakness and a 12-fold increase in CK levels over four months after semaglutide exposure, findings indicative of immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) likely related to semaglutide. The presence of histopathological features typical of drug-induced IMNM, absence of other signs pointing to a connective tissue disorder, and persistent recovery following withdrawal of semaglutide and initial immunosuppressive therapy further substantiate this link.

It is worth noting that she exhibited elevated transaminase levels before initiating semaglutide, a finding sometimes associated with myopathies. Nevertheless, a liver biopsy conducted three years prior to semaglutide use confirmed hepatosteatosis, and her mildly increased AST and ALT values remained even after her CK concentrations returned to normal. Therefore, her chronic transaminase elevation likely stems from hepatic causes rather than muscle injury. Additionally, her cardiac assessment did not definitively indicate current myocarditis. Considering the clinical context and her swift improvement, stress-related myopathy would also be a plausible diagnosis.

The presence of SS-B antibodies in the absence of ANA and SS-A antibodies was unusual. To date, there are no documented cases of IMNM associated with isolated SS-B positivity alongside negative ANA and SS-A. It was not possible to obtain a creatine kinase value from before the introduction of semaglutide, so a low-grade autoimmune IMNM preceding semaglutide administration cannot be ruled out with certainty. Nonetheless, the marked 12-fold increase in CK and gradual onset of proximal muscle weakness following semaglutide use suggest a higher probability that this IMNM episode was triggered by the medication. Furthermore, the patient's pronounced IMNM responded to a brief regimen of corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin combined with discontinuation of semaglutide. This sequence of events is characteristic of drug-induced IMNM, which typically improves after a short period of immunosuppressive treatment following cessation of the causative drug, rather than idiopathic IMNM, which often necessitates sustained and aggressive immunosuppressive management.

Treatment:

She received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for five days, after which she was transitioned to oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg per day. Given ongoing weakness and significant symptoms, IVIG was administered at 2 g/kg, divided over four days. Multiple follow-up visits in the outpatient clinic demonstrated ongoing recovery of her physical abilities and overall condition, with consistently normal CK results. This allowed for a rapid taper of prednisone, completed within two months. Because of her stable clinical status without semaglutide and financial considerations, no further IVIG infusions were provided after the initial hospitalization course.

Outcome and follow-up:

Ten months after her first assessment and commencement of therapy, she was last reviewed in the outpatient clinic and had returned to her usual level of function, having been off immunosuppressive medication for seven months. Her creatine kinase values stayed within normal limits after hospitalisation and immunosuppressive treatment. Follow-up echocardiogram and cardiac MRI demonstrated resolution of previously noted abnormalities. She remained free of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation during this period.

Discussion:

GLP-1 receptor agonists like semaglutide are increasingly utilized and have shown efficacy in treating metabolic conditions such as obesity and type 2 diabetes. While gastrointestinal symptoms are the most frequently observed side effects, there are reports linking these drugs to reduced muscle mass and, in rare cases, rhabdomyolysis. In this case, however, the patient’s presentation aligns more closely with IMNM than with rhabdomyolysis. The creatine kinase (CK) increased twelvefold over a span of four months after a single dose of semaglutide, which differs from what is typically expected in drug-induced rhabdomyolysis, where CK normalization would be anticipated within several weeks if semaglutide were directly responsible. This is exemplified by a prior rhabdomyolysis case related to tirzepatide, in which the patient experienced an acute, substantial rise in CK (57,014 at initial evaluation) followed by prompt improvement after discontinuation of the medication and hydration within days, without the need for immunosuppressive therapy.

Based on the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) Causality Categories and Criteria, there appears to be a probable link between semaglutide administration and IMNM in our patient, given an appropriate temporal association, the low likelihood of IMNM arising from alternate disorders or medications, and an evident improvement after discontinuation of semaglutide. Re-exposure to semaglutide would not be ethical due to the seriousness of the adverse event observed. Nevertheless, we recognize that definitive causality cannot be established in this instance. With semaglutide use expanding rapidly, our case is presented to alert clinicians to this possible complication and to strengthen pharmacovigilance efforts. Further research will be required to determine whether this relationship is causal or coincidental.

The underlying factors that may have contributed to our patient’s heightened susceptibility to semaglutide-induced IMNM are not yet known. As GLP-1 receptor agonists become increasingly widespread, future research examining risk factors will be vital, particularly if additional cases demonstrating a similar temporal association with semaglutide exposure are identified.
