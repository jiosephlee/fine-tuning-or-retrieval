Title: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

Brian Lam, Eleni Tiniakou, Xinhai Robert Zhang, Shervin Assassi

Abstract:

Summary: This case report details a woman in her 60s who developed immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) following administration of semaglutide. She experienced a gradual onset of worsening dysphagia, dysphonia, proximal muscle weakness, and elevated creatine kinase levels spanning four months. The onset of her clinical manifestations occurred after a single dose of semaglutide. Diagnostic evaluation demonstrated findings consistent with necrotising myopathy and showed inflammatory cell infiltrates on muscle biopsy. Treatment with immunosuppressive therapy, specifically corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), resulted in clinical improvement. She eventually regained her baseline level of function and remained off immunosuppression for several months, avoiding further exposure to glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists. This report presents the first documented case of IMNM likely linked to GLP-1 receptor agonist exposure, which resolved following a brief period of immunosuppressive and IVIG therapy.

Background:

Over the past few years, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have become widely utilized in managing metabolic conditions such as type 2 diabetes and obesity. One of the noted side effects of these agents is reduction in skeletal muscle mass. Recently, a published case documented rhabdomyolysis with necrotizing characteristics attributed to tirzepatide, a GLP-1 receptor agonist in frequent use. The patient involved experienced an increase in creatine kinase (CK) levels reaching several thousand units during ongoing therapy, with CK concentrations beginning to decline just a few days after stopping the medication, indicating a pattern typical of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis.

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) represents a unique form of myopathy, marked by rapidly progressing weakness affecting the proximal muscles and histological evidence of muscle fiber necrosis with minimal lymphocytic infiltration. Autoantibodies against hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and signal recognition particle (SRP) are strongly correlated with IMNM. Notably, statins—frequently prescribed for metabolic conditions—are closely linked to the presence of HMGCR autoantibodies. Before the connection to HMGCR autoantibodies was established, numerous patients who had received statins presented with necrotising myopathy and were often misdiagnosed as having toxic myopathy or polymyositis. In these cases, muscular symptoms and elevated creatine kinase levels frequently persisted despite discontinuation of statin therapy, and subsequent improvement with immunosuppressive treatments indicated an autoimmune process. This clinical pattern contributed to the eventual identification of HMGCR autoantibody as a diagnostic indicator of the condition. It should be emphasized, however, that approximately one-third of IMNM patients do not test positive for known autoantibodies. This seronegative population presents additional diagnostic difficulties and implies the existence of further undiscovered autoantibodies and precipitating factors.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 60s with past medical history notable for obesity, hepatic steatosis, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and a distant episode of deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism presented with worsening proximal muscle weakness accompanied by difficulty swallowing and speaking.

Prior to her current decline, she was generally healthy and able to perform her daily activities without assistance. Three years before her symptoms worsened, she was evaluated as an outpatient for elevated liver enzymes. At that time, she had mild increases in AST and ALT—both about two times the upper limit of normal, with ALT exceeding AST. Her alkaline phosphatase, albumin, bilirubin, and platelet count all fell within normal parameters. She reported no alcohol consumption and was not using any medications known to cause hepatotoxicity. Abdominal ultrasound identified an enlarged liver with diffuse echogenicity, most consistent with fatty infiltration. Tests for viral hepatitis, and autoantibodies including anti-nuclear, anti-mitochondrial, anti-smooth muscle, and liver-kidney microsomal antibodies, yielded negative results. Liver biopsy revealed steatohepatitis characteristic of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) with a NAFLD Activity Score of 4/8 and stage 0 fibrosis. She was instructed to maintain a calorie-restricted diet and engage in regular physical activity.

Because her hepatic steatosis and obesity persisted 2.5 years following her NAFLD diagnosis, semaglutide therapy was started. Soon after administering the initial subcutaneous semaglutide dose of 0.25 mg, she developed nausea and vomiting that continued. Two days later, she required hospital admission due to ongoing nausea, vomiting, inadequate oral intake, dyspnoea, acute kidney injury, and profound metabolic acidosis. Management included intravenous fluids, bicarbonate, and anti-emetic medications. A brief intensive care unit admission was necessary for non-invasive ventilatory support. Her renal impairment resolved with aggressive hydration, with no requirement for haemodialysis or haemofiltration. On admission, her creatine kinase was elevated at 337 U/L. She reported no additional symptoms such as skin rash, myalgia, or dark (cola-coloured) urine. During this hospital stay, she developed intermittent atrial fibrillation; however, serum troponins remained within the normal range. She was released from hospital after a 5-day admission. While gastrointestinal complaints had subsided at discharge, she demonstrated functional decline. Over the subsequent 4 months, she experienced gradually worsening weakness. She specifically denied any previous use of statins, intake of other substances linked to IMNM (for example, mushrooms or red yeast rice), or any nutritional supplements. She also reported no relevant family history of muscle disorders, recent physical exertion, or the use of alcohol or illicit substances.

Four months following her initial and sole administration of semaglutide, her condition continued to worsen. She was unable to stand or sit upright without help and had experienced difficulty swallowing both liquids and solids for several days. As a result, she sought care at an outside hospital, where laboratory testing revealed an elevated CK level of 3992 U/L (reference range: 12–191 U/L) at the time of admission. Neurology was consulted via telemedicine, and she received 1000 mg intravenous methylprednisolone daily for five days. Afterwards, she was transferred to a tertiary care facility for further evaluation by rheumatology. Upon arrival, she was found to be afebrile, normotensive, and appeared comfortable. Examination demonstrated marked weakness, particularly in the proximal muscle groups (see table 1). No rashes, skin changes, crackles, synovitis, edema, sicca symptoms, oral ulcers, or other neurological abnormalities were observed.

After receiving pulse methylprednisolone therapy, oral prednisone at a dose of 1 mg/kg per day was initiated. Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at 2 g/kg, divided across 4 days, in response to her ongoing weakness and severe clinical presentation. Over a period of 2 weeks, her CK levels showed significant improvement (figure 1). She was released from the hospital following a nearly 4-week admission. Twelve days post-discharge, she was readmitted for 10 days with septic shock secondary to pseudomonal urinary tract infection; during this hospitalization, her CK was found to be within normal limits.

She attended multiple outpatient follow-up appointments, during which she showed sustained gains in both functional ability and general health, along with consistently normal CK values. On this basis, her prednisone was tapered quickly without difficulty. Given the combined factors of expense and her marked improvement after discontinuing semaglutide, she did not undergo further IVIG therapy beyond her original hospitalization.

At her most recent outpatient visit, which occurred 10 months following her first assessment and commencement of treatment, she had recovered her baseline functional capacity and had been free from immunosuppressive therapy for 8 months.

Investigations:

Laboratory evaluation revealed an increased creatine kinase (CK) level of 1237 U/L at the time of assessment, with a peak value of 3992 U/L recorded one week earlier at the referring facility. Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) was within reference range. Aspartate aminotransferase (AST) was measured at 191 U/L and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) at 165 U/L, against a background of elevated serum CK and known fatty liver disease. Autoantibody testing showed negative results for anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-Sjogren’s syndrome type A (anti-SSA), anti-Smith antibodies, anti-U1 ribonucleoprotein (anti-U1 RNP), anti-double-stranded DNA, antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), and anti-mitochondrial antibodies. Complement levels and creatinine were within normal limits, and there was no evidence of cytopenia. The myositis-specific antibody panel was negative for SRP, HMGCR, Jo-1, Pl-7, Pl-12, EJ, OJ, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-y, NXP-2, and cN-1A autoantibodies. There was, however, a low-titer positive SS-B autoantibody detected at 2.4 (reference: <1.0). Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was raised at 22 mm/hour and C-reactive protein measured at 22.8 mg/L. Additionally, there was a notable elevation in high-sensitivity troponin (HS-Troponin) at 62 pg/mL (reference ≤53 pg/mL) and pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (proBNP) at 4358 pg/mL (reference range: 0–125 pg/mL).

Significant imaging studies included MRI of both femurs, echocardiography, cardiac MRI, liver ultrasound with Doppler, high-resolution CT of the chest, and abdominal MRI. MRI of the femurs displayed mild, bilateral, and symmetrical muscle edema, most pronounced in the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps, as well as subcutaneous edema laterally on both thighs (see figure 2). Echocardiography demonstrated an estimated left ventricular ejection fraction of 65%–70%, with a right ventricular systolic pressure of 46 mm Hg. Cardiac MRI identified mild hypokinesis of the mid-anteroseptal and anterior walls along with myocardial edema, absent clear late gadolinium enhancement. These findings could suggest early myocarditis or localized stress cardiomyopathy. There was no evidence of significant myocardial scar formation, infarction, or notable valvular abnormality. Liver ultrasound with Doppler was remarkable for hepatosteatosis. High-resolution chest CT did not reveal interstitial lung disease or lymphadenopathy. Abdominal MRI noted fatty enlargement of the liver but was otherwise normal, with no lymphadenopathy detected.

Further diagnostic evaluation included a repeat liver biopsy and a biopsy of the thigh muscle. The liver biopsy, performed during hospitalization, revealed multiple tissue samples exhibiting small and large droplet macrovesicular steatosis involving approximately 70% of the submitted hepatic tissue, with absence of significant portal inflammation or interface hepatitis. These results were aligned with her prior history of hepatic steatosis and indicated mild worsening of her liver condition, corresponding to a NAFLD Activity Score of grade 5 out of 8, stage 1. The biopsy of the right vastus lateralis muscle showed scattered necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers (figure 3A,B). Immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated acid phosphatase (figure 3D) and CD68, a macrophage marker, along with evidence of myophagocytosis (figure 4B). Additionally, staining for membrane attack complex was positive on both necrotic and non-necrotic muscle fibers (figure 4C), suggesting an inflammatory process. Rare CD3-positive cells, indicative of T-cell involvement (figure 4A), as well as perivascular inflammatory infiltrates (figure 3C), were observed. The muscle biopsy findings supported a diagnosis of immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM).

Differential diagnosis:

The patient exhibited a rapid and pronounced response following her first and only administration of semaglutide, indicative of drug intolerance. Initial manifestations involved gastrointestinal disturbances and acute renal failure believed to be related to hypovolemia. At presentation, creatine kinase (CK) was only mildly raised, not meeting criteria for rhabdomyolysis, but her clinical deterioration persisted after discontinuing semaglutide. The pattern of muscle weakness and a 12-fold escalation in CK over four months post-semaglutide initiation points toward an immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) likely associated with semaglutide. Additionally, biopsy findings consistent with drug-induced IMNM, absence of other signs suggesting connective tissue disease, and ongoing improvement following immunosuppressive therapy and discontinuation of semaglutide lend further credence to this relationship.

It is important to note that she exhibited elevated transaminases before initiating semaglutide, a finding frequently observed in myopathies. Nevertheless, liver biopsy conducted three years prior to semaglutide administration confirmed hepatosteatosis, and the slight increase in AST/ALT continued even after normalization of CK concentrations. Therefore, her chronic transaminase elevation is most likely attributable to hepatic etiology rather than muscle injury. Cardiac evaluation did not definitively indicate ongoing myocarditis. Considering the clinical context and her prompt improvement, stress-induced myopathy remains a plausible explanation.

The presence of a positive SS-B antibody alongside negative ANA and SS-A was an unusual finding. To date, the literature does not describe cases where isolated SS-B positivity with negative ANA and SS-A has been associated with IMNM. Unfortunately, a creatine kinase (CK) value obtained prior to starting semaglutide was not available, making it impossible to rule out a pre-existing subclinical autoimmune IMNM before semaglutide was introduced. Nonetheless, the 12-fold increase in CK and progressive proximal muscle weakness that occurred following semaglutide initiation suggest that this instance of IMNM is likely associated with the medication. Furthermore, the patient’s pronounced IMNM resolved after a brief course of corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), as well as cessation of semaglutide. This clinical trajectory is more consistent with drug-induced IMNM, which may respond to short-term immunosuppression following discontinuation of the causative drug, compared to idiopathic IMNM, which typically necessitates sustained and intensive immunosuppressive treatment.

Treatment:

She received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg for 5 days, and was subsequently started on oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg per day. Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at 2 g/kg, divided over 4 days, because of ongoing weakness and pronounced symptoms. Multiple outpatient follow-up visits demonstrated progressive recovery in her physical capabilities and general well-being, with consistently normal creatine kinase (CK) measurements. As a result, she was able to taper off prednisone rapidly within a two-month period. Taking into account financial constraints and continued clinical stability in the absence of semaglutide, no further IVIG was given following her initial inpatient regimen.

Outcome and follow-up:

At her most recent outpatient visit, which occurred 10 months after starting therapy, she had recovered her pre-illness level of function and had discontinued immunosuppressive medications for 7 months. Her creatine kinase concentrations stayed normal after hospital discharge and immunosuppressive treatment. Repeat echocardiogram and cardiac MRI demonstrated resolution of previously observed abnormalities. She remained free of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation episodes.

Discussion:

GLP-1 receptor agonists, including semaglutide, are increasingly utilized and have proven beneficial for conditions such as obesity and type 2 diabetes. Although gastrointestinal adverse effects are most frequently reported, these agents may also lead to reduced muscle mass and, in rare cases, rhabdomyolysis. In this instance, the patient’s progression was more indicative of IMNM rather than rhabdomyolysis. The patient’s CK levels rose twelvefold over a 4-month period after a single administration of semaglutide, whereas a direct toxic effect of semaglutide would likely have resulted in CK normalization within a few weeks, as documented in drug-induced rhabdomyolysis cases. For example, a prior rhabdomyolysis report with tirzepatide presented with a sharp acute increase in CK (CK=57 014 at the time of presentation) and swift recovery following discontinuation of the drug and hydration over several days, without requiring immunosuppressive therapy.

According to the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) Causality Categories and Criteria, semaglutide use in this case likely has a probable association with IMNM, given the appropriate temporal sequence, the improbability of IMNM being attributable to alternative illnesses or medications, and the clinically justified improvement upon discontinuation. Re-exposure was not considered ethical due to the severity of the adverse event associated with semaglutide. Nevertheless, we recognize that definitive causality cannot be established in this instance. In light of semaglutide’s rapidly increasing utilization, this report is intended to inform clinicians of this possible adverse effect and encourage strengthened pharmacovigilance. Additional research will be necessary to determine whether this relationship is indeed causal or merely coincidental.

The precise factors contributing to our patient’s pronounced reaction to semaglutide remain unidentified. With the expanding use of GLP-1 receptor agonists, future investigations into potential susceptibility factors will be increasingly important, particularly if additional cases temporally linked to semaglutide administration are observed.
