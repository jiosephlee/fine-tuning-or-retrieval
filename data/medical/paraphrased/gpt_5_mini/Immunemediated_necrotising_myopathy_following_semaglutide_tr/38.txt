Title: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

Brian Lam, Eleni Tiniakou, Xinhai Robert Zhang, Shervin Assassi

Abstract:

Summary: This case report presents a woman in her 60s who developed immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) following the administration of semaglutide. She experienced worsening dysphagia, dysphonia, proximal muscle weakness, and elevated creatine kinase over a 4-month period, with symptom onset after a single dose of semaglutide. Diagnostic evaluation showed findings consistent with necrotising myopathy, including inflammatory infiltration on muscle biopsy. The patient was treated with corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), resulting in a gradual return to her baseline functional abilities. She remained off immunosuppressive therapy for several months and continued to avoid glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists. This report describes the first documented instance of IMNM likely related to GLP-1 receptor agonist exposure, demonstrating apparent improvement with short-term immunosuppressive treatment and IVIG.

Background:

In the past few years, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have become frequently utilized for managing metabolic conditions such as type 2 diabetes and obesity. Among the documented side effects for these agents, a reduction in skeletal muscle mass has been reported. A recent case report detailed the occurrence of rhabdomyolysis with necrotising characteristics linked to tirzepatide, another widely prescribed GLP-1 receptor agonist. In that instance, the patient experienced a significant increase in creatine kinase (CK) levels—reaching the thousands—during active therapy, and the CK values began to decline within several days after discontinuation of the medication, which aligns with a pattern seen in drug-induced rhabdomyolysis.

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) represents a unique form of myopathy, marked by severe weakness of proximal muscles and histological findings of necrotising muscle fibers with minimal lymphocytic infiltration. IMNM is strongly linked with two autoantibodies: anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (SRP). Statins, commonly prescribed for metabolic disorders, are notably associated with positivity for HMGCR autoantibodies. Historically, individuals who received statin therapy and developed signs of necrotising myopathy were often diagnosed with toxic myopathy or polymyositis, before the connection to HMGCR autoantibodies was established. In these instances, many continued to experience myopathic symptoms and elevated CK levels after discontinuing statins. Improvement with immunosuppressive treatment highlighted the autoimmune basis of their condition and contributed to the recognition of HMGCR autoantibodies as a defining feature. It should be highlighted that approximately one-third of IMNM patients are seronegative, which complicates diagnosis and indicates the possibility of additional yet unidentified autoantibodies or triggers.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 60s with a history including obesity, hepatic steatosis, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and distant past deep vein thrombosis/pulmonary embolism presented with gradually worsening proximal muscle weakness, difficulty swallowing, and hoarseness.

Prior to her decline, she was in overall good health and performed daily activities without assistance. Three years before her symptoms progressed, she was evaluated as an outpatient for elevated transaminase levels. At that time, her aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) were mildly increased, approximately double the upper normal limit, with ALT exceeding AST. Alkaline phosphatase, albumin, bilirubin, and platelet count were all within reference ranges. She reported abstaining from alcohol and had not been using any hepatotoxic medications. Abdominal ultrasonography revealed hepatomegaly with diffusely increased echogenicity, most consistent with hepatic steatosis. Testing for viral hepatitis as well as anti-nuclear, anti-mitochondrial, anti-smooth muscle, and liver-kidney microsomal antibodies was negative. Liver biopsy demonstrated steatohepatitis in line with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), yielding an NAFLD Activity Score of 4 out of 8, stage 0. Recommendations included adopting a low-calorie diet and engaging in regular exercise.

Owing to continued hepatic steatosis and obesity 2.5 years following her initial NAFLD diagnosis, semaglutide therapy was commenced. She developed nausea and vomiting within hours of the first semaglutide administration (0.25 mg subcutaneous). These symptoms continued, resulting in hospital admission two days later for persistent nausea, vomiting, decreased oral intake, dyspnea, acute kidney injury, and marked metabolic acidosis. Management included intravenous fluids, bicarbonate, and anti-emetic medications. The patient was temporarily transferred to the intensive care unit to receive non-invasive ventilatory support. Afterward, her renal function improved with hydration alone; dialysis or haemofiltration was not necessary. Creatine kinase measured on admission was elevated to 337 U/L. She exhibited no other related symptoms, including rash, myalgia, or dark urine suggestive of myoglobinuria. During her hospital stay, she also experienced episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, although serum troponin remained within normal range. She was discharged after 5 days. Upon discharge, her gastrointestinal complaints had resolved but she remained physically deconditioned. Over the subsequent 4 months, she experienced progressive weakness. She reported no history of statin use, significant intake of supplements associated with IMNM—such as mushrooms or red yeast rice—or other nutritional supplements. She also denied any noteworthy family history of muscle disease, recent strenuous or extended exercise, or use of alcohol or illicit substances.

Four months following her first and only administration of semaglutide, her clinical condition continued to worsen. She was unable to either stand or sit upright independently and had experienced dysphagia for both liquids and solids over several days. These symptoms led her to seek care at an external hospital, where laboratory evaluation on admission revealed a creatine kinase level of 3992 U/L (reference range: 12–191 U/L). During her hospital stay, she received 1000 mg of intravenous methylprednisolone each day for 5 days, overseen by neurology via telemedicine. Subsequently, she was moved to a tertiary center for rheumatology assessment. Upon arrival, her vital signs showed she was afebrile and normotensive, and she was otherwise comfortable. The physical examination demonstrated pronounced weakness, particularly in proximal muscle groups (see table 1). There were no signs of rash, cutaneous changes, pulmonary crackles, synovitis, edema, sicca symptoms, oral ulcers, or other neurologic abnormalities.

After receiving pulse therapy with methylprednisolone, the patient was initiated on oral prednisone at a dosage of 1 mg/kg per day. Due to ongoing weakness and pronounced symptoms, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at 2 g/kg, divided across 4 days. Over the next 2 weeks, a significant reduction in her CK levels was noted (figure 1). Her initial hospitalization lasted nearly 4 weeks before she was discharged. Approximately 12 days later, she required readmission for 10 days because of septic shock secondary to pseudomonal urinary tract infection, during which her CK levels remained within the normal range.

She attended multiple outpatient follow-up appointments, during which her functional capacity and general health steadily improved, and her CK remained within normal limits. Consequently, prednisone tapering was completed swiftly. Because of financial constraints and her clinical progress after discontinuing semaglutide, no further IVIG was administered apart from the original inpatient course.

At her most recent outpatient assessment, occurring 10 months post-initial evaluation and commencement of therapy, she had restored her pre-illness level of function and had been free of immunosuppressive therapy for 8 months.

Investigations:

Her laboratory assessment revealed a creatine kinase (CK) level of 1237 U/L at presentation, with a recorded peak of 3992 U/L one week earlier at the referring institution. Thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH) was found to be within normal parameters. Aspartate aminotransferase (AST) measured 191 U/L and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) was 165 U/L, both elevated in the context of increased serum CK and a past medical history of hepatic steatosis. The autoantibody testing showed negative results for anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-Sjogren’s syndrome antibodies type A (anti-SSA), anti-Smith antibodies, anti-U1 ribonucleoprotein antibodies (anti-U1 RNP), anti-double-stranded DNA antibodies, antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), and anti-mitochondrial antibodies. Complement values were within normal limits; serum creatinine was normal, and no evidence of cytopenia was noted. A myositis-specific antibody panel was also negative for signal recognition particle (SRP), 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-coenzyme A reductase (HMGCR), and autoantibodies Jo-1, Pl-7, Pl-12, EJ, OJ, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-y, NXP-2, and cN-1A. However, SS-B autoantibody was detected at a low titre of 2.4 (reference range: <1.0). Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was raised at 22 mm/hour, and C-reactive protein measured 22.8 mg/L. Of note, high-sensitivity troponin was increased at 62 pg/mL (reference range ≤53 pg/mL), and pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (proBNP) was markedly elevated at 4358 pg/mL (reference range: 0–125 pg/mL).

Imaging studies performed included MRI of both femurs, echocardiogram, cardiac MRI, Doppler liver ultrasound, high-resolution chest CT, and abdominal MRI. MRI of the femurs demonstrated mild, symmetrical edema in the musculature, most pronounced within the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps, as well as subcutaneous edema along the lateral portions of both thighs (see figure 2). The echocardiogram reported a left ventricular ejection fraction estimated at 65%–70% and a right ventricular systolic pressure of 46 mm Hg. Cardiac MRI identified mild hypokinesis in the mid-anteroseptal and anterior walls accompanied by myocardial edema, without definite late gadolinium enhancement, findings that could suggest early myocarditis or possibly a localized stress-induced cardiomyopathy. There was no evidence of significant myocardial scar, infarction, or major valvular disease. Doppler ultrasound of the liver indicated the presence of hepatic steatosis. The high-resolution CT scan of the chest showed no signs of interstitial lung disease or lymphadenopathy. MRI of the abdomen was notable for fatty enlargement of the liver, but was otherwise unremarkable without lymphadenopathy.

Additional advanced testing included repeating the liver biopsy and performing a biopsy of thigh muscle tissue. The liver biopsy, carried out during her hospital stay, revealed several cores of liver tissue showing small and large droplet macrovesicular steatosis affecting about 70% of the sampled hepatic parenchyma, but without notable portal inflammation or evidence of interface hepatitis. The histological results aligned with her previous diagnosis of hepatosteatosis, with mild advancement to NAFLD Activity Score grade 5/8, stage 1. Biopsy of the right vastus lateralis indicated scattered necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers (figure 3A,B). Immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated acid phosphatase (figure 3D) and cluster of Differentiation-68 (CD68), marking macrophages, alongside evidence of myophagocytosis (figure 4B). Positive staining for membrane attack complex was observed in both necrotising and non-necrotising myofibres (figure 4C), indicating inflammatory activity. Additional findings included infrequent CD3-positive cells, which are markers for T lymphocytes (figure 4A), and perivascular inflammatory cells (figure 3C). Muscle biopsy results were characteristic of IMNM.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient had a pronounced and rapid adverse response following her first and only administration of semaglutide, indicating drug intolerance. Initial manifestations included gastrointestinal disturbances and acute renal impairment, likely related to volume depletion. Although creatine kinase (CK) was only mildly elevated and did not align with rhabdomyolysis at first assessment, the patient's progressive deterioration off semaglutide, pattern of muscle weakness, and a twelvefold rise in CK over four months post-exposure indicate the presence of immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) possibly linked to semaglutide. Additionally, muscle biopsy findings characteristic of drug-induced IMNM, absence of clinical signs suggesting an underlying connective tissue disorder, and maintained recovery following both immunosuppressive therapy and withdrawal of semaglutide lend further support to this association.

It is noteworthy that elevated transaminase levels were present before she began taking semaglutide, which may occur in cases of myopathy. Nevertheless, three years prior to semaglutide initiation, liver biopsy had confirmed hepatosteatosis, and her mild increases in AST and ALT continued even after normalization of CK values. Therefore, her chronic transaminase elevations are most likely attributable to hepatic causes rather than muscle injury. Additionally, her cardiac evaluation did not yield definitive evidence for ongoing myocarditis. In this context, stress-related myopathy would be plausible, particularly considering her swift improvement.

The detection of SS-B antibody alone, in the context of negative ANA and SS-A, was unexpected. To date, no literature describes IMNM associated with isolated SS-B seropositivity when both ANA and SS-A are negative. Unfortunately, a CK level prior to the initiation of semaglutide was not available, precluding definitive exclusion of a pre-existing, subclinical autoimmune IMNM. Nevertheless, the 12-fold elevation in CK and the onset of progressively worsening proximal muscle weakness following the start of semaglutide suggest an increased probability that this episode of IMNM was induced by medication. Furthermore, the patient’s severe IMNM improved with only a brief course of corticosteroids and IVIG, in addition to discontinuing semaglutide. The trajectory of recovery is consistent with drug-induced IMNM, which may respond to short-term immunosuppression after cessation of the culprit medication, as opposed to idiopathic IMNM, which typically necessitates prolonged, intensive immunosuppressive treatment.

Treatment:

She received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for 5 days, then transitioned to oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg per day. Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at a total dose of 2 g/kg, divided across 4 days, given her ongoing significant weakness and severe clinical presentation. She underwent multiple outpatient evaluations, during which she demonstrated progressive gains in functional abilities and overall health, as well as consistently normal creatine kinase (CK) values. This enabled a successful and rapid tapering of prednisone, discontinuing the medication over a span of 2 months. Owing to financial factors and her continued clinical stability off semaglutide, no further IVIG treatments were administered following her initial hospitalization course.

Outcome and follow-up:

At her most recent outpatient follow-up, occurring 10 months post initial assessment and commencement of therapy, she had returned to her pre-illness functional capabilities and had been without immunosuppressive medications for the preceding 7 months. Her creatine kinase (CK) values stayed within normal limits throughout the period after hospitalization and immunosuppressive intervention. Subsequent echocardiography and cardiac MRI indicated resolution of previously noted abnormalities. Additionally, she did not have any further episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation.

Discussion:

GLP-1 receptor agonists, including semaglutide, are increasingly utilized and have shown efficacy in treating metabolic conditions such as obesity and type 2 diabetes. Although gastrointestinal side effects are the most frequent, these agents may also contribute to reductions in muscle mass and, in rare instances, rhabdomyolysis. In this particular case, the patient’s disease trajectory aligns more closely with IMNM rather than rhabdomyolysis. Following a single administration of semaglutide, the patient’s CK rose twelvefold over a span of four months, whereas a direct toxic effect from semaglutide would likely cause CK normalization within weeks, as observed in other instances of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis. Additionally, a previously documented case of rhabdomyolysis related to tirzepatide presented with a typical course: there was a pronounced acute CK elevation (CK=57 014 at the time of presentation), followed by rapid improvement after discontinuation of the drug and hydration within days, without immunosuppressive therapy.

According to the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) Causality Categories and Criteria, there is a probable association between semaglutide administration and IMNM in this patient, based on an appropriate temporal relationship, lack of alternative explanations such as other diseases or medications, and notable clinical improvement following discontinuation. A rechallenge would not be ethical due to the seriousness of the adverse event attributed to semaglutide. Nevertheless, we recognize that a definitive causal link in this case cannot be established. Given the widespread and rapidly increasing use of semaglutide, this report seeks to inform clinicians about this potential side effect and emphasizes the importance of vigilant pharmacovigilance. Additional research will help determine whether this relationship is truly causal or merely coincidental.

The factors predisposing our patient to this pronounced reaction to semaglutide are also not yet understood. With the rising use of GLP-1 receptor agonists, investigations into risk factors for adverse reactions will be essential, particularly if more cases with similar timing in relation to semaglutide exposure are identified.
