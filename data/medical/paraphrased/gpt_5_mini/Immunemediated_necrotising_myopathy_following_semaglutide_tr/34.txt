Title: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

Brian Lam, Eleni Tiniakou, Xinhai Robert Zhang, Shervin Assassi

Abstract:

Summary: This case report details a woman in her 60s who developed immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) following administration of semaglutide. Over a four-month period, she experienced worsening dysphagia, dysphonia, proximal muscle weakness, and elevated creatine kinase levels, all beginning after a single semaglutide dose. Diagnostic evaluation identified necrotising myopathy with inflammatory infiltrates on muscle biopsy. She was treated with corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), to which she responded positively. Her condition steadily improved and she was able to regain her baseline functional status, remaining off immunosuppressive therapy for several months, while continuing to avoid glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists. This report documents the first known case of IMNM potentially linked to GLP-1 receptor agonist administration, demonstrating favorable outcome after brief immunosuppressive and IVIG therapy.

Background:

Over the past several years, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have become frequently prescribed for metabolic conditions such as type 2 diabetes and obesity. Although various adverse reactions have been reported for these medications, reductions in skeletal muscle mass have been noted. Recently, a case report detailed an episode of rhabdomyolysis with necrotizing features attributed to tirzepatide, a GLP-1 receptor agonist that is also widely used. In that instance, the patient exhibited a creatine kinase (CK) level in the thousands while continuing therapy, and this elevation began to decline within several days after cessation of the medication, which supports a diagnosis of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis.

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) represents a unique form of myopathy, noted for severe proximal muscle weakness, necrotic muscle fibers, and limited lymphocyte infiltration seen on histological examination. IMNM is closely linked to two autoantibodies: anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (SRP). There is a particularly strong correlation between statin use—which is widespread in the management of metabolic disorders—and the presence of HMGCR autoantibodies. Before the identification of the association with HMGCR autoantibodies, several patients who developed necrotising myopathy following statin therapy were previously categorized as having toxic myopathy or polymyositis. In many instances, these individuals exhibited ongoing myopathy and elevated CK levels even after discontinuing the statin. The persistence of symptoms combined with a favorable response to immunosuppressive therapy indicated an autoimmune mechanism and eventually resulted in recognition of the HMGCR autoantibody as a key feature of this disease. Of note, approximately one-third of IMNM cases are seronegative for known autoantibodies. The absence of detectable autoantibodies in these patients creates further diagnostic difficulties and implies the existence of additional related autoantibodies and possible causative factors that have yet to be identified.

Case presentation:

A woman in her sixties, with prior medical conditions including obesity, hepatic steatosis, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and a distant history of deep vein thrombosis/pulmonary embolism, presented with worsening weakness affecting proximal muscles, difficulty swallowing, and voice changes.

Before the onset of these symptoms, she enjoyed good overall health and was able to manage her daily tasks independently. Three years before her current decline, she was evaluated as an outpatient due to elevated liver transaminases. At that assessment, her aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels were mildly increased, approximately two times the upper limit of normal, with ALT higher than AST. Alkaline phosphatase, albumin, bilirubin, and platelet count remained normal. She reported no alcohol consumption and was not using medications associated with liver toxicity. Abdominal ultrasound revealed an enlarged liver with diffusely increased echogenicity, suggestive of hepatic steatosis. Serologic tests for viral hepatitis and autoimmune markers, including anti-nuclear, anti-mitochondrial, anti-smooth muscle, and liver-kidney microsomal antibodies, were negative. Liver biopsy findings indicated steatohepatitis compatible with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), with an NAFLD Activity Score of 4 out of 8, stage 0. Lifestyle modification involving a low-calorie diet and regular exercise was recommended to her.

Because she continued to have fatty liver and obesity 2.5 years following her NAFLD diagnosis, semaglutide therapy was initiated. Shortly after her initial subcutaneous dose of semaglutide (0.25 mg), she developed nausea and vomiting. These symptoms persisted, resulting in her admission to the hospital two days later for ongoing nausea and vomiting, inadequate oral intake, dyspnoea, acute renal failure, and severe metabolic acidosis. Management included intravenous fluids, bicarbonate administration, and anti-emetics. She required a temporary stay in the intensive care unit for non-invasive ventilatory support. Renal function improved after hydration, eliminating the need for haemofiltration or haemodialysis. Her creatine kinase (CK) level at admission was elevated at 337 U/L, though she did not report other symptoms such as rash, muscle pain, or dark urine. During her hospital stay, she experienced episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, with normal serum troponin levels. She was released from the hospital after 5 days; at discharge, gastrointestinal complaints had resolved, but she remained physically deconditioned. Over the next 4 months, she developed progressive weakness. She affirmed no history of statin usage, consumption of mushrooms, red yeast rice, or other supplements associated with IMNM, nor any nutritional supplements. Additionally, she denied any notable family history of myopathy, recent strenuous or prolonged physical activity, alcohol, or illicit drug use.

Four months after her first and only administration of semaglutide, her condition continued to worsen. She became unable to stand or sit upright without support and developed dysphagia involving both liquids and solids over the course of several days. This led her to seek care at another hospital, where laboratory tests revealed a creatine kinase (CK) level of 3992 U/L (reference range: 12–191 U/L) upon admission. Neurology consulted via telemedicine, and she was initiated on 1000 mg intravenous methylprednisolone daily for 5 days. Subsequently, she was transferred to a tertiary facility for rheumatology assessment. On arrival, she was found to be afebrile, with normal blood pressure and in no apparent distress. Physical assessment demonstrated marked muscular weakness, predominantly affecting proximal muscles (see table 1). No rashes, skin alterations, pulmonary crackles, synovitis, edema, sicca symptoms, oral ulcers, or additional neurologic abnormalities were observed.

After administration of pulse methylprednisolone, the patient was started on daily oral prednisone at a dose of 1 mg/kg. Given ongoing weakness and significant clinical features, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was provided at a total dose of 2 g/kg, divided over 4 days. Over a 2-week period, her creatine kinase (CK) levels demonstrated significant improvement (figure 1). She was discharged following a hospital stay of nearly 4 weeks. Twelve days post-discharge, she was readmitted for 10 days due to septic shock resulting from a pseudomonal urinary tract infection, during which her CK remained within normal limits.

She had multiple outpatient visits, during which she demonstrated ongoing improvements in both functional capacity and general health, with CK values remaining within normal limits. Consequently, prednisone was able to be tapered quickly and discontinued. She did not receive further IVIG therapy beyond the initial regimen administered during her hospitalization, largely due to financial considerations and marked clinical improvement without semaglutide.

Her final follow-up in the outpatient clinic occurred 10 months after the commencement of her treatment, at which point she had returned to her pre-illness functional level and had been free from immunosuppressive therapy for the preceding 8 months.

Investigations:

Her laboratory evaluation revealed a CK level of 1237 U/L at presentation, with a prior peak of 3992 U/L recorded one week earlier at the referring facility. Thyroid stimulating hormone was within reference range. Transaminases were elevated with AST at 191 U/L and ALT at 165 U/L, findings noted in the context of raised CK and a background of hepatic steatosis. Autoantibody screening demonstrated negative results for anti-nuclear antibody (ANA), anti-Sjogren’s syndrome antibody type A (anti-SSA), anti-Smith antibody, anti-U1 ribonucleoprotein (anti-U1 RNP), anti-double-stranded DNA, antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA), and anti-mitochondrial antibody. Complement levels and creatinine were unremarkable, and there were no cytopenias. The myositis-specific antibody panel was negative for SRP, HMGCR, Jo-1, Pl-7, Pl-12, EJ, OJ, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-y, NXP-2, and cN-1A antibodies. However, there was a low-titre positive SS-B autoantibody at 2.4 (reference: <1.0). The erythrocyte sedimentation rate was raised at 22 mm/hour, and C-reactive protein was 22.8 mg/L. Of note, high-sensitivity troponin was elevated at 62 pg/mL (normal ≤53 pg/mL), and pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (proBNP) was markedly increased at 4358 pg/mL (reference: 0–125 pg/mL).

Relevant imaging assessments included MRI scans of both femurs, an echocardiogram, cardiac MRI, liver ultrasound with Doppler, high-resolution CT of the chest, and abdominal MRI. The MRI of the femurs showed mild, symmetric edema in both thigh muscles, predominantly in the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps, along with subcutaneous edema on the lateral thighs (figure 2). Echocardiography estimated the left ventricular ejection fraction at 65%–70% and noted a right ventricular systolic pressure of 46 mm Hg. Cardiac MRI identified mild hypokinesis in the mid-anteroseptal and anterior walls in the setting of myocardial edema, with no definitive late gadolinium enhancement, findings possibly related to myocardial inflammation or early myocarditis versus a focal stress cardiomyopathy. There was no evidence of substantial myocardial fibrosis, infarction, or significant valvular abnormalities. Liver ultrasound with Doppler demonstrated hepatosteatosis. High-resolution CT of the chest showed no signs of interstitial lung disease or lymphadenopathy. Abdominal MRI revealed an enlarged, fatty liver but was otherwise unremarkable and without lymphadenopathy.

Additional advanced diagnostic tests included a repeat liver biopsy and a muscle biopsy of the thigh. The liver biopsy, conducted during her hospital stay, revealed multiple samples of hepatic tissue displaying small and large droplet macrovesicular steatosis involving around 70% of the examined liver parenchyma, without notable portal inflammation or evidence of interface hepatitis. These results aligned with her established hepatosteatosis, showing mild advancement of liver disease to a NAFLD Activity Score of 5 out of 8 and stage 1. The right vastus lateralis muscle biopsy showed scattered necrotic as well as regenerating muscle fibers (figure 3A,B), and immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated positive staining for acid phosphatase (figure 3D) and CD68, a macrophage indicator, highlighting myophagocytosis (figure 4B). A membrane attack complex stain was also positive on both necrotising and non-necrotising myofibres (figure 4C), indicative of an ongoing inflammatory response. Rare CD3-positive cells, representing T-cell markers, were detected (figure 4A), along with perivascular inflammatory infiltrates (figure 3C). Collectively, these muscle biopsy findings supported a diagnosis of IMNM.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient developed an acute and pronounced adverse response following her first and only administration of semaglutide, indicative of intolerance. She initially presented with gastrointestinal disturbances and renal insufficiency, believed to result from hypovolaemia. Although creatine kinase (CK) was only slightly raised at the first assessment—findings not compatible with rhabdomyolysis—her neurological condition deteriorated despite discontinuing semaglutide. The specific muscle weakness distribution and a twelvefold CK elevation occurring over four months post-exposure are indicative of an immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) likely linked to semaglutide. Additionally, the muscle biopsy showed patterns typical of drug-induced IMNM; the absence of symptoms suggestive of a systemic connective tissue disorder and the lasting improvement after withdrawing semaglutide and initiating immunosuppression further reinforce the presumed association.

It is significant that elevated transaminases were present before she began semaglutide therapy, a finding sometimes observed in myopathies. Nevertheless, a liver biopsy performed three years before starting semaglutide demonstrated hepatosteatosis, and her marginally increased AST and ALT levels continued even after normalization of CK levels. Therefore, her persistent transaminase elevation likely stems from chronic liver pathology rather than muscle injury. Her cardiac assessments did not provide definitive evidence of ongoing myocarditis. Given her swift improvement, stress-induced myopathy would also be a plausible explanation in this context.

The detection of a positive SS-B antibody alongside negative ANA and SS-A was surprising. To date, there are no documented cases of isolated SS-B seropositivity with concurrent negative ANA and SS-A resulting in IMNM. Regrettably, we lacked data for CK levels prior to the initiation of semaglutide, making it impossible to conclusively rule out a preexisting, low-level autoimmune IMNM before treatment began. Nonetheless, the twelvefold increase in CK and the emergence of worsening proximal muscle weakness after starting semaglutide suggest a stronger association with medication-induced IMNM. Furthermore, the patient's severe IMNM responded completely to a brief regimen of corticosteroids and IVIG together with discontinuation of semaglutide. This pattern is consistent with drug-induced IMNM, which tends to resolve with short-term immunosuppression following cessation of the causative drug, rather than idiopathic IMNM, which typically necessitates extended, intensive immunosuppressive management.

Treatment:

She received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for 5 days, then transitioned to oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg per day. Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at a total dose of 2 g/kg, divided across 4 days, because of her ongoing weakness and severe clinical presentation. Multiple outpatient follow-up visits demonstrated continuous enhancement in both her functional abilities and overall health, with CK levels remaining within the normal range. This allowed for a rapid tapering and discontinuation of prednisone over a 2-month period. Owing to financial constraints and her maintained clinical improvement after discontinuing semaglutide, she did not require additional IVIG beyond the initial treatment during her hospitalization.

Outcome and follow-up:

Ten months after her initial assessment and the commencement of therapy, she returned for outpatient follow-up, having recovered her original functional capabilities and had discontinued immunosuppressive therapy for seven months. Her creatine kinase concentrations stayed within normal limits after her hospitalization and immunosuppressive treatment. Subsequent echocardiography and cardiac MRI demonstrated resolution of previously noted abnormalities. In addition, she did not have any further episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation.

Discussion:

GLP-1 receptor agonists, including semaglutide, are increasingly utilized and have shown efficacy in treating metabolic conditions such as obesity and type 2 diabetes mellitus. While gastrointestinal adverse effects are most frequently observed, these agents have also been implicated in the reduction of muscle mass and possibly in the development of rhabdomyolysis. In this particular case, the patient’s trajectory aligns more closely with IMNM rather than rhabdomyolysis. The CK values rose to 12 times the baseline over a 4-month period after a single administration of semaglutide. This is in contrast to drug-induced rhabdomyolysis, where CK normalization typically occurs within weeks if the drug’s direct toxicity is responsible, as reported in other instances. Furthermore, the previously published case of tirzepatide-associated rhabdomyolysis followed the expected course: the individual exhibited a significant acute increase in CK (CK=57,014 at initial presentation), accompanied by prompt recovery upon cessation of the drug and intravenous hydration within days, without immunosuppressive therapy.

Applying the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) Causality Categories and Criteria, semaglutide appears to have a probable association with IMNM in this instance, supported by an appropriate temporal link, lack of other likely explanations for IMNM such as alternative diseases or medications, and a clinically justified improvement following discontinuation. Rechallenge was not considered due to ethical concerns, as the severity of the adverse event precludes further exposure to semaglutide. Nonetheless, we recognize that a definitive causal relationship cannot be confirmed in this case. As semaglutide usage increases rapidly, this case report serves to alert clinicians to this possible adverse reaction and underscores the need for robust pharmacovigilance. Additional research will be required to determine if the observed association is indeed causal or merely coincidental.

The factors underlying our patient's susceptibility to this significant reaction to semaglutide remain uncertain. With GLP-1 receptor agonists gaining wider usage, further investigation into potential risk factors is essential, especially if more cases with close temporal association to semaglutide are documented in the future.
