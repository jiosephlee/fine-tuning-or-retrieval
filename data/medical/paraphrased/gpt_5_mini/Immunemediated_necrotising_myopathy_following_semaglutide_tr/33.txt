Title: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

Brian Lam, Eleni Tiniakou, Xinhai Robert Zhang, Shervin Assassi

Abstract:

Summary: This case involves a woman in her sixties who developed immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) following exposure to semaglutide. Over four months, she experienced worsening dysphagia, dysphonia, proximal muscle weakness, and raised creatine kinase levels, all of which began after a single administration of semaglutide. Diagnostic evaluation demonstrated findings compatible with necrotising myopathy and the presence of inflammatory infiltrates on muscle biopsy. Treatment with corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) led to clinical improvement. After gradually recovering, she returned to her previous functional state while remaining off immunosuppressive therapy for several months and avoiding glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists. This report presents the first documented instance of IMNM likely related to GLP-1 receptor agonist exposure, showing improvement following a brief regimen of immunosuppression and IVIG.

Background:

In recent years, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have become widely utilized in the management of metabolic conditions such as type 2 diabetes and obesity. Among the reported side effects for this group of drugs, decreased skeletal muscle mass has been noted. One recent case report detailed rhabdomyolysis exhibiting necrotizing characteristics linked to tirzepatide, a frequently used GLP-1 receptor agonist. In the case described, the individual experienced a marked increase in creatine kinase (CK) levels reaching the thousands while undergoing treatment. Following cessation of the medication, CK values began to decline within a few days, indicating drug-induced rhabdomyolysis.

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) is a unique form of myopathy characterized clinically by rapidly progressing weakness of proximal muscles and histopathologically by myofibre necrosis with minimal lymphocytic infiltration. IMNM is strongly linked to two specific autoantibodies: anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (SRP). Notably, statins—widely prescribed for metabolic disorders—have a robust association with positivity for HMGCR autoantibodies. Prior to recognition of this connection, patients receiving statins who developed necrotising myopathy were commonly categorized as having toxic myopathy or polymyositis. In these cases, many individuals exhibited sustained muscle symptoms and elevated creatine kinase levels even after discontinuing statin therapy. The subsequent response to immunosuppressive treatment suggested an underlying autoimmune process, which ultimately led to the identification of HMGCR autoantibody as a diagnostic marker for this condition. It should be emphasized that approximately one-third of IMNM patients do not test positive for either of these autoantibodies. This seronegativity presents additional hurdles for diagnosis and implies there may be other, as yet unidentified, autoantibodies or triggers associated with IMNM.

Case presentation:

A woman in her sixties with a past medical history notable for obesity, hepatic steatosis, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and a remote episode of deep vein thrombosis with pulmonary embolism presented with gradually worsening proximal muscle weakness, swallowing difficulties, and voice changes.

Prior to these symptoms, she was in good health and able to perform all activities of daily living independently. Three years before her decline, she had been evaluated as an outpatient for elevated transaminases. During this period, both aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) were mildly elevated at about twice the upper limit of normal, with ALT being higher than AST. Alkaline phosphatase, albumin, bilirubin, and platelet counts remained within normal range. She reported no alcohol consumption and was not on any medications known to be hepatotoxic. Abdominal ultrasound revealed enlargement of the liver with uniformly increased echogenicity, likely indicative of hepatic steatosis. Testing for viral hepatitis, anti-nuclear, anti-mitochondrial, anti-smooth muscle, and liver-kidney microsomal antibodies was negative. Liver biopsy demonstrated steatohepatitis consistent with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), NAFLD Activity Score of 4 out of 8, stage 0. She was counseled to adopt a calorie-restricted diet and engage in regular physical activity.

Because her fatty liver and obesity continued 2.5 years after being diagnosed with NAFLD, semaglutide therapy was started. Following the first subcutaneous dose of semaglutide (0.25 mg), she developed nausea and vomiting within hours. These symptoms persisted, leading to hospital admission two days later due to ongoing nausea, vomiting, decreased oral intake, dyspnea, acute renal failure, and marked metabolic acidosis. Management included intravenous fluids, bicarbonate, and anti-emetic agents. She was temporarily moved to the intensive care unit for non-invasive ventilation. Hydration resulted in improvement of renal function, so neither haemofiltration nor haemodialysis was needed. On admission, her creatine kinase level measured 337 U/L. She lacked additional symptoms such as rash, muscle pain, or dark-colored urine. During her inpatient stay, she also experienced paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, though serum troponin remained within normal range. She was discharged after 5 days; at that point, her gastrointestinal complaints had resolved, but she remained deconditioned. Over the next 4 months, she experienced increasing weakness. She stated she had never used statins, nor consumed supplements linked to IMNM including mushrooms or red yeast rice, and had not taken any nutritional supplements. She denied a significant family history of myopathy, recent intense or prolonged physical exertion, as well as alcohol or illicit drug use.

Four months following her initial and sole dose of semaglutide, her condition continued to worsen. She became unable to stand or sit upright independently and experienced swallowing difficulties with both liquids and solids for several days. This led her to seek care at an external hospital, where laboratory tests revealed a creatine kinase (CK) level of 3992 U/L (reference range: 12–191 U/L) upon admission. Management included 1000 mg intravenous methylprednisolone administered daily for 5 days, guided by neurology via telemedicine consultation. Subsequently, she was transferred to a tertiary facility for evaluation by rheumatology. Upon assessment, she was afebrile, had normal blood pressure, and displayed no signs of distress. Physical examination demonstrated marked muscle weakness, especially in the proximal muscle groups (see table 1). There were no observed rashes, dermatological changes, pulmonary crackles, synovitis, edema, sicca symptoms, oral ulcers, or other neurological abnormalities.

After administration of pulse methylprednisolone, oral prednisone was initiated at a dose of 1 mg/kg per day. Given her ongoing weakness and significant clinical manifestations, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was provided at a total dose of 2 g/kg, distributed across 4 days. Her creatine kinase (CK) levels showed a substantial decrease within a two-week period (see figure 1). She was discharged following a nearly four-week hospital stay. Twelve days post-discharge, she was re-admitted for 10 days with septic shock secondary to a pseudomonal urinary tract infection; during that hospitalization, CK remained within the normal range.

She underwent multiple outpatient follow-up visits, during which she exhibited ongoing enhancement in both her functional abilities and general health, alongside consistently normal CK measurements. As a result, her prednisone was tapered quickly without issue. Owing to a combination of financial constraints and notable clinical progress following discontinuation of semaglutide, she was not administered further IVIG after her initial inpatient course.

Her most recent outpatient visit occurred 10 months post initial assessment and initiation of therapy, at which time she had restored her previous level of function and had remained off immunosuppressive therapy for 8 months.

Investigations:

Her laboratory evaluation revealed a creatine kinase (CK) level of 1237 U/L at presentation, with a maximum value of 3992 U/L recorded one week earlier at the facility where she was initially managed. Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) was found to be within the reference range. In the context of raised CK and known fatty liver disease, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) measured 191 U/L and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) was 165 U/L. Autoantibody screening demonstrated negative results for anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-Sjogren’s syndrome antibodies type A (anti-SSA), anti-Smith antibodies, anti-U1 ribonucleoprotein (RNP) antibodies, anti-double-stranded DNA, antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), and anti-mitochondrial antibodies. Complement levels were within normal limits, creatinine was normal, and she displayed no evidence of cytopenia. The myositis panel was negative for signal recognition particle (SRP), HMG-CoA reductase (HMGCR), Jo-1, Pl-7, Pl-12, EJ, OJ, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-y, NXP-2, and cN-1A autoantibodies. A low-titre positive SS-B autoantibody was observed at 2.4 (reference: <1.0). Her erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was raised at 22 mm/hour, and C-reactive protein (CRP) was elevated at 22.8 mg/L. Of note, high-sensitivity troponin was elevated at 62 pg/mL (reference range ≤53 pg/mL), and pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (proBNP) was markedly increased at 4358 pg/mL (reference: 0–125 pg/mL).

Significant imaging studies comprised MRI scans of both femurs, echocardiography, cardiac MRI, liver ultrasound with Doppler, high-resolution chest CT, and abdominal MRI. MRI of the femurs demonstrated mild, symmetric muscular edema bilaterally, primarily observed in the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps regions, with additional subcutaneous edema present on the lateral sides of both thighs (figure 2). The echocardiogram indicated an estimated left ventricular ejection fraction of 65%–70% and measured right ventricular systolic pressure at 46 mm Hg. Cardiac MRI identified mild hypokinesia involving the mid-anteroseptal and anterior myocardial wall segments, correlating with myocardial edema, but without clear late gadolinium enhancement; these findings were suggestive of myocardial inflammation/early myocarditis or possibly a localized stress-induced cardiomyopathy. No evidence of substantial myocardial scarring, infarction, or significant valvular abnormalities was detected. Liver ultrasound with Doppler revealed findings consistent with hepatic steatosis. The high-resolution chest CT ruled out interstitial lung disease and lymphadenopathy. Abdominal MRI confirmed steatotic hepatomegaly, with otherwise normal findings and absence of lymphadenopathy.

Comprehensive diagnostic evaluation included a repeat liver biopsy as well as a biopsy of the thigh musculature. The liver biopsy, conducted during the hospitalization, revealed multiple cores containing hepatic tissue with both small and large droplet macrovesicular steatosis, affecting roughly 70% of the sampled hepatic parenchyma. Notably, there was an absence of significant portal inflammation or interface hepatitis. These results aligned with her prior diagnosis of hepatosteatosis, showing mild advancement of her liver pathology to a NAFLD Activity Score of 5 out of 8, classified as stage 1. Examination of the right vastus lateralis muscle biopsy uncovered scattered necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers (see figure 3A,B). Immunohistochemical analysis identified acid phosphatase (figure 3D) and cluster of Differentiation-68 (CD68) staining, indicative of macrophages, demonstrating myophagocytosis (figure 4B). Additionally, staining for membrane attack complex showed positivity in both necrotic and non-necrotic myofibers (figure 4C), reflecting an active inflammatory process. A small number of CD3-positive T-cell marker cells (figure 4A), as well as perivascular inflammatory infiltrates (figure 3C), were also noted. The histopathological features observed in the muscle biopsy were consistent with a diagnosis of IMNM.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient displayed an immediate and pronounced response to her first and only administration of semaglutide, indicative of intolerance. She initially presented with gastrointestinal symptoms as well as acute renal failure believed to be due to hypovolemia. Although her creatine kinase (CK) was only mildly elevated and not compatible with rhabdomyolysis on presentation, the ongoing deterioration after stopping semaglutide, the pattern of muscle weakness, and a 12-fold rise in CK levels over the subsequent four months point toward an immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) likely linked to semaglutide exposure. Additionally, biopsy findings typical of drug-induced IMNM, the absence of clinical indications for another connective tissue disorder, and marked recovery following early immunosuppressive therapy and discontinuation of semaglutide reinforce this probable association.

It is noteworthy that elevated transaminases were present before she began taking semaglutide, a finding that can occur in myopathies. Nevertheless, she was diagnosed with hepatosteatosis confirmed by biopsy three years prior to initiating semaglutide, and her mildly increased AST/ALT continued even after her CK levels returned to normal. Therefore, it is probable that her persistent transaminase abnormalities stem from hepatic rather than muscular causes. Cardiac assessments did not definitively demonstrate active myocarditis. In light of her clinical context and swift improvement, stress-induced myopathy remains a plausible consideration.

The detection of SS-B antibody positivity with concurrent negative ANA and SS-A results was unanticipated. To date, there are no documented cases reporting isolated SS-B positivity in the absence of ANA and SS-A that subsequently led to IMNM. Regrettably, there was no creatine kinase measurement available prior to commencing semaglutide, making it impossible to conclusively rule out pre-existing low-grade autoimmune IMNM before semaglutide was started. Nevertheless, the twelve-fold increase in CK levels and worsening proximal muscle weakness observed following semaglutide administration suggest that the IMNM episode may be related to the medication. Furthermore, the patient’s severe IMNM responded to a short duration of corticosteroids and IVIG, together with discontinuation of semaglutide. This pattern of recovery is more consistent with drug-induced IMNM—which often responds rapidly to brief immunosuppression and removal of the causative agent—than with idiopathic IMNM, which typically demands extended and aggressive immunosuppressive management.

Treatment:

She received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for 5 days, after which she was transitioned to oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg per day. IVIG was administered at 2 g/kg, divided over 4 days owing to her ongoing weakness and pronounced clinical features. Multiple outpatient follow-up visits were conducted, during which she demonstrated further functional recovery, overall health gains, and consistently normal CK measurements. As a result, her prednisone was tapered off successfully within 2 months. Because of both financial factors and her maintained clinical stability after discontinuation of semaglutide, she did not require further IVIG beyond the initial inpatient course.

Outcome and follow-up:

At her most recent outpatient follow-up, which occurred 10 months after starting therapy, she had returned to her pre-illness level of function and had been free of immunosuppressive medication for the previous 7 months. Her creatine kinase concentrations stayed within standard limits after hospitalization and immunosuppressive therapy. Subsequent echocardiography and cardiac magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated resolution of previously detected abnormalities. Additionally, she had no further episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation.

Discussion:

GLP-1 receptor agonists like semaglutide are increasingly utilized and have shown efficacy in treating metabolic conditions such as obesity and type 2 diabetes. Although gastrointestinal side effects are most frequently observed, these agents have also been linked to reductions in muscle mass and, on occasion, to rhabdomyolysis. In this instance, the patient’s presentation aligns more closely with IMNM than with rhabdomyolysis. The patient’s CK levels rose twelvefold over a span of four months following administration of a single dose of semaglutide, whereas one would expect CK normalization within weeks if semaglutide-induced rhabdomyolysis were responsible, as documented in other drug-related rhabdomyolysis reports. Importantly, a prior case of rhabdomyolysis associated with tirzepatide exhibited the classic pattern: a pronounced acute CK elevation (CK=57,014 at presentation) and rapid improvement after stopping the drug and providing hydration in just a few days, without immunosuppressive therapy being required.

According to the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) Causality Categories and Criteria, our case demonstrates probable causality between semaglutide administration and the development of IMNM, supported by appropriate temporal association, lack of alternative explanations from other diseases or medications, and a clinically significant improvement following discontinuation of the drug. A rechallenge was not performed due to ethical concerns related to the severity of the adverse event. Nevertheless, we recognize that causality cannot be definitively established in this instance. With the widespread and increasing utilization of semaglutide, this case seeks to alert clinicians to this potential side effect and emphasizes the importance of pharmacovigilance. Further research will be necessary to determine if this observed association is causal or coincidental.

The underlying factors contributing to our patient’s pronounced reaction to semaglutide are still unknown. As GLP-1 receptor agonists become increasingly prevalent, future investigations examining predisposing characteristics will be crucial, especially if additional cases with similar temporal connections to semaglutide are documented.
