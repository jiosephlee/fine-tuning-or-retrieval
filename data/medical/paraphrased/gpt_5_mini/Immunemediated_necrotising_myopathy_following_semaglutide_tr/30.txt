Title: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

Brian Lam, Eleni Tiniakou, Xinhai Robert Zhang, Shervin Assassi

Abstract:

Summary: This case involves a woman in her 60s who developed immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) following administration of semaglutide. Over a period of four months, she experienced progressive proximal muscle weakness, difficulty swallowing, difficulty speaking, and an increase in creatine kinase levels. The onset of her symptoms occurred after she received a single dose of semaglutide. Diagnostic evaluation revealed findings indicative of necrotising myopathy with inflammatory infiltrates present on muscle biopsy. She demonstrated clinical improvement following treatment with corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG). Ultimately, she achieved gradual recovery and returned to her premorbid level of function, remaining off immunosuppressive therapy for several months, while consistently avoiding glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists. This report presents the first documented instance of IMNM potentially related to the use of a GLP-1 receptor agonist, with favorable response observed after a brief period of immunosuppressive therapy and IVIG.

Background:

Over the past few years, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have been widely utilized for the management of metabolic conditions such as obesity and type 2 diabetes. Among the side effects reported for these agents, reductions in skeletal muscle mass have been noted. A recent case report detailed a presentation of necrotising rhabdomyolysis related to tirzepatide, a frequently prescribed GLP-1 receptor agonist. The patient in question experienced a marked increase in creatine kinase (CK) levels into the thousands during ongoing therapy, and the CK concentrations began to decline within days following cessation of the medication, indicating drug-related rhabdomyolysis.

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) represents a unique form of myopathy, distinguished by severe proximal muscle weakness, histopathological evidence of myofibre necrosis, and limited lymphocytic infiltration. IMNM is closely linked to the presence of two autoantibodies: anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (SRP). Statins, commonly prescribed for metabolic disorders, have a strong correlation with positivity for HMGCR autoantibodies. Prior to the identification of HMGCR autoantibodies, some patients treated with statins developed necrotising myopathy symptoms, often mistakenly attributed to toxic myopathy or polymyositis. Notably, in many such cases, muscle weakness and elevated creatine kinase persisted even after discontinuation of statins. Subsequent improvement upon administration of immunosuppressive therapy indicated an autoimmune basis, ultimately leading to recognition of HMGCR autoantibody as a defining feature of this condition. However, it is estimated that approximately one third of IMNM cases do not exhibit detectable autoantibodies. The absence of autoantibodies in these patients not only introduces further challenges in diagnosis but also points to the possibility of as-yet unidentified associated autoantibodies or contributing factors.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 60s with a history of obesity, hepatic steatosis, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and a remote episode of deep vein thrombosis/pulmonary embolism presented with gradually worsening proximal muscle weakness, difficulty swallowing, and hoarseness.

Previously, she enjoyed good health and was entirely independent in her daily activities. Three years before her recent decline, she was evaluated on an outpatient basis for elevated liver enzymes. At that point, laboratory tests demonstrated mild elevations of aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT), each at roughly twice the upper limit of normal, with ALT higher than AST. Alkaline phosphatase, albumin, bilirubin, and platelet counts remained within normal values. The patient denied alcohol consumption and was not using medications known to be hepatotoxic. Abdominal ultrasonography revealed an enlarged liver with increased, diffuse echogenicity suggestive of hepatic steatosis. Serologic testing for viral hepatitis, as well as autoantibodies including anti-nuclear, anti-mitochondrial, anti-smooth muscle, and liver-kidney microsomal antibodies, were all negative. Liver biopsy indicated steatohepatitis in line with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), yielding a NAFLD Activity Score of 4/8 and fibrosis stage 0. The patient was counseled to adopt a calorie-restricted diet and engage in routine physical activity.

Owing to ongoing hepatic steatosis and obesity 2.5 years following her NAFLD diagnosis, semaglutide therapy was started. Shortly after the initial subcutaneous administration of semaglutide (0.25 mg), she developed nausea and vomiting. These symptoms persisted, leading to hospital admission 2 days later due to persistent nausea, vomiting, limited oral intake, shortness of breath, acute kidney injury, and marked metabolic acidosis. Management included intravenous fluids, bicarbonate, and anti-emetic agents. She required a brief transfer to the intensive care unit for non-invasive respiratory support. Her renal function subsequently improved with hydration, with no necessity for haemofiltration or dialysis. Her admission creatine kinase (CK) was noted to be elevated at 337 U/L. She did not report additional symptoms such as rash, myalgia, or dark (cola-colored) urine. During her hospital stay, she experienced episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, though serum troponin levels were within normal range. She was discharged after 5 days; gastrointestinal symptoms had abated, though she remained deconditioned. Progressive weakness developed over the subsequent 4 months. She reported no prior exposure to statins, nor intake of supplements linked with IMNM—including mushrooms, red yeast rice, or any nutritional supplements. She also denied a significant family history of myopathy, recent excessive physical exertion, or alcohol or illicit drug consumption.

Four months after receiving her first and only dose of semaglutide, her condition continued to worsen. She was unable to stand or sit upright independently and developed dysphagia affecting both liquids and solids over the course of several days. This led her to seek care at another hospital, where laboratory tests revealed a CK level of 3992 U/L (reference range: 12–191 U/L) at admission. Neurology was consulted via telemedicine, and she was administered 1000 mg intravenous methylprednisolone each day for 5 days. Subsequently, she was transferred to a tertiary facility for rheumatology assessment, where she was found to be afebrile, with normal blood pressure and no signs of distress. Examination demonstrated pronounced muscle weakness, particularly in the proximal muscle groups (see table 1). No rashes, skin abnormalities, crackles, synovitis, edema, sicca symptoms, oral ulcers, or other neurological deficits were observed.

After completing pulse methylprednisolone therapy, the patient began oral prednisone at a dose of 1 mg/kg per day. Because of ongoing weakness and significant symptoms, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at a total dose of 2 g/kg, divided across 4 days. Her creatine kinase (CK) levels demonstrated substantial improvement over a 2-week period (figure 1). She was released from the hospital following an admission of almost 4 weeks. Twelve days post-discharge, she was readmitted for 10 days with septic shock secondary to a pseudomonal urinary tract infection, during which her CK level was found to be normal.

She attended multiple outpatient visits during which her functional abilities and general health kept improving, with her CK values remaining within normal limits. Consequently, prednisone tapering proceeded swiftly and without issue. Because of both financial considerations and sustained progress following discontinuation of semaglutide, she received no further IVIG beyond the initial inpatient administration.

Her most recent outpatient assessment occurred 10 months after starting therapy, by which time she had fully returned to her pre-morbid level of function, having been off immunosuppressive medication for 8 months.

Investigations:

Her laboratory evaluation revealed an increased creatine kinase (CK) level of 1237 U/L at the time of assessment, with a maximum of 3992 U/L documented a week earlier at the referring hospital. Thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH) levels were within the normal range. Aspartate aminotransferase (AST) measured 191 U/L and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) was 165 U/L, findings consistent with her elevated CK and past history of fatty liver. Autoantibody screening returned negative results for anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-Sjögren’s syndrome type A antibodies (anti-SSA), anti-Smith antibodies, anti-U1 ribonucleoprotein antibodies (anti-U1 RNP), anti-double-stranded DNA, antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), and anti-mitochondrial antibodies. Complement concentrations and creatinine were both within normal parameters and no cytopenias were detected. A myositis-specific antibody panel was negative for SRP, HMGCR, Jo-1, Pl-7, Pl-12, EJ, OJ, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-γ, NXP-2, and cN-1A autoantibodies. However, she did exhibit a low-titer positive SS-B autoantibody at 2.4 (reference: <1.0). The erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was 22 mm/hour and C-reactive protein was 22.8 mg/L, both elevated. Of note, high sensitivity troponin was 62 pg/mL (reference ≤53 pg/mL) and her pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (proBNP) was markedly increased at 4358 pg/mL (reference range: 0–125 pg/mL).

Imaging studies included MRI scans of both femurs, echocardiography, cardiac MRI, liver ultrasound with Doppler, high-resolution chest CT, and abdominal MRI. The MRI of both femurs identified mild, symmetric edema in both muscles, particularly prominent in the proximal portions of the vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps, along with subcutaneous edema along the lateral sides of both thighs (figure 2). Echocardiography demonstrated a left ventricular ejection fraction estimated at 65%–70% and a right ventricular systolic pressure measuring 46 mm Hg. Cardiac MRI revealed mild hypokinesis of the mid-anteroseptal and anterior wall coupled with myocardial edema, but there was no clear late gadolinium enhancement; findings suggested either myocardial inflammation/early myocarditis or possibly a localized, stress-induced cardiomyopathy. No substantial myocardial scar, infarction, or significant valvular abnormalities were seen. Liver ultrasound with Doppler detected hepatosteatosis. High-resolution chest CT was negative for interstitial lung disease or lymphadenopathy. The abdominal MRI revealed hepatomegaly due to steatosis, otherwise normal findings, and no lymphadenopathy.

Additional advanced investigations included another liver biopsy and a biopsy of thigh muscle. The liver biopsy, conducted during her hospital stay, showed several samples of hepatic tissue exhibiting macrovesicular steatosis—with both small and large lipid droplets—occupying roughly 70% of the analyzed hepatic parenchyma. No substantial portal inflammation or interface hepatitis was present. These results aligned with her known hepatosteatosis and indicated a mild progression to NAFLD Activity Score of 5 out of 8, stage 1. Biopsy of the right vastus lateralis muscle revealed scattered necrotic as well as regenerating muscle fibers (figure 3A,B). Immunohistochemical analysis showed staining for acid phosphatase (figure 3D) and for cluster of Differentiation-68 (CD68), which marks macrophages, demonstrating evidence of myophagocytosis (figure 4B). Additionally, a membrane attack complex-positive stain was observed on both necrotizing and non-necrotizing muscle fibers (figure 4C), indicating an inflammatory process. There were also occasional cells positive for CD3, a T-cell marker (figure 4A), along with perivascular inflammatory cell infiltrates (figure 3C). Collectively, these muscle biopsy findings supported a diagnosis of IMNM.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient exhibited a pronounced and rapid response to her first and only administration of semaglutide, indicative of intolerance to the drug. She initially developed gastrointestinal symptoms along with renal failure thought to be due to hypovolemia. At her first assessment, creatine kinase (CK) was only slightly elevated, making rhabdomyolysis unlikely. However, her clinical deterioration despite discontinuing semaglutide, the pattern of muscular weakness, and a subsequent 12-fold rise in CK over four months following exposure to semaglutide point towards an IMNM potentially related to the medication. Additionally, biopsy findings consistent with drug-induced IMNM, absence of clinical signs suggestive of connective tissue disease, and ongoing improvement after beginning immunosuppressive therapy and stopping semaglutide further reinforce the likelihood of this association.

It is significant that she exhibited elevated transaminases before initiating semaglutide therapy, a finding sometimes associated with myopathies. Nevertheless, she was previously diagnosed with hepatosteatosis based on biopsy three years before starting semaglutide, and her mildly increased AST/ALT values continued even after CK concentrations returned to normal. Therefore, her chronically raised transaminase levels are most likely attributable to a hepatic etiology rather than muscle injury. In addition, her cardiac assessment did not yield definitive evidence for ongoing myocarditis. Considering the clinical context and her swift improvement, stress-induced myopathy remains a plausible explanation.

The presence of SS-B antibody positivity, despite negative ANA and SS-A results, was unanticipated. To date, there are no documented cases linking isolated SS-B positivity in the absence of ANA and SS-A to the development of IMNM. It is important to note that a creatine kinase (CK) level was not obtained prior to starting semaglutide, which limits our ability to completely rule out pre-existing, subclinical autoimmune IMNM. Nevertheless, the observation of a twelvefold elevation in CK, along with worsening proximal muscle weakness following semaglutide initiation, supports the hypothesis that this IMNM episode may be attributable to the medication. Furthermore, the patient’s marked improvement in IMNM after a brief course of corticosteroids and IVIG, combined with cessation of semaglutide, is more indicative of drug-induced IMNM, a condition that often resolves with short-term immunosuppression and drug withdrawal, as opposed to idiopathic IMNM, which typically necessitates extended and more aggressive immunosuppressive treatment.

Treatment:

She received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for 5 days, after which she was started on oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg per day. Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at 2 g/kg, divided over 4 days, in response to her ongoing weakness and pronounced symptoms. She underwent regular outpatient follow-up appointments, demonstrating ongoing gains in functional abilities and general well-being, with creatine kinase (CK) levels remaining within normal limits. Owing to this sustained improvement, prednisone was tapered quickly and discontinued over a 2-month period. Taking into account both cost issues and her continued clinical recovery after stopping semaglutide, no further IVIG was provided beyond her initial hospitalization.

Outcome and follow-up:

Ten months after her first assessment and the start of therapy, she returned for follow-up in the outpatient clinic. At that time, she had returned to her previous level of functioning and had been without immunosuppressive medications for seven months. Her creatine kinase values stayed normal post-hospitalization and subsequent immunosuppressive treatment. Repeat imaging studies, including echocardiogram and cardiac MRI, showed resolution of the previously noted cardiac abnormalities. Additionally, she had no further episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation.

Discussion:

GLP-1 receptor agonists like semaglutide are increasingly prescribed and have shown efficacy in treating metabolic conditions, including obesity and type 2 diabetes. While gastrointestinal side effects are frequently reported, these agents may also lead to reduced muscle mass and, in some cases, rhabdomyolysis. In this case, the patient's presentation aligns more closely with IMNM rather than rhabdomyolysis. The patient's CK values rose twelvefold over a four-month period after a single dose of semaglutide, whereas a return to normal CK would be expected within weeks if semaglutide’s direct toxicity were responsible, as observed in other instances of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis. Importantly, a previously documented rhabdomyolysis case involving tirzepatide exhibited the classic pattern: an abrupt, significant increase in CK (CK=57 014 at presentation) followed by a rapid normalization with discontinuation of the medication and hydration within days, and no requirement for immunosuppressive therapy.

According to the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) Causality Categories and Criteria, there is a probable causal link between semaglutide administration and IMNM in our patient, as there was an appropriate temporal association, IMNM was unlikely attributable to other medical conditions or medications, and the patient showed clinically appropriate improvement upon discontinuation of semaglutide. A rechallenge with semaglutide is not ethically feasible in light of the severity of the adverse event. Nevertheless, we recognize that this does not confirm definite causality in this instance. Given the rapid increase in semaglutide use, this case report seeks to alert clinicians to this possible adverse effect and to strengthen pharmacovigilance efforts. Further research is needed to determine whether this relationship is causal or simply coincidental.

The factors that may have rendered our patient more susceptible to this marked reaction to semaglutide remain undefined. With GLP-1 receptor agonists becoming increasingly prevalent, investigations into potential predisposing factors will be critical, especially if additional cases exhibiting a clear temporal association with semaglutide use are observed.
