Title: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

Brian Lam, Eleni Tiniakou, Xinhai Robert Zhang, Shervin Assassi

Abstract:

Summary: This case concerns a woman in her 60s who developed immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) following administration of semaglutide. Over a 4-month period, she experienced worsening dysphagia, dysphonia, proximal muscle weakness, and increased creatine kinase levels. The onset of her symptoms was shortly after a single semaglutide dose. Diagnostic evaluation showed necrotising myopathy with inflammatory infiltrate on muscle biopsy. Treatment with corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) led to gradual clinical improvement. She eventually returned to her baseline level of functioning and remained stable off immunosuppressive therapy for several months while continuing to avoid glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists. This report documents the first known case of IMNM likely associated with GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy and demonstrates apparent efficacy of brief immunosuppression and IVIG.

Background:

In the past several years, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have been widely utilized in the management of metabolic conditions such as type 2 diabetes and obesity. Among the various adverse effects reported for these agents, a reduction in skeletal muscle mass has been noted. A recent case report detailed an instance of rhabdomyolysis exhibiting necrotising characteristics attributed to tirzepatide, which is also frequently prescribed as a GLP-1 receptor agonist. In the documented case, the patient experienced a marked increase in creatine kinase (CK) levels, reaching into the thousands during active therapy, and these elevated CK values began to decrease within a few days following cessation of the medication, supporting the diagnosis of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis.

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) represents a unique category of myopathies, distinguished by rapidly progressive weakness of the proximal muscles, necrosis of muscle fibers, and limited lymphocytic infiltration observed on tissue examination. IMNM is strongly associated with the autoantibodies anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (SRP). Notably, statin medications, commonly prescribed for metabolic conditions, are linked to the presence of HMGCR autoantibodies. Prior to the recognition of HMGCR autoantibody involvement, several individuals receiving statins who presented with symptoms of necrotising myopathy were often diagnosed with toxic myopathy or polymyositis. In numerous instances, affected patients experienced ongoing muscle symptoms and elevated creatine kinase (CK) levels despite discontinuing the statin. Subsequent improvement with immunosuppressive therapy pointed toward an immune-mediated etiology, ultimately resulting in the characterization of HMGCR autoantibody as a diagnostic marker of the disorder. It should be emphasized that approximately one-third of IMNM patients do not exhibit identifiable autoantibodies. The absence of autoantibodies in these cases presents further diagnostic complexity and implies the existence of additional associated autoantibodies or environmental factors yet to be elucidated.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 60s, whose medical history includes obesity, hepatic steatosis, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and a remote episode of deep venous thrombosis/pulmonary embolism, presented with gradually worsening proximal muscle weakness, difficulty swallowing, and changes in her voice.

Prior to the onset of these symptoms, she was in good health and maintained full independence in daily activities. Three years before this decline, she had been evaluated as an outpatient for elevated transaminase levels. At that time, both aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) were mildly elevated, roughly two times the upper limit of normal, with ALT exceeding AST. Alkaline phosphatase, albumin, bilirubin, and platelets were all within normal ranges. She reported no alcohol consumption and was not using any hepatotoxic drugs. Abdominal ultrasound revealed an enlarged liver with uniformly increased echogenicity, which was considered most consistent with hepatic steatosis. Laboratory investigations including viral hepatitis serology, anti-nuclear, anti-mitochondrial, anti-smooth muscle, and liver-kidney microsomal antibodies returned negative results. Liver biopsy confirmed steatohepatitis in line with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), with a NAFLD Activity Score of 4 out of 8 and stage 0. Recommendations included adhering to a low-calorie diet and engaging in regular physical activity.

Because she continued to exhibit signs of fatty liver and obesity 2.5 years after her NAFLD diagnosis, semaglutide therapy was initiated. Shortly after receiving her initial dose of semaglutide (0.25 mg administered subcutaneously), she developed nausea and vomiting. These symptoms persisted, leading to hospital admission two days later due to ongoing nausea, vomiting, insufficient oral intake, shortness of breath, acute renal failure, and pronounced metabolic acidosis. Management included intravenous fluids, bicarbonate, and anti-emetic medications. She was temporarily transferred to the intensive care unit for non-invasive ventilatory support. Following hydration, her renal function recovered and she did not require haemofiltration or haemodialysis. On admission, creatine kinase was measured at 337 U/L, which was elevated; however, she had no accompanying symptoms such as rash, muscle pain, or dark urine. During her hospital stay, she developed episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, though her serum troponin remained within normal limits. After a 5-day hospitalization, she was discharged, with resolution of gastrointestinal complaints but notable deconditioning. Over the subsequent four months, she reported worsening weakness. She stated she had never used statins, nor ingested mushrooms, red yeast rice, or other supplements commonly linked to IMNM; she also did not take any other nutritional supplements. She reported no substantial family history of myopathies, no recent intense or prolonged physical activity, and no alcohol or illicit drug use.

Four months following her initial and only administration of semaglutide, the patient continued to experience deterioration. She could no longer maintain an upright posture or stand without help and reported several days of difficulty swallowing both liquids and solids. This led her to seek care at an outside hospital, where laboratory tests revealed an elevated creatine kinase (CK) level of 3992 U/L (normal range: 12–191 U/L) upon admission. She was managed with intravenous methylprednisolone at a dosage of 1000 mg daily for 5 days, overseen remotely by neurology via telemedicine. Subsequently, she was transferred to a tertiary center for evaluation by rheumatology. Upon arrival, her vital signs were stable—she was afebrile and normotensive—with no apparent distress. On examination, marked weakness was observed, particularly affecting proximal muscles (see table 1). No rashes, skin abnormalities, pulmonary crackles, synovitis, edema, sicca symptoms, oral ulcers, or other neurological deficits were noted.

After receiving pulse methylprednisolone, she continued therapy with oral prednisone at a daily dose of 1 mg/kg. Given ongoing muscle weakness and pronounced symptoms, she was administered intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) at a total dose of 2 g/kg, divided over 4 days. Her creatine kinase (CK) levels showed substantial improvement within a 2-week period (figure 1). She was released following an inpatient stay of approximately 4 weeks. Twelve days post-discharge, she was readmitted for 10 days with septic shock secondary to a pseudomonal urinary tract infection, during which her CK remained within normal limits.

She was monitored in outpatient clinics on multiple occasions, demonstrating ongoing enhancements in both functional capacity and general well-being, alongside sustained normal creatine kinase readings. Consequently, prednisone was tapered quickly without complication. Owing to financial constraints and her marked clinical recovery following cessation of semaglutide, she did not undergo further intravenous immunoglobulin therapy beyond the initial hospital-administered course.

At her most recent outpatient visit, which occurred 10 months following initial assessment and commencement of therapy, she had returned to her baseline level of function and had remained off immunosuppressive medications for the preceding 8 months.

Investigations:

Her laboratory evaluation revealed an elevated CK of 1237 U/L at the time of assessment, with a maximum value of 3992 U/L recorded one week earlier at the referring facility. Thyroid-stimulating hormone was within the reference range. AST was measured at 191 U/L and ALT at 165 U/L, findings interpreted in the context of increased serum CK and a background of fatty liver disease. Autoimmune serology demonstrated negative anti-nuclear antibody (ANA), anti-Sjogren’s syndrome antibody type A (anti-SSA), anti-Smith, anti-U1 RNP, anti-double-stranded DNA, ANCA, and anti-mitochondrial antibodies. Complement components and creatinine levels were unremarkable, and there was no evidence of cytopenias. Myositis-specific antibodies, including SRP, HMGCR, Jo-1, Pl-7, Pl-12, EJ, OJ, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-γ, NXP-2, and cN-1A, were all negative. She exhibited a low titer positive SS-B autoantibody at 2.4 (reference range <1.0). Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was increased at 22 mm/hr, and C-reactive protein was 22.8 mg/L. Additionally, her high sensitivity troponin was elevated at 62 pg/mL (reference ≤53 pg/mL), and proBNP was increased to 4358 pg/mL (reference range: 0–125 pg/mL).

Key imaging studies consisted of MRI scans of both femurs, echocardiogram, cardiac MRI, liver ultrasound with Doppler, high-resolution chest CT, and MRI abdomen. The MRI of the bilateral femurs identified mild, symmetric muscular edema, most pronounced in the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps muscles, in addition to subcutaneous edema along the lateral surfaces of both thighs (see figure 2). Echocardiography estimated a left ventricular ejection fraction between 65% and 70%, with right ventricular systolic pressure measured at 46 mm Hg. Cardiac MRI demonstrated mild hypokinesis in the mid-anteroseptal and anterior walls, accompanied by myocardial edema without evidence of late gadolinium enhancement; these findings were suggestive of either myocardial inflammation or early myocarditis, or possibly focal stress-induced cardiomyopathy. No significant myocardial scarring, infarction, or valvular dysfunction was detected. Liver ultrasound with Doppler indicated hepatosteatosis. High-resolution CT of the chest was unremarkable for interstitial lung disease or lymphadenopathy. Abdominal MRI revealed steatotic hepatomegaly but was otherwise normal, with no lymphadenopathy noted.

Further advanced diagnostic evaluations included repeating a liver biopsy and performing a biopsy of the thigh musculature. The liver biopsy, conducted during her hospitalization, showed several hepatic tissue cores characterized by both small and large droplet macrovesicular steatosis affecting roughly 70% of the sampled hepatic parenchyma, while notable portal inflammation or interface hepatitis was absent. These results aligned with her established hepatosteatosis and indicated slight progression of liver pathology to NAFLD Activity Score grade 5/8, stage 1. The biopsy obtained from the right vastus lateralis revealed scattered necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers (figure 3A,B), with immunohistochemical analysis demonstrating acid phosphatase (figure 3D) and cluster of Differentiation-68 (CD68)—a macrophage marker—alongside myophagocytosis (figure 4B). Additionally, there was positive membrane attack complex staining on both necrotic and non-necrotic muscle fibers (figure 4C), which supports an inflammatory reaction. Rare CD3-positive cells, indicative of T-cell presence (figure 4A), and perivascular inflammatory infiltrates (figure 3C) were observed as well. The findings from the muscle biopsy corresponded with a diagnosis of IMNM.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient developed a pronounced and acute response following her first and only administration of semaglutide, indicative of a possible intolerance. Her presenting complaints consisted of gastrointestinal symptoms accompanied by renal failure, which was believed to be due to hypovolemia. Although creatine kinase (CK) levels were only mildly raised—insufficient to meet criteria for rhabdomyolysis—during her initial assessment, the progression of her symptoms despite discontinuing semaglutide, the distribution of muscular weakness, and a twelvefold elevation in CK over four months post-exposure point toward an immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) likely related to semaglutide. Additionally, the presence of histological patterns typical of drug-induced IMNM, absence of clinical indicators for other connective tissue diseases, and lasting clinical improvement following early immunosuppressive therapy and withdrawal of semaglutide, further corroborate this association.

It is important to recognize that she demonstrated elevated transaminase levels before initiating semaglutide, a finding sometimes observed in myopathies. Nevertheless, her history includes biopsy-confirmed hepatosteatosis three years prior to semaglutide therapy, and her marginally increased AST and ALT values remained after normalization of her CK. Therefore, her chronic transaminase elevations most likely stem from liver pathology rather than muscle injury. Her cardiac evaluation did not establish evidence for ongoing myocarditis. In view of her prompt improvement, stress-induced myopathy is also a plausible consideration in this clinical context.

Her finding of a positive SS-B antibody with concurrently negative ANA and SS-A was unusual. To date, the literature does not document cases of IMNM arising from isolated SS-B positivity with negative ANA and SS-A. Regrettably, a creatine kinase (CK) value prior to the introduction of semaglutide was unavailable, preventing the absolute exclusion of pre-existing, subclinical autoimmune IMNM before starting semaglutide. Nevertheless, the twelvefold increase in CK and emergence of progressive proximal muscle weakness following semaglutide initiation suggest a medication-induced IMNM is more likely. Furthermore, her marked IMNM improved after a brief regimen of corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin, in combination with cessation of semaglutide. This trajectory is consistent with drug-associated IMNM, which typically responds to short-term immunosuppression after the causal agent is stopped, as opposed to idiopathic IMNM, which generally necessitates ongoing, intensive immunosuppressive therapy.

Treatment:

She received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for 5 days, after which she was transitioned to oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg per day. Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at a total dose of 2 g/kg, divided over 4 days in light of her ongoing significant weakness and severe manifestations. Subsequent outpatient follow-up visits documented a steady recovery in her functional capabilities and general well-being, along with consistently normal creatine kinase (CK) measurements. Based on these findings, prednisone was rapidly tapered off within a 2-month period. Owing to financial constraints and her ongoing clinical stability after discontinuing semaglutide, she did not require further IVIG treatment beyond the initial inpatient course.

Outcome and follow-up:

She was most recently evaluated in the outpatient clinic 10 months post initial assessment and start of treatment, having recovered her original level of functioning and been without immunosuppressive therapy for 7 months. Her CK measurements stayed normal after her hospitalization and immunosuppressive management. Follow-up echocardiogram and cardiac MRI demonstrated resolution of earlier abnormal findings. Additionally, she did not have any further episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation.

Discussion:

GLP-1 receptor agonists, including semaglutide, are increasingly prescribed and have shown efficacy in treating metabolic conditions like obesity and type 2 diabetes. While gastrointestinal side effects are commonly reported, these agents have also been linked to reductions in muscle mass and, in some instances, rhabdomyolysis. In this particular case, the patient's trajectory aligns more closely with IMNM rather than rhabdomyolysis. The creatine kinase (CK) concentration rose twelvefold over a period of four months after a single administration of semaglutide, which contrasts with expectations for drug-induced rhabdomyolysis, where CK levels typically return to normal within weeks. For example, a prior case involving tirzepatide-related rhabdomyolysis exhibited the classic pattern: an abrupt and significant CK increase (CK=57 014 at initial assessment) followed by a rapid resolution after cessation of the drug and hydration over several days, without requiring immunosuppressive therapy.

Applying the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) Causality Categories and Criteria to this case, semaglutide demonstrates a probable causal relationship with IMNM, as evidenced by an appropriate temporal association, exclusion of alternative disease or medication etiologies, and clinical improvement following cessation of the drug. Re-exposing the patient to semaglutide would be inappropriate due to the severity of the adverse event. Nonetheless, we recognize that definitive causality has not been established. With semaglutide utilization rapidly increasing, the purpose of this report is to inform clinicians about this possible adverse effect and to promote pharmacovigilance. Additional research is required to determine if this link is truly causal or merely coincidental.

The factors that may have rendered this patient susceptible to such a marked reaction from semaglutide remain uncertain. As GLP-1 receptor agonists gain in popularity, investigations into potential risk factors will be essential, particularly if other cases emerge with similarly tight temporal associations to semaglutide initiation.
