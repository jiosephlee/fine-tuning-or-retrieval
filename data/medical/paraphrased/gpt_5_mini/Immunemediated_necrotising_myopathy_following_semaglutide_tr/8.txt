Title: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

Brian Lam, Eleni Tiniakou, Xinhai Robert Zhang, Shervin Assassi

Abstract:

Summary: This case details a woman in her 60s who developed immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) following exposure to semaglutide. She presented with worsening dysphagia, dysphonia, proximal muscle weakness, and increased creatine kinase levels over a period of four months, which began after a single administration of semaglutide. Diagnostic evaluation showed necrotising myopathy with an inflammatory infiltrate on muscle biopsy. The patient was treated with corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), resulting in progressive recovery. She ultimately returned to her prior level of function after discontinuation of immunosuppression for several months and refraining from further GLP-1 receptor agonist use. This report discusses the first documented instance of IMNM likely related to GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy, which demonstrated an apparent improvement following brief immunosuppression and IVIG treatment.

Background:

Over the past several years, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have become frequently prescribed for metabolic conditions such as type 2 diabetes and obesity. Loss of skeletal muscle mass is among the side effects reported for this class of agents. Recently, a case report documented rhabdomyolysis exhibiting necrotising characteristics in connection with tirzepatide, another widely used GLP-1 receptor agonist. In the documented instance, the patient developed marked increases in creatine kinase (CK) into the thousands during ongoing treatment; CK levels began to decline within days after the medication was stopped, consistent with rhabdomyolysis induced by the drug.

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) represents a unique form of myopathy distinguished by severe proximal muscle weakness, the presence of necrotic muscle fibers, and limited lymphocytic infiltration observed on histological examination. IMNM is closely associated with two autoantibodies: anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (SRP). Statin medications, frequently prescribed for metabolic conditions, have a strong correlation with HMGCR autoantibody positivity. Historically, several individuals taking statins developed manifestations consistent with necrotising myopathy, which were often misclassified as toxic myopathy or polymyositis before the link with HMGCR autoantibodies was recognized. In these instances, many patients exhibited ongoing muscle pathology and elevated CK levels, even after discontinuing statins. Clinical improvement upon administration of immunosuppressive therapy supported the notion of an autoimmune myopathy, ultimately leading to the identification of HMGCR autoantibody as a defining feature of the condition. However, it is important to note that approximately one-third of IMNM patients do not display any identifiable autoantibodies. This seronegative subgroup not only presents additional diagnostic difficulties but also implies the existence of other, yet undiscovered, autoantibodies and potential causative factors.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 60s, whose medical background included obesity, hepatic steatosis, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and a distant history of deep venous thrombosis and pulmonary embolism, presented with gradually worsening proximal muscle weakness, difficulty swallowing, and voice changes.

Prior to this decline, she enjoyed good health and was fully autonomous in her daily activities. Three years before her worsening symptoms, she was evaluated as an outpatient for elevated transaminases. At that point, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels were mildly increased, roughly two times above the upper limit of normal (with ALT exceeding AST). Levels of alkaline phosphatase, albumin, bilirubin, and platelets fell within normal ranges. She reported no alcohol consumption and was not receiving medications known to cause liver toxicity. Abdominal ultrasound revealed an enlarged liver with diffusely increased echogenicity, most suggestive of hepatic steatosis. Serologic testing for viral hepatitis, along with assays for anti-nuclear, anti-mitochondrial, anti-smooth muscle, and liver-kidney microsomal antibodies, were all negative. Histologic examination of liver tissue demonstrated steatohepatitis consistent with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), with a NAFLD Activity Score of 4/8 and stage 0. Lifestyle modifications including adherence to a calorie-restricted diet and regular physical activity were recommended.

Owing to ongoing hepatic steatosis and obesity 2.5 years following the initial diagnosis of NAFLD, treatment with semaglutide was commenced. Shortly after her first administration of semaglutide (0.25 mg via subcutaneous injection), she developed nausea and vomiting. These symptoms were sustained, and two days later she was admitted to the hospital for persistent nausea, vomiting, reduced oral intake, dyspnoea, acute renal failure, and severe metabolic acidosis. Management included intravenous fluids and bicarbonate, alongside anti-emetic therapy. She required a brief stay in the intensive care unit for non-invasive ventilatory support. Her renal function subsequently improved with continued hydration, obviating the need for haemofiltration or haemodialysis. Creatine kinase measured at the time of admission was elevated at 337 U/L. She did not report additional manifestations such as rash, myalgia, or dark urine. During the admission, she experienced paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, although serum troponin levels remained within normal limits. After five days, she was discharged. Her gastrointestinal complaints had abated by discharge, but she was noted to be deconditioned. Over the ensuing four months, she experienced worsening muscle weakness. She denied any history of statin use, notable consumption of supplements linked with IMNM, including mushrooms or red yeast rice, or use of other nutritional supplements. Additionally, she reported no significant family history of myopathy, recent excessive or prolonged exercise, nor alcohol or illicit substance use.

Four months following her first and only administration of semaglutide, her condition continued to worsen. She became unable to stand or sit upright without support, and experienced several days of difficulty swallowing both liquids and solids. These symptoms led her to seek care at an outside hospital, where laboratory testing showed a creatine kinase level of 3992 U/L (reference range: 12–191 U/L) upon admission. Management included 1000 mg of intravenous methylprednisolone administered daily for 5 days, coordinated via neurology consultation through telemedicine. Subsequently, she was transferred to a tertiary facility for rheumatologic assessment, where she was found to be afebrile, normotensive, and otherwise not in acute distress. On physical examination, marked weakness was noted, particularly in the proximal muscles (see table 1). She had no rashes, skin changes, pulmonary crackles, synovitis, edema, sicca symptoms, oral ulcers, or other neurological deficits.

After administration of pulse methylprednisolone, she began oral prednisone at a daily dose of 1 mg/kg. Due to ongoing muscle weakness and significant symptoms, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at 2 g/kg, divided over a period of 4 days. Over 2 weeks, there was a notable reduction in her CK levels (figure 1). She was released following an almost 4-week hospital stay. Twelve days post-discharge, she was readmitted for 10 days because of septic shock secondary to a pseudomonal urinary tract infection; during this hospitalization, her CK level was found to be within normal range.

She attended multiple outpatient follow-up appointments, during which her functional abilities and general well-being steadily improved, and her CK levels remained within normal limits. Consequently, her prednisone was tapered quickly without issue. Since her condition had improved following discontinuation of semaglutide and cost considerations, she did not undergo further IVIG administration apart from the original inpatient course.

At her most recent outpatient visit, which occurred 10 months after her first assessment and commencement of therapy, she had returned to her pre-illness level of function and had been free from immunosuppressive treatment for 8 months.

Investigations:

Her laboratory evaluation revealed an elevated creatine kinase (CK) of 1237 U/L upon assessment, with a maximum value of 3992 U/L recorded one week prior at the referring institution. Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) was within the reference range. Aspartate aminotransferase (AST) measured 191 U/L and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) was 165 U/L, findings that occurred in the context of raised CK and a past medical history of hepatic steatosis. Autoantibody testing was significant for negative results for anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-Sjogren’s syndrome type A antibodies (anti-SSA), anti-Smith antibodies, anti-U1 ribonucleoprotein (anti-U1 RNP), anti-double-stranded DNA, anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA), and anti-mitochondrial antibodies. Complement levels were within normal limits, renal function was unremarkable, and there was no evidence of cytopenias. The myositis antibody panel was negative for signal recognition particle (SRP), 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-coenzyme A reductase (HMGCR), as well as Jo-1, Pl-7, Pl-12, EJ, OJ, Mi-2, melanoma differentiation-associated gene 5 (MDA-5), transcription intermediary factor 1-gamma (TIF1-y), nuclear matrix protein 2 (NXP-2), and cytosolic 5'-nucleotidase 1A (cN-1A) autoantibodies. She did show a low-titer positive result for SS-B autoantibody at 2.4 (normal <1.0). Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was elevated at 22 mm/hour, with C-reactive protein (CRP) also increased at 22.8 mg/L. Of note, high-sensitivity troponin (HS-Troponin) was mildly elevated at 62 pg/mL (normal ≤53 pg/mL), and pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (proBNP) was 4358 pg/mL (normal range: 0–125 pg/mL).

Imaging studies performed included MRI scans of both femurs, echocardiogram, cardiac MRI, liver ultrasound with Doppler, high-resolution chest CT, and abdominal MRI. The MRI of the bilateral femurs demonstrated mild, symmetric oedema in both muscles, most pronounced in the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps, as well as subcutaneous oedema localized to the lateral thighs (see figure 2). Echocardiography showed a left ventricular ejection fraction estimated at 65%–70% and right ventricular systolic pressure measured at 46 mm Hg. Cardiac MRI revealed mild hypokinesis affecting the mid-anteroseptal and anterior walls, which was associated with myocardial oedema; there was no obvious late gadolinium enhancement. These findings may indicate myocardial inflammation or early myocarditis, or possibly focal stress-induced cardiomyopathy. There was an absence of marked myocardial scarring or infarction and no significant valvular abnormality. Liver ultrasound with Doppler showed hepatosteatosis. High-resolution CT scan of the chest found no evidence of interstitial lung disease or lymphadenopathy. Abdominal MRI indicated steatotic hepatomegaly but was otherwise unremarkable and revealed no lymphadenopathy.

Additional advanced diagnostic tests were performed, including a second liver biopsy and a biopsy of the thigh musculature. The liver biopsy, obtained during hospitalization, revealed multiple samples of hepatic tissue displaying both small and large droplet macrovesicular steatosis affecting around 70% of the examined liver parenchyma, with no notable portal inflammation or interface hepatitis. These results aligned with her known history of hepatosteatosis, indicating a mild worsening of her chronic liver condition, classified as NAFLD Activity Score grade 5/8, stage 1. Examination of the right vastus lateralis muscle biopsy showed scattered necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers (figure 3A,B); immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated positive staining for acid phosphatase (figure 3D) and cluster of Differentiation-68 (CD68), a macrophage marker, accompanied by myophagocytosis (figure 4B). Staining for membrane attack complex was positive in both necrotic and non-necrotic muscle fibers (figure 4C), indicative of an inflammatory response. There were also rare CD3-positive cells, marking T lymphocytes (figure 4A), along with perivascular inflammatory cell infiltration (figure 3C). Overall, these muscle biopsy findings supported a diagnosis of IMNM.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient developed a pronounced and rapid response following her first and only administration of semaglutide, raising suspicion for drug intolerance. Her presenting symptoms were gastrointestinal disturbances and acute renal failure, believed to be due to hypovolaemia. At initial assessment, creatine kinase was only mildly raised, not consistent with rhabdomyolysis. However, her condition deteriorated despite discontinuing semaglutide, with progressive weakness and a twelvefold elevation in CK over four months post-exposure, indicating the development of an immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) likely related to semaglutide. Additional support for this association is provided by biopsy findings typical for drug-induced IMNM, absence of alternative features for connective tissue disease, and sustained clinical improvement following cessation of semaglutide and initiation of immunosuppressive therapy.

It is significant that elevated transaminase levels were present before she began taking semaglutide, a phenomenon observed in myopathies. Nevertheless, she was diagnosed with hepatosteatosis via biopsy three years prior to semaglutide initiation, and her mildly increased AST and ALT values remained even after normalization of CK levels. Therefore, her persistent transaminase elevation is most likely attributable to chronic liver pathology, rather than muscle injury. Additionally, her cardiac assessment did not clearly indicate ongoing myocarditis. In this clinical context, stress-induced myopathy could also be considered, particularly given her swift improvement.

Detection of SS-B antibody positivity in the absence of both ANA and SS-A was unexpected. To our knowledge, no literature exists documenting cases of IMNM associated with isolated SS-B antibody positivity and negative ANA and SS-A. Regrettably, a CK level prior to semaglutide therapy was not available, making it impossible to completely rule out an underlying, low-grade autoimmune IMNM before semaglutide initiation. Nevertheless, the twelvefold increase in CK and the progressive proximal muscle weakness that emerged following semaglutide administration suggest a greater likelihood of medication-induced IMNM. Additionally, the patient’s severe IMNM responded to a brief course of corticosteroids and IVIG combined with discontinuation of semaglutide. This pattern is consistent with drug-induced IMNM, which typically improves with short-term immunosuppressive treatment and cessation of the causative agent, unlike idiopathic IMNM that generally necessitates sustained and aggressive immunosuppressive therapy.

Treatment:

She received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for 5 days, then transitioned to oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg per day. Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at 2 g/kg, divided over 4 days, given her ongoing weakness and pronounced symptoms. Subsequent outpatient follow-up visits showed ongoing enhancement of her functional capacity and overall condition, with creatine kinase (CK) values remaining within normal limits throughout. As a result, her prednisone was able to be tapered rapidly over a 2-month period. Taking into account both financial factors and her maintained improvement off semaglutide, no further IVIG was given beyond the initial regimen during her hospital admission.

Outcome and follow-up:

At her most recent outpatient follow-up, conducted 10 months after beginning treatment, she had returned to her previous level of functioning and had been without immunosuppressive therapy for 7 months. Her CK values remained stable and within normal limits throughout the post-hospital course and subsequent immunosuppressive management. Repeat imaging with echocardiography and cardiac MRI demonstrated resolution of previous cardiac anomalies. Additionally, she did not have any further episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation.

Discussion:

GLP-1 receptor agonists, including semaglutide, have seen increased use and have shown efficacy in treating metabolic conditions such as obesity and type 2 diabetes. Gastrointestinal adverse events are common; however, these agents may also contribute to reduced muscle mass and, in rare instances, rhabdomyolysis. In this particular case, the patient's presentation aligns more closely with IMNM rather than rhabdomyolysis. After a single dose of semaglutide, CK concentrations rose by a factor of 12 over 4 months, whereas a normalization of CK within weeks would be anticipated if semaglutide-induced rhabdomyolysis were responsible, similar to other cases of drug-related rhabdomyolysis. It is worth highlighting that a previously published rhabdomyolysis case related to tirzepatide exhibited a typical course: the individual had a pronounced acute CK increase (CK=57 014 at presentation) and rapid resolution following cessation of the drug and hydration, all within several days and without immunosuppressive treatment.

Based on the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) Causality Categories and Criteria, the association between semaglutide and IMNM in our patient is considered probable, as the timing is appropriate, alternate causes including other diseases or drugs appear unlikely, and there was a clinically plausible improvement after discontinuation. Because of the seriousness of the adverse effect linked to semaglutide, rechallenging the patient would be unethical. Nonetheless, we recognize that a definite causal relationship cannot be established in this instance. With semaglutide use rising rapidly, the purpose of this report is to alert clinicians to this possible adverse event and promote robust pharmacovigilance. Further research will determine whether this association reflects causality or mere coincidence.

The underlying factors contributing to our patient’s susceptibility to this pronounced reaction from semaglutide are still uncertain. As GLP-1 receptor agonists continue to gain widespread use, identifying predisposition through future investigations will be crucial should similar cases with temporal correlation to semaglutide use emerge.
