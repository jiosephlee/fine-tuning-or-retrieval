Title: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

Brian Lam, Eleni Tiniakou, Xinhai Robert Zhang, Shervin Assassi

Abstract:

Summary: This case concerns a woman in her 60s who developed immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) following administration of semaglutide. Over a period of four months, she experienced worsening dysphagia, dysphonia, proximal muscle weakness, and elevated creatine kinase. Her initial symptom onset followed a single dose of semaglutide. Diagnostic evaluation identified necrotising myopathy, characterized by inflammatory infiltrates observed on muscle biopsy. Treatment with corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) resulted in clinical improvement. Ultimately, she regained her prior level of function, remaining off immunosuppressive therapy for several months, while abstaining from further use of glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists. This case constitutes the first reported instance of IMNM likely linked to GLP-1 receptor agonist exposure, showing favorable response to brief immunosuppression and IVIG.

Background:

In recent years, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have been widely prescribed for metabolic conditions such as type 2 diabetes and obesity. Adverse reactions attributed to this drug class have included reductions in skeletal muscle mass. A recent case report highlighted the occurrence of rhabdomyolysis with necrotising components in association with tirzepatide, a frequently used GLP-1 receptor agonist. The patient involved experienced a significant rise in creatine kinase (CK), reaching values in the thousands while on therapy. After cessation of the drug, CK levels began to decline within a few days, supporting the diagnosis of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis.

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) is a unique form of muscle disease that presents with severe weakness of the proximal muscles, showing necrotic muscle fibers and minimal lymphocyte infiltration upon histological examination. The autoantibodies anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (SRP) are strongly linked to IMNM. Notably, statin therapy, which is frequently prescribed for metabolic disorders, is closely correlated with the presence of HMGCR autoantibodies. Historically, several patients on statins who exhibited signs of necrotising myopathy were often misclassified as having either toxic myopathy or polymyositis prior to the recognition of the association with HMGCR autoantibodies. In these cases, many individuals continued to have muscle symptoms and elevated creatine kinase (CK) levels even after statin discontinuation. The resolution of symptoms following immunosuppressive treatment provided evidence of an autoimmune etiology, eventually resulting in the identification of HMGCR autoantibodies as a defining feature of this condition. It should be recognized, however, that approximately one third of IMNM patients do not test positive for these autoantibodies. The absence of autoantibody detection in this subgroup complicates diagnosis and indicates that additional related autoantibodies and underlying triggers remain undiscovered.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 60s with a medical history notable for obesity, hepatic steatosis, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and a distant history of deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism presented with gradually worsening proximal muscle weakness, difficulty swallowing, and changes in her voice.

She had previously enjoyed good health and was completely independent with her daily routines. Three years before the onset of her current symptoms, she underwent outpatient assessment due to elevated liver transaminases. At that point, laboratory tests revealed mild increases in aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT), both roughly two times above the normal range, with ALT being higher than AST. Other labs, including alkaline phosphatase, albumin, bilirubin, and platelets, were normal. She reported no alcohol consumption and was not using hepatotoxic medications. Abdominal ultrasound demonstrated enlarged liver and diffusely increased echogenicity suggestive of hepatic steatosis. Testing for viral hepatitis, anti-nuclear, anti-mitochondrial, anti-smooth muscle, and liver-kidney microsomal antibodies yielded negative results. Liver biopsy revealed steatohepatitis consistent with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), with a NAFLD Activity Score of 4 out of 8 and stage 0 disease. Recommendations included adhering to a calorie-restricted diet and engaging in regular physical activity.

Due to ongoing hepatic steatosis and obesity 2.5 years after being diagnosed with NAFLD, semaglutide therapy was commenced. She received her initial dose of semaglutide (0.25 mg subcutaneous), and developed nausea and vomiting within hours. These symptoms continued, leading to hospital admission 2 days later because of persistent nausea, vomiting, inadequate oral intake, dyspnoea, acute kidney injury, and marked metabolic acidosis. Treatment consisted of intravenous fluids and bicarbonate, in addition to anti-emetics. She required a short transfer to the intensive care unit for non-invasive ventilation. With intravenous hydration, renal function improved, and neither haemofiltration nor haemodialysis was necessary. Her admission creatine kinase (CK) was elevated at 337 U/L. She exhibited no other symptoms such as rash, myalgia, or dark urine. During the same hospital stay, she developed episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, although her serum troponin was within normal limits. She was discharged after 5 days; by discharge, gastrointestinal complaints had resolved but she remained deconditioned. Over the ensuing 4 months, she reported progressively worsening weakness. She stated that she had never taken statins, nor consumed mushrooms, red yeast rice, or any other dietary supplements associated with IMNM. She also denied any significant family history of myopathy, recent strenuous or prolonged exercise, or use of alcohol or illicit drugs.

Four months after her first and only administration of semaglutide, her condition continued to worsen. She lost the ability to stand or sit upright unaided and began experiencing dysphagia with both liquids and solids for several days. As a result, she sought care at an external hospital, where laboratory studies revealed her creatine kinase (CK) was elevated to 3992 U/L (reference range: 12–191 U/L) upon arrival. Neurology was consulted via telemedicine and she received 1000 mg of intravenous methylprednisolone daily over 5 days. Subsequently, she was transferred to a tertiary care facility for assessment by rheumatology. Upon arrival, she was afebrile, had normal blood pressure, and appeared comfortable with no acute distress. Examination showed pronounced muscular weakness, predominantly affecting her proximal muscle groups (see table 1). No rashes, skin abnormalities, pulmonary crackles, synovitis, oedema, dryness of mucous membranes, oral ulcers, or additional neurologic deficits were observed.

After administration of pulse methylprednisolone, she began oral prednisone at a dosage of 1 mg/kg per day. Due to ongoing muscle weakness and significant disease manifestations, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at a total dose of 2 g/kg over the span of 4 days. Her creatine kinase (CK) levels showed substantial improvement within 2 weeks (see figure 1). Her initial hospitalization lasted nearly 4 weeks before discharge. She was readmitted 12 days later, staying for 10 days as a result of septic shock caused by a pseudomonal urinary tract infection, during which her CK remained within normal limits.

She underwent multiple outpatient follow-up visits, during which her functional capacity and general health continued to improve, and her CK levels remained within normal limits. As a result, prednisone tapering was achieved rapidly. She did not receive further IVIG beyond the initial course administered during hospitalization, owing both to financial constraints and the sustained improvement observed after discontinuing semaglutide.

Her most recent outpatient assessment occurred 10 months following her initial presentation and start of treatment, at which point she had returned to her baseline level of function and had been free from immunosuppressive therapy for 8 months.

Investigations:

Her laboratory evaluation revealed a creatine kinase (CK) level of 1237 U/L at presentation, with a documented peak of 3992 U/L one week earlier at the referring facility. Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) was within normal parameters. Aminotransferases were elevated, with an AST of 191 U/L and ALT of 165 U/L, observed in the context of raised serum CK and prior diagnosis of hepatic steatosis. The autoantibody assessment demonstrated negativity for antinuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-SSA (anti-Sjögren’s syndrome antibodies type A), anti-Smith, anti-U1 RNP, anti-double-stranded DNA, antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), and anti-mitochondrial antibodies. Complement fractions and creatinine remained within normal reference ranges, and there was no evidence of cytopenia. A comprehensive myositis panel showed no reactivity to SRP, HMGCR, Jo-1, Pl-7, Pl-12, EJ, OJ, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-y, NXP-2, or cN-1A autoantibodies. However, SS-B autoantibody was detected at a low positive titer of 2.4 (normal <1.0). Markers of inflammation were increased, with an erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) of 22 mm/hour and C-reactive protein (CRP) at 22.8 mg/L. Additionally, high-sensitivity troponin was measured at 62 pg/mL (normal ≤53 pg/mL) and proBNP was significantly raised at 4358 pg/mL (normal: 0–125 pg/mL).

The patient's imaging workup included bilateral femur MRI, echocardiography, cardiac MRI, Doppler liver ultrasound, high-resolution chest CT, and abdominal MRI. The bilateral femur MRI showed mild, symmetrical edema in both muscles, primarily affecting the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps, in addition to subcutaneous edema along the lateral surfaces of both thighs (see figure 2). Echocardiography found the left ventricular ejection fraction to be approximately 65%–70%, with a right ventricular systolic pressure measured at 46 mm Hg. Cardiac MRI revealed mild hypokinesia in the mid-anteroseptal and anterior walls, accompanied by myocardial edema and absence of clear late gadolinium enhancement. These findings may be indicative of myocardial inflammation or early myocarditis, or alternatively, focal stress-related cardiomyopathy. There was no evidence of major myocardial scarring, infarct, or noteworthy valvular abnormality. Doppler liver ultrasound indicated fatty infiltration of the liver. High-resolution chest CT did not demonstrate interstitial lung disease or lymphadenopathy. Abdominal MRI showed fatty enlargement of the liver, but otherwise was unremarkable and without lymphadenopathy.

Comprehensive evaluation included a second liver biopsy and a biopsy of the thigh musculature. The liver biopsy, undertaken during the current hospitalization, revealed several samples of hepatic tissue exhibiting both small and large droplet macrovesicular steatosis, affecting about 70% of the examined hepatic parenchyma, without marked portal inflammation or evidence of interface hepatitis. This histopathology corresponded with the patient’s established diagnosis of hepatosteatosis and indicated mild advancement to a NAFLD Activity Score of 5 out of 8, stage 1. The right vastus lateralis muscle biopsy showed scattered necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers (figure 3A, B), and immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated acid phosphatase (figure 3D) as well as cluster of Differentiation-68 (CD68), indicative of macrophages, with features of myophagocytosis (figure 4B). In addition, staining for membrane attack complex was positive in both necrotic and non-necrotic muscle fibers (figure 4C), suggesting an inflammatory process. The specimen also revealed infrequent CD3-positive cells—markers of T lymphocytes (figure 4A)—and perivascular infiltration by inflammatory cells (figure 3C). The muscle biopsy results supported a diagnosis consistent with IMNM.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient exhibited an immediate and pronounced adverse response to her first and only administration of semaglutide, indicating intolerance. She initially developed gastrointestinal symptoms along with renal failure, which was thought to be due to hypovolemia. At first assessment, creatine kinase (CK) was only slightly increased and not in the range typically seen in rhabdomyolysis; however, persistent clinical decline after stopping semaglutide, the pattern of muscle weakness, and a subsequent 12-fold rise in CK over four months post-semaglutide are consistent with an immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) potentially linked to semaglutide. Additionally, biopsy findings suggestive of drug-related IMNM, the absence of other connective tissue disease indicators, and ongoing clinical improvement following the discontinuation of semaglutide and initiation of immunosuppressive therapy lend further support to this association.

Of interest, she exhibited elevated transaminases before commencing semaglutide, a feature sometimes observed in myopathies. Nevertheless, she was diagnosed with hepatosteatosis via biopsy three years before starting semaglutide, and her mildly increased AST/ALT levels continued even after CK returned to normal. Therefore, it appears her chronic transaminase elevations are hepatic in nature rather than attributable to muscle injury. Additionally, her cardiac assessments did not clearly indicate ongoing myocarditis. In this context, stress-induced myopathy is also a plausible explanation, especially considering her swift improvement.

The finding of SS-B antibody positivity while ANA and SS-A remained negative was unusual. To date, there are no documented cases detailing isolated SS-B positivity with negative ANA and SS-A resulting in IMNM. We were not able to obtain a creatine kinase (CK) value preceding semaglutide therapy, thus making it impossible to definitively rule out subclinical autoimmune IMNM prior to semaglutide initiation. Nevertheless, the twelve-fold increase in CK and the gradual onset of proximal muscle weakness following introduction of semaglutide suggest a higher probability that this instance of IMNM was drug-induced. Moreover, the patient’s significant IMNM was reversed after a brief course of corticosteroids and IVIG, accompanied by discontinuation of semaglutide. This clinical pattern is more indicative of medication-associated IMNM, which can respond to short-term immunosuppressive treatment after removal of the causative drug, rather than idiopathic IMNM, which frequently necessitates sustained, intensive immunosuppressive management.

Treatment:

The patient received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for five days, after which she was started on oral prednisone at a daily dose of 1 mg/kg. Owing to ongoing muscle weakness and pronounced clinical manifestations, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at 2 g/kg, divided across four days. She was monitored regularly in outpatient clinics, during which she continued to demonstrate progressive recovery in both function and general well-being, with creatine kinase levels remaining consistently within normal limits. Based on her sustained improvement, prednisone tapering was accomplished successfully and relatively rapidly within two months. Considering both the financial implications and her ongoing recovery following cessation of semaglutide, no further IVIG therapy was provided after the initial inpatient treatment.

Outcome and follow-up:

At her most recent outpatient follow-up, which occurred 10 months after starting therapy and initial assessment, she had returned to her previous level of function and had been free from immunosuppressive therapy for 7 months. Her creatine kinase values stayed within normal limits after her hospitalization and immunosuppressive treatment. Subsequent echocardiogram and cardiac MRI demonstrated resolution of previously noted abnormalities. Furthermore, she did not have any further episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation.

Discussion:

GLP-1 receptor agonists like semaglutide are increasingly prescribed and have shown efficacy in treating metabolic conditions such as obesity and type 2 diabetes. While gastrointestinal side effects are frequently reported, there is also evidence linking these agents to reduced muscle mass and, in some cases, rhabdomyolysis. In this particular patient, however, the progression of symptoms aligns more with IMNM than with rhabdomyolysis. The patient’s CK concentrations rose twelvefold over four months after a single dose of semaglutide, which differs from the expected normalization of CK within a few weeks typically observed in cases of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis caused by direct toxicity. For example, a previously documented rhabdomyolysis case related to tirzepatide displayed the classic presentation: a significant acute CK spike (CK=57 014 upon admission), followed by rapid improvement after discontinuing the medication and administering hydration, without requiring immunosuppressive therapy.

Applying the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) Causality Categories and Criteria, the association between semaglutide administration and IMNM in our patient is judged as probable. This assessment is based on the appropriate temporal sequence, the improbability of alternative etiologies including other diseases or medications, and the favorable clinical outcome following cessation of semaglutide. Re-exposure to the drug would not be ethical, given the severity of the adverse reaction observed. Nonetheless, we recognize that definitive causality cannot be established in this instance. Due to the rapidly increasing use of semaglutide, the purpose of this report is to alert clinicians to this possible adverse event and encourage vigilant pharmacovigilance. Further research will be necessary to determine whether this association is causal or merely coincidental.

The specific factors that may have rendered our patient susceptible to this severe reaction to semaglutide are still unknown. As GLP-1 receptor agonists gain widespread adoption, investigating predisposing elements will be increasingly important, particularly if additional cases temporally linked to semaglutide exposure emerge.
