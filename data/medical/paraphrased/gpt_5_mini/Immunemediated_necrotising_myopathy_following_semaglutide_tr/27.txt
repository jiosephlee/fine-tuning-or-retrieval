Title: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

Brian Lam, Eleni Tiniakou, Xinhai Robert Zhang, Shervin Assassi

Abstract:

Summary: This case details a woman in her 60s who developed immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) following administration of semaglutide. Over a period of four months, she experienced worsening dysphagia, dysphonia, proximal muscle weakness, and elevated creatine kinase. These manifestations began after just one dose of semaglutide. Diagnostic evaluation revealed necrotising myopathy with an accompanying inflammatory infiltrate on muscle biopsy. The patient responded to treatment with corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), achieving gradual recovery and restoration of her baseline functional abilities. She remained off immunosuppressive therapy for several months, avoiding glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists. This represents the first documented case of IMNM likely associated with GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy, with improvement following brief immunosuppression and IVIG.

Background:

Over the past few years, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have become widely utilized in managing metabolic conditions such as type 2 diabetes and obesity. Adverse effects attributed to this drug class include reported reductions in skeletal muscle mass. A case report recently documented rhabdomyolysis with necrotising characteristics linked to tirzepatide, another frequently prescribed GLP-1 receptor agonist. In that instance, the patient exhibited a markedly elevated creatine kinase (CK) in the thousands during therapy, and this elevation began to decline within several days following cessation of the medication, aligning with a diagnosis of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis.

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) represents a unique form of myopathy distinguished by rapid-onset proximal muscle weakness, accompanied histologically by necrotic muscle fibers and minimal lymphocytic infiltration. Two autoantibodies, anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (SRP), show strong correlation with IMNM. Statins, frequently prescribed for metabolic conditions, have a significant association with the presence of HMGCR autoantibodies. Prior to the recognition of this association, several individuals on statins developed necrotising myopathy, often initially misclassified as toxic myopathy or polymyositis. In such instances, patients commonly experienced ongoing muscle weakness and elevated creatine kinase levels, which did not resolve upon discontinuation of statin therapy. The subsequent response to immunosuppressive treatment indicated an autoimmune process, ultimately leading to the identification of HMGCR autoantibodies as a key feature of the condition. It is important to note that approximately one third of IMNM cases do not exhibit identifiable autoantibodies. The absence of detectable autoantibodies in these patients not only increases the complexity of diagnosis but also implies the existence of additional yet-undetermined autoantibodies and triggers.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 60s, whose past medical history includes obesity, hepatosteatosis, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and a previous episode of deep venous thrombosis/pulmonary embolism, presented with gradually worsening proximal muscle weakness, difficulties swallowing, and voice changes.

Prior to this decline, she had enjoyed good health and was entirely self-sufficient in daily activities. Three years before this onset, she was being assessed in the outpatient setting due to elevated liver transaminases. At that point, both aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels were mildly increased, roughly two times above the upper reference limit, with ALT exceeding AST. Levels of alkaline phosphatase, albumin, bilirubin, and platelet count were all within normal parameters. The patient reported no alcohol intake and was not using medications associated with hepatotoxicity. An abdominal ultrasound revealed an enlarged liver with uniformly increased echogenicity, suggesting hepatic steatosis. Laboratory results for viral hepatitis serologies, anti-nuclear, anti-mitochondrial, anti-smooth muscle, and liver-kidney microsomal antibodies were negative. Histological evaluation of a liver biopsy demonstrated steatohepatitis compatible with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) and an NAFLD Activity Score of 4/8, stage 0. She was counseled to adopt a calorie-restricted diet and engage in regular physical activity.

Given ongoing hepatic steatosis and obesity 2.5 years post-NAFLD diagnosis, semaglutide therapy was begun. Shortly after administration of her initial 0.25 mg subcutaneous dose, she developed nausea and vomiting. These symptoms did not abate, leading to hospital admission 2 days later with complaints of nausea, vomiting, reduced oral intake, dyspnea, acute renal insufficiency, and marked metabolic acidosis. Management included intravenous fluids, bicarbonate, and anti-emetic therapy. She required a short stay in the intensive care unit to receive non-invasive ventilation. Her renal function subsequently recovered following hydration alone, without need for haemofiltration or dialysis. Upon admission, creatine kinase was elevated at 337 U/L, but she exhibited no rash, myalgia, or dark urine. During this admission, paroxysmal atrial fibrillation developed, although serum troponin remained within normal limits. She was discharged after 5 days. Gastrointestinal symptoms resolved at discharge, but she remained physically deconditioned. Over the next 4 months, she reported progressive weakness. She stated no history of statin use, nor significant intake of supplements linked to IMNM, including mushrooms or red yeast rice, or other nutritional supplements. She also denied any family history of myopathy, recent strenuous or prolonged exercise, or use of alcohol or illicit drugs.

Four months following her first and only administration of semaglutide, her condition progressively worsened. She became unable to maintain a standing or sitting posture without support and reported difficulties with swallowing both liquids and solids over several days. This led her to seek care at an external hospital, where laboratory evaluation revealed a creatine kinase (CK) level of 3992 U/L (reference range: 12–191 U/L) upon admission. She received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for 5 days, managed remotely with neurology input via telemedicine. Subsequently, she was moved to a tertiary care facility for rheumatologic assessment. At the time of evaluation, she was afebrile, with normal blood pressure, and displayed no signs of distress. Physical assessment identified pronounced muscle weakness, particularly affecting the proximal muscle groups (see table 1). The examination did not show any rashes, dermatologic abnormalities, pulmonary crackles, synovitis, edema, sicca symptoms, oral ulcers, or additional neurological impairments.

After receiving pulse methylprednisolone, the patient was initiated on daily oral prednisone at a dose of 1 mg/kg. Because her weakness persisted and symptoms remained severe, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at 2 g/kg divided over four days. Over two weeks, her CK levels showed significant improvement (figure 1). She was discharged following an inpatient stay of almost four weeks. Twelve days post-discharge, she was readmitted for ten days with septic shock secondary to a pseudomonal urinary tract infection, during which her CK levels were found to be within normal range.

She underwent multiple outpatient follow-up visits, during which she showed ongoing enhancement in functional capacity and general well-being, accompanied by consistently normal creatine kinase values. Consequently, she was quickly tapered off prednisone. Given both financial constraints and marked progress after discontinuing semaglutide, no further IVIG infusions were administered beyond her original inpatient regimen.

Her most recent outpatient assessment occurred 10 months following her initial presentation and start of therapy, at which point she had returned to her baseline functional level, remaining off immunosuppressive treatment for 8 months.

Investigations:

Her laboratory evaluation revealed a creatine kinase (CK) level of 1237 U/L at presentation, with a maximum value of 3992 U/L documented one week earlier at the referring facility. Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) was found to be within normal reference ranges. Aspartate aminotransferase (AST) was measured at 191 U/L and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) at 165 U/L, which was interpreted in the context of increased serum CK and known fatty liver disease. Autoantibody assessment showed negative results for antinuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-Sjogren’s syndrome type A (anti-SSA), anti-Smith, anti-U1 ribonucleoprotein (anti-U1 RNP), anti-double-stranded DNA, ANCA, and anti-mitochondrial antibodies. Complement levels and creatinine were both within normal limits, and there was no evidence of cytopenias. The myositis antibody panel did not detect antibodies against SRP, HMGCR, Jo-1, Pl-7, Pl-12, EJ, OJ, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-y, NXP-2, or cN-1A. The only positive autoantibody was a low-titre SS-B, with a value of 2.4 (reference <1.0). Inflammatory markers included an erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) of 22 mm/hour and a C-reactive protein level of 22.8 mg/L. Additionally, high-sensitivity troponin (HS-Troponin) was elevated at 62 pg/mL (reference ≤53 pg/mL), and the pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (proBNP) was measured at 4358 pg/mL (normal 0–125 pg/mL).

Key imaging studies comprised MRI scans of both femurs, echocardiogram, cardiac MRI, liver ultrasound with Doppler, high-resolution chest CT, and abdominal MRI. The MRI of the femurs identified mild, symmetric muscular edema, predominantly affecting the proximal portions of the vastus lateralis and the distal quadriceps bilaterally, along with subcutaneous edema along the lateral regions of both thighs (figure 2). Echocardiographic assessment showed a left ventricular ejection fraction estimated at 65%–70%, and a right ventricular systolic pressure measured at 46 mm Hg. Cardiac MRI revealed mild hypokinesis involving the mid-anteroseptal and anterior cardiac walls with accompanying myocardial edema, but did not demonstrate definite late gadolinium enhancement; findings were interpreted as possibly attributable to myocardial inflammation or early myocarditis, or as focal stress-related cardiomyopathy. There was no evidence for significant myocardial scarring, infarction, or marked valvular disease. Liver ultrasound with Doppler indicated the presence of hepatosteatosis. High-resolution chest CT excluded both interstitial lung disease and lymphadenopathy. MRI of the abdomen showed steatotic hepatomegaly, with otherwise normal findings and absence of lymphadenopathy.

Further diagnostic evaluation included repeating the liver biopsy and obtaining a biopsy from the thigh muscle. The liver biopsy carried out during this hospitalization revealed several fragments of liver tissue showing macrovesicular steatosis with both small and large fat droplets, occupying roughly 70% of the examined hepatic parenchyma. There was no evidence of significant portal inflammation or interface hepatitis. This pathology aligned with her known hepatosteatosis, indicating a mild worsening to NAFLD Activity Score of 5 out of 8 and stage 1 disease. Biopsy of the right vastus lateralis muscle showed scattered necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers (figure 3A,B). Immunohistochemical analysis detected acid phosphatase (figure 3D) as well as cluster of Differentiation-68 (CD68)—a marker of macrophages—and myophagocytosis (figure 4B). Positive membrane attack complex staining was observed on both necrotic and non-necrotic muscle fibers (figure 4C), signifying an inflammatory reaction. Additionally, a small number of CD3-positive T cells (figure 4A) and perivascular inflammatory cells (figure 3C) were found. These histologic features in the muscle biopsy supported a diagnosis of IMNM.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient exhibited a pronounced and immediate adverse response following her first and only administration of semaglutide, indicating intolerance. She initially developed gastrointestinal side effects as well as renal failure presumed to be due to hypovolemia. At the time of her first assessment, her CK was only mildly increased, which was not consistent with rhabdomyolysis. However, her condition continued to worsen despite stopping semaglutide, and the pattern of her weakness, combined with a twelvefold rise in CK over four months following semaglutide exposure, indicate an IMNM likely linked to semaglutide. Additionally, biopsy findings typical of drug-induced IMNM, absence of other clinical features pointing to an underlying connective tissue disorder, and ongoing clinical improvement after both initial immunosuppressive therapy and discontinuation of semaglutide further reinforce this connection.

Of note, the patient exhibited elevated transaminase levels before initiating semaglutide, a finding observed in certain myopathies. Nonetheless, a liver biopsy conducted three years prior to starting semaglutide confirmed hepatosteatosis, and the mild increases in AST and ALT remained following normalization of CK levels. Therefore, the persistent transaminase elevation is most likely attributable to hepatic etiology rather than muscle injury. Additionally, her cardiac evaluation did not yield definitive evidence for ongoing myocarditis. In this clinical context, stress-related myopathy is also plausible, particularly considering her swift improvement.

The detection of SS-B antibody positivity, accompanied by negative ANA and SS-A results, was unanticipated. To date, no literature documents isolated SS-B antibody presence in the absence of ANA and SS-A as an antecedent to IMNM. Regrettably, we lacked access to CK measurements preceding the administration of semaglutide, making it impossible to categorically rule out an underlying autoimmune IMNM prior to starting semaglutide. Nonetheless, the twelvefold elevation in CK coupled with worsening proximal muscle weakness following semaglutide initiation supports the likelihood of this IMNM episode being associated with medication use. Furthermore, the patient experienced resolution of pronounced IMNM after a brief period of corticosteroids and IVIG, together with discontinuation of semaglutide. This pattern of recovery is characteristic of drug-induced IMNM, where short-term immunosuppression and cessation of the inciting medication often lead to improvement, in contrast to idiopathic IMNM, which typically necessitates long-term and aggressive immunosuppressive interventions.

Treatment:

She received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for five days, after which she was transitioned to oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg per day. IVIG was administered at a total dose of 2 g/kg, divided across four days, owing to her ongoing weakness and pronounced clinical manifestations. Subsequent outpatient follow-up visits demonstrated consistent gains in her functional ability and overall health, with creatine kinase levels remaining within normal range. Consequently, her prednisone was tapered off quickly over a two-month period. Because of financial considerations and her continued progress after discontinuing semaglutide, no further IVIG was provided beyond the initial regimen during her hospitalization.

Outcome and follow-up:

At her most recent outpatient follow-up, which occurred 10 months after starting therapy, she had returned to her baseline level of functioning and had been free from immunosuppressive medications for 7 months. Her creatine kinase values stayed within normal limits after hospitalization and immunosuppressive treatment. Subsequent echocardiography and cardiac MRI demonstrated resolution of the previously observed abnormalities. Additionally, there was no recurrence of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation.

Discussion:

GLP-1 receptor agonists, including semaglutide, are increasingly prescribed and have proven efficacy in managing metabolic conditions such as obesity and type 2 diabetes. Although gastrointestinal symptoms are frequent side effects, these agents may also contribute to reduced muscle mass and, in some instances, rhabdomyolysis. In this case, the patient’s presentation aligns more closely with IMNM rather than rhabdomyolysis. The CK concentration rose twelvefold over a four-month period after a single administration of semaglutide, whereas CK would be expected to return to normal within weeks if semaglutide-induced toxicity were responsible, as observed with other forms of medication-induced rhabdomyolysis. Importantly, a previously described case of rhabdomyolysis related to tirzepatide exemplified the classical pattern: the patient developed a significant acute CK elevation (CK=57 014 at presentation) and experienced swift improvement following drug discontinuation and hydration over several days, without requiring immunosuppressive therapy.

According to the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) Causality Categories and Criteria, a probable association exists between semaglutide administration and the development of IMNM in this patient, as the temporal correlation is appropriate, alternative etiologies including other diseases and medications are unlikely, and discontinuing semaglutide resulted in a plausible clinical improvement. A rechallenge was not considered, owing to the severity of the adverse event, and would be unethical. Nonetheless, we recognize that definitive causality cannot be established in this instance. Given the rapidly increasing use of semaglutide, this case serves to inform clinicians of this possible adverse effect and emphasizes the importance of continued pharmacovigilance. Further investigation is required to determine if this relationship is causative or merely coincidental.

Additionally, the underlying factors that may have made our patient susceptible to such a severe reaction to semaglutide remain unknown. With the expanding use of GLP-1 receptor agonists, research into risk factors will be critical, especially if other cases with a similar temporal association to semaglutide arise in the future.
