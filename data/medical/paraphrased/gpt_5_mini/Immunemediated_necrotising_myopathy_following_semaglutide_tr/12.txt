Title: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

Brian Lam, Eleni Tiniakou, Xinhai Robert Zhang, Shervin Assassi

Abstract:

Summary: This case concerns a woman in her 60s who developed immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) following administration of semaglutide. Over a four-month period, she experienced progressively worsening dysphagia, dysphonia, proximal muscle weakness, and elevated creatine kinase levels. These symptoms emerged after a single semaglutide dose. Diagnostic evaluation demonstrated findings consistent with necrotising myopathy, including inflammatory cell infiltrate on muscle biopsy. She was treated with immunosuppressive therapy comprising corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), resulting in clinical improvement. Ultimately, she regained her pre-illness level of function and remained off immunosuppression for several months while continuing to avoid glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists. This represents the first documented instance of IMNM likely linked to GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy, with apparent benefit from brief immunosuppression and IVIG.

Background:

Over the past several years, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have become widely utilized in the management of metabolic conditions such as type 2 diabetes and obesity. Although various adverse reactions have been reported for this drug class, reductions in skeletal muscle mass have been documented. A recent published case detailed the occurrence of rhabdomyolysis with necrotizing characteristics in association with tirzepatide, a GLP-1 receptor agonist in frequent clinical use. In that instance, the patient experienced creatine kinase (CK) levels rising to several thousand units while receiving the medication; notably, CK concentrations began to decrease within several days after stopping the drug, supporting a diagnosis of drug-related rhabdomyolysis.

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) represents a unique type of myopathy, marked by rapidly progressive proximal muscle weakness, histological evidence of necrotic muscle fibers, and minimal lymphocytic infiltration. IMNM is strongly linked to the presence of anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (SRP) autoantibodies. Notably, statin medications—commonly prescribed for metabolic diseases—have a well-established association with HMGCR antibody positivity. Before the connection between HMGCR antibodies and IMNM was recognized, numerous patients who exhibited signs of necrotising myopathy following statin therapy were frequently misclassified as having toxic myopathy or polymyositis. In many such cases, muscle symptoms and elevated creatine kinase (CK) persisted even after statin discontinuation, with subsequent improvement only after initiating immunosuppressive therapy, thus supporting an autoimmune etiology. This observation ultimately contributed to the identification of HMGCR autoantibodies as a key disease marker. It should be emphasized, however, that approximately one-third of individuals with IMNM do not test positive for these autoantibodies. The absence of detectable autoantibodies in this group complicates the diagnostic process and indicates that additional autoantibodies and triggers remain unidentified.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 60s, whose past medical history included obesity, hepatic steatosis, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and a remote episode of deep vein thrombosis/pulmonary embolism, presented with gradually worsening proximal muscle weakness along with difficulty swallowing and changes in her voice.

She had previously maintained good health and was fully independent in daily activities. Three years before this decline, she was being evaluated as an outpatient due to elevated liver transaminases. At that point, she demonstrated mildly raised aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT), both approximately twice the upper limit of normal, with ALT levels higher than AST. Alkaline phosphatase, albumin, bilirubin, and platelet levels remained within reference ranges. She reported no alcohol consumption and was not using any medications known to cause hepatotoxicity. An abdominal ultrasound revealed enlarged liver with diffusely increased echotexture, consistent with hepatic steatosis. Serologic tests for viral hepatitis as well as autoantibodies including anti-nuclear, anti-mitochondrial, anti-smooth muscle, and liver-kidney microsomal antibodies returned negative. Liver biopsy identified steatohepatitis in line with a diagnosis of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), with a NAFLD Activity Score of grade 4/8 and stage 0. She was counseled to adopt a calorie-restricted diet and engage in regular physical activity.

Given ongoing obesity and hepatic steatosis 2.5 years post-NAFLD diagnosis, semaglutide therapy was initiated. Soon after the initial 0.25 mg subcutaneous injection of semaglutide, the patient developed nausea and vomiting within hours. Symptoms continued, leading to hospital admission two days later with persistent nausea, vomiting, decreased oral intake, shortness of breath, acute kidney injury, and marked metabolic acidosis. Management included intravenous fluids, bicarbonate administration, and antiemetic therapy. She required a brief stay in the intensive care unit to receive non-invasive ventilatory support. Her renal function subsequently recovered with hydration alone, obviating the need for hemofiltration or hemodialysis. On admission, her creatine kinase was measured at 337 U/L. She exhibited no additional symptoms such as muscle pain, rash, or dark-colored urine. During hospitalization, she developed episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, but her serum troponin was within normal limits. She was discharged after five days, with resolution of gastrointestinal complaints, though she remained deconditioned. Over the following four months, she experienced gradually worsening weakness. The patient denied any previous statin use, intake of other supplements linked to IMNM such as mushrooms or red yeast rice, or any use of nutritional supplements. She also reported no significant family history of muscle disease, recent intensive or prolonged exercise, or use of alcohol or illicit drugs.

Four months after receiving her first and only dose of semaglutide, the patient’s condition continued to deteriorate. She became unable to stand or sit upright without support and reported several days of increasing difficulty swallowing both liquids and solids. As a result, she sought care at an outside hospital, where her creatine kinase (CK) was found to be markedly elevated at 3992 U/L (reference range: 12–191 U/L) upon admission. She was started on intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for five days, with neurology guidance provided via telemedicine. The patient was subsequently transferred to a tertiary center for rheumatologic assessment, where she was afebrile, had normal blood pressure, and appeared otherwise comfortable. On examination, there was notable weakness, most pronounced in proximal muscle groups (see table 1). She showed no rashes, dermatologic abnormalities, crackles, joint inflammation, edema, sicca symptoms, oral ulcers, or other neurologic impairments.

After receiving pulse-dose methylprednisolone, she was started on oral prednisone at a dosage of 1 mg/kg daily. Because of ongoing weakness and pronounced clinical severity, she also received intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) at a total dose of 2 g/kg, administered over 4 days. Over the subsequent 2 weeks, there was a significant reduction in her CK levels (see figure 1). She was discharged after nearly 4 weeks of inpatient care. Twelve days following discharge, she was readmitted for 10 days due to septic shock secondary to a pseudomonal urinary tract infection, during which her CK was noted to be within the normal range.

She was monitored in the outpatient setting on multiple occasions, demonstrating continual progress in physical abilities and overall well-being, with CK levels remaining normal throughout. Consequently, her prednisone was tapered off successfully at a rapid rate. Owing to both the cost considerations and her substantial clinical recovery after discontinuation of semaglutide, no further IVIG was administered beyond her initial hospital course.

She was last seen in the outpatient setting 10 months after her initial evaluation and treatment initiation and had regained her baseline functional status, off immunosuppression for 8 months.

Investigations:

Her laboratory evaluation revealed an elevated creatine kinase (CK) of 1237 U/L at the time of assessment, with a recorded peak of 3992 U/L one week earlier at the referring facility. Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) values were within normal range. Aspartate aminotransferase (AST) measured 191 U/L and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) was 165 U/L, both increased in the context of raised CK levels and a history of hepatic steatosis. The autoantibody assessment showed negative results for anti-nuclear antibody (ANA), anti-Sjogren’s syndrome antigen A (anti-SSA), anti-Smith, anti-U1 ribonucleoprotein (anti-U1 RNP), anti-double-stranded DNA, anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA), and anti-mitochondrial antibody. Complement concentrations, renal function, and blood counts were all within normal limits. The myositis-specific antibody panel was negative for SRP, HMGCR, Jo-1, Pl-7, Pl-12, EJ, OJ, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-γ, NXP-2, and cN-1A antibodies. However, she tested positive for SS-B (anti-La) autoantibody at low titre, measuring 2.4 (reference: <1.0). Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was elevated at 22 mm/hour and C-reactive protein (CRP) was also raised at 22.8 mg/L. Of note, high-sensitivity troponin (HS-Troponin) was increased at 62 pg/mL (reference ≤53 pg/mL) and pro–B-type natriuretic peptide (proBNP) was elevated at 4358 pg/mL (reference range: 0–125 pg/mL).

Key imaging studies performed included MRI of both femurs, echocardiography, cardiac MRI, liver ultrasound with Doppler, high-resolution chest CT, and abdominal MRI. Bilateral femur MRI demonstrated mild, symmetric edema within both thigh muscles, particularly noticeable in the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps, along with subcutaneous edema on the lateral sides of both thighs (figure 2). Echocardiogram revealed an estimated left ventricular ejection fraction of 65%–70% and a right ventricular systolic pressure measuring 46 mm Hg. Cardiac MRI indicated mild hypokinesis in the mid-anteroseptal and anterior left ventricular walls accompanied by myocardial edema, but without definitive late gadolinium enhancement. These findings may be consistent with either myocardial inflammation/early myocarditis or focal stress-induced cardiomyopathy. No significant myocardial fibrosis, infarction, or notable valvular pathology was observed. Liver ultrasound with Doppler identified hepatosteatosis. High-resolution CT of the chest showed no evidence of interstitial lung disease or lymphadenopathy. Abdominal MRI showed steatotic enlargement of the liver, but was otherwise unremarkable and did not reveal lymphadenopathy.

Further diagnostic evaluations included repeating a liver biopsy and obtaining a thigh muscle biopsy. The liver biopsy, conducted during her hospital stay, revealed multiple tissue samples with both small and large droplet macrovesicular steatosis affecting about 70% of the examined liver parenchyma, without notable portal inflammation or interface hepatitis. These results aligned with her previous diagnosis of hepatosteatosis, showing a mild progression to a NAFLD Activity Score of 5 out of 8, stage 1. The biopsy of the right vastus lateralis muscle showed scattered necrotic and regenerating fibers (figure 3A,B). Immunohistochemical staining demonstrated the presence of acid phosphatase (figure 3D) and cluster of Differentiation-68 (CD68), indicative of macrophages, along with myophagocytosis (figure 4B). Additionally, staining for membrane attack complex was positive in both necrotic and non-necrotic muscle fibers (figure 4C), signifying an inflammatory response. Rare CD3-positive T cells (figure 4A) and perivascular inflammatory cells were also observed (figure 3C). The findings on the muscle biopsy were indicative of immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM).

Differential diagnosis:

The patient exhibited a pronounced and rapid response following her first and only administration of semaglutide, indicating a likely intolerance. Her presenting symptoms were gastrointestinal disturbances accompanied by renal insufficiency, thought to be due to hypovolemia. At her initial assessment, creatine kinase was only slightly raised and did not align with a diagnosis of rhabdomyolysis; however, the patient's progressive decline despite discontinuation of semaglutide, the specific distribution of muscle weakness, and a twelvefold increase in creatine kinase over four months subsequent to semaglutide exposure all point toward the development of an immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) probably linked to semaglutide. Additionally, muscle biopsy findings that are typical of drug-induced IMNM, the absence of other indicators for underlying connective tissue disease, and a sustained clinical improvement after both stopping semaglutide and initiating immunosuppressive therapy provide further evidence for this causal relationship.

It is important to highlight that elevated transaminase levels were present before she initiated semaglutide, a feature that can occur with myopathies. Nonetheless, a liver biopsy conducted three years before she started semaglutide confirmed hepatosteatosis, and her mildly increased AST/ALT remained even after her CK concentrations returned to normal. Therefore, the persistent transaminase elevations are most likely hepatic in origin rather than attributable to muscle injury. Additionally, her cardiac assessment did not provide definitive evidence for ongoing myocarditis. Considering her swift clinical improvement, stress-related myopathy would also be a plausible consideration in this context.

The detection of a positive SS-B antibody alongside negative ANA and SS-A results was unanticipated. To our knowledge, there are no previous accounts documenting IMNM arising in the context of isolated SS-B positivity with concurrent negative ANA and SS-A. We were also unable to locate a baseline CK value preceding the patient’s exposure to semaglutide, limiting our ability to fully rule out an underlying subclinical autoimmune IMNM prior to starting semaglutide. Nonetheless, the observed twelvefold increase in CK and the emergence of progressive proximal muscle weakness following semaglutide introduction suggest a stronger association with drug-induced IMNM. Moreover, the patient's pronounced IMNM resolved after a brief regimen of corticosteroids and IVIG, paired with discontinuation of semaglutide. This pattern of resolution is more consistent with medication-induced IMNM, which tends to respond to short-term immunosuppression following withdrawal of the causative drug, as opposed to idiopathic IMNM, which generally necessitates more sustained and intensive immunosuppressive management.

Treatment:

She received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for 5 days, after which she was transitioned to oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg per day. Owing to ongoing weakness and pronounced symptoms, IVIG was administered at 2 g/kg, divided over 4 days. She underwent multiple outpatient follow-up visits, during which she showed continued gains in both functional ability and general health, along with consistently normal CK measurements. These improvements allowed her prednisone to be tapered off swiftly over a period of 2 months. Given the combination of financial factors and her maintained progress after discontinuation of semaglutide, she did not require further IVIG beyond the initial inpatient treatment.

Outcome and follow-up:

She was most recently evaluated as an outpatient 10 months following her initial assessment and initiation of therapy, by which time she had returned to her pre-illness functional level and had discontinued immunosuppressive therapy for 7 months. Her creatine kinase levels stayed within the normal range after her hospitalization and immunosuppressive treatment. Follow-up echocardiography and cardiac MRI demonstrated resolution of the previously identified abnormalities. Additionally, she had no further episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation.

Discussion:

GLP-1 receptor agonists like semaglutide are increasingly prescribed and have shown efficacy in treating metabolic conditions such as type 2 diabetes and obesity. While gastrointestinal side effects are most frequently observed, these agents have also been linked to reduced muscle mass and, on rare occasions, rhabdomyolysis. In this report, the patient’s symptoms are more compatible with IMNM rather than rhabdomyolysis. The creatine kinase rose by a factor of 12 over a four-month interval after a single dose of semaglutide, whereas in cases of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis, creatine kinase would generally return to normal within several weeks if semaglutide’s direct toxicity were responsible. For comparison, a published case of rhabdomyolysis attributed to tirzepatide followed the expected course: the individual presented with an acute, significant CK elevation (CK = 57,014) and experienced a rapid decrease in levels after discontinuing the drug and initiating hydration therapy within a few days, with no immunosuppressive treatment necessary.

According to the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) Causality Categories and Criteria, there is a probable link between semaglutide administration and the development of IMNM in our patient, due to an appropriate temporal association, the unlikelihood of alternative etiologies or medications being responsible, and a clinically appropriate improvement following discontinuation of the drug. Rechallenge with semaglutide is not ethically permissible in this instance, given the severity of the adverse event observed. Nonetheless, we recognize that definitive causality cannot be established in this case. With the widespread and increasing use of semaglutide, we present this case report to alert healthcare providers to this potential side effect and to strengthen pharmacovigilance efforts. Further research will be necessary to determine whether this observed relationship is causal or merely coincidental.

Additionally, the specific factors that may have rendered our patient susceptible to this profound adverse reaction to semaglutide remain undefined. As GLP-1 receptor agonists see rising usage, identifying risk factors through dedicated research will become especially important if further cases with a similar temporal link to semaglutide are identified.
