Title: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

Brian Lam, Eleni Tiniakou, Xinhai Robert Zhang, Shervin Assassi

Abstract:

Summary: This case report outlines the clinical course of a woman in her 60s who developed immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) following exposure to semaglutide. Over a period of four months, she experienced worsening dysphagia, dysphonia, proximal muscle weakness, and elevated creatine kinase levels. The onset of her symptoms followed a single administration of semaglutide. Diagnostic evaluation revealed findings indicative of necrotising myopathy with inflammatory cell infiltrates noted on muscle biopsy. The patient showed improvement after receiving immunosuppressive therapy with corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG). She progressively recovered and regained her baseline level of function, maintaining clinical stability off immunosuppressive medications for several months, provided that glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists were avoided. This case represents the first documented instance of IMNM potentially linked to GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy, with notable improvement after a brief regimen of immunosuppression and IVIG.

Background:

Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have increasingly been utilized in the management of metabolic conditions such as type 2 diabetes and obesity in recent years. Notably, reports have identified skeletal muscle mass loss as an adverse effect linked to this medication class. Recently, rhabdomyolysis with necrotising features has been reported in association with tirzepatide, another widely used GLP-1 receptor agonist. In that case, the patient experienced a marked elevation in creatine kinase (CK) levels, reaching into the thousands, during ongoing therapy; notably, CK levels began to decline within several days of discontinuing the drug, supporting a diagnosis of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis.

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) represents a unique form of myopathy, defined by rapidly progressive proximal muscle weakness and histological findings of necrotic muscle fibers with minimal lymphocytic infiltration. Two autoantibodies, anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (SRP), show a strong association with IMNM. Notably, use of statin medications, frequently prescribed for metabolic conditions, is closely linked to the presence of HMGCR autoantibodies. Historically, patients on statins who subsequently presented with necrotising myopathy were often misclassified as cases of toxic myopathy or polymyositis, prior to the recognition of the connection to HMGCR autoantibodies. In these instances, patients frequently exhibited ongoing muscle weakness and elevated creatine kinase (CK) levels even after discontinuing the statin, with subsequent clinical improvement noted only after introduction of immunosuppressive therapies. These observations ultimately contributed to identifying HMGCR autoantibody as a defining feature of this disorder. Nonetheless, approximately one-third of IMNM cases are found to be seronegative. The absence of detectable autoantibodies in this group not only creates further diagnostic complexity but also implies that additional, yet unidentified, autoantibodies and potential causative factors may exist.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 60s with a background of obesity, hepatic steatosis, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and a distant episode of deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism presented with gradually worsening proximal muscle weakness, as well as difficulty swallowing and hoarseness.

Before this decline, she had been healthy and completely self-sufficient in her daily activities. Three years before the onset of her neurological symptoms, she had been evaluated in the outpatient setting due to elevated liver transaminases. At that assessment, her aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels were mildly increased, roughly two times the upper limit of normal, with ALT being more elevated than AST. Her alkaline phosphatase, albumin, bilirubin, and platelet count were all within normal ranges. The patient reported no alcohol consumption and denied use of hepatotoxic medications. An abdominal ultrasound demonstrated hepatomegaly with uniformly increased echogenicity, most consistent with fatty infiltration of the liver. Laboratory studies for viral hepatitis, as well as anti-nuclear, anti-mitochondrial, anti-smooth muscle, and liver-kidney microsomal antibodies, were all negative. Histological evaluation of a liver biopsy revealed steatohepatitis in keeping with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), specifically an NAFLD Activity Score of 4 out of 8 and stage 0 fibrosis. She was counseled to follow a calorie-restricted diet and to exercise regularly.

Given ongoing hepatic steatosis and obesity 2.5 years following her NAFLD diagnosis, semaglutide therapy was commenced. Shortly after administration of the initial 0.25 mg subcutaneous injection of semaglutide, the patient developed nausea and vomiting. Her symptoms did not subside, leading to hospital admission two days later for continued nausea, vomiting, decreased oral intake, dyspnea, acute renal insufficiency, and pronounced metabolic acidosis. Management included intravenous fluids, bicarbonate, and antiemetic agents. She required a brief transfer to the intensive care unit where non-invasive ventilation was provided. Following adequate hydration, her renal parameters normalized, obviating the need for haemofiltration or dialysis. Creatine kinase measured at admission was elevated to 337 U/L. She did not exhibit other typical symptoms such as rash, myalgia, or dark-colored urine. During this stay, she developed paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, though serum troponin values remained within the normal range. Discharge followed after a 5-day hospitalization; gastrointestinal symptoms had resolved, but she remained generally weak. Progressive weakness developed over the ensuing four months. The patient reported no previous statin use, intake of supplements commonly associated with IMNM such as mushrooms or red yeast rice, or any other nutritional supplements. She also denied notable family history of myopathy, recent significant or prolonged exercise, as well as alcohol or recreational drug use.

Four months following administration of her first and only dose of semaglutide, her condition continued to worsen. She became unable to stand or maintain an upright sitting position without help, and over several days developed dysphagia affecting both liquids and solids. As a result, she sought care at another hospital, where her creatine kinase (CK) level was found to be elevated at 3992 U/L (reference range: 12–191 U/L) upon admission. Treatment consisted of intravenous methylprednisolone 1000 mg daily for 5 days, initiated with the guidance of neurology via telemedicine. Subsequently, she was transferred to a tertiary care facility for rheumatology assessment, at which time she was noted to be afebrile, had normal blood pressure, and appeared comfortable otherwise. On physical examination, marked muscle weakness was identified, particularly in the proximal muscle groups (see table 1). No rashes, cutaneous changes, pulmonary crackles, synovitis, edema, sicca symptoms, oral ulcers, or focal neurological deficits were present.

After completing pulse therapy with methylprednisolone, she was transitioned to oral prednisone at a dose of 1 mg/kg per day. Ongoing pronounced weakness and severe clinical findings led to administration of intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) at a total dose of 2 g/kg, divided over 4 days. Over the subsequent 2 weeks, her creatine kinase (CK) levels showed significant reduction (see figure 1). She was discharged from the hospital following a nearly 4-week inpatient stay. Approximately 12 days post-discharge, she was readmitted for 10 days because of septic shock secondary to a Pseudomonas urinary tract infection, during which her CK was found to be within the normal range.

Multiple outpatient visits followed, with sustained improvement in her functionality and general condition, as well as consistently normal CK values. Given this progress, prednisone was able to be tapered off quickly and successfully. Considering financial factors and her marked recovery after discontinuing semaglutide, no further IVIG was administered beyond the initial course she received while hospitalized.

She was last seen in the outpatient setting 10 months after her initial evaluation and treatment initiation and had regained her baseline functional status, off immunosuppression for 8 months.

Investigations:

Her laboratory studies revealed a creatine kinase (CK) level of 1237 U/L at presentation, with a prior maximum of 3992 U/L recorded a week earlier at the referring facility. Thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH) was within the reference range. Aspartate aminotransferase (AST) measured 191 U/L and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) was 165 U/L, findings observed alongside increased serum CK and a background of hepatic steatosis. Autoantibody testing demonstrated negativity for anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-Sjogren’s syndrome antibody type A (anti-SSA), anti-Smith antibodies, anti-U1 ribonucleoprotein (anti-U1 RNP), anti-double-stranded DNA, antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), and anti-mitochondrial antibodies. Complement components and creatinine were unremarkable, and there was no evidence of cytopenia. A myositis-specific panel returned negative for SRP, HMGCR, and other antibodies including Jo-1, Pl-7, Pl-12, EJ, OJ, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-y, NXP-2, and cN-1A. She exhibited a low-titer positive SS-B autoantibody at 2.4 (with normal defined as <1.0). Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was elevated to 22 mm/hour, and C-reactive protein measured 22.8 mg/L. She also showed a high-sensitivity troponin (HS-Troponin) increase to 62 pg/mL (reference ≤53 pg/mL) and an elevated pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (proBNP) at 4358 pg/mL (normal 0–125 pg/mL).

Relevant imaging studies included MRI of both femurs, echocardiogram, cardiac MRI, liver ultrasound with Doppler, high-resolution chest CT, and abdominal MRI. MRI of the femurs showed mild, symmetrical muscular edema in both legs, most prominent in the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps muscles, along with subcutaneous edema along the outer thighs bilaterally (see figure 2). The echocardiogram indicated a left ventricular ejection fraction estimated at 65%–70% and a right ventricular systolic pressure measured at 46 mm Hg. Cardiac MRI revealed mild hypokinesis in the mid-anteroseptal and anterior wall areas, accompanied by myocardial edema without clear late gadolinium enhancement; these findings could suggest myocardial inflammation/early myocarditis or possibly a localized, stress-induced cardiomyopathy. No significant myocardial scarring, infarction, or valvular dysfunction was observed. Liver ultrasound with Doppler identified hepatosteatosis. The high-resolution CT scan of the chest showed no evidence of interstitial lung disease or lymphadenopathy. Abdominal MRI revealed steatotic enlargement of the liver but was otherwise unremarkable, with no lymphadenopathy detected.

Additional diagnostic evaluations included a repeat liver biopsy and biopsy of the thigh muscle. The liver biopsy, carried out during her hospital stay, revealed multiple samples of hepatic tissue displaying both small and large droplet macrovesicular steatosis affecting about 70% of the examined liver parenchyma, without notable portal inflammation or interface hepatitis. These pathological findings matched her established diagnosis of hepatosteatosis and indicated mild advancement of her liver pathology to NAFLD Activity Score grade 5 out of 8, stage 1. The right vastus lateralis muscle biopsy showed scattered necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers (figure 3A,B). Immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated positive acid phosphatase staining (figure 3D), as well as CD68—a macrophage marker—showing myophagocytosis (figure 4B). Additionally, membrane attack complex staining was evident on both necrotic and non-necrotic myofibers (figure 4C), aligning with an inflammatory process. Occasional CD3-positive cells, marking T-cells (figure 4A), and perivascular infiltrates of inflammatory cells (figure 3C) were also identified. These muscle biopsy results were diagnostic of an immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM).

Differential diagnosis:

The patient exhibited a pronounced and rapid adverse response following her first and only administration of semaglutide, indicating possible intolerance to the drug. Her presenting complaints comprised gastrointestinal disturbances and renal failure believed to be secondary to hypovolemia. Although her creatine kinase (CK) levels were only mildly raised at initial assessment—insufficient for a diagnosis of rhabdomyolysis—her ongoing clinical deterioration after discontinuing semaglutide, combined with the pattern of muscle weakness and a twelvefold elevation in CK over four months post-exposure, are indicative of an immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) potentially linked to semaglutide. Furthermore, muscle biopsy findings compatible with drug-induced IMNM, the absence of additional signs suggestive of connective tissue disease, and her persistent clinical improvement after early immunosuppressive therapy and withdrawal of semaglutide, all lend further credence to this association.

It is important to point out that elevated transaminase levels were present before initiation of semaglutide, a finding that can occur in various myopathies. Nonetheless, she had a liver biopsy confirming hepatosteatosis three years prior to starting semaglutide, and her mildly increased AST and ALT persisted even after normalization of her CK values. Therefore, it is probable that her chronically raised transaminases stem from hepatic, rather than muscular, pathology. Additionally, her cardiac assessment did not provide definitive evidence of ongoing myocarditis. Considering her swift clinical improvement, a diagnosis of stress-induced myopathy would also be plausible in this scenario.

The detection of SS-B antibody positivity alongside negative ANA and SS-A was unanticipated. To date, there are no documented cases linking isolated SS-B seropositivity, without ANA and SS-A, to the development of IMNM. We did not have access to a CK value measured before the patient commenced semaglutide, making it impossible to rule out the presence of a slowly progressive autoimmune IMNM prior to semaglutide exposure. Nevertheless, the twelvefold CK elevation and gradual onset of proximal muscle weakness following semaglutide initiation suggest a higher probability of drug-induced IMNM in this scenario. Furthermore, the patient's marked IMNM symptoms resolved following a brief course of corticosteroids and IVIG, accompanied by discontinuation of semaglutide. This response is more characteristic of medication-induced IMNM, which typically ameliorates with short-term immunosuppression once the causative drug is withdrawn, in contrast to idiopathic IMNM that often necessitates sustained and intensive immunosuppressive therapy.

Treatment:

She received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for 5 days, then transitioned to oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg per day. Due to ongoing muscle weakness and significant symptoms, IVIG was administered at 2 g/kg, divided across 4 days. Her progress was monitored through multiple outpatient visits, during which she continued to show improvements in functional abilities and overall wellbeing, with creatine kinase levels remaining within normal limits. This positive trajectory allowed for a rapid taper of prednisone over a period of 2 months. In light of both financial constraints and sustained recovery after stopping semaglutide, no additional courses of IVIG were given following her initial inpatient treatment.

Outcome and follow-up:

She was most recently evaluated in the outpatient clinic 10 months following her initial presentation and commencement of therapy, by which time she had returned to her pre-morbid level of function and had discontinued immunosuppressive therapy for 7 months. Her creatine kinase values stayed normal after discharge and while receiving immunosuppressive treatment. Subsequent echocardiography and cardiac MRI showed resolution of the previously noted abnormalities. Additionally, there were no further episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation.

Discussion:

GLP-1 receptor agonists like semaglutide are increasingly prescribed and have shown efficacy in the management of metabolic conditions such as obesity and type 2 diabetes. Although gastrointestinal adverse effects are most frequently observed, these agents may also contribute to reduced muscle mass and, in some cases, rhabdomyolysis. In the current case, the clinical features align more closely with IMNM rather than rhabdomyolysis. The patient's CK rose by a factor of 12 over a four-month period after a single dose of semaglutide, whereas with a purely toxic effect of semaglutide as seen in other drug-induced rhabdomyolysis reports, CK levels would typically be expected to return to normal within weeks. It is worth noting that the previously documented tirzepatide-related rhabdomyolysis case exhibited a classic pattern: the individual presented with a sharply elevated CK (CK=57,014 on admission) and demonstrated rapid improvement in CK levels within days after discontinuation of the medication and initiation of hydration, without requiring immunosuppressive therapy.

According to the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) Causality Categories and Criteria, our case demonstrates a probable association between semaglutide administration and the onset of IMNM. This is supported by an appropriate temporal association, the absence of alternative explanations involving other conditions or medications, and clinical improvement following drug discontinuation. Performing a rechallenge was deemed unethical due to the severity of the adverse effect attributed to semaglutide. Nonetheless, we recognize that definitive causality cannot be established in this case. With the increasing prevalence of semaglutide usage, this case report intends to inform healthcare professionals of this possible side effect and promote heightened pharmacovigilance. Future research will be necessary to determine whether this observed relationship is causative or coincidental.

Additionally, the factors that might have predisposed our patient to this pronounced reaction to semaglutide are still unknown. As GLP-1 receptor agonists continue to gain popularity, it will be essential for future investigations to explore possible predisposing factors if other temporally linked instances are documented.
