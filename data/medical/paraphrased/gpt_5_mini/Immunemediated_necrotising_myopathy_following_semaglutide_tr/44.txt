Title: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

Brian Lam, Eleni Tiniakou, Xinhai Robert Zhang, Shervin Assassi

Abstract:

Summary: This case report details a woman in her 60s who developed immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) following administration of semaglutide. Over a period of four months, she experienced worsening dysphagia, dysphonia, proximal muscle weakness, and elevated creatine kinase levels. These symptoms began shortly after receiving a single dose of semaglutide. Diagnostic evaluation indicated necrotising myopathy characterized by inflammatory infiltration on muscle biopsy. She was treated with corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), resulting in gradual symptom improvement. Ultimately, she regained her previous level of function and remained stable without immunosuppressive therapy for several months while avoiding glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists. This report presents the first documented occurrence of IMNM likely associated with GLP-1 receptor agonist exposure, demonstrating a favorable response to brief immunosuppressive and IVIG therapy.

Background:

In recent years, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have frequently been prescribed for metabolic disorders such as type 2 diabetes and obesity. Among the reported side effects for these agents, a reduction in skeletal muscle mass has been noted. A recent case report detailed the occurrence of rhabdomyolysis exhibiting necrotising characteristics in association with tirzepatide, another widely utilized GLP-1 receptor agonist. In this particular case, the patient experienced a marked increase in creatine kinase (CK), reaching values in the thousands during ongoing therapy. The CK levels began to decline within several days following cessation of the drug, indicating a pattern consistent with drug-induced rhabdomyolysis.

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) represents a unique form of myopathy distinguished by severe proximal muscle weakness, histologically marked by necrotic muscle fibers and minimal lymphocytic infiltration. Autoantibodies directed against hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and signal recognition particle (SRP) are closely linked to IMNM. Statins, widely prescribed for metabolic conditions, have a strong association with HMGCR autoantibody positivity. Before the HMGCR autoantibody connection was established, several individuals exposed to statins developed necrotising myopathy, often misclassified as toxic myopathy or polymyositis. Many such cases persisted with muscle weakness and elevated creatine kinase levels even after stopping statin therapy. The subsequent response to immunosuppressive treatment indicated an autoimmune mechanism, which ultimately led to identifying HMGCR autoantibody as a defining feature of this disorder. Nevertheless, approximately one-third of IMNM patients lack detectable autoantibodies. This seronegative subgroup poses additional difficulties in diagnosis and implies the existence of other yet unidentified autoantibodies and precipitants.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 60s with a history including obesity, hepatic steatosis, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and past episodes of deep vein thrombosis/pulmonary embolism presented with gradually worsening proximal muscle weakness, difficulties swallowing, and altered voice.

She had previously been healthy and able to manage all daily tasks independently. Three years before the onset of her current symptoms, she had been evaluated in an outpatient setting for elevated liver transaminases. At that time, she demonstrated mildly increased levels of aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT), roughly twice the upper normal limit, with ALT higher than AST. Laboratory tests showed normal results for alkaline phosphatase, albumin, bilirubin, and platelet count. The patient reported no alcohol consumption and denied using medications known to harm the liver. Abdominal ultrasound revealed an enlarged liver with uniformly increased echogenicity, suggestive of hepatic steatosis. Testing for viral hepatitis, as well as for anti-nuclear, anti-mitochondrial, anti-smooth muscle, and liver-kidney microsomal antibodies, yielded negative results. Liver biopsy indicated steatohepatitis consistent with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), reflected by an NAFLD Activity Score of 4 out of 8 and stage 0. She was counseled to adopt a reduced-calorie diet and engage in routine physical activity.

Owing to ongoing hepatic steatosis and obesity 2.5 years following her NAFLD diagnosis, semaglutide therapy was initiated. A few hours after receiving her initial subcutaneous semaglutide dose (0.25 mg), she began experiencing nausea and vomiting. These symptoms persisted, leading to hospital admission two days later due to ongoing nausea, vomiting, reduced oral intake, dyspnoea, acute renal insufficiency, and pronounced metabolic acidosis. Management included intravenous fluids and bicarbonate, along with anti-emetic agents. She was temporarily transferred to the ICU for non-invasive ventilatory support. Following rehydration, her renal function improved, obviating the need for haemofiltration or haemodialysis. On admission, her creatine kinase was elevated at 337 U/L; however, she did not report accompanying symptoms such as rash, myalgia, or dark urine. During her stay, she developed intermittent atrial fibrillation, though her serum troponin remained within normal limits. She was discharged after five days. By discharge, her gastrointestinal complaints had resolved, but she remained physically deconditioned. Over the ensuing four months, she experienced gradual worsening of weakness. She denied ever using statins, consuming supplements linked to IMNM (including mushrooms or red yeast rice), or other nutritional products. Additionally, she reported no notable family history of myopathy, no recent intense or extended physical activity, nor alcohol or illicit drug use.

Four months following her first and only administration of semaglutide, her condition continued to deteriorate. She was unable to stand or sit upright without support and reported increasing difficulty in swallowing both liquids and solids over the course of several days. This led her to seek care at a separate hospital, where laboratory tests demonstrated a creatine kinase (CK) level of 3992 U/L (reference range: 12–191 U/L) at admission. Management included intravenous methylprednisolone at a dosage of 1000 mg daily for 5 days, with neurology consultation provided via telemedicine. Subsequently, she was transferred to a tertiary care facility for further rheumatological assessment, at which point she was afebrile, had normal blood pressure, and was not otherwise distressed. Examination revealed marked muscle weakness, particularly affecting proximal muscle groups (table 1). There were no signs of rash, skin abnormalities, crackles, synovitis, edema, sicca symptoms, oral ulcers, or additional neurologic impairment.

After completing a course of pulse methylprednisolone, she was subsequently administered oral prednisone at a dose of 1 mg/kg daily. Because she continued to exhibit significant weakness and other severe symptoms, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was provided at a total dose of 2 g/kg, divided over 4 days. Over the subsequent 2-week period, her CK levels showed considerable improvement (see figure 1). She was released from the hospital after an inpatient stay of nearly 4 weeks. Twelve days following her discharge, she was re-admitted for 10 days with septic shock secondary to a pseudomonal urinary tract infection; during this admission, her CK remained within the normal range.

She underwent multiple outpatient follow-up visits, during which she maintained ongoing enhancements in both her functional capacity and general health, alongside consistently normal creatine kinase measurements. As a result, prednisone was tapered quickly and discontinued. Because of financial constraints and her marked recovery without semaglutide, she did not undergo further intravenous immunoglobulin administration beyond the first cycle received during her hospitalization.

Her most recent outpatient evaluation occurred 10 months post initial assessment and commencement of therapy, at which time she had fully restored her baseline level of function and had been free of immunosuppressive therapy for 8 months.

Investigations:

Her laboratory evaluation revealed a CK level of 1237 U/L on assessment, with a prior peak of 3992 U/L reported one week before at the referring facility. Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) levels were within the reference range. Aminotransferase levels were increased, with AST at 191 U/L and ALT at 165 U/L, correlating with elevated serum CK and her background of fatty liver. Autoantibody screening was significant for negative results for anti-nuclear antibody (ANA), anti-SSA (anti-Sjogren’s syndrome antibodies type A), anti-Smith, anti-U1 RNP, anti-double-stranded DNA, ANCA, and anti-mitochondrial antibody. Complement levels were within normal limits, and her creatinine was normal; there were no findings of cytopenia. Myositis autoantibody panel was negative for SRP, HMGCR, Jo-1, Pl-7, Pl-12, EJ, OJ, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-y, NXP-2, and cN-1A antibodies. A low-titer positive SS-B antibody at 2.4 (reference <1.0) was detected. Inflammatory markers were elevated, with ESR at 22 mm/hour and C-reactive protein at 22.8 mg/L. High-sensitivity troponin was noted to be elevated at 62 pg/mL (reference ≤53 pg/mL), and pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (proBNP) was measured at 4358 pg/mL (reference: 0–125 pg/mL).

Relevant imaging studies encompassed bilateral femoral MRI, echocardiography, cardiac MRI, Doppler liver ultrasound, high-resolution chest CT, and abdominal MRI. Bilateral femoral MRI indicated mild, symmetrical muscle edema in both thighs, particularly affecting the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps muscles, alongside subcutaneous edema on the lateral sides of both thighs (see figure 2). Echocardiography demonstrated a left ventricular ejection fraction estimated at 65%–70% and a right ventricular systolic pressure measured at 46 mm Hg. Cardiac MRI revealed mild hypokinesia of the mid-anteroseptal and anterior wall coupled with myocardial edema, absent any definitive late gadolinium enhancement. These findings were suggestive of possible myocardial inflammation, early myocarditis, or alternatively a focal stress-induced cardiomyopathy. No notable myocardial fibrosis, infarction, or valvular pathology was identified. Doppler liver ultrasound disclosed evidence of hepatic steatosis. The high-resolution CT scan of the chest did not reveal interstitial lung disease or lymphadenopathy. Abdominal MRI showed enlarged steatotic liver but was otherwise unremarkable, without detection of lymphadenopathy.

Additional diagnostic evaluation included repeating a liver biopsy and obtaining a sample from the thigh muscle. The liver biopsy, conducted during her hospitalization, showed multiple samples of hepatic tissue exhibiting both small and large droplet macrovesicular steatosis affecting roughly 70% of the examined hepatic parenchyma, without notable portal inflammation or evidence of interface hepatitis. These results aligned with her prior diagnosis of hepatosteatosis, demonstrating mild advancement of liver pathology to a NAFLD Activity Score of 5 out of 8, stage 1. The muscle biopsy of the right vastus lateralis revealed scattered necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers (figure 3A,B). Immunohistochemical analysis indicated positive staining for acid phosphatase (figure 3D) and CD68—a macrophage marker—along with evidence of myophagocytosis (figure 4B). Additionally, membrane attack complex staining was present in both necrotic and non-necrotic myofibers (figure 4C), indicative of an ongoing inflammatory process. Rare CD3-positive T-cells (figure 4A) and clusters of perivascular inflammatory cells (figure 3C) were also identified. The constellation of findings in the muscle biopsy was in keeping with a diagnosis of IMNM.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient developed a pronounced and rapid reaction following her first and only administration of semaglutide, indicative of drug intolerance. Her presenting complaints consisted of gastrointestinal symptoms and renal failure likely attributable to hypovolemia. At initial assessment, her creatine kinase (CK) was only mildly elevated, which did not fit the criteria for rhabdomyolysis. However, persistent clinical deterioration in the absence of further semaglutide exposure, alongside the pattern of muscle weakness and a twelvefold elevation in CK across four months post-semaglutide, pointed toward immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) likely linked to semaglutide. Additionally, muscle biopsy findings supporting drug-induced IMNM, the absence of signs suggesting a primary connective tissue disorder, and a durable clinical response following discontinuation of semaglutide and initiation of immunosuppression further reinforce this suspected relationship.

It is important to highlight that elevated transaminases were present before she began treatment with semaglutide, a finding often associated with myopathies. Nevertheless, she was diagnosed with hepatosteatosis confirmed by liver biopsy three years prior to semaglutide initiation, and her marginally increased AST/ALT levels remained after normalization of her CK. Thus, the persistent elevation of her transaminases likely stems from hepatic causes rather than muscle injury. Additionally, her cardiac evaluation did not definitively indicate ongoing myocarditis. Given the clinical circumstances and her swift improvement, stress-related myopathy remains a plausible consideration.

The presence of a positive SS-B antibody, despite negative ANA and SS-A, was an unusual finding. To date, there are no documented cases of isolated SS-B positivity, with both ANA and SS-A negative, resulting in IMNM. Regrettably, no CK measurement was available before the patient started semaglutide, so a pre-existing, subclinical autoimmune IMNM cannot be entirely ruled out. Nevertheless, the twelvefold increase in CK and the worsening proximal muscle weakness following semaglutide initiation suggest a medication-induced IMNM episode is more probable. Additionally, the patient’s significant IMNM improved after a brief regimen of corticosteroids and IVIG in combination with cessation of semaglutide. This pattern of recovery is more typical of drug-induced IMNM—which may respond to short-term immunosuppression once the causative medication is discontinued—unlike idiopathic IMNM, which usually necessitates prolonged and aggressive immunosuppressive therapy.

Treatment:

She received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for five days, then transitioned to oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg per day. Because of ongoing muscle weakness and pronounced symptoms, IVIG was administered at 2 g/kg, divided over four days. Follow-up visits in the outpatient clinic showed progressive gains in functional ability and overall health, along with consistently normal CK measurements. As a result, her prednisone was tapered successfully and discontinued within two months. Given the combination of cost factors and her maintained improvement after stopping semaglutide, no further IVIG was given beyond the initial inpatient treatment course.

Outcome and follow-up:

Her most recent outpatient visit occurred 10 months post-initial assessment and start of therapy, at which point she had returned to her pre-illness functional level and had been free from immunosuppressive therapy for 7 months. Following hospitalization and immunosuppressive treatment, her CK values consistently stayed within normal limits. Subsequent echocardiogram and cardiac MRI demonstrated resolution of earlier abnormalities. Additionally, she had no further episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation.

Discussion:

GLP-1 receptor agonists, such as semaglutide, are increasingly prescribed and have shown efficacy in treating metabolic conditions like obesity and type 2 diabetes. Although gastrointestinal side effects are most frequently observed, these agents may also lead to reduced muscle mass and, in some cases, rhabdomyolysis. In this report, the patient’s clinical trajectory aligns more closely with IMNM rather than rhabdomyolysis. The patient’s CK levels rose by a factor of 12 over a 4-month period after a single dose of semaglutide; if semaglutide’s direct toxicity had been responsible, one would expect CK normalization within weeks, as occurs in drug-induced rhabdomyolysis cases. For instance, a previous rhabdomyolysis case related to tirzepatide exhibited the typical sequence: the individual had an acute spike in CK (CK=57,014 upon presentation) and rapid improvement after drug discontinuation and hydration within a few days, with no immunosuppressive therapy needed.

According to the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) Causality Categories and Criteria, the association between semaglutide administration and IMNM in this patient is considered probable due to the appropriate temporal correlation, the low likelihood that IMNM was caused by alternate medications or illnesses, and the notable clinical improvement observed upon discontinuation of semaglutide. Due to the severity of the adverse event linked to semaglutide, performing a rechallenge would not be ethically permissible. Nonetheless, we recognize that definitive causality has not been established in this instance. Given the rapid increase in semaglutide usage, this case report is intended to inform clinicians of this potential side effect and promote improved pharmacovigilance. Further research will be necessary to determine whether this relationship is causal or merely coincidental.

Additionally, the underlying factors that may have rendered our patient susceptible to this severe reaction to semaglutide are not yet understood. With the widespread adoption of GLP-1 receptor agonists, investigating possible predispositions will become increasingly important should additional cases with a strong temporal link to semaglutide use emerge.
