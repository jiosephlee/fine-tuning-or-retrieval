Title: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

Brian Lam, Eleni Tiniakou, Xinhai Robert Zhang, Shervin Assassi

Abstract:

Summary: This case concerns a woman in her sixties who developed immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) following administration of semaglutide. She presented with a 4-month history of worsening dysphagia, dysphonia, proximal muscle weakness, and elevated creatine kinase, which began after receiving a single dose of semaglutide. Diagnostic evaluation demonstrated findings of necrotising myopathy with inflammatory cells noted on muscle biopsy. Her clinical status improved with initiation of immunosuppressive therapy, including corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG). Over time, she achieved complete functional recovery and remained stable off immunosuppressive medication for several months, as long as she continued to avoid glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists. To our knowledge, this is the initial report linking IMNM to probable GLP-1 receptor agonist exposure, with favorable outcomes following a brief course of immunosuppression and IVIG.

Background:

In recent years, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have been widely utilized for the treatment of metabolic conditions such as type 2 diabetes and obesity. One of the adverse effects reported with this medication class is a reduction in skeletal muscle mass. A recent case report detailed an instance of rhabdomyolysis exhibiting necrotizing features that was linked to tirzepatide, a frequently prescribed GLP-1 receptor agonist. In that particular case, the patient experienced a creatine kinase (CK) rise into the thousands during ongoing therapy, and the CK levels began to decrease within several days following cessation of the drug, a pattern characteristic of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis.

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) represents a unique form of muscle disease, distinguished by rapid-onset weakness affecting proximal muscles, histopathological evidence of myofibre necrosis, and minimal lymphocytic infiltration. The autoantibodies anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (SRP) are strongly linked to IMNM. Notably, statin therapy, commonly prescribed for metabolic conditions, shows a significant association with HMGCR autoantibody positivity. Prior to recognizing the relationship with HMGCR autoantibodies, numerous patients exposed to statins who developed necrotising myopathy were previously diagnosed with toxic myopathy or polymyositis. In several cases, muscular symptoms and elevated creatine kinase persisted after stopping statin treatment. The subsequent response to immunosuppressive agents implied an autoimmune etiology and eventually contributed to the identification of the HMGCR autoantibody as a defining feature of IMNM. However, it is important to highlight that approximately one-third of individuals with IMNM are seronegative for autoantibodies. This absence of detectable autoantibodies poses further diagnostic difficulties and indicates the likelihood of other associated autoantibodies and causative factors yet to be identified.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 60s with a past medical history notable for obesity, hepatic steatosis, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and a remote episode of deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism presented with progressively worsening proximal muscle weakness, difficulty swallowing, and voice changes.

Prior to the onset of these symptoms, she maintained good health and was entirely independent in daily functioning. Three years before her clinical decline, she was evaluated in the outpatient setting for elevated liver transaminases. At that point, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) were mildly elevated, at roughly twice the upper limit of normal, with ALT being higher than AST. Laboratory results for alkaline phosphatase, albumin, bilirubin, and platelet count were all within normal reference ranges. She reported no alcohol consumption and was not exposed to any known hepatotoxic drugs. Abdominal ultrasound revealed hepatomegaly and a diffusely increased echotexture, findings suggestive of hepatic steatosis. Testing for viral hepatitis, as well as serologies for anti-nuclear, anti-mitochondrial, anti-smooth muscle, and liver-kidney microsomal antibodies, returned negative. A liver biopsy demonstrated steatohepatitis consistent with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), with a NAFLD Activity Score of 4 out of 8 and fibrosis stage 0. She was counseled to adopt a calorie-restricted diet and maintain regular physical activity.

Because of ongoing hepatic steatosis and obesity two and a half years following her NAFLD diagnosis, semaglutide therapy was started. Soon after administration of the initial subcutaneous dose of semaglutide (0.25 mg), she developed nausea and vomiting. These symptoms continued, leading to hospital admission two days later with persistent nausea and vomiting, reduced oral intake, dyspnea, acute kidney injury, and marked metabolic acidosis. Management included intravenous fluids, bicarbonate, and anti-emetic medications. She was temporarily transferred to the intensive care unit for non-invasive ventilatory support. Her renal function improved following rehydration and did not require hemofiltration or dialysis. Creatine kinase was measured on admission and noted to be raised at 337 U/L. There were no other symptoms such as rash, myalgia, or dark urine. During the hospital stay, she also had episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation; however, troponin levels remained within normal limits. She was discharged after five days. At discharge, her gastrointestinal complaints had resolved, although she exhibited physical deconditioning. Over the next four months, she experienced gradually worsening muscle weakness. She reported no previous statin exposure, ingestion of supplements known to be linked to IMNM, such as mushrooms or red yeast rice, nor any other dietary supplements. Additionally, she denied a family history of myopathy, recent unusual physical exertion, or use of alcohol or illicit substances.

Four months following her first and only administration of semaglutide, her condition continued to deteriorate. She became unable to stand or maintain an upright seated position without support, and reported several days of difficulty swallowing both liquids and solids. This led her to seek care at an external hospital, where her admission labs showed a markedly elevated CK level of 3992 U/L (reference range: 12–191 U/L). She received 1000 mg of intravenous methylprednisolone each day for 5 days, under neurology guidance via telemedicine. Subsequently, she was transferred to a tertiary care center for rheumatology assessment. On arrival, she was found to have normal temperature and blood pressure, without distress. Examination revealed pronounced muscle weakness, most notably in the proximal groups (see table 1). She did not have any rashes, skin changes, pulmonary crackles, synovitis, oedema, sicca symptoms, oral ulcers, or other neurologic abnormalities.

After receiving pulse methylprednisolone, the patient was placed on a daily regimen of oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg. Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at a total dose of 2 g/kg, divided over four days, in response to her ongoing weakness and pronounced clinical manifestations. Over a period of two weeks, her CK levels demonstrated significant reduction (figure 1). She was discharged following an almost four-week hospitalization. Approximately 12 days post-discharge, she was readmitted for ten days owing to septic shock secondary to a pseudomonal urinary tract infection, during which her CK was noted to be within the normal range.

She attended multiple outpatient follow-up visits, during which her functional capacity and general health consistently improved, and her CK levels remained within the normal range. This allowed for rapid discontinuation of prednisone. Additionally, owing to financial considerations and the sustained progress without semaglutide, she did not undergo any further IVIG therapy beyond the initial administration during her hospitalization.

At her most recent outpatient visit, which occurred 10 months following her initial assessment and commencement of treatment, she had returned to her baseline level of function and had remained off immunosuppressive therapy for 8 months.

Investigations:

Laboratory evaluation revealed an elevated creatine kinase (CK) of 1237 U/L at the time of assessment, with the highest value recorded at 3992 U/L one week earlier at the referring facility. Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) levels remained within normal parameters. Aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) were measured at 191 U/L and 165 U/L, respectively, in the context of both increased serum CK and a background of fatty liver disease. Her autoimmune serology demonstrated negative results for anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-Sjogren’s syndrome type A (anti-SSA), anti-Smith, anti-U1 ribonucleoprotein (anti-U1 RNP), anti-double-stranded DNA, antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), and anti-mitochondrial antibodies. Complement concentrations and creatinine were normal, and no cytopenias were identified. Comprehensive myositis antibody testing did not detect autoantibodies against SRP, HMGCR, Jo-1, Pl-7, Pl-12, EJ, OJ, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-γ, NXP-2, or cN-1A. She tested positive for SS-B autoantibody at a low titer of 2.4 (reference: <1.0). Inflammatory markers were elevated, with an erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) of 22 mm/hour and a C-reactive protein of 22.8 mg/L. Additionally, high-sensitivity troponin was raised at 62 pg/mL (normal ≤53 pg/mL), and pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (proBNP) was measured at 4358 pg/mL (reference range: 0–125 pg/mL).

Key imaging studies consisted of MRI scans of both femurs, echocardiography, cardiac MRI, liver ultrasound with Doppler, high-resolution CT of the chest, and abdominal MRI. MRI of the femurs identified mild, symmetrical edema of the muscles bilaterally, most pronounced in the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps muscles, along with subcutaneous edema along the lateral thighs (figure 2). Echocardiography revealed an estimated left ventricular ejection fraction of 65%–70% and a right ventricular systolic pressure measuring 46 mm Hg. The cardiac MRI demonstrated mild hypokinesis in the mid-anteroseptal and anterior wall regions accompanied by myocardial edema, with no clear late gadolinium enhancement, findings which may correspond to early myocardial inflammation/myocarditis or potentially focal stress-related cardiomyopathy. There was no evidence of major myocardial scarring, infarct, or significant valvular abnormalities. Doppler liver ultrasound revealed hepatosteatosis. High-resolution chest CT ruled out interstitial lung disease and lymphadenopathy. Abdominal MRI showed enlarged steatotic liver but was otherwise unremarkable and did not reveal any lymphadenopathy.

Additional advanced investigations included a second liver biopsy and a biopsy of the thigh muscle. The liver biopsy, conducted during her hospitalization, showed several samples of hepatic tissue exhibiting small and large droplet macrovesicular steatosis that affected approximately 70% of the examined hepatic parenchyma, without notable portal inflammation or interface hepatitis. These results aligned with her history of hepatosteatosis, indicating mild advancement of her liver pathology, graded as NAFLD Activity Score 5/8, stage 1. Biopsy of the right vastus lateralis muscle revealed scattered necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers (figure 3A,B), and immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated positive staining for acid phosphatase (figure 3D) and cluster of Differentiation-68 (CD68), a macrophage marker, highlighting myophagocytosis (figure 4B). A membrane attack complex positive reaction was observed in both necrotic and non-necrotic muscle fibers (figure 4C), suggestive of an inflammatory process. Additionally, rare CD3-positive cells—indicative of T-cell presence (figure 4A)—and perivascular inflammatory cells (figure 3C) were identified. The overall muscle biopsy findings were indicative of an IMNM.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient developed a pronounced and immediate adverse response following her first and only administration of semaglutide, indicative of drug intolerance. She initially reported gastrointestinal complaints and acute kidney injury, which was presumed to be due to hypovolemia. Although her creatine kinase (CK) level was only slightly raised—an amount not consistent with rhabdomyolysis—during her first assessment, her subsequent clinical deterioration after discontinuing semaglutide, the distribution of muscle weakness, and a twelvefold increase in CK over four months post-exposure are indicative of an immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) likely related to semaglutide. Additionally, biopsy findings that aligned with drug-induced IMNM, absence of clinical signs of other underlying connective tissue diseases, and sustained clinical improvement following both the withdrawal of semaglutide and the initiation of immunosuppressive therapy, all reinforce this probable association.

It is significant that elevated transaminases were present before she initiated semaglutide therapy, a finding that may occur in myopathic conditions. Nevertheless, three years prior to starting semaglutide, liver biopsy confirmed hepatosteatosis, and her mildly increased AST/ALT levels remained after normalization of CK values. Therefore, it is probable that her chronic transaminase elevation is attributable to liver pathology rather than muscle injury. Cardiac assessment did not definitively indicate active myocarditis. Considering her swift improvement, stress-induced myopathy could also be a plausible explanation in this clinical context.

The detection of SS-B antibodies alone, alongside negative ANA and SS-A, was unanticipated. To date, the literature has not documented cases in which isolated SS-B seropositivity with absent ANA and SS-A results in IMNM. We were unable to access a creatine kinase (CK) value predating the introduction of semaglutide, so a pre-existing subclinical autoimmune IMNM cannot be entirely ruled out. Nonetheless, the 12-fold increase in CK levels and onset of worsening proximal muscle weakness following initiation of semaglutide suggest that the IMNM episode was more likely associated with drug exposure. Moreover, resolution of the patient’s severe IMNM occurred after a brief regimen of corticosteroids and IVIG and discontinuation of semaglutide. This pattern is characteristic of drug-induced IMNM, which typically responds to short-term immunosuppression upon removal of the culprit medication, contrasting with idiopathic IMNM that often necessitates extended and aggressive immunosuppressive treatment.

Treatment:

She received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for five days, after which she was started on oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg per day. Because of ongoing weakness and significant clinical findings, IVIG was administered at 2 g/kg, divided over four days. The patient was seen for follow-up on multiple occasions in the outpatient clinic, showing ongoing gains in her functional ability and general well-being, with consistently normal creatine kinase (CK) values. As a result, prednisone was tapered off successfully and relatively quickly, within two months. Owing to both financial considerations and her stable improvement while off semaglutide, she did not receive further IVIG beyond the initial hospitalization course.

Outcome and follow-up:

Ten months after her initial assessment and commencement of therapy, her most recent follow-up occurred in the outpatient clinic. At that time, she had returned to her pre-illness level of function and had been free from immunosuppressive medication for 7 months. Throughout this period, her creatine kinase remained within the normal limits post-hospitalization and immunosuppressive therapy. Subsequent echocardiogram and cardiac MRI demonstrated resolution of previously observed abnormalities. She also did not have any further episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation.

Discussion:

GLP-1 receptor agonists, including semaglutide, are increasingly prescribed and have shown efficacy in managing metabolic conditions such as obesity and type 2 diabetes. Although gastrointestinal side effects are common, these agents may also be linked to reductions in muscle mass and, in rare cases, rhabdomyolysis. In this particular case, the progression aligns more closely with IMNM rather than rhabdomyolysis. The patient's CK levels rose twelvefold over four months after a single dose of semaglutide, whereas a direct toxic response causing rhabdomyolysis would generally result in CK normalization within weeks, as observed in other drug-induced cases. Of note, a previously documented episode of rhabdomyolysis associated with tirzepatide exhibited the expected course: the patient experienced a pronounced, acute increase in CK (CK=57,014 on presentation) and showed rapid recovery upon discontinuation of the drug and hydration within days, without requiring immunosuppressive therapy.

Based on the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) Causality Categories and Criteria, the connection between semaglutide administration and the development of IMNM in our patient is classified as probable. This assessment is supported by an appropriate temporal association, the low likelihood that IMNM was triggered by other medications or underlying conditions, and the clear clinical improvement observed upon discontinuation of semaglutide. Re-exposure to the drug is considered unethical due to the severity of the adverse event that occurred. Nonetheless, we recognize that definitive causality cannot be established in this case. With semaglutide usage increasing rapidly, this case report seeks to inform healthcare professionals about this potential complication and promote rigorous pharmacovigilance. Further research will be necessary to determine whether this observed relationship is causal or merely coincidental.

The factors that may have made our patient susceptible to such a profound reaction to semaglutide remain uncertain. Given the increasing prevalence of GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy, investigations into potential predisposing elements will be especially important if future cases emerge displaying a similar temporal pattern with semaglutide exposure.
