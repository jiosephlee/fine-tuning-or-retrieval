Title: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

Brian Lam, Eleni Tiniakou, Xinhai Robert Zhang, Shervin Assassi

Abstract:

Summary: This case report presents a woman in her 60s who developed immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) following administration of semaglutide. She exhibited a 4-month progression of symptoms including dysphagia, dysphonia, proximal muscle weakness, and elevated creatine kinase levels. Notably, symptom onset followed a single dose of semaglutide. Diagnostic evaluation demonstrated necrotising myopathy with inflammatory cell infiltration on muscle biopsy. Treatment with corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) led to clinical improvement. The patient was able to regain her previous level of function after discontinuing immunosuppression for several months and by avoiding further exposure to glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists. This account represents the initial report of IMNM possibly linked to GLP-1 receptor agonist use, with symptom resolution following a brief course of immunosuppressive therapy and IVIG.

Background:

Over the past few years, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have seen widespread use in the management of metabolic conditions such as type 2 diabetes and obesity. Among the side effects reported with these agents, reduction in skeletal muscle mass has been noted. Recently, a case report documented an instance of rhabdomyolysis exhibiting necrotizing characteristics linked to tirzepatide, a widely prescribed GLP-1 receptor agonist. In that case, the patient experienced a creatine kinase (CK) elevation reaching several thousand units while on therapy, with CK levels beginning to decrease within a few days following cessation of the medication—findings that align with drug-induced rhabdomyolysis.

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) represents a distinct form of myopathy marked by rapidly progressive proximal muscle weakness, histological evidence of myofibre necrosis, and minimal lymphocytic infiltration. The presence of autoantibodies against hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and signal recognition particle (SRP) is strongly linked to IMNM. Statin therapy, frequently prescribed for metabolic conditions, has a notable association with HMGCR antibody positivity. Historically, several individuals on statins who manifested necrotising myopathy were categorized as having toxic myopathy or polymyositis prior to recognizing the relationship with HMGCR autoantibodies. In these instances, muscle weakness and elevated creatine kinase levels often persisted despite discontinuing the statin, and subsequent clinical improvement with immunosuppressive treatment pointed toward an autoimmune etiology, which culminated in the recognition of HMGCR autoantibody as a signature marker for this condition. Nonetheless, approximately one-third of IMNM patients test negative for known autoantibodies. This seronegativity presents both diagnostic difficulties and implies that other, as yet unidentified, autoantibodies or triggers may be implicated in the disease.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 60s with a past medical history of obesity, hepatic steatosis, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and a remote episode of deep vein thrombosis/pulmonary embolism presented with gradually worsening proximal muscle weakness along with difficulties in swallowing and speaking.

Prior to this decline, she had enjoyed good health and was fully capable of managing her daily living activities. Three years before her condition worsened, she was being evaluated as an outpatient for persistently elevated liver transaminases. At that time, mild increases in aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) were found—about twice the upper limit of normal—with ALT higher than AST. Other liver markers, including alkaline phosphatase, albumin, bilirubin, and platelet count, were all normal. The patient reported no alcohol consumption and was not on any medications associated with liver toxicity. Abdominal ultrasound identified an enlarged liver with diffusely increased echogenicity, suggestive of hepatic steatosis. Laboratory workup for viral hepatitis and autoantibodies—including anti-nuclear, anti-mitochondrial, anti-smooth muscle, and liver-kidney microsomal antibodies—was negative. Liver biopsy confirmed steatohepatitis compatible with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), with an NAFLD Activity Score of 4 out of 8 and stage 0 fibrosis. She was instructed to adhere to a calorie-restricted diet and engage in consistent physical activity.

Ongoing hepatic steatosis and obesity were noted 2.5 years following her NAFLD diagnosis, prompting the initiation of semaglutide therapy. Shortly after the first administration of semaglutide (0.25 mg subcutaneously), she developed nausea and vomiting that continued. Two days later, due to persistent symptoms including nausea, vomiting, limited oral intake, shortness of breath, acute renal failure, and marked metabolic acidosis, she was admitted to the hospital. Management included intravenous fluids, bicarbonate, and antiemetics. She required a brief intensive care unit stay for non-invasive ventilatory support. Her kidney function subsequently recovered with hydration, obviating the need for haemodialysis or haemofiltration. On admission, creatine kinase was measured at 337 U/L. She exhibited no other related signs, such as myalgia, rash, or dark-colored urine. During hospitalization, she also experienced episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, though her serum troponin remained within normal limits. She was released from the hospital after 5 days. Although her gastrointestinal complaints had subsided at discharge, she exhibited physical deconditioning. In the ensuing 4 months, she reported gradually worsening weakness. She specifically denied previous statin use, significant ingestion of supplements linked to IMNM such as mushrooms or red yeast rice, or other nutritional supplements. Additionally, there was no notable family history of myopathy, nor recent intensive or prolonged physical exertion, or alcohol or illicit drug use.

Four months following her initial and only administration of semaglutide, her condition progressively worsened. She became unable to stand or maintain an upright seated position without support and experienced several days of increasing difficulty swallowing both liquids and solids. Consequently, she sought care at an outside hospital, where admission laboratory tests revealed a creatine kinase (CK) level of 3992 U/L (reference range: 12–191 U/L). Management included intravenous methylprednisolone 1000 mg daily for five days, administered with guidance from neurology via telemedicine. Subsequently, she was transferred to a tertiary care facility for rheumatology assessment. On arrival, her vital signs showed she was afebrile and normotensive, and she appeared comfortable otherwise. Examination demonstrated marked weakness predominantly in proximal muscle groups (see table 1). There were no observed rashes, cutaneous abnormalities, pulmonary crackles, synovitis, edema, sicca features, oral ulcers, or additional neurological deficits.

After receiving pulse therapy with methylprednisolone, she transitioned to daily oral prednisone at a dose of 1 mg/kg. Given her ongoing muscle weakness and significant clinical severity, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at 2 g/kg, divided over a period of 4 days. Within 2 weeks, her creatine kinase (CK) levels demonstrated substantial improvement (see figure 1). Her hospitalization spanned almost 4 weeks before she was discharged. Subsequently, 12 days post-discharge, she was readmitted for 10 days owing to septic shock secondary to a pseudomonal urinary tract infection; during this admission, her CK remained within the normal range.

She continued to be evaluated in the outpatient setting, where further improvements were noted in both her physical functioning and general health, with CK levels consistently normal. These findings permitted a successful and expedited tapering of her prednisone. Because of her continued clinical progress and the cost of therapy, she did not receive further IVIG following her initial hospital-based course, particularly after discontinuing semaglutide.

She was last seen in the outpatient setting 10 months after her initial evaluation and treatment initiation and had regained her baseline functional status, off immunosuppression for 8 months.

Investigations:

Her laboratory assessment revealed an elevated creatine kinase (CK) level of 1237 U/L at the time of evaluation, with a maximum value of 3992 U/L documented one week earlier at the referring institution. Thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH) was within the normal range. Aspartate aminotransferase (AST) was 191 U/L and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) was 165 U/L, findings observed in the context of increased CK and a background of hepatic steatosis. The autoantibody screen demonstrated negative results for anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-Sjogren’s syndrome antibody type A (anti-SSA), anti-Smith antibodies, anti-U1 ribonucleoprotein (anti-U1 RNP), anti-double-stranded DNA, antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), and anti-mitochondrial antibodies. Complement concentrations and creatinine were within normal limits, and no cytopenias were identified. Myositis-specific antibody testing was negative for SRP, HMGCR, Jo-1, Pl-7, Pl-12, EJ, OJ, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-y, NXP-2, and cN-1A antibodies. She did have a positive SS-B autoantibody at low titre (2.4; reference <1.0). Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was elevated at 22 mm/hour and C-reactive protein (CRP) was increased at 22.8 mg/L. Additional significant findings included a high-sensitivity troponin level of 62 pg/mL (reference range ≤53 pg/mL) and a pro B-type natriuretic peptide (proBNP) of 4358 pg/mL (reference: 0–125 pg/mL).

Significant imaging studies encompassed MRI scans of both femurs, an echocardiogram, cardiac MRI, liver ultrasound with Doppler, high-resolution CT of the chest, and an abdominal MRI. The MRI of both femurs demonstrated mild, bilateral, and symmetrical muscular edema, predominantly seen in the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps muscles, in addition to subcutaneous edema over the lateral regions of both thighs (see figure 2). The echocardiogram revealed an estimated left ventricular ejection fraction between 65% and 70%, with a right ventricular systolic pressure measured at 46 mm Hg. Cardiac MRI identified mild hypokinesis in the mid-anteroseptal and anterior wall, accompanied by myocardial edema, without clear evidence of late gadolinium enhancement, suggestive of myocardial inflammation or early myocarditis, though focal stress-induced cardiomyopathy could not be excluded. No notable myocardial infarction, scarring, or valvular dysfunction was detected. The liver ultrasound with Doppler identified hepatosteatosis. High-resolution chest CT was unremarkable for interstitial lung disease or lymphadenopathy. Abdominal MRI showed steatotic enlargement of the liver but was otherwise within normal limits and showed no evidence of lymphadenopathy.

Additional diagnostic evaluations included a repeat liver biopsy as well as a biopsy of thigh muscle. The liver biopsy, carried out during her hospital stay, showed multiple cores of liver tissue featuring both small and large droplet macrovesicular steatosis involving approximately 70% of the hepatic parenchyma sampled, without notable portal inflammation or evidence of interface hepatitis. These histologic results aligned with her prior diagnosis of hepatosteatosis, indicating mild progression of hepatic pathology corresponding to a NAFLD Activity Score of grade 5/8 and stage 1 disease. 

Examination of the right vastus lateralis muscle biopsy revealed scattered necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers (figure 3A,B). Immunohistochemical staining displayed acid phosphatase positivity (figure 3D) along with cluster of Differentiation-68 (CD68) expression—a marker for macrophages—demonstrating myophagocytosis (figure 4B). In addition, staining for the membrane attack complex was positive in both necrotic and intact muscle fibers (figure 4C), consistent with the presence of an inflammatory process. Scattered CD3-positive T cells were detected (figure 4A), alongside perivascular inflammatory cells (figure 3C). The constellation of findings on muscle biopsy supported a diagnosis of immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM).

Differential diagnosis:

The patient developed a pronounced and immediate reaction following her first and only administration of semaglutide, which indicated intolerance. She initially presented with gastrointestinal symptoms and acute renal failure presumed to be secondary to hypovolemia. Although her creatine kinase (CK) was only mildly raised at the first assessment, which did not match typical rhabdomyolysis, her clinical deterioration despite discontinuing semaglutide, the pattern of her muscle weakness, and a twelvefold increase in CK over the subsequent four months after exposure all indicate the likely development of immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) related to semaglutide. Additionally, the histopathological findings compatible with drug-induced IMNM, the absence of signs of an underlying connective tissue disorder, and her ongoing improvement following initial immunosuppressive therapy and withdrawal of semaglutide provide further evidence supporting this connection.

It is significant that she exhibited elevated transaminases before initiating semaglutide, a finding that may occur in myopathies. Nevertheless, a liver biopsy conducted three years prior to starting semaglutide confirmed hepatosteatosis, and her marginally increased AST/ALT levels remained despite normalization of CK values. Therefore, the chronic elevation of her transaminases is most plausibly attributable to hepatic, rather than muscular, causes. Furthermore, her cardiac evaluation did not provide definitive evidence of ongoing myocarditis. Given the context and her swift improvement, stress-induced myopathy would also represent a plausible explanation.

Her isolated SS-B antibody positivity, in conjunction with negative ANA and SS-A results, was unexpected. There are no documented cases in the literature of isolated SS-B positivity with negative ANA and SS-A as a precursor to IMNM. We were unable to locate a CK level from before she began semaglutide therapy, so it is not possible to fully rule out pre-existing subclinical autoimmune IMNM prior to semaglutide initiation. Nonetheless, the twelvefold elevation in CK and the emergence of progressive proximal muscle weakness following the start of semaglutide suggest a higher probability that this IMNM episode was induced by the medication. Furthermore, her severe IMNM remitted after a brief regimen of corticosteroids and IVIG, combined with the discontinuation of semaglutide. This pattern is more characteristic of drug-induced IMNM, which typically responds to short-term immunosuppression upon withdrawal of the causative medication, whereas idiopathic IMNM usually necessitates more extended and aggressive immunosuppressive management.

Treatment:

She received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for 5 days, which was followed by daily oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg. Because her weakness persisted and she exhibited severe manifestations, IVIG was administered at a total dose of 2 g/kg, divided over 4 days. She was evaluated multiple times in the outpatient clinic, where she continued to show gains in functional capacity and general health, and her CK levels consistently remained within normal limits. Owing to this progress, her prednisone could be tapered quickly, allowing discontinuation over a 2-month period. Considering both financial factors and her ongoing stable condition after discontinuation of semaglutide, she did not require any further IVIG beyond the initial hospital treatment.

Outcome and follow-up:

At her most recent outpatient follow-up, conducted 10 months after beginning therapy, she had returned to her prior level of functioning and had been off immunosuppressive medication for the previous 7 months. Her creatine kinase (CK) measurements stayed within normal limits following her hospitalization and immunosuppressive treatment. Subsequent echocardiogram and cardiac MRI demonstrated resolution of the earlier abnormalities. Additionally, there was no recurrence of her paroxysmal atrial fibrillation.

Discussion:

GLP-1 receptor agonists like semaglutide are increasingly prescribed and have shown efficacy in treating metabolic conditions such as obesity and type 2 diabetes. While gastrointestinal side effects are most frequently observed, these agents have also been linked to reduced muscle mass and, in some instances, rhabdomyolysis. In this case, the patient’s presentation aligns more closely with IMNM than with rhabdomyolysis. The patient’s CK values rose 12-fold over a 4-month period after a single administration of semaglutide, whereas if semaglutide toxicity were responsible—consistent with patterns seen in other instances of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis—CK would be anticipated to return to baseline within weeks. Furthermore, a previously described rhabdomyolysis case related to tirzepatide exhibited the expected pattern: an acute, significant increase in CK (with a presentation value of 57,014), followed by rapid resolution upon discontinuation of the drug and hydration within several days, and did not require immunosuppressive therapy.

Based on the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) Causality Categories and Criteria, there appears to be a probable association between the administration of semaglutide and the development of IMNM in this patient. This assessment is supported by an appropriate temporal correlation, the lack of alternative explanations such as other illnesses or medications, and a notable clinical improvement upon discontinuation of the drug. Re-exposure to semaglutide would be considered unethical in light of the severity of the adverse event observed. Nevertheless, we recognize that this evidence does not establish a definitive causal connection in this case. Given the widespread increase in semaglutide use, we present this case to alert clinicians to a potential serious adverse reaction and to emphasize the importance of pharmacovigilance. Additional research will be necessary to determine whether the relationship is indeed causal or merely coincidental.

Furthermore, the specific factors that may have made our patient susceptible to this pronounced reaction to semaglutide remain undetermined. As the use of GLP-1 receptor agonists continues to rise, investigations into possible risk factors will become increasingly important if more cases with a strong temporal link to semaglutide are documented.
