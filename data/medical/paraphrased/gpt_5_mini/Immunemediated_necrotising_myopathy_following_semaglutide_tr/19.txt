Title: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

Brian Lam, Eleni Tiniakou, Xinhai Robert Zhang, Shervin Assassi

Abstract:

Summary: This case report presents a woman in her sixties who developed immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) following administration of semaglutide. Over a four-month period, she experienced worsening dysphagia, dysphonia, proximal muscle weakness, and elevated creatine kinase levels. The onset of her symptoms followed a single dose of semaglutide. Diagnostic evaluation demonstrated necrotising myopathy with inflammatory infiltrates on muscle biopsy. She showed improvement after being treated with corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG). The patient progressively recovered and ultimately regained her previous functional abilities, remaining off immunosuppressive therapy for several months while continuing to abstain from glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists. This report details the first known instance of IMNM likely linked to GLP-1 receptor agonist use, with resolution after a brief course of immunosuppression and IVIG.

Background:

Recently, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have become widely prescribed for metabolic conditions such as type 2 diabetes and obesity. Loss of skeletal muscle mass has been noted as one of the side effects linked to this group of drugs. A new case report documented rhabdomyolysis with necrotizing characteristics associated with tirzepatide, another GLP-1 receptor agonist commonly used in practice. In this instance, the patient experienced marked creatine kinase (CK) elevation, reaching values in the thousands, while on the medication, and CK levels began to decline within several days following discontinuation of the drug, indicative of drug-related rhabdomyolysis.

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) represents a specific category of myopathy distinguished by severe proximal muscle weakness, histological evidence of myofibre necrosis, and minimal lymphocytic infiltration. Anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (SRP) antibodies are strongly linked to IMNM. Statins, frequently prescribed for metabolic conditions, have a notable correlation with the presence of HMGCR autoantibodies. Prior to the recognition of the HMGCR autoantibody, several patients on statin therapy developed necrotising myopathy manifestations and were often misdiagnosed as having toxic myopathy or polymyositis. In such cases, myopathy and elevated CK levels persisted despite discontinuing statins, with subsequent improvement observed upon administration of immunosuppressive therapy. These findings indicated an underlying autoimmune process and eventually led to the identification of the HMGCR autoantibody as a key feature of this disorder. However, it is important to recognize that approximately one-third of IMNM cases lack detectable autoantibodies. The absence of autoantibodies in these individuals poses diagnostic difficulties and implies that there may be additional associated autoantibodies or triggering factors yet to be characterized.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 60s with a past medical history including obesity, hepatic steatosis, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and prior episodes of deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism presented with worsening proximal muscle weakness, difficulties with swallowing, and changes in her voice.

She had previously enjoyed good health and remained fully independent in daily life. Three years before this decline, she underwent outpatient workup due to elevated transaminase levels. At that time, her aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels were mildly increased, roughly two times the upper limit of normal, with ALT being higher than AST. Her alkaline phosphatase, albumin, bilirubin, and platelet levels were within normal ranges. She denied alcohol intake and was not on any medications known to cause liver injury. Abdominal ultrasound revealed hepatomegaly with uniformly increased echogenicity, likely indicative of hepatic steatosis. Screening for viral hepatitis, anti-nuclear, anti-mitochondrial, anti-smooth muscle, and liver-kidney microsomal antibodies was negative. Liver biopsy demonstrated steatohepatitis aligned with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), yielding a NAFLD Activity Score of 4/8 and stage 0 fibrosis. The patient was counseled to adopt a low-calorie diet and to engage in routine physical activity.

Owing to ongoing hepatic steatosis and obesity 2.5 years following her NAFLD diagnosis, semaglutide therapy was initiated. Shortly after administration of her initial subcutaneous semaglutide dose (0.25 mg), she developed nausea and vomiting. These symptoms continued, leading to her hospitalisation two days later for nausea, vomiting, reduced oral intake, dyspnoea, acute renal failure, and severe metabolic acidosis. Management included intravenous fluids and bicarbonate, alongside anti-emetic medications. She required a brief stay in the intensive care unit for non-invasive ventilation. Her renal function subsequently improved with intravenous hydration, and neither haemofiltration nor haemodialysis was necessary. On admission, her creatine kinase was measured at 337 U/L. She reported no accompanying symptoms such as rash, myalgia, or dark-coloured urine. During the same hospital admission, she developed paroxysmal atrial fibrillation; serum troponin values were within normal limits. The patient was discharged after five days. Upon discharge, her gastrointestinal complaints had subsided, although she exhibited deconditioning. Over the next four months, she experienced worsening weakness. She stated she had never used statins and denied significant intake of IMNM-associated supplements, such as mushrooms or red yeast rice, as well as any other dietary supplements. She also reported no relevant family history of myopathy, recent excessive or prolonged exercise, alcohol consumption, or illicit drug use.

Four months after her initial and only administration of semaglutide, her condition continued to worsen. She became unable to maintain an upright posture, either standing or sitting, without help, and began experiencing difficulty swallowing both liquids and solids over several days. These symptoms led her to seek care at an outside hospital, where her admission laboratory testing showed an elevated CK of 3992 U/L (reference range: 12–191 U/L). Neurology was consulted remotely, and she was started on 1000 mg intravenous methylprednisolone daily for 5 days. Subsequently, she was transferred to a tertiary hospital for further rheumatology assessment. Upon arrival, she was afebrile, had a normal blood pressure, and was not in apparent distress. On physical examination, there was pronounced weakness, particularly involving the proximal muscle groups (see table 1), and there were no signs of rash, cutaneous changes, pulmonary crackles, synovitis, edema, sicca symptoms, oral ulcerations, or additional neurological findings.

After receiving pulse methylprednisolone, she was transitioned to oral prednisone at a dosage of 1 mg/kg per day. Due to ongoing weakness and severe symptoms, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at 2 g/kg divided across 4 days. Over the subsequent two weeks, her creatine kinase (CK) levels significantly improved (figure 1). She was discharged following a hospitalization of nearly 4 weeks. Twelve days post-discharge, she was readmitted for 10 days because of septic shock secondary to pseudomonal urinary tract infection, during which her CK remained within normal limits.

She attended multiple outpatient follow-up visits, during which her functional capacity and general health continued to improve, with CK levels remaining consistently within normal limits. As a result, she underwent a rapid taper off prednisone without issue. Given financial considerations and her sustained clinical progress following cessation of semaglutide, she received no further IVIG beyond the initial series administered during her hospitalization.

At her most recent outpatient assessment, 10 months after starting therapy, she had restored her pre-illness functional status and had remained off immunosuppressive treatment for 8 months.

Investigations:

Her laboratory evaluation revealed a creatine kinase (CK) level of 1237 U/L at the time of assessment, with a prior maximum value of 3992 U/L recorded one week earlier at the referring institution. Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) was within the normal range. Aspartate aminotransferase (AST) measured 191 U/L and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) 165 U/L, in the context of elevated CK and known fatty liver disease. Autoantibody testing showed negative results for anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-Sjogren’s syndrome antibodies type A (anti-SSA), anti-Smith antibodies, anti-U1 ribonucleoprotein antibodies (anti-U1 RNP), anti-double-stranded DNA, antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), and anti-mitochondrial antibodies. Complement levels and creatinine were within normal limits, and there was no evidence of cytopenia. Myositis-specific autoantibody testing returned negative for SRP, HMGCR, Jo-1, Pl-7, Pl-12, EJ, OJ, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-y, NXP-2, and cN-1A. However, SS-B autoantibody was detected at a low titer of 2.4 (reference range: <1.0). Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was elevated at 22 mm/hour, and C-reactive protein (CRP) was increased at 22.8 mg/L. Additionally, high-sensitivity troponin measured 62 pg/mL (normal ≤53 pg/mL), and pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (proBNP) was found to be 4358 pg/mL (reference range: 0–125 pg/mL).

Imaging studies performed included MRI of both femurs, echocardiogram, cardiac MRI, liver ultrasound with Doppler, high-resolution chest CT, and abdominal MRI. MRI of the femurs showed mild and symmetric muscle edema bilaterally, particularly in the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps, along with subcutaneous edema on the lateral sides of both thighs (figure 2). Echocardiography demonstrated an estimated left ventricular ejection fraction between 65% and 70%, and a right ventricular systolic pressure measured at 46 mm Hg. Cardiac MRI revealed mild hypokinesis of the mid-anteroseptal and anterior wall areas accompanied by myocardial edema, but lacked definitive late gadolinium enhancement, findings which could be consistent with myocardial inflammation or early myocarditis, as well as possible focal stress-induced cardiomyopathy. No notable myocardial scarring or infarction was found, and there was no evidence of significant valvular disease. Liver ultrasound with Doppler showed hepatosteatosis. High-resolution CT of the chest did not show interstitial lung disease or lymphadenopathy. Abdominal MRI demonstrated steatotic enlargement of the liver without other abnormal findings or lymphadenopathy.

Further diagnostic evaluation included a repeat hepatic biopsy and a biopsy of the thigh muscle. The liver biopsy performed during hospitalization revealed several cores of liver tissue exhibiting both small and large droplet macrovesicular steatosis, involving roughly 70% of the examined hepatic parenchyma. There was no evidence of notable portal inflammation or interface hepatitis. These results aligned with her previous diagnosis of hepatosteatosis, indicating mild worsening of her liver disease to a Nonalcoholic Fatty Liver Disease (NAFLD) Activity Score of 5 out of 8, stage 1. The right vastus lateralis muscle biopsy showed scattered necrotic as well as regenerating muscle fibers (figure 3A,B). Immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated acid phosphatase staining (figure 3D) and CD68 positivity, a macrophage marker, associated with myophagocytosis (figure 4B). A positive stain for membrane attack complex was noted on both necrotic and intact myofibres (figure 4C), indicating an inflammatory reaction. Additionally, sporadic CD3-positive cells, a T-cell marker (figure 4A), and perivascular inflammatory infiltrates (figure 3C) were identified. The histopathological features observed in the muscle biopsy were indicative of immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM).

Differential diagnosis:

The patient developed an immediate and notable adverse response to her first and only dose of semaglutide, indicative of drug intolerance. Her initial presentation was marked by gastrointestinal symptoms and renal failure, likely due to hypovolemia. Although creatine kinase (CK) was only mildly elevated at the time—making rhabdomyolysis unlikely—her progressive deterioration after discontinuing semaglutide, the pattern of muscle weakness, and a twelvefold rise in CK levels over four months following exposure point towards an IMNM possibly linked to semaglutide. Additionally, muscle biopsy findings compatible with drug-induced IMNM, absence of clinical signs of a coexisting connective tissue disorder, and persistent recovery following early immunosuppressive therapy and withdrawal of semaglutide lend further evidence to this correlation.

It is significant that elevated transaminases were present before she started semaglutide therapy, a finding sometimes observed in myopathies. Nevertheless, liver biopsy had demonstrated hepatosteatosis three years before initiating semaglutide, and her mild increase in AST/ALT continued even after CK concentrations returned to normal. Therefore, her chronically raised transaminase levels are most likely hepatic in etiology, rather than secondary to muscle injury. Additionally, cardiac assessments did not definitively indicate ongoing myocarditis. Considering the clinical context and her swift improvement, stress-induced myopathy would also be a plausible diagnosis.

The finding of a positive SS-B antibody alongside negative ANA and SS-A was unanticipated. To date, there are no documented cases in the literature detailing isolated SS-B positivity with concurrent negative ANA and SS-A resulting in IMNM. Regrettably, no creatine kinase (CK) level was obtained prior to the initiation of semaglutide, making it impossible to fully rule out a pre-existing, subclinical autoimmune IMNM before starting semaglutide. Nonetheless, the 12-fold increase in CK and the gradual onset of proximal muscle weakness following semaglutide use support the possibility that IMNM in this case was linked to the medication. Furthermore, the patient’s severe IMNM subsided following a brief regimen of corticosteroids and IVIG combined with discontinuation of semaglutide. This trajectory is more typical of drug-induced IMNM, which often responds to short-term immunosuppressive treatment once the causative agent is stopped, in contrast to idiopathic IMNM, which generally demands prolonged and more aggressive immunosuppression.

Treatment:

She received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for 5 days, then transitioned to oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg each day. Because of ongoing weakness and severe symptoms, IVIG was administered at 2 g/kg, divided over 4 days. Subsequent follow-up visits in the outpatient clinic showed steady gains in both functional ability and overall health, with creatine kinase (CK) levels remaining consistently within normal limits. As a result, prednisone was able to be discontinued over a relatively short 2-month period. Given her maintained improvement off semaglutide and financial factors, no additional IVIG was provided following her initial inpatient course.

Outcome and follow-up:

Ten months after her initial assessment and commencement of therapy, she was evaluated again in the outpatient clinic. At that time, she had restored her previous level of function and had been off immunosuppressive therapy for seven months. Her creatine kinase levels stayed within normal limits subsequent to hospitalisation and immunosuppressive treatment. Follow-up echocardiography and cardiac MRI demonstrated resolution of the previously identified abnormalities. Additionally, she remained free of further episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation.

Discussion:

GLP-1 receptor agonists, including semaglutide, are increasingly prescribed and have shown efficacy in treating metabolic conditions such as obesity and type 2 diabetes. These agents frequently cause gastrointestinal side effects; however, there is also evidence to suggest a link to reduced muscle mass and possible rhabdomyolysis. In this case, the patient’s symptoms align more closely with IMNM rather than rhabdomyolysis. The patient’s CK values rose 12-fold over a period of 4 months after a single administration of semaglutide, whereas a direct myotoxic effect from semaglutide, as observed in other instances of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis, would typically be accompanied by normalization of CK within several weeks. Additionally, a previously described instance of tirzepatide-related rhabdomyolysis followed the expected course: an acute substantial increase in CK (CK=57 014 upon presentation) with rapid resolution after discontinuation of the drug and hydration, and no requirement for immunosuppressive therapy.

Applying the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) Causality Categories and Criteria, semaglutide is assessed as probably associated with IMNM in this instance, since the temporal association is plausible, IMNM is unlikely to be attributable to alternative diseases or medications, and there was an appropriate clinical improvement following discontinuation. Re-exposure to semaglutide is inadvisable due to the significant adverse event. Nonetheless, we recognize that a definitive causal relationship has not been established. With the widespread use of semaglutide, this case report seeks to inform clinicians of this possible side effect and reinforce the importance of pharmacovigilance. Further research will be required to determine whether the relationship is causative or merely coincidental.

The underlying predisposition for our patient’s marked response to semaglutide remains unknown. Given the increasing use of GLP-1 receptor agonists, investigations into potential predisposing factors will be essential, particularly if additional cases with a close temporal link to semaglutide are identified.
