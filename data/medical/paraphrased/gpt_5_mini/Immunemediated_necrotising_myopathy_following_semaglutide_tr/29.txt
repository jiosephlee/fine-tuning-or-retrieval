Title: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

Brian Lam, Eleni Tiniakou, Xinhai Robert Zhang, Shervin Assassi

Abstract:

Summary: This case involves a woman in her 60s who developed immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) following administration of semaglutide. She experienced worsening dysphagia, dysphonia, proximal muscle weakness, and elevated creatine kinase over a 4-month period, with symptoms emerging after a single semaglutide dose. Diagnostic evaluation demonstrated findings compatible with necrotising myopathy, including biopsy evidence of inflammatory infiltrates. She demonstrated clinical improvement upon treatment with corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG). Over time, the patient was able to regain her baseline level of function, remained off immunosuppressive therapy for several months, and continued to abstain from glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists. This report details the first known instance of IMNM likely linked to use of a GLP-1 receptor agonist, in which the patient showed improvement after a brief course of immunosuppression and IVIG.

Background:

Over the past few years, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have become widely utilized in the management of metabolic conditions such as obesity and type 2 diabetes. Although these medications are generally well-tolerated, reports have documented a reduction in skeletal muscle mass as a potential adverse effect. Recently, a case report detailed an instance of rhabdomyolysis with necrotizing characteristics linked to tirzepatide, a GLP-1 receptor agonist that is frequently prescribed. In this report, the patient experienced a marked increase in creatine kinase (CK), reaching levels in the thousands during ongoing therapy. Following cessation of the drug, CK concentrations began to decrease within several days, indicating a drug-induced rhabdomyolysis.

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) represents a unique form of myopathy defined by rapid onset of weakness affecting proximal muscles, histopathologically marked by necrotic muscle fibers with scant lymphocyte infiltration. Autoantibodies targeting hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and signal recognition particle (SRP) have a strong association with IMNM. Notably, statin therapy—which is frequently prescribed for metabolic disorders—has been linked to HMGCR autoantibody positivity. Prior to recognizing the relationship with HMGCR autoantibodies, multiple patients exposed to statins developed signs of necrotising myopathy and were commonly misclassified as having toxic myopathy or polymyositis. These individuals typically exhibited ongoing muscle dysfunction and elevated creatine kinase levels even after discontinuing statins. The resolution of symptoms upon introduction of immunosuppressive treatment implied an autoimmune basis, ultimately leading to identification of HMGCR autoantibody as a defining feature. It should be highlighted, nevertheless, that approximately one-third of IMNM cases lack detectable autoantibodies. This seronegativity complicates diagnosis and points toward the existence of undiscovered associated autoantibodies and possible etiological factors.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 60s, whose past medical history included obesity, hepatosteatosis, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and a distant history of deep venous thrombosis and pulmonary embolism, presented with worsening proximal muscle weakness along with difficulty swallowing and speaking.

Previously, she was healthy and fully independent in daily activities. Three years before her symptoms worsened, she underwent outpatient workup for elevated transaminases. At that time, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) were mildly increased, roughly double the upper normal threshold, with ALT levels exceeding AST. Alkaline phosphatase, albumin, bilirubin, and platelet counts were all within reference ranges. She reported no alcohol consumption and was not taking medications associated with liver toxicity. Abdominal ultrasound revealed an enlarged liver with uniformly increased echotexture, likely indicative of hepatic steatosis. Testing for viral hepatitis, as well as anti-nuclear, anti-mitochondrial, anti-smooth muscle, and liver-kidney microsomal antibodies, yielded negative results. Liver biopsy demonstrated steatohepatitis consistent with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) and a NAFLD Activity Score of 4/8, stage 0. She was instructed to adopt a low-calorie diet and engage in regular physical activity.

Following ongoing fatty liver and obesity 2.5 years after being diagnosed with NAFLD, semaglutide therapy was initiated. Shortly after administration of her first subcutaneous dose (0.25 mg), she developed nausea and vomiting. These symptoms continued, leading to hospital admission two days later for nausea, vomiting, decreased oral intake, dyspnoea, acute renal failure, and marked metabolic acidosis. Management included intravenous fluids, bicarbonate, and anti-emetics. She was briefly moved to the ICU for non-invasive ventilatory support. Her kidney function subsequently recovered with intravenous hydration and neither haemofiltration nor haemodialysis were required. Creatine kinase (CK) on admission was elevated at 337 U/L. She exhibited no additional symptoms such as skin rash, muscle aches, or dark urine. While hospitalised, she experienced episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, but her serum troponin remained normal. Discharge occurred after five days; gastrointestinal symptoms had abated although she remained physically deconditioned. In the months after discharge, she developed progressive weakness. She reported no previous statin use, did not take supplements linked with IMNM such as mushrooms or red yeast rice, nor any other nutritional supplements. Additionally, she denied a pertinent family history of myopathy, recent intense or prolonged exercise, or use of alcohol or illicit drugs.

Four months after her first and only dose of semaglutide, her condition further deteriorated. She became unable to stand or sit upright without assistance, and reported several days of difficulty swallowing both liquids and solids. These symptoms led her to seek care at an outside hospital, where initial laboratory testing showed a creatine kinase (CK) of 3992 U/L (reference range: 12–191 U/L). She received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for 5 days, managed by a neurology team via telemedicine. Subsequently, she was transferred to a tertiary care center for rheumatology assessment. On arrival, she was afebrile, normotensive, and appeared comfortable. Physical exam demonstrated marked muscle weakness, most pronounced in the proximal muscle groups (see table 1). No rashes, skin abnormalities, crackles, synovitis, edema, sicca symptoms, oral ulcers, or other neurological deficits were observed.

After receiving pulse therapy with methylprednisolone, oral prednisone was initiated at a dosage of 1 mg/kg per day. Due to ongoing muscle weakness and pronounced symptoms, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at 2 g/kg divided across 4 days. Over the subsequent 2 weeks, her CK levels demonstrated significant improvement (figure 1). She was discharged following an almost 4-week inpatient stay. Approximately 12 days post-discharge, she was readmitted for a 10-day period due to septic shock secondary to pseudomonal urinary tract infection, during which her CK values remained within normal limits.

She underwent multiple outpatient follow-up visits, during which she demonstrated ongoing enhancement in both functional ability and general well-being, alongside consistently normal CK measurements. Consequently, her prednisone was able to be tapered quickly. Owing to financial considerations and her sustained improvement in the absence of semaglutide, no further IVIG was administered beyond the initial hospital course.

Her most recent outpatient appointment occurred 10 months after the initial assessment and start of therapy, at which time she had returned to her baseline level of function and had been off immunosuppressive therapy for 8 months.

Investigations:

Her laboratory evaluation revealed a CK of 1237 U/L at the time of assessment, with a highest recorded value of 3992 U/L documented one week earlier at the facility from which she was transferred. TSH was found to be within the reference range. AST measured 191 U/L and ALT was 165 U/L, in the context of elevated CK levels and a previous diagnosis of fatty liver. The autoantibody screen was significant for negative results in ANA, anti-SSA, anti-Smith, anti-U1 RNP, anti-double-stranded DNA, ANCA, and anti-mitochondrial antibodies. Complement levels and creatinine were normal, and there was no evidence of cytopenias. A myositis-specific antibody panel returned negative results for SRP, HMGCR, Jo-1, Pl-7, Pl-12, EJ, OJ, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-y, NXP-2, and cN-1A autoantibodies. However, SS-B autoantibody was detected at low titre, measuring 2.4 (reference <1.0). ESR was raised at 22 mm/hour, as was C-reactive protein at 22.8 mg/L. Of note, high-sensitivity troponin was elevated to 62 pg/mL (reference ≤53 pg/mL), and proBNP was 4358 pg/mL (reference: 0–125 pg/mL).

Significant imaging studies included MRI of both femurs, echocardiography, cardiac MRI, liver ultrasound with Doppler, high-resolution chest CT, and abdominal MRI. The MRI of the bilateral femurs demonstrated mild, symmetric edema in both proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps muscles, as well as subcutaneous edema along the lateral sides of both thighs (figure 2). The echocardiogram showed a left ventricular ejection fraction estimated at 65%–70% and a right ventricular systolic pressure measured at 46 mm Hg. Cardiac MRI demonstrated mild hypokinesis in the mid-anteroseptal and anterior walls, accompanied by myocardial edema without definitive late gadolinium enhancement, findings which may indicate myocardial inflammation or early myocarditis, or possibly localized stress-induced cardiomyopathy. There was no evidence of significant myocardial scarring, infarction, or major valvular abnormalities. Liver ultrasound with Doppler revealed fatty infiltration of the liver. The high-resolution CT scan of the chest did not demonstrate interstitial lung disease or lymphadenopathy. Abdominal MRI indicated hepatomegaly due to steatosis, but was otherwise unremarkable and without lymphadenopathy.

Additional diagnostic evaluations involved a repeated liver biopsy as well as a biopsy of the thigh musculature. The liver biopsy, carried out during hospitalization, showed several sections of hepatic tissue featuring both small and large droplet macrovesicular steatosis affecting around 70% of the examined liver parenchyma, but without significant portal inflammation or evidence of interface hepatitis. These results matched her prior diagnosis of hepatosteatosis, revealing a slight increase in disease severity to NAFLD Activity Score grade 5/8, stage 1. The biopsy from the right vastus lateralis muscle exhibited scattered necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers (figure 3A,B). Immunohistochemical analysis identified acid phosphatase (figure 3D) and cluster of Differentiation-68 (CD68), indicative of macrophages, with evidence of myophagocytosis (figure 4B). Staining for membrane attack complex was positive in both necrotic and non-necrotic myofibers (figure 4C), signifying an inflammatory reaction. Furthermore, occasional CD3-positive T-cells (figure 4A), along with perivascular inflammatory cells (figure 3C), were observed. Collectively, the muscle biopsy findings were indicative of IMNM.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient developed a rapid and marked adverse reaction following her first and only administration of semaglutide, indicative of drug intolerance. Initial manifestations included gastrointestinal disturbances and renal impairment thought to be secondary to hypovolemia. Although creatine kinase (CK) was only mildly raised at the time of presentation and did not suggest rhabdomyolysis, the patient’s ongoing deterioration after discontinuing semaglutide, specific pattern of muscle weakness, and a twelvefold rise in CK over the subsequent four months imply an immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) potentially linked to semaglutide. Additionally, muscle biopsy findings characteristic of drug-induced IMNM, the absence of clinical signs of connective tissue disease, and sustained clinical improvement following cessation of semaglutide and initiation of immunosuppressive therapy further reinforce this suspected association.

It is significant that elevated transaminase levels were present before she began semaglutide, a pattern observed in some myopathic conditions. Nevertheless, three years before initiating semaglutide, a liver biopsy had confirmed hepatosteatosis, and her mild increases in AST and ALT continued even after CK levels returned to normal. Therefore, her chronically raised transaminases most likely stem from hepatic causes rather than muscle injury. Cardiac evaluation did not definitively support a diagnosis of active myocarditis. In this context, stress-related myopathy remains a plausible explanation, especially considering her swift improvement.

The presence of a positive SS-B antibody alongside negative ANA and SS-A results was an unanticipated finding. Currently, there is no literature documenting cases of IMNM associated with isolated SS-B positivity in the absence of ANA and SS-A. Regrettably, we were unable to obtain a CK value from before the initiation of semaglutide, preventing us from completely ruling out the possibility of a preexisting, indolent autoimmune IMNM. Nevertheless, the twelve-fold increase in CK and the gradual onset of proximal muscle weakness following semaglutide exposure suggest a higher probability that this episode of IMNM was triggered by medication. Furthermore, the patient’s severe IMNM resolved rapidly following a short regimen of corticosteroids and IVIG and by discontinuing semaglutide. This clinical trajectory is more consistent with drug-induced IMNM, which often responds to temporary immunosuppression after cessation of the inciting drug, rather than idiopathic IMNM, which typically necessitates extended and intensive immunosuppressive treatment.

Treatment:

She received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for five days, after which she transitioned to oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg per day. Because her weakness and severe symptoms persisted, she was administered IVIG at 2 g/kg, divided over four days. Subsequent follow-up visits in the outpatient clinic showed ongoing improvement in her functional capacity and general well-being, with CK levels remaining consistently within the normal range. These favorable outcomes enabled a rapid tapering and discontinuation of prednisone over a two-month period. Owing to both the financial burden and her continued progress in the absence of semaglutide, no further IVIG was administered beyond the initial regimen provided during her hospital admission.

Outcome and follow-up:

At her most recent outpatient follow-up, which occurred 10 months after the first assessment and beginning of therapy, she had returned to her baseline level of functioning and had been without immunosuppressive medications for the previous 7 months. Her creatine kinase values stayed within normal limits after hospitalization and immunosuppressive treatment. Subsequent echocardiogram and cardiac MRI demonstrated resolution of the previously observed abnormalities. Additionally, she did not have further episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation.

Discussion:

GLP-1 receptor agonists, including semaglutide, are increasingly utilized and have proven effective in treating metabolic conditions such as obesity and type 2 diabetes. Although gastrointestinal side effects are most frequently observed, these agents may also be linked to reduced muscle mass and, in rare instances, rhabdomyolysis. In this case, the patient’s progression aligns more closely with IMNM rather than rhabdomyolysis. Creatine kinase (CK) rose twelve times the baseline over four months after just one semaglutide dose, whereas with a direct semaglutide-induced toxic effect—as documented in other drug-induced rhabdomyolysis cases—we would anticipate CK normalization within weeks. Of note, the earlier reported instance of rhabdomyolysis related to tirzepatide displayed the usual trajectory: the individual exhibited a pronounced acute CK increase (CK=57 014 upon presentation) and showed swift improvement upon discontinuation of the drug and hydration, within days, without requiring immunosuppressive therapy.

Utilizing the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) Causality Categories and Criteria, the association between semaglutide administration and IMNM in our patient appears probable. This conclusion is based on the temporal proximity, the improbability of IMNM stemming from alternative diseases or medications, and an appropriate clinical response following cessation of semaglutide. Furthermore, a rechallenge was not pursued due to ethical considerations regarding the severity of the adverse event. Nevertheless, we concede that a definitive causal link cannot be confirmed in this instance. In light of the widespread adoption of semaglutide, our case report seeks to alert healthcare providers to this potential adverse reaction and reinforce the importance of pharmacovigilance. Future research will determine whether this observed relationship is genuinely causal or merely coincidental.

The underlying susceptibility that contributed to our patient's marked response to semaglutide is also unresolved. As GLP-1 receptor agonists continue to gain popularity, investigations into possible risk factors will be essential, particularly if further reports emerge with a close chronological association to semaglutide exposure.
