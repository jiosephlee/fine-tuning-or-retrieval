Title: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

Yi-Chen Liu, Sheng-Chiang Su, Feng-Chih Kuo, Yi-Jen Hung, Yen-Lin Chen

Abstract:

Summary: Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) is a monogenic diabetic disorder inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern. This case report details a patient with HNF1A-MODY resulting from a newly identified heterozygous mutation in the hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A) gene. The individual exhibited hyperglycaemia and glycosuria without evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis. The family history included instances of diabetes with early onset. Genetic analysis identified a pathogenic HNF1A mutation that has not been previously described. Administration of a GLP-1 receptor agonist in combination with metformin led to a decrease in glycated haemoglobin from 10.2% to 6.0% over a period of three months. This case underscores the value of genetic testing in cases of early-onset diabetes, which is often incorrectly classified as either type 1 or type 2 diabetes, and contributes to the understanding of HNF1A mutations significant for individualized therapeutic approaches.

Background:

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) comprises a set of dominantly inherited, non-autoimmune forms of diabetes that result from a primary impairment in pancreatic β-cell development and function. These disorders generally present before age 25 and are commonly diagnosed during this period. MODY accounts for roughly 1% to 6% of diabetes cases with early onset. The prevalence of MODY differs among various ethnicities and races and may be underrecognized in some groups. Most previous investigations have focused on White European cohorts; however, recent research has begun to assess the frequency of MODY in Asian and Australian populations. Reported rates of MODY prevalence demonstrate considerable variability, frequently influenced by the fact that genetic testing is usually directed toward individuals who meet specific clinical criteria, even in population-based studies.

Mutations in more than 15 genes have been associated with the MODY monogenic diabetes phenotype, with each mutation carrying distinct therapeutic implications. Most reported MODY cases are linked to mutations in genes including hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A), glucokinase, hepatocyte nuclear factor 4-alpha, and hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-beta. HNF1A-MODY is the most common form, accounting for approximately 30%–65% of all MODY cases, and is marked by an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern of symptomatic diabetes within families. In the early adolescent years, clinical manifestations may be absent, and hyperglycemia is often detected incidentally during standard blood investigations, typically in the absence of features indicative of insulin resistance. Postprandial glucose measurement or a 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) may reveal a marked rise in blood glucose (≥11.1 mmol/L). Glycosuria can also be present, secondary to a diminished renal threshold for glucose reabsorption caused by impaired glucose transport within renal tubules. As the disease advances, symptoms such as increased thirst, urination, appetite, and unintended weight loss—paralleling those seen in type 1 and type 2 diabetes (T2D)—may emerge. Thus, distinguishing MODY from type 1 and T2D is challenging due to substantial clinical overlap.

Multiple diagnostic protocols for MODY have been described in the literature. Following an initial evaluation of clinical features and a detailed family history suggestive of MODY, and if diabetes-associated autoantibodies typically seen in type 1 diabetes—such as glutamic acid decarboxylase antibody (GADA), insulinoma-associated antigen-2 autoantibody (IA-2A), and zinc transporter 8 (ZnT8)—are negative, along with corroborative biochemical findings, molecular genetic analysis becomes pivotal for confirming the diagnosis. Accurate identification of the MODY subtype is essential for optimizing therapeutic strategies, predicting possible complications, evaluating prognosis, and guiding genetic counselling for affected families. Recent research has shown that numerous distinct mutations within the HNF1A gene are linked to HNF1A-MODY.

In the case presented herein, genetic testing utilizing a next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel revealed a previously unrecognized heterozygous nucleotide change, p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G), located in exon 4 of the HNF1A gene in a patient with HNF1A-MODY. The variant was assessed for pathogenicity in accordance with the interpretation framework recommended by the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG). This specific mutation had not been documented in existing literature, nor was it present in the VarSome or ClinVar databases. The patient provided written informed consent for publication of this case report.

Case presentation:

A young adult presented for assessment of elevated blood glucose levels and glycosuria identified at a routine annual check-up. His physical evaluation revealed a BMI of 24.6 kg/m2, blood pressure measuring 112/70 mmHg, and a heart rate of 78 beats per minute. There was no evidence of acanthosis nigricans found on physical inspection. The family history included multiple instances of early-onset diabetes: his maternal grandmother developed diabetes in her 30s, his mother in her late 20s, and his younger brother at age 20. There were no reported cases of early-onset cardiovascular disease among family members affected by diabetes.

Investigations:

His HbA1c measured 10.2% (88 mmol/mol), with a fasting plasma glucose of 12.4 mmol/L. The 2-hour plasma glucose following a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test reached 19.8 mmol/L. There were no clinical features of diabetic ketoacidosis. The patient described polydipsia, polyphagia, and recent involuntary weight loss. Lipid panel indicated an HDL cholesterol level of 1.01 mmol/L and triglycerides of 4.31 mmol/L, raising the suspicion for metabolic syndrome (refer to tables 1 and 2). The patient denied any notable prior medical or surgical issues.

During glucagon stimulation testing, the serum C-peptide was 0.39 nmol/L at baseline and 0.83 nmol/L at 6 minutes, demonstrating reduced endogenous insulin production. This assessment is used to determine the extent of remaining β-cell functionality by measuring post-glucagon C-peptide concentrations. Clinically, a post-stimulus C-peptide above 0.60 nmol/L generally suggests maintained endogenous insulin secretion, while values below this threshold imply β-cell impairment. Nevertheless, interpretation should be adjunctive, particularly when autoantibody panels are negative or clinical findings are atypical. Testing for microalbuminuria was negative. Assessment of sudomotor function, utilizing electrochemical skin conductance (e.g., Sudoscan), showed markedly diminished conductance in the hands, consistent with pronounced small fiber autonomic neuropathy. Results for both glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA) and islet antigen-2 antibodies (IA-2A) were negative. Genetic analysis for MODY conducted one month following diagnosis revealed a heterozygous, pathogenic variant in HNF1A (p.N270S [HNF1A:c.809A>G]), confirming a diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY.

Treatment:

Upon diagnosis, the patient exhibited markedly increased HbA1c and plasma glucose concentrations. The initial management plan included basal insulin degludec at eight units per day, alongside metformin at 1500 mg daily and saxagliptin at 5 mg daily, reflecting an initial assessment favoring T2D. This regimen was selected to swiftly regulate blood sugar levels and address possible insulin resistance.

Beyond pharmacological intervention, recommendations were made for the patient to follow a structured diet and to participate in consistent physical activity, as part of an integrative approach to diabetes care. The patient’s progress in glycaemic control and treatment response was monitored at scheduled follow-up visits in the endocrinology and metabolism clinic.

Nonetheless, considering the patient's moderately preserved but inadequate β-cell function determined by a glucagon stimulation test (stimulated C-peptide: 0.83 nmol/L), the absence of islet autoantibodies, and a notable family history of early-onset diabetes, clinicians suspected a diagnosis of MODY.

Treatment was initiated prior to receiving genetic test results, relying on the clinical presentation and laboratory evidence observed at the time of diagnosis.

Outcome and follow-up:

One month afterward, the patient’s HbA1c dropped to 9.1%. After genetic verification of HNF1A-MODY and factoring in the patient’s moderately retained but inadequate β-cell capacity, as well as improved HbA1c at 9.1%, insulin was withdrawn and dulaglutide (a GLP-1 receptor agonist, 1.5 mg weekly) was initiated to enhance glycaemic regulation and help preserve pancreatic function. Six months into this regimen, HbA1c declined further to 6.0%, demonstrating considerable progress. With glycaemic stability achieved, treatment was streamlined to a maintenance protocol consisting of metformin (1500 mg/day) and the DPP-4 inhibitor saxagliptin (5 mg/day). The patient continues to be followed routinely, maintaining HbA1c levels consistently below 6.5%. Up to this point, no evidence of microalbuminuria or diabetic retinopathy has been detected, and surveillance for possible long-term complications remains ongoing.

Discussion:

The HNF1A gene encodes a transcription factor situated on chromosome 12 at the q24 region. This gene consists of 10 exons covering 23,790 base pairs, and it codes for a protein containing 631 amino acids, which is present in multiple organs such as the pancreas, kidneys, and liver. Alterations in HNF1A are associated with a gradual deterioration of β-cell activity, evidenced by reduced transcription of insulin and GLUT2 genes and lowered activity of pyruvate kinase, ultimately causing diminished insulin release and impaired glucose tolerance.

In this instance, the diagnostic workup followed established guidelines for identifying MODY, which include clinical, biochemical, and genetic evidence such as early diabetes onset, notable familial clustering, postprandial hyperglycemia, and absent diabetes-related autoantibodies. Fasting C-peptide concentrations above 0.6 ng/mL support a diagnosis outside of type 1 diabetes. Ongoing assessment of C-peptide is warranted, as normal levels can be present in initial phases of type 1 diabetes as well. Insufficient recognition and suspicion among clinicians frequently hampers accurate MODY diagnosis, resulting in misclassification as type 1 or type 2 diabetes. The MODY probability calculator created by the Exeter team is a resource for recognizing patients suitable for genetic testing; nevertheless, as it is based on a White European cohort, it might inflate MODY probability in non-White populations. Using this calculator with our patient's details produced an estimated MODY likelihood of 62.4% (equivalent to a 1 in 1.6 chance). There are reports documenting autoantibody positivity in some individuals with MODY, including those carrying HNF1A mutations, although most MODY patients do not have detectable autoantibodies. Previous studies indicate that the frequency of obesity and metabolic syndrome in MODY is lower compared to type 2 diabetes; however, as obesity has become more common, differentiating MODY from type 2 diabetes based on BMI or metabolic syndrome is less reliable, potentially explaining the higher BMI observed in this patient. Moreover, while family history plays a key diagnostic role in MODY, its value can be diminished by factors such as under-recognition, de novo mutations, and the widespread prevalence of type 2 diabetes within families.

Management of HNF1A-MODY is guided by the patient’s age and HbA1c measurements. Low-dose sulfonylureas are recommended as initial therapy because they stimulate insulin secretion by targeting ATP-sensitive potassium channels. As β-cell function diminishes in individuals with HNF1A-MODY—especially those at increased risk for hypoglycaemia—GLP-1 receptor agonists may represent a safer and potentially superior option compared to sulfonylureas. Evidence from a small, randomized, double-blind, crossover study found that liraglutide and glimepiride both successfully decreased fasting plasma glucose and reduced postprandial glycaemic excursions, without significant differences in efficacy between them for patients with HNF1A-MODY. Furthermore, GLP-1 receptor agonists were associated with a lower incidence of mild hypoglycaemic episodes versus glimepiride. Utilizing genetic testing, we were able to withdraw insulin therapy upon achieving stable glycaemic control, transitioning the patient to a GLP-1 receptor agonist, which continued to provide adequate glucose regulation. This underscores the importance of genetic diagnostics in informing therapeutic choices. The occurrence of glycosuria in HNF1A-MODY is due to decreased SGLT2 expression, governed by HNF1A transcriptional activity. According to one report, individuals with HNF1A-MODY may face pronounced dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis as a result of significant inhibition of remaining SGLT-2 activity. The potential risks of SGLT-2 inhibitor therapy in HNF1A-MODY—including heightened dehydration, possible vascular collapse, genital infections, and diabetic ketoacidosis—may exceed any therapeutic benefit. In this scenario, treatment with liraglutide and metformin proved to be both effective and safe, aligning with previously published findings.

Individualized treatment is essential for MODY, as many individuals with HNF1A-MODY do not consistently fulfill all clinical criteria outlined in current diagnostic guidelines. At present, there are no effective biomarkers to distinguish HNF1A-MODY from either type 1 or type 2 diabetes. Chen et al demonstrated that MODY prevalence among 2,429 young Chinese adults with early-onset T2D was 1.5%, with 0.74% possessing pathogenic or likely pathogenic (LP) MODY-related genetic variants. Additionally, Møller et al found that 10% of 39 Danish Caucasian type 1 diabetes patients who lacked any high-risk HLA haplotypes carried HNF1A gene mutations within a large population-based sample. In parallel, a recent population-based Russian study examined 506 subjects suspected of hereditary diabetes without autoimmune features—defined by negative IAA, ICA, GADA, and ZnT8 antibodies at diagnosis, preserved C-peptide levels, minimal insulin needs over five years, and absence of marked obesity. This investigation uncovered pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in 46.4% of cases, with HNF1A among the most frequently mutated genes. Confirming MODY suspicion relies on DNA sequencing techniques. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) makes possible the simultaneous analysis of extensive patient DNA, thus enhancing MODY screening efficiency. The expansion of NGS technology has led to the identification of numerous novel genetic variants. According to ACMG standards, variants are evaluated to determine pathogenicity. Over 1,000 unique HNF1A variants have been documented in repositories such as ClinVar, categorized as pathogenic, likely pathogenic, benign, likely benign, or of uncertain significance. In our report, the heterozygous HNF1A variant (c.809A>G, p.N270S) was not present in ClinVar or other established variant databases, underscoring its novelty rather than disputing its clinical impact. This variant alters an amino acid position adjacent to established pathogenic residues, demonstrates a high AlphaMissense score (0.995), and co-segregates with diabetes in affected relatives—all supporting its LP status per ACMG guidelines. Therefore, although ClinVar serves as a comprehensive catalogue for variant reporting, multiple independent genetic and clinical data confirm the pathogenic nature of this variant, even in the absence of prior database annotation.

Research has demonstrated that most detected mutations reside within the DNA-binding domain, whereas mutations in the transactivation domain are less frequently observed. The novel variant described in this report is also located in the DNA-binding domain. Certain investigations have found that specific HNF1A mutations, particularly those classified as P, exert limited influence on HNF1A protein function. Moreover, there is evidence that some HNF1A variants that do not independently cause MODY may still contribute to a heightened risk of developing T2D.

In summary, this case underscores the significance of precise identification of HNF1A-MODY and its implications for tailored therapy. Ongoing research efforts will target the creation of more robust NGS-based screening methodologies to enhance MODY detection rates, optimize healthcare spending, and advance clinical outcomes.
