Title: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

Yi-Chen Liu, Sheng-Chiang Su, Feng-Chih Kuo, Yi-Jen Hung, Yen-Lin Chen

Abstract:

Summary: Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) represents a monogenic, autosomal dominant subtype of diabetes. This case report details an individual with HNF1A-MODY arising from a previously unrecognized heterozygous mutation in the HNF1A gene. The individual exhibited hyperglycaemia and glycosuria but did not develop diabetic ketoacidosis. The patient’s family history was significant for diabetes with early age at onset. Genetic analysis identified a novel pathogenic variant in HNF1A. Administration of a GLP-1 receptor agonist together with metformin lowered glycated haemoglobin from 10.2% to 6.0% over a period of 3 months. This case underscores the value of genetic testing for early-onset diabetes, which is often mistaken for type 1 or type 2 diabetes, and broadens the catalogue of HNF1A mutations pertinent to personalized therapeutic approaches.

Background:

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) encompasses a collection of autosomal dominant, non-autoimmune diabetes syndromes marked by an intrinsic defect affecting the formation and function of pancreatic β-cells. These disorders usually appear and are diagnosed prior to 25 years of age. MODY accounts for roughly 1%–6% of diabetes cases with early onset. Prevalence rates for MODY can differ among ethnic and racial groups and may be subject to underreporting. Historically, investigations have predominantly focused on White European cohorts. However, more recent research has explored MODY prevalence in both Asian and Australian settings. Reported MODY prevalence spans a wide range, frequently because genetic analyses tend to be restricted to patients selected for particular clinical features, even when evaluating larger populations.

More than 15 gene mutations have been associated with the MODY-monogenic phenotype, with each mutation linked to distinct treatment approaches. The most frequently encountered forms of MODY are largely due to pathogenic variants in genes such as hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A), glucokinase, hepatocyte nuclear factor 4-alpha, and hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-β. Among these, HNF1A-MODY is the most common subtype, accounting for approximately 30%–65% of MODY cases and is characterized by a well-defined autosomal dominant inheritance pattern of diabetes within affected families. During early adolescence, clinical signs may not be evident, and hyperglycemia is often incidentally found through standard blood screening, with no typical signs of insulin resistance. Measurement of blood glucose following meals or performance of a 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) may reveal markedly elevated postprandial glucose levels (≥11.1 mmol/L). Glycosuria may also be present, resulting from a lower renal threshold for glucose reabsorption due to impaired tubular glucose transport. Nonetheless, as the disease advances, classic symptoms such as excessive thirst, increased urination, heightened hunger, and unintentional weight loss can develop, mirroring clinical features seen in both type 1 and type 2 diabetes (T2D). Consequently, distinguishing MODY from type 1 and T2D remains challenging due to significant similarities in presentation.

Several diagnostic pathways for MODY have been described in various studies. Upon recognition of clinical features consistent with MODY during physical examination and review of family history, and confirmation of the absence of autoantibodies typically found in type 1 diabetes—namely glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA), insulinoma antigen-2 autoantibody (IA-2A), and zinc transporter 8 (ZnT8)—in addition to relevant biochemical investigations, molecular genetic analysis is integral to establishing the diagnosis. Accurate identification of MODY is essential for guiding appropriate therapy, predicting risks of complications, informing prognosis, and supporting genetic counseling for families. Recent research has demonstrated that HNF1A-MODY is caused by hundreds of distinct mutations affecting the HNF1A gene.

In this case, we discovered a previously unreported heterozygous variant, p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G), located in exon 4 of the HNF1A gene in a patient with HNF1A-MODY, identified via next-generation sequencing (NGS) gene panel and evaluated for pathogenicity according to the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) criteria for variant assessment. This specific alteration is absent from the literature, the VarSome database, and the ClinVar database. Written informed consent was directly obtained from the patient for publication of this case.

Case presentation:

A young adult presented for assessment following detection of hyperglycaemia and glycosuria at his routine yearly check-up. Physical examination revealed a BMI of 24.6 kg/m2, a blood pressure of 112/70 mmHg, and a heart rate of 78 bpm. There was no evidence of acanthosis nigricans on inspection. His family history showed instances of early-onset diabetes: his maternal grandmother had been diagnosed in her 30s, his mother in her late 20s, and his younger brother at age 20. No family members diagnosed with diabetes had a documented history of early-onset cardiovascular disease.

Investigations:

His HbA1c was measured at 10.2% (88 mmol/mol), with a fasting glucose concentration of 12.4 mmol/L. Following administration of a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test, his 2-hour plasma glucose reached 19.8 mmol/L. There was no evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis. The patient described polydipsia, increased appetite, and unintentional recent weight loss. Lipid studies demonstrated a high-density lipoprotein of 1.01 mmol/L and triglycerides at 4.31 mmol/L, findings in keeping with possible metabolic syndrome (refer to tables 1 and 2). Past medical and surgical history was not significant.

On glucagon stimulation, serum C-peptide concentrations were 0.39 nmol/L at baseline and 0.83 nmol/L after 6 minutes, suggestive of inadequate endogenous insulin production. The glucagon stimulation test is utilized to estimate remaining pancreatic β-cell function by assessing C-peptide release following glucagon administration. In clinical settings, a stimulated C-peptide above 0.60 nmol/L typically reflects adequate endogenous insulin output, while reduced levels are indicative of β-cell compromise. Nonetheless, this test is generally interpreted as an adjunct rather than conclusive diagnostic tool, particularly when islet autoantibodies are negative or the clinical presentation is uncharacteristic. No microalbuminuria was identified. Assessment of sudomotor function revealed marked impairment across the hands, as determined by electrochemical skin conductance testing (e.g., via Sudoscan), a non-invasive measure of small fiber autonomic nerve integrity. The patient's results confirmed severely diminished conductance in the hands, pointing to profound autonomic dysfunction. Testing for autoantibodies, specifically GADA and IA-2A, was negative. A MODY genetic panel was performed one month following the initial diagnosis and detected a heterozygous pathogenic variant in HNF1A (p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G)), supporting a diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY.

Treatment:

At diagnosis, the patient exhibited markedly increased HbA1c and plasma glucose concentrations. The initial therapeutic approach included basal insulin degludec (eight units per day), together with metformin (1500 mg per day) and saxagliptin (5 mg per day), reflecting an initial working diagnosis of T2D. This regimen sought to quickly establish glycaemic control and address possible insulin resistance.

Alongside medication, the patient received recommendations for a regimented nutritional program and consistent exercise, as components of an integrated diabetes management plan. Subsequent follow-up appointments were scheduled in the endocrinology and metabolism outpatient department to track glycaemic status and evaluate response to therapy.

Nonetheless, based on the patient’s moderately retained but suboptimal β-cell function on the glucagon stimulation assay (stimulated C-peptide: 0.83 nmol/L), absence of islet autoantibodies, and prominent family history of diabetes with early onset, suspicion for MODY remained high.

The initiation of therapy occurred before genetic testing data were available, guided by the clinical picture and laboratory values observed at the time of presentation.

Outcome and follow-up:

One month later, the patient’s HbA1c had fallen to 9.1%. With the diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY confirmed genetically and recognizing the patient’s β-cell capacity as only moderately retained but inadequate, and noting the improvement in HbA1c, insulin was discontinued. Dulaglutide (GLP-1 receptor agonist, 1.5 mg weekly) was initiated to facilitate glycaemic management and aid in preserving pancreatic function. After six months on this regimen, the patient’s HbA1c was reduced further to 6.0%, demonstrating notable improvement. Once glycaemic control was achieved, therapy was adjusted to a more streamlined regimen consisting of metformin (1500 mg/day) together with saxagliptin (DPP-4 inhibitor, 5 mg/day) for ongoing maintenance. The patient continues regular monitoring, with HbA1c levels consistently below 6.5%. Up to this point, there has been no evidence of microalbuminuria or diabetic retinopathy, and monitoring for chronic diabetes-related complications is ongoing.

Discussion:

The HNF1A gene, a transcription factor, is situated at chromosome 12q24. It contains 10 exons that span 23,790 base pairs and codes for a protein consisting of 631 amino acids. This protein is present in a variety of tissues, such as the pancreas, liver, and kidneys. Alterations in HNF1A contribute to a gradual loss of β-cell function, which manifests as disrupted insulin and GLUT2 gene expression and diminished pyruvate kinase activity. These changes ultimately lead to reduced insulin output and impaired glucose tolerance.

In this case, the approach to diagnosis was consistent with established MODY criteria, incorporating clinical, biochemical, and genetic factors like early diabetes onset, notable family history, intolerance to postprandial glucose, and negative autoantibody results. A preserved fasting C-peptide above 0.6 ng/mL supports the diagnosis of a non-type 1 diabetes form, although ongoing C-peptide assessment remains crucial since typical values can also appear in initial stages of type 1 diabetes. One of the major obstacles to recognizing MODY lies in limited clinician awareness and suspicion, which frequently results in patients being incorrectly classified as having either type 1 or type 2 diabetes. The MODY probability calculator created by the Exeter group may facilitate identification of patients for genetic testing; however, it was formulated based on data from a White European cohort and could overpredict MODY likelihood in populations of other ethnicities. When the clinical characteristics of our patient were entered into the calculator, the predicted probability of MODY was 62.4%, equivalent to a 1 in 1.6 chance. There have been multiple reports highlighting the presence of autoantibodies in MODY patients, with some carriers of HNF1A variants exhibiting autoantibodies, though the majority do not. Previous research indicates that MODY patients generally experience less obesity and metabolic syndrome than those with type 2 diabetes. Nonetheless, the increasing prevalence of obesity has rendered BMI and metabolic syndrome less discriminative between MODY and T2DM, which may clarify the higher BMI noted in our patient. Furthermore, while family history is a valuable aspect for diagnosing MODY, its effectiveness may be reduced due to under-reporting, occurrence of de novo mutations, and frequent T2DM family history.

Management of HNF1A-MODY is based on patient age and HbA1c values. Sulfonylureas at low doses are the preferred initial therapy, as they stimulate insulin release via ATP-sensitive potassium channels. For individuals with HNF1A-MODY who experience declining β-cell function or are susceptible to hypoglycaemia, GLP-1 receptor agonists offer a safer and potentially more efficacious alternative to sulfonylureas. In a randomized, double-blind, crossover study, both liraglutide and glimepiride were shown to significantly lower fasting plasma glucose and postprandial glucose excursions in HNF1A-MODY patients, with no notable difference between the two drugs. Furthermore, treatment with GLP-1 receptor agonists carries a reduced risk of mild hypoglycaemia compared to glimepiride. Following genetic diagnosis in our patient, we were able to stop insulin once glucose levels were stabilized and switched to GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy, which continued to provide good glycaemic control. This underscores the essential role of genetic testing for tailoring therapy. Glycosuria in HNF1A-MODY results from decreased SGLT2 expression, regulated by HNF1A transcription. One publication described severe dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis in patients with HNF1A-MODY owing to substantial inhibition of remaining SGLT-2 function. In individuals with HNF1A-MODY, the drawbacks of SGLT-2 inhibitors—including increased risk of dehydration, possible vascular collapse, genital infections, and diabetic ketoacidosis—may surpass their therapeutic advantages. For this patient, combined treatment with liraglutide and metformin proved both safe and effective, in line with findings from previous research.

Precision medicine plays an essential role in MODY, given that individuals with HNF1A-MODY frequently do not satisfy all the clinical diagnostic criteria despite established guidelines. There are presently no adequate biomarkers to distinguish HNF1A-MODY from type 1 or type 2 diabetes. Chen et al reported that among 2429 young Chinese adults with early-onset type 2 diabetes, MODY was present in 1.5%, and 0.74% possessed pathogenic (P) or likely pathogenic (LP) MODY gene variants. Møller et al's investigation revealed that, within a sizable Danish Caucasian cohort of type 1 diabetes patients, 10% of 39 individuals lacking any high-risk HLA haplotypes exhibited HNF1A gene mutations. Correspondingly, a recent population study from Russia assessed 506 subjects suspected of monogenic diabetes who had no autoimmune markers, determined by negative IAA, ICA, GADA, and ZnT8 antibodies, maintained C-peptide secretion, minimal insulin requirements within 5 years of diagnosis, and absence of marked obesity. This Russian study detected P or LP variants in 46.4% of participants, with HNF1A among the most frequently implicated genes. Confirmatory diagnosis of MODY relies on DNA sequencing. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) enables comprehensive analysis of patient DNA samples in a single procedure, enhancing the practicality of MODY detection. With NGS advancements, numerous novel genetic variants have been uncovered. The ACMG offers guidance to assess the pathogenic status of these variants. In databases like ClinVar, more than 1000 unique HNF1A variants have been catalogued, ranging from P, LP, benign, likely benign, to variants of uncertain significance. In this patient, the previously unreported heterozygous HNF1A variant (c.809A>G, p.N270S) was not listed in ClinVar or other widely used variant databases, confirming its novelty rather than its clinical significance. Notably, this alteration impacts an amino acid residue directly adjacent to established pathogenic residues, demonstrates a high AlphaMissense score (0.995), and segregates with diabetes within the pedigree, supporting a classification as LP according to ACMG criteria. Therefore, while ClinVar provides valuable documentation of genetic variants, the pathogenicity of this specific variant is substantiated by several independent genetic and clinical lines of evidence outside database listings.

Research has demonstrated that most mutations detected are predominantly found within the DNA-binding domain, whereas mutations are less frequently observed in the transactivation domain. The novel variant described in this report is also positioned within the DNA-binding domain. Certain investigations suggest that HNF1A mutations, particularly P, exert only minimal influence on HNF1A protein activity. Furthermore, some HNF1A variants that do not independently cause MODY may nevertheless be linked to an elevated risk of type 2 diabetes.

To summarize, this case emphasizes the critical role of precise diagnosis for HNF1A-MODY and its subsequent effect on personalized therapy. Ongoing studies will aim to develop improved next-generation sequencing (NGS) screening approaches to enhance MODY diagnostic rates, ultimately leading to superior cost-effectiveness and clinical outcomes.
