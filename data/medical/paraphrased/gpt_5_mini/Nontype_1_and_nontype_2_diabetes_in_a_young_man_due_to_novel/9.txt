Title: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

Yi-Chen Liu, Sheng-Chiang Su, Feng-Chih Kuo, Yi-Jen Hung, Yen-Lin Chen

Abstract:

Summary: Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) is a monogenic form of diabetes inherited in an autosomal dominant manner. This case report outlines a patient diagnosed with HNF1A-MODY attributed to a newly identified heterozygous mutation in the HNF1A gene. The individual exhibited elevated blood glucose and glycosuria, but did not develop diabetic ketoacidosis. A notable aspect of the history was early-onset diabetes among relatives. Molecular analysis revealed a previously undescribed pathogenic variant in HNF1A. Initiation of therapy with a GLP-1 receptor agonist and metformin led to a decrease in glycated haemoglobin from 10.2% to 6.0% over a 3-month period. The case underscores the significance of genetic testing in early-onset diabetes, as it is often misclassified as type 1 or type 2 diabetes, and adds to the known mutations in HNF1A relevant for personalized treatment approaches.

Background:

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) encompasses a set of autosomal dominant, non-autoimmune diabetes syndromes marked by an intrinsic defect in the maturation and function of pancreatic β-cells. These disorders generally present and are identified before individuals reach 25 years of age. MODY accounts for approximately 1%–6% of diabetes cases with early onset. The frequency of MODY varies among different racial and ethnic groups, and may frequently be underestimated. Most current investigations have focused on White European populations, though recent research has begun to assess MODY prevalence in Asian and Australian populations. Reported rates of MODY differ greatly, largely because genetic testing is usually performed on subjects selected based on specific clinical criteria, even in population-based research.

Mutations in more than 15 distinct genes have been associated with the MODY monogenic phenotype, each implicating specific treatment approaches. Most commonly, mutations in genes like hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A), glucokinase, hepatocyte nuclear factor 4-alpha, and hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-beta account for the majority of diagnosed MODY subtypes. Among these, HNF1A-MODY is the most prevalent form, making up approximately 30%–65% of all MODY cases, and typically demonstrates a clear pattern of autosomal dominant inheritance of diabetes symptoms across generations. During early adolescence, clinical signs may be absent and hyperglycemia is often discovered incidentally during standard laboratory assessment, generally without manifestations of insulin resistance. Elevated postprandial glucose (≥11.1 mmol/L) can be detected by testing blood glucose levels after meals or performing a 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT). Glycosuria may also be observed, owing to a diminished renal threshold for glucose secondary to impaired glucose reabsorption in the renal tubules. Nonetheless, in advanced disease, individuals may exhibit symptoms such as excessive thirst, increased urination, heightened appetite, and weight loss, paralleling features seen in both type 1 and type 2 diabetes (T2D). As a result, distinguishing MODY from type 1 and T2D can be challenging due to shared clinical characteristics.

Several diagnostic protocols for MODY have been developed and described in various studies. Once clinical suspicion for MODY is raised following a thorough physical examination and assessment of family history, and autoantibodies typically seen in type 1 diabetes—such as glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA), insulinoma antigen-2 autoantibody (IA-2A), and zinc transporter 8 (ZnT8)—are found to be negative, along with corroborating biochemical evidence, molecular genetic analysis becomes an essential step for confirming the diagnosis. Accurate identification of the specific MODY subtype informs optimal treatment, enables prediction of possible complications, guides prognosis, and supports family counseling. Recent research has identified numerous distinct pathogenic variants within the HNF1A gene contributing to HNF1A-MODY.

In the case described in this report, genetic analysis via next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel revealed a previously unreported heterozygous variant, p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G), located in exon 4 of HNF1A in a patient diagnosed with HNF1A-MODY. The pathogenic potential of this variant was evaluated following the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) variant interpretation guidelines. This specific mutation is not documented in current literature, the VarSome database, or the ClinVar database. The patient provided written informed consent for this case to be published.

Case presentation:

A young adult presented for assessment after hyperglycaemia and glycosuria were identified at his routine annual check-up. Physical examination revealed a BMI of 24.6 kg/m2, a blood pressure of 112/70 mmHg, and a heart rate of 78 bpm. There were no clinical signs of acanthosis nigricans. The family history was significant for early-onset diabetes: his maternal grandmother developed the condition in her 30s, his mother in her late 20s, and his younger brother at age 20. No instances of early-onset cardiovascular disease had been reported in diabetic relatives.

Investigations:

The patient's HbA1c was elevated at 10.2% (88 mmol/mol), and fasting plasma glucose measured 12.4 mmol/L. Following a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test, the 2-hour glucose value reached 19.8 mmol/L. There was no evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis. Clinical symptoms included polydipsia, polyphagia, and recent weight reduction. The lipid panel showed high-density lipoprotein at 1.01 mmol/L and triglycerides at 4.31 mmol/L, findings that may indicate metabolic syndrome (refer to tables 1 and 2). The patient's medical and surgical history was unremarkable.

During a glucagon stimulation test, serum C-peptide concentrations were 0.39 nmol/L at baseline and rose to 0.83 nmol/L after 6 minutes, signifying inadequate insulin production. This procedure assesses residual β-cell activity by determining the C-peptide response after glucagon administration. In routine use, a stimulated C-peptide exceeding 0.60 nmol/L implies ongoing endogenous insulin synthesis, whereas values below this threshold are associated with β-cell impairment. Nonetheless, this test serves mainly as a supplemental diagnostic tool, especially when autoantibody panels are negative or the clinical context does not follow typical patterns. Microalbuminuria testing was negative. Sudomotor assessment indicated markedly diminished function in the hands. Evaluation was conducted using electrochemical skin conductance (such as Sudoscan), a noninvasive technique that measures small fiber autonomic nerve integrity. The results revealed pronounced autonomic dysfunction in the hands, as shown by low conductance values. Both anti-GAD (GADA) and islet antigen-2 (IA-2A) antibodies tested negative. One month following diagnosis, genetic testing for maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) identified a heterozygous pathogenic mutation in HNF1A (p.N270S [HNF1A:c.809A>G]), confirming the diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY.

Treatment:

At diagnosis, the patient exhibited markedly increased HbA1c and plasma glucose concentrations. Initial management included basal insulin degludec at eight units per day, along with metformin at 1500 mg daily and saxagliptin at 5 mg daily, based on an initial working diagnosis of T2D. This regimen was selected to rapidly control hyperglycemia and address possible insulin resistance.

Beyond medication, the patient was counseled to follow a specific dietary program and participate in consistent physical exercise as elements of a comprehensive strategy for diabetes care. Follow-up visits occurred in the endocrinology and metabolism outpatient setting to review glycaemic parameters and treatment efficacy.

Despite therapy, due to moderately preserved yet inadequate β-cell reserve demonstrated by a stimulated C-peptide value of 0.83 nmol/L on the glucagon stimulation test, absence of islet cell autoantibodies, and a notable family history of early-onset diabetes, a strong suspicion for MODY was maintained.

Initial treatment was started prior to the availability of genetic testing results, based on the clinical presentation and laboratory findings at diagnosis.

Outcome and follow-up:

After one month, the patient's HbA1c had dropped to 9.1%. With genetic confirmation of HNF1A-MODY and the observation of moderately retained but inadequate β-cell function, as well as an HbA1c improvement to 9.1%, insulin treatment was halted and substituted with dulaglutide (a GLP-1 receptor agonist, 1.5 mg administered weekly) in order to enhance glycaemic control and maintain pancreatic function. Over a 6-month period on this regimen, the patient's HbA1c fell further to 6.0%, demonstrating marked improvement. Once glycaemic stability was achieved, the therapeutic approach was streamlined to metformin (1500 mg per day) together with the DPP-4 inhibitor saxagliptin (5 mg daily) for ongoing maintenance. The patient continues to receive regular monitoring, with HbA1c levels remaining stably below 6.5%. Up to the present, there have been no indications of microalbuminuria or diabetic retinopathy, and long-term complication monitoring is ongoing.

Discussion:

The HNF1A gene encodes a transcription factor found at chromosomal region 12q24. It consists of 10 exons covering 23,790 base pairs, producing a 631-amino acid protein expressed in multiple organs, such as the liver, kidneys, and pancreas. Mutations in HNF1A cause a gradual loss of β-cell function, characterized by deficient expression of insulin and GLUT2 genes and diminished activity of pyruvate kinase. This sequence of events impairs insulin release and results in glucose intolerance.

In this case, diagnosis followed established MODY criteria, which include clinical presentation, laboratory results, and genetic confirmation, such as early-onset diabetes, a pronounced family history, abnormal postprandial glucose tolerance, and absent autoantibodies. A fasting C-peptide above 0.6 ng/mL supports a diagnosis of non-type 1 diabetes, but serial C-peptide assessments are advised because values can remain normal in initial type 1 diabetes stages. Recognition of MODY is often hampered by insufficient awareness and low clinical suspicion, resulting in frequent misclassification as type 1 or type 2 diabetes. The MODY probability calculator from the Exeter group can assist in recognizing patients who should undergo genetic testing, though it was designed using data from White European populations and may not yield accurate estimates for non-White individuals. Applying this tool to our patient's data produced a MODY probability of 62.4% (equivalent to 1 in 1.6 odds). Literature documents cases of MODY with detectable autoantibodies, including some with HNF1A mutations, though most MODY patients lack these antibodies. Earlier research found lower frequencies of obesity and metabolic syndrome among MODY patients compared to those with type 2 diabetes. With increasing obesity prevalence, differentiating MODY from type 2 diabetes by BMI or presence of metabolic syndrome has become more challenging, potentially explaining the higher BMI observed in our patient. Furthermore, while a positive family history is a helpful diagnostic clue for MODY, its reliability is limited by issues such as underreporting, de novo mutations, and the common occurrence of type 2 diabetes within families.

The management of HNF1A-MODY is guided by both the patient's age and their HbA1c values. Low-dose sulfonylureas are considered the initial therapy of choice, as they promote insulin secretion by acting on ATP-dependent potassium channels. As β-cell function wanes in individuals with HNF1A-MODY, GLP-1 receptor agonists are a safer and more effective substitute for sulfonylureas, especially in those who are susceptible to hypoglycemic episodes. Evidence from a small, randomised, double-blind, crossover study showed that both liraglutide and glimepiride significantly lower fasting and postprandial plasma glucose levels, without significant differences in efficacy for HNF1A-MODY patients. Furthermore, use of GLP-1 receptor agonists results in a decreased incidence of mild hypoglycemia when compared to glimepiride. Genetic testing enabled us to safely discontinue insulin following the achievement of glycaemic targets and to switch the patient to GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy, which continued to provide good glucose control. This underscores the essential importance of molecular diagnostics in selecting appropriate therapies. The occurrence of glycosuria in HNF1A-MODY is due to diminished SGLT2 expression regulated by HNF1A. One report suggested that people with HNF1A-MODY could suffer profound dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis because of high degrees of SGLT2 inhibition affecting their remaining transporter activity. Therefore, the adverse effects associated with SGLT2 inhibitors in this population—including increased risks of dehydration, possible vascular collapse, genital infections, and diabetic ketoacidosis—may override any therapeutic advantage these drugs might confer. In this patient's case, the combination of liraglutide and metformin has proven to be both safe and effective, aligning with previously published evidence.

Precision medicine plays a pivotal role in the management of MODY, given that individuals with HNF1A-MODY frequently do not satisfy all diagnostic criteria according to current clinical guidelines. At present, there are no effective biomarkers to distinguish HNF1A-MODY from type 1 or type 2 diabetes. In a cohort study conducted by Chen et al involving 2429 young Chinese adults with early-onset T2D, MODY was identified in 1.5% of participants, and 0.74% harbored pathogenic or likely pathogenic (LP) MODY variants. Furthermore, research by Møller et al found that among a broad, population-based cohort of Danish Caucasian patients with type 1 diabetes, 10% of the 39 individuals lacking any high-risk HLA haplotypes were discovered to carry HNF1A mutations. Similarly, a recent population-level investigation from Russia evaluated 506 individuals with clinical suspicion of hereditary diabetes who did not display autoimmune markers—defined by the absence of IAA, ICA, GADA, and ZnT8 antibodies at diagnosis, continued C-peptide production, low insulin needs for at least 5 years, and no severe obesity. In this group, pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants were identified in 46.4% of cases, with HNF1A representing one of the most commonly mutated genes. DNA sequencing remains a necessary step to validate clinical suspicions of MODY. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) enables the comprehensive assessment of extensive segments of patient DNA in a single analysis, facilitating broader MODY screening. With advancements in NGS, numerous novel genetic variants have been uncovered. The ACMG has published standards for evaluating whether such variants are pathogenic. Databases such as ClinVar currently catalogue more than 1000 unique HNF1A variants, which range from pathogenic, likely pathogenic, benign, likely benign, to variants of uncertain significance. In our report, the newly identified heterozygous HNF1A mutation (c.809A>G, p.N270S) was not present in ClinVar or other standard variant databases, emphasizing its originality and not undermining its potential clinical impact. Notably, this mutation involves an amino acid adjacent to previously recognized pathogenic residues, demonstrates a high AlphaMissense score (0.995), and co-segregates with diabetes within the family, meeting ACMG criteria for likely pathogenicity. Therefore, while ClinVar serves as a comprehensive repository for known variants, multiple genetic and clinical indicators reinforce the likely pathogenic nature of this variant, independent of database representation.

Research has demonstrated that most detected mutations are found within the DNA-binding domain, whereas the mutation frequency in the transactivation domain is lower. The novel variant identified in this patient is located within the DNA-binding domain. Certain studies suggest that, while present, HNF1A mutations may exert only minor effects on the protein’s function. Moreover, some HNF1A variants that are insufficient to cause MODY independently may still be linked to a higher susceptibility to T2D.

In summary, this case emphasizes the critical role of precise identification of HNF1A-MODY and its influence on personalized management. Ongoing investigations aim to establish more efficient NGS-based screening methods to enhance the detection rate of MODY, thus facilitating improved cost-effectiveness and clinical outcomes.
