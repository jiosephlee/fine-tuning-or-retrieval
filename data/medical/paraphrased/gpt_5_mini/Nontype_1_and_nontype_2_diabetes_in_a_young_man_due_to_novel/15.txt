Title: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

Yi-Chen Liu, Sheng-Chiang Su, Feng-Chih Kuo, Yi-Jen Hung, Yen-Lin Chen

Abstract:

Summary: Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) represents an autosomal dominant, monogenic form of diabetes. This case report discusses a patient with HNF1A-MODY associated with a previously unidentified heterozygous mutation in the HNF1A gene. The individual exhibited hyperglycaemia and glycosuria but did not develop diabetic ketoacidosis. A notable family history included cases of diabetes with early onset. Genetic analysis revealed a pathogenic HNF1A variant not previously documented. Administration of a GLP-1 receptor agonist together with metformin led to a reduction in glycated haemoglobin from 10.2% to 6.0% over a 3-month period. This case underscores the significance of genetic evaluation in patients with early-onset diabetes—often mistakenly categorized as type 1 or type 2 diabetes—and broadens the range of HNF1A variants important for targeted therapeutic strategies.

Background:

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) refers to a collection of autosomal dominant, non-autoimmune diabetes syndromes that result from an intrinsic abnormality in the formation and function of pancreatic β-cells. These disorders usually present and are identified before individuals reach 25 years old. MODY accounts for an estimated 1%–6% of cases of early-onset diabetes. The prevalence of MODY varies among different racial and ethnic populations, and it is likely that these differences may be underestimated. Research has largely focused on cohorts of White European ancestry. However, more recent investigations have explored MODY rates within Asian and Australian populations. Reported frequencies of MODY differ widely, partly because genetic analyses are most often restricted to persons selected by clinical criteria, even in population-based studies.

Pathogenic variants in more than 15 genes have been associated with the MODY-monogenic phenotype, and each is linked to specific management approaches. Most cases among the different MODY subtypes result from mutations in genes including hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A), glucokinase, hepatocyte nuclear factor 4-alpha, and hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-β. 

HNF1A-MODY is the most frequent MODY subtype, accounting for approximately 30%–65% of all MODY diagnoses, and demonstrates a clear autosomal dominant pattern of inheritance, with affected family members developing symptomatic diabetes. During early adolescence, affected individuals are often asymptomatic, and elevated glycemia is typically identified incidentally during standard laboratory testing, usually in the absence of any clinical features of insulin resistance. Postprandial glucose monitoring or a 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) may reveal a marked elevation in blood glucose (≥11.1 mmol/L). Furthermore, glycosuria may be present, reflecting a lowered renal threshold for glucose secondary to impaired tubular glucose reabsorption. As the disease progresses, classical symptoms such as polydipsia, polyuria, polyphagia, and weight loss—resembling those seen in type 1 and type 2 diabetes (T2D)—may develop. Consequently, distinguishing MODY from type 1 and T2D poses a significant diagnostic challenge because of overlapping phenotypic features.

Several diagnostic pathways for MODY have been delineated in the literature. Once a clinical suspicion of MODY is raised through physical examination and evaluation of family history, the finding of negative diabetes-associated autoantibodies—specifically glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA), insulinoma antigen-2 autoantibody (IA-2A), and zinc transporter 8 (ZnT8)—alongside relevant biochemical data, underscores the importance of molecular genetic testing for confirmation. Achieving an accurate diagnosis is essential for guiding appropriate management strategies, predicting the risk of complications, informing prognosis, and supporting genetic counseling for families. Recent studies indicate that a wide variety of mutations, numbering in the hundreds, within the HNF1A gene underlie cases of HNF1A-MODY.

In the present case, genetic analysis utilizing a next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel revealed a previously unreported heterozygous missense change, p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G), located in exon 4 of HNF1A in an individual with HNF1A-MODY. The pathogenicity of this variant was evaluated in accordance with American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) criteria for variant assessment. This particular mutation had not been described previously in the published literature, the VarSome database, or the ClinVar database. The patient provided written informed consent for the case report’s publication.

Case presentation:

A young adult was referred for assessment following detection of hyperglycaemia and glycosuria at his routine annual health check-up. Upon evaluation, his BMI measured 24.6 kg/m2, his blood pressure was recorded at 112/70 mmHg, and his pulse was 78 beats per minute. Physical examination revealed no evidence of acanthosis nigricans. The family history indicated cases of early-onset diabetes: his maternal grandmother was diagnosed in her 30s, his mother in her late 20s, and his younger brother at age 20. No family members diagnosed with diabetes had a reported history of early-onset cardiovascular disease.

Investigations:

The patient’s HbA1c was elevated at 10.2% (88 mmol/mol), with a fasting plasma glucose of 12.4 mmol/L. Following a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test, his 2-hour plasma glucose measured 19.8 mmol/L. There was no clinical or laboratory evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis. He described polydipsia, increased appetite, and a recent loss of weight. Lipid studies showed a high-density lipoprotein level of 1.01 mmol/L and triglycerides of 4.31 mmol/L, findings that may be compatible with metabolic syndrome (refer to tables 1 and 2). The patient did not have any notable past medical or surgical history.

A glucagon stimulation test yielded a serum C-peptide of 0.39 nmol/L at baseline, rising to 0.83 nmol/L six minutes after glucagon administration, demonstrating suboptimal insulin production. This assay assesses residual pancreatic β-cell capacity by measuring stimulated C-peptide after glucagon injection. Clinically, a stimulated C-peptide >0.60 nmol/L usually signifies maintained endogenous insulin output, whereas lower values are indicative of β-cell impairment. Nonetheless, the glucagon stimulation test serves as a supportive, rather than conclusive, assessment tool, especially when autoantibody testing is inconclusive or the clinical profile is unusual. Testing for microalbuminuria was negative. Evaluation of sudomotor activity revealed profound reduction in function in both hands, as determined by non-invasive electrochemical skin conductance measurement (for example, with Sudoscan), which assesses small fibre autonomic nerve integrity. His results indicated a marked decrease in hand conductance, reflecting significant autonomic neuropathy. Antibody screening—including both GAD autoantibodies (GADA) and islet antigen 2 autoantibodies (IA-2A)—was negative. Genetic testing for MODY was performed one month after diagnosis and identified a heterozygous pathogenic variant in the HNF1A gene (p.N270S [HNF1A:c.809A>G]), confirming the diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY.

Treatment:

At diagnosis, the patient exhibited marked elevations in HbA1c and plasma glucose. The initial therapeutic approach included basal insulin degludec (eight units daily) alongside metformin (1500 mg per day) and saxagliptin (5 mg daily), reflecting an initial working diagnosis of T2D. This regimen was intended to rapidly control hyperglycaemia while addressing possible insulin resistance.

Beyond medication, the patient was counseled to follow a structured nutritional plan and engage in consistent physical exercise as components of a holistic diabetes care strategy. Subsequent assessments took place in the endocrinology and metabolism clinic to track glycaemic status and evaluate response to therapy.

Nonetheless, due to moderately preserved but inadequate β-cell capacity on the glucagon stimulation test (stimulated C-peptide: 0.83 nmol/L), absence of islet autoantibodies, and a notable family history of early-onset diabetes, there was a strong suspicion for MODY.

Initial treatment was started prior to the availability of genetic testing results, based on the clinical presentation and laboratory findings at diagnosis.

Outcome and follow-up:

After one month, the patient's HbA1c had fallen to 9.1%. With the genetic diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY confirmed and assessment showing moderately maintained but inadequate β-cell function, alongside the HbA1c improvement to 9.1%, insulin treatment was ceased and substituted with dulaglutide (a GLP-1 receptor agonist, 1.5 mg administered weekly) to enhance glycaemic management and help preserve pancreatic function. Six months into therapy, the patient’s HbA1c dropped to 6.0%, reflecting a marked clinical benefit. With stabilization of glycaemic parameters, the treatment plan was further streamlined to metformin (1500 mg daily) and the DPP-4 inhibitor saxagliptin (5 mg daily) for ongoing glycaemic control. The patient continues to attend regular follow-up appointments, with HbA1c reliably kept under 6.5%. So far, there is no evidence of microalbuminuria or diabetic retinopathy, and surveillance for chronic complications remains in place.

Discussion:

The HNF1A gene encodes a transcription factor situated on chromosome 12q24. This gene consists of 10 exons over a stretch of 23,790 base pairs and produces a protein with 631 amino acids. This protein is found in multiple tissues such as the pancreas, liver, and kidneys. Mutations affecting HNF1A cause progressive β-cell dysfunction, resulting in diminished expression of both insulin and GLUT2 genes and lower pyruvate kinase activity. Consequently, affected individuals experience reduced insulin release and subsequent glucose intolerance.

The diagnostic approach in this case followed established MODY criteria, which integrate clinical, biochemical, and genetic findings. These include an early onset of diabetes, a notable familial prevalence, abnormal postprandial glycemic control, and the absence of diabetes-related autoantibodies. A fasting C-peptide measurement above 0.6 ng/mL supports a diagnosis distinct from type 1 diabetes; nevertheless, ongoing assessment of C-peptide is crucial, as normal values can appear during the initial phase of type 1 diabetes. A primary challenge in recognizing MODY lies in limited clinical awareness and suspicion, leading to frequent misclassification as either type 1 diabetes or T2D. The Exeter group’s MODY probability calculator can assist providers in selecting appropriate patients for genetic screening, though it was developed using data from a White European cohort and might overstate the likelihood of MODY in patients from other ethnic backgrounds. For our patient, inputting her clinical data into this calculator yielded a MODY probability of 62.4%, or roughly 1 in 1.6. There have been published reports of patients with MODY who also demonstrate autoantibody positivity; while some with HNF1A mutations present with autoantibodies, most MODY cases are autoantibody negative. Previous research indicates that rates of obesity and metabolic syndrome are lower in MODY compared to T2D. However, with increasing obesity prevalence, using BMI or metabolic syndrome as discriminators between MODY and T2D has become less helpful, which could contribute to the elevated BMI observed in this case. Although family history remains a significant diagnostic consideration for MODY, its value may be reduced owing to factors such as under-recognition, occurrence of novel mutations, and the common presence of T2D within family histories.

Management of HNF1A-MODY is tailored based on the patient's age and HbA1c levels. Initial therapy typically involves administering low-dose sulfonylureas, which stimulate insulin release by targeting ATP-sensitive potassium channels. As β-cell function deteriorates in HNF1A-MODY, GLP-1 receptor agonists may be a more effective and safer option compared to sulfonylureas, especially for those at elevated risk for hypoglycaemia. Findings from a small randomized, double-blind, crossover study revealed that liraglutide and glimepiride are both capable of lowering fasting plasma glucose and attenuating postprandial glucose excursions, with no substantial differences observed between the two in individuals with HNF1A-MODY. Furthermore, GLP-1 receptor agonists are associated with a reduced incidence of mild hypoglycaemia relative to glimepiride. Genetic testing allowed us to discontinue insulin therapy after glycaemic stabilization and shift the patient to treatment with a GLP-1 receptor agonist, successfully maintaining glycaemic control. This case underscores the importance of genetic testing in directing therapy choices. The presence of glycosuria in HNF1A-MODY results from decreased expression of SGLT2, a transporter regulated by HNF1A. Previous reports have noted that patients with HNF1A-MODY could develop significant dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis, attributed to pronounced inhibition of remaining SGLT-2 function. The use of SGLT-2 inhibitors in HNF1A-MODY may present significant hazards—including greater risk of dehydration, vascular collapse, genital infections, and diabetic ketoacidosis—potentially exceeding any therapeutic benefit. In our patient, combination therapy with liraglutide and metformin proved to be both effective and safe, consistent with previously published findings.

Precision medicine plays a vital role in managing MODY, since individuals with HNF1A-MODY frequently do not fulfill all diagnostic criteria even when current clinical guidelines are applied. At present, no reliable biomarkers exist to distinguish HNF1A-MODY from type 1 diabetes or T2D. Chen et al reported that, among 2429 young Chinese adults diagnosed with early-onset T2D, the prevalence of MODY was 1.5%, with 0.74% possessing pathogenic or likely pathogenic (LP) MODY variants. In a population-based study by Møller et al, involving Danish Caucasian patients with type 1 diabetes, 10% of the 39 individuals lacking high-risk HLA haplotypes were discovered to carry HNF1A mutations. Another recent Russian population-based analysis screened 506 patients presumed to have hereditary diabetes without autoimmune features—determined by negative IAA, ICA, GADA, and ZnT8 antibodies at presentation, preserved C-peptide levels, low insulin requirements over the initial five years, and no history of severe obesity. In this group, pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants were found in 46.4% of subjects, with HNF1A being among the genes most frequently affected. Confirming clinical suspicion of MODY necessitates DNA sequencing. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) enables simultaneous examination of substantial portions of the genome, thus increasing the practicality of MODY genetic screening. As NGS technology advances, it has facilitated the discovery of numerous new genetic alterations. The ACMG offers recommendations for the classification of these variants as pathogenic. Databases like ClinVar list over 1,000 distinct HNF1A variants, including those that are pathogenic, likely pathogenic, benign, likely benign, or of uncertain significance. In the case presented here, the unique heterozygous HNF1A variant (c.809A>G, p.N270S) was not documented in ClinVar or other widely referenced variant repositories, signifying its novelty rather than casting doubt on its clinical significance. Notably, this variant alters an amino acid adjacent to residues known to be pathogenic, displays a high AlphaMissense score (0.995), and segregates with diabetes within the family—each of which aligns with ACMG standards for likely pathogenic classification. Therefore, while ClinVar serves as an important compendium of reported variants, the evidence for pathogenicity of this specific variant arises from diverse and independent clinical and genetic data beyond simple database reporting.

Research has demonstrated that most detected mutations occur within the DNA-binding domain, whereas the transactivation domain exhibits a comparatively lower frequency of mutations. The novel variant described in this report is positioned within the DNA-binding domain. Several studies suggest that certain HNF1A mutations, while present, exert minimal impact on the functional capacity of the HNF1A protein. Moreover, some HNF1A variants that do not independently lead to MODY may nonetheless confer an elevated risk of developing T2D.

In summary, this case underscores the critical role of precise HNF1A-MODY diagnosis and its implications for personalized therapy. Ongoing investigations will aim to establish improved NGS screening methods to enhance MODY detection rates, thereby optimizing cost efficiency and clinical outcomes.
