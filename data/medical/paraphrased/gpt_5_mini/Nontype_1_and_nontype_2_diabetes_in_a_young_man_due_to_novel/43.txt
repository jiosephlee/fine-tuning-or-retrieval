Title: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

Yi-Chen Liu, Sheng-Chiang Su, Feng-Chih Kuo, Yi-Jen Hung, Yen-Lin Chen

Abstract:

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) is a monogenic form of diabetes inherited in an autosomal dominant manner. This case concerns a patient with HNF1A-MODY resulting from a novel heterozygous mutation in the HNF1A gene. The individual exhibited elevated blood glucose and glucose in the urine without evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis. The family history revealed several members with early-onset diabetes. Molecular analysis identified a previously undescribed pathogenic variant in HNF1A. Administration of a GLP-1 receptor agonist together with metformin led to a decline in glycated haemoglobin from 10.2% to 6.0% after 3 months. This case underscores the significance of genetic testing in instances of early-onset diabetes, which is commonly misclassified as type 1 or type 2 diabetes, and contributes to the growing list of HNF1A variants relevant for individualized therapy.

Background:

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) represents a collection of dominantly inherited diabetes disorders that are non-autoimmune in nature, marked by a fundamental defect affecting both the development and function of pancreatic β-cells. These disorders usually appear and are identified in individuals before they reach 25 years of age. MODY accounts for roughly 1%–6% of cases of diabetes presenting at a young age. Prevalence rates for MODY differ across distinct ethnic and racial populations and may, in some instances, be underestimated. Existing literature has predominantly focused on White European cohorts. More recently, studies have started to assess MODY prevalence among Asian and Australian groups. Reported rates of MODY prevalence are highly variable, largely because genetic testing is frequently restricted to those with specific clinical presentations, including studies involving population samples.

More than 15 genes have been implicated in causing the monogenic phenotype characteristic of MODY, with each mutation associated with distinct management approaches. Most instances across the various MODY subtypes stem predominantly from alterations in genes like hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A), glucokinase, hepatocyte nuclear factor 4-alpha, and hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-beta (HNF1B). HNF1A-MODY is the most frequently encountered MODY subtype, accounting for approximately 30%–65% of all diagnosed cases, and is marked by a pronounced autosomal dominant inheritance pattern leading to symptomatic diabetes within affected families. During early teenage years, affected individuals are often asymptomatic, and hyperglycemia is usually incidentally discovered during standard laboratory screenings, typically in the absence of clinical markers suggestive of insulin resistance. Assessment of blood glucose after meals or performing a 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) can reveal a considerable elevation in postprandial glucose (≥11.1 mmol/L). Glycosuria may also be present, resulting from a decreased renal threshold for glucose reabsorption due to impaired glucose handling in the renal tubules. As the condition progresses, affected individuals may develop classic diabetic manifestations such as polydipsia, polyuria, polyphagia, and unintended weight loss—findings common to both type 1 and type 2 diabetes (T2D). The significant overlap in clinical presentation makes it challenging to distinguish MODY from type 1 or T2D.

To date, multiple diagnostic pathways for MODY have been described in the literature. Following a detailed physical examination and evaluation of family history that indicates a clinical suspicion for MODY, along with confirmation of negative autoantibody status for markers associated with type 1 diabetes—such as glutamic acid decarboxylase antibody (GADA), insulinoma-associated antigen-2 antibody (IA-2A), and zinc transporter 8 (ZnT8)—supplemented by relevant laboratory investigations, molecular genetic analysis becomes a key component of the diagnostic work-up. Accurate identification of the underlying genetic defect is essential for selecting appropriate therapeutic options, estimating risks for diabetes-related complications, predicting disease progression, and enabling genetic counselling for family members. Recent studies have demonstrated that a large array of mutations within the HNF1A gene is implicated in HNF1A-MODY.

In the current report, genetic analysis utilizing a next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel identified a previously undescribed heterozygous variant, p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G), located in exon 4 of HNF1A in an individual with HNF1A-MODY. The pathogenicity of this substitution was assessed following the variant interpretation guidelines provided by the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG). This particular genetic alteration was absent from existing publications and was not catalogued in the VarSome or ClinVar databases. Written informed consent for the publication of this case was directly obtained from the patient.

Case presentation:

A young adult was assessed following referral for elevated blood glucose and the presence of glucose in the urine identified at his routine annual health check. On physical examination, his body mass index was 24.6 kg/m^2, blood pressure measured 112/70 mmHg, and pulse was 78 beats per minute. There were no signs of acanthosis nigricans on physical inspection. His family history was significant for diabetes diagnosed at a young age: his maternal grandmother in her 30s, his mother in her late 20s, and his younger brother at 20 years old. No family members with diabetes were reported to have experienced premature cardiovascular disease.

Investigations:

His glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) was elevated at 10.2% (88 mmol/mol), and his fasting plasma glucose measured 12.4 mmol/L. Two hours following a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), the glucose value reached 19.8 mmol/L. There was no evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis. The patient described polydipsia, increased hunger, and recent loss of body weight. The lipid panel indicated high-density lipoprotein cholesterol at 1.01 mmol/L and triglyceride concentration of 4.31 mmol/L, findings suggestive of potential metabolic syndrome (refer to tables 1 and 2). The individual denied any notable prior medical or surgical conditions.

A glucagon stimulation test yielded serum C-peptide levels of 0.39 nmol/L at baseline and 0.83 nmol/L at 6 minutes, reflecting reduced insulin secretory capacity. This test is utilized to assess remaining β-cell activity by determining C-peptide release following glucagon administration. Typically, a stimulated C-peptide level exceeding 0.60 nmol/L reflects preserved endogenous insulin production, whereas lower values are indicative of compromised β-cell function. Nonetheless, the glucagon stimulation test provides supportive—rather than confirmatory—evidence, especially when antibody testing is negative or the clinical features are atypical. Testing for microalbuminuria was negative. Evaluation of sudomotor activity revealed markedly impaired sudomotor responses in the hands. Sudomotor function assessment, performed with electrochemical skin conductance methods such as Sudoscan, non-invasively assesses small fiber autonomic nerve function. In this case, measurements demonstrated severely diminished conductance in the hands, signifying substantial autonomic neuropathy. Antibody assays for GADA and IA-2A were negative. One month following diagnosis, molecular testing for MODY was performed, which identified a heterozygous pathogenic (P) variant in the HNF1A gene (p.N270S [HNF1A:c.809A>G]), establishing a diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY.

Treatment:

Upon diagnosis, the patient exhibited markedly elevated HbA1c and plasma glucose values. The first-line therapy included basal insulin degludec at eight units daily, together with metformin at a daily dose of 1500 mg and saxagliptin at 5 mg per day, reflecting an initial assessment consistent with T2D. The objective of this regimen was to quickly achieve glycaemic control while targeting possible insulin resistance.

Alongside medication, the patient received instructions to follow a carefully regulated diet and to participate in consistent physical activity, forming part of a holistic approach to diabetes care. Subsequent follow-up visits were arranged at the endocrinology and metabolism outpatient clinic to track glycaemic status and evaluate response to therapy.

Nonetheless, due to moderately maintained yet insufficient β-cell function evidenced by a glucagon stimulation test (stimulated C-peptide: 0.83 nmol/L), negative results for islet autoantibodies, and a pronounced familial history of early-onset diabetes, MODY was strongly suspected.

The initial management was initiated before genetic testing results became available, relying on the presenting clinical characteristics and laboratory data at the time of diagnosis.

Outcome and follow-up:

One month later, the patient’s HbA1c dropped to 9.1%. Upon confirming HNF1A-MODY genetically and taking into account the patient’s partial but inadequate β-cell function and improved HbA1c (9.1%), insulin treatment was discontinued. Dulaglutide, a GLP-1 receptor agonist, was started at a dose of 1.5 mg weekly to aid in glycaemic regulation and support pancreatic function. After 6 months of this regimen, the patient's HbA1c fell to 6.0%, demonstrating marked progress. As glycaemic control stabilized, therapy was transitioned to a maintenance protocol consisting of metformin (1500 mg/day) and saxagliptin, a DPP-4 inhibitor (5 mg/day). The patient is monitored regularly, with HbA1c levels consistently remaining below 6.5%. Thus far, there is no evidence of microalbuminuria or diabetic retinopathy, and periodic assessment for chronic complications continues.

Discussion:

The HNF1A gene encodes a transcription factor situated on chromosome 12 at the q24 locus. This gene consists of 10 exons spanning 23,790 base pairs and produces a protein comprising 631 amino acids, which is present in multiple organs such as the liver, pancreas, and kidneys. Mutations in HNF1A result in the progressive loss of β-cell activity, demonstrated by decreased transcription of insulin and GLUT2, along with lower pyruvate kinase function. These alterations lead to impaired insulin secretion and the development of glucose intolerance.

Diagnosis in this patient followed contemporary criteria for MODY, which require clinical, biochemical, and genetic findings, including diabetes onset at a young age, a notable familial pattern, postprandial hyperglycemia, and negative diabetes-related autoantibodies. Fasting C-peptide levels above 0.6 ng/mL are indicative of diabetes that is not type 1. Nevertheless, ongoing assessment of C-peptide is important, as normal readings may be present in early type 1 diabetes. One major impediment to MODY detection is insufficient awareness and clinical suspicion, leading to frequent misclassification as type 1 or type 2 diabetes. The MODY probability calculator, created by the Exeter team, helps clinicians pinpoint individuals for genetic analysis. However, this calculator was built from data on a White European population and might overpredict MODY likelihood in individuals from other backgrounds. Applying this calculator to our patient's clinical data yielded a MODY probability of 62.4% (approximately a 1 in 1.6 chance). Multiple case reports have documented the presence of autoantibodies in certain individuals with MODY; some harboring HNF1A mutations may display autoantibodies, though most MODY patients do not. Previous research indicates MODY is associated with lower rates of obesity and metabolic syndrome compared to type 2 diabetes, but increasing global obesity has made differentiating MODY from T2DM based on BMI or metabolic risk factors less reliable, which could account for the elevated BMI observed in this case. While a family history of diabetes remains a key component for MODY identification, it may be less informative due to factors such as reporting inaccuracies, de novo mutations, and the frequent occurrence of type 2 diabetes within families.

Management of HNF1A-MODY is guided by patient age and HbA1c measurements. The initial preferred treatment consists of low-dose sulfonylureas, which stimulate insulin release by targeting potassium-sensitive ATP channels. As β-cell function diminishes in HNF1A-MODY, GLP-1 receptor agonists may provide a safer and more efficacious option, especially for those with an elevated risk of hypoglycaemia. Results from a small randomised, double-blind, crossover trial indicated that liraglutide and glimepiride both reduced fasting plasma glucose and postprandial glycaemic variability in HNF1A-MODY individuals, with no notable differences observed between the two agents. Moreover, GLP-1 receptor agonists are associated with a diminished incidence of mild hypoglycaemia compared to glimepiride. Genetic diagnosis facilitated the discontinuation of insulin after achieving glycaemic stability, allowing for successful transition to GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy that maintained glucose control. This case underscores the essential role of genetic testing in personalising treatment strategies. Glycosuria in HNF1A-MODY arises from decreased SGLT2 expression, regulated transcriptionally by HNF1A. A previous report described that patients with HNF1A-MODY may develop pronounced dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis as a consequence of robust inhibition of residual SGLT-2 activity. Potential hazards from SGLT-2 inhibitor use in these patients—including enhanced risk of dehydration, vascular collapse, genital infection, and diabetic ketoacidosis—may surpass any expected therapeutic gain. In this instance, the combination therapy with liraglutide and metformin proved both effective and safe, corroborating findings from prior research.

Personalized medicine is particularly important for MODY, since individuals with HNF1A-MODY may not fulfill all established diagnostic criteria despite the presence of clinical guidelines. At present, there are no reliable biomarkers that distinguish HNF1A-MODY from either type 1 or T2D. Chen et al demonstrated that among 2429 young adults of Chinese descent diagnosed early with T2D, MODY prevalence was 1.5%, and 0.74% carried pathogenic (P) or likely pathogenic (LP) MODY variants. Møller et al reported that, in a sizable population-based cohort of Danish Caucasian patients with type 1 diabetes, 10% of 39 individuals lacking any high-risk HLA haplotypes possessed mutations in the HNF1A gene. Likewise, a contemporary population-based investigation in Russia evaluated 506 subjects suspected of having hereditary diabetes who exhibited no autoimmune markers—characterized by negative IAA, ICA, GADA, and ZnT8 at onset, maintained C-peptide secretion, reduced insulin needs over five years, and no pronounced obesity. In this group, P or LP variants were detected in 46.4%, with HNF1A being among the most frequently implicated genes. Confirmation of MODY typically relies on DNA sequencing. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) enables the examination of extensive patient genetic material within a single reaction, which enhances the efficiency of MODY detection. With advances in NGS, numerous new genetic alterations have been found. The ACMG has issued criteria for assessing the pathogenic status of these variants. Databases such as ClinVar now list more than 1000 different HNF1A variants, encompassing pathogenic, likely pathogenic, benign, likely benign, and variants of uncertain significance. In this report, the newly identified heterozygous HNF1A variant (c.809A>G, p.N270S) was not documented in ClinVar or other standard variant databases, underscoring its uniqueness rather than its potential clinical impact. Significantly, the affected amino acid is positioned adjacent to other known pathogenic residues, the variant is characterized by a high AlphaMissense score (0.995), and it segregates with diabetes in the family, satisfying ACMG criteria for an LP designation. Consequently, although ClinVar serves as an important repository of reported variants, the pathogenicity of this particular variant is corroborated by multiple, independent genetic and clinical findings that extend beyond database listing alone.

Research has demonstrated that most detected mutations are concentrated within the DNA-binding domain, whereas the transactivation domain exhibits a lower incidence of mutations. The novel variant described in this report is also located in the DNA-binding domain. Certain investigations have revealed that HNF1A mutations, specifically P, exert limited influence on HNF1A protein function. Moreover, several HNF1A variants that do not independently cause MODY may nonetheless increase susceptibility to T2D.

In summary, this case emphasizes the necessity of precise identification of HNF1A-MODY and its implications for tailored therapy. Ongoing studies will aim to develop more efficient NGS-based screening approaches to enhance the MODY detection rate, optimize healthcare costs, and yield improved clinical outcomes.
