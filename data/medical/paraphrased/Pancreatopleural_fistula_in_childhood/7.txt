Title: Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood

Hans Vaish, Vipin Vaish, Shalini Suri, Vasudha Tomar

Abstract:

Summary: A male child in late childhood experienced repeated right-sided pleural effusions, along with episodes of chest pain and respiratory difficulty, which showed only transient relief following thoracentesis. The aspirated pleural fluid consistently appeared dark red. Due to the persistence of effusions, a right intercostal drain was inserted. Further history indicated a recent incident of blunt abdominal trauma involving a bicycle handle. Computed tomography of the chest revealed patchy consolidation on the right, a hydropneumothorax, and a multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right paraesophageal region to the pancreatic tail. Elevated levels of amylase and lipase were detected in both serum and pleural fluid samples. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography demonstrated bilateral pancreatopleural fistulas, with the right side more severely affected. Management included keeping the patient nil per oral, initiation of total parenteral nutrition, and administration of Octreotide. Over the course of 10 days, pleural drainage decreased, the chest tube was withdrawn, and the patient was discharged in stable condition.

Background:

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) represents a highly uncommon clinical condition characterized by recurrent, substantial, and often blood-tinged pleural effusions. PPF is associated with episodes of acute or chronic pancreatitis as well as pancreatic injury. Its clinical presentation is frequently misleading, as patients manifest respiratory symptoms resulting from repeated pleural effusions, which may divert initial diagnostic attention to thoracic causes rather than the underlying pancreatic abnormality. This diagnostic challenge can contribute to delays in identification and appropriate management. To date, the literature documents only 47 instances of PPF in pediatric patients, whereas over 300 adult cases have been described.

Case presentation:

A child presented to the outpatient clinic, accompanied by his mother, reporting a one-week history of respiratory difficulty and right-sided chest discomfort. According to the mother, his symptoms had gradually worsened over the course of seven days. There was no background of consanguinity. The mother disclosed that the child had been in contact with his grandfather, who had previously undergone treatment for pulmonary Koch’s. Dietary assessment revealed frequent intake of processed, high-calorie foods with little consumption of fresh fruits, vegetables, or nutritionally balanced home-prepared meals. On physical examination, his weight was recorded at 44 kg and height at 112 cm, yielding a body mass index of 35. The respiratory rate was 20/min, indicating mild tachypnoea. Movement of the right chest wall was diminished. Percussion demonstrated stony dullness over the right subscapular, right infra-axillary, and axillary regions. Vocal fremitus was noticeably decreased on the right compared to the left. Breath sounds were reduced in the aforementioned areas as well. There was no evidence of pallor, cyanosis, icterus, digital clubbing, or lymphadenopathy. The abdomen was soft without evidence of hepatosplenomegaly. Neurologically, the child was alert and oriented to time, place, and person. The cardiovascular examination showed tachycardia without murmurs. The recorded oxygen saturation was 88%–89% on ambient air, and the heart rate was 110/min. Given the presence of tachypnoea and respiratory distress, the patient was transferred to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit and initiated on oxygen therapy via facemask at 5 l/min. A chest X-ray was performed, and empirical piperacillin-tazobactam was commenced following blood culture collection. Imaging indicated a right-sided pleural effusion with obliteration of the right costophrenic angle (figure 1) (Table 2). Point-of-care lung ultrasound estimated the effusion volume at approximately one litre (figure 2) (Table 2). Pleural fluid was aspirated from the right side under strict aseptic conditions, yielding roughly one litre of dark red fluid. Following thoracocentesis, the tachypnoea resolved and oxygen saturation improved to 95%–98% on room air. The patient’s chest pain lessened, and oxygen therapy was discontinued within 24 hours. The child was subsequently moved to the general ward the following day and discharged. The aspirated pleural fluid was sent for laboratory analysis.

However, the patient returned six days later exhibiting similar symptoms—right-sided chest pain, tachypnoea, and respiratory difficulty—and was readmitted to the PICU. A repeat chest X-ray again demonstrated right-sided pleural effusion. Thoracocentesis was repeated, with aspiration of another litre of dark red fluid. Pleural fluid Adenosine Deaminase (ADA) was found to be mildly elevated (Table 1), and due to the known exposure to Koch’s, empirical anti-tubercular therapy was initiated. Five days after discharge, the child was hospitalized a third time, again presenting with right-sided chest pain and breathing difficulty. On re-evaluating the clinical history, the patient disclosed sustaining blunt abdominal trauma with a bicycle handle one month prior. Before intercostal drainage (ICD) insertion, a contrast-enhanced CT scan of the chest was obtained. The scan revealed patchy consolidation in the right lung, a right-sided hydropneumothorax, and an elongated, multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right pleural space and tracking retroperitoneally up to the pancreatic tail (Table 2). An ICD was inserted, resulting in drainage of approximately one litre of dark red fluid (figure 3). This combination of imaging findings, pleural fluid appearance, and trauma history suggested a diagnosis of PPF. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) was then performed, identifying features consistent with pancreatitis and a tubular cystic collection in the distal pancreatic body and tail, which extended posteriorly into the retroperitoneum and ascended through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity. There was also a narrow channel communicating with the left pleural cavity. Communication with the distal pancreatic duct was visualized, along with ductal narrowing upstream of the lesion. MRCP findings confirmed PPF (figures 4–6) (table 1). Serum and pleural fluid levels of amylase and lipase were measured, and pleural analysis demonstrated markedly elevated amylase (6118 U/L) and lipase (7211 U/L) concentrations (table 2), substantiating the diagnosis of PPF. Differential diagnosis:

The patient exhibited pleural effusion accompanied by chest pain and had a clear account of abdominal trauma occurring one month prior. All etiologies of pleural effusion aside from post-traumatic pancreatitis had been excluded. The child lacked symptoms such as fever, chronic cough, or weight loss. While there was a familial history of Koch’s contact and a mildly increased pleural fluid ADA level, GeneXpert testing for MTB returned negative, thus excluding pulmonary Koch’s. Additionally, the absence of fever and neutrophilic exudate in the pleural fluid ruled out empyema. Although neoplastic processes (lung cancer, leukaemia, lymphoma, or metastatic tumors) may be associated with amylase-enriched pleural fluid, factors such as no fever, lack of weight loss, a normal hematologic profile, and unremarkable peripheral smear made malignancy improbable. Furthermore, pleural fluid cytology did not detect any malignant cells. Boerhaave’s syndrome (esophageal perforation) was also considered, but the diagnosis was dismissed since the child exhibited regular bowel movements and reported no chest pain or vomiting. Acquired diaphragmatic hernia was not supported by imaging findings, as no abdominal organs were present within the thoracic cavity. Hepatic hydrothorax was excluded based on unremarkable liver function tests, normal liver imaging, and no clinical history of chronic liver disease. Finally, both renal imaging and function tests were normal, thereby excluding renal pathology or hydronephrosis.

Treatment:

In the PICU, the patient received humidified oxygen via mask to provide respiratory support. Thoracocentesis was performed twice under ultrasound guidance, with each procedure yielding approximately one litre of blood-tinged pleural fluid. Antitubercular medications were initiated on an empirical basis but discontinued once MRCP confirmed the diagnosis. During the third hospitalization, an intercostal drain (ICD) was inserted on the right side under strict aseptic technique, again resulting in drainage of roughly one litre of blood-stained pleural fluid into the ICD bag. As PPF is an infrequent and rare occurrence in paediatric patients and there are no standardized therapeutic protocols, treatment decisions were informed by existing case reports. Initial management consisted of conservative measures: the child was made nil per oral and commenced on Octreotide infusion at a dose of 30 µg/kg/day (range: 10–40 µg/kg/day). Total parenteral nutrition was instituted alongside pancreatic enzyme supplementation. Over a 10-day period, drainage from the ICD progressively diminished and ultimately ceased, allowing for removal of the ICD and subsequent hospital discharge of the patient.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following his release from the hospital, the child has not exhibited any symptoms. He continues to see me for monthly follow-up appointments. Since his discharge, there have been no further instances of recurrent pleural effusions (S17). He has adopted lifestyle changes, including maintaining a balanced diet and engaging in regular physical activity as recommended. The patient has resumed his normal daily routines and attends school consistently. He is able to participate in vigorous activities such as running and playing without experiencing any problems.

Discussion:

PPF represents an exceedingly rare and sporadic complication of pancreatic pathology in children. To date, only 47 paediatric cases of PPF have been reported in the literature. This entity can occur in both acute and chronic pancreatitis or following paediatric abdominal trauma. The exact frequency in paediatric patients remains undetermined; however, it complicates approximately 0.4% of all pancreatitis cases overall. Genetic alterations, including mutations in Serine peptidase inhibitor Kazal type 1 (SPINK1) and PRSS1, contribute significantly, accounting for up to 30% of chronic pancreatitis in children. Blunt force abdominal trauma is also a substantial non-genetic trigger, comprising 32% of patients in a 25-case series reported by Yang et al. Cases without obvious cause—possibly due to unrecognized genetic predispositions or ductal variants—are still observed.

The pathophysiology involves pancreatic fluid escaping from a disrupted pancreatic duct or pseudocyst, which may travel through diaphragmatic hiatuses (most often the aortic or oesophageal hiatus), or less commonly through direct erosion into the diaphragm, ultimately resulting in a fistula connecting to the pleural space. Frequently, the initial presentation features thoracic complaints, which may divert clinicians toward pulmonary etiologies, while the underlying disorder is intra-abdominal. Literature review revealed 21 pertinent studies, indicating that 68% of cases exhibited predominant respiratory symptoms, including massive, recurrent, unilateral or bilateral pleural effusions, as well as dyspnoea and cough. Fewer than 20% of patients reported gastrointestinal manifestations such as epigastric pain, vomiting, or weight loss, while 24% presented with constitutional symptoms (fever). A report by Zi Yin Shang in Frontiers in Paediatrics 2023 detailed an 8-year-old male with PPF who presented exclusively with a bulging left chest wall of two weeks’ duration—an exceedingly unusual presentation. In such instances, pleural effusion probably accumulates progressively post-trauma; effective compensatory function by the contralateral lung allows adaptation and masks classical respiratory symptoms. Moreover, psychosocial factors—such as school bullying in this case—may lead children to withhold important clinical information (e.g., about preceding trauma). This patient initially underwent extensive evaluation for tuberculosis and malignancy due to haemorrhagic pleural effusion before PPF was recognized as the aetiology.

Similarly, in our own experience, a child presented with recurrent chest pain and right-sided pleural effusions. A history of abdominal trauma emerged only after repeated interviewing, and MRCP findings pointed toward pancreatic disease. This underscores the potential for PPF to “masquerade” as a purely pulmonary disorder, which may misdirect clinicians unless careful history-taking and targeted imaging are pursued.

The accumulation of pleural fluid in PPF typically results from the rupture of a pancreatic duct or pseudocyst—secondary to trauma or infection—resulting in leakage of enzyme-rich pancreatic secretions. If the rupture involves the anterior pancreatic surface, fluid accumulates within the peritoneal cavity as ascites. Posterior ductal disruption, on the other hand, causes fluid to track retroperitoneally, ascend via the path of least resistance through natural diaphragmatic hiatuses into the mediastinum, and breach the pleura, producing pleural effusion. This creates a scenario of internal pancreatic fistulization, effectively channeling pancreatic output into the chest.

Whereas alcohol is the predominant factor in chronic pancreatitis leading to PPF among adults—Uchiyama et al.’s comprehensive review of 113 Japanese cases found 99% linked to chronic alcoholic pancreatitis, with thoracic symptoms dominant in 68%—in paediatric patients, inherited genetic abnormalities are more significant. Well-established contributors include PRSS1 mutations (particularly R122H and N291 in exon 3 and 2, respectively), CFTR mutations, SPINK1, and chymotrypsin C gene defects. In cases of recurrent or persistent childhood pancreatitis, clinicians should collect thorough family histories and maintain suspicion for hereditary aetiologies.

Delayed diagnosis is common, given that many patients initially undergo extensive pulmonary investigations before a pancreatic source is considered. A pleural fluid amylase level of $\geq1000\,\mathrm{IU/L}$ is diagnostic for PPF; however, tuberculosis, oesophageal rupture, and malignancies may also cause elevated amylase, necessitating differentiation by determining the amylase isoenzyme (pancreatic vs. salivary). Amylase isoenzyme typing can thus be integral to the diagnostic workup.
