Title: Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood

Hans Vaish, Vipin Vaish, Shalini Suri, Vasudha Tomar

Abstract:

Summary: A male child in late childhood exhibited repeated episodes of right-sided pleural effusions accompanied by chest pain and breathing difficulties, with symptoms showing temporary relief following fluid aspiration. The pleural effusion was consistently described as dark red in appearance. Because the effusions recurred, a right intercostal drainage tube was inserted. Upon further inquiry, the patient was found to have suffered recent abdominal trauma caused by a bicycle handlebar. Computed tomography of the chest revealed patchy areas of consolidation on the right, the presence of hydropneumothorax, and a multiloculated, hypodense fluid collection extending from the right paraesophageal area to the pancreatic tail. Elevated levels of amylase and lipase were detected in both the serum and pleural fluid. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography demonstrated the existence of bilateral pancreatopleural fistulas, more prominent on the right side. Management included maintaining the patient nil per oral, administering total parenteral nutrition, and treating with Octreotide. Within 10 days, the output from the drainage decreased sufficiently, the chest tube was withdrawn, and the patient was subsequently discharged.

Background:

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) represents an exceedingly uncommon condition, characterized by the presence of recurrent, voluminous, and occasionally hemorrhagic pleural effusions. It typically occurs in the context of acute or chronic pancreatitis, as well as following pancreatic trauma. The clinical presentation may be misleading, as patients often exhibit symptoms of respiratory compromise associated with persistent pleural effusions, while the underlying cause is pancreatic in origin. This often results in diagnostic delays, since initial investigations focus primarily on thoracic causes. To date, there have been only 47 documented cases of PPF in the paediatric population, whereas the number of reported cases in adults exceeds 300.

Case presentation:

A young boy was presented to the outpatient clinic with his mother, reporting a one-week history of breathing difficulty and right-sided chest pain. According to the mother, these symptoms had gradually worsened over the previous seven days. There was no familial consanguinity. The mother also mentioned exposure to a household case of pulmonary Koch’s disease (the child’s grandfather), who had completed appropriate treatment. Dietary recall indicated a pattern of frequent processed and calorie-dense food consumption, with a noted paucity of fruits, vegetables, and balanced home meals. Anthropometric assessment revealed a weight of 44 kg, height of 112 cm, and a body mass index of 35.

Upon examination, the child’s respiratory rate was 20 per minute, with signs of mild tachypnoea. Physical exam showed diminished movement of the right side of the chest. Percussion produced a stony dull note over the right subscapular, infra-axillary, and axillary regions. Vocal fremitus on the right was markedly less compared to the left side. Breath sounds were attenuated in the aforementioned regions. There were no signs of pallor, cyanosis, icterus, clubbing, or lymphadenopathy. The abdomen was soft, without evidence of hepatosplenomegaly. The child remained alert and appropriately oriented. Cardiovascular examination identified tachycardia but no murmurs. Oxygen saturation was 88%–89% on ambient air with a pulse of 110 beats/min.

Given his tachypnoea and respiratory distress, the patient was transferred to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit, where supplemental oxygen (5 l/min via mask) was commenced. A chest X-ray was requested, and after sending blood cultures, empirical antibiotic treatment with piperacillin-tazobactam was started. The chest X-ray showed a right-sided pleural effusion and blunting of the right costophrenic angle (figure 1) (Table 2). Point-of-care lung ultrasound demonstrated an approximately one-litre pleural effusion (figure 2) (Table 2). Using sterile precautions, right-sided thoracocentesis was performed, draining about one litre of dark red pleural fluid. After this intervention, tachypnoea resolved and room air oxygen saturation improved to 95%–98%. The child’s chest pain abated, and oxygen therapy was discontinued within 24 hours. He was subsequently transferred to the general pediatric ward and discharged, with the aspirated pleural fluid sent for laboratory evaluation.

Six days post-discharge, the patient was re-admitted with recurrence of right-sided chest pain, tachypnoea, and breathing difficulties. Again shifted to the PICU, chest radiography confirmed a recurrent right-sided pleural effusion. Repeat thoracocentesis yielded another litre of dark red fluid. Pleural fluid Adenosine Deaminase (ADA) was marginally raised (Table 1), and given the positive history of Koch’s contact, empirical anti-tubercular therapy was initiated.

Despite these interventions, the child was admitted for a third time five days later, presenting again with similar symptoms. On this occasion, a detailed history revealed that he had sustained blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle one month earlier. Before placement of an intercostal drain (ICD), a contrast-enhanced chest CT was conducted, showing patchy air space consolidation in the right lung, right hydropneumothorax, and an elongated multiloculated hypodense collection stretching from the right pleural space and tracking from the right paraoesophageal region to the upper left retroperitoneum to the pancreatic tail (Table 2). Approximately one litre of dark red pleural fluid was again drained via an ICD (figure 3).

The combination of CT findings, dark red pleural fluid, and history of abdominal trauma suggested a diagnosis of a PPF. Accordingly, MRCP was performed the same day, revealing pancreatitis with a tubular cystic collection in the distal pancreatic body and tail, extending retroperitoneally and tracking superiorly through the oesophageal hiatus into the right pleural space. A narrow tract to the left pleural space was observed. The collection communicated with the distal pancreatic duct, which exhibited proximal narrowing. MRCP findings confirmed the diagnosis of PPF (figures 4-6) (Table 1). Serum and pleural fluid samples were analyzed for amylase and lipase, showing markedly elevated levels in the pleural fluid: amylase 6118 U/L, lipase 7211 U/L (Table 2), corroborating the diagnosis of PPF.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient exhibited pleural effusion and chest pain, with a documented incident of abdominal trauma occurring one month prior. After thorough evaluation, alternative etiologies for pleural effusion apart from post-traumatic pancreatitis were excluded. The patient had no prior episodes of fever, persistent cough, or unintended weight loss. Although there was a family history of Koch’s contact and the pleural fluid adenosine deaminase (ADA) level was mildly raised, GeneXpert testing for MTB yielded a negative result, effectively excluding pulmonary Koch’s as a diagnosis. The absence of fever and neutrophilic exudate in the pleural fluid further ruled out empyema. While neoplastic causes, such as lung cancer, leukaemia, lymphoma, or metastatic disease, are associated with amylase-rich pleural effusion, these were discounted due to the lack of fever or weight loss, a normal hematological profile, unremarkable peripheral smear, and negative pleural fluid cytology for malignant cells. Boerhaave’s syndrome (oesophageal perforation), another possible cause of elevated amylase in pleural fluid, was considered unlikely given the patient’s regular bowel movements and absence of chest pain or vomiting. Acquired diaphragmatic hernia was excluded due to the absence of abdominal organs within the thoracic cavity on imaging studies. The possibility of hepatic hydrothorax was disregarded because both liver imaging and liver function tests were normal, and there was no evidence or history suggestive of chronic liver disease. Finally, both normal renal imaging and function tests ruled out underlying renal pathology, including hydronephrosis.

Treatment:

In the PICU, the patient received humidified oxygen via mask to provide respiratory assistance. Ultrasound-guided thoracocentesis was performed twice, with approximately one litre of blood-tinged pleural fluid removed each time. Empirical antitubercular therapy was initiated but discontinued upon diagnostic clarification through MRCP. During the third hospital admission, an intercostal drain (ICD) was aseptically placed on the right side, yielding a further litre of hemorrhagic pleural fluid collected in the ICD bag. Due to the rarity of PPF in paediatric cases and the absence of established treatment protocols, management was informed by existing case reports. A conservative approach was adopted: the child was maintained nil per oral and commenced on an Octreotide infusion at 30 µg/kg/day (with a permissible range of 10–40 µg/kg/day). Total parenteral nutrition was initiated alongside pancreatic enzyme supplementation. Over the subsequent 10 days, the volume of drainage from the ICD gradually lessened and eventually ceased. The ICD was subsequently removed, and the patient was discharged home.

Outcome and follow-up:

Since leaving the hospital, the child has not exhibited any symptoms. He continues to attend scheduled monthly follow-up appointments. There have not been additional occurrences of recurrent pleural effusions since his discharge (S17). He has implemented recommended lifestyle modifications, maintaining a balanced diet and participating in regular physical activity. The child has resumed his normal routines, including consistent school attendance. He is able to engage in physically demanding activities such as running and playing without experiencing any problems.

Discussion:

PPF represents a highly uncommon and seldom-seen complication arising from pancreatic disease in children. To date, literature documents just 47 paediatric cases of PPF. The condition is associated with both acute and chronic forms of pancreatitis, as well as traumatic events affecting children. Its precise incidence within paediatric cohorts remains indeterminate, although PPF is estimated to complicate approximately 0.4% of pancreatitis cases overall. Notably, genetic mutations such as those in serine peptidase inhibitor Kazal type 1 (SPINK1) and PRSS1 are frequently implicated, accounting for nearly 30% of chronic paediatric pancreatitis cases. In addition, blunt trauma to the abdomen serves as a significant non-genetic precipitating factor, responsible for 32% of cases in a 25-patient cohort reviewed by Yang et al. Nonetheless, idiopathic cases are also documented, potentially attributable to unrecognized genetic predispositions or ductal anomalies. 

The mechanism by which pancreatic fluid migrates to the pleural cavity involves leakage from a compromised pancreatic duct or pseudocyst, traversing normal diaphragmatic apertures—most commonly the aortic or oesophageal hiatus—or, less frequently, by direct transdiaphragmatic erosion, thereby forming a fistula into the pleural space. Because the majority of paediatric PPF cases present predominantly with thoracic manifestations, initial clinical focus and diagnostic resources are often directed towards investigating respiratory etiologies, resulting in the true intra-abdominal source being overlooked. A review of the literature yielded 21 relevant case reports; 68% of these featured prominent respiratory complaints, including substantial and often recurrent pleural effusions (unilateral or bilateral), dyspnoea, and cough. In contrast, fewer than one-fifth displayed abdominal complaints like epigastric pain, vomiting, or weight loss, and 24% reported constitutional signs such as fever. For example, a case presented by Zi Yin Shang in *Frontiers in Paediatrics* (2023) detailed an 8-year-old boy whose initial symptom was only a progressive left-sided chest bulge over two weeks—a presentation that is exceedingly rare. In this patient, pleural effusion developed gradually following a traumatic abdominal incident. Due to compensatory adaptation by the unaffected lung, classic symptoms such as dyspnoea or cough were absent. Compounding the diagnostic challenge, the patient concealed his trauma history due to social factors, delaying recognition of the underlying cause. Initially, haemorrhagic pleural effusion prompted an evaluation for tuberculosis and malignancy, but the diagnostic pathway ultimately redirected to a pancreatic etiology.

Similarly, in our observed case, a child repeatedly experienced right-sided pleural fluid collections accompanied by chest pain. Only after repeated, detailed history-taking was a prior abdominal trauma revealed; subsequent MRCP findings were pivotal in identifying the pancreatic source. This scenario underlines how PPF may be misattributed to a primary pulmonary process—the so-called ‘pulmonary mask’—leading clinicians astray unless a thorough clinical history is obtained and appropriate abdominal imaging is pursued.

The pathogenesis of PPF entails disruption of the pancreatic duct or rupture of a pseudocyst, secondary to trauma or infection, allowing pancreatic secretions to escape. The anatomical site of ductal rupture dictates the direction of fluid leakage: anterior disruptions release fluid into the peritoneal cavity causing ascites, while posterior disruptions allow tracking into the retroperitoneum and subsequently upward through the aortic or oesophageal hiatus into the mediastinum, where the fluid may penetrate the pleura and form a pleural effusion. The process effectively creates an internal pancreatic fistula, siphoning pancreatic fluids into the thoracic cavity. 

In adults, chronic alcoholic pancreatitis is the predominant cause of PPF. Uchiyama et al provided an extensive analysis of 113 Japanese cases, demonstrating that 99% were attributed to alcoholic pancreatitis, with respiratory symptoms prevailing in 68%. Contrastingly, in the paediatric demographic, genetic etiologies are more prevalent. Well-characterized mutations—including those in the PRSS1, CF transmembrane regulator, SPINK1, and chymotrypsin C genes—are recognised causes of hereditary pancreatitis, particularly PRSS1 gene mutations R122H and N291 in exons 3 and 2. Therefore, paediatric patients with recurrent or chronic pancreatitis should be thoroughly evaluated for familial history and considered for hereditary pancreatitis.

Delays in diagnosis are common, as initial assessment typically involves extensive pulmonary evaluation before the possibility of a pancreatic origin is considered. A pleural fluid amylase concentration ≥1000 IU/L is considered diagnostic for PPF. However, it is imperative to distinguish PPF from conditions such as tuberculosis, oesophageal perforation, or malignancy—entities also associated with increased pleural fluid amylase.
