Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood

Hans Vaish, Vipin Vaish, Shalini Suri, Vasudha Tomar

Abstract:

Summary: A male child in late childhood was admitted with multiple episodes of right-sided pleural effusion, accompanied by chest pain and respiratory difficulty, which transiently resolved following thoracentesis. The aspirated pleural fluid consistently appeared dark red. Due to the recurrence of effusions, a right-sided intercostal drain was inserted. Clinical history uncovered recent blunt abdominal injury caused by impact with a bicycle handle. Computed tomography of the chest identified patchy areas of consolidation in the right lung, the presence of a hydropneumothorax, and a multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right paraesophageal area to the pancreatic tail. Laboratory analysis demonstrated elevated amylase and lipase in both serum and pleural fluid samples. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography further delineated bilateral pancreatopleural fistulas, more pronounced on the right. Management included bowel rest, provision of total parenteral nutrition, and administration of Octreotide. Within ten days, the volume of drainage lessened, the chest drain was removed, and the patient was discharged.

Background:

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) represents a highly uncommon condition characterized by recurrent, large-volume, and often hemorrhagic pleural effusions. This complication has been documented in the context of acute and chronic pancreatitis as well as following pancreatic trauma. The clinical presentation is often misleading, as symptoms primarily relate to respiratory distress stemming from repeated pleural effusions, while the underlying cause is pancreatic in origin. This frequently leads to diagnostic delays, as initial investigations typically focus on thoracic etiologies rather than abdominal sources. To date, the literature contains reports of only 47 pediatric cases of PPF, in contrast to over 300 cases documented in adults.

Case presentation:

A pediatric patient presented to the outpatient clinic, accompanied by his mother, with a primary complaint of progressive respiratory difficulty and right-sided chest pain that had persisted for one week. According to his mother, the child’s breathing problems worsened incrementally over the course of seven days. There was no reported family history of consanguinity. She did report, however, that the child had been in close contact with his grandfather, who was previously diagnosed and appropriately managed for pulmonary Koch's. Dietary history revealed that the child habitually consumed processed, high-calorie foods while his intake of fresh fruits, vegetables, and nutritionally balanced home-cooked meals was limited. On evaluation, his weight measured 44 kg, height 112 cm, and body mass index was 35.

Clinical examination revealed a respiratory rate of 20 /min and mild tachypnoea. The right side of his chest exhibited decreased movement, and percussion was stony dull over the right subscapular, right infra-axillary, and axillary areas. Vocal fremitus was markedly diminished on the right compared to the left. Auscultation detected reduced breath sounds in the right subscapular, right infra-axillary, and axillary zones. There was no evidence of pallor, cyanosis, icterus, digital clubbing, or lymphadenopathy. The abdomen was soft with no detectable hepatosplenomegaly, and the child was alert, well oriented in terms of time, place, and person. Cardiovascular assessment identified tachycardia without the presence of murmurs. Room air oxygen saturation was 88%–89%, and heart rate was 110 /min.

Given the child’s respiratory distress and tachypnoea, he was admitted to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit and administered supplemental oxygen via face mask at 5 L/min. A chest radiograph was obtained, and empirical antibiotic therapy (piperacillin-tazobactam) was initiated after collection of blood cultures. Chest X-ray indicated a right-sided pleural effusion with obliteration of the right costophrenic angle (figure 1) (Table 2). Bedside lung ultrasound estimated the effusion at roughly one litre (figure 2) (Table 2). Under sterile conditions, thoracentesis yielded approximately one litre of dark red pleural fluid. Following this, tachypnoea subsided and oxygen saturation on room air improved to 95%–98%. Chest pain also subsided and he was able to discontinue oxygen therapy within 24 hours, after which he was transferred to the ward and later discharged. The pleural aspirate was sent for laboratory analysis.

However, six days post-discharge, the patient was readmitted with a recurrence of right-sided chest pain, increased respiratory rate, and dyspnea. Readmission to the PICU occurred, with repeat chest radiograph again revealing a right pleural effusion. Repeat thoracocentesis was performed, draining about one litre of similarly dark red fluid. Pleural fluid Adenosine Deaminase (ADA) was slightly raised (Table 1). Given the positive history of tuberculosis exposure, antitubercular therapy was empirically initiated.

The child presented for a third hospitalization five days later, again with right-sided chest pain and respiratory distress. A thorough history-taking at this point revealed that he had experienced blunt trauma to the abdomen with a bicycle handle a month prior. A contrast-enhanced CT of the chest was requested before placement of an intercostal drain (ICD). CT imaging identified patchy consolidation in the right lung, a right-sided hydropneumothorax, and an elongated, lobulated, multiloculated hypodense fluid collection extending from the right pleural space, traversing from the right paraoesophageal region to the upper left retroperitoneum up to the tail of the pancreas (Table 2). Placement of an ICD facilitated drainage of about one litre of dark red pleural fluid (figure 3). These radiologic findings, combined with the clinical history of blunt abdominal trauma and recurrent, sanguineous pleural effusions, suggested a diagnosis of PPF.

Magnetic Resonance Cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) was therefore performed the same day, revealing findings consistent with pancreatitis and a tubular cystic collection at the distal pancreatic body and tail that tracked posteriorly within the retroperitoneum and ascended through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural space. There was also a narrow tract communicating with the left pleural cavity, and the collection was in continuity with the distal pancreatic duct, which was noted to be narrowed proximally. MRCP findings confirmed the diagnosis of PPF (figures 4–6) (Table 1). Differential diagnosis:

The patient exhibited pleural effusion accompanied by chest pain and had sustained an abdominal trauma one month prior. Following a thorough evaluation, all other potential etiologies of pleural effusion were excluded apart from post-traumatic pancreatitis. The child lacked symptoms such as fever, persistent cough, or weight loss. Although there was a familial history of Koch’s contact and the pleural fluid ADA level was slightly raised, GeneXpert for MTB returned negative, excluding pulmonary Koch’s. No evidence of fever or neutrophilic exudate was observed in the pleural fluid, thereby excluding empyema. While malignancies—including lung cancer, leukaemia, lymphoma, and metastatic tumors—may be associated with amylase-rich pleural effusion, these were discounted on the basis of absent fever, lack of weight loss, unremarkable blood investigations, and a normal peripheral smear. Pleural fluid cytology also failed to detect any malignant cells. Boerhaave’s syndrome, characterized by oesophageal rupture and the possibility of amylase-rich effusion, was considered unlikely as the child maintained regular bowel movements and had no reported chest pain or vomiting. Acquired diaphragmatic hernia was also excluded since imaging did not reveal any abdominal organs within the thoracic cavity. Hepatic hydrothorax was discounted due to normal liver function tests and imaging, coupled with no prior history suggestive of chronic liver disease. Finally, normal renal imaging and function effectively ruled out renal causes such as hydronephrosis.

Treatment:

The patient received humidified oxygen via mask as respiratory support in the PICU. Thoracocentesis, performed under ultrasound guidance, was carried out twice, with each procedure yielding approximately one litre of pleural fluid tinged with blood. Antitubercular therapy was initiated empirically but discontinued once a definitive diagnosis was established through MRCP. During the third hospitalization, an intercostal drain (ICD) was inserted on the right side under sterile conditions, again draining about one litre of blood-stained pleural fluid into the ICD collection bag. Due to the rarity of PPF in paediatric patients and the absence of standardized management protocols, treatment decisions were informed by available case literature. Initial management involved conservative measures: the patient was kept nil per os and commenced on an Octreotide infusion at a dosage of 30 µg/kg/day (with a therapeutic range of 10–40 µg/kg/day). Total parenteral nutrition and supplementation with pancreatic enzymes were also initiated. Over the next 10 days, there was a gradual decline in ICD drainage, which eventually ceased. The ICD was subsequently removed, and the patient was discharged in stable condition.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following his release from the hospital, the patient has not exhibited any further symptoms. He continues to attend scheduled monthly follow-up appointments with me. Since being discharged, there have been no additional occurrences of recurrent pleural effusions (S17). The patient has adopted lifestyle changes, incorporating a balanced diet and regular physical activity as per medical recommendations. He has resumed his normal routine and attends school consistently. Moreover, he is able to engage in vigorous activities, including running and playing, without experiencing any issues.

Discussion:

PPF represents a highly uncommon and sporadic complication of pancreatic disorders in children. To date, only 47 cases of PPF have been reported in the paediatric population. PPF may develop secondary to either acute or chronic pancreatitis or result from trauma in children. The precise prevalence among paediatric patients remains undefined, although, in the broader context of pancreatitis, it complicates approximately 0.4% of cases. Notably, mutations in serine peptidase inhibitor Kazal type 1 (SPINK1) and PRSS1 contribute to as much as 30% of paediatric chronic pancreatitis cases. Traumatic injury to the abdomen serves as another key non-genetic precipitant, representing 32% of the 25 paediatric patients reviewed by Yang et al. Idiopathic presentations are still observed, possibly attributable to yet unidentified genetic or ductal anomalies. 

The mechanism of PPF entails leakage of pancreatic secretions from either a disrupted duct or a pseudocyst, which may migrate through physiological diaphragmatic openings—such as the aortic or oesophageal hiatus—or, more rarely, via direct diaphragmatic erosion, thus establishing a fistulous connection to the pleural cavity. Most cases are initially overlooked since clinical presentation typically involves thoracic symptoms, diverting diagnostic focus toward pulmonary disease, when in reality the underlying pathology is intra-abdominal. In a comprehensive literature review, 21 pertinent studies were identified, with 68% of reported cases manifesting predominantly respiratory symptoms such as substantial, recurrent pleural effusions (unilateral or bilateral), shortness of breath, and cough. Abdominal complaints—like epigastric pain, vomiting, and unintended weight loss—were noted in under 20% of cases, whereas constitutional symptoms, including fever, appeared in 24%. 

A case described by Zi Yin Shang in Frontiers in Paediatrics 2023 highlighted an 8-year-old male who initially presented solely with a protruding left chest for two weeks—such a presentation is exceedingly rare. It was inferred that progressive pleural effusion occurred following abdominal trauma, with compensation by the unaffected lung resulting in the absence of classical respiratory symptoms. The patient, furthermore, concealed a history of trauma due to bullying at school. An extensive workup, including investigations for tuberculosis and malignancy, failed to reveal the etiology until the correct diagnosis was considered. Similarly, in our experience, the patient presented with repeated pulmonary complaints, namely chest pain and right-sided pleural effusions; it was only after repeated, thorough history-taking and imaging with MRCP that a pancreatic source was identified. Thus, PPF can mimic pulmonary diseases and mislead clinicians unless comprehensive history and targeted diagnostic studies are pursued.

The fluid accumulation in PPF can be precipitated by mechanical forces or infection, leading to pancreatic duct or pseudocyst rupture and subsequent fluid leakage. Anterior ductal rupture allows pancreatic fluid into the peritoneal cavity, producing ascites, while posterior rupture results in fluid translocating retroperitoneally, then upwards through the diaphragmatic hiatus to the mediastinum, and finally into the pleural space, generating an effusion. Essentially, the internal pancreatic fistula functions as a conduit diverting pancreatic fluids to the chest. In adult patients, chronic pancreatitis leading to PPF is predominantly due to excessive alcohol use. Uchiyama et al. conducted an extensive Japanese review (113 cases), finding that 99% of PPF cases stemmed from chronic alcoholic pancreatitis, with thoracic symptoms observed in 68%. By contrast, in paediatric cases, hereditary factors predominate, particularly mutations in genes such as PRSS1, cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR), SPINK1, and chymotrypsin C. Specifically, the R122H and N291 mutations in exons 3 and 2, respectively, of the PRSS1 gene are most notable. Recurrent or chronic pancreatitis in children should prompt an in-depth family history and consideration of hereditary aetiology.

Diagnosis is frequently delayed as these patients often undergo extensive pulmonary evaluation before the pancreatic origin is suspected. A pleural fluid amylase concentration $\geq1000$ IU/L is characteristic of PPF. Nevertheless, alternative causes such as tuberculosis, oesophageal rupture, and neoplasms of the lung or pancreas, which can also elevate pleural fluid amylase, must be excluded. PPF is differentiated by the pancreatic type of amylase in the pleural fluid, in contrast to the salivary type seen in the aforementioned other conditions. Therefore, amylase typing is an important component of the diagnostic approach. MRCP yields a sensitivity of 70%–80% in depicting fistulas and ductal structures, while CT may demonstrate pancreatic calcifications, pseudocysts, or ductal dilation, yet directly identifies the fistula in only 33%–47
