Title: Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood

Hans Vaish, Vipin Vaish, Shalini Suri, Vasudha Tomar

Abstract:

Summary: An older boy presented with recurrent pleural effusions on the right side, accompanied by chest pain and difficulty breathing, which temporarily resolved following aspiration. The pleural fluid consistently exhibited a dark red coloration. Owing to multiple episodes of effusion, a right-sided intercostal drain was inserted. Further history identified recent abdominal trauma caused by impact with bicycle handlebars. Chest CT imaging revealed patchy consolidation on the right, hydropneumothorax, and a multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right paraesophageal area to the pancreatic tail. Laboratory analysis showed elevated amylase and lipase levels in both serum and pleural fluid samples. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography confirmed the presence of bilateral pancreatopleural fistulas, with a greater involvement on the right. The patient was managed by instituting nil per oral, initiating total parenteral nutrition, and administering Octreotide. Within 10 days, drainage lessened, the chest tube was withdrawn, and the patient was discharged.

Background:

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is a highly uncommon condition that typically manifests as repeated, large-volume, and sometimes blood-tinged pleural effusions. This complication may arise in the context of acute or chronic pancreatitis, as well as following pancreatic trauma. Clinically, PPF can pose a diagnostic challenge since patients often exhibit symptoms of respiratory compromise from persistent pleural effusions, while the underlying cause originates in the pancreas. This misdirection frequently leads to delayed diagnosis, as initial evaluations primarily focus on identifying intrathoracic abnormalities. To date, there have been only 47 reported cases of PPF in children, in contrast to more than 300 documented cases in the adult population.

Case presentation:

A child presented to the outpatient clinic with his mother, reporting one week of breathing difficulties and right-sided chest pain. According to his mother, these symptoms had progressively worsened over the past 7 days. There was no history of parental consanguinity. The mother noted that the child had been in contact with his grandfather, who had previously received appropriate treatment for pulmonary tuberculosis (Koch’s). Dietary assessment revealed the child frequently consumed processed, high-calorie foods, with minimal intake of fresh fruits, vegetables, and nutritionally balanced home-prepared meals. On assessment, his weight was 44 kg, height measured 112 cm, and body mass index (BMI) was 35. Examination found a respiratory rate of 20/min with mild tachypnea, decreased chest movement on the right, and stony dull percussion over the right subscapular, right infra-axillary, and axillary areas. Vocal fremitus was notably reduced on the right side, and breath sounds were diminished in the same regions. There were no signs of pallor, cyanosis, jaundice, clubbing, or lymphadenopathy. Abdominal examination revealed a soft abdomen without hepatosplenomegaly. The child was alert and oriented. Cardiovascular examination showed tachycardia without murmurs. His oxygen saturation on room air was 88–89%, with a pulse rate of 110/min. Due to persistent tachypnea and respiratory distress, he was transferred to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit (PICU) and received oxygen by mask at 5 L/min. A chest X-ray was performed, and empirical piperacillin-tazobactam was initiated after drawing blood cultures. Imaging revealed a right-sided pleural effusion with blunting of the right costophrenic angle (see figure 1; Table 2). Bedside lung ultrasound estimated the effusion at approximately one liter (see figure 2; Table 2). Right pleural fluid aspiration was undertaken under strict aseptic precautions, yielding about one liter of dark red fluid. Following thoracocentesis, tachypnea resolved and oxygen saturation improved to 95–98% on room air. Chest pain lessened and supplemental oxygen was discontinued within 24 hours. The child was transferred to the general ward the following day and later discharged. Aspirated pleural fluid was sent for analysis.

However, six days post-discharge, the child was readmitted with recurrence of right-sided chest pain, tachypnea, and respiratory difficulty. He was returned to the PICU, where chest X-ray again identified right-sided pleural effusion. Repeat thoracocentesis drained around one liter of similar dark red fluid. Pleural fluid adenosine deaminase (ADA) was mildly elevated (Table 1), and with the known history of tuberculosis exposure, empiric antitubercular therapy was commenced. Despite this, the child was hospitalized a third time, five days later, again for right-sided chest pain and dyspnea. On reassessment, the child disclosed a prior episode of blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle one month earlier. Contrast-enhanced CT of the chest was performed prior to placing an intercostal drainage (ICD) tube. The CT demonstrated patchy air-space opacities in the right lung with a right hydropneumothorax and an elongated, lobulated, multiloculated hypodense collection arising from the right pleural space, tracking from the right paraesophageal region down to the upper left retroperitoneum reaching the pancreatic tail (Table 2). An ICD was inserted, and approximately one liter of dark red pleural fluid was evacuated (see figure 3). Based on CT findings, the characteristic pleural aspirate, and the history of abdominal trauma, a pancreaticopleural fistula (PPF) was considered. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) was scheduled the same day, demonstrating pancreatitis with a tubular cystic fluid collection in the distal pancreatic body and tail that extended posteriorly in the retroperitoneum and superiorly through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity. There was a narrow extension to the left pleural cavity, and communication was noted with the distal pancreatic duct with proximal ductal narrowing. MRCP confirmed the diagnosis of PPF (see figures 4–6; Table 1). Amylase and lipase measurements were obtained from both serum and pleural fluid, which showed markedly elevated pleural amylase (6118 U/L) and lipase (7211 U/L) concentrations (Table 2), corroborating the diagnosis of PPF. Differential diagnosis:

The patient presented with pleural effusion accompanied by chest pain and had a clear history of abdominal trauma one month prior. Besides post-traumatic pancreatitis, all other etiologies for pleural effusion were excluded. The child had no previous episodes of fever, persistent cough, or weight loss. Although there was a positive family history of Koch’s contact and a slight elevation in pleural fluid ADA, GeneXpert for MTB yielded a negative result, thereby excluding pulmonary Koch’s. Absence of fever and lack of a neutrophilic exudate in the pleural fluid eliminated the possibility of empyema. Amylase-rich pleural fluid may also be found with malignancies such as lung cancer, leukemia, lymphoma, or metastatic tumors; however, normal hematological findings, absence of fever or weight loss, and a normal peripheral smear, as well as pleural fluid cytology showing no malignant cells, excluded malignancy. Boerhaave’s syndrome (esophageal perforation) can likewise lead to amylase-rich pleural fluid, but this was ruled out since the child’s bowel function was normal and there was no history of chest pain or vomiting. Acquired diaphragmatic hernia was excluded based on imaging, which showed no abdominal organs in the chest. Hepatic hydrothorax was not considered since both liver function tests and imaging were normal and the patient had no evidence or history of chronic liver disease. Finally, renal pathology, including hydronephrosis, was ruled out due to normal renal imaging and function tests.

Treatment:

In the PICU, the patient received humidified oxygen via mask for respiratory support. Two separate occasions of ultrasound-guided thoracocentesis were performed, during which approximately one litre of blood-tinged pleural fluid was extracted each time. Antitubercular therapy was initiated empirically, but discontinued once MRCP established the diagnosis. At the patient's third admission, a right-sided ICD was placed under strict aseptic technique, yielding another one litre of blood-stained pleural fluid collected in the ICD bag. Due to the rarity of PPF in paediatric patients and the absence of definitive management protocols, therapy was guided by reported cases. Conservative measures were chosen initially; the child was kept nil per os and commenced on Octreotide infusion at a dosage of 30 µg/kg/day (with a range of 10–40 µg/kg/day). Total parenteral nutrition and supplementation with pancreatic enzymes were also started. Over the course of 10 days, the volume of pleural fluid drained by the ICD progressively decreased and ultimately ceased. The ICD was subsequently removed, and the child was discharged from the hospital.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following hospital discharge, the child has not experienced any recurrence of symptoms. He attends scheduled monthly follow-up appointments with me. Since leaving the hospital, there have been no additional incidents of recurrent pleural effusions (S17). He has adopted recommended lifestyle modifications, including a balanced diet and consistent physical activity. The patient has resumed normal daily routines and is back to attending school on a regular basis. He is able to participate in vigorous activities such as running and playing without limitation.

Discussion:

PPF is a particularly uncommon complication arising from pancreatic disease in children. To date, only 47 cases of paediatric PPF have been recorded. The condition can occur secondary to both acute and chronic pancreatitis, or after paediatric trauma. The precise rate of occurrence in paediatric patients remains undefined; however, PPF is a complication in approximately 0.4% of pancreatitis cases overall. Mutations in serine peptidase inhibitor Kazal type 1 (SPINK1) and PRSS1 genes are notable contributors, accounting for as much as 30% of chronic pancreatitis among children. Additionally, blunt trauma to the abdomen is a significant non-genetic risk factor, making up 32% of cases in a 25-patient series reported by Yang et al. Idiopathic presentations persist, possibly reflecting overlooked genetic or ductal abnormalities. Pancreatic fluid may escape through a disrupted pancreatic duct or pseudocyst, traversing diaphragmatic hiatuses—typically the aortic or oesophageal hiatus—or less frequently via direct trans-diaphragmatic erosion, establishing a fistulous passage into the pleural cavity. Many of these cases are overlooked, as their initial presentation often includes thoracic symptoms, leading clinicians to concentrate investigations on pulmonary conditions rather than the underlying abdominal issue. A review of the literature identified 21 pertinent articles, which found that 68% of cases presented primarily with respiratory manifestations such as substantial, recurrent unilateral or bilateral pleural effusions, shortness of breath, and cough. Fewer than 20% exhibited abdominal complaints, including epigastric pain, vomiting, and weight loss. Constitutional symptoms like fever appeared in 24% of patients. The case documented by Zi Yin Shang in Frontiers in Paediatrics 2023 detailed an 8-year-old boy with PPF whose only symptom was a bulging left chest over two weeks—an exceedingly rare clinical presentation. In this particular scenario, pleural effusion seems to have gradually developed after abdominal injury. Preserved respiratory function on the uninjured side enabled adaptation, thus typical symptoms such as dyspnoea or cough were absent. Furthermore, the child was subjected to bullying, purposely hiding the trauma history. Diagnostic efforts initially focused on tuberculosis and cancer-related etiologies for hemorrhagic pleural effusion, but were redirected after further assessment. In our own case, the patient experienced persistent pulmonary manifestations—chest pain and recurring right-sided pleural effusions. Abdominal trauma was revealed only after repeated history-taking, and MRCP findings steered us toward a pancreatic disorder. This illustrates how PPF can masquerade as a pulmonary condition, potentially leading clinicians astray unless careful history and appropriate imaging are pursued. Fluid accumulation in PPF results from external trauma or infection, causing rupture of the pancreatic duct or pseudocyst and subsequent leakage of pancreatic fluid. If the rupture is on the anterior surface, fluid escapes into the peritoneal cavity, generating ascites; if posterior, fluid enters the retroperitoneal space, travels along the path of least resistance upward into the mediastinum via aortic or oesophageal hiatus, then breaches the pleura, resulting in pleural effusion. The internal fistula acts as a conduit for pancreatic secretions into the chest. In adult populations, chronic pancreatitis is mainly attributed to alcohol, and Uchiyama et al’s comprehensive Japanese review cited 113 cases—99% due to chronic alcohol-induced pancreatitis, with chest symptoms prevailing in 68%. Conversely, paediatric chronic pancreatitis more frequently arises from genetic abnormalities. Mutations in PRSS1, CFTR, SPINK1, and chymotrypsin C genes are well-established causes, particularly R122H and N291 mutations in PRSS1 exons 3 and 2. If paediatric pancreatitis recurs or is chronic, a thorough familial evaluation should be done to consider hereditary causes. Diagnostic delays are frequent, as most patients receive extensive pulmonary investigations before pancreatic pathology is suspected. Pleural fluid amylase levels ≥1000 IU/L are considered pathognomonic for PPF. Differential diagnoses including tuberculosis, oesophageal rupture, and lung or pancreatic neoplasms should also be considered, as these may elevate pleural fluid amylase. Pancreatic amylase in pleural fluid distinguishes PPF from these other conditions, which typically display salivary-type amylase. Therefore, pleural fluid amylase typing should be included in the workup for suspected PPF. MRCP visualizes the fistula and ductal anatomy with 70%–80% sensitivity. CT imaging is effective for detecting pancreatic calcifications, pseudocysts, or ductal dilation but reveals the actual fistula in only 33%–47% of cases.
