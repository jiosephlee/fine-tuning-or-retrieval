Title: Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood

Hans Vaish, Vipin Vaish, Shalini Suri, Vasudha Tomar

Abstract:

Summary: An older child developed recurrent right-sided pleural effusions accompanied by chest pain and difficulty breathing, with transient improvement following thoracentesis. The drained pleural fluid was consistently dark red. Due to ongoing effusions, a right-sided intercostal chest drain was inserted. On further history, the patient reported sustaining abdominal injury from impact with a bicycle handlebar. Chest computed tomography revealed areas of right pulmonary consolidation, hydropneumothorax, and a multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right paraesophageal area toward the tail of the pancreas. Laboratory analysis demonstrated increased amylase and lipase levels in both serum and pleural fluid. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography demonstrated bilateral pancreatopleural fistulas, more prominent on the right side. Management included fasting (nil per oral), provision of total parenteral nutrition, and administration of Octreotide. Over the course of 10 days, output from the drain declined, the chest tube was withdrawn, and the patient was discharged.

Background:

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) represents a highly uncommon condition characterized by repeated, large-volume pleural effusions that may contain blood. It has been documented in individuals with acute pancreatitis, chronic pancreatitis, as well as following pancreatic injury. The clinical presentation often involves respiratory distress secondary to recurring pleural effusions, which can complicate identification of the underlying pancreatic etiology and may result in delayed diagnosis due to focus on potential thoracic causes. In pediatric patients, only 47 cases of PPF have been documented, while adults account for over 300 reported cases.

Case presentation:

A young patient was brought to the outpatient clinic by his mother, presenting with a one-week history of breathing difficulties and right-sided chest pain. According to his mother, his symptoms had worsened over the past week. There was no reported history of parental consanguinity. The mother also provided a history of contact with a relative (the child's grandfather) who had previously been treated for pulmonary Koch's disease. Dietary assessment indicated a pattern of regular intake of processed, calorie-dense foods, with limited fresh produce and balanced meals. The child’s weight was 44 kg, height measured at 112 cm, yielding a BMI of 35. On physical examination, the patient had a respiratory rate of 20/min and exhibited mild tachypnea, along with decreased movement of the right side of the chest. Percussion produced a stony dull note over the right subscapular, infra-axillary, and axillary regions. Vocal fremitus was appreciably diminished on the right compared to the left. Breath sounds were reduced in the same right-sided regions. No clinical evidence of pallor, cyanosis, jaundice, digital clubbing, or lymphadenopathy was observed. Abdominal examination revealed a soft abdomen without hepatosplenomegaly. The patient was alert and fully oriented. Cardiovascular assessment demonstrated tachycardia, without audible murmurs. Oxygen saturation measured 88–89% on room air, with a heart rate of 110/min. Due to the presence of respiratory distress and tachypnea, the child was admitted to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit and administered oxygen via face mask at 5 l/min. A chest radiograph was obtained, and empirical antibiotic therapy with piperacillin-tazobactam was commenced following the collection of blood cultures. Imaging revealed a right-sided pleural effusion with obliteration of the right costophrenic angle (figure 1) (Table 2). Point-of-care lung ultrasound identified an estimated pleural effusion volume of about one litre (figure 2) (Table 2). Thoracentesis of the right pleural space was performed under aseptic conditions, yielding approximately one litre of dark red fluid. The patient's tachypnea abated post-procedure, and oxygen saturation on room air rose to 95–98%. There was notable improvement in chest pain, and supplemental oxygen was discontinued within 24 hours. The following day, he was transferred to the general ward and subsequently discharged. The aspirated pleural fluid was submitted for laboratory analysis.

However, six days after discharge, the patient was readmitted with recurrence of right-sided chest pain, tachypnea, and respiratory difficulty. He was transferred again to the PICU, and chest X-ray once more revealed a right-sided pleural effusion. Repeated thoracentesis evacuated roughly one litre of dark red pleural fluid. Pleural fluid analysis showed a mildly increased Adenosine Deaminase (ADA) level (Table 1). Given his known exposure to Koch's disease, empiric anti-tubercular therapy was initiated.

The patient was admitted for a third time five days later, presenting again with right-sided chest pain and respiratory distress. A more detailed history revealed that he had sustained blunt abdominal trauma to the abdomen from a bicycle handle one month prior. A contrast-enhanced chest CT scan was obtained before considering intercostal drainage (ICD) placement. The CT scan indicated patchy airspace consolidation in the right lung, right hydropneumothorax, and an elongated, lobulated, multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right pleural cavity and tracking from the right paraesophageal region to the upper left retroperitoneum, terminating near the pancreatic tail (Table 2). An ICD was placed and approximately one litre of dark red fluid was drained (figure 3). The combined imaging findings, appearance of the fluid, and trauma history pointed towards pancreaticopleural fistula (PPF) as a diagnosis. MRCP performed the same day demonstrated features of pancreatitis with a tubular cystic collection at the distal body and tail of pancreas, extending posteriorly into the retroperitoneum and tracking through the esophageal hiatus to the right pleural cavity; a narrow passage to the left pleural space was also noted. Communication with the distal pancreatic duct, accompanied by proximal ductal narrowing, was observed. MRCP findings were diagnostic for PPF (figures 4–6) (Table 1). Amylase and lipase measurements were obtained from both serum and pleural fluid. Pleural fluid analysis revealed markedly raised amylase (6118 U/L) and lipase (7211 U/L) levels (Table 2), further substantiating the PPF diagnosis. Differential diagnosis:

The patient arrived with pleural effusion and chest pain, accompanied by a documented abdominal injury from one month prior. Apart from post-traumatic pancreatitis, alternative etiologies for pleural effusion were excluded. This pediatric patient lacked a history of fever, persistent cough, or weight loss. Although there was familial exposure to tuberculosis and the pleural fluid ADA level was slightly raised, GeneXpert MTB testing was negative. Consequently, pulmonary tuberculosis was eliminated as a cause. There were no indications of fever or presence of neutrophilic exudate in pleural fluid, thereby excluding empyema. Malignancies—such as lung cancer, leukemia, lymphoma, or metastatic tumors—may also result in amylase-rich pleural fluid; however, the absence of fever, weight loss, and a normal blood profile and peripheral smear made malignancy unlikely. Pleural fluid cytology did not demonstrate malignant cells. Amylase-rich pleural fluid can also be seen in Boerhaave’s syndrome (esophageal perforation), but this diagnosis was excluded since the child had regular bowel movements and no associated chest pain or vomiting. Acquired diaphragmatic hernia was considered but ruled out due to absence of abdominal organs in the chest on imaging. Hepatic hydrothorax was not supported as liver function tests and imaging were within normal limits, and there was no prior history of chronic liver disease. Finally, renal causes, including hydronephrosis, were ruled out based on normal renal imaging and renal function tests.

Treatment:

In the PICU, the patient received humidified oxygen via mask for respiratory support. Thoracocentesis was performed twice under ultrasound guidance, with approximately one liter of blood-tinged pleural fluid extracted each time. Antitubercular therapy was initiated empirically, but discontinued upon confirmation of the diagnosis by MRCP. During the third hospitalization, a right-sided intercostal drain (ICD) was placed using strict aseptic technique, yielding another liter of blood-stained pleural fluid collected in the ICD reservoir. Due to the rarity of PPF in the pediatric population and the absence of established management protocols, treatment was informed by previously published case reports. Conservative management was pursued initially; the patient was kept nil per oral and commenced on Octreotide infusion at a dosage of 30 µg/kg/day (range 10–40 µg/kg/day). Total parenteral nutrition and pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy were also initiated. Over the course of ten days, the drainage from the ICD progressively decreased and ultimately ceased. The ICD was subsequently removed, and the patient was discharged.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following hospital discharge, the patient has remained asymptomatic. He attends monthly follow-up appointments with me. Since leaving the hospital, there have been no additional occurrences of recurrent pleural effusions (S17). He has made adjustments to his lifestyle, adhering to a balanced diet and engaging in regular physical activity as recommended. He has resumed his normal routine and is consistently attending school. He is able to participate in vigorous activities such as running and playing without any limitations.

Discussion:

Panpleuroperitoneal fistula (PPF) is a very rare complication associated with pancreatic disease in children. To date, only 47 cases have been documented within the paediatric population. PPF can arise in situations of acute or chronic pancreatitis, or following traumatic injury in children. Although its exact incidence in paediatric patients remains unestablished, PPF complicates approximately 0.4% of pancreatitis cases overall. Mutations in the serine peptidase inhibitor Kazal type 1 (SPINK1) and PRSS1 genes are significant contributors, responsible for as much as 30% of paediatric chronic pancreatitis. Non-genetic causes such as blunt trauma to the abdomen account for a substantial proportion, representing 32% in a cohort of 25 children assessed by Yang et al. Cases with no apparent cause (idiopathic) are still reported and may reflect undetected genetic or ductal abnormalities.

Pancreatic fluid can track from ruptured pancreatic ducts or pseudocysts through natural hiatuses in the diaphragm, most frequently the aortic or oesophageal hiatus, and less often via direct erosion across the diaphragm, thereby forming a fistula into the pleural space. Due to the predominance of thoracic symptoms at presentation, many cases are overlooked, with diagnostic efforts focused on respiratory pathologies when the underlying issue is abdominal. Literature review identified 21 pertinent cases, of which 68% displayed chiefly pulmonary manifestations, such as large, recurring unilateral or bilateral pleural effusions, dyspnoea, and coughing. Fewer than 20% reported abdominal complaints like epigastric pain, vomiting, or weight loss. Constitutional symptoms such as fever occurred in 24% of cases. A report by Zi Yin Shang (Frontiers in Paediatrics 2023) describes an 8-year-old boy with PPF who presented with a left-sided chest bulge over two weeks; initial symptomatology of chest bulging is uncommon in PPF. In this case, pleural effusion appears to have evolved after abdominal trauma. Adequate function of the unaffected lung permitted gradual adaptation, preventing typical respiratory symptoms like cough or shortness of breath. Further, the child withheld details about abdominal trauma due to school bullying. Investigation focused on tuberculosis and neoplastic conditions for haemorrhagic pleural effusion, but the diagnostic pathway shifted.

Our case also featured recurring pulmonary issues—chest pain and repeated right-sided pleural effusions. The history of abdominal trauma emerged after repeated patient interviews, and MRCP findings clarified the pancreatic origin. Thus, PPF may present with a ‘pulmonary mask’, potentially leading clinicians towards incorrect diagnoses unless thorough history and targeted imaging are pursued.

PPF arises when external trauma or infection prompts rupture of the pancreatic duct or a pseudocyst, with subsequent leakage of pancreatic fluid. Anterior ductal rupture leads to ascites as fluid leaks into the abdominal cavity. Posterior rupture causes pancreatic fluid to spill into the retroperitoneum, which may travel upward through the aortic or oesophageal hiatus to the mediastinum, ultimately breaking through the pleura and causing pleural effusion. An internal pancreatic fistula functions as a leak diverting pancreatic secretions into the thoracic cavity. In adults, chronic pancreatitis leading to PPF is most often attributable to alcohol; Uchiyama et al, in a comprehensive review of Japanese literature, identified 113 cases and found 99% were associated with chronic alcoholic pancreatitis and 68% presented with chest symptoms. Conversely, paediatric cases are more frequently genetic in origin. Genes such as PRSS1, CFTR, SPINK1, and chymotrypsin C have established roles in hereditary pancreatitis, most notably R122H and N291 mutations in PRSS1 exon 3 and exon 2, respectively. For recurrent or chronic paediatric pancreatitis, detailed family history and hereditary etiology must be considered.

Diagnosis is often delayed because most cases undergo extensive respiratory evaluation prior to suspecting pancreatic involvement. Pleural fluid amylase levels above 1000 IU/L are pathognomonic for PPF. However, other conditions including tuberculosis, oesophageal perforation, and malignancies of the lung or pancreas also manifest with elevated pleural fluid amylase. Differentiation relies on identifying pancreatic-type amylase within pleural fluid; salivary-type amylase occurs in other causes. Pleural fluid amylase typing may be useful in the diagnostic assessment of suspected PPF.

MRCP has a 70%–80% sensitivity for detecting fistulae and assessing ductal architecture. CT can reliably identify pancreatic calcifications, pseudocysts, and ductal dilation, but only visualizes the fistulous tract in 33%–47% of cases.
