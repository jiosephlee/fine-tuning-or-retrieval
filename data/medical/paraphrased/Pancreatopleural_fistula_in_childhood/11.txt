Title: Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood

Hans Vaish, Vipin Vaish, Shalini Suri, Vasudha Tomar

Abstract:

Summary: A male child in late childhood exhibited multiple episodes of right-sided pleural effusion, accompanied by chest pain and breathing difficulty, which showed transient improvement following aspiration. The pleural fluid repeatedly appeared dark red. Persistent effusions necessitated placement of a right intercostal drainage tube. Further history-taking uncovered recent abdominal injury caused by a bicycle handlebar. Chest CT revealed right-sided areas of consolidation, hydropneumothorax, and a multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right paraesophageal area to the tail of the pancreas. Elevated levels of amylase and lipase were found in both serum and pleural fluid samples. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography established the presence of bilateral pancreatopleural fistulas, with greater involvement on the right side. The patient was managed with cessation of oral intake, total parenteral nutrition, and Octreotide. Within 10 days, drainage lessened, the chest drain was withdrawn, and the patient was subsequently discharged.

Background:

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is a highly uncommon condition, typically manifesting as recurrent, large-volume, and occasionally hemorrhagic pleural effusions. It has been described in the context of acute or chronic pancreatitis as well as after pancreatic trauma. Due to its clinical presentation with recurrent respiratory symptoms stemming from pleural effusions, PPF is often initially mistaken for primary thoracic disease, thus potentially delaying recognition of its pancreatic origin. In the pediatric population, only 47 instances of PPF have been documented, whereas over 300 adult cases have been reported in the literature.

Case presentation:

A pediatric patient was brought to the outpatient clinic by his mother, presenting with a one-week history of breathing difficulty and right-sided chest pain. The mother reported that the respiratory discomfort had progressively worsened over the course of seven days. There was no consanguinity in the family. The mother also mentioned the child had close exposure to his grandfather, who had been treated for pulmonary tuberculosis (Koch’s disease). Dietary history revealed that the patient frequently consumed processed, high-calorie foods and rarely ate fresh fruits, vegetables, or balanced home-cooked meals. His weight measured 44 kg, height 112 cm, and body mass index calculated to 35.

Upon physical assessment, the child exhibited a respiratory rate of 20/min, consistent with mild tachypnea. There was diminished movement of the right hemithorax. Percussion demonstrated a stony dull note in the right subscapular, infra-axillary, and axillary regions. Palpation revealed markedly decreased vocal fremitus on the right as compared to the left. Auscultation showed reduced breath sounds over these same right-sided areas. No signs of pallor, cyanosis, jaundice, digital clubbing, or lymphadenopathy were present. The abdominal exam was unremarkable, with a soft abdomen and no enlargement of the liver or spleen. The child was alert and appropriately oriented. Cardiovascular examination revealed tachycardia in the absence of any heart murmurs. The patient’s oxygen saturation was 88%–89% in room air, and the heart rate was 110/min.

Owing to the tachypnea and respiratory distress, he was transferred to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit (PICU) and provided with oxygen via facemask at 5 l/min. A chest radiograph was obtained, and following collection of blood cultures, empirical therapy with piperacillin-tazobactam was initiated. Imaging identified a right-sided pleural effusion with obliteration of the right costophrenic angle (figure 1) (Table 2). Point-of-care lung ultrasound quantified the effusion at approximately one litre (figure 2) (Table 2). Thoracentesis of the right pleural space, performed under sterile conditions, yielded about one litre of dark red pleural fluid. Following drainage, the patient’s tachypnea improved, with oxygen saturation in room air rising to 95%–98%. His chest pain resolved and he was able to be weaned from oxygen within 24 hours. He was moved to the general ward the following day and subsequently discharged. The aspirated pleural fluid was sent for laboratory analysis.

Six days later, the child was readmitted with similar symptoms of right-sided chest pain, tachypnea, and breathlessness. He was again placed in the PICU, and chest X-ray again confirmed right-sided pleural effusion. Repeat thoracentesis resulted in drainage of about one litre of dark red pleural fluid. Pleural fluid adenosine deaminase (ADA) was mildly elevated (Table 1), and due to the prior contact with pulmonary tuberculosis, empirical anti-tubercular therapy was commenced.

Despite this, the child was hospitalized for a third time five days later with persistent right-sided chest pain and dyspnea. Further discussion with the child revealed a history of blunt trauma to the abdomen from a bicycle handle approximately a month prior. A contrast-enhanced chest CT scan was performed before placing an intercostal drain (ICD), which revealed patchy areas of consolidation in the right lung, right hydropneumothorax, and an elongated multiloculated hypodense fluid collection extending from the right pleural space, tracking from the right paraesophageal area down into the upper left retroperitoneum as far as the tail of the pancreas (Table 2). Placement of an ICD drained approximately one litre of dark red pleural fluid (figure 3). In conjunction with the CT findings, the color of the aspirated fluid, and the reported abdominal trauma, a diagnosis of pancreaticopleural fistula (PPF) was considered.

Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) was arranged the same day, which showed evidence of pancreatitis, with a tubular cystic collection in the distal body and tail of the pancreas, extending posteriorly in the retroperitoneum and ascending via the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity. There was also a small tract communicating with the left pleural space. The collection was in continuity with the distal pancreatic duct, and there was narrowing of the duct proximal to the communication. MRCP supported the diagnosis of PPF (figures 4-6) (Table 1). Laboratory analysis of pleural fluid and serum showed markedly raised amylase (6118 U/L) and lipase (7211 U/L) levels (Table 2), confirming PPF.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient exhibited pleural effusion and chest pain, with a clear history of abdominal trauma one month prior. Apart from post-traumatic pancreatitis, other etiologies for pleural effusion had been excluded. The child did not report any episodes of fever, persistent cough, or weight loss. Although there was a family history of contact with Koch’s and the pleural fluid ADA was mildly raised, GeneXpert testing for MTB was negative, effectively ruling out pulmonary Koch’s. Additionally, absence of fever and a lack of neutrophilic exudate in the pleural fluid excluded empyema. While malignancies such as pulmonary, leukemic, lymphomatous, or metastatic tumors may present with amylase-rich pleural fluid, malignancy was deemed unlikely given the absence of fever, lack of weight loss, a normal hematologic profile, and normal peripheral smear. Furthermore, pleural fluid cytology showed no malignant cells. Boerhaave’s syndrome (esophageal perforation) was also considered but excluded due to normal stooling patterns and no history of chest pain or vomiting. An acquired diaphragmatic hernia was ruled out because imaging did not reveal any abdominal organs within the thoracic cavity. Hepatic hydrothorax was excluded based on normal liver function tests and liver imaging, as well as no clinical indication of chronic liver disease. Normal findings on renal imaging and renal function testing dismissed any renal causes, including hydronephrosis.

Treatment:

The patient received humidified oxygen via face mask in the PICU. Thoracocentesis was performed twice under ultrasound guidance, yielding approximately one liter of hemorrhagic pleural fluid on each occasion. Antitubercular therapy was initiated empirically but discontinued once a definitive diagnosis was made based on MRCP findings. During a third hospitalization, an intercostal drainage (ICD) tube was placed on the right side under strict aseptic conditions, and once more about one liter of blood-tinged pleural fluid was collected in the ICD bag. As paediatric pancreaticopleural fistula (PPF) is rare and there are no established management protocols, therapeutic decisions were informed by case reports. The initial approach was conservative: the child was kept nil per os and commenced on an octreotide infusion at a dose of 30 µg/kg/day (range 10–40 µg/kg/day). Total parenteral nutrition and pancreatic enzyme supplementation were also provided. Over a span of 10 days, the output through the ICD progressively diminished and eventually ceased. The ICD was subsequently removed, and the patient was discharged.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following hospital discharge, the child has remained asymptomatic. He attends routine monthly follow-up appointments. Since leaving the hospital, there have not been any further instances of recurrent pleural effusions (S17). He has implemented lifestyle changes, adhering to a balanced diet and consistent exercise as recommended. The patient has resumed his usual daily routines and attends school consistently. He is able to engage in physically demanding activities, such as running and playing, without limitations.

Discussion:

Pancreaticopleural fistula (PPF) is an exceedingly uncommon complication arising from pancreatic disorders in children. To date, only 47 pediatric cases of PPF have been described. PPF may develop secondary to either acute or chronic pancreatitis, or as a consequence of pediatric abdominal trauma. The precise incidence in children remains undetermined, but PPF occurs in about 0.4% of overall pancreatitis cases. Notably, mutations in the serine peptidase inhibitor Kazal type 1 (SPINK1) and PRSS1 genes are implicated in up to 30% of pediatric chronic pancreatitis. In a cohort reviewed by Yang et al., blunt trauma accounted for 32% of cases, highlighting the importance of non-hereditary etiologies. Cases considered idiopathic continue to emerge and may reflect undetected genetic or ductal anomalies. 

Pathophysiologically, pancreatic secretions can pass from a ruptured pancreatic duct or pseudocyst via anatomical diaphragmatic hiatuses, most frequently the aortic or esophageal, or less commonly by direct trans-diaphragmatic erosion, establishing a fistulous tract into the pleural space. Often, these presentations are overlooked due to the prevalence of thoracic symptoms and focus on pulmonary etiologies, when the primary problem lies intra-abdominally. A review of the literature yielded 21 pertinent case reports, revealing that 68% of patients experienced predominantly respiratory symptoms, including extensive or recurrent unilateral or bilateral pleural effusions, dyspnea, and cough. Fewer than 20% of cases presented with abdominal complaints like epigastric pain, vomiting, or weight loss, while 24% exhibited constitutional features such as fever.

An 8-year-old boy described by Zi Yin Shang in *Frontiers in Paediatrics* (2023) presented only with a two-week history of left-sided chest bulging—a rare initial manifestation. In this situation, the pleural fluid likely developed gradually after abdominal trauma, with preserved function in the unaffected lung mitigating typical respiratory symptoms. The child, who concealed a trauma history due to bullying, underwent evaluation for tuberculosis and malignancy before an alternative diagnosis was reached. In the case under discussion, recurrent pulmonary symptoms manifested as chest pain and repeated right-sided pleural fluid collections. Only with repeated history-taking and subsequent MRCP imaging did the history of abdominal trauma and subsequent pancreatic etiology become apparent. This underscores how PPF can "masquerade" as a pulmonary illness, leading clinicians astray unless comprehensive history and targeted imaging are performed.

Fluid accumulation in PPF is generally the result of traumatic injury or infection, causing rupture of the pancreatic duct or pseudocyst and leakage of pancreatic secretions. If rupture involves the anterior pancreatic aspect, the result is intraperitoneal leakage and ascites. Posterior rupture leads to leakage into the retroperitoneum, with pancreatic fluid ascending into the mediastinum through the aortic or esophageal hiatus, then breaching the pleura to produce a pleural effusion. The resultant internal pancreatic fistula diverts pancreatic fluid into the thoracic cavity, similar to a leaking conduit.

In adults, chronic pancreatitis causing PPF is most often attributable to alcohol use, as noted in Uchiyama et al.’s comprehensive Japanese literature review of 113 cases—99% linked to alcoholic pancreatitis and thoracic symptoms in 68%. Conversely, pediatric chronic pancreatitis is mainly of genetic origin. Known causative mutations include the PRSS1, CFTR, SPINK1, and chymotrypsin C genes, notably PRSS1 exon 3 R122H and exon 2 N291. In children with recurrent or chronic pancreatitis, clinicians should pursue a detailed family history and consider hereditary causes.

Diagnosis is frequently delayed, as many patients initially undergo an exhaustive pulmonary evaluation before pancreatic disease is considered. Pleural fluid amylase exceeding 1000 IU/L is diagnostic for PPF. However, tuberculosis, esophageal perforation, and malignancy may also elevate pleural fluid amylase and must be excluded. Distinguishing features include the pancreatic isoenzyme type in PPF, whereas other diseases tend to cause raised salivary type amylase. Pleural fluid amylase isoenzyme analysis can aid in diagnosis.

Imaging modalities contribute variably: MRCP visualizes the fistulous tract and ductal anatomy with a sensitivity of 70%–80%; CT effectively identifies pancreatic calcifications, pseudocysts, or ductal dilatation, yet demonstrates the fistula in only 33%–47% of cases. Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) is the gold standard for both diagnosis and planning therapy. ERCP has enabled visualization of the fistula in 38%–78% of pediatric cases.
