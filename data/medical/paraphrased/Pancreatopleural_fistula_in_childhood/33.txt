Title: Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood

Hans Vaish, Vipin Vaish, Shalini Suri, Vasudha Tomar

Abstract:

A preadolescent boy presented with recurring pleural effusions on the right side, accompanied by chest pain and breathing difficulties that showed transient improvement following aspiration of the fluid. The pleural aspirate consistently appeared dark red in color. Owing to the frequent effusions, a right intercostal drain was inserted. Medical history included recent abdominal injury caused by a bicycle handlebar. Chest CT imaging demonstrated patchy consolidation in the right lung, the presence of a hydropneumothorax, and a multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right paraesophageal area to the pancreatic tail. Elevated levels of amylase and lipase were found in both blood and pleural fluid samples. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography identified bilateral pancreatopleural fistulas, with greater involvement on the right side than the left. Management included fasting (nil per os), total parenteral nutrition, and administration of Octreotide. Over a span of ten days, drainage diminished, allowing removal of the chest tube, and the patient was subsequently discharged.

Background:

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is a very uncommon condition characterized by repeated episodes of large, bloody pleural effusions. It has been documented in individuals with acute pancreatitis, chronic pancreatitis, or injury to the pancreas. PPF can be challenging to recognize because its clinical manifestation is dominated by symptoms of respiratory compromise resulting from recurrent pleural effusions, despite the underlying cause being pancreatic in origin. This frequently leads to a delay in diagnosis as initial investigations focus on finding a cause within the thoracic cavity. To date, only 47 pediatric cases of PPF have been documented, while over 300 adult cases have been reported.

Case presentation:

A child was brought to the outpatient clinic by his mother, presenting with complaints of difficulty breathing and right-sided chest pain persisting for one week. According to the mother, the symptoms had progressively worsened over the past seven days. There was no reported history of parental consanguinity. The mother did mention exposure to tuberculosis, as the child’s grandfather had previously received treatment for pulmonary Koch’s disease. Dietary history revealed the child frequently ate processed, high-calorie foods, but consumed few fresh fruits, vegetables, or nutritionally balanced home-cooked meals. He weighed 44 kg, had a height of 112 cm, and a body mass index of 35.

On clinical assessment, the child’s respiratory rate was 20 per minute, indicating mild tachypnoea. There was noticeable reduction in right chest wall movement. Percussion produced a stony dull note in the right subscapular, right infra-axillary, and axillary regions. Vocal fremitus on the right was significantly diminished compared to the left side. Breath sounds were decreased in the corresponding right-sided areas. There were no signs of pallor, cyanosis, jaundice, finger clubbing, or lymphadenopathy. The abdomen was soft and non-tender, with no evidence of hepatosplenomegaly. The patient was alert and oriented. Cardiovascular exam revealed tachycardia but no murmurs. Oxygen saturation on room air was measured at 88–89%, and heart rate was 110 beats per minute.

Given the tachypnoea and respiratory distress, the child was transferred to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit and commenced on oxygen therapy at 5 L/min via mask. A chest radiograph was performed and, after collecting blood cultures, empiric antibiotic therapy with piperacillin-tazobactam was started. Imaging revealed a right pleural effusion with blunting of the right costophrenic angle (figure 1) (Table 2). Bedside lung ultrasound estimated approximately one litre of pleural fluid (figure 2) (Table 2). Under sterile technique, right pleural aspiration was carried out, yielding about one litre of dark red fluid. The tachypnoea subsided post-thoracocentesis, and oxygen saturation improved to 95–98% on room air. The chest pain abated, and the child was weaned off supplemental oxygen within 24 hours, then transferred to the ward and subsequently discharged. The pleural fluid was sent for analysis.

Six days later, the child was readmitted with recurrence of right-sided chest pain, tachypnoea, and respiratory difficulty. He was again moved to the PICU, where chest X-ray again demonstrated a right pleural effusion. Thoracocentesis was repeated, retrieving roughly one litre of dark red pleural fluid. Pleural fluid adenosine deaminase (ADA) was modestly raised (Table 1), and given the positive tuberculosis exposure, empirical antitubercular therapy was initiated.

Despite this, a third admission occurred five days later for similar symptoms of right-sided chest pain and dyspnoea. Upon detailed history-taking, the child disclosed sustaining blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle a month earlier. Prior to inserting an intercostal drainage (ICD) tube, contrast-enhanced chest CT was obtained. The scan revealed patchy consolidation in the right lung, right hydropneumothorax, and an elongated, multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right pleural space, tracking from the right paraoesophageal area to the upper left retroperitoneum, reaching the tail of the pancreas (Table 2). An ICD was placed, draining approximately one litre of dark red fluid (figure 3). These CT findings, together with the dark red pleural fluid and history of abdominal trauma, suggested a pancreaticopleural fistula (PPF). Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) was performed the same day, showing features of pancreatitis and a tubular cystic collection in the distal pancreas and tail, extending posteriorly in the retroperitoneum and superiorly through the oesophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity. There was also a narrow tract communicating with the left pleural space. The collection communicated with the distal pancreatic duct, which was narrowed proximally. MRCP confirmed the suspicion of PPF (figures 4-6) (Table 1). Serum and pleural fluid samples were sent for amylase and lipase. Analysis revealed highly elevated pleural fluid amylase (6118 U/L) and lipase (7211 U/L) levels (Table 2), supporting the diagnosis of PPF.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient arrived with pleural effusion and chest pain, having sustained abdominal trauma one month prior. We excluded all etiologies of pleural effusion except for post-traumatic pancreatitis. The child denied symptoms such as fever, persistent cough, or weight loss. Although there was a family history of Koch’s contact and pleural fluid ADA was slightly raised, GeneXpert testing for MTB yielded negative results, eliminating pulmonary Koch’s from consideration. Empyema was discounted due to lack of fever and the absence of neutrophilic exudate in pleural fluid. Malignancies—including lung cancer, leukemia, lymphoma, and metastases—may present with elevated pleural fluid amylase; however, the absence of fever, weight loss, a normal hematological profile, and a normal peripheral smear argued against malignancy. Pleural fluid cytology did not identify any malignant cells. Boerhaave’s syndrome (esophageal rupture), which is associated with amylase-rich pleural fluid, was ruled out since the child had normal bowel movements and lacked symptoms such as vomiting or chest pain. Acquired diaphragmatic hernia was excluded as imaging did not show abdominal organs within the thoracic cavity. Hepatic hydrothorax was dismissed due to normal liver function tests, normal hepatic imaging, and absence of chronic liver disease in the child’s history. Finally, renal disease or hydronephrosis was eliminated as both renal imaging and function were unremarkable.

Treatment:

In the PICU, the patient received respiratory support with humidified oxygen delivered via mask. Thoracocentesis guided by ultrasound was performed twice, yielding approximately one liter of blood-tinged pleural fluid on each occasion. Empirical antitubercular therapy was initiated but discontinued following confirmation of the diagnosis via MRCP. Upon the third hospital admission, a right-sided intercostal drain (ICD) was placed under sterile conditions, draining another liter of blood-stained pleural fluid into the collection bag. Since pleuro-pancreatic fistula (PPF) is a rare and unusual condition in children and lacks established treatment protocols, management was informed by case reports. A conservative approach was employed; the patient was made nil per oral and commenced on Octreotide infusion at a dosage of 30 µg/kg/day (range: 10–40 µg/kg/day). Additionally, total parenteral nutrition and supplementation with pancreatic enzymes were initiated. Over the course of 10 days, the volume of fluid draining from the ICD progressively diminished until it ceased completely. The ICD was subsequently removed, and the patient was discharged from hospital care.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following his discharge from the hospital, the child has not experienced any further symptoms. He attends scheduled monthly follow-up appointments with me. Since leaving the hospital, there have been no additional instances of recurrent pleural effusions (S17). He has adopted recommended lifestyle changes, including maintaining a balanced diet and participating in regular physical activity. The patient has resumed normal daily routines and is consistently going to school. He is able to engage in vigorous activities, such as running and playing, without any issues.

Discussion:

Panpleural fistula (PPF) represents an exceptionally uncommon complication of pancreatic disease in children, with only 47 reported cases in the paediatric demographic to date. It may occur in the setting of either acute or chronic pancreatitis, or as a result of childhood trauma. Although the exact frequency in paediatric cases remains undetermined, PPF is implicated in approximately 0.4% of pancreatitis cases overall. Genetic abnormalities such as serine peptidase inhibitor Kazal type 1 (SPINK1) and PRSS1 mutations are found in up to 30% of paediatric chronic pancreatitis cases. Blunt abdominal trauma is another notable non-genetic factor, accounting for 32% within a cohort of 25 patients analysed by Yang et al. Some cases appear to be idiopathic, likely reflecting unrecognised genetic or ductal variants.

The pathophysiology involves pancreatic fluid escaping from a ruptured pancreatic duct or pseudocyst via natural diaphragmatic openings—most often the aortic or oesophageal hiatus—or by direct erosion through the diaphragm, thereby establishing a fistulous route to the pleural space. Many cases are overlooked because the initial presentation is commonly thoracic, leading investigations to focus on pulmonary disorders, despite the underlying abdominal pathology.

A review of the literature yielded 21 pertinent publications; 68% of cases featured predominant respiratory manifestations such as sizable, recurrent unilateral or bilateral pleural effusions, dyspnoea, and cough. Fewer than 20% exhibited abdominal complaints like epigastric discomfort, vomiting, or weight loss, while constitutional symptoms including fever occurred in 24%. An example is described by Zi Yin Shang in Frontiers in Paediatrics (2023): an 8-year-old boy presented solely with a protruding left chest for two weeks. Such presentations with chest bulging as the initial symptom are exceedingly rare. In this instance, the pleural fluid likely accumulated over time following abdominal trauma; compensation by the non-affected lung enabled gradual adjustment, obscuring classic symptoms such as cough or dyspnoea. Additionally, the child concealed the history of trauma due to school bullying, delaying recognition of the abdominal insult. Diagnostic work-up initially focused on tuberculosis and malignancy because of the haemorrhagic pleural effusion but ultimately diverged when a pancreatic etiology was identified.

Similarly, in our case, the child experienced repeated pulmonary symptoms, presenting with chest pain and recurrent right-sided pleural collections. The abdominal trauma history emerged only after multiple history-taking sessions, and MRCP findings confirmed a pancreatic source. This illustrates how PPF can masquerade as a “pulmonary” disorder and mislead clinicians without meticulous history and targeted imaging.

The accumulation of fluid in PPF results from external trauma or bacterial infection causing rupture of the pancreatic duct or pseudocyst, allowing pancreatic fluid to leak. Rupture on the anterior aspect of the pancreas leads to fluid entering the peritoneal cavity, producing ascites. Posterior rupture allows pancreatic fluid to escape into the retroperitoneal space, tracking cephalad through the aortic or oesophageal hiatus, and passing through the pleura to generate a pleural effusion, thus creating an internal pancreatic fistula that diverts pancreatic secretions to the thorax.

In adult populations, alcohol is the predominant cause of chronic pancreatitis leading to PPF. The comprehensive review by Uchiyama et al included 113 cases from Japan, finding that 99% were associated with chronic alcoholic pancreatitis, and chest symptoms prevailed in 68% of patients. By contrast, genetic factors are the primary cause of chronic pancreatitis in children. Well-documented pathogenic mutations involve the PRSS1 gene, CFTR gene, SPINK1 gene, and chymotrypsin C gene. PRSS1 mutations at exon 3 (R122H) and exon 2 (N291) are particularly representative. When paediatric pancreatitis is recurrent or chronic, comprehensive family history and consideration of hereditary etiologies are necessary.

The diagnostic process is frequently prolonged since pulmonary pathology is usually pursued first, rather than recognising the pancreatic origin. An amylase concentration of ≥1000 IU/L in pleural fluid is diagnostic for PPF. Conditions such as tuberculosis, esophageal perforation, and malignancies of the lung or pancreas should be excluded, as elevated pleural fluid amylase may also be found in these diseases. Importantly, amylase isoform analysis distinguishes pancreatic-type amylase (seen in PPF) from salivary-type amylase (found in other conditions), so pleural fluid amylase typing is valuable for diagnosis.

MRCP provides 70%–80% sensitivity for visualising the fistula and ductal architecture, while CT is useful for detecting calcifications, pseudocysts, or duct dilation, but directly visualises the fistula in just 33%–47% of cases.
