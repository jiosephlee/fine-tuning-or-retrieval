Title: Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood

Hans Vaish, Vipin Vaish, Shalini Suri, Vasudha Tomar

Abstract:

A preadolescent male developed recurrent pleural effusions on the right side accompanied by chest discomfort and difficulty breathing, which temporarily subsided following thoracentesis. The drained pleural fluid consistently appeared dark red. Because of ongoing effusions, a right intercostal drain was inserted. Further history indicated he had recently suffered abdominal injury from impact with a bicycle handlebar. Chest CT imaging revealed patchy consolidation on the right, a hydropneumothorax, and a multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right paraesophageal area toward the pancreatic tail. Both serum and pleural fluid amylase and lipase levels were elevated. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography established the presence of pancreatopleural fistulas bilaterally, with greater involvement on the right. Management included keeping the patient nil per oral, providing total parenteral nutrition, and administering Octreotide. After 10 days, drainage diminished, the chest tube was withdrawn, and the boy was discharged.

Background:

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is an exceedingly uncommon condition characterized by repeated, large-volume, and occasionally hemorrhagic pleural effusions. This complication may arise in the context of acute or chronic pancreatitis, as well as following pancreatic injury. The diagnosis may be challenging because clinical presentation is often dominated by respiratory symptoms from ongoing pleural effusions, despite the underlying issue being pancreatic in origin. As a result, the evaluation frequently focuses on identifying thoracic disease, which can postpone recognition of the true cause. To date, only 47 instances of PPF have been documented in children, compared to over 300 reported cases among adults.

Case presentation:

A child presented to my outpatient clinic, accompanied by his mother, with a history of breathing difficulties and right-sided chest pain persisting for one week. According to the mother, the severity of respiratory distress had gradually worsened over the past seven days. There was no background of consanguinity. The mother reported exposure to Koch’s, as the child’s grandfather had pulmonary Koch’s and received appropriate treatment. The dietary history revealed a preference for processed, high-calorie foods, with infrequent consumption of fresh fruits, vegetables, or nutritionally balanced home-cooked meals. The child’s weight was 44 kg, his height 112 cm, yielding a body mass index of 35.

Physical examination showed a respiratory rate of 20/min with mild tachypnoea. Chest expansion was reduced on the right. Percussion over the right subscapular, infra-axillary, and axillary areas produced a stony dull note. Vocal fremitus was markedly diminished on the right compared to the left. Breath sounds were decreased over the right subscapular, infra-axillary, and axillary regions. No pallor, cyanosis, icterus, clubbing, or lymphadenopathy was observed. The abdomen was soft and non-tender, with no evidence of hepatosplenomegaly. The child was alert and oriented to time, place, and person. The cardiovascular assessment indicated tachycardia without murmurs. Room air oxygen saturation measured 88%–89%, and heart rate was 110/min.

Due to tachypnoea and respiratory distress, the child was admitted to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit and started on oxygen at 5 l/min via mask. A chest X-ray was performed and empirical piperacillin-tazobactam antibiotics initiated after blood cultures were obtained. Chest X-ray demonstrated a right-sided pleural effusion, with blunting of the right costophrenic angle (figure 1, Table 2). Bedside lung ultrasound identified approximately one litre of pleural fluid (figure 2, Table 2). Under aseptic technique, a right-sided thoracocentesis was performed, yielding about one litre of dark red pleural fluid. Following thoracocentesis, tachypnoea resolved and oxygen saturation improved to 95%–98% on room air. The child’s chest pain lessened and he no longer required oxygen within 24 hours. He was subsequently transferred to the ward the next day and discharged. The aspirated pleural fluid was sent for laboratory analysis.

Six days later, the child was re-admitted with recurring symptoms of right-sided chest pain, tachypnoea, and respiratory difficulty. He was moved back to the PICU, where repeat chest X-ray again showed a right-sided pleural effusion. Another thoracocentesis was performed, again aspirating about one litre of dark red pleural fluid. Pleural fluid adenosine deaminase (ADA) was mildly elevated (Table 1) and, considering the positive history of Koch’s contact, empirical antitubercular treatment was started.

The child was hospitalized again for a third episode after five days, presenting with similar complaints of right-sided chest pain and breathing difficulty. Upon re-evaluation, the child disclosed having sustained blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle a month prior. Before insertion of an intercostal drainage (ICD), a contrast-enhanced CT scan of the chest was conducted, revealing patchy airspace consolidation in the right lung, right-sided hydropneumothorax, and a lobulated multiloculated hypodense collection originating in the right pleural space, tracking from the right paraoesophageal level to the upper left retroperitoneum as far as the pancreatic tail (Table 2). An ICD was placed, draining around one litre of dark red pleural fluid (figure 3). Based on CT findings, the color of the aspirated fluid, and the history of abdominal trauma, a diagnosis of pancreaticopleural fistula (PPF) was considered. The same day, MRCP was performed, showing pancreatitis with a tubular cystic collection in the distal body and tail of the pancreas, extending posteriorly in the retroperitoneum and superiorly through the oesophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity, with a narrow channel leading to the left pleural cavity. The collection was communicating with the distal pancreatic duct, with narrowing proximal to the point of communication. MRCP findings suggested a PPF (figures 4–6, Table 1).

Differential diagnosis:

The patient arrived with pleural effusion and chest pain, following a documented abdominal injury one month prior. Aside from post-traumatic pancreatitis, alternative etiologies for pleural effusion were excluded. This child lacked any preceding fever, prolonged cough, or unintended weight loss. While there was a family history of exposure to Koch’s, and pleural fluid ADA was moderately raised, the GeneXpert assay for MTB returned negative, effectively ruling out pulmonary Koch’s. Additionally, the absence of fever and a lack of neutrophilic exudate in the pleural fluid made empyema unlikely. Certain malignancies—such as those originating in the lung, leukaemia, lymphoma, or metastatic tumors—can present with pleural fluid rich in amylase; nevertheless, the lack of systemic symptoms (fever, weight loss), normal hematological profile, and unremarkable peripheral smear excluded malignant causes. Cytological evaluation of the pleural fluid was also negative for malignancy. Boerhaave’s syndrome (esophageal rupture) is another cause of elevated amylase in pleural fluid, but this was discounted since the child had normal bowel movements and no history of vomiting or chest pain. Acquired diaphragmatic hernia was ruled out by imaging, as no abdominal organs were seen within the thoracic cavity. Hepatic hydrothorax was excluded due to normal liver imaging and function tests, with no indication of chronic liver disease in history. Normal renal function and imaging excluded renal disease or hydronephrosis.

Treatment:

In the pediatric intensive care unit (PICU), respiratory support was administered using humidified oxygen delivered via mask. The patient underwent two sessions of thoracocentesis, each guided by ultrasound, during which approximately one liter of pleural fluid with blood was removed each time. Antitubercular therapy was initiated empirically but discontinued once magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) established the diagnosis. During the third hospitalization, an intercostal drain (ICD) was placed under strict aseptic technique on the right side, resulting in the drainage of about one liter of blood-tinged pleural fluid into the ICD collection bag. Since pancreatic pleural fistula (PPF) is an uncommon diagnosis in pediatric patients and lacks standardized management protocols, treatment was guided by previously reported cases. Conservative therapy was pursued initially: the child was kept nil by mouth and started on Octreotide infusion at a dose of 30 µg/kg/day (within the range of 10–40 µg/kg/day). Total parenteral nutrition was provided along with pancreatic enzyme supplementation. Over the course of 10 days, the volume of fluid draining from the ICD progressively decreased and eventually ceased. The ICD was subsequently removed, and the patient was discharged from the hospital.

Outcome and follow-up:

Since leaving the hospital, the patient has remained without symptoms. He attends scheduled monthly follow-up appointments. Since discharge, there have been no additional instances of pleural effusion (S17). Following recommendations, he has adopted a balanced diet and engages in routine physical activity. He has resumed his normal routines and is back to attending school consistently. He is able to participate in demanding physical activities, including running and playing, without limitation.

Discussion:

PPF is a particularly rare complication arising from pancreatic disease in children. To date, only 47 paediatric cases of PPF have been reported in the literature. The condition may be encountered in both acute and chronic pancreatitis or following trauma. The actual incidence among paediatric patients is not well established, although PPF is known to complicate approximately 0.4% of overall pancreatitis cases. Genetic mutations, notably serine peptidase inhibitor Kazal type 1 (SPINK1) and PRSS1, are implicated in nearly 30% of paediatric chronic pancreatitis cases. Non-genetic etiologies, such as blunt abdominal trauma, also play a substantial role—accounting for 32% in a 25-patient group reviewed by Yang et al. Some cases are idiopathic, which could reflect undetected genetic or ductal abnormalities. 

Pancreatic fluid may escape from a disrupted duct or pseudocyst, tracking into the thoracic cavity via anatomical pathways through diaphragmatic hiatuses, most frequently the aortic or oesophageal, or less often by direct diaphragmatic erosion, resulting in a fistulous link to the pleura. Because presentations are mainly thoracic, diagnosis is often delayed while clinicians pursue investigations focused on pulmonary disease, overlooking the abdominal source. Among the 21 relevant publications found, respiratory symptoms predominated in 68% of cases—typically presenting as massive, recurrent unilateral or bilateral pleural effusions, dyspnoea, and cough—while fewer than 20% exhibited gastrointestinal complaints, such as epigastric pain, vomiting, or weight loss. Constitutional symptoms like fever appeared in 24%. Zi Yin Shang's 2023 report in Frontiers in Paediatrics described an 8-year-old with PPF presenting solely with a bulging left chest over two weeks—a presentation seldom seen. In this instance, pleural effusion likely evolved after abdominal trauma. Compensation by the healthy lung enabled gradual adaptation, concealing typical symptoms like cough or shortness of breath. The child, experiencing bullying, intentionally withheld a history of trauma. Initial work-up for haemorrhagic pleural effusion focused on tuberculosis and malignancy, but the diagnosis ultimately shifted. Similarly, our patient developed recurrent chest pain and right-sided pleural effusions, with repeated history-taking eventually uncovering abdominal trauma, and MRCP findings indicating a pancreatic origin. These cases illustrate how PPF may "masquerade" as a pulmonary disorder, misleading physicians until detailed history and appropriate imaging reveal its true nature.

Fluid accumulation in PPF results from trauma or infection, causing rupture of the pancreatic duct or pseudocyst and leakage of pancreatic fluid. If rupture occurs anteriorly, fluid escapes into the abdominal cavity, causing ascites. Posterior ruptures allow leakage into the retroperitoneum, where fluid travels upward through the diaphragmatic hiatuses and enters the mediastinum, eventually passing through the pleura and forming an effusion. An internal fistula thus reroutes pancreatic secretions into the thoracic space. In adults, chronic pancreatitis due to alcohol is the primary cause of PPF. Uchiyama et al’s extensive review in Japan documented 113 cases, with 99% attributed to chronic alcoholic pancreatitis, and chest symptoms noted in 68%. Conversely, hereditary factors predominate in paediatric chronic pancreatitis, involving mutations in genes such as PRSS1, CFTR, SPINK1, and chymotrypsin C; key PRSS1 mutations include R122H and N291 within exon 3 and exon 2, respectively. When paediatric pancreatitis is recurrent or chronic, family history should be carefully evaluated for potential hereditary pancreatitis.

The diagnosis is often delayed as pulmonary workup precedes consideration of a pancreatic cause. Pleural fluid amylase ≥1000 IU/L is diagnostic for PPF; nevertheless, tuberculosis, oesophageal rupture, and malignancies can also elevate amylase. Pancreatic-type amylase differentiates PPF, as opposed to the salivary-type seen in other conditions. Amylase isoenzyme analysis in pleural fluid is a helpful adjunct in the diagnostic process. MRCP yields 70%–80% sensitivity for demonstrating fistulous tracts and ductal anatomy. CT is effective in identifying pancreatic calcifications, pseudocysts, or ductal dilation but shows the fistula itself in only 33%–47% of cases. ERCP remains the definitive method for therapeutic planning, with fistula detection rates in children ranging from 38% to 78%.

Management of PPF consists of medical, endoscopic, and surgical options. Because of its rarity, treatment protocols are not standardized.
