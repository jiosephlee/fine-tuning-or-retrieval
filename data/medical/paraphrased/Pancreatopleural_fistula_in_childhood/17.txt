Title: Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood

Hans Vaish, Vipin Vaish, Shalini Suri, Vasudha Tomar

Abstract:

Summary: A preadolescent male presented with recurrent right-sided pleural effusions accompanied by chest pain and episodes of respiratory distress, which transiently resolved following thoracentesis. The pleural aspirate repeatedly appeared dark red in color. Given the persistence of effusions, a right-sided intercostal chest drain was inserted. Further history revealed recent blunt abdominal trauma caused by a bicycle handlebar. Computed tomography of the thorax demonstrated right-sided patchy pulmonary consolidation, a hydropneumothorax, and a multiloculated, low-density fluid collection extending from the right paraesophageal area to the pancreatic tail. Laboratory evaluation showed increased amylase and lipase levels in both the blood and pleural effusion. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography identified bilateral pancreatopleural fistulae, more pronounced on the right. Management consisted of keeping the patient nil per oral, administering total parenteral nutrition, and treatment with Octreotide. Within 10 days, the drainage subsided, permitting chest tube removal, and the patient was subsequently discharged.

Background:

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) represents a highly uncommon condition, typically manifesting as recurrent, large, and occasionally hemorrhagic pleural effusions. It has been documented in settings such as acute or chronic pancreatitis as well as pancreatic trauma. The diagnosis can be challenging because patients often present with respiratory symptoms related to repeated pleural fluid accumulation, diverting attention to potential thoracic etiologies, while the underlying issue resides in the pancreas. To date, there have been only 47 reported cases of PPF in children, in contrast to over 300 cases described in adults.

Case presentation:

A pediatric patient presented to the outpatient clinic accompanied by his mother, reporting a one-week history of respiratory difficulty and right-sided chest pain. The mother noted a progressive increase in symptoms over the past week. There was no report of parental consanguinity. The mother also disclosed that the child had been in contact with his grandfather, who had been treated for pulmonary Koch's. The child's nutritional history was characterized by regular intake of processed, calorie-dense foods with minimal consumption of fresh fruits, vegetables, and balanced home-prepared meals. His recorded weight was 44 kg, height 112 cm, resulting in a BMI of 35.

Upon assessment, the child demonstrated a respiratory rate of 20/min and exhibited mild tachypnea. Diminished movement of the right chest was observed. Percussion elicited a stony dull note over the right subscapular, right infra-axillary, and axillary regions. Vocal fremitus was considerably decreased on the right when compared to the left. Breath sounds were likewise reduced in these right-sided regions. There were no findings of pallor, cyanosis, jaundice, clubbing, or lymphadenopathy. The abdomen was soft, without evidence of hepatosplenomegaly. The child was alert and appropriately oriented. Cardiac examination revealed tachycardia with no murmurs. Oxygen saturation ranged between 88%–89% on ambient air, with a pulse rate of 110/min. In light of ongoing tachypnea and respiratory distress, transfer to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit was effected, and supplemental oxygen via mask at 5 l/min was administered. A chest radiograph was ordered, and empiric therapy with piperacillin-tazobactam was initiated following blood culture sampling. Imaging showed a right-sided pleural effusion and blunting of the right costophrenic angle (figure 1) (Table 2). Point-of-care lung ultrasound estimated the effusion at approximately one litre (figure 2) (Table 2). Right-sided thoracentesis was performed under sterile conditions, and roughly one litre of dark red pleural fluid was evacuated. The child’s tachypnea resolved post-procedure and oxygen saturation on room air improved to 95%–98%. Chest pain diminished and oxygen support was discontinued within 24 hours. The following day, transfer to the general ward occurred and subsequently the child was discharged. Pleural fluid samples were submitted for analysis.

Six days later, the child was readmitted presenting again with right-sided chest pain, tachypnea, and respiratory difficulty. Repeat transfer to the PICU occurred, and chest X-ray again demonstrated right-sided pleural effusion. Repeat thoracentesis removed about one litre of dark red fluid. Pleural fluid adenosine deaminase (ADA) was slightly raised (Table 1), and given a positive contact history for Koch’s, empiric antitubercular treatment was commenced. The child was hospitalized a third time, five days thereafter, presenting with recurrent right-sided chest pain and respiratory distress. Upon further history, the patient revealed having sustained blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle approximately one month prior. Prior to intercostal drain (ICD) placement, a contrast-enhanced chest CT was requested. CT imaging disclosed patchy areas of consolidation within the right lung, right-sided hydropneumothorax, and an elongated, multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right pleural cavity, tracking from the right paraesophageal area to the upper left retroperitoneal space, reaching the tail of the pancreas (Table 2). An ICD was inserted, and around one litre of dark red pleural fluid was collected (figure 3).

In the context of CT findings, characteristic fluid appearance, and trauma history, a pancreaticopleural fistula (PPF) was suspected. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) performed the same day revealed pancreatitis and a tubular cystic collection at the distal pancreatic body and tail, which extended into the retroperitoneum and tracked superiorly through the esophageal hiatus to the right pleural space. There was also a narrow connection to the left pleural cavity and communication between the collection and the distal pancreatic duct, with associated narrowing of the proximal duct. These MRCP findings supported a diagnosis of PPF (figures 4-6) (Table 1). Serum and pleural fluid amylase and lipase were tested. Pleural analysis showed substantially elevated amylase (6118 U/L) and lipase (7211 U/L) (Table 2), affirming the PPF diagnosis.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient arrived with pleural effusion and chest pain, along with a confirmed episode of abdominal trauma one month prior. Aside from post-traumatic pancreatitis, alternative etiologies for pleural effusion had been excluded. The child’s history lacked any episodes of fever, persistent cough, or weight loss. While there was a family history of Koch’s contact and the pleural fluid ADA was slightly raised, GeneXpert testing for MTB returned negative, thereby excluding pulmonary Koch’s disease. Absence of fever and the lack of neutrophilic exudate in the pleural fluid made empyema unlikely. Malignancies—including lung cancer, leukemia, lymphoma, or metastatic tumors—can also manifest with amylase-rich pleural effusions. However, in this case, the absence of fever, lack of weight loss, normal hematologic results, and unremarkable peripheral smear effectively excluded a malignant process. Furthermore, pleural fluid cytology did not demonstrate malignant cells. Boerhaave’s syndrome (esophageal perforation) can result in amylase-rich effusions as well, but this was excluded as the patient reported normal stool passage, and had no history of vomiting or chest pain. Acquired diaphragmatic hernia was dismissed based on imaging that showed no abdominal viscera within the thoracic cavity. Hepatic hydrothorax was excluded given normal liver function tests, normal liver imaging, and the absence of chronic liver disease. Lastly, renal etiologies, including hydronephrosis, were ruled out owing to normal renal imaging and kidney function results.

Treatment:

In the pediatric intensive care unit, the patient received humidified oxygen via face mask for respiratory support. Ultrasound-guided thoracocentesis was performed twice, yielding approximately one liter of hemorrhagic pleural fluid with each procedure. Empirical antitubercular therapy was initiated but discontinued once MRCP confirmed the diagnosis. Upon a third hospitalization, an intercostal chest drain (ICD) was inserted on the right side under full aseptic technique, with another liter of blood-tinged pleural fluid evacuated into the ICD collection bag. Due to the rarity of PPF in children and the absence of established management protocols, treatment was guided by published case reports. Initial management was conservative: the patient was made nil per os and commenced on a continuous infusion of Octreotide at 30 µg/kg/day (within a range of 10–40 µg/kg/day). Additionally, total parenteral nutrition and supplementation with pancreatic enzymes were provided. Over a period of 10 days, the output from the ICD progressively decreased until cessation. The ICD was then removed, and the patient was subsequently discharged.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following hospital discharge, the patient has remained asymptomatic. He continues to attend monthly follow-up appointments with me. No further episodes of recurrent pleural effusion have been observed since his discharge (S17). The patient has implemented lifestyle changes, maintaining a balanced diet and engaging in regular physical activity as recommended. He has resumed his normal routines, attends school consistently, and is able to participate in vigorous activities such as running and playing without limitation.

Discussion:

PPF represents a highly uncommon and sporadic complication arising from pancreatic pathology within the pediatric population. To date, there have only been 47 recorded pediatric cases of PPF. This condition may manifest in the context of either acute or chronic pancreatitis or following trauma in children. The precise incidence in the pediatric population remains undetermined, although PPF complicates approximately 0.4% of all pancreatitis cases. Mutations in the serine peptidase inhibitor Kazal type 1 (SPINK1) and PRSS1 genes are found frequently, responsible for up to 30% of childhood chronic pancreatitis cases. Non-genetic factors, such as blunt abdominal trauma, are also significant, comprising 32% of a 25-patient cohort in the analysis by Yang et al. Idiopathic presentations persist, which may be attributed to underrecognized genetic anomalies or ductal malformations. Pancreatic secretions can escape from a disrupted duct or pseudocyst, following natural diaphragmatic openings—most often the aortic or esophageal hiatus—or, less commonly, via direct diaphragmatic erosion to form a communication with the pleural cavity. Frequently, these presentations are overlooked, as thoracic symptoms predominate and clinical evaluation often focuses on pulmonary etiologies, while the underlying pathology is abdominal. Review of the literature yielded 21 pertinent articles, showing that 68% of pediatric PPF cases presented primarily with respiratory complaints such as large, recurrent unilateral or bilateral pleural effusions, dyspnea, and cough. Fewer than one-fifth of the cases had abdominal complaints, including epigastric pain, vomiting, or weight loss, and about 24% had systemic symptoms like fever. A case by Zi Yin Shang in *Frontiers in Pediatrics* (2023) detailed PPF in an 8-year-old boy whose only sign was a left-sided chest bulge over two weeks, highlighting the rarity of bulging chest as the presenting symptom. In this example, pleural effusion apparently developed gradually post-abdominal trauma, and intact function of the opposite lung allowed the boy to accommodate slowly, resulting in an absence of classical respiratory symptoms such as cough or breathlessness. Additionally, the child, facing bullying at school, withheld the history of the initial trauma. As a result, investigations initially targeted tuberculosis and malignancy because of the bloody pleural effusion, before the diagnosis eventually shifted. In our own patient, recurrent chest pain and right-sided pleural effusions were noted, with a history of trauma only revealed after repeated history-taking, and MRCP findings led to a pancreatic etiology. Accordingly, PPF can present in a manner mimicking primary pulmonary disease and may divert physicians toward alternative, often unrelated, diagnoses unless careful history and appropriate imaging are obtained.

Fluid accumulation in PPF is typically caused by external trauma or infection, causing pancreatic duct or pseudocyst rupture with leakage of pancreatic secretions. Anterior ruptures result in pancreatic fluid entering the peritoneum, producing ascites; posterior ruptures direct the fluid into the retroperitoneum, from which it moves along the path of least resistance to the mediastinum (via the aortic or esophageal hiatus) and finally through the pleura, leading to pleural effusion. In this setting, an internal pancreatic fistula acts as a conduit for enzyme-rich pancreatic secretions to enter the thoracic cavity. In adults, the predominant cause of chronic pancreatitis leading to PPF is alcohol. Uchiyama and colleagues performed an extensive review of the Japanese literature, finding 113 cases, of which 99% were attributable to chronic alcoholic pancreatitis, with chest symptoms reported in 68%. In children, hereditary factors prevail as the underlying cause, with mutations in PRSS1, the CFTR gene, SPINK1, and chymotrypsin C gene recognized as frequent contributors. In PRSS1, the R122H and N291 variants in exons 3 and 2, respectively, are representative. When pediatric pancreatitis is recurrent or takes a chronic course, clinicians should obtain detailed family histories and consider hereditary pancreatitis as a diagnosis.

Delay in diagnosis is common due to initial extensive pulmonary evaluation before the pancreatic source is recognized. A pleural fluid amylase concentration of at least 1000 IU/L is diagnostic for PPF. However, clinicians must also consider tuberculosis, esophageal rupture, and malignancies of the lung or pancreas, as these can also be associated with elevated pleural fluid amylase. PPF is specifically characterized by pancreatic-type amylase within pleural fluid, distinguishing it from other causes, which typically have salivary-type amylase. Amylase isoenzyme typing in pleural fluid can aid in the evaluation of suspected PPF. MRCP has a sensitivity of 70–80% for visualizing the fistula and ductal anatomy.
