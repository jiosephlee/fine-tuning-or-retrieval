Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood

Hans Vaish, Vipin Vaish, Shalini Suri, Vasudha Tomar

Abstract:

Summary: A male child in late childhood presented with repeated episodes of right-sided pleural effusion, accompanied by chest pain and difficulty breathing, with symptoms temporarily alleviated by aspiration. The pleural fluid was persistently dark red in color. Due to the recurrence of effusions, a right intercostal drain was inserted. On further inquiry, it was found that the child had sustained recent abdominal trauma from the handlebar of a bicycle. Computed tomography of the chest identified patchy consolidation in the right lung, hydropneumothorax, and a multiloculated hypodense fluid collection extending from the right paraesophageal area to the pancreatic tail. Amylase and lipase levels were elevated in both the serum and pleural fluid samples. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography verified the existence of bilateral pancreatopleural fistulas, more prominent on the right. Management involved keeping the patient nil per oral, administration of total parenteral nutrition, and Octreotide therapy. Within 10 days, pleural drainage diminished, the chest drain was withdrawn, and the patient was subsequently discharged.

Background:

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) constitutes a highly uncommon condition characterized by recurrent, large-volume, and often hemorrhagic pleural effusions. This complication may arise in the context of acute pancreatitis, chronic pancreatitis, or pancreatic trauma. Diagnostically, PPF poses considerable challenges as it often manifests with symptoms of respiratory compromise secondary to repeated pleural effusions, despite its etiology originating from pancreatic pathology. As a result, the identification of PPF may be postponed since initial clinical assessments typically focus on thoracic causes. To date, the literature documents only 47 pediatric cases of PPF, while over 300 instances have been described in the adult population.

Case presentation:

A pediatric patient presented to the outpatient clinic, accompanied by his mother, with primary concerns of shortness of breath and right-sided chest pain persisting for one week. The mother reported a progressive worsening of respiratory distress over the preceding seven days. There was no familial consanguinity noted. Relevant medical history included contact with a family member (the child's grandfather) previously treated for pulmonary Koch's disease. Dietary assessment revealed that the child regularly consumed processed, high-calorie foods and had a low intake of fresh produce and nutritionally balanced home-cooked meals. Anthropometric measurements showed a weight of 44 kg, height of 112 cm, and a body mass index of 35.

Clinical evaluation revealed the child had a respiratory rate of 20 /min and demonstrated mild tachypnoea. There was diminished chest wall movement on the right side. Percussion revealed stony dullness over the right subscapular, infra-axillary, and axillary regions. Tactile vocal fremitus was markedly reduced on the right when compared to the left. Auscultation noted decreased breath sounds in corresponding right-sided regions. There were no signs of pallor, cyanosis, icterus, clubbing, or lymphadenopathy. The abdominal exam was unremarkable, with no evidence of hepatosplenomegaly. The patient was alert and oriented, with a soft, non-tender abdomen and no organomegaly. Cardiac examination revealed tachycardia but no murmurs. Pulse oximetry demonstrated oxygen saturation between 88%–89% on room air and a heart rate of 110 /min. Owing to the respiratory compromise and tachypnoea, the patient was transferred to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit (PICU) and provided oxygen via face mask at 5 L/min.

A chest X-ray was performed, and after collecting blood cultures, empirical antibiotic therapy with piperacillin-tazobactam was initiated. Radiographic imaging indicated a right-sided pleural effusion with blunting of the costophrenic angle (figure 1; Table 2). Point-of-care lung ultrasound estimated the effusion volume at approximately one litre (figure 2; Table 2). Therapeutic aspiration of the right-sided effusion was carried out under aseptic technique, yielding about one litre of dark red pleural fluid. Post-procedure, the patient’s tachypnoea resolved and oxygen saturation normalized (95%–98%) on ambient air. Chest pain subsided, and supplemental oxygen was discontinued within 24 hours. The child was subsequently transferred to the ward and then discharged. The aspirated pleural fluid was sent for analysis.

Six days later, the patient was re-admitted with recurrent right-sided chest pain, tachypnoea, and breathing difficulties. In the PICU, repeat chest radiography showed recurrence of the right pleural effusion. A second thoracocentesis was performed, again draining about one litre of dark red fluid. Pleural fluid Adenosine Deaminase (ADA) was mildly increased (Table 1), and given the positive history of exposure to tuberculosis, empirical anti-tubercular therapy was commenced. However, five days later, the child was admitted a third time with similar symptoms of right-sided chest pain and respiratory difficulty.

A more detailed history at this stage revealed the child had experienced blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle approximately one month prior. Contrast-enhanced chest CT, performed prior to intercostal drainage (ICD) tube placement, revealed patchy right lung air space consolidation, right-sided hydropneumothorax, and an elongated, multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right pleural space along the right paraoesophageal region down to the upper retroperitoneum adjacent to the pancreatic tail (Table 2). An ICD was placed, draining about one litre of dark red pleural fluid (figure 3). Considering CT findings, the color of aspirate, and trauma history, a diagnosis of pancreaticopleural fistula (PPF) was suspected. Magnetic Resonance Cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) was ordered the same day and identified pancreatitis with a tubular, cystic collection extending from the pancreatic body and tail posteriorly through the retroperitoneum, tracking superiorly through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity. A narrow tract also connected with the left pleural space. The collection communicated with the distal pancreatic duct, which was narrowed proximally. MRCP supported the diagnosis of PPF (figures 4–6; Table 1). Amylase and lipase concentrations in both serum and pleural fluid were measured, and pleural fluid analysis demonstrated substantially increased amylase (6118 U/L) and lipase (7211 U/L) levels (Table 2), corroborating the diagnosis of PPF.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient exhibited pleural effusion and chest pain, with a documented episode of abdominal trauma occurring one month prior. Beyond considering post-traumatic pancreatitis, alternative etiologies for the pleural effusion were systematically excluded. The patient did not report any history of fever, prolonged cough, or weight loss. Although there was familial exposure to Koch’s and the pleural fluid ADA was slightly raised, the GeneXpert assay for MTB returned negative results, thereby excluding pulmonary Koch’s disease. Empyema was considered unlikely due to the absence of fever and the lack of a neutrophilic exudate in pleural fluid analysis. Malignant conditions—including primary lung cancer, leukemia, lymphoma, or metastatic tumors—may present with elevated pleural fluid amylase; nevertheless, no accompanying fever, no weight loss, unremarkable blood work, and a normal peripheral smear all argued against malignancy. Furthermore, cytological examination of the pleural fluid did not detect malignant cells. Although Boerhaave’s syndrome (esophageal perforation) can result in amylase-enriched pleural fluid, this diagnosis was excluded based on the patient's regular bowel movements and the absence of preceding vomiting or chest pain episodes. Imaging studies did not demonstrate abdominal organs within the thoracic cavity, ruling out acquired diaphragmatic hernia. Hepatic hydrothorax was excluded since both liver function tests and hepatic imaging were within normal limits and there was no clinical history suggestive of chronic liver disease. Renal pathology, including hydronephrosis, was dismissed due to normal findings on renal imaging and renal function assessment.

Treatment:

In the PICU, the patient received respiratory support through administration of humidified oxygen via mask. Thoracocentesis, guided by ultrasound, was performed on two occasions, yielding approximately one litre of blood-tinged pleural fluid each time. Initially, empirical antitubercular therapy was initiated but was discontinued upon confirmation of diagnosis via MRCP. Upon the child’s third hospital admission, an ICD was aseptically placed on the right side, with a subsequent drainage of about one litre of blood-stained pleural fluid into the ICD collection bag. As PPF is a rare diagnosis in paediatric patients and there are no established treatment protocols, management strategies are largely derived from individual case reports. A conservative approach was initially employed: the patient was kept nil per oral and commenced on an Octreotide infusion at a dosage of 30 µg/kg/day (range: 10–40 µg/kg/day). Additionally, total parenteral nutrition and pancreatic enzyme supplementation were started. Over a period of 10 days, the volume of pleural fluid drained via the ICD steadily diminished and eventually ceased altogether. The ICD was subsequently removed, and the patient was discharged in stable condition.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following his hospital discharge, the child has not exhibited any symptoms. He continues to attend monthly follow-up appointments with me. Since leaving the hospital, there have been no further occurrences of pleural effusions (S17). He has adopted recommended lifestyle changes, including a balanced diet and consistent physical activity. He has resumed his routine life and attends school without interruption. Additionally, he is able to participate in vigorous activities such as running and playing without experiencing any problems.

Discussion:

PPF represents a particularly uncommon complication arising from pancreatic disorders in children. To date, only 47 paediatric instances have been recorded in the literature. PPF may develop in the context of both acute and chronic pancreatitis, or following trauma in childhood. Although the precise frequency in the paediatric population is unclear, PPF complicates approximately 0.4% of all pancreatitis cases. Genetic mutations, notably in the serine peptidase inhibitor Kazal type 1 (SPINK1) and PRSS1 genes, account for up to 30% of chronic pancreatitis among children. Non-genetic factors, particularly blunt trauma to the abdomen, are significant as well, constituting 32% of the 25 paediatric cases reviewed by Yang et al. Some presentations are labelled as idiopathic, possibly due to yet-unidentified genetic or anatomical ductal variations. Mechanistically, pancreatic secretions may escape from a ruptured pancreatic duct or pseudocyst, traversing natural diaphragmatic pathways—most commonly the aortic or oesophageal hiatus—or, less frequently, via direct diaphragmatic erosion to form a pleuro-pancreatic fistula. Many cases go unrecognized because initial symptoms are usually thoracic in nature, prompting investigations focused on pulmonary etiologies, rather than abdominal causes. In a literature review yielding 21 pertinent articles, it was found that 68% of cases manifested primarily with respiratory features—such as massive, recurring pleural effusions (unilateral or bilateral), dyspnoea, and cough. Fewer than 20% of children reported abdominal symptoms like epigastric discomfort, emesis, or weight loss, while about 24% exhibited constitutional signs such as fever. For example, Zi Yin Shang described an 8-year-old with PPF presenting solely with a protruding left hemithorax of two weeks’ duration—a particularly atypical manifestation. In this instance, pleural effusion developed gradually post abdominal trauma. The patient adapted due to preserved function in the opposite lung, explaining the lack of more common pulmonary complaints. Furthermore, psychosocial factors (bullying at school) led the child to conceal the history of injury. Diagnostic work-up was initially directed towards tuberculosis and malignancy because of the haemorrhagic effusion, but the ultimate diagnosis differed. Similarly, in our case, recurrent chest pain and right-sided pleural effusions were observed; it was only after detailed repeated histories and MRCP findings that an underlying pancreatic disorder was suspected and confirmed. This clinical scenario underscores the concept of a “Pulmonary mask,” by which underlying pancreatic pathology can mimic pulmonary disease, confounding diagnosis unless a comprehensive history and appropriate imaging are employed. 

Fluid accumulation in PPF results from either traumatic or infectious insult causing disruption of the pancreatic duct or rupture of a pseudocyst, with subsequent leakage of pancreatic juice. If the anterior pancreatic surface is breached, the fluid accumulates in the peritoneal cavity, producing ascites. However, posterior disruption enables the fluid to migrate into the retroperitoneum, ascend through diaphragmatic hiatuses into the mediastinum, and finally breach the pleural space, giving rise to effusions. This creates an internal fistula that redirects pancreatic secretions toward the thoracic cavity. 

In adult patients, the majority of PPF cases are secondary to alcohol-induced chronic pancreatitis. A seminal Japanese review by Uchiyama et al. identified 113 cases in that population, noting a 99% association with alcoholic pancreatitis, and predominant chest symptoms in 68%. In contrast, hereditary factors are more common triggers in children. Genetic alterations—especially in the PRSS1 gene, the cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator gene, SPINK1, and chymotrypsin C—are implicated, with specific mutations such as R122H and N291 in exons 3 and 2, respectively, of PRSS1 being most prevalent. For paediatric patients with recurring or chronic pancreatitis, a thorough familial evaluation is necessary to assess for hereditary aetiologies.

Diagnosis of PPF is often delayed due to the overwhelming focus on pulmonary diagnostics, which typically precede consideration of a pancreatic origin. Pathognomonic for PPF is a pleural fluid amylase concentration of $\geq$1000 IU/L. However, raised pleural fluid amylase may also be encountered in tuberculosis, oesophageal perforation, and malignancies of the lung or pancreas, necessitating further distinction. Unlike these other conditions, which feature salivary-type amylase, PPF is characterized by pancreatic-type amylase in the effusion, a point which may be clarified through isoenzyme analysis. MRCP demonstrates a sensitivity of 70%–80% for identifying the fistula and ductal anatomy.
