Title: Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood

Hans Vaish, Vipin Vaish, Shalini Suri, Vasudha Tomar

Abstract:

Summary: An older male child developed recurrent pleural effusions on the right side, accompanied by chest discomfort and difficulty breathing, with symptoms temporarily alleviated following fluid aspiration. The pleural aspirate consistently appeared dark red. Ongoing effusions led to insertion of a right intercostal drain. Further history revealed recent blunt abdominal injury sustained from a bicycle handlebar. Chest CT revealed patchy consolidation on the right, hydropneumothorax, and a multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right paraesophageal area toward the pancreatic tail. Amylase and lipase levels were elevated in both blood and pleural fluid samples. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography demonstrated bilateral pancreatopleural fistulas, predominating on the right. Management included fasting, total parenteral nutrition, and Octreotide therapy. Within 10 days, the volume of drained fluid declined, the chest tube was withdrawn, and the patient was discharged.

Background:

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is a highly uncommon condition that manifests as repeated, large, and blood-tinged pleural effusions. This complication may arise in patients with acute or chronic pancreatitis as well as those who have experienced pancreatic injury. Diagnostically, it is often challenging because patients exhibit symptoms of respiratory compromise resulting from recurrent pleural effusions, though the underlying problem originates from the pancreas. This frequently leads to a delayed diagnosis since the initial clinical approach often focuses on thoracic causes. In the pediatric population, only 47 cases of PPF have been documented, while the adult literature reports over 300 cases.

Case presentation:

A pediatric patient was brought to the outpatient clinic by his mother due to complaints of shortness of breath and right-sided chest pain persisting for one week. According to the mother, the child's respiratory discomfort had been gradually worsening over the course of seven days. There was no reported history of parental consanguinity. The mother disclosed that the child had been in contact with his grandfather, who had been treated for pulmonary Koch's disease. The child’s diet consisted mainly of processed, calorie-dense foods with minimal fresh fruit, vegetables, or nutritionally balanced home-cooked dishes. The child's weight was recorded at 44 kg and height at 112 cm, yielding a BMI of 35. 

On physical examination, the respiratory rate was noted at 20/min, indicating mild tachypnea. Diminished right-sided chest movements were observed. Percussion produced a stony dull note in the right subscapular, infra-axillary, and axillary areas. Tactile vocal fremitus was substantially decreased on the right compared to the left, and breath sounds were reduced in the same right-sided regions. The patient exhibited no pallor, cyanosis, jaundice, clubbing, or lymphadenopathy. The abdomen was soft without evidence of hepatosplenomegaly. The child was alert and oriented, and abdominal examination remained unremarkable. Cardiovascular assessment demonstrated tachycardia without murmurs. Oxygen saturation measured 88%–89% in room air, with a heart rate of 110/min. 

Given the tachypnea and respiratory distress, he was admitted to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit and commenced on oxygen therapy at 5 l/min via mask. A chest radiograph was obtained, and empirical antibiotics (piperacillin-tazobactam) were started after obtaining blood cultures. The chest X-ray showed a right-sided pleural effusion with blunting of the right costophrenic angle (figure 1) (Table 2). Point-of-care lung ultrasound identified an approximate one-litre pleural effusion (figure 2) (Table 2). Pleural fluid aspiration was performed aseptically, yielding about one litre of dark red fluid. Following thoracocentesis, the patient's tachypnea resolved, and oxygen saturation on room air improved to 95%–98%. Chest pain subsided, and he was weaned off supplemental oxygen within 24 hours. He was transferred to the general ward the following day and subsequently discharged. The aspirated pleural fluid was sent for analysis.

Six days later, the child was readmitted with recurrent right-sided chest pain, tachypnea, and respiratory difficulty. He was taken back to the PICU, where chest X-ray again revealed a right-sided pleural effusion. Repeat thoracocentesis extracted another approximately one litre of dark red pleural fluid. Pleural fluid adenosine deaminase (ADA) was mildly elevated (Table 1), and, considering the known history of Koch’s contact, empiric antitubercular therapy was initiated. However, the child was admitted a third time five days later, again with complaints of right-sided chest pain and respiratory difficulty. 

Upon reevaluation, the patient disclosed a history of blunt abdominal injury from a bicycle handle one month prior. A contrast-enhanced CT scan of the chest was conducted before insertion of an intercostal drain (ICD). The CT demonstrated patchy areas of airspace consolidation in the right lung, right-sided hydropneumothorax, and an elongated, lobulated, multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right pleural space, tracking from the right paraesophageal region to the upper left retroperitoneum up to the tail of the pancreas (Table 2). ICD placement allowed drainage of about one litre of dark red pleural fluid (figure 3). Given the CT findings, pleural fluid characteristics, and trauma history, a pancreaticopleural fistula (PPF) was suspected. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) was performed the same day, revealing pancreatitis with a tubular cystic collection in the distal body and tail of the pancreas that extended posteriorly into the retroperitoneum and ascended through the oesophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity, with a narrow passage to the left pleural space. The collection also communicated with the distal pancreatic duct, where upstream duct narrowing was evident. MRCP confirmed the presence of PPF (figures 4-6) (table 1). Serum and pleural fluid amylase and lipase levels were measured. Pleural fluid analysis demonstrated markedly increased amylase (6118 U/L) and lipase (7211 U/L) (table 2), reinforcing the diagnosis of PPF. Differential diagnosis:

The patient exhibited pleural effusion and chest pain, with a clear history of abdominal trauma occurring 1 month prior. Except for post-traumatic pancreatitis, alternative etiologies for pleural effusion were excluded. The child lacked any history of fever, persistent cough, or weight loss. While there was a positive history of Koch’s contact within the family and pleural fluid ADA was slightly increased, GeneXpert testing for MTB returned negative, thereby excluding pulmonary Koch’s disease. The absence of fever and lack of neutrophilic exudate in the pleural fluid eliminated empyema from consideration. Certain malignancies (such as lung cancer, leukemia, lymphoma, or metastatic tumors) may be associated with amylase-rich pleural fluid; nonetheless, malignancy was considered unlikely given the absence of fever and weight loss, along with a normal blood count and peripheral smear. Additionally, pleural fluid cytology did not reveal malignant cells. Boerhaave’s syndrome (esophageal perforation) can also produce amylase-rich pleural effusions, but this was deemed improbable, as the child had regular bowel movements and no reports of chest pain or vomiting. Acquired diaphragmatic hernia was excluded due to the lack of abdominal organs seen in the thoracic cavity on imaging studies. Hepatic hydrothorax was dismissed since liver function tests and imaging were normal, with no evidence or history of chronic liver disease. Renal pathologies or hydronephrosis were ruled out due to normal findings on renal imaging and kidney function tests.

Treatment:

In the PICU, the patient received humidified oxygen administered via a mask for respiratory support. Thoracocentesis, guided by ultrasound, was performed twice, and approximately one litre of blood-tinged pleural fluid was aspirated on each occasion. Antitubercular medications were initiated empirically, but discontinued promptly after confirmation of diagnosis using MRCP. During the third hospital admission, an intercostal drainage (ICD) tube was placed on the right side under aseptic conditions, with a further drainage of about one litre of blood-tinged pleural fluid collected in the ICD bag. Due to the rarity of PPF in pediatric patients and the absence of established treatment protocols, management was guided by published case reports. Conservative therapy was selected as the initial approach, including keeping the child nil per oral and commencing an Octreotide infusion at a dose of 30 µg/kg/day (dose range 10–40 µg/kg/day). Total parenteral nutrition was initiated along with pancreatic enzyme supplementation. Over the course of 10 days, the drainage from the ICD progressively decreased until it ceased. The ICD was subsequently removed and the child was discharged from the hospital.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following hospital discharge, the child has remained asymptomatic. He continues to attend scheduled monthly follow-up appointments. Since leaving the hospital, no further episodes of recurrent pleural effusion have occurred (S17). He has implemented recommended lifestyle changes, adhering to a balanced diet and routine physical activity. The patient has resumed normal daily routines, including consistent school attendance. He is able to engage in vigorous activities, such as running and playing, without limitation.

Discussion:

PPF represents a rare and seldom-seen complication of pancreatic disorders in children. To date, only 47 paediatric cases of PPF have been reported in the literature. It has been documented in instances of both acute and chronic pancreatitis, as well as following paediatric abdominal trauma. Although the exact prevalence in children remains undefined, overall, PPF arises in approximately 0.4% of pancreatitis cases. Genetic mutations including serine peptidase inhibitor Kazal type 1 (SPINK1) and PRSS1 account for up to 30% of paediatric chronic pancreatitis cases. Blunt trauma to the abdomen is also a notable non-genetic precipitant, comprising 32% of cases in a cohort of 25 patients studied by Yang et al. Cases without a clear etiology still occur, possibly related to unrecognized genetic or ductal anomalies.

Pancreatic fluid may pass from a damaged pancreatic duct or pseudocyst through anatomical openings in the diaphragm—most commonly via the aortic or oesophageal hiatus—or less frequently, by direct erosion through the diaphragm, leading to a fistulous communication with the pleural space. The majority of these cases are overlooked, as presenting symptoms often mimic thoracic conditions; thus, diagnostic investigations tend to focus on pulmonary causes, when the origin is actually abdominal.

A review of the literature found 21 relevant cases, with 68% manifesting chiefly respiratory symptoms such as sizable, recurrent, unilateral or bilateral pleural effusions, shortness of breath, and cough. Fewer than 20% demonstrated abdominal symptoms like pain in the epigastrium, vomiting, and loss of weight. Constitutional signs including fever were present in 24% of cases. For example, Zi Yin Shang reported in Frontiers in Paediatrics 2023 a PPF case involving an 8-year-old boy who presented solely with a two-week history of left-sided chest swelling. Initial presentation with chest wall bulging is exceedingly uncommon. In this instance, pleural effusion likely developed gradually after abdominal trauma, and compensatory respiratory mechanisms allowed adaptation, masking classic respiratory complaints such as cough or shortness of breath. Furthermore, the boy concealed a history of abdominal trauma due to school bullying. Diagnostic work-up focused on tuberculosis and malignancy for his haemorrhagic pleural fluid, but the correct diagnosis proved to be different. In our own case, the patient had repeated pulmonary symptoms consisting of chest pain and recurrent right-sided pleural fluid accumulation. The history of trauma was disclosed only after extensive interviewing, with MRCP findings pointing to pancreatic involvement.

Hence, PPF may present as a ‘Pulmonary mask,’ potentially misdirecting clinicians away from the underlying diagnosis, unless a thorough clinical history and appropriate imaging are obtained. Fluid accumulation in PPF results from either external trauma or infection, leading to rupture of the pancreatic duct or pseudocyst and subsequent leakage of pancreatic secretions. If the anterior pancreas ruptures, fluid enters the peritoneal cavity, resulting in ascites. Rupture posteriorly leads fluid into the retroperitoneal space, where it travels upward along the path of least resistance, reaching the mediastinum via the aortic or oesophageal hiatus and then breaches the pleura, producing pleural effusion. This internal fistula behaves much like a defective conduit, diverting pancreatic exocrine secretions into the thoracic cavity.

Among adults, chronic pancreatitis associated with alcohol use is the most frequent cause of PPF. Uchiyama and colleagues conducted a comprehensive review of Japanese literature, documenting 113 cases in which 99% were linked to chronic alcoholic pancreatitis, with 68% presenting chest complaints. Contrastingly, paediatric cases predominantly stem from genetic factors. Mutations in the PRSS1 gene, CF transmembrane regulator gene, SPINK1 gene, and chymotrypsin C gene are recognized as drivers of hereditary pancreatitis, with PRSS1 mutations such as R122H and N291 (exons 3 and 2 respectively) being the most representative. When paediatric pancreatitis is recurrent or displays chronicity, it is imperative to undertake an exhaustive family history and consider hereditary forms.

Diagnosis of PPF is frequently delayed as most patients undergo prolonged investigations for respiratory disease before the possibility of pancreatic etiology is explored. A pleural fluid amylase concentration ≥1000 IU/L is considered diagnostic for PPF. Conditions such as tuberculosis, oesophageal rupture, and pulmonary or pancreatic malignancies may also elevate pleural fluid amylase and should be ruled out. Detection of pancreatic-type amylase in pleural fluid differentiates PPF from other causes, as elevated levels from salivary-type amylase are found in alternative diagnoses.
