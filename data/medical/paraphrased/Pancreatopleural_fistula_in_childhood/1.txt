Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood

Hans Vaish, Vipin Vaish, Shalini Suri, Vasudha Tomar

Abstract:

Summary: An older child presented with multiple episodes of right-sided pleural effusion accompanied by chest pain and difficulty breathing, which would transiently resolve after thoracentesis. The drained pleural fluid persistently appeared dark red. Because the effusions kept recurring, a right intercostal chest tube was inserted. On further inquiry, the child was found to have sustained recent abdominal trauma from impact with a bicycle handlebar. Computed tomography of the chest revealed right-sided areas of consolidation, hydropneumothorax, and a multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right paraesophageal area down to the pancreatic tail. Amylase and lipase levels were raised in both serum and pleural fluid. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography demonstrated bilateral pancreatopleural fistulas, with a greater involvement on the right. Management consisted of keeping the patient nil per oral, administering total parenteral nutrition, and starting Octreotide. Over the next 10 days, the fluid output decreased, leading to chest tube removal and eventual discharge of the patient.

Background:

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) represents a highly uncommon condition, characterized by repeated, large-volume, and occasionally hemorrhagic pleural effusions. This phenomenon occurs in association with acute pancreatitis, chronic pancreatitis, or pancreatic injury. Clinically, PPF often presents a diagnostic challenge as patients typically exhibit respiratory symptoms related to ongoing pleural effusion, yet the underlying cause resides within the pancreas. This discrepancy can result in a delayed diagnosis, since initial investigations are frequently directed towards identifying thoracic causes. To date, the literature documents only 47 paediatric cases of PPF, whereas over 300 cases have been described in the adult population.

Case presentation:

A male child presented to the outpatient department, accompanied by his mother, reporting a one-week history of breathlessness and right-sided chest discomfort. According to the mother, his respiratory distress had progressively worsened during this time. There was no known consanguinity. She also reported household exposure to Koch’s, as the child's grandfather had previously undergone successful treatment for pulmonary Koch’s. Dietary history revealed frequent intake of energy-dense processed foods, with a notable lack of fresh produce and balanced meals. The child's anthropometric measurements were notable for a weight of 44 kg, a height of 112 cm, and a calculated body mass index of 35.

On physical assessment, the child’s respiratory rate was 20 breaths per minute with mild tachypnoea. Inspection showed reduced movement of the right chest. Percussion was stony dull in the right subscapular, infra-axillary, and axillary regions, and vocal fremitus was significantly diminished on the right. Breath sounds were decreased in these same areas. No signs of pallor, cyanosis, jaundice, clubbing, or lymphadenopathy were observed. The abdomen was soft without palpable liver or spleen enlargement. The child was alert and oriented. Cardiovascular examination indicated tachycardia without any audible murmurs. His oxygen saturation was low at 88%–89% in ambient air, with a pulse rate of 110 beats per minute.

Given the clinical signs of tachypnoea and respiratory distress, the patient was admitted to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit (PICU) and initiated on oxygen therapy via face mask at 5 L/min. A chest radiograph was performed, and empiric intravenous piperacillin-tazobactam commenced after blood cultures were sent. Chest X-ray demonstrated a large right-sided pleural effusion with blunting of the costophrenic angle (figure 1) (Table 2). Point-of-care lung ultrasonography confirmed the presence of approximately one litre of pleural fluid (figure 2) (Table 2). Using aseptic technique, therapeutic thoracentesis was performed, draining about one litre of dark red fluid. The patient's tachypnoea subsided post-procedure, and room air oxygen saturation improved to 95%–98%. He was rapidly weaned off oxygen within 24 hours, reported marked relief of chest pain, and was transferred to the general ward for subsequent discharge. The pleural fluid obtained was sent for laboratory analysis.

However, the child returned 6 days later with recurrent right-sided chest pain, tachypnoea, and breathing difficulty, necessitating another PICU admission. Chest X-ray once again confirmed a right-sided pleural effusion. Repeat thoracentesis yielded about one litre of similarly coloured pleural fluid. The pleural fluid Adenosine Deaminase (ADA) was mildly elevated (Table 1). Considering the positive Koch's exposure history, empiric antitubercular treatment was initiated.

Five days thereafter, the patient was admitted for a third time with comparable symptoms. On further history-taking, the child disclosed sustaining blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle approximately one month prior. Prior to placing an intercostal drainage (ICD) catheter, a contrast-enhanced CT scan of the chest was ordered. The CT scan revealed patchy consolidation in the right lung, right-sided hydropneumothorax, and an elongated, multiloculated, hypodense collection extending from the right pleural space along the right paraoesophageal area to the left upper retroperitoneum, reaching the tail of the pancreas (Table 2). An ICD was placed, draining an additional litre of dark red fluid (figure 3). The findings from the CT scan, the distinct fluid colour, and the recent abdominal trauma suggested a diagnosis of pancreaticopleural fistula (PPF). An MRCP was arranged that same day, demonstrating pancreatitis with a tubular cystic collection in the distal body and tail of the pancreas, tracking posteriorly through the retroperitoneum and traversing the oesophageal hiatus into the right pleural space. There was also a narrow tract communicating with the left pleural space. The collection communicated with the distal pancreatic duct, which showed proximal narrowing. MRCP confirmed the diagnosis of PPF (figures 4-6) (Table 1).

Amylase and lipase were measured in both serum and pleural fluid. The analysis revealed marked elevation in pleural fluid amylase (6118 U/L) and lipase (7211 U/L) (Table 2), supporting the diagnosis of PPF. Consequently, antitubercular therapy was discontinued. The patient was kept nil per oral, initiated on total parenteral nutrition, and octreotide was started at 30 µg/kg/day (within the range of 10–40 µg/kg/day).

Differential diagnosis:

The patient exhibited pleural effusion and chest pain, with a documented incident of abdominal trauma occurring one month prior. After evaluating potential etiologies, post-traumatic pancreatitis was considered the primary cause, as alternative explanations for pleural effusion were excluded. The child’s medical history lacked episodes of fever, persistent cough, or significant weight reduction. Although there was a familial history of Koch’s contact and the pleural fluid ADA levels were slightly raised, GeneXpert testing for MTB yielded negative results, effectively excluding pulmonary Koch’s. Absence of both fever and neutrophilic exudate in pleural fluid eliminated empyema as a differential diagnosis. While neoplastic conditions such as lung cancer, leukaemia, lymphoma, or metastatic tumors can be associated with elevated amylase in pleural fluid, malignancy was excluded based on normal hematological findings, stable weight, absence of fever, and a negative pleural fluid cytology. Boerhaave’s syndrome, or esophageal perforation, was deemed unlikely given the patient’s regular bowel movements and lack of chest pain or vomiting history. Imaging did not reveal displacement of abdominal organs into the chest, thereby ruling out acquired diaphragmatic hernia. Hepatic hydrothorax was discounted since both liver function and imaging results were normal, with no evidence of chronic liver disease. Finally, normal findings from renal imaging and function tests excluded the possibility of renal pathology or hydronephrosis.

Treatment:

In the PICU, the patient received respiratory support via humidified oxygen administered through a mask. Thoracocentesis was performed twice under ultrasound guidance, yielding approximately one litre of blood-tinged pleural fluid with each procedure. Initial management included empirical initiation of antitubercular therapy, which was discontinued following confirmation of the diagnosis through MRCP. Upon the patient’s third hospitalization, an intercostal drainage (ICD) tube was aseptically inserted on the right side, and an additional litre of blood-stained pleural fluid was collected through the ICD. Due to the rarity of PPF in paediatric patients, standardized treatment protocols are lacking, and existing management strategies are mainly derived from individual case reports. As a first-line approach, conservative management was employed: the child was made nil per oral and commenced on Octreotide infusion at a dose of 30 µg/kg/day (within the range of 10–40 µg/kg/day). Total parenteral nutrition as well as pancreatic enzyme supplementation were also initiated. Over the course of ten days, the fluid drainage from the ICD progressively diminished until it ceased completely. Subsequently, the ICD was removed and the patient was discharged in stable condition.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following hospital discharge, the patient has remained asymptomatic. He continues to attend monthly follow-up appointments under my care. Since leaving the hospital (S17), he has experienced no additional instances of recurrent pleural effusions. The patient has adopted a healthier lifestyle, adhering to recommendations for a balanced diet and consistent physical activity. He has fully resumed his routine activities, including regular school attendance, and is able to engage in vigorous activities such as running and playing without limitation.

Discussion:

PPF represents an exceptionally rare and infrequently encountered sequela of pancreatic pathology within paediatric cohorts. To date, literature documents just 47 paediatric instances of PPF. This condition may arise following episodes of either acute or chronic pancreatitis or may be secondary to trauma in children. The precise prevalence in paediatric populations remains undetermined, yet PPF complicates roughly 0.4% of overall pancreatitis cases. Notably, mutations in Serine peptidase inhibitor Kazal type 1 (SPINK1) and PRSS1 collectively contribute to up to 30% of childhood chronic pancreatitis cases. Additionally, blunt abdominal trauma constitutes a significant non-genetic precipitant, representing 32% of the cases in the 25-child series analyzed by Yang et al. Idiopathic presentations persist, potentially reflecting unrecognized genetic predispositions or ductal variants.

In PPF, pancreatic secretions may traverse from a compromised duct or a pseudocyst, passing through anatomical diaphragmatic openings—predominantly the aortic or oesophageal hiatus—or, less commonly, via direct transdiaphragmatic erosion, eventually forming a fistulous link to the pleural space. This condition is often initially overlooked, as most cases manifest primarily with thoracic complaints; clinical investigations, therefore, frequently focus on pulmonary etiologies, missing the underlying abdominal source. Among 21 relevant reports found through literature review, 68% of patients exhibited dominant respiratory manifestations including large, recurring unilateral or bilateral pleural effusions, breathlessness, and cough. Fewer than 20% presented with abdominal features such as epigastric pain, vomiting, or weight loss, while systemic symptoms (e.g., fever) were noted in 24%.

A case described by Zi Yin Shang in *Frontiers in Paediatrics* (2023) involved an 8-year-old boy presenting exclusively with a prominent left chest over two weeks—such presentations, characterized by chest protrusion as an initial symptom, are extremely uncommon. In that scenario, pleural effusion is presumed to have evolved incrementally after abdominal trauma. The compensatory capacity of the uninjured lung permitted the patient to adjust, resulting in minimal overt respiratory complaints. Complicating the diagnosis further, the child deliberately withheld a trauma history due to school bullying. Despite initial investigations for tuberculosis and malignancy owing to haemorrhagic pleural effusion, the diagnosis ultimately diverged. In our experience, recurrent respiratory symptoms, notably chest pain and repeated right-sided pleural effusions, were observed. It was only through persistent history-taking and confirmatory MRCP imaging that abdominal trauma and underlying pancreatic etiology were unveiled. Thus, PPF may clinically mimic a primary pulmonary disorder—a ‘pulmonary mask’—potentially leading to misdirected management if thorough history and appropriate imaging are not pursued.

PPF fluid accumulation results from either external traumatic forces or infectious processes that disrupt the pancreatic duct or pseudocyst, causing pancreatic enzyme leakage. Anterior pancreatic ductal disruption leads to leakage into the peritoneal cavity and resultant ascites; posterior ductal rupture, on the other hand, causes fluid to migrate retroperitoneally and ascend into the mediastinum via anatomical hiatuses, ultimately transgressing the pleural barrier to form an effusion. This process functions akin to a hidden leak, shunting pancreatic secretions into the thoracic cavity.

While alcohol is the predominant cause of chronic pancreatitis and subsequent PPF in adults, as illustrated in Uchiyama et al.’s review of 113 Japanese cases (with 99% attributed to chronic alcoholic pancreatitis and 68% of cases presenting chest symptoms), hereditary and genetic etiologies are primary contributors in children. Variants in PRSS1, CF transmembrane regulator, SPINK1, and chymotrypsin C genes underlie hereditary pancreatitis in paediatrics, with specific PRSS1 mutations such as R122H (exon 3) and N291 (exon 2) being particularly notable. Therefore, for paediatric pancreatitis of chronic or recurrent nature, comprehensive family assessment and consideration of hereditary factors are imperative.

Delayed diagnosis is common, as extensive respiratory evaluation typically precedes the consideration of a pancreatic source. Pathognomonic evidence of PPF includes pleural fluid amylase concentrations ≥1000 IU/L, though it is essential to exclude differentials such as tuberculosis, oesophageal perforation, and neoplasms of the pancreas or lung—each of which may also elevate pleural amylase levels. Importantly, amylase subtype determination may distinguish PPF (pancreatic type) from other causes (usually salivary type).
