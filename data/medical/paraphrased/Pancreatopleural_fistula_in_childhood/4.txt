Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood

Hans Vaish, Vipin Vaish, Shalini Suri, Vasudha Tomar

Abstract:

Summary: A boy in late childhood experienced repeated right-sided pleural effusions, accompanied by chest pain and episodes of respiratory distress, which showed only transient relief following aspiration. The pleural fluid persistently appeared dark red. Given the recurrence of effusions, a right intercostal drain was inserted. Clinical history uncovered a recent abdominal injury caused by the handlebar of a bicycle. Chest CT imaging demonstrated right-sided patchy consolidation, hydropneumothorax, and a multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right paraesophageal region toward the pancreatic tail. Elevated levels of amylase and lipase were found in both serum and pleural fluid. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography revealed bilateral pancreatopleural fistulas, more pronounced on the right. Management included keeping the patient nil per oral, administration of total parenteral nutrition, and Octreotide therapy. Over the course of ten days, pleural drainage diminished, allowing for removal of the chest tube, and the patient was subsequently discharged.

Background:

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) represents a particularly uncommon condition, typically characterized by repeated episodes of substantial, occasionally hemorrhagic, pleural effusions. It is most often encountered in the context of acute or chronic pancreatitis, as well as following pancreatic injury. PPF poses diagnostic challenges due to its clinical manifestation primarily as respiratory compromise stemming from recurring pleural collections, which often misleads clinicians to prioritize the investigation of thoracic causes; consequently, recognition of its pancreatic origin is frequently delayed. In the paediatric population, only 47 instances of PPF have been documented, while adult literature cites over 300 reported cases.

Case presentation:

A young boy presented to the outpatient clinic, brought by his mother, with a history of right-sided chest pain and breathing difficulties persisting for one week. According to his mother, his respiratory distress had been worsening progressively over this period. There was no familial relationship between his parents. The mother also reported a prior exposure to tuberculosis (“Koch’s contact”) through the child’s grandfather, who had been diagnosed with pulmonary tuberculosis and had received appropriate treatment. Dietary history revealed that the child frequently consumed processed and high-calorie foods while his intake of fresh produce and balanced home-cooked meals was limited. He weighed 44 kg, stood at 112 cm, and his body mass index was calculated at 35.

Upon examination, the child had a mildly increased respiratory rate at 20 /min and demonstrated mild tachypnoea. Right-sided chest movement was reduced. Percussion yielded stony dullness over the right subscapular, infra-axillary, and axillary regions. Vocal fremitus was significantly diminished on the right compared to the left. Breath sounds were similarly reduced over these same right-sided regions. There were no signs of pallor, cyanosis, icterus, clubbing, or lymphadenopathy. The abdomen was soft, non-tender, and no hepatosplenomegaly was noted. The child was alert and fully oriented. Cardiovascular examination revealed tachycardia, but no murmurs were present. Oxygen saturation on room air was measured at 88%–89%, with a heart rate of 110 /min. Due to the presence of tachypnoea and respiratory distress, he was transferred to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit (PICU), administered oxygen via mask at 5 l/min, and an empirical course of piperacillin-tazobactam was initiated after drawing blood cultures. Chest radiography showed right-sided pleural effusion with blunting of the right costophrenic angle (figure 1) (Table 2). Point-of-care lung ultrasound detected an effusion estimated at one litre (figure 2) (Table 2). Under sterile conditions, a right-sided thoracocentesis was performed, and approximately one litre of dark red pleural fluid was removed. Following the procedure, tachypnoea resolved and oxygen saturation on room air improved to 95%–98%. The child’s chest pain subsided and he was weaned off oxygen within 24 hours. He was subsequently transferred to the general ward and later discharged. The aspirated pleural fluid was sent for further analysis.

However, six days after discharge, the child was re-admitted with recurrent right-sided chest pain, tachypnoea, and dyspnoea. He was once again placed in the PICU, where repeat chest radiograph indicated a recurrent right-sided pleural effusion. Another thoracocentesis yielded roughly one litre of similar dark red fluid. Pleural fluid Adenosine Deaminase (ADA) levels were mildly raised (Table 1) and, given the history of Koch’s contact, antitubercular therapy was empirically started. Nonetheless, five days later, he required a third admission for persistent symptoms of right-sided chest pain and breathing difficulty.

On this occasion, a more detailed history was elicited, and the child disclosed that he had experienced blunt trauma to the abdomen with a bicycle handle one month prior. A contrast-enhanced chest CT scan was obtained prior to considering an intercostal drainage (ICD) procedure. Imaging showed patchy consolidations in the right lung, a right-sided hydropneumothorax, and a large, elongated, multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right pleural space at the right paraoesophageal area to the upper left retroperitoneum reaching the tail of the pancreas (Table 2). An ICD was placed, and approximately one litre of dark red pleural fluid was drained (figure 3). The CT findings, in combination with the nature of the pleural fluid and the history of prior abdominal trauma, raised suspicion for a pancreatico-pleural fistula (PPF). Accordingly, MRCP was performed on the same day and revealed evidence of pancreatitis with a tubular cystic collection extending from the distal body and tail of the pancreas into the posterior retroperitoneum and then superiorly through the oesophageal hiatus into the right pleural space. There was also a narrow channel communicating with the left pleural cavity, and the collection was seen connected to the distal pancreatic duct with proximal ductal narrowing. MRCP thus supported the diagnosis of PPF (figures 4–6) (table 1).

Differential diagnosis:

The patient exhibited pleural effusion and chest pain, with a clear antecedent of abdominal trauma occurring one month prior. All etiologies for pleural effusion, apart from post-traumatic pancreatitis, were systematically excluded. Notably, the patient did not experience fever, persistent cough, or weight loss. Although there was a documented exposure to Koch’s within the family and a slight increase in pleural fluid ADA, GeneXpert testing for MTB was negative, thereby excluding Pulmonary Koch’s as a cause. The absence of fever and a lack of neutrophilic exudate in pleural fluid negated the possibility of empyema. Regarding malignancy (including pulmonary cancer, leukaemia, lymphoma, and metastatic lesions), which may occasionally result in amylase-enriched pleural fluid, this was considered improbable due to the lack of fever, unremarkable weight, normal hematological parameters, and unremarkable peripheral smear; cytological examination of pleural fluid showed no malignant cells. Boerhaave’s syndrome (esophageal perforation) was similarly excluded on the basis that the patient maintained normal bowel movements and lacked any history of vomiting or chest pain. Acquired diaphragmatic hernia was considered unlikely as imaging did not reveal abdominal viscera within the thoracic cavity. Hepatic hydrothorax was dismissed due to normal liver function assessments, unremarkable hepatic imaging, and the absence of chronic liver disease in the patient's history. Additionally, both normal renal imaging and renal function excluded the presence of renal pathology or hydronephrosis.

Treatment:

In the PICU, the patient received humidified oxygen therapy via mask for respiratory support. Ultrasound-guided thoracocentesis was performed on two occasions, yielding approximately one litre of blood-tinged pleural fluid each time. Empirical antitubercular therapy was initiated but subsequently discontinued once the diagnosis was confirmed through MRCP. During the third hospitalization, an ICD was inserted on the right side under sterile conditions, and again, about one litre of blood-stained pleural fluid was drained into the collection bag. Given the rarity of PPF in the paediatric age group and the absence of standardized treatment protocols, management was guided by individual case reports. Initial management involved conservative measures: the patient was kept nil per oral and began receiving an Octreotide infusion at a dosage of 30 µg/kg/day (range 10–40 µg/kg/day). Total parenteral nutrition and supplementation with pancreatic enzymes were also started. Over the subsequent ten days, the volume of pleural fluid draining from the ICD progressively diminished and ultimately ceased. The ICD was then removed, and the patient was discharged.

Outcome and follow-up:

Since being discharged from the hospital, the child has not experienced any further symptoms. I continue to see him for scheduled monthly follow-up appointments. Since his discharge, there have been no additional occurrences of recurrent pleural effusions (S17). The child has adopted recommended lifestyle changes, maintaining a balanced diet and engaging in regular physical activity. He has resumed normal routines, attends school consistently, and participates in vigorous activities such as running and playing without any issues.

Discussion:

PPF is a notably rare complication of pancreatic disorders in the paediatric demographic. To date, only 47 cases of PPF have been documented among children. PPF can occur in the context of both acute and chronic pancreatitis or following trauma in paediatric patients. The exact frequency of this complication in children remains indeterminate; however, it is estimated to affect approximately 0.4% of all pancreatitis cases. Mutations in the Serine peptidase inhibitor Kazal type 1 (SPINK1) and PRSS1 genes are significantly implicated, accounting for up to 30% of paediatric chronic pancreatitis cases. Blunt force to the abdomen is another significant non-genetic etiological factor, representing 32% of the 25 paediatric cases described by Yang et al. Instances without an apparent cause continue to emerge, possibly reflecting unrecognised genetic or ductal anomalies. Anatomically, pancreatic fluid can traverse from a ruptured duct or pseudocyst through natural openings in the diaphragm—predominantly the aortic or oesophageal hiatuses—or less commonly via direct erosion through the diaphragm, thereby establishing a fistulous link to the pleural cavity. Many cases evade detection because initial presentations frequently involve thoracic complaints, leading clinicians to focus diagnostic efforts on pulmonary causes, while the primary pathology resides in the abdomen. Review of the literature yielded 21 pertinent case reports, where 68% of patients primarily presented with respiratory symptoms, such as large, recurrent unilateral or bilateral pleural effusions, shortness of breath, and cough. Fewer than 20% exhibited abdominal signs, including epigastric pain, vomiting, or weight loss, while constitutional symptoms like fever occurred in 24%. For example, Zi Yin Shang’s case in Frontiers in Paediatrics (2023) details an 8-year-old presenting solely with a left-sided chest bulge over a two-week period—such presentations are exceedingly uncommon. In this instance, the pleural effusion likely formed gradually following blunt abdominal trauma. Adaptation via compensatory respiration by the unaffected lung enabled the patient to avoid classic respiratory complaints such as cough or dyspnoea. Additionally, the child’s reluctance to disclose his history of trauma, due to experiences with bullying, further complicated the clinical picture. The investigative approach in this case initially targeted tuberculosis and malignancy as causes of the haemorrhagic effusion, before the correct diagnosis emerged. Similarly, our patient manifested with recurrent chest pain and repeated right-sided pleural effusions; it was only after several detailed interviews that a history of abdominal trauma was revealed, and MRCP findings indicated an underlying pancreatic disorder. Consequently, PPF can present as a ‘pulmonary mask,’ misleading clinicians towards alternative diagnoses unless thorough histories and appropriate imaging are pursued.

Fluid accumulation in PPF generally arises either from external mechanical forces or infectious processes, leading to pancreatic duct or pseudocyst rupture and subsequent leakage of pancreatic secretions. If the disruption occurs on the anterior surface of the pancreas, fluid leaks into the abdominal cavity, resulting in ascites. In contrast, posterior rupture allows fluid to flow into the retroperitoneal space, ascend along paths of least resistance, traverse the aortic or oesophageal hiatus into the mediastinum, and eventually breach the pleura to cause effusion. Such internal pancreatic fistulas essentially divert pancreatic secretions into the thoracic cavity, akin to a leaking conduit. In adults, alcohol is the predominant driver of chronic pancreatitis complicated by PPF. Uchiyama et al.’s comprehensive Japanese case series documented 113 instances, attributing 99% to chronic alcoholic pancreatitis, with thoracic symptoms seen in 68%. In paediatric cases, however, inherited factors prevail, including mutations in PRSS1, the CF transmembrane regulator gene, SPINK1, and chymotrypsin C. The PRSS1 gene, particularly mutations R122H (exon 3) and N291 (exon 2), is prototypical. In instances of recurrent or chronic pancreatitis in children, it is vital to take a detailed family history and evaluate for hereditary aetiologies.

Delayed diagnosis is typical because extensive pulmonary investigations usually precede suspicion of pancreatic pathology. A pleural fluid amylase concentration ≥1000 IU/L is diagnostic for PPF. Nevertheless, raised pleural amylase can also occur in tuberculosis, oesophageal perforation, and malignancies, necessitating exclusion of these conditions. Specifically, the identification of pancreatic-type amylase in the effusion confirms PPF, distinguishing it from causes involving elevated salivary amylase. Amylase isoenzyme analysis should therefore be part of the evaluation in suspected PPF. MRCP has a sensitivity of 70%–80% for delineating fistulous tracts and ductal architecture.
