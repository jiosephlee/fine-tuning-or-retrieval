Title: Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood

Hans Vaish, Vipin Vaish, Shalini Suri, Vasudha Tomar

Abstract:

Summary: A pre-adolescent male presented with repeated right pleural effusions, chest discomfort, and respiratory compromise, with only transient relief following thoracentesis. The pleural aspirate was consistently described as dark red. Due to persistence of effusions, a right-sided intercostal drain was inserted. The patient’s history was notable for recent blunt abdominal injury caused by a bicycle handlebar. Computed tomography of the thorax revealed areas of right-sided pulmonary consolidation, a hydropneumothorax, and a complex, multi-loculated hypodense collection extending from the right paraesophageal space to the pancreatic tail. Both pleural fluid and serum analyses showed elevated amylase and lipase levels. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography demonstrated bilateral pancreatopleural fistulas, with greater involvement on the right. Management included withholding oral intake, administration of total parenteral nutrition, and treatment with Octreotide. Over ten days, pleural drainage diminished, the chest tube was withdrawn, and the patient was subsequently discharged.

Background:

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) represents a very uncommon condition, characterized by repeated, large-volume, often blood-tinged pleural effusions. This complication arises in the context of acute or chronic pancreatitis, as well as following pancreatic injury. The clinical picture can be misleading, as patients primarily exhibit respiratory symptoms attributable to recurrent pleural fluid accumulation, while the underlying cause is pancreatic in origin. This frequently results in a delayed diagnosis, as initial evaluations tend to focus on thoracic etiologies. To date, only 47 pediatric cases of PPF have been documented, in contrast to over 300 cases reported in the adult population.

Case presentation:

A young boy presented to the outpatient clinic with his mother, reporting one week of breathing difficulty and pain on the right side of his chest. The mother described a gradual worsening of his respiratory distress over the past seven days. There was no reported consanguinity in the family. She noted a history of tuberculosis exposure, as the child’s grandfather had been treated for pulmonary Koch's. The child’s diet was predominantly composed of processed, high-calorie foods, with infrequent consumption of fresh fruits, vegetables, or nutritionally balanced home-cooked meals. His weight was 44 kg, height measured 112 cm, resulting in a BMI of 35. Physical examination revealed a respiratory rate of 20 breaths per minute and mild tachypnea. Chest expansion was diminished on the right side, and percussion yielded a stony dull note in the right subscapular, infra-axillary, and axillary areas. Vocal fremitus was notably reduced on the right compared to the left. Breath sounds were decreased in the same right-sided regions. There was no evidence of pallor, cyanosis, icterus, digital clubbing, or lymph node enlargement. The abdomen was soft and non-tender, with no organomegaly. The child was alert and oriented, and systemic exam of the abdomen remained normal. Cardiovascular assessment identified tachycardia without any murmurs. Oxygen saturation on ambient air was 88%–89%, with a pulse rate of 110 beats per minute. Due to ongoing tachypnea and respiratory compromise, the patient was transferred to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit and began oxygen therapy via mask at 5 l/min. Empiric intravenous antibiotics (piperacillin-tazobactam) were initiated following the procurement of blood cultures, and a chest radiograph was obtained. Imaging demonstrated a right-sided pleural effusion with blunting of the corresponding costophrenic angle (figure 1) (Table 2). Point-of-care lung ultrasound identified an estimated one-liter pleural effusion on the right (figure 2) (Table 2). Under sterile technique, thoracocentesis of the right pleural space was performed, yielding approximately one liter of dark red fluid. Following drainage, his tachypnea subsided and room air oxygenation improved to 95%–98%. His chest pain lessened and he was weaned off oxygen support within 24 hours, then transferred to the ward the subsequent day and discharged. The aspirated pleural fluid was submitted for laboratory analysis. However, six days post-discharge, he was readmitted due to recurrence of right-sided chest pain, tachypnea, and respiratory distress. He returned to the PICU where imaging again showed a right pleural effusion. A repeat thoracocentesis retrieved about one liter of dark red effusion. Pleural fluid adenosine deaminase (ADA) was mildly elevated (Table 1), and with the history of tuberculosis contact, empirical antitubercular therapy was begun. Nevertheless, five days later, the child was hospitalized a third time with similar right-sided pleuritic pain and dyspnea. Upon re-examination of his history, he reported that he sustained blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle one month previously. Prior to planned intercostal drain (ICD) insertion, a contrast-enhanced chest CT was ordered, revealing patchy consolidations in the right lung, right-sided hydropneumothorax, and an elongated, lobulated, multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right pleura, tracking from the right paraesophageal region to the left upper retroperitoneum toward the pancreatic tail (Table 2). An ICD was placed, and roughly one liter of dark red pleural fluid was removed (figure 3). The imaging features, presence of dark red pleural effusion, and history of abdominal injury led to suspicion of pancreaticopleural fistula (PPF). Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) was arranged the same day, demonstrating pancreatitis with a tubular cystic fluid collection in the distal pancreas body and tail extending posteriorly in the retroperitoneum and tracking superiorly through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity; there was also a narrow connection to the left pleural space. The collection communicated with the distal pancreatic duct, with proximal ductal narrowing. MRCP indicated a diagnosis of PPF (figures 4-6) (table 1). Serum and pleural fluid were tested for amylase and lipase. Analysis revealed marked elevation of pleural fluid amylase (6118 U/L) and lipase (7211 U/L), corroborating the diagnosis of PPF (Table 2). Differential diagnosis:

The patient arrived with chest pain and pleural effusion, having experienced abdominal trauma one month prior. Aside from post-traumatic pancreatitis, all alternative etiologies for pleural effusion were excluded. The child lacked any previous history of fever, prolonged cough, or weight loss. Although there was a family history of Koch’s contact and the pleural fluid adenosine deaminase (ADA) was mildly increased, testing for Mycobacterium tuberculosis with GeneXpert was negative. Consequently, pulmonary Koch’s disease was excluded. The absence of fever and a neutrophilic exudate in the pleural fluid also excluded empyema. Certain malignancies, such as pulmonary tumors, leukemia, lymphoma, or metastases, can present with pleural fluid high in amylase; however, malignancy was discounted due to the lack of fever, unintentional weight loss, and normal hematological findings on blood counts and peripheral smear. Additionally, cytological examination of the pleural fluid showed no malignant cells. Boerhaave’s syndrome (esophageal perforation), another cause of amylase-rich pleural fluid, was considered unlikely since the child was having normal bowel movements and there was no report of vomiting or chest pain. Imaging did not show abdominal organs within the thoracic cavity, effectively excluding an acquired diaphragmatic hernia. Hepatic hydrothorax was ruled out as liver function tests and liver imaging were unremarkable and there was no history suggestive of chronic liver disease. Finally, with normal kidney imaging and function tests, renal causes such as hydronephrosis were excluded.

Treatment:

In the PICU, the patient received humidified oxygen via mask for respiratory support. Thoracocentesis guided by ultrasound was performed twice, with approximately one liter of blood-tinged pleural fluid aspirated during each procedure. Empirical antitubercular therapy was initiated but discontinued once the diagnosis was established on MRCP. Upon the child's third hospitalization, an intercostal drain (ICD) was placed on the right side under strict aseptic conditions, draining another liter of hemorrhagic pleural fluid into the ICD bag. As pancreatico-pleural fistula (PPF) is an infrequent diagnosis in children and lacks established management protocols, treatment followed recommendations found in case reports. Initially, conservative management was chosen: the patient was made nil per oral, and an infusion of Octreotide was started at 30 µg/kg/day (range 10–40 µg/kg/day). Total parenteral nutrition and pancreatic enzyme supplementation were also initiated. Over a span of 10 days, there was a gradual reduction in ICD drainage, which eventually ceased. The ICD was then removed, and the patient was subsequently discharged.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following hospital discharge, the patient has remained asymptomatic. He continues to attend monthly follow-up appointments with me. Since leaving the hospital, there have been no further instances of recurrent pleural effusions (S17). He has adopted lifestyle modifications, maintaining a balanced diet and engaging in regular physical activity as recommended. He has resumed his routine daily activities and consistently attends school. He is able to participate in vigorous activities such as running and playing without experiencing any problems.

Discussion:

PPF represents a highly unusual and rare complication of pancreatic disorders in children. To date, only 47 paediatric cases of PPF have been described in the literature. PPF may arise from either acute or chronic pancreatitis, or following paediatric trauma. The actual rate of occurrence in the paediatric population remains undefined, but PPF is known to complicate approximately 0.4% of all pancreatitis cases. Mutations in serine peptidase inhibitor Kazal type 1 (SPINK1) and PRSS1 are significant, being responsible for up to 30% of paediatric chronic pancreatitis cases. Blunt abdominal trauma is also a notable acquired cause, as evidenced by the fact that it accounted for 32% of 25 paediatric PPF patients reviewed by Yang et al. Cases labelled as idiopathic persist and may reflect under-recognized genetic alterations or anatomical variants in the pancreatic duct. PPF results when pancreatic secretions escape from a disrupted pancreatic duct or pseudocyst, tracking through diaphragmatic openings—most commonly the aortic or oesophageal hiatuses, or less frequently through direct erosion of the diaphragm—ultimately forming a fistulous tract into the pleural space. Diagnostic delays are common because the majority of patients exhibit thoracic symptoms and are thus investigated predominantly for pulmonary conditions, although the underlying disease is intra-abdominal. Review of published literature identified 21 pertinent reports, revealing that 68% of paediatric PPF cases presented primarily with respiratory findings, including large, recurrent, unilateral or bilateral pleural effusions, shortness of breath, and cough. Fewer than 20% reported abdominal complaints like epigastric pain, vomiting, or weight loss. Constitutional manifestations such as fever occurred in 24% of cases. For instance, the case reported by Zi Yin Shang in Frontiers in Paediatrics 2023 documented an 8-year-old boy who presented solely with a protruding left chest for two weeks; initial chest prominence as a presenting symptom is exceedingly rare. In that instance, the pleural effusion appeared to have developed slowly after abdominal trauma, while preserved pulmonary function on the unaffected side allowed gradual adaptation and a lack of classic respiratory symptoms such as dyspnoea or cough. Complicating diagnosis, this child concealed a history of abdominal injury due to bullying at school. Initial investigations, therefore, focused on tuberculosis and malignancy as potential causes of haemorrhagic pleural effusion, ultimately leading to a different diagnosis. In our own case, the child experienced repeated chest pain and recurrent right-sided pleural effusions, and the trauma history only emerged after persistent inquiry, with MRCP facilitating identification of pancreatic pathology. The often misleading ‘pulmonary mask’ of this disorder can thus divert clinicians from the correct diagnosis unless an exhaustive clinical history is obtained and appropriate imaging is performed. The accumulation of fluid in PPF results from either external trauma or infection, which can rupture the pancreatic duct or pseudocyst, allowing pancreatic fluid to leak. If rupture occurs anteriorly, this fluid accumulates in the peritoneal cavity as ascites; if posterior, the leakage follows the path of least resistance via the retroperitoneal space, progressing upward through the aortic or oesophageal hiatus into the mediastinum, breaching the pleura to produce pleural effusion. The fistula acts much like a perforated conduit, redirecting pancreatic secretions into the thoracic cavity. In the adult population, chronic pancreatitis is most frequently linked to alcohol use as a cause for PPF. Uchiyama et al’s comprehensive review of Japanese cases reported 113 occurrences, with 99% attributed to chronic pancreatitis secondary to alcohol, where chest symptoms predominated in 68% of cases. In contrast, children more often develop chronic pancreatitis due to genetic mutations, including the PRSS1 gene, cystic fibrosis transmembrane regulator, SPINK1, and chymotrypsin C genes; among these, the R122H and N291 mutations in exons 3 and 2 of PRSS1 are particularly significant. Thus, in children with recurring or chronic pancreatitis, clinicians should obtain a thorough family history and consider the possibility of inherited forms. Diagnostic delays are common since most cases undergo substantial investigation for lung disease prior to recognition of pancreatic etiology. Detection of pleural fluid amylase concentrations ≥1000 IU/L is diagnostic for PPF. Nonetheless, alternative causes—such as tuberculosis, oesophageal rupture, and pulmonary or pancreatic tumors—must be ruled out, as they can also elevate amylase. The identification of pancreatic isoenzyme in pleural fluid, as opposed to salivary isoenzyme, differentiates PPF from other etiologies; thus, amylase isoenzyme analysis should be included in the diagnostic approach to suspected PPF.
