Title: Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood

Hans Vaish, Vipin Vaish, Shalini Suri, Vasudha Tomar

Abstract:

Summary: An older male child was evaluated for recurring right-sided pleural effusions accompanied by chest pain and difficulty breathing, which temporarily resolved following thoracentesis. The aspirated pleural fluid consistently exhibited a dark red coloration. Given the frequent effusions, a right intercostal drain was inserted. Further history elicited recent blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handlebar. Chest computed tomography demonstrated patchy consolidation in the right lung, hydropneumothorax, and a multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right paraesophageal area toward the pancreatic tail. Laboratory assessment showed elevated amylase and lipase levels in both serum and pleural fluid samples. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography identified bilateral pancreatopleural fistulas, more pronounced on the right. Management involved keeping the patient nil per os, providing total parenteral nutrition, and administering Octreotide. Within 10 days, the volume of pleural drainage diminished, the chest tube was withdrawn, and the child was discharged.

Background:

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) represents a very uncommon condition characterized by the development of recurrent, large, and blood-tinged pleural effusions. This complication has been documented in the setting of acute and chronic pancreatitis as well as following pancreatic injury. The clinical presentation often poses a diagnostic challenge since patients primarily exhibit symptoms of respiratory compromise secondary to repeated pleural effusions, while the underlying etiology originates from pancreatic pathology. As a result, diagnostic workup is frequently focused on thoracic causes, contributing to delays in correctly identifying the source. In the pediatric population, there have been only 47 reported cases of PPF, compared to over 300 cases documented in adults.

Case presentation:

A pediatric patient presented to the outpatient clinic with his mother, reporting a one-week history of dyspnea and right-sided chest pain. According to the mother, the respiratory discomfort had worsened over the past seven days. There was no history of parental consanguinity. The mother disclosed that the child had been in contact with his grandfather, who had previously received treatment for pulmonary Koch’s. Dietary history indicated frequent consumption of processed, calorie-dense foods, and minimal intake of fruits, vegetables, or balanced home-cooked meals. The child’s weight measured 44 kg, his height 112 cm, and body mass index calculated at 35.

Upon examination, his respiratory rate was 20 per minute with mild tachypnea. Right-sided chest excursion was reduced. Percussion revealed a stony dull note in the right subscapular, right infra-axillary, and axillary areas. Vocal fremitus was notably diminished on the right in comparison to the left. Decreased breath sounds were observed in the same right-sided regions. No signs of pallor, cyanosis, icterus, clubbing, or lymphadenopathy were evident. The abdomen was soft, and hepatosplenomegaly was absent. The child remained alert and oriented. The abdomen continued to be soft without hepatosplenomegaly. Cardiovascular evaluation showed tachycardia but no murmurs. Oxygen saturation was measured at 88%–89% on room air, and heart rate was 110/min.

Given the tachypnea and respiratory distress, he was admitted to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit and initiated on oxygen therapy by mask at 5 l/min. A chest X-ray was ordered, and empirical antibiotics (piperacillin-tazobactam) were started following collection of blood cultures. Imaging identified a right-sided pleural effusion with blunting of the right costophrenic angle (figure 1) (Table 2). Bedside lung ultrasound estimated approximately one litre of pleural fluid (figure 2) (Table 2). A right-sided thoracocentesis was performed under aseptic technique, yielding roughly one litre of dark red pleural fluid. Post-procedure, tachypnea resolved and oxygen saturation on room air improved to 95%–98%. Chest pain subsided, and he discontinued oxygen support within 24 hours. He was transferred to the ward the next day and subsequently discharged. The aspirated pleural fluid was sent for analysis.

However, six days later, he was re-admitted with similar symptoms: right-sided chest pain, tachypnea, and difficulty breathing. He was taken back to PICU, where chest X-ray once more showed a right-sided pleural effusion. Another thoracocentesis was carried out, draining about one litre of dark red fluid. Pleural fluid adenosine deaminase (ADA) was mildly elevated (Table 1), and in view of the positive Koch’s contact, empirical antitubercular therapy was initiated.

The child presented for a third admission five days later with persistent right-sided chest pain and dyspnea. On re-evaluation, he revealed a history of blunt abdominal trauma by a bicycle handle one month prior. A contrast-enhanced chest CT was ordered before placing an intercostal drain (ICD). Imaging demonstrated patchy airspace consolidations in the right lung, right-sided hydropneumothorax, and a lobulated, multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right pleural cavity, along the right paraoesophageal region down to the upper left retroperitoneum near the tail of the pancreas (Table 2). An ICD was inserted, and approximately one litre of dark red pleural fluid was drained (figure 3). The CT findings, coupled with the pleural fluid color and trauma history, raised suspicion for a pancreaticopleural fistula (PPF).

MRCP performed the same day demonstrated pancreatitis, with a tubular cystic collection at the distal body and tail of the pancreas, extending posteriorly into the retroperitoneum and superiorly through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity. A slender communication to the left pleural space was also visualized. The collection communicated with the distal pancreatic duct, showing ductal narrowing proximal to this connection. These MRCP findings supported PPF diagnosis (figures 4-6) (table 1). Serum and pleural fluid amylase and lipase levels were obtained. Pleural fluid analysis revealed markedly elevated amylase (6118 U/L) and lipase (7211 U/L) values (table 2), confirming PPF.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient exhibited pleural effusion accompanied by chest pain and had a confirmed incident of abdominal trauma 1 month prior. After considering post-traumatic pancreatitis, alternative etiologies of pleural effusion were excluded. The child had no previous history of fever, chronic cough, or weight loss. Although there was documented exposure to Koch’s in the household and a slight increase in pleural fluid ADA, GeneXpert testing for MTB yielded negative results. Consequently, pulmonary Koch’s was excluded. The absence of fever and lack of neutrophilic exudate in pleural fluid eliminated the possibility of empyema. Malignancies such as lung cancer, leukemia, lymphoma, and metastatic tumors can present with amylase-rich pleural fluid; however, normal blood counts, peripheral smears, absence of weight loss, and fever, along with negative pleural fluid cytology for malignancy, made this diagnosis unlikely. Boerhaave’s syndrome (esophageal perforation) is another cause of elevated pleural fluid amylase, but was discounted due to the patient’s normal stool passage and lack of chest pain or vomiting history. Acquired diaphragmatic hernia was not considered because imaging did not show abdominal organs within the thoracic cavity. Hepatic hydrothorax was excluded due to normal liver imaging, liver function tests, and absence of chronic liver disease history. Renal disease or hydronephrosis were also excluded based on unremarkable renal function and imaging findings.

Treatment:

In the PICU, the patient received humidified oxygen via mask for respiratory support. Thoracocentesis was performed twice under ultrasound guidance, with approximately one liter of blood-tinged pleural fluid evacuated during each procedure. Antitubercular therapy was initiated empirically, but discontinued after diagnosis was confirmed with MRCP. Upon the third hospitalization, an intercostal drain (ICD) was inserted under strict aseptic conditions on the right side, yielding roughly another liter of blood-stained pleural fluid collected in the drain bag. Due to the rarity of PPF in pediatric cases and the absence of established treatment protocols, management relied on published case reports. Conservative measures were employed initially; the patient was made nil per oral and began receiving Octreotide infusion at a dose of 30 µg/kg/day (range 10–40 µg/kg/day). Total parenteral nutrition and pancreatic enzyme supplementation were started. Over ten days, the drainage output from the ICD progressively decreased and ultimately ceased. The ICD was subsequently removed, and the patient was discharged.

Outcome and follow-up:

Since leaving the hospital, the patient has experienced no recurrence of symptoms. He attends scheduled monthly follow-up appointments. No additional episodes of recurrent pleural effusions have occurred since his discharge (S17). He has adopted recommended lifestyle changes, following a nutritious diet and engaging in consistent physical activity. He has resumed his routine and attends school without interruption. The patient is able to participate in vigorous activities, such as running and playing, without any limitations.

Discussion:

PPF is a highly unusual and rarely encountered complication associated with pancreatic disorders in children. To date, only 47 cases of paediatric PPF have been reported in the literature. This condition may arise in both acute and chronic pancreatitis or following trauma in paediatric patients. The precise frequency in children is not established, but overall, PPF complicates about 0.4% of pancreatitis cases. Mutations in the Serine peptidase inhibitor Kazal type 1 (SPINK1) and PRSS1 genes are prominent contributors, accounting for approximately 30% of paediatric chronic pancreatitis instances. Blunt abdominal trauma represents another significant, non-genetic factor, making up 32% of the cohort of 25 patients evaluated by Yang et al. Idiopathic cases still occur, potentially related to unidentified genetic or ductal anomalies. Pancreatic fluid may pass from a damaged duct or pseudocyst through diaphragmatic hiatuses, typically the aortic or oesophageal, or, less frequently, through direct diaphragmatic erosion, establishing a fistulous channel to the pleural space. These cases are commonly overlooked since most present with thoracic manifestations, prompting exhaustive investigation for pulmonary disease, while the primary pathology resides in the abdomen. Literature review yielded 21 pertinent reports, of which 68% described patients with mainly respiratory presentations, such as large, recurrent unilateral or bilateral pleural effusions, shortness of breath, and cough. Fewer than 20% presented with gastrointestinal complaints like upper abdominal pain, nausea, and weight loss, and 24% displayed constitutional symptoms, including fever. Zi Yin Shang, in Frontiers in Paediatrics 2023, described an instance of PPF in an 8-year-old boy whose only complaint was a protruding left chest for 2 weeks. Cases presenting initially with a chest bulge are exceedingly uncommon. In that example, the pleural effusion was likely a slow consequence of earlier abdominal trauma. The unaffected lung’s compensatory function permitted gradual adaptation, so typical respiratory symptoms such as cough or shortness of breath were absent. Additionally, the patient had concealed a history of abdominal trauma due to bullying at school. He was investigated for tuberculosis and malignancy as the cause of hemorrhagic pleural effusion before a different diagnosis was made. In our experience, the child had repeated pulmonary complaints including chest pain and recurrent right-sided pleural effusions. Upon taking the history multiple times and using MRCP findings, the abdominal trauma history was revealed, directing attention to a pancreatic source. This condition therefore may masquerade as a ‘pulmonary mask’, leading clinicians astray unless a comprehensive history and appropriate imaging are undertaken. In PPF, fluid accumulation results from external injury or infection, causing rupture of the pancreatic duct or pseudocyst, with subsequent leakage of pancreatic secretions. If the rupture is at the anterior pancreas, fluid leaks into the peritoneal cavity, producing ascites; if posteriorly, it escapes into the retroperitoneal space and, following the path of least resistance upwards, enters the mediastinum through the aortic or oesophageal hiatus and breaches the pleura to form a pleural effusion. An internal pancreatic fistula functions as an abnormal conduit, redirecting pancreatic fluid into the chest. In adults, chronic pancreatitis leading to PPF is usually related to alcohol use. Uchiyama et al performed the most extensive national review, reporting 113 cases from Japanese sources, showing that 99% were linked to alcoholic chronic pancreatitis, and chest symptoms dominated in 68%. Conversely, paediatric cases most often stem from genetic factors. Notable mutations include the PRSS1 gene, cystic fibrosis transmembrane regulator gene, SPINK1 gene, and chymotrypsin C gene, with PRSS1 mutations—specifically R122H and N291 in exon 3 and exon 2, respectively—being particularly relevant. When pancreatitis in children is recurrent or chronic, it is crucial to obtain a detailed family history and evaluate for hereditary forms. Diagnostic delays are frequent as patients often undergo exhaustive pulmonary assessment before a pancreatic cause is considered. A pleural fluid amylase level ≥1000 IU/L is considered diagnostic for PPF. Differential diagnoses include tuberculosis, oesophageal perforation, and malignant lesions of the lung or pancreas, as these also result in elevated pleural fluid amylase. Amylase typing in pleural fluid identifies pancreatic-origin amylase in PPF, whereas other causes yield salivary-type amylase. Pleural fluid amylase typing may thus be useful in cases where PPF is suspected. MRCP offers a sensitivity of 70%–80% in visualizing fistula and duct structure.
