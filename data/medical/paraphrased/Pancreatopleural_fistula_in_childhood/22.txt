Title: Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood

Hans Vaish, Vipin Vaish, Shalini Suri, Vasudha Tomar

Abstract:

A pre-adolescent male developed repeated right-sided pleural effusions accompanied by chest discomfort and difficulty breathing, with transient relief following fluid aspiration. The pleural fluid consistently appeared dark red in color. Due to persistent recurrence, a right intercostal drain was inserted. His medical history included recent abdominal injury caused by a bicycle handlebar. Chest computed tomography revealed patchy consolidation in the right lung, hydropneumothorax, and a multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right paraesophageal area to the pancreatic tail. Both serum and pleural fluid assays demonstrated elevated amylase and lipase levels. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography identified bilateral pancreatopleural fistulas, predominantly on the right side. Management consisted of withholding oral intake, initiating total parenteral nutrition, and administering Octreotide. Drain output decreased within ten days, enabling removal of the chest tube, and the patient was subsequently discharged.

Background:

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is a very uncommon condition, characterized by repeated episodes of large, blood-tinged pleural effusions. It can occur in individuals with acute pancreatitis, chronic pancreatitis, or following pancreatic trauma. The diagnosis is often challenging because patients exhibit respiratory symptoms secondary to recurring pleural effusions, yet the underlying issue originates in the pancreas. This may lead to delays in identification as clinical focus tends to be directed towards potential thoracic causes. In pediatric patients, only 47 instances of PPF have been documented, while adults have accounted for more than 300 reported cases.

Case presentation:

A child was brought to the outpatient clinic by his mother, reporting one week of breathing difficulty and right-sided chest pain. The mother noted that the symptoms had progressively worsened over the past seven days. There was no consanguinity reported in the family. According to his mother, the child had been exposed to tuberculosis via his grandfather, who had previously received treatment for pulmonary Koch’s. The child’s diet was characterized by frequent consumption of processed, high-calorie foods and inadequate intake of fresh fruits, vegetables, and balanced meals at home. His weight was 44 kg, height measured 112 cm, and his BMI was 35. On physical examination, his respiratory rate was 20 breaths per minute, with evidence of mild tachypnoea. Chest expansion was diminished on the right side. Percussion revealed stony dullness over the right subscapular, infra-axillary, and axillary areas. Vocal fremitus was markedly decreased on the right when compared with the left. Breath sounds were also reduced in the aforementioned regions on the right. Examination found no pallor, cyanosis, jaundice, clubbing, or lymphadenopathy. The abdomen was soft and non-tender with no enlargement of the liver or spleen. The child was alert and oriented. Cardiovascular assessment showed tachycardia but no murmurs. Oxygen saturation on room air was between 88% and 89%, and heart rate was 110 bpm. Due to tachypnoea and respiratory distress, he was admitted to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit and provided with oxygen at 5 l/min via mask. A chest X-ray was ordered, and after collecting blood cultures, empirical treatment with piperacillin-tazobactam was initiated. Chest radiography demonstrated a right-sided pleural effusion with blunting of the costophrenic angle (figure 1) (Table 2). Point-of-care lung ultrasonography estimated roughly one litre of pleural fluid (figure 2) (Table 2). Under sterile conditions, aspiration of the right pleural effusion yielded about one litre of dark red fluid. The tachypnoea resolved following thoracentesis, and the child’s room air oxygen saturation rose to 95%–98%. Chest pain improved and supplemental oxygen was discontinued within 24 hours. He was subsequently transferred to the ward the next day and then discharged. The aspirated pleural fluid was sent for analysis.

Six days later, the child was re-admitted with similar symptoms: right-sided chest pain, tachypnoea, and difficulty breathing. He was moved to the PICU again, where chest radiography once more revealed a right pleural effusion. Repeat thoracocentesis retrieved approximately one litre of dark red fluid. Pleural fluid Adenosine Deaminase (ADA) was mildly increased (Table 1), and, given the previous exposure to tuberculosis, empiric antitubercular therapy was started. The child was admitted a third time five days after that, again with right-sided chest pain and respiratory difficulty. On reevaluating the history, the child disclosed having suffered blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle about one month earlier. Prior to insertion of an intercostal drainage (ICD), a contrast-enhanced CT chest was performed, revealing patchy areas of airspace consolidation in the right lung, right-sided hydropneumothorax, and an elongated, multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right pleural space at the right paraoesophageal level to the upper left retroperitoneum near the pancreatic tail (Table 2). An ICD was inserted and approximately one litre of dark red pleural fluid was evacuated (figure 3). The CT results, combined with the dark red pleural fluid and the trauma history, raised suspicion for a pancreaticopleural fistula (PPF). MRCP was performed that same day, demonstrating pancreatitis with a tubular cystic collection at the distal pancreatic body and tail, extending posteriorly in the retroperitoneum and tracking superiorly through the oesophageal hiatus to the right pleural cavity. There was also a narrow connection with the left pleural space. The collection communicated with the distal pancreatic duct, with proximal duct narrowing. MRCP findings were indicative of PPF (figures 4-6) (table 1). Serum and pleural fluid amylase and lipase were measured; pleural fluid studies showed markedly raised amylase (6118 U/L) and lipase (7211 U/L) levels (table 2), which supported the PPF diagnosis. Antitubercular therapy was discontinued. The child was kept nil per oral and managed with total parenteral nutrition. Octreotide infusion was initiated at 30 µg/kg/day (range 10–40 µg/kg/day). Differential diagnosis:

The patient arrived with symptoms of pleural effusion and chest pain, along with a documented episode of abdominal trauma one month prior. After considering and excluding alternative etiologies for pleural effusion aside from post-traumatic pancreatitis, it was determined that this child had no previous complaints of fever, persistent cough, or weight loss. While there was a documented family history of contact with Koch’s disease and a slight elevation in pleural fluid ADA levels, GeneXpert testing for MTB yielded negative results, excluding pulmonary Koch’s as the underlying cause. The absence of febrile episodes and the lack of neutrophilic exudate in the pleural fluid made empyema unlikely. Although conditions such as lung cancer, leukemia, lymphoma, or metastatic tumors may present with pleural fluid rich in amylase, the absence of fever, weight loss, normal hematological parameters, and a normal peripheral blood smear did not support malignancy. Furthermore, pleural fluid cytology did not identify any malignant cells. Boerhaave’s syndrome, known for causing amylase-enriched pleural effusions due to esophageal rupture, was dismissed given the child’s normal stool passage, absence of chest pain, and no episodes of vomiting. Acquired diaphragmatic hernia was not supported by imaging, as no abdominal structures were visualized in the thoracic cavity. Hepatic hydrothorax was excluded due to unremarkable liver function tests, normal liver imaging, and a lack of history suggestive of chronic liver disease. Renal pathology, including hydronephrosis, was ruled out based on normal renal imaging and renal function assessments.

Treatment:

The patient received humidified oxygen via mask for respiratory support in the PICU. Ultrasound-guided thoracocentesis was performed twice, each occasion yielding approximately one liter of blood-tinged pleural fluid. Empirical antitubercular therapy was initiated but discontinued upon definitive diagnosis obtained from MRCP. During the third admission, a right-sided ICD was placed using strict aseptic technique, with another one liter of blood-stained pleural fluid drained into the collection bag. Due to the rarity of PPF in pediatric cases, standardized treatment protocols are not established; management is informed by individual case reports. Initially, conservative therapy was employed: the child was made nil per oral and commenced on Octreotide infusion at a dose of 30 µg/kg/day (range: 10–40 µg/kg/day). Total parenteral nutrition and supplementation with pancreatic enzymes were provided. Over the ensuing 10 days, the volume of fluid drained via the ICD progressively declined and eventually ceased. The ICD was subsequently removed, and the patient was discharged from hospital care.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following his hospital discharge, the patient has experienced no symptoms. He continues to attend scheduled monthly follow-up appointments with me. Since leaving the hospital, there have not been any further instances of recurrent pleural effusions (S17). The child has adapted his lifestyle and now maintains a balanced diet and participates in routine physical activity, as previously recommended. He has resumed normal daily routines and attends school consistently. He is able to engage in vigorous activities, such as running and playing, without limitation.

Discussion:

PPF is an exceptionally uncommon complication arising from pancreatic disease within paediatric patients. To date, only 47 cases of PPF in children have been reported in the literature. It can occur in the context of both acute and chronic pancreatitis, as well as following paediatric abdominal trauma. The precise frequency in the paediatric demographic remains undetermined; overall, PPF affects approximately 0.4% of pancreatitis cases. Genetic mutations such as SPINK1 and PRSS1 are significant, accounting for up to 30% of paediatric chronic pancreatitis cases. Blunt trauma to the abdomen represents another critical non-genetic precipitating factor, making up 32% of a cohort of 25 paediatric patients as described by Yang et al. Idiopathic cases persist, likely due to under-recognised genetic or ductal anomalies. In PPF, pancreatic fluid can escape from a ruptured duct or pseudocyst, passing through diaphragmatic openings, most commonly the aortic or oesophageal hiatus, or, less frequently, by directly eroding the diaphragm, establishing a fistula to the pleural cavity. The majority of instances are overlooked, as affected children typically display thoracic manifestations, and diagnostic efforts are concentrated on identifying pulmonary causes rather than abdominal origins. Literature review revealed 21 pertinent publications, indicating that 68% of paediatric PPF cases presented with primary respiratory complaints, including large, recurrent unilateral or bilateral pleural effusions, dyspnoea, and cough. Fewer than 20% exhibited abdominal signs such as epigastric pain, vomiting, or weight loss, and 24% had constitutional features, including fever. Zi Yin Shang's report in Frontiers in Paediatrics 2023 described an 8-year-old boy with PPF, whose initial symptom was a left-sided chest bulge lasting 2 weeks. Presentation with chest bulging as a primary symptom is exceedingly uncommon. In this instance, pleural effusion developed gradually following abdominal trauma, and the patient’s unaffected lung compensated, leading to a lack of typical respiratory complaints like cough or dyspnoea. Additionally, the child concealed the history of abdominal trauma due to bullying at school. Diagnostic evaluation for haemorrhagic pleural effusion included tuberculosis and oncology studies, but the underlying cause differed. In our case, the patient also experienced repeated pulmonary symptoms, including chest pain and recurrent collections of right-sided pleural fluid. The history of abdominal trauma was uncovered only after multiple interviews with the boy, and MRCP findings pointed towards a pancreatic etiology. Therefore, PPF can present as a "pulmonary mask," potentially leading clinicians away from the actual diagnosis unless a comprehensive history and appropriate imaging are undertaken. Fluid accumulation in PPF stems from external trauma or bacterial infection, causing rupture of the pancreatic duct or pseudocyst and the consequent leakage of pancreatic secretions. When the rupture is anterior, pancreatic fluid enters the abdominal cavity, resulting in ascites. In the case of posterior rupture, the fluid enters the retroperitoneal space, migrates along the path of least resistance upwards into the mediastinum through the aortic or oesophageal hiatus, and eventually breaches the pleura to cause pleural effusion. The internal pancreatic fistula functions as a leaking conduit, diverting pancreatic fluid into the thoracic compartment. In adults, chronic pancreatitis is most commonly caused by alcohol use, resulting in PPF. Uchiyama et al conducted an extensive review in Japan, documenting 113 cases from the national literature, finding that 99% of cases originated from chronic alcoholic pancreatitis, with predominant chest symptoms in 68%. In contrast, in paediatric patients, genetic causes predominate as the etiology for chronic pancreatitis. Mutations in genes such as PRSS1, CFTR, SPINK1, and chymotrypsin C are recognised contributors to hereditary pancreatitis, with PRSS1 exon 3 R122H and exon 2 N291 mutations most frequently cited. For children with recurrent or chronic pancreatitis, a thorough family history should be assessed and hereditary pancreatitis considered. Diagnosis is typically delayed since extensive pulmonary investigations are commonly undertaken before a pancreatic origin is suspected. Pleural fluid amylase ≥1000 IU/L is diagnostic for PPF. It is critical to rule out conditions such as tuberculosis, oesophageal perforation, or malignancies of the lung or pancreas, as they also yield elevated pleural fluid amylase. PPF is distinguished by pancreatic type amylase in pleural fluid, whereas other causes with increased amylase feature the salivary type. Typing pleural fluid amylase can assist in the diagnostic evaluation. MRCP provides 70–80% sensitivity for detecting the fistulous tract and visualising ductal anatomy.
