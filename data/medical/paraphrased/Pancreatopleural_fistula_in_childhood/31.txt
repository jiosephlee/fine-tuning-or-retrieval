Title: Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood

Hans Vaish, Vipin Vaish, Shalini Suri, Vasudha Tomar

Abstract:

Summary: An older boy presented with repeated right-sided pleural effusions, accompanied by chest pain and episodes of respiratory distress, which showed transient relief after thoracentesis. The pleural fluid extracted was persistently dark red in color. Due to ongoing effusions, a right intercostal drain was inserted. Further history taking revealed that he had recently sustained abdominal trauma from a bicycle handlebar. Chest computed tomography demonstrated right patchy lung consolidation, hydropneumothorax, and a multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right paraesophageal area towards the tail of the pancreas. Amylase and lipase levels were raised in both serum and pleural fluid. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography confirmed the presence of bilateral pancreatopleural fistulas, predominantly on the right. Management included keeping the patient nil per os, initiation of total parenteral nutrition, and administration of Octreotide. Over the course of 10 days, pleural drainage diminished, the chest tube was withdrawn, and the child was subsequently discharged.

Background:

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is a highly uncommon condition characterized by repeated episodes of large, blood-tinged pleural effusions. It has been identified in individuals with acute or chronic pancreatitis, as well as following pancreatic injury. The diagnosis can be challenging, as patients typically present with respiratory symptoms attributable to persistent pleural effusions, even though the primary abnormality originates from the pancreas. This often leads to delays in diagnosis while investigations focus on potential thoracic causes. To date, there have been just 47 documented pediatric cases of PPF, in contrast to over 300 cases reported in adults.

Case presentation:

A young boy presented to the outpatient clinic with his mother, complaining of breathing difficulties and right-sided chest pain that had persisted for one week. According to his mother, his respiratory discomfort had gradually worsened over the previous seven days. There was no reported consanguinity in the family. The mother noted a prior history of exposure to Koch’s (tuberculosis) via the child’s grandfather, who had previously received appropriate treatment for pulmonary Koch’s. The child’s dietary history indicated frequent intake of processed, calorie-dense foods, with minimal consumption of fresh fruits, vegetables, or balanced homemade meals. His measurements included a weight of 44 kg, height of 112 cm, and a BMI of 35. Physical examination found a respiratory rate of 20/min, with evidence of mild tachypnoea. On inspection, chest expansion was decreased on the right, and percussion revealed stony dullness in the right subscapular, infra-axillary, and axillary regions. Vocal fremitus was much diminished on the right compared to the left. Breath sounds were diminished in the right subscapular and infra-axillary regions. No signs of pallor, cyanosis, jaundice, clubbing, or lymphadenopathy were observed. Abdominal examination showed a soft, non-tender abdomen without hepatosplenomegaly. The child was alert and fully oriented. Cardiovascular findings included tachycardia but no murmurs. Oxygen saturation was between 88% and 89% on ambient air, with a heart rate of 110/min. Due to ongoing tachypnoea and respiratory compromise, he was transferred to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit (PICU) and received oxygen via mask at 5 l/min. A chest X-ray was ordered, and after obtaining blood cultures, empirical piperacillin-tazobactam was initiated. The chest radiograph showed a right-sided pleural effusion with blunting of the right costophrenic angle (figure 1) (Table 2). Bedside lung ultrasonography detected a pleural effusion measuring about one litre (figure 2) (Table 2). Under strict aseptic technique, right-sided thoracentesis was performed, yielding approximately one litre of dark red pleural fluid. Post-procedure, the child’s tachypnoea resolved and room air oxygen saturation improved to 95%–98%. Chest pain decreased, and he was weaned from oxygen within 24 hours. He was transferred back to the ward the following day and subsequently discharged. The aspirated pleural fluid was sent for analysis. Six days later, the child was readmitted with recurrent right-sided chest pain, tachypnoea, and respiratory distress. He was again managed in the PICU, where repeat chest X-ray confirmed a recurrent right-sided pleural effusion. Another thoracentesis was performed, draining roughly one litre of dark red fluid. Pleural fluid adenosine deaminase (ADA) was mildly elevated (Table 1), and considering the history of Koch’s exposure, empirical antitubercular therapy was started. Nevertheless, the child was hospitalized for a third time five days later, with similar symptoms of right-sided chest pain and breathing difficulty. On further history taking, the patient revealed that he had experienced blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handlebar one month earlier. A contrast-enhanced CT chest was ordered prior to insertion of an intercostal drain (ICD). The scan showed patchy areas of consolidation in the right lung, right-sided hydropneumothorax, and a large, multiloculated, hypodense collection originating from the right pleural space and tracking from the right paraoesophageal region to the upper left retroperitoneum as far as the tail of the pancreas (Table 2). An ICD was inserted, draining about one litre of dark red pleural fluid (figure 3). The constellation of CT findings, the dark red pleural aspirate, and the trauma history suggested a pancreaticopleural fistula (PPF). Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) performed the same day demonstrated pancreatitis with a tubular cystic collection in the distal pancreatic body and tail, tracking posteriorly into the retroperitoneum and extending superiorly via the oesophageal hiatus to the right pleural cavity; a narrow communication with the left pleural space was also visualized. The collection was in continuity with the distal pancreatic duct, with proximal ductal narrowing. MRCP was consistent with the diagnosis of PPF (figures 4–6) (Table 1). Amylase and lipase levels were measured in both serum and pleural fluid, and pleural fluid analysis revealed marked elevation of amylase (6118 U/L) and lipase (7211 U/L) (Table 2), corroborating the diagnosis of PPF. Differential diagnosis:

The patient exhibited pleural effusion and chest pain, with a clear history of abdominal trauma occurring one month prior. After excluding alternative etiologies for pleural effusion, post-traumatic pancreatitis was considered. The child lacked symptoms such as fever, persistent cough, or weight loss. There was family exposure to Koch’s, and pleural fluid ADA levels were slightly increased; however, the GeneXpert assay for MTB yielded a negative result, thereby excluding pulmonary Koch’s. The absence of fever and lack of a neutrophilic exudate in the pleural fluid negated the possibility of empyema. Although malignancies—including pulmonary, leukemia, lymphoma, or metastatic tumors—may present with pleural fluid elevated in amylase, no fever or weight loss was observed, and the complete blood count along with peripheral smear were unremarkable, discounting malignancy. Additionally, cytologic evaluation of the pleural fluid revealed no malignant cells. Boerhaave’s syndrome (esophageal perforation), which may also cause elevated amylase in pleural fluid, was excluded since the child exhibited normal bowel movements and denied any chest pain or vomiting. The possibility of acquired diaphragmatic hernia was disregarded because abdominal organs were not visible in the thoracic cavity on imaging studies. Hepatic hydrothorax was ruled out based on normal liver function tests and imaging, as well as a lack of chronic liver disease history. Normal findings in renal imaging and function also eliminated renal pathology or hydronephrosis from consideration.

Treatment:

In the pediatric intensive care unit, the patient received humidified oxygen via facemask for respiratory support. Thoracocentesis guided by ultrasound was performed twice; approximately one liter of pleural fluid with a bloody appearance was collected on each occasion. Initially, empirical antitubercular therapy was initiated, but discontinued following diagnosis confirmation through MRCP. During a third hospitalization, an intercostal drain (ICD) was placed on the right side under strict aseptic technique, again resulting in the drainage of about one liter of blood-tinged pleural fluid into the ICD collection bag. Given the rarity of PPF in pediatric patients and the absence of established management protocols, therapy was guided by previous case reports. Conservative management was chosen initially: the patient was made nil by mouth and began receiving Octreotide infusion at a dosage of 30 µg/kg/day (within the range of 10–40 µg/kg/day). The patient was commenced on total parenteral nutrition and supplemental pancreatic enzymes. Over a period of 10 days, drainage from the ICD progressively decreased and ultimately ceased. The ICD was then removed, and the patient was subsequently discharged.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following hospital discharge, the patient has not experienced any symptoms. He attends scheduled monthly follow-up appointments with me. Since leaving the hospital, there have been no additional occurrences of recurrent pleural effusions (S17). He has adopted recommended lifestyle modifications, now maintaining a balanced diet and participating in regular physical activity. He has resumed his usual routines and is going to school consistently. He is able to engage in vigorous activities such as running and playing without limitation.

Discussion:

PPF represents a highly unusual and rarely encountered complication of pancreatic disease in children. To date, only 47 cases of pediatric PPF have been reported in the literature. This condition may arise in the context of either acute or chronic pancreatitis, or following pediatric trauma. The precise incidence in this age group remains undefined, although it complicates approximately 0.4% of pancreatitis cases overall. Genetic alterations, particularly in the serine peptidase inhibitor Kazal type 1 (SPINK1) and PRSS1 genes, are significant contributors, accounting for up to 30% of chronic pancreatitis among pediatric patients. Blunt abdominal trauma serves as a notable non-genetic cause, constituting 32% of cases in a 25-patient series analyzed by Yang et al. There are also idiopathic presentations, possibly reflecting unidentified genetic or ductal variations. 

Pancreatic fluid from a disrupted duct or pseudocyst may traverse natural diaphragmatic openings—most commonly the aortic or esophageal hiatus—or, less frequently, penetrate directly through the diaphragm, creating a fistulous connection with the pleural space. Many of these cases are overlooked because clinical resources often focus on diagnosing a pulmonary origin for symptoms, while the underlying abnormality is actually abdominal. Review of published cases revealed 21 articles, of which 68% described patients presenting primarily with respiratory manifestations—such as massive, recurrent unilateral or bilateral pleural effusions, shortness of breath, and cough. Fewer than 20% exhibited abdominal complaints including epigastric pain, vomiting, or weight loss. Additionally, 24% reported constitutional signs like fever. One case described by Zi Yin Shang in Frontiers in Pediatrics 2023 involved an 8-year-old boy who initially had only a protruding left chest for two weeks, a rare presenting symptom for PPF. In this patient, pleural effusion developed gradually following abdominal trauma. The compensatory function of the unaffected lung enabled adaptation without typical respiratory complaints. Moreover, the child concealed his history of trauma due to bullying. Hemorrhagic pleural effusion prompted workup for tuberculosis and malignancy, but the eventual diagnosis diverged. In our own case, the patient developed recurrent pulmonary symptoms—specifically, chest pain and repeated right-sided pleural fluid accumulations. Detailed history taking and MRCP findings eventually pointed to an underlying pancreatic abnormality after multiple interviews revealed trauma.

Accordingly, PPF may manifest with a 'Pulmonary mask,' potentially misleading clinicians toward alternative diagnoses unless thorough history and appropriate imaging are performed. Fluid accumulation in PPF results from traumatic or infectious rupture of the pancreatic duct or a pseudocyst, leading to leakage of pancreatic secretions. Anterior duct disruption allows leakage into the abdominal cavity and causes ascites. In contrast, posterior duct rupture releases fluid into the retroperitoneum, which then ascends through the aortic or esophageal hiatus into the mediastinum and breaches the pleural space, creating a pleural effusion. Internal pancreatic fistulas effectively divert pancreatic exocrine fluids into the chest, similar to a defective pipe.

Among adults, chronic pancreatitis resulting from alcohol predominates as the cause of PPF. A comprehensive Japanese literature review by Uchiyama et al reported 113 cases, finding 99% were associated with chronic alcoholic pancreatitis, with respiratory symptoms noted in 68%. In children, however, hereditary etiologies prevail. Mutations in PRSS1, CF transmembrane regulator, SPINK1, and chymotrypsin C genes are implicated, particularly R122H and N291 mutations in exons 3 and 2, respectively, of PRSS1. Recurring or chronic pediatric pancreatitis should prompt consideration of familial causes, warranting careful family history evaluation.

Diagnosis of PPF is often delayed as patients generally undergo comprehensive pulmonary workup before the pancreatic source is suspected. A pleural fluid amylase of ≥1000 IU/L is specific for PPF. It is essential to exclude tuberculosis, esophageal perforation, and pulmonary or pancreatic malignancies, which may also elevate pleural amylase. PPF is characterized by pancreatic amylase in the effusion, while other conditions typically involve the salivary variant; thus, amylase subtype testing in pleural fluid may aid diagnosis.

MRCP has a sensitivity between 70% and 80% for identifying fistulous tracts and pancreatic ductal anatomy. CT imaging effectively detects pancreatic calcifications, pseudocysts, or duct dilation, though it identifies fistulas in only 33%–47% of cases. ERCP is considered the gold standard for treatment planning, with fistula identification rates in children ranging from 38% to 78%.
