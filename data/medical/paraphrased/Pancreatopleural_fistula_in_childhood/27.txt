Title: Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood

Hans Vaish, Vipin Vaish, Shalini Suri, Vasudha Tomar

Abstract:

Summary: An older child presented with repeated right-sided pleural effusions, chest pain, and difficulty breathing, which temporarily subsided following aspiration. The pleural fluid remained consistently dark red in color. Ongoing effusions prompted placement of a right intercostal drain. Further history revealed recent blunt abdominal trauma caused by a bicycle handle. Chest CT demonstrated right patchy areas of consolidation, hydropneumothorax, and a multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right paraesophageal area to the pancreatic tail. Both serum and pleural fluid tests showed elevated amylase and lipase. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography identified bilateral pancreatopleural fistulas, more pronounced on the right than the left. Management included keeping the patient nil by mouth, providing total parenteral nutrition, and administering Octreotide. Over 10 days, drainage lessened, the chest tube was taken out, and the patient was discharged.

Background:

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is a very uncommon condition that manifests with repeated, substantial, and often bloody pleural effusions. It can occur in the setting of acute pancreatitis, chronic pancreatitis, or following injury to the pancreas. The diagnosis of PPF may be challenging because patients typically exhibit symptoms of respiratory distress caused by recurrent pleural fluid accumulation, directing clinical attention toward thoracic causes, even though the underlying issue originates in the pancreas. In the pediatric population, only 47 instances of PPF have been documented, compared to over 300 cases reported in adults.

Case presentation:

A pediatric patient presented to the outpatient clinic accompanied by his mother, reporting breathing difficulties and right-sided chest pain persisting for one week. According to the mother, the child’s respiratory symptoms had worsened over the previous seven days. There was no reported history of parental consanguinity. The mother mentioned exposure to a family member (grandfather) with pulmonary Koch's disease, for which the grandfather had completed treatment. Dietary evaluation revealed the child frequently consumed processed, high-calorie foods, with little fresh produce and infrequent intake of balanced, home-cooked meals. Anthropometric measurements included a weight of 44 kg, height of 112 cm, and a BMI of 35. On physical assessment, the respiratory rate was noted at 20 breaths per minute with evidence of mild tachypnea. Reduced chest wall movement was found on the right. Percussion over the right subscapular, infra-axillary, and axillary zones produced stony dullness. Palpation revealed significantly decreased vocal fremitus on the right compared to the left. Auscultation showed diminished breath sounds over the corresponding right regions. No signs of pallor, cyanosis, icterus, clubbing, or lymphadenopathy were observed. The abdomen was soft and non-tender, without evidence of hepatosplenomegaly. The patient was alert, oriented, and exhibited no abnormalities in mental status. Cardiovascular examination disclosed tachycardia without the presence of murmurs. Oxygen saturation measured 88%–89% on room air, and the heart rate was 110 beats per minute. Due to the tachypnea and respiratory distress, the patient was transferred to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit (PICU) and commenced on 5 L/min oxygen via mask. A chest radiograph was performed, and empiric piperacillin-tazobactam was initiated following collection of blood cultures. Imaging revealed a right-sided pleural effusion with obliteration of the right costophrenic angle (figure 1) (Table 2). Bedside lung ultrasound estimated the pleural effusion at approximately one liter in volume (figure 2) (Table 2). Right-sided thoracentesis was conducted under sterile technique, aspirating about one liter of dark red fluid. The patient’s tachypnea resolved after fluid removal, and oxygen saturations on room air rose to 95%–98%. Chest pain abated, and the child was weaned off oxygen within 24 hours. The following day, he was transferred to the general ward and subsequently discharged. Pleural fluid samples were sent for laboratory analysis. However, the child was readmitted six days later with recurrence of right-sided chest pain, tachypnea, and difficulty breathing. He was again transferred to the PICU, and chest X-ray showed a repeated right pleural effusion. Thoracentesis was performed once more, yielding approximately one liter of dark red pleural fluid. Adenosine deaminase (ADA) levels in the pleural fluid were slightly raised (Table 1), and given the positive history of Koch’s contact, empirical antitubercular therapy was started. Nevertheless, the patient returned a third time five days later with recurrent right chest pain and respiratory symptoms. A more thorough history uncovered that he had experienced blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle one month earlier. A contrast-enhanced chest CT was ordered prior to insertion of an intercostal drain (ICD). Imaging demonstrated patchy regions of airspace consolidation in the right lung, a right hydropneumothorax, and a multiloculated, lobulated, hypodense collection extending from the right pleural space, tracking from the right paraesophageal region to the upper left retroperitoneum and continuing to the tail of the pancreas (Table 2). An ICD was placed, draining approximately one liter of dark red pleural fluid (figure 3). These CT findings, in the context of the pleural fluid appearance and recent abdominal trauma, raised suspicion for a pancreatico-pleural fistula (PPF). Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) was performed the same day, revealing evidence of pancreatitis with a tubular cystic collection located at the distal body and tail of the pancreas, extending retroperitoneally and ascending through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural space. There was also a narrow tract leading to the left pleural cavity. The collection communicated with the distal pancreatic duct, with upstream ductal narrowing at the site of communication. MRCP findings supported the diagnosis of PPF (figures 4–6) (table 1). Amylase and lipase assays were conducted on serum and pleural fluid samples. Differential diagnosis:

The patient exhibited pleural effusion and chest pain, with a confirmed episode of abdominal trauma occurring 1 month prior. Aside from post-traumatic pancreatitis, alternative etiologies for pleural effusion were excluded. The child lacked history of fever, persistent cough, or weight loss. Although there was documented family contact with tuberculosis and the pleural fluid ADA was slightly elevated, GeneXpert for MTB yielded a negative result. Consequently, pulmonary tuberculosis was excluded. Absence of fever and the lack of a neutrophilic exudate within the pleural fluid further excluded empyema. Malignancies such as lung cancer, leukaemia, lymphoma, or metastatic tumors may present with amylase-enriched pleural fluid, but normal blood counts and smear, as well as no fever or weight loss, made this diagnosis unlikely. Pleural fluid cytology showed no malignant cells. Amylase-rich pleural fluid may also be associated with Boerhaave’s syndrome (esophageal rupture), but this was dismissed as the child had normal bowel movements and no history of vomiting or chest pain. Acquired diaphragmatic hernia was not supported, given imaging did not show abdominal organs within the thoracic cavity. Hepatic hydrothorax was excluded based on normal liver imaging and function, and absence of chronic liver disease history. Normal findings on renal imaging and function ruled out renal disease or hydronephrosis.

Treatment:

Humidified oxygen delivered by mask was administered for respiratory support in the PICU. The patient underwent ultrasound-guided thoracocentesis on two occasions, with approximately one liter of blood-tinged pleural fluid withdrawn each time. Empirical initiation of antitubercular therapy occurred but was discontinued upon diagnostic confirmation via MRCP. During the third hospitalization, an intercostal drain (ICD) was placed under full aseptic technique on the right side, leading to drainage of about one liter of blood-stained pleural fluid into the ICD bag. Because pleuro-pancreatic fistula (PPF) is rarely seen in the pediatric population and lacks standardized management protocols, treatment was guided by case report evidence. A conservative approach was chosen, with the child placed on nil per oral status and started on an Octreotide infusion at a dose of 30 µg/kg/day (range 10–40 µg/kg/day). Total parenteral nutrition and pancreatic enzyme supplementation were initiated. Over ten days, the output from the ICD progressively diminished and eventually ceased. The ICD was then removed and the patient was discharged from the hospital.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following his discharge from the hospital, the patient has remained asymptomatic. He continues to see me monthly for scheduled follow-ups. Since leaving the hospital, there have been no further incidents of recurrent pleural effusions (S17). He has made changes to his lifestyle, now maintaining a balanced diet and engaging in consistent exercise as recommended. He has resumed his usual routines, is attending school consistently, and is able to participate in vigorous activities such as running and playing without limitation.

Discussion:

Panopleural fistula (PPF) is a highly uncommon sequela of pancreatic disorders among paediatric patients. To date, only 47 paediatric cases of PPF have been reported. This condition can result from either acute or chronic pancreatitis, or following traumatic injury in children. The exact prevalence in paediatrics remains uncertain; however, PPF arises in approximately 0.4% of all pancreatitis cases. Genetic mutations in serine peptidase inhibitor Kazal type 1 (SPINK1) and PRSS1 are significant contributors, associated with up to 30% of childhood chronic pancreatitis cases. Blunt trauma to the abdomen also serves as an important non-genetic precipitant, comprising 32% of the 25 patients studied by Yang et al. Idiopathic presentations persist, which may reflect unrecognized genetic or ductal anomalies. Pancreatic fluid can migrate from a damaged duct or pseudocyst across natural diaphragmatic openings—typically the aortic or oesophageal hiatus, or, less frequently, via direct erosion through the diaphragm—producing a fistulous tract to the pleural space. Frequently, these cases are overlooked as most children initially exhibit thoracic symptoms, prompting pulmonary investigations, whereas the true source lies within the abdomen. Review of the literature identified 21 pertinent articles; among these, 68% described primarily respiratory symptoms such as large, recurring unilateral or bilateral pleural effusions, breathlessness, and cough. Fewer than 20% reported abdominal complaints such as epigastric pain, vomiting, or weight loss; and 24% had systemic symptoms including fever. A case published by Zi Yin Shang in Frontiers in Paediatrics in 2023 highlighted an 8-year-old boy with PPF whose initial manifestation was left chest bulging for two weeks, a presentation exceedingly rare for PPF. In this instance, pleural effusion likely progressed after abdominal trauma. Compensation by the intact lung enabled gradual physiological adaptation, so the child lacked classic respiratory symptoms such as cough or shortness of breath. Additionally, the child concealed the abdominal injury due to bullying, which delayed uncovering the trauma history. Initial workup was directed toward tuberculosis and malignancy because of the hemorrhagic effusion, but diagnosis subsequently changed course. In our case, the patient experienced recurrent pulmonary manifestations—chest pain and recurring right-sided pleural collections. Repeated clinical history-taking ultimately revealed abdominal trauma, and MRCP findings directed attention to underlying pancreatic disease. Accordingly, this pathology can present as a ‘pulmonary mask’, misleading clinicians until detailed history and appropriate imaging are pursued. The fluid accumulation in PPF arises from external trauma or bacterial infection, causing rupture of the pancreatic duct or pseudocyst with subsequent leakage. Anterior rupture allows pancreatic fluid into the peritoneal cavity resulting in ascites, while posterior rupture results in retroperitoneal leakage, which ascends along least resistance into the mediastinum via diaphragmatic hiatuses and eventually breaches the pleural space to form a pleural effusion—a pancreatic fistula effectively shunts pancreatic secretions into the thoracic cavity. Among adults, chronic pancreatitis due to alcohol predominates as the cause for PPF. Uchiyama et al provided the most expansive analysis from a single country, compiling 113 Japanese cases and finding that 99% stemmed from chronic alcohol-induced pancreatitis with thoracic symptoms present in 68%. Contrastingly, genetic factors predominate in childhood chronic pancreatitis. Mutations in the PRSS1 gene, CFTR gene, SPINK1 gene, and chymotrypsin C gene are documented as causes of hereditary pancreatitis; the R122H and N291 mutations on exons 3 and 2 of the PRSS1 gene, respectively, are especially prominent. Recurrence or chronic presentation of paediatric pancreatitis should prompt thorough family history and consideration of hereditary factors. Diagnosis of PPF is often postponed, as extensive pulmonary evaluations precede recognition of pancreatic involvement. A pleural fluid amylase of ≥1000 IU/L is diagnostic for PPF. Differential diagnoses such as tuberculosis, oesophageal perforation, and lung or pancreatic cancer must also be excluded since these can raise pleural amylase. In PPF, pleural fluid contains pancreatic-type amylase, while elevated salivary-type amylase may indicate other conditions. Amylase isoform typing in pleural fluid is useful for suspected PPF cases. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) offers 70–80% sensitivity for fistula and duct visualization. CT is sensitive in detecting pancreatic calcifications, pseudocysts, or ductal dilation but identifies the fistula in only 33–47% of cases.
