Title: Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood

Hans Vaish, Vipin Vaish, Shalini Suri, Vasudha Tomar

Abstract:

Summary: A preadolescent boy experienced multiple episodes of right-sided pleural effusions accompanied by chest pain and difficulty breathing, with temporary relief following fluid aspiration. The aspirated pleural fluid consistently appeared dark red in color. Owing to the persistence of effusions, a right intercostal drain was inserted. Further inquiry uncovered a recent incident of abdominal trauma resulting from a bicycle handle injury. Chest CT imaging revealed patchy consolidation on the right, hydropneumothorax, and a multiloculated hypodense area extending from the right paraesophageal space to the pancreatic tail. Elevated levels of amylase and lipase were found in both serum and pleural fluid samples. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography identified the presence of bilateral pancreatopleural fistulas, with a greater effect on the right side. Management involved keeping the patient nil per oral, administration of total parenteral nutrition, and treatment with Octreotide. Over ten days, the pleural drainage diminished, allowing removal of the chest tube, and the patient was ultimately discharged.

Background:

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) represents an exceptionally uncommon condition, typically manifesting as repeated, large-volume, and often blood-tinged pleural effusions. This complication may arise in the setting of acute or chronic pancreatitis, as well as following pancreatic injury. Diagnostic challenges frequently occur, since patients usually display respiratory compromise resulting from recurrent pleural collections, even though the underlying problem originates in the pancreas. As a result, clinicians may focus primarily on investigating thoracic causes, leading to delayed recognition of the true etiology. To date, there have been only 47 documented cases of PPF in the paediatric population, whereas more than 300 cases have been reported in adults.

Case presentation:

A young patient was presented to the outpatient clinic, accompanied by his mother, with a 1-week history of breathing difficulties and right-sided chest discomfort. According to his mother, his respiratory symptoms had gradually worsened over the previous week. There was no parental consanguinity. The mother reported prior exposure to tuberculosis (Koch’s contact), as the child's grandfather had previously been diagnosed and adequately treated for pulmonary tuberculosis. The child’s dietary history indicated frequent ingestion of processed and high-calorie foods, while intake of fresh fruits, vegetables, and balanced home-cooked meals was notably insufficient. His measured weight was 44 kg, height was 112 cm, and body mass index calculated at 35. On physical examination, he was mildly tachypnoeic with a respiratory rate of 20/min. The right side of his chest demonstrated diminished movement. Percussion revealed a stony dull note in the right subscapular, infra-axillary, and axillary regions. Vocal fremitus was also considerably weaker on the right than on the left. Breath sounds were reduced in the same regions on the right. There was no evidence of pallor, cyanosis, icterus, clubbing, or lymphadenopathy. His abdomen was soft, and there was no hepatosplenomegaly. The child was alert and fully oriented. Cardiovascular assessment demonstrated tachycardia (heart rate 110/min) without murmurs, and his oxygen saturation on room air was between 88%–89%. Given his tachypnoea and signs of respiratory distress, he was transferred to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit and placed on supplemental oxygen at 5 l/min via face mask. A chest radiograph was obtained, and empirical antibiotics (piperacillin-tazobactam) were initiated following blood culture collection. The chest X-ray indicated a right-sided pleural effusion with blunting of the right costophrenic angle (figure 1) (Table 2). A bedside lung ultrasound estimated the effusion volume at approximately one litre (figure 2) (Table 2). Right-sided pleural fluid was aspirated under strict aseptic conditions, yielding roughly one litre of dark red fluid. Following thoracocentesis, the child's tachypnoea subsided, and oxygen saturation improved to 95%–98% on room air. His chest pain abated, and he was able to discontinue oxygen within 24 hours. He was transferred to the general ward the next day and later discharged. The aspirated pleural fluid was sent for laboratory analysis. However, six days later, he was readmitted with recurrent symptoms of right-sided chest pain, tachypnoea, and dyspnoea. On readmission to the PICU, chest radiography again confirmed a right-sided pleural effusion. Repeat thoracocentesis removed another litre of dark red pleural fluid. Pleural fluid Adenosine Deaminase (ADA) was modestly elevated (Table 1) and, combined with a positive contact history, empirical antitubercular therapy was initiated. Nonetheless, the patient was admitted a third time five days later for recurrent right-sided chest pain and breathlessness. Further questioning revealed that one month earlier he had experienced blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle. A contrast-enhanced CT scan of the chest was performed prior to placing an intercostal drainage (ICD) tube. The CT showed patchy consolidation in the right lung, right-sided hydropneumothorax, and an elongated, multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right pleural space, traversing the right paraoesophageal region into the upper left retroperitoneum to the tail of the pancreas (Table 2). An ICD was inserted, draining approximately one litre of dark red fluid (figure 3). The combination of CT findings, the colour and amount of aspirate, and history of abdominal trauma suggested the diagnosis of a PPF. Magnetic Resonance Cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) was conducted the same day, revealing pancreatitis with a tubular, cystic collection from the distal pancreatic body and tail, extending posteriorly in the retroperitoneum and ascending through the oesophageal hiatus into the right pleural space. A small communication with the left pleural cavity was also noted. The collection was seen communicating with the distal pancreatic duct, which showed narrowing proximal to the communication. MRCP findings supported the diagnosis of a PPF (figures 4-6) (table 1). Amylase and lipase levels in both serum and pleural fluid were requested. Pleural fluid analysis demonstrated substantially increased amylase (6118 U/L) and lipase (7211 U/L) values (table 2), confirming the PPF diagnosis. Antitubercular therapy was discontinued, the child was started on nil per oral with total parenteral nutrition

Differential diagnosis:

The patient exhibited pleural effusion and chest pain, with a documented episode of abdominal trauma occurring one month prior. After eliminating other etiologies of pleural effusion, post-traumatic pancreatitis remained the most plausible cause. The child had no complaints of fever, persistent cough, or weight loss. Although there was a familial history of Koch’s contact and a slight elevation in pleural fluid ADA, GeneXpert testing for MTB was negative, excluding pulmonary Koch’s disease as a diagnosis. Additionally, the absence of fever and neutrophilic exudate in the pleural fluid effectively excluded empyema. Malignancies such as lung cancer, leukemia, lymphoma, or metastatic tumors can result in pleural fluid with high amylase content, but these were excluded based on the absence of fever, absence of weight loss, a normal blood profile, and a normal peripheral smear. Furthermore, cytological analysis of pleural fluid revealed no malignant cells. Boerhaave’s syndrome, an oesophageal perforation that may produce amylase-rich pleural fluid, was also dismissed given the child's normal bowel habits and lack of preceding chest pain or vomiting. The possibility of an acquired diaphragmatic hernia was eliminated since imaging did not show any abdominal organs in the thoracic cavity. Hepatic hydrothorax was discounted due to normal liver function tests and liver imaging, along with no history suggestive of chronic liver disease. Finally, normal renal imaging and renal function assessments excluded any renal pathology or hydronephrosis.

Treatment:

In the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit (PICU), the patient received humidified oxygen administered via mask for respiratory assistance. Ultrasound-guided thoracocentesis was performed on two occasions, each yielding approximately one litre of pleural fluid with blood staining. Empirical initiation of antitubercular therapy was undertaken; however, these medications were discontinued following a definitive diagnosis established by MRCP. During a third hospital admission, an intercostal drain (ICD) was aseptically inserted on the right side, resulting in the drainage of an additional litre of blood-stained pleural fluid into the ICD collection system. As PPF is infrequently encountered among pediatric patients and lacks standardized management protocols, existing interventions are generally extrapolated from individual case reports. Conservative measures were prioritized, including keeping the patient nil per os and commencing an Octreotide infusion at a dosage of 30 µg/kg/day (range 10–40 µg/kg/day). Total parenteral nutrition and pancreatic enzyme supplementation were also provided. Over a period of ten days, the volume of fluid drainage via ICD diminished progressively and ultimately ceased. Following ICD removal, the patient was subsequently discharged.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following his discharge, the patient has remained asymptomatic. He continues to attend scheduled monthly follow-up appointments. Since leaving the hospital, there have been no further incidents of recurrent pleural effusions (S17). He has adopted lifestyle modifications, maintaining a balanced diet and engaging in regular physical activity as recommended. The child has resumed normal daily routines and attends school consistently. He is able to participate in vigorous activities such as running and playing without limitations.

Discussion:

PPF represents a highly uncommon and seldom-reported complication arising from pancreatic disease in children. To date, there are only 47 known paediatric cases documented in the literature. This condition may be seen in the context of both acute and chronic pancreatitis or subsequent to abdominal trauma in paediatric patients. While its precise incidence in children remains unclear, PPF complicates approximately 0.4% of all pancreatitis cases. Genetic mutations, notably those in Serine peptidase inhibitor Kazal type 1 (SPINK1) and PRSS1, are identified in up to 30% of paediatric chronic pancreatitis instances. Additionally, blunt abdominal trauma accounts for a significant proportion of non-genetic causes—32% of cases in the cohort examined by Yang et al. Some cases are classified as idiopathic, possibly reflecting underdiagnosed genetic mutations or variations of the pancreatic duct. 

Pancreatic fluid may escape from a compromised pancreatic duct or pseudocyst, traversing through natural diaphragmatic openings such as the aortic or oesophageal hiatus, or, less commonly, by direct erosion through the diaphragm, establishing a fistulous tract to the pleural cavity. Many cases are overlooked because the clinical presentation is often dominated by thoracic complaints; as a result, diagnostic efforts are typically directed towards finding a primary lung pathology, although the disease actually originates from the abdomen. A review of published reports identified 21 articles, in which respiratory symptoms—such as large, recurrent pleural effusions (unilateral or bilateral), breathlessness, and cough—were present in 68% of patients, whereas fewer than 20% reported abdominal symptoms (including epigastric pain, vomiting, and weight loss), and constitutional symptoms (e.g., fever) were seen in 24%. An example from Zi Yin Shang in Frontiers in Paediatrics 2023 highlighted a unique presentation in an 8-year-old with two weeks of left-sided chest swelling as the only symptom. Presentations like these, where chest bulging is the initial manifestation, are exceedingly rare. In that scenario, the pleural effusion likely accumulated progressively post-abdominal trauma. Intact function of the unaffected lung enabled compensatory adaptation, thus masking typical respiratory symptoms like cough and dyspnoea. Additionally, the patient's reticence in reporting his trauma history—stemming from school bullying—further delayed identification of the aetiology. Initial workup was directed at tuberculosis and malignancy due to haemorrhagic pleural fluid, ultimately resulting in an alternative diagnosis. In our case, repeated episodes of right-sided pleural effusion with recurrent chest pain were present. Only after repeated history taking and further imaging (MRCP) was pancreatic involvement suspected. This highlights the potential for PPF to mimic respiratory disease—a “pulmonary mask”—misleading clinicians unless thorough history and appropriate imaging are pursued.

Fluid accumulation in PPF arises from external trauma or infection, leading to pancreatic duct or pseudocyst rupture and subsequent leakage of pancreatic fluid. Rupture at the anterior pancreas typically leads to leakage into the peritoneal cavity and ascites, while rupture at the posterior aspect directs fluid into the retroperitoneal space, from where it may ascend through the aortic or oesophageal hiatus to enter the mediastinum, and finally cross the pleura to produce a pleural effusion. This process constitutes an internal pancreatic fistula, analogous to a leaking pipe, diverting pancreatic secretions into the thoracic cavity. Among adults, chronic pancreatitis associated with alcoholism is the predominant aetiology of PPF. Uchiyama et al., in an extensive analysis of the Japanese literature, reported 113 cases, of which 99% were due to chronic alcoholic pancreatitis, with respiratory symptoms predominating in 68%. Conversely, genetic causes are more prominent in children. Well-established mutations—including those in the PRSS1, cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator, SPINK1, and chymotrypsin C genes—account for many cases of hereditary pancreatitis, with PRSS1 mutations at R122H and N291 (exons 3 and 2, respectively) being characteristic. When chronic or recurrent pancreatitis is seen in children, detailed family histories are necessary, and genetic predisposition should be considered.

Delayed diagnosis is frequent, as the initial workup usually focuses on the pulmonary system, delaying the recognition of pancreatic aetiology. A pleural fluid amylase of at least 1000 IU/L is pathognomonic for PPF, though tuberculosis, oesophageal rupture, and neoplasms of the pancreas or lung must be excluded as they may also yield elevated pleural amylase. It is the presence of pancreatic-type amylase in pleural fluid which is distinctive for PPF; by contrast, other aforementioned conditions are associated with the salivary amylase isoform.
