Title: Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood

Hans Vaish, Vipin Vaish, Shalini Suri, Vasudha Tomar

Abstract:

Summary: An older child developed recurring right-sided pleural effusions, experiencing chest pain and episodes of respiratory distress, with symptoms temporarily alleviated following fluid aspiration. The aspirated pleural fluid consistently exhibited a dark red appearance. Due to the persistence of these effusions, a right intercostal drain was inserted. Upon review, it was found that the patient had recently suffered abdominal trauma caused by impact with a bicycle handlebar. Chest CT imaging revealed right-sided patchy lung consolidation, hydropneumothorax, and a multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right paraesophageal space towards the pancreatic tail. Both serum and pleural fluid demonstrated elevated amylase and lipase levels. Subsequent magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography identified bilateral pancreatopleural fistulas, more pronounced on the right. Management consisted of withholding oral intake, administering total parenteral nutrition, and treating with Octreotide. Over 10 days, effusion output lessened, the chest tube was removed, and the patient was subsequently discharged.

Background:

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is a highly uncommon condition characterized by repeated, large, and often blood-tinged pleural effusions. It occurs in the context of acute pancreatitis, chronic pancreatitis, or following pancreatic injury. Clinically, PPF often mimics primary thoracic disease because patients typically exhibit symptoms of respiratory compromise secondary to persistent pleural effusions, which can complicate and postpone the correct diagnosis since initial assessments usually focus on identifying a thoracic etiology. While the literature documents only 47 cases of PPF in pediatric patients, over 300 adult cases have been reported.

Case presentation:

A young patient presented to the outpatient clinic, accompanied by his mother, reporting symptoms of dyspnea and right-sided chest pain persisting for a week. According to the mother, these symptoms had progressively worsened over the previous seven days. There was no known consanguinity. The mother disclosed a history of contact with a family member—specifically, the child’s grandfather—who had been diagnosed with pulmonary Koch’s and had completed appropriate treatment. The child’s dietary assessment revealed frequent intake of processed, high-calorie foods, with a scarcity of fresh fruits, vegetables, and nutritionally balanced home-prepared meals. On measurement, his weight was 44 kg, height 112 cm, and his BMI calculated to 35. 

Clinical evaluation revealed a respiratory rate of 20 /min and mild tachypnea. Observed chest excursions were diminished on the right side. Percussion yielded stony dullness over the right subscapular, infra-axillary, and axillary zones. On palpation, vocal fremitus was notably decreased on the right compared to the left. Auscultation confirmed reduced breath sounds over these same regions. There was an absence of pallor, cyanosis, icterus, digital clubbing, and lymphadenopathy. Abdominal examination found the abdomen soft without hepatosplenomegaly. The child was alert and oriented, with no hepatic or splenic enlargement palpated. Cardiovascular assessment showed tachycardia but no audible murmurs. Oxygen saturation on room air measured between 88% and 89%, and heart rate was 110 /min. Due to evident tachypnea and respiratory distress, he was admitted to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit and commenced on oxygen therapy by mask at a flow rate of 5 l/min. A chest X-ray was requested, and empirical antibiotic treatment with piperacillin-tazobactam was initiated after obtaining blood cultures. Radiographs indicated a right-sided pleural effusion with blunting of the right costophrenic angle (figure 1) (Table 2). Point-of-care lung ultrasound revealed approximately one litre of pleural effusion (figure 2) (Table 2). Under sterile conditions, thoracocentesis was performed on the right, resulting in drainage of around one litre of dark red pleural fluid. Following the procedure, tachypnea resolved and oxygen saturation on room air rose to 95%–98%. The child's chest pain abated, and oxygen support was discontinued within 24 hours. He was transferred to the general ward the following day and subsequently discharged. Pleural fluid samples were sent for laboratory analysis. 

However, six days post-discharge, the child was readmitted with recurrence of right-sided chest pain, tachypnea, and breathing difficulty. He was transferred again to the PICU, where chest X-ray once more showed a right-sided pleural effusion. Repeat thoracocentesis yielded approximately one litre of dark red pleural fluid. Analysis indicated mildly elevated Adenosine Deaminase (ADA) in the pleural fluid (Table 1), and, given the history of contact with pulmonary Koch’s, empirical antitubercular therapy was initiated. Five days later, the child was admitted a third time for similar complaints of right-sided chest pain and dyspnea. On re-taking the history, the child revealed that he had sustained blunt abdominal trauma due to impact from a bicycle handle about a month prior. Before intercostal drainage (ICD) insertion, contrast-enhanced CT chest was performed, which showed patchy right lung consolidation, right-sided hydropneumothorax, and an elongated, multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right pleural space, tracking from the right paraoesophageal region down to the upper left retroperitoneum adjacent to the pancreatic tail (Table 2). An ICD was placed, draining about one litre of dark red pleural fluid (figure 3). The CT findings, combined with the appearance of the pleural fluid and the trauma history, suggested the diagnosis of a PPF. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) was ordered, revealing evidence of pancreatitis with a tubular cystic collection in the distal body and tail of the pancreas, extending posteriorly in the retroperitoneum and tracking upwards through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity; a small tract to the left pleural space was also visible. The collection communicated with the distal pancreatic duct, where narrowing was observed proximally. These MRCP findings were consistent with a PPF (figures 4-6) (table 1). Both serum and pleural fluid amylase and lipase were evaluated, with pleural fluid results indicating marked elevations in amylase (6118 U/L) and lipase (7211 U/L) (Table 2), substantiating the diagnosis

Differential diagnosis:

The patient exhibited pleural effusion and chest pain, with a clear history of abdominal trauma occurring one month prior. Aside from post-traumatic pancreatitis, alternative etiologies for the pleural effusion were excluded. The child lacked symptoms such as fever, persistent cough, or weight loss. Although there was a family history of Koch’s contact and the pleural fluid ADA was slightly raised, GeneXpert for MTB returned negative, thereby excluding pulmonary Koch’s. The absence of fever and the lack of a neutrophilic exudate in pleural fluid excluded empyema as a cause. Although malignancies (including lung cancer, leukaemia, lymphoma, and metastatic tumors) may be associated with pleural fluid rich in amylase, this diagnosis was considered unlikely due to normal hematological findings, normal peripheral smear, lack of weight loss, and no evidence of fever. Cytological analysis of the pleural fluid did not detect malignant cells. The possibility of Boerhaave’s syndrome (esophageal perforation), another cause of amylase-rich pleural effusion, was discounted as the child demonstrated normal bowel movements and reported no history of chest pain or vomiting suggestive of this syndrome. Acquired diaphragmatic hernia was excluded since radiological imaging showed no abdominal organs in the thoracic cavity. Hepatic hydrothorax was not considered since liver function and imaging were within normal limits, and there was no prior history of chronic liver disease. Additionally, normal renal imaging and function tests ruled out renal pathology, including hydronephrosis.

Treatment:

In the PICU, the patient received humidified oxygen therapy via face mask as respiratory support. Thoracocentesis, performed under ultrasound guidance, was carried out on two occasions, each time yielding approximately one litre of pleural fluid with a blood-stained appearance. Initially, empirical antitubercular therapy was initiated; however, these medications were discontinued upon confirmation of the diagnosis by MRCP. During a third hospital admission, an intercostal drain (ICD) was placed aseptically on the right side, once again resulting in drainage of roughly one litre of blood-stained pleural fluid into the ICD collection bag. Given the rarity of PPF in paediatric cases and the consequent lack of standardized management protocols, treatment decisions are informed primarily by individual case reports. The patient was first managed conservatively, with oral intake withheld and Octreotide infusion commenced at a dosage of 30 µg/kg/day (range: 10–40 µg/kg/day). In addition, total parenteral nutrition and pancreatic enzyme supplementation were provided. Over the following 10 days, a progressive reduction in ICD fluid output was observed, ultimately ceasing altogether. The ICD was then removed, and the child was subsequently discharged.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following his release from the hospital, the child has not experienced any further symptoms. I continue to monitor his progress with scheduled monthly follow-up appointments. Since his discharge, there have been no additional occurrences of recurrent pleural effusions (S17). He has adopted recommended lifestyle changes, maintaining a nutritious diet alongside consistent physical activity. The child has resumed his routine activities, including regular school attendance, and is able to participate in physically demanding tasks such as running and playing without limitation.

Discussion:

PPF represents an exceptionally uncommon and sporadic complication associated with pancreatic disorders in children. To date, literature documents only 47 paediatric cases. The condition may arise in the context of acute or chronic pancreatitis, or secondary to traumatic abdominal injury in paediatric patients. The true incidence among children remains undetermined, but PPF complicates approximately 0.4% of all pancreatitis cases. Mutations in the serine peptidase inhibitor Kazal type 1 (SPINK1) and PRSS1 genes have substantial etiological significance, collectively responsible for up to 30% of chronic paediatric pancreatitis cases. Traumatic events, specifically blunt abdominal trauma, serve as significant non-genetic precipitants, representing 32% of cases in a 25-child cohort reported by Yang et al. Additionally, idiopathic presentations persist, possibly reflecting undetected genetic or ductal anomalies.

Mechanistically, pancreatic secretions may escape from a ruptured pancreatic duct or pseudocyst, traversing natural diaphragmatic hiatuses (typically aortic or oesophageal, and less frequently through direct diaphragmatic erosion), resulting in fistulous communication with the pleural cavity. These occurrences are frequently underrecognized since most children manifest with thoracic complaints, which often prompt a pulmonary-focused workup, overlooking abdominal origins. A review of available literature highlighted 21 relevant publications, noting that 68% of cases displayed primarily respiratory manifestations, such as large, recurrent pleural effusions (unilateral or bilateral), shortness of breath, and cough. Abdominal symptoms—epigastric discomfort, vomiting, and weight loss—were noted in fewer than 20%, whereas constitutional signs like fever occurred in 24%. A 2023 case reported by Zi Yin Shang in Frontiers in Paediatrics detailed an 8-year-old presenting solely with left chest bulging lasting two weeks—a distinctly rare initial symptom. In this scenario, pleural effusion likely developed gradually post-abdominal trauma, and compensatory function of the healthy lung permitted adaptation without classic respiratory complaints. The child’s history of bullying at school resulted in concealment of the trauma, complicating diagnosis, which initially focused on infectious or neoplastic causes of haemorrhagic effusion. In our case, recurrent chest pain and right-sided pleural fluid were observed; only upon repeated inquiry and MRCP assessment was the association with abdominal trauma and pancreatic pathology established.

Thus, PPF may clinically mimic primary pulmonary disease, misdirecting diagnostic efforts unless clinicians thoroughly elicit historical details and pursue targeted imaging. The accumulation of fluid in PPF typically follows extrinsic injury or infection causing disruption of the pancreatic duct or a pseudocyst, allowing pancreatic secretions to escape. Rupture on the anterior pancreas results in ascites, whereas posterior leakage flows retroperitoneally, ascending through the aortic or oesophageal hiatus to the mediastinum and eventually breaching the pleura to yield pleural effusion—functionally creating an internal pancreatic fistula. While chronic alcoholic pancreatitis predominates as an etiology in adult PPF, comprehensive Japanese case analysis by Uchiyama et al (113 cases) revealed 99% related to alcohol use, with thoracic symptoms prevailing in 68%. In contrast, genetic abnormalities are the primary cause in paediatric chronic pancreatitis, with pathogenic variants in the PRSS1 gene (notably R122H and N291 in exons 3 and 2), the CF transmembrane regulator, SPINK1, and chymotrypsin C genes being implicated. Therefore, repeated or chronic pancreatitis in children necessitates a detailed familial inquiry and consideration of hereditary causes.

Diagnosis of PPF is frequently delayed because the initial approach is directed toward respiratory illness, with the pancreatic source often suspected only after an extensive pulmonary evaluation. Detection of pleural fluid amylase $\geq 1000\,\mathrm{IU/L}$ is considered pathognomonic for PPF. Nonetheless, conditions such as tuberculosis, oesophageal perforation, and malignancies of the lung or pancreas should be excluded, since they may also elevate pleural fluid amylase. PPF is distinguished by the presence of pancreatic-type amylase, whereas the alternative diagnoses usually yield a salivary-type enzyme; thus, amylase isoenzyme typing in pleural fluid can aid diagnosis. MRCP demonstrates a sensitivity of 70%–80% for identifying fistulous tracts and delineating ductal anatomy. CT imaging provides evidence for pancreatic calcifications, ductal dilatation, or pseudocysts but visualizes the fistula directly in only about 33%–47% of cases. Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) remains the definitive modality for both diagnosis and interventional planning, with reported visualization rates of the fistula in children between 38% and 78%.
