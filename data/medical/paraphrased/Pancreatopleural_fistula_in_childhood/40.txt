Title: Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood

Hans Vaish, Vipin Vaish, Shalini Suri, Vasudha Tomar

Abstract:

Summary: An older boy developed repeated right-sided pleural effusions accompanied by chest pain and difficulty breathing, which temporarily resolved after thoracentesis. The pleural fluid consistently appeared dark red in color. Due to ongoing effusions, a right intercostal drain was inserted. History disclosed a recent episode of abdominal trauma caused by a bicycle handlebar. Chest CT demonstrated patchy consolidation on the right, hydropneumothorax, and a multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right paraesophageal area to the pancreatic tail. Amylase and lipase levels were found to be elevated in both the serum and pleural fluid. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography revealed bilateral pancreatopleural fistulas, with the right side being more pronounced. Management included withholding oral intake, initiation of total parenteral nutrition, and administration of Octreotide. Within 10 days, drainage diminished, the chest tube was removed, and the boy was discharged.

Background:

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is a highly uncommon condition characterized by repeated, large, and often hemorrhagic pleural effusions. It may occur in the context of acute or chronic pancreatitis, as well as pancreatic trauma. The diagnosis is often challenging since patients primarily exhibit symptoms of respiratory compromise secondary to recurrent pleural effusions, which may divert attention toward a thoracic cause, despite the origin of the disease being pancreatic. In the pediatric population, only 47 instances of PPF have been documented, while there have been reports of over 300 cases in adults.

Case presentation:

A pediatric patient was brought to the outpatient clinic by his mother due to complaints of breathing difficulty and right-sided chest pain persisting for one week. The mother reported that his respiratory distress had worsened progressively over the past seven days. There was no background of consanguinity. She also indicated that the child had household exposure to tuberculosis, as his grandfather, who had pulmonary Koch's, had received appropriate treatment. The child’s dietary patterns included frequent intake of processed, calorie-dense foods, with minimal consumption of fresh fruits, vegetables, or nutritionally balanced home-cooked dishes. On physical evaluation, his weight was 44 kg, height 112 cm, and BMI was 35. The respiratory rate was 20 breaths per minute, and there was mild tachypnea. Decreased chest excursions were observed on the right. Percussion was stony dull over the right subscapular, right infra-axillary, and axillary regions. Tactile fremitus was much diminished on the right compared to the left. Breath sounds were also reduced in the aforementioned right-sided areas. No evidence of pallor, cyanosis, icterus, clubbing, or lymphadenopathy was found. The abdominal exam was soft, without hepatosplenomegaly. He was alert and oriented to time, place, and person. Cardiovascular examination revealed tachycardia with no detectable murmurs. Oxygen saturation was 88%–89% on ambient air with a heart rate of 110 bpm. Because of persistent tachypnea and respiratory distress, the patient was admitted to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit and commenced on oxygen via face mask at 5 L/min. A chest radiograph was obtained and broad-spectrum antibiotics (piperacillin-tazobactam) initiated empirically after blood cultures were drawn. Imaging demonstrated a right pleural effusion with obliteration of the right costophrenic angle (figure 1) (Table 2). Bedside lung ultrasound revealed an effusion of approximately one liter (figure 2) (Table 2). Right pleural aspiration was performed aseptically, yielding about one liter of dark red fluid. Following thoracocentesis, the tachypnea subsided and oxygen saturation improved to 95%–98% on room air. The patient experienced relief of chest pain and weaned off oxygen within 24 hours. He was transferred to the general ward the next day and subsequently discharged. The aspirated fluid was sent for laboratory analysis. 

However, he was readmitted six days later with recurrence of right-sided chest pain, tachypnea, and dyspnea. The patient returned to the PICU, where chest X-ray again displayed a right-sided pleural effusion. Repeat thoracocentesis was performed, with another one liter of dark red pleural fluid aspirated. Pleural fluid adenosine deaminase (ADA) was marginally elevated (Table 1) and, given the history of Koch’s contact, empirical anti-tubercular therapy was initiated. Nonetheless, the child presented once more after five days with recurrent right-sided chest pain and dyspnea. Upon revisiting the history, it was disclosed that he had sustained blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle one month prior. Before considering insertion of an intercostal drain (ICD), a contrast-enhanced chest CT was ordered, which revealed patchy areas of airspace consolidation in the right lung, right hydropneumothorax, and an elongated, multiloculated, hypodense collection extending from the right pleural region, tracking from the right paraesophageal area to the upper left retroperitoneum and up to the pancreatic tail (Table 2). An ICD was placed, draining about one liter of dark red fluid (figure 3). The findings on CT, in combination with the character of the aspirated fluid and recent abdominal trauma, suggested a pancreaticopleural fistula (PPF). Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) was performed on the same day, demonstrating pancreatitis with a tubular cystic collection in the distal body and tail of the pancreas, tracking posteriorly in the retroperitoneum and superiorly through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural space. A narrow communication to the left pleural space was noted. The collection was seen communicating with the distal pancreatic duct, which displayed a proximal narrowing. MRCP was highly suggestive of PPF (figures 4-6) (Table 1). Amylase and lipase assays were conducted on both serum and pleural fluid. Pleural analysis revealed markedly elevated amylase (6118 U/L) and lipase (7211 U/L) concentrations (Table 2), reinforcing the diagnosis of PPF. Differential diagnosis:

The child arrived with chest pain and pleural effusion, following a clear history of abdominal trauma one month prior. Aside from post-traumatic pancreatitis, all other etiologies for pleural effusion had been excluded. There were no reports of fever, prolonged cough, or weight loss. While there was a known family contact with Koch’s and the pleural fluid ADA levels were slightly raised, GeneXpert testing for MTB yielded negative results. Therefore, pulmonary Koch’s was eliminated from consideration. The lack of fever and absence of neutrophilic exudate in pleural fluid excluded empyema. Although amylase-rich pleural fluid can also be seen in malignancies (including lung cancer, leukemia, lymphoma, or metastatic tumors), malignancy was ruled out due to the absence of fever and weight loss, as well as a normal blood profile and peripheral smear. Pleural fluid cytology confirmed no malignant cells were present. Boerhaave’s syndrome (esophageal perforation), which may result in amylase-rich pleural fluid, was also excluded since the child was defecating normally and had no chest pain or vomiting history. Imaging did not reveal abdominal organs within the chest, ruling out acquired diaphragmatic hernia. Hepatic hydrothorax was excluded on the basis of normal liver function tests and imaging, and a lack of chronic liver disease history. Renal pathology and hydronephrosis were ruled out with normal renal imaging and renal function assessments.

Treatment:

In the PICU, the patient received humidified oxygen administered via face mask for respiratory support. Ultrasound-guided thoracocentesis was performed twice, with approximately one liter of blood-tinged pleural fluid evacuated on each occasion. Empirical antitubercular therapy was initiated but discontinued following diagnostic confirmation with MRCP. During the third hospitalization, an intercostal drain (ICD) was placed on the right side under aseptic conditions, resulting in drainage of another liter of blood-stained pleural fluid into the ICD collection bag. Pediatric PPF is a rare condition without established treatment protocols; management relies on published case reports. Initially, a conservative approach was pursued, with the child kept nil per os and started on an Octreotide infusion at 30 µg/kg/day (dosage range: 10–40 µg/kg/day). Total parenteral nutrition as well as pancreatic enzyme supplementation were commenced. Over ten days, drainage from the ICD gradually decreased and ceased. The ICD was subsequently removed, and the patient was discharged from the hospital.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following hospital discharge, the patient has remained asymptomatic. He continues with monthly follow-up visits under my care. Since leaving the hospital, there have been no additional occurrences of recurrent pleural effusions (S17). The patient has adopted recommended lifestyle modifications, including a balanced diet and consistent physical activity. He has resumed normal daily routines, is attending school consistently, and is able to participate in vigorous activities such as running and playing without limitations.

Discussion:

PPF represents a very uncommon and rare complication associated with pancreatic disease in children. To date, only 47 cases of paediatric PPF have been documented. The condition may arise from both acute and chronic pancreatitis, as well as paediatric trauma. The actual frequency in the paediatric population remains undetermined, though overall, PPF complicates approximately 0.4% of pancreatitis cases. Mutations in serine peptidase inhibitor Kazal type 1 (SPINK1) and PRSS1 are significant contributors, implicated in up to 30% of paediatric chronic pancreatitis cases. Non-genetic factors such as blunt abdominal trauma are also notable, accounting for 32% in a 25-patient series reported by Yang et al. Unexplained, or idiopathic cases, persist, possibly related to under-recognized genetic or ductal anomalies.

Pancreatic fluid may escape from a damaged duct or pseudocyst and track through natural diaphragmatic openings—usually the aortic or oesophageal hiatus—or, more rarely, via direct erosion through the diaphragm, thus establishing a fistulous pathway to the pleural space. Many cases go unrecognized since patients frequently present with thoracic complaints, leading clinicians to investigate for pulmonary conditions, while the actual source resides in the abdomen. A literature review revealed 21 pertinent articles, with 68% of paediatric cases initially presenting with dominant respiratory complaints, such as large or recurrent pleural effusions, breathlessness, and coughing. Fewer than one in five exhibited abdominal symptoms, such as epigastric discomfort, nausea, or weight loss. Constitutional complaints, including fever, occurred in 24% of reports. A 2023 case from Zi Yin Shang in Frontiers in Paediatrics described an 8-year-old boy presenting solely with left-sided chest bulging over two weeks. Such presentations are particularly rare. In the described case, pleural effusion likely developed gradually following abdominal trauma. The child’s healthy lung compensated, allowing adaptation and absence of classic symptoms like dyspnoea or cough. Moreover, the patient concealed a history of abdominal trauma due to bullying, hindering recognition of the underlying cause. Initially, he underwent evaluations for tuberculosis and malignancy due to haemorrhagic pleural fluid, but the diagnosis ultimately diverged.

In our experience, the child similarly had recurring chest pain and repeated collections of pleural fluid on the right side. Multiple rounds of history-taking finally elicited the abdominal trauma, and MRCP findings indicated pancreatic pathology. As such, PPF can mask itself as a pulmonary disorder, potentially misleading clinicians unless thorough history and appropriate imaging are obtained.

The pathogenesis of fluid accumulation in PPF involves either external injury or infection, resulting in duct or pseudocyst rupture and subsequent leakage of pancreatic contents. Anterior duct rupture permits pancreatic fluid to enter the peritoneal cavity, causing ascites, whereas posterior rupture drives fluid into the retroperitoneum, which follows the path of least resistance, passes through the aortic or oesophageal hiatus, and ultimately invades the pleural space, forming a fistula. This creates an internal fistulous tract, channeling pancreatic secretions into the chest. While alcohol is a dominant cause of chronic pancreatitis—and thus PPF—in adults, genetic factors predominate in children. Uchiyama et al’s extensive review in Japan described 113 cases, revealing that 99% related to chronic alcoholic pancreatitis, with chest symptoms noted in 68% of patients. Conversely, paediatric cases are linked mainly to gene mutations such as PRSS1, CFTR, SPINK1, and chymotrypsin C. PRSS1 mutations at R122H (exon 3) and N291 (exon 2) are especially prevalent. If paediatric pancreatitis recurs or is chronic, a detailed family history should be performed, considering the possibility of hereditary disease.

Diagnostic delays are common, as extensive pulmonary investigations typically precede consideration of pancreatic causes. Pleural fluid amylase levels exceeding 1000 IU/L are diagnostic for PPF. Nevertheless, it is essential to rule out tuberculosis, oesophageal perforation, or malignancy, since these may also elevate pleural fluid amylase. Identifying the pancreatic isoenzyme distinguishes PPF from others, as alternative conditions generally yield salivary-type amylase. Amylase typing in pleural fluid should be incorporated into workups for suspected PPF. MRCP detects the fistula and ductal anatomy with 70%–80% sensitivity; CT readily identifies pancreatic calcifications, pseudocysts, or duct dilation but visualizes the fistula in only 33%–47% of cases. Endoscopic cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) remains the preferred method for therapeutic planning, with fistula detection rates ranging from 38% to 78% in paediatric patients.
