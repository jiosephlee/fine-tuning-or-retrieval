Title: Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood

Hans Vaish, Vipin Vaish, Shalini Suri, Vasudha Tomar

Abstract:

Summary: An older child developed recurring pleural effusions on the right side, accompanied by chest pain and difficulty breathing, which subsided transiently after thoracentesis. The pleural fluid obtained was repeatedly described as dark red. Due to ongoing effusions, a right-sided intercostal drain was inserted. The patient’s history included recent abdominal injury from striking a bicycle handlebar. Chest CT identified patchy consolidation in the right lung, hydropneumothorax, and a multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right paraesophageal area to the pancreatic tail. Amylase and lipase levels were raised in both blood and pleural fluid. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography demonstrated bilateral pancreatopleural fistulas, more prominent on the right. Management involved making the patient nil per oral, administering total parenteral nutrition, and starting Octreotide. Within 10 days, drainage diminished, the chest drain was withdrawn, and the child was subsequently discharged.

Background:

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) represents a highly uncommon condition characterized by repeated, large-volume, often hemorrhagic pleural effusions. This complication can occur in the context of acute pancreatitis, chronic pancreatitis, or pancreatic trauma. The clinical picture may be perplexing, as patients often present with respiratory compromise resulting from recurrent pleural effusions, while the underlying issue originates in the pancreas. Consequently, diagnosis may be delayed because attention is frequently focused on identifying a pulmonary or thoracic cause. To date, only 47 cases have been described in children, compared to over 300 documented instances among adults.

Case presentation:

A pediatric patient presented to the outpatient clinic with his mother, reporting difficulty breathing and right-sided chest pain for the past week. The mother noted a gradual worsening of respiratory distress over seven days. There was no consanguinity in the family. She described a history of contact with his grandfather, who had pulmonary Koch’s and received appropriate treatment. The child’s dietary history included frequent intake of processed, calorie-dense foods and limited consumption of fresh fruits, vegetables, or balanced homemade meals. His measurements were 44 kg in weight, 112 cm in height, yielding a body mass index of 35. 

On physical examination, the child exhibited a respiratory rate of 20/min with mild tachypnea. Chest movement was reduced on the right side. Percussion revealed stony dullness in the right subscapular, infra-axillary, and axillary areas. Vocal fremitus was markedly diminished on the right versus the left. Breath sounds were reduced in the same regions. There were no signs of pallor, cyanosis, icterus, clubbing, or lymphadenopathy. The abdomen was soft without evidence of hepatosplenomegaly. The patient was alert and oriented. Cardiovascular examination showed tachycardia, but murmurs were absent. The child’s oxygen saturation measured 88%–89% on room air with a heart rate of 110/min.

Given the tachypnea and respiratory distress, the patient was moved to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit and received oxygen via mask at 5 l/min. A chest radiograph was obtained, and empiric antibiotic therapy with piperacillin-tazobactam was initiated following blood culture collection. The chest X-ray revealed a right-sided pleural effusion with blunted right costophrenic angle (figure 1, Table 2). Bedside lung ultrasound detected approximately one liter of pleural effusion (figure 2, Table 2). Right-sided thoracentesis was performed under aseptic conditions, yielding roughly one liter of dark red pleural fluid. Post-thoracentesis, tachypnea resolved, and room air oxygen saturation improved to 95%–98%. Chest pain subsided, and oxygen was discontinued within 24 hours. The child was transferred to the general ward the next day and subsequently discharged. The aspirated pleural fluid was sent for laboratory analysis.

Six days later, the child was readmitted with recurrence of right-sided chest pain, tachypnea, and respiratory difficulty. He was again managed in the PICU, and repeat chest radiograph demonstrated a right-sided pleural effusion. Thoracentesis was repeated, producing about one liter of dark red fluid. Pleural fluid Adenosine Deaminase (ADA) levels were mildly elevated (Table 1), and with the history of Koch’s contact, empirical antitubercular therapy was initiated.

After a further five days, he was hospitalized for a third time with similar right-sided chest symptoms and breathing difficulties. Upon detailed history-taking, the child disclosed sustaining blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle one month prior. Before inserting an intercostal drainage (ICD), a contrast-enhanced chest CT scan was performed, revealing patchy airspace consolidation in the right lung, a right-sided hydropneumothorax, and an elongated, lobulated, multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right pleural space, tracking from the right paraoesophageal region to the upper left retroperitoneum up to the pancreatic tail (Table 2). An ICD was placed, draining approximately one liter of dark red pleural fluid (figure 3). The combination of CT findings, the pleural fluid appearance, and the trauma history led to a suspected diagnosis of pancreaticopleural fistula (PPF). 

Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) was performed the same day, demonstrating pancreatitis with a tubular cystic collection in the distal pancreatic body and tail, tracking posteriorly in the retroperitoneum and superiorly via the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity. A narrow communication to the left pleural space was also noted, with the collection connecting to the distal pancreatic duct and ductal narrowing proximal to the communication. MRCP supported the diagnosis of PPF (figures 4–6; Table 1). Antitubercular medication was discontinued, and the child was placed on nothing by mouth with total parenteral nutrition. Differential diagnosis:

The patient arrived with pleural effusion and chest pain, following a clear abdominal trauma event one month prior. After considering and excluding other etiologies for pleural effusion apart from post-traumatic pancreatitis, additional work-up was performed. The patient had no complaints of fever, prolonged cough, or loss of weight. There was a documented family history of Koch’s contact, with a slightly elevated pleural fluid ADA; nonetheless, GeneXpert testing for MTB was negative. Therefore, pulmonary Koch’s was excluded. The absence of fever and lack of neutrophilic exudate in pleural fluid also ruled out empyema. Malignant conditions such as lung cancer, leukemia, lymphoma, or metastatic tumors may produce amylase-enriched pleural fluid, but the patient’s lack of fever, absence of weight loss, a normal complete blood count, and unremarkable peripheral smear excluded malignancy. Pleural fluid cytology showed no evidence of malignant cells. Boerhaave’s syndrome (esophageal perforation) is another cause of amylase-rich pleural fluid, yet this diagnosis was ruled out since the patient had normal bowel movements and denied any chest pain or vomiting. Acquired diaphragmatic hernia was excluded because imaging did not show abdominal organs in the thoracic cavity. Hepatic hydrothorax was not considered given normal liver function test results, normal liver imaging, and no history of chronic liver disease. Finally, renal pathology and hydronephrosis were dismissed based on normal renal imaging and function assessments.

Treatment:

In the PICU, humidified oxygen was administered via mask for respiratory support. Ultrasound-guided thoracocentesis was performed twice, each time yielding approximately one litre of blood-tinged pleural fluid. Empiric antitubercular therapy was initiated, but discontinued once the diagnosis was established via MRCP. During the third hospitalization, an intercostal drain (ICD) was placed on the right side under strict aseptic technique, resulting again in the drainage of about one litre of hemorrhagic pleural fluid into the ICD collection bag. Because PPF is rare in children and lacks established management protocols, therapeutic decisions were based on existing case reports. Conservative therapy was chosen initially; the patient was made nil per os and started on an Octreotide infusion at a dose of 30 µg/kg/day (range: 10–40 µg/kg/day). Total parenteral nutrition and pancreatic enzyme supplementation were initiated. Over the next 10 days, the volume of fluid from the ICD gradually diminished and eventually ceased. The ICD was subsequently withdrawn, and the patient was discharged.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following hospital discharge, the patient has remained asymptomatic. He attends monthly follow-up appointments with me. Since leaving the hospital, there have been no further instances of recurrent pleural effusions (S17). He has adjusted his lifestyle, adhering to recommendations for a balanced diet and routine physical activity. He has resumed normal daily routines and is regularly present at school. The child participates in vigorous activities such as running and playing without experiencing any limitations.

Discussion:

Panpleural fistula (PPF) represents a notably uncommon complication of pancreatic disease within the paediatric population. To date, there are only 47 recorded instances of paediatric PPF. This condition may arise in the context of either acute or chronic pancreatitis or following traumatic events in children. The true incidence in paediatric patients remains unidentified, but it is estimated to complicate approximately 0.4% of pancreatitis cases overall. Mutations in the serine peptidase inhibitor Kazal type 1 (SPINK1) and PRSS1 genes are prominent in paediatric chronic pancreatitis, accounting for up to 30% of cases. Furthermore, blunt abdominal trauma serves as a significant acquired trigger, comprising 32% of the cohort evaluated by Yang et al. Idiopathic cases persist in the literature, likely attributable to unrecognized genetic or ductal anomalies.

Pancreatic fluid may escape from a disrupted duct or pseudocyst and traverse natural diaphragmatic hiatuses—most often the aortic or oesophageal hiatus—or less frequently through direct erosion of the diaphragm, forming a fistulous tract into the pleural space. Many cases are not identified promptly since the majority initially exhibit thoracic symptoms, prompting clinicians to focus on pulmonary etiologies while the true pathology resides in the abdomen. Literature review highlighted 21 relevant studies, revealing that 68% of patients manifested predominant respiratory complaints, including large, recurrent unilateral or bilateral pleural effusions, dyspnoea, and cough. Fewer than 20% experienced abdominal manifestations like epigastric discomfort, vomiting, and weight reduction. Approximately 24% presented with constitutional features such as fever.

A case reported by Zi Yin Shang in Frontiers in Paediatrics 2023 described an 8-year-old boy whose sole presenting feature was a left-sided chest protrusion persisting for two weeks. First symptoms like chest bulging are exceptionally rare. Here, pleural effusion likely evolved gradually following abdominal trauma, with compensation by the unaffected lung permitting adaptation and masking typical respiratory symptoms, such as cough or breathlessness. Moreover, the child concealed his history of trauma, partly due to bullying at school. Haemorrhagic pleural effusion prompted evaluation for tuberculosis and neoplastic processes, but the diagnosis diverged toward PPF. In our case, the child also demonstrated recurrent pulmonary symptoms, characterized by chest pain and repeated right-sided pleural fluid accumulation. Only after multiple interviews was the history of abdominal trauma elicited, and MRCP subsequently indicated pancreatic pathology. Thus, this condition can mimic primary lung disease—the so-called 'pulmonary mask'—and potentially mislead physicians unless thorough history and appropriate imaging are employed.

The mechanism underlying fluid accumulation in PPF involves external trauma or bacterial infection causing rupture of the pancreatic duct or pseudocyst, with consequent leakage of pancreatic fluid. If rupture occurs anteriorly, fluid enters the abdominal cavity and produces ascites; posterior rupture results in retroperitoneal leakage, which follows the path of least resistance upward through the aortic or oesophageal hiatus into the mediastinum and ultimately through the pleura as an effusion. This internal pancreatic fistula functions akin to a leaking conduit, diverting secretions to the thoracic cavity.

In adults, chronic pancreatitis—predominantly due to alcohol—commonly leads to PPF. Uchiyama et al conducted a comprehensive review from Japan, identifying 113 cases, with 99% related to chronic alcoholic pancreatitis and 68% presenting with chest symptoms. In contrast, genetic etiologies are more significant in paediatric chronic pancreatitis, involving mutations in PRSS1, CFTR, SPINK1, and chymotrypsin C genes, notably R122H and N291 variants in exons 3 and 2, respectively, of PRSS1. For recurrent or chronic paediatric pancreatitis, comprehensive family history and consideration of hereditary causes are recommended.

Diagnosis is frequently delayed since extensive pulmonary assessment precedes suspicion of pancreatic involvement. A pleural fluid amylase concentration ≥1000 IU/L is considered diagnostic for PPF. However, tuberculosis, oesophageal perforation, and malignancies (pulmonary or pancreatic) must be excluded, as they can also exhibit elevated amylase. Determining amylase isoenzyme in pleural fluid helps distinguish pancreatic from salivary origins. Such amylase typing can be integral to diagnosing suspected PPF.

Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) provides 70%–80% sensitivity in visualizing fistulae and ductal anatomy. Computed tomography (CT) is reliable for detecting calcifications, pseudocysts, and ductal dilation but directly visualizes the fistula in only 33%–47% of cases.
