Title: Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood

Hans Vaish, Vipin Vaish, Shalini Suri, Vasudha Tomar

Abstract:

Summary: An older child presented with recurring right-sided pleural effusions accompanied by chest pain and respiratory difficulty, which showed temporary improvement following aspiration. The pleural fluid consistently exhibited a dark red color. Given the persistence of effusions, a right intercostal drain was inserted. History uncovered a recent episode of abdominal trauma caused by contact with a bicycle handlebar. Chest CT imaging demonstrated right-sided patchy lung consolidation, hydropneumothorax, and a multiloculated hypodense collection that extended from the right paraesophageal area toward the pancreatic tail. Amylase and lipase levels were elevated in both serum and pleural fluid samples. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography identified bilateral pancreatopleural fistulas, more pronounced on the right. Management included withholding oral intake, initiation of total parenteral nutrition, and administration of Octreotide. Over 10 days, the drainage volume diminished, allowing removal of the chest tube, after which the patient was discharged.

Background:

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is a very uncommon condition, typically manifesting as repeated, large-volume, and often hemorrhagic pleural effusions. It has been described in the context of acute and chronic pancreatitis as well as pancreatic injury. Diagnosis can be challenging, as the clinical presentation is dominated by symptoms of respiratory compromise related to ongoing pleural effusions, while the underlying abnormality originates from the pancreas. As a result, evaluation may initially focus on thoracic sources, potentially delaying identification of the true etiology. In the pediatric population, only 47 cases have been documented, compared to over 300 cases reported in adults.

Case presentation:

A child was presented to the outpatient clinic by his mother, who reported that he had experienced breathing difficulties and pain on the right side of his chest for the past week. According to the mother, his symptoms had progressively worsened over these seven days. There was no history of consanguinity. The mother also mentioned that the child’s grandfather had previously been treated for pulmonary Koch’s disease, indicating a known history of contact with a tuberculosis case. The child’s dietary pattern featured frequent intake of processed, calorie-dense foods, while consumption of fresh fruits, vegetables, and nutritious home-prepared meals was limited. He weighed 44 kg, stood 112 cm tall, and had a body mass index of 35.

On physical examination, the child’s respiratory rate was 20 per minute, with mild tachypnea. Reduced movement was observed in the right hemithorax. Percussion yielded a stony dull note in the right subscapular, right infra-axillary, and axillary areas. There was a marked decrease in vocal fremitus on the right compared to the left. Diminished breath sounds were noted in the same regions. No pallor, cyanosis, jaundice, clubbing, or lymphadenopathy was detected. The abdomen was soft and non-tender without enlargement of the liver or spleen. The patient was conscious and fully oriented. Cardiovascular evaluation revealed tachycardia but no murmurs. Oxygen saturation measured 88%–89% on room air, and heart rate was 110 per minute.

Given the tachypnea and respiratory distress, the child was transferred to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit and started on oxygen therapy at 5 L/min via mask. A chest X-ray was performed and empirical piperacillin-tazobactam was initiated after drawing blood cultures. The chest radiograph showed a right-sided pleural effusion with blunting of the right costophrenic angle (figure 1) (Table 2). Point-of-care lung ultrasonography estimated a pleural effusion of roughly one liter (figure 2) (Table 2). Under aseptic technique, approximately one liter of dark red pleural fluid was aspirated from the right side. Following thoracocentesis, the child’s tachypnea subsided and oxygenation improved to 95%–98% on room air. Chest pain resolved and oxygen support was discontinued within 24 hours. The patient was moved to the general ward the next day and subsequently discharged. The aspirated pleural fluid was sent for laboratory analysis.

Six days later, the child was readmitted with recurrence of right-sided chest pain, tachypnea, and difficulty breathing. He was transferred again to the PICU, where repeat chest radiography showed another right pleural effusion. Thoracocentesis was repeated, yielding another liter of dark red pleural fluid. Pleural fluid adenosine deaminase (ADA) was slightly elevated (Table 1), and given the TB exposure history, empirical antitubercular treatment was initiated.

Five days after this, the child was hospitalized for a third episode of right-sided chest pain and dyspnea. On revisiting the medical history, the child disclosed having experienced blunt trauma to the abdomen from a bicycle handle about one month earlier. Before placing an intercostal drainage (ICD) tube, a contrast-enhanced CT of the chest was obtained, which revealed patchy air space consolidation in the right lung, a right-sided hydropneumothorax, and a large, elongated, multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right pleural space—tracking from the right paraesophageal region to the upper left retroperitoneum up to the tail of the pancreas (Table 2). An ICD was placed and about one liter of dark red fluid was drained (figure 3).

The combination of CT findings, presence of dark red pleural fluid, and prior abdominal trauma raised suspicion for a pancreaticopleural fistula (PPF). An MRCP was subsequently conducted, showing evidence of pancreatitis and a tubular cystic collection in the distal body and tail of the pancreas, extending posteriorly into the retroperitoneum and then cranially via the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity. A narrow connection to the left pleural space was also detected. The collection communicated with the distal pancreatic duct, with proximal ductal narrowing visible. MRCP confirmed the impression of PPF (figures 4-6) (Table 1). Both serum and pleural fluid amylase and lipase levels were assessed, with pleural fluid studies revealing marked elevation of amylase (6118 U/L) and lipase (7211 U/L) (Table 2), corroborating the diagnosis.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient developed pleural effusion and chest pain and had a documented episode of abdominal trauma 1 month prior. Following exclusion of other etiologies of pleural effusion, post-traumatic pancreatitis remained as a consideration. This child did not report any fever, persistent cough, or weight loss. Although there was a family history of Koch’s contact and pleural fluid ADA showed slight elevation, GeneXpert for MTB was negative. Therefore, pulmonary Koch’s was excluded. Empyema was not considered as there was no fever and pleural fluid did not display neutrophilic exudate. Malignancies such as lung cancer, leukemia, lymphoma, and metastatic disease are known to cause amylase-rich pleural fluid, but malignancy was excluded given the lack of fever or weight loss, unremarkable blood investigations and peripheral smear. Pleural fluid cytology failed to detect malignant cells as well. The possibility of Boerhaave’s syndrome (esophageal perforation) was also dismissed, since the child was defecating normally and lacked any history of chest pain or vomiting. Acquired diaphragmatic hernia was excluded because imaging revealed no abdominal organs in the thoracic cavity. Hepatic hydrothorax was ruled out on the basis of normal liver function and liver imaging, and the absence of any chronic liver disease history. Finally, normal renal imaging and renal function tests ruled out renal abnormalities, including hydronephrosis.

Treatment:

The patient received humidified oxygen via mask for respiratory support while in the PICU. Two ultrasound-guided thoracocentesis procedures were performed, each yielding approximately one liter of blood-tinged pleural fluid. Antitubercular medications were initiated empirically but discontinued once MRCP established the diagnosis. During the third hospitalization, a right-sided intercostal drain (ICD) was placed with strict aseptic technique, which again drained around one liter of blood-stained pleural fluid into the ICD bag. Given that PPF is an infrequently encountered condition in pediatric patients, established treatment protocols are lacking, and management is generally informed by case report literature. Initially, conservative therapy was chosen; the child was made nil per oral and commenced on an Octreotide infusion at a dose of 30 µg/kg/day (range 10–40 µg/kg/day). Total parenteral nutrition and pancreatic enzyme supplementation were also initiated. Over the subsequent ten days, the volume of fluid drained from the ICD progressively decreased and eventually ceased. The ICD was subsequently removed, and the patient was discharged from the hospital.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following hospital discharge, the patient has remained asymptomatic. He attends scheduled monthly follow-up appointments with me. Since leaving the hospital, there have been no additional occurrences of recurrent pleural effusion (S17). He has adopted lifestyle changes, including a balanced diet and consistent exercise as recommended. He has resumed his routine activities and is now regularly going to school. He is able to participate in physically demanding activities such as running and playing without limitations.

Discussion:

Pancreatic pleural fistula (PPF) represents a notably uncommon complication stemming from pancreatic pathology within paediatric populations. To date, only 47 paediatric cases of PPF have been reported. This condition may develop as a consequence of either acute or chronic pancreatitis, or following trauma in children. While the precise frequency of PPF in paediatrics is not established, overall it is known to complicate approximately 0.4% of pancreatitis cases. Genetic mutations—including serine peptidase inhibitor Kazal type 1 (SPINK1) and PRSS1—are significant contributors, implicated in up to 30% of paediatric chronic pancreatitis instances. Additionally, blunt abdominal trauma acts as a key acquired precipitant, accounting for 32% in Yang et al’s analysis of 25 patients. Idiopathic cases are still reported and may be due to unrecognized genetic factors or ductal anomalies.

Pancreatic fluid may migrate from a disrupted duct or pseudocyst through diaphragmatic hiatuses (most commonly the aortic or esophageal) or, less frequently, via direct erosion through the diaphragm, forming a fistula with the pleural space. These presentations are frequently overlooked since many affected patients exhibit thoracic symptoms; clinicians often concentrate efforts on identifying respiratory disease, whereas the underlying pathology originates in the abdomen.

A review of the literature revealed 21 pertinent articles, with respiratory complaints—such as large or recurrent unilateral/bilateral pleural effusions, dyspnoea, and cough—dominating in 68% of cases. Abdominal complaints (epigastric discomfort, vomiting, weight loss) appeared in fewer than 20%, while constitutional symptoms (like fever) were noted in 24%. Shang (Frontiers in Paediatrics, 2023) described PPF in an 8-year-old boy presenting solely with left chest swelling for two weeks, a rare initial symptom. In this instance, the effusion appeared to have gradually developed after an abdominal injury, with compensatory respiratory ability from the healthy lung concealing usual symptoms like cough or dyspnoea. The patient, affected by school bullying, intentionally withheld history of abdominal trauma.

The workup in this child initially focused on tuberculosis and malignancy as causes for hemorrhagic pleural effusion, only later leading to the correct diagnosis. Similarly, our case involved recurrent respiratory symptoms and repeated right-sided pleural fluid accumulations; upon repeated history-taking and MRCP, pancreatic pathology was confirmed, with previous trauma emerging as a key factor. Consequently, PPF can obscure its underlying cause with “pulmonary masking,” leading clinicians toward erroneous diagnoses unless thorough history and appropriate imaging are obtained.

Fluid accumulation in PPF arises from external trauma or infection causing rupture of the pancreatic duct or a pseudocyst, and subsequent leakage of pancreatic fluid. When the anterior pancreas ruptures, fluid accumulates within the peritoneal cavity (ascites); posterior rupture enables fluid to enter the retroperitoneal space, ascend via the path of least resistance, travel through diaphragmatic hiatuses to the mediastinum, and then breach the pleura, resulting in pleural effusion. The internal pancreatic fistula effectively acts as a channel redirecting pancreatic secretions into the thoracic cavity.

In adults, chronic pancreatitis leading to PPF most often results from alcohol abuse. Uchiyama et al’s extensive review of 113 Japanese cases highlighted that 99% originated from chronic alcoholic pancreatitis, with chest symptoms present in 68% of patients. Conversely, paediatric chronic pancreatitis is largely genetically driven, with well-characterized mutations in PRSS1, CFTR, SPINK1, and chymotrypsin C genes being implicated. The PRSS1 gene, especially its R122H and N291 mutations in exons 3 and 2, is prototypical. Accordingly, children with recurrent or chronic pancreatitis should be evaluated with a detailed family history, and hereditary pancreatitis considered.

Diagnosis is typically delayed, as these cases commonly undergo extensive pulmonary investigations before pancreatic origin is suspected. Pathognomonic for PPF, pleural fluid amylase concentration ≥1000 IU/L is observed. However, other diseases including tuberculosis, esophageal rupture, and malignancies of the lung or pancreas can also yield elevated pleural fluid amylase and should be ruled out. Differentiation of amylase isoenzymes in pleural fluid—pancreatic versus salivary—assists in distinguishing PPF. Pleural fluid amylase typing may thus be warranted in suspected cases.
