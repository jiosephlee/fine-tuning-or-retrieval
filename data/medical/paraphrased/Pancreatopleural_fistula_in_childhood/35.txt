Title: Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood

Hans Vaish, Vipin Vaish, Shalini Suri, Vasudha Tomar

Abstract:

A boy in late childhood experienced repeated episodes of right-sided pleural effusions accompanied by chest pain and difficulty breathing, which temporarily subsided following aspiration. The pleural aspirate consistently appeared dark red in color. In light of ongoing effusions, a right intercostal chest drain was inserted. On further evaluation, it was noted that he had recently suffered abdominal trauma caused by a bicycle handle. Chest CT imaging demonstrated patchy consolidation in the right lung, the presence of a hydropneumothorax, and a multiloculated, hypodense fluid collection extending from the right paraesophageal space toward the pancreatic tail. Amylase and lipase levels were found to be raised in both the serum and pleural fluid samples. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography identified pancreatopleural fistulas on both sides, more pronounced on the right. Management consisted of making the patient nil per os, providing total parenteral nutrition, and administering Octreotide. Over the ensuing 10 days, drainage diminished, permitting chest tube removal, and the patient was subsequently discharged.

Background:

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is a very uncommon condition characterized by repeated, large-volume, and sometimes blood-tinged pleural effusions. This condition can occur in the context of acute or chronic pancreatitis, as well as following pancreatic injury. Its clinical presentation often includes symptoms of respiratory compromise due to persistent pleural fluid accumulation, which can be misleading, as the underlying problem originates in the pancreas rather than the chest. This discrepancy often results in a delayed diagnosis, with initial investigations directed toward identifying a pulmonary cause. To date, only 47 pediatric cases of PPF have been documented, compared to more than 300 reported cases in the adult population.

Case presentation:

A young patient was brought to the outpatient clinic by his mother, reporting difficulty breathing and right-sided chest pain persisting for one week. The mother indicated that these symptoms had progressively worsened over seven days. There was no reported history of consanguinity. She mentioned prior contact with a relative—specifically, the child’s grandfather—who had been diagnosed and treated for pulmonary Koch's. Dietary history revealed the child frequently consumed processed and high-calorie foods with minimal intake of fresh fruits, vegetables, or well-balanced homemade meals. His recorded weight was 44 kg, height 112 cm, and a BMI of 35.

On clinical examination, the respiratory rate measured 20/min, with mild tachypnoea observed. Right-sided chest excursion was reduced. Percussion noted a stony dullness over the right subscapular, infra-axillary, and axillary areas. Vocal fremitus was notably decreased on the right compared to the left. There was diminished breath sound in these same regions on the right. No evidence of pallor, cyanosis, icterus, clubbing, or lymphadenopathy was detected. Abdominal exam revealed a soft abdomen without hepatosplenomegaly. The child was alert and oriented to time, place, and person. Cardiovascular assessment showed tachycardia with no murmurs. Oxygen saturation was 88%–89% in ambient air, and the heart rate was 110/min.

Due to tachypnoea and respiratory distress, the child was transferred to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit and started on mask oxygen at 5 l/min. Chest radiography was performed, and empirical antibiotics (piperacillin-tazobactam) were initiated after collecting blood cultures. Imaging showed a right-sided pleural effusion with blunted costophrenic angle (figure 1) (Table 2). Bedside lung ultrasonography confirmed the presence of approximately one litre of pleural fluid (figure 2) (Table 2). Pleural fluid aspiration was performed under sterile conditions, removing about one litre of dark red fluid. After thoracocentesis, tachypnoea resolved, oxygen saturation improved to 95%–98% on room air, chest pain subsided, and oxygen supplementation was discontinued within 24 hours. The child was subsequently transferred to the ward and later discharged. Pleural fluid obtained during the procedure was sent for laboratory analysis.

Six days following discharge, the patient was readmitted, presenting with recurrent right-sided chest pain, tachypnoea, and respiratory difficulty. He was managed again in PICU, and chest X-ray again revealed a right-sided pleural effusion. Thoracocentesis was repeated, extracting roughly one litre of dark red pleural fluid. Pleural fluid adenosine deaminase (ADA) was mildly increased (Table 1). Given the positive history of Koch’s contact, empirical antitubercular therapy was started.

Five days later, the child was admitted a third time for similar symptoms—right-sided chest pain and breathing difficulties. Upon further history taking, the child disclosed an episode of blunt abdominal trauma via bicycle handle one month earlier. Prior to placement of an intercostal drain (ICD), a contrast-enhanced CT scan of the thorax was ordered. Imaging indicated patchy air space consolidation in the right lung with right-sided hydropneumothorax, and a lobulated, multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right pleural space, traversing from the right paraoesophageal area to the left-sided retroperitoneum, up to the pancreatic tail (Table 2). An ICD was inserted, and another litre of dark red pleural fluid was evacuated (figure 3).

The combination of CT findings, dark red pleural fluid, and history of abdominal trauma directed consideration to pancreaticopleural fistula (PPF). MRCP was performed the same day, revealing features of pancreatitis with tubular cystic collections in the distal pancreatic body and tail extending posteriorly into the retroperitoneum and then ascending through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural space. There was a narrow communication seen with the left pleural cavity as well. The collection communicated with the distal pancreatic duct, which exhibited proximal narrowing. MRCP supported the diagnosis of PPF (figures 4–6) (Table 1). Serum and pleural fluid amylase and lipase tests were requested. Analysis revealed markedly increased pleural fluid amylase (6118 U/L) and lipase (7211 U/L) levels (Table 2), corroborating the diagnosis.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient was admitted with pleural effusion and chest pain, following a documented episode of abdominal trauma one month prior. Aside from post-traumatic pancreatitis, alternative etiologies for the pleural effusion were excluded. The patient’s history lacked any fever, prolonged cough, or significant weight loss. Although there was a family history of contact with tuberculosis (Koch’s), and the pleural fluid adenosine deaminase (ADA) level was slightly increased, GeneXpert testing for MTB yielded negative results, eliminating pulmonary tuberculosis from consideration. Empyema was discounted because there was no fever nor evidence of neutrophilic exudate within the pleural fluid. Malignancies, such as those of the lung, leukemia, lymphoma, and metastatic tumors, can sometimes result in pleural fluid with elevated amylase; however, the absence of fever and weight loss, along with a normal hematological profile and peripheral smear, effectively excluded malignancy. Cytologic examination of the pleural fluid found no malignant cells. Boerhaave’s syndrome (esophageal perforation), another potential source of amylase-rich pleural fluid, was excluded due to regular stool passage, absence of chest pain, and no history of vomiting. An acquired diaphragmatic hernia was ruled out since imaging showed no abdominal organs within the thoracic cavity. Hepatic hydrothorax was eliminated, as liver function tests and hepatic imaging were normal, and there was no history suggestive of chronic liver disease. Renal pathology, including hydronephrosis, was also excluded based on normal renal imaging and laboratory renal function.

Treatment:

In the PICU, the patient received humidified oxygen via mask as respiratory support. Two ultrasound-guided thoracocentesis procedures were performed, yielding approximately one litre of blood-tinged pleural fluid each time. Antitubercular therapy was initiated empirically but discontinued once MRCP confirmed the diagnosis. During a third hospitalization, an intercostal drain (ICD) was placed on the right side under strict aseptic technique, draining an additional one litre of blood-stained pleural fluid into the ICD collection bag. Given the rarity of PPF in the paediatric population and the absence of established management protocols, the therapeutic approach was informed by published case reports. Initial conservative management was pursued, including keeping the patient nil per oral and commencing Octreotide infusion at 30 µg/kg/day (range 10–40 µg/kg/day). Total parenteral nutrition and pancreatic enzyme supplementation were started. Over the ensuing 10 days, the volume of fluid drained via the ICD progressively decreased and eventually ceased. The ICD was subsequently removed, and the patient was discharged.

Outcome and follow-up:

Since being discharged, the patient has not experienced any further symptoms. He attends scheduled monthly follow-up visits with me. There have been no additional occurrences of pleural effusions since leaving the hospital (S17). Following recommendations, he has adopted lifestyle changes, now maintaining a balanced diet and engaging in routine physical activity. He has resumed normal routines, including consistent school attendance, and is able to participate in vigorous activities such as running and playing without limitations.

Discussion:

PPF represents a very rare and uncommon complication arising from pancreatic disease within the paediatric population. To date, only 47 paediatric cases of PPF have been reported. It can develop secondary to either acute or chronic pancreatitis, or following traumatic injury in children. The true incidence among paediatric patients remains unclear, although overall PPF complicates about 0.4% of pancreatitis cases. Mutations in genes such as serine peptidase inhibitor Kazal type 1 (SPINK1) and PRSS1 account for up to 30% of chronic pancreatitis in paediatric patients. Blunt abdominal trauma is a significant non-genetic precipitant, responsible for 32% of cases in a 25-child series evaluated by Yang et al. Idiopathic presentations persist, potentially reflecting undetected genetic or ductal anomalies.

Pancreatic fluid may traverse from a disrupted duct or pseudocyst through physiologic diaphragmatic openings, most commonly the aortic or oesophageal hiatus, but occasionally through direct erosion of the diaphragm, thus creating a fistulous passage into the pleural space. Many such cases are missed because they present with predominant thoracic manifestations, often leading clinicians to focus resources on pulmonary investigations while the true pathology lies abdominally. Literature review revealed 21 pertinent reports; among these, 68% of cases presented chiefly with respiratory complaints such as large, repeated, unilateral or bilateral pleural effusions, shortness of breath, and cough. Abdominal symptoms, including epigastric pain, vomiting, and weight loss, occurred in under 20% of patients, while constitutional features like fever were noted in 24%.

Zi Yin Shang (Frontiers in Paediatrics 2023) described PPF in an 8-year-old presenting solely with a 2-week history of left-sided chest bulge. Initial presentation with chest swelling is exceedingly uncommon. In that instance, pleural effusion evolved slowly after abdominal trauma, and preserved function in the healthy lung allowed the child to acclimate, thus lacking classic respiratory symptoms such as cough or dyspnoea. Additionally, psychosocial factors led the patient to hide his trauma history. Diagnostic workup initially targeted tuberculosis and malignancies as causes of haemorrhagic pleural effusion, but the eventual diagnosis diverged.

In our experience, the child also exhibited recurrent respiratory symptoms, including chest pain and repeated right-sided pleural effusions. The history of abdominal trauma was ultimately elicited after multiple interviews, and MRCP clarified the underlying pancreatic disorder. Therefore, this entity often masquerades as a pulmonary disease, potentially misleading clinicians away from its abdominal origin without thorough history and appropriate imaging.

Fluid accumulation in PPF arises from trauma or infection, leading to rupture of the pancreatic duct or pseudocyst and subsequent leakage. If rupture is anterior, fluid collects intraperitoneally, resulting in ascites. Posterior rupture, however, directs fluid retroperitoneally upwards via paths of least resistance, entering the mediastinum through the aortic or oesophageal hiatus and breaching the pleura to generate pleural effusion—a pancreatic fistula behaves like a faulty pipe, diverting secretions into the thoracic cavity.

In adults, chronic pancreatitis due to alcohol is the principal cause of PPF. A comprehensive analysis by Uchiyama et al of Japanese literature documented 113 cases, with 99% attributable to chronic alcoholic pancreatitis and 68% showing primary thoracic symptoms. By contrast, paediatric chronic pancreatitis is mainly hereditary, linked to mutations in PRSS1, CF transmembrane regulator, SPINK1, and chymotrypsin C genes, notably R122H and N291 in PRSS1 exon 3 and exon 2, respectively. Persistent or recurrent paediatric pancreatitis warrants thorough family history and assessment for hereditary causes.

The diagnostic process is frequently delayed, as extensive pulmonary evaluation precedes suspicion of pancreatic origin. Pleural fluid amylase ≥1000 IU/L is diagnostic for PPF. Differential diagnosis includes tuberculosis, oesophageal perforation, and neoplasms, which may also cause elevated pleural amylase. Pancreatic-type amylase in pleural fluid distinguishes PPF from conditions with salivary-type amylase. Pleural fluid amylase subtype analysis may assist in evaluation.

MRCP demonstrates 70%–80% sensitivity for identifying fistulae and ductal anatomy, while CT reliably detects pancreatic calcifications, pseudocysts, or ductal dilatation but only identifies fistulae in 33%–47% of cases. Endoscopic cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) remains the standard for therapeutic planning; fistula visualization rates in children range from 38% to 78% with ERCP.
