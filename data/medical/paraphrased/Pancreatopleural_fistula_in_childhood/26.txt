Title: Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood

Hans Vaish, Vipin Vaish, Shalini Suri, Vasudha Tomar

Abstract:

A preadolescent male presented with repeated right-sided pleural effusions, accompanied by chest pain and difficulty breathing; symptoms temporarily abated following aspiration. The aspirated pleural fluid persistently exhibited a dark red coloration. Due to ongoing effusions, a right intercostal drain was inserted. The clinical history revealed a recent episode of abdominal trauma sustained from impact with a bicycle handle. Chest computed tomography demonstrated patchy consolidation on the right, hydropneumothorax, and a multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right paraesophageal area toward the pancreatic tail. Both serum and pleural fluid analyses showed elevated amylase and lipase levels. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography identified bilateral pancreatopleural fistulas, with greater involvement on the right side. Management included fasting (nil per os), total parenteral nutrition, and administration of Octreotide. Over a ten-day span, drainage diminished, allowing for removal of the chest tube and subsequent discharge of the child.

Background:

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is a very uncommon condition characterized by repeated, large, and occasionally blood-tinged pleural effusions. It has been documented in patients with acute pancreatitis, chronic pancreatitis, as well as pancreatic injuries. Managing PPF can be challenging, as it manifests predominantly with symptoms of respiratory distress due to recurring pleural effusions, although the underlying disorder involves the pancreas. This often leads to a delay in diagnosis because initial investigations focus primarily on thoracic causes. To date, only 47 cases have been documented in children, whereas in adults, there have been over 300 reported cases.

Case presentation:

A child was brought to the outpatient clinic by his mother, reporting a week-long history of shortness of breath and right-sided chest pain. According to the mother, the respiratory difficulty had progressively worsened over the previous seven days. There was no history of consanguinity. The mother mentioned exposure to pulmonary Koch’s disease, as the child's grandfather had tuberculosis and received appropriate treatment. The child’s nutritional history showed frequent consumption of processed, high-calorie foods, and low intake of fresh fruits, vegetables, and balanced home-cooked meals. The child weighed 44 kg and measured 112 cm in height, with a body mass index of 35.

On physical examination, the respiratory rate was 20/min with mild tachypnea. Diminished chest movement was observed on the right side. Percussion produced a stony dull note in the right subscapular, infra-axillary, and axillary regions. Vocal fremitus was notably reduced on the right compared to the left. Breath sounds were decreased in the same right-sided regions. The child did not exhibit pallor, cyanosis, icterus, clubbing, or lymphadenopathy. The abdomen was soft and no hepatosplenomegaly was present. The child was alert and oriented to time, place, and person. Cardiovascular assessment revealed tachycardia without murmurs. Oxygen saturation was measured at 88–89% on room air, with a heart rate of 110/min.

Due to tachypnea and respiratory distress, the child was transferred to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit and initiated on mask oxygen at 5 l/min. A chest X-ray was requested, and empirical piperacillin-tazobactam was commenced after blood cultures were drawn. Imaging showed a right pleural effusion with blunting of the right costophrenic angle (figure 1) (Table 2). Bedside lung ultrasound identified approximately one liter of pleural fluid on the right (figure 2) (Table 2). Right-sided pleural aspiration was performed using aseptic technique, yielding about one liter of dark red fluid. The tachypnea resolved following thoracocentesis, and oxygen saturation improved on room air to 95–98%. Chest pain subsided and supplemental oxygen was discontinued within 24 hours. The child was transferred to the ward the next day and subsequently discharged. Pleural fluid samples were sent for analysis.

However, the child was re-admitted after six days with recurrent right-sided chest pain, tachypnea, and breathing difficulty. He was again moved to PICU, where chest X-ray confirmed a recurrent right pleural effusion. A further thoracocentesis drew another liter of dark red fluid. Pleural fluid adenosine deaminase (ADA) was mildly elevated (Table 1), and with a positive Koch’s contact history, empirical antitubercular therapy was initiated.

After another five days, the child was admitted for a third time with right chest pain and breathing difficulty. Upon a detailed history review, the child reported sustaining blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle one month prior. A contrast-enhanced chest CT was performed prior to intercostal drainage (ICD) placement, revealing patchy air space consolidation in the right lung, right hydropneumothorax, and an elongated, lobulated, multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right pleural space at the paraoesophageal level down to the left retroperitoneum and pancreatic tail region (Table 2). An ICD was inserted, and about one liter of dark red fluid was drained (figure 3). The imaging findings, the fluid appearance, and history of abdominal trauma raised suspicion of a pancreaticopleural fistula (PPF).

MRCP was scheduled on the same day, demonstrating pancreatitis with a tubular cystic collection in the distal body and tail of the pancreas, tracking posteriorly through the retroperitoneum and then upward via the oesophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity. A narrow channel to the left pleural space was also evident. The collection communicated with the distal pancreatic duct, which was narrowed proximal to the connection. MRCP thus supported the diagnosis of PPF (figures 4–6) (Table 1).

Amylase and lipase levels in both serum and pleural fluid were measured. Pleural fluid analysis showed markedly raised amylase (6118 U/L) and lipase (7211 U/L) concentrations (Table 2), corroborating PPF diagnosis. Antitubercular therapy was discontinued, and the child was kept nil per oral with total parenteral nutrition started. Octreotide infusion was commenced at 30 µg/kg/day (10–40 µg/kg/day). Differential diagnosis:

The patient exhibited pleural effusion accompanied by chest pain, with a documented abdominal injury occurring 1 month prior. Aside from post-traumatic pancreatitis, alternative etiologies for pleural effusion were excluded. The child reported no fever, persistent cough, or unintended weight loss. Although there was a family history of tuberculosis exposure and the pleural fluid ADA level was slightly increased, the GeneXpert test for MTB was negative, eliminating pulmonary tuberculosis from consideration. The absence of fever and neutrophilic exudate within the pleural fluid made empyema unlikely. Malignancies such as lung cancer, leukemia, lymphoma, or metastatic neoplasms can also produce pleural fluid rich in amylase; however, the lack of fever, lack of weight loss, unremarkable blood studies, and normal peripheral smear excluded malignancy. Pleural fluid cytology found no evidence of malignant cells. Amylase-rich pleural fluid may be observed in Boerhaave’s syndrome (esophageal perforation), yet this was dismissed because the patient had regular bowel movements and did not report chest pain or vomiting. Acquired diaphragmatic hernia was not considered since imaging did not reveal abdominal organs within the thoracic cavity. Hepatic hydrothorax was ruled out due to normal liver function tests and imaging and the absence of chronic liver disease in the child's history. Additionally, renal pathology or hydronephrosis was excluded owing to normal renal imaging and laboratory results.

Treatment:

Humidified oxygen administered via mask was provided in the PICU for respiratory support. The patient underwent two ultrasound-guided thoracocentesis procedures, during which approximately one litre of blood-tinged pleural fluid was removed each time. Antitubercular medications were initiated empirically, but these were discontinued upon confirmation of the diagnosis through MRCP. On the third hospitalization, an intercostal drain (ICD) was placed on the right side under strict aseptic conditions, draining about one litre of blood-stained pleural fluid into the ICD collection bag. Pediatric PPF, being rare, does not have established treatment protocols and management generally relies on case reports. Initially, conservative therapy was chosen; the child was made nil per oral and commenced on Octreotide infusion at 30 µg/kg/day (range 10–40 µg/kg/day). Total parenteral nutrition as well as pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy was initiated. Over the ensuing 10 days, the output from the ICD progressively diminished and eventually ceased. The ICD was subsequently removed and the patient was discharged from the hospital.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following hospital discharge, the patient has remained asymptomatic. He attends scheduled monthly follow-up appointments with me. No additional incidents of recurrent pleural effusion have occurred post-discharge (S17). The patient has adopted lifestyle modifications, maintaining a balanced diet and participating in regular physical activity as recommended. He has resumed his normal routine, including regular school attendance, and is able to engage in vigorous activities such as running and playing without limitation.

Discussion:

PPF is a very uncommon complication arising from pancreatic disease in children, with only 47 recorded cases in the paediatric demographic to date. It can develop following either acute or chronic pancreatitis, or as a result of paediatric trauma. The true rate of incidence in children remains undetermined, but it affects approximately 0.4% of pancreatitis cases overall. Notably, mutations in the serine peptidase inhibitor Kazal type 1 (SPINK1) and PRSS1 genes are observed frequently, contributing to up to 30% of paediatric chronic pancreatitis cases. Blunt trauma to the abdomen is another significant non-genetic cause, making up 32% of a 25-patient group examined by Yang et al. Cases without a clear cause continue to be reported, likely reflecting unrecognized genetic or ductal abnormalities.

Pancreatic fluid in PPF can escape through a compromised pancreatic duct or a pseudocyst, traveling via the natural openings in the diaphragm—most often the aortic or oesophageal hiatus—or occasionally eroding directly through the diaphragm to form a fistulous tract with the pleural space. Since most of these patients present with chest symptoms, many cases are missed, and diagnostic efforts tend to focus on respiratory conditions rather than abdominal pathology. The literature review uncovered 21 pertinent reports, where 68% of cases manifested primarily with respiratory complaints, including substantial, recurrent unilateral or bilateral pleural effusions, breathlessness, and cough. Fewer than 20% had gastrointestinal symptoms like epigastric pain, vomiting, or weight reduction, while 24% displayed general signs such as fever.

Zi Yin Shang’s report in Frontiers in Paediatrics 2023 described an 8-year-old boy presenting solely with a protruding left chest over a two-week span; such presentations with chest bulge as the initial finding are exceedingly rare. In this instance, the pleural effusion likely developed gradually after abdominal injury. The child’s normal respiratory function on the unaffected lung allowed for adaptation, and the absence of typical respiratory symptoms like cough or shortness of breath was noted. Additionally, the boy experienced school bullying and intentionally concealed his history of abdominal trauma. Workup focused on tuberculosis and malignancy because of hemorrhagic pleural fluid, but the actual diagnosis diverged. In our experience, the patient displayed repeated chest pain and right-sided pleural fluid accumulations. Only after several rounds of history-taking and the findings of MRCP did the antecedent abdominal trauma become clear, steering the diagnosis toward pancreatic pathology.

Thus, PPF can mimic pulmonary disease and lead clinicians astray unless a comprehensive clinical history and suitable imaging are obtained. The source of fluid collection in PPF is external trauma or bacterial infection, leading to rupture of the pancreatic duct or pseudocyst and leakage of pancreatic secretions. If the anterior pancreas is ruptured, the fluid enters the peritoneal cavity, resulting in ascites; posterior rupture permits pancreatic fluid to infiltrate the retroperitoneal space and ascend through the least resistant route into the mediastinum via the aortic or oesophageal hiatus, eventually breaching the pleura to cause pleural effusion. This constitutes an internal pancreatic fistula, allowing pancreatic fluids to seep into the thoracic compartment.

In adults, chronic pancreatitis leading to PPF is most commonly attributable to alcohol intake. Uchiyama et al conducted the most expansive single-country analysis with 113 cases documented in Japanese literature, revealing 99% were linked to chronic alcoholic pancreatitis, and 68% presented primarily with chest complaints. Conversely, in paediatric patients, hereditary factors predominate. Recognized mutations including those in PRSS1, CF transmembrane regulator, SPINK1, and chymotrypsin C genes are causative for hereditary pancreatitis; among these, R122H and N291 mutations in exon 3 and exon 2, respectively, of the PRSS1 gene, are characteristic. For recurrent or chronic pancreatitis in children, thorough family history and consideration for hereditary pancreatitis are essential.

Diagnosis is usually delayed because extensive pulmonary evaluations precede suspicion of pancreatic involvement. Pleural fluid amylase concentrations of ≥1000 IU/L are diagnostic for PPF. Differential diagnoses such as tuberculosis, oesophageal rupture, and pulmonary or pancreatic malignancies must be excluded, as elevated pleural fluid amylase may occur in these scenarios as well. Pancreatic-type amylase in the pleural fluid differentiates PPF from these other conditions, which instead show salivary-type amylase; thus, amylase typing is a valuable diagnostic adjunct in suspected PPF.
