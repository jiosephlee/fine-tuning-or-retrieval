Title: Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood

Hans Vaish, Vipin Vaish, Shalini Suri, Vasudha Tomar

Abstract:

Summary: A preadolescent male presented with multiple episodes of right-sided pleural effusions, accompanied by chest discomfort and difficulty breathing, with symptoms showing transient relief following thoracentesis. The pleural fluid was repeatedly noted to be dark red in appearance. Given the frequency of the effusions, a right intercostal drain was inserted. On further inquiry, recent abdominal injury involving contact with a bicycle handlebar was discovered. Chest CT demonstrated right-sided patchy consolidation, hydropneumothorax, and a multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right paraesophageal area down to the pancreatic tail. Levels of amylase and lipase were raised in both the serum and the pleural fluid. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography identified the presence of pancreatopleural fistulas on both sides, more pronounced on the right. Management included keeping the patient nil per os, administering total parenteral nutrition, and initiating Octreotide. Within 10 days, fluid output subsided, allowing for removal of the chest drain and subsequent discharge of the patient.

Background:

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is a notably uncommon condition characterized by the occurrence of recurrent, large, and sometimes hemorrhagic pleural effusions. This complication arises in the context of acute or chronic pancreatitis as well as pancreatic trauma. Due to its presentation with respiratory symptoms resulting from persistent pleural fluid accumulation, PPF can be challenging to recognize; clinical attention often focuses on identifying a thoracic etiology, which can delay the diagnosis, as the underlying disorder involves the pancreas. In the pediatric population, only 47 cases have been documented, compared with over 300 adult cases described in the literature.

Case presentation:

A pediatric patient presented to my outpatient clinic accompanied by his mother, who reported that her child had been experiencing shortness of breath and pain on the right side of the chest for one week. According to the mother, these symptoms had progressively worsened over the past seven days. There was no family history of consanguinity. She did, however, mention that the child had been exposed to a relative with pulmonary tuberculosis—specifically, his grandfather, who had previously completed treatment for the disease. Dietary history revealed that the child frequently ate processed and high-calorie foods, with inadequate consumption of fresh fruits, vegetables, and nutritionally balanced home-prepared meals. On assessment, his weight was 44 kg, height 112 cm, and his body mass index was calculated as 35. Clinical examination showed a respiratory rate of 20 per minute, with mild tachypnea. Right-sided chest expansion was diminished. Percussion revealed stony dullness in the right subscapular, infra-axillary, and axillary regions. Tactile vocal fremitus was markedly reduced on the right compared to the left. Breath sounds were decreased in the same right-sided regions. There were no signs of pallor, cyanosis, icterus, digital clubbing, or lymphadenopathy. The abdomen was soft, with no evidence of hepatosplenomegaly. Neurologically, he was alert and oriented to time, place, and person. Cardiovascular evaluation found tachycardia without any murmurs. Room air oxygen saturation measured between 88% and 89%, and his pulse was 110 beats per minute. Due to ongoing respiratory distress and tachypnea, he was transferred to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit, started on oxygen via face mask at 5 liters per minute, and a chest radiograph was requested. Empirical treatment with piperacillin-tazobactam was initiated after drawing blood cultures. Imaging demonstrated a right pleural effusion with blunting of the right costophrenic angle (figure 1, Table 2). Point-of-care lung ultrasound estimated the effusion at approximately one liter (figure 2, Table 2). Under aseptic conditions, a right-sided thoracentesis was performed, yielding about one liter of dark red fluid. Following this, tachypnea resolved, and his room air saturation improved to 95–98%. The child's chest pain subsided, and oxygen supplementation was discontinued within 24 hours. He was transferred to the ward the following day and subsequently discharged. Pleural fluid obtained during thoracentesis was sent for analysis.

Six days later, the child was readmitted with recurrence of right-sided chest pain, tachypnea, and shortness of breath. He was again moved to the PICU, where repeat chest X-ray identified another right-sided pleural effusion. A second thoracentesis was performed, once again yielding about one liter of dark red fluid. Pleural fluid analysis demonstrated a mildly increased adenosine deaminase (ADA) level (Table 1), and considering the reported contact with tuberculosis, empirical antitubercular therapy was commenced.

Despite treatment, a third hospital admission occurred five days later for similar symptoms—right-sided chest pain and respiratory difficulty. Upon further questioning, the child recalled having sustained blunt trauma to the abdomen from a bicycle handle one month earlier. Before considering placement of an intercostal drainage (ICD) catheter, a contrast-enhanced chest CT was performed. Imaging showed patchy areas of parenchymal consolidation in the right lung, right hydropneumothorax, and a multiloculated, elongated, hypodense collection extending from the right pleural space and tracking from the right paraesophageal area into the upper left retroperitoneum, reaching up to the pancreatic tail (Table 2). An ICD was inserted, resulting in drainage of about one liter of dark red fluid (figure 3). These CT findings, together with the bloody nature of the pleural fluid and the history of abdominal trauma, led to consideration of a pancreaticopleural fistula (PPF). Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) was performed, which demonstrated pancreatitis with a tubular cystic collection arising from the distal body and tail of the pancreas, extending retroperitoneally and tracking through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity, with a narrow tract communicating with the left pleural cavity as well. The collection was seen in communication with the distal pancreatic duct, and there was ductal narrowing proximal to the communication site. MRCP strongly indicated the presence of a PPF (figures 4–6, Table 1). Differential diagnosis:

The patient arrived with pleural effusion and chest pain, along with a clear account of abdominal trauma that had occurred one month earlier. Besides post-traumatic pancreatitis, alternative causes of pleural effusion were excluded. The child lacked a history of fever, prolonged cough, or weight loss. Although there was documented family contact with tuberculosis and the pleural fluid ADA was slightly raised, GeneXpert testing for MTB yielded a negative result, thus pulmonary Koch’s was excluded. The absence of fever and lack of neutrophilic exudate in pleural fluid helped to rule out empyema. While malignancies such as lung cancer, leukemia, lymphoma, or metastatic tumors may present with amylase-rich pleural fluid, their likelihood was discounted due to the lack of fever, absence of weight loss, a normal blood profile, and a normal peripheral smear; additionally, cytological analysis of pleural fluid showed no malignant cells. Boerhaave’s syndrome (esophageal perforation), another cause of amylase-rich pleural effusion, was dismissed since the patient was passing stools without difficulty and reported no history of chest pain or vomiting. Acquired diaphragmatic hernia was excluded because chest imaging did not reveal any abdominal organs in the thoracic cavity. Hepatic hydrothorax was considered unlikely given the normal liver function tests, unremarkable liver imaging, and no background of chronic liver disease. Finally, with normal findings on renal imaging and renal function assessments, renal pathology or hydronephrosis was excluded.

Treatment:

In the PICU, the patient received humidified oxygen via mask for respiratory support. Ultrasound-guided thoracocentesis was performed on two occasions, each time yielding approximately one litre of blood-tinged pleural fluid. Empirical antitubercular therapy was initiated but discontinued once the diagnosis was established through MRCP. During the third hospital admission, an intercostal drain (ICD) was placed on the right side under sterile conditions, resulting in the drainage of another litre of hemorrhagic pleural fluid into the ICD bag. Due to the rarity of PPF in children and the absence of established treatment protocols, management relied on previously documented case reports. Initial conservative management was chosen, with the child kept nil per oral and commenced on an Octreotide infusion at a dose of 30 µg/kg/day (range: 10–40 µg/kg/day). Total parenteral nutrition as well as pancreatic enzyme supplementation were also provided. Over a period of 10 days, the output from the ICD gradually diminished and eventually ceased. The ICD was subsequently removed, and the child was discharged.

Outcome and follow-up:

Since leaving the hospital, the child has experienced no recurrence of symptoms. He continues to attend scheduled monthly follow-up appointments with me. There have been no additional incidents of pleural effusion since his hospital discharge (S17). The patient has adopted recommended lifestyle changes, maintaining a balanced diet and engaging in regular physical activity. He has resumed his usual routine and is consistently attending school. The child is able to participate in vigorous activities such as running and playing without any limitations.

Discussion:

Pancreaticopleural fistula (PPF) is an exceedingly rare and unusual complication of pancreatic disorders in children. To date, only 47 cases of PPF have been reported in the paediatric age group. This entity may arise in association with either acute or chronic pancreatitis, or following paediatric abdominal trauma. The precise frequency in children is not established, but PPF complicates approximately 0.4% of pancreatitis cases overall. Genetic mutations such as Serine Peptidase Inhibitor Kazal type 1 (SPINK1) and PRSS1 play a significant role, being implicated in up to 30% of childhood chronic pancreatitis. Non-genetic triggers, such as blunt trauma to the abdomen, are also significant, representing 32% of 25 children included in the analysis by Yang et al. Idiopathic instances persist in the literature, likely owing to undetected genetic predispositions or ductal anomalies. Disruption of the pancreatic duct or a pseudocyst can permit pancreatic fluid to ascend through diaphragmatic openings—most commonly the aortic or oesophageal hiatus—or more rarely, by direct trans-diaphragmatic extension, leading to the formation of a fistulous tract into the pleural space. These cases are commonly missed due to their predominant thoracic symptomatology; diagnostic efforts are often focused on the respiratory system when the underlying cause is intra-abdominal. Review of the literature yielded 21 pertinent reports; in 68% of these, presenting features were respiratory, including extensive, persistent unilateral or bilateral pleural effusions, dyspnoea, and cough. Fewer than 20% of patients experienced abdominal complaints, like epigastric pain, vomiting, or weight loss. About 24% exhibited constitutional symptoms such as fever. Zi Yin Shang's 2023 case in Frontiers in Paediatrics described PPF in an 8-year-old presenting solely with a left-sided chest bulge of two weeks' duration—such initial symptoms are exceptionally uncommon. In this situation, pleural effusion likely developed gradually post-abdominal trauma. Adaptive respiratory capacity in the unaffected lung permitted the patient to compensate, masking the typical signs such as dyspnoea or cough. Furthermore, school bullying led the child to conceal the history of abdominal trauma. The initial workup investigated haemorrhagic pleural fluid for tuberculosis and malignancy, but an alternative diagnosis emerged. In our experience, the child similarly presented with recurrent pulmonary symptoms including chest pain and repeated right pleural fluid collections. Eliciting a trauma history required several interviews with the patient, and subsequent MRCP findings directed attention to a pancreatic etiology. Therefore, PPF may mimic a primary pulmonary disorder and can mislead clinicians unless comprehensive history taking and relevant imaging are pursued. Fluid in PPF accumulates as a result of trauma or infection causing rupture of the pancreatic duct or pseudocyst and leakage of pancreatic secretions. Should the anterior portion of the pancreas rupture, fluid enters the peritoneal cavity, causing ascites; if the posterior pancreas is affected, secretions dissect through the retroperitoneum upward, traverse the aortic or oesophageal hiatus, and penetrate the pleura, resulting in pleural effusion. Essentially, the internal pancreatic fistula resembles a defective pipe, channeling pancreatic fluids into the thoracic cavity. In adult cases, chronic pancreatitis is most frequently linked to alcohol use. Uchiyama et al provided a comprehensive review of 113 Japanese cases, noting that 99% were due to chronic alcoholic pancreatitis, with 68% experiencing chest symptoms. By contrast, paediatric chronic pancreatitis predominantly arises from genetic factors. Mutations in genes such as PRSS1, the CF transmembrane regulator, SPINK1, and chymotrypsin C are established causes. Notably, PRSS1 mutations R122H (exon 3) and N291 (exon 2) are prototypical. For children with recurrent or chronic pancreatitis, a thorough family history is indicated, with a high index of suspicion for hereditary aetiology. Diagnosis is frequently delayed because patients undergo extensive respiratory investigations before the possibility of pancreatic involvement is entertained. A pleural fluid amylase concentration of ≥1000 IU/L is diagnostic of PPF. However, tuberculosis, oesophageal rupture, or tumours (lung or pancreas) must be excluded, as these may also elevate pleural fluid amylase. The pancreatic isoenzyme within pleural fluid differentiates PPF from other sources; elevated salivary-type amylase may be found in the other conditions. Therefore, typing of pleural fluid amylase should be included in diagnostic protocols for suspected PPF.
