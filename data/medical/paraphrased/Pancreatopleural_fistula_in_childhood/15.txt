Title: Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood

Hans Vaish, Vipin Vaish, Shalini Suri, Vasudha Tomar

Abstract:

Summary: A preadolescent male experienced multiple episodes of right-sided pleural effusions, presenting with chest pain and respiratory distress that transiently resolved following fluid aspiration. The aspirated pleural fluid was consistently dark red in appearance. Due to frequent recurrences, a right intercostal chest drain was inserted. His medical history was notable for recent abdominal trauma sustained from impact with a bicycle handlebar. Chest CT imaging revealed right-sided patchy pulmonary consolidation, a hydropneumothorax, and a multiloculated hypodense area extending from the right paraesophageal region towards the pancreatic tail. Laboratory analysis demonstrated elevated amylase and lipase levels in both serum and pleural fluid. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography identified bilateral pancreatopleural fistulas, more pronounced on the right. Management included initiation of nil per os (NPO), administration of total parenteral nutrition, and Octreotide therapy. Over 10 days, the volume of chest drainage diminished, the intercostal drain was removed, and the patient was discharged home.

Background:

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is a very uncommon condition characterized by recurrent, large, and often blood-tinged pleural effusions. It has been identified in patients with acute or chronic pancreatitis as well as following pancreatic injury. The clinical picture can be misleading, as individuals typically develop symptoms of respiratory compromise secondary to repeated pleural fluid accumulation, prompting an initial focus on thoracic causes rather than pancreatic disease, which can result in diagnostic delays. In pediatric patients, only 47 cases have been documented, compared to over 300 adult cases reported in the literature.

Case presentation:

A child was brought to the outpatient clinic by his mother with a one-week history of difficulty breathing and right-sided chest pain. According to the mother, the respiratory discomfort had progressively worsened over the past seven days. There was no reported history of parental consanguinity. The mother mentioned a contact history of tuberculosis (Koch’s) with the child’s grandfather, who had been adequately treated for pulmonary tuberculosis. The child’s diet mainly consisted of processed, high-calorie foods with few fresh fruits, vegetables, and nutritionally balanced home meals. On assessment, his weight was 44 kg, height 112 cm, and his BMI was calculated as 35. Physical examination showed a respiratory rate of 20 breaths per minute, with mild tachypnea present. There was diminished movement of the right side of the chest, and percussion produced a stony dull note over the right subscapular, right infra-axillary, and axillary areas. Vocal fremitus on the right was notably reduced compared to the left. Decreased breath sounds were detected in the right subscapular, right infra-axillary, and axillary regions. No pallor, cyanosis, icterus, clubbing, or lymphadenopathy was observed. The abdomen was soft with no evidence of hepatosplenomegaly. The child was alert and oriented to time, place, and person. Cardiovascular evaluation revealed tachycardia without murmurs. His oxygen saturation measured 88%–89% on ambient air, with a pulse rate of 110 per minute. Due to respiratory distress and tachypnea, he was transferred to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit and initiated on oxygen therapy via mask at a rate of 5 L/min. A chest X-ray was requested, and empiric therapy with piperacillin-tazobactam was commenced following blood culture collection. Imaging identified a right-sided pleural effusion with blunting of the right costophrenic angle (figure 1) (Table 2). Bedside lung ultrasonography revealed a pleural effusion estimated at one liter (figure 2) (Table 2). Right-sided pleural fluid aspiration was performed under aseptic conditions, yielding approximately one liter of dark red fluid. Following thoracocentesis, tachypnea improved, and the child’s oxygen saturation on room air increased to 95%–98%. Chest pain also lessened, allowing discontinuation of oxygen within 24 hours. He was moved to the general ward the next day and then discharged. The aspirated pleural fluid was sent for laboratory analysis. However, six days later, the child was readmitted with recurrence of right-sided chest pain, tachypnea, and respiratory difficulty. He was again admitted to the PICU, and chest X-ray once more demonstrated a right pleural effusion. Thoracocentesis was repeated, removing about one liter of dark red pleural fluid. Pleural fluid ADA (Adenosine Deaminase) was modestly elevated (Table 1), and given the history of Koch’s contact, antitubercular therapy was empirically initiated. The child was admitted for a third time five days later with similar symptoms of right chest pain and dyspnea. On further history taking, he disclosed having experienced blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handlebar one month prior. Before consideration of intercostal drainage (ICD), a contrast-enhanced CT scan of the chest was performed. The CT demonstrated patchy consolidation in the right lung, right hydropneumothorax, and a complex multiloculated, hypodense collection from the right pleural space tracking from the paraesophageal region down to the upper left retroperitoneum near the pancreatic tail (Table 2). An ICD was inserted, draining around one liter of dark red pleural fluid (figure 3). The combination of CT findings, dark red aspirated fluid, and a trauma history suggested a pancreaticopleural fistula (PPF). Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) was obtained the same day and showed evidence of pancreatitis with a tubular cystic collection arising from the distal pancreatic body and tail extending posteriorly in the retroperitoneum and superiorly through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity; a slender tract to the left pleural space was also identified. The collection communicated with the distal pancreatic duct, with proximal ductal narrowing. MRCP favored a diagnosis of PPF (figures 4–6) (Table 1). Amylase and lipase levels in serum and pleural fluid were measured. Differential diagnosis:

The patient exhibited pleural effusion accompanied by chest pain and had a clear prior episode of abdominal trauma one month earlier. All causes of pleural effusion aside from post-traumatic pancreatitis were excluded. This patient had no reports of fever, persistent cough, or weight loss. There was a reported household exposure to Koch’s disease, and the pleural fluid adenosine deaminase (ADA) was slightly increased; nevertheless, GeneXpert testing for Mycobacterium tuberculosis was negative. Pulmonary tuberculosis was, therefore, excluded. The absence of fever and lack of neutrophilic exudate in the pleural fluid eliminated empyema as a possibility. Malignancies—such as pulmonary cancer, leukemia, lymphoma, or metastatic disease—may also present with pleural fluid rich in amylase; however, the lack of fever, no weight loss, unremarkable blood findings, and a normal peripheral blood smear helped to rule out malignancy. Cytological examination of the pleural fluid also revealed no malignant cells. Boerhaave’s syndrome, characterized by esophageal perforation and often accompanied by amylase-rich pleural fluid, was discounted due to the patient's normal bowel movements and absence of prior chest pain or vomiting. Imaging did not reveal any abdominal organs in the chest, thereby excluding an acquired diaphragmatic hernia. Hepatic hydrothorax was not considered given normal liver function studies and imaging, as well as no history indicative of chronic liver disease. Renal causes, including hydronephrosis, were ruled out due to normal renal imaging and function.

Treatment:

In the PICU, the patient received humidified oxygen via face mask for respiratory support. Thoracocentesis was performed under ultrasound guidance on two separate occasions, yielding approximately one liter of blood-tinged pleural fluid each time. Antitubercular therapy was initiated on an empirical basis but discontinued following definitive diagnosis via MRCP. During the third hospital admission, an intercostal drain (ICD) was inserted on the right side with strict aseptic technique, resulting in drainage of about one liter of blood-stained pleural fluid into the collection bag. Since PPF is an uncommon and rare diagnosis in children, standardized treatment protocols are lacking, and management decisions rely largely on individual case reports. Conservative therapy was chosen initially, and the child was kept nil per os while Octreotide infusion was administered at a dosage of 30 µg/kg/day (range: 10–40 µg/kg/day). Total parenteral nutrition along with pancreatic enzyme supplementation was commenced. Over a 10-day period, the drainage output from the ICD diminished progressively and eventually ceased. The ICD was subsequently removed, and the child was discharged from the hospital.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following his hospital discharge, the child has remained asymptomatic. He continues to see me for scheduled monthly follow-up visits. Since leaving the hospital, there have been no further occurrences of pleural effusions (S17). The patient has adopted lifestyle changes, adhering to a balanced diet and engaging in regular physical activity as recommended. He has resumed his usual daily routines and is consistently attending school. He participates in vigorous activities such as running and playing without experiencing any problems.

Discussion:

Pancreaticopleural fistula (PPF) represents a very rare and uncommon consequence of pancreatic pathology in children. To date, there are only 47 reported paediatric PPF cases. It may develop in the context of either acute or chronic pancreatitis or as a result of trauma in paediatric patients. The actual frequency in children is not established; however, in the general population, it is a complication in approximately 0.4% of pancreatitis cases. Mutations in the serine peptidase inhibitor Kazal type 1 (SPINK1) and PRSS1 genes play a significant role, with these alterations being identified in up to 30% of paediatric chronic pancreatitis instances. Blunt trauma to the abdomen is also a considerable non-genetic precipitant, being responsible for 32% of PPF cases among a 25-patient series reviewed by Yang and colleagues. Cases with no clear etiology persist, likely attributable to as yet undetected genetic factors or variations in pancreatic duct anatomy.

Fluid from the pancreas may escape through a disrupted duct or pseudocyst, migrating via normal diaphragmatic passages—typically the aortic or esophageal hiatus—or less commonly by directly eroding through the diaphragm, resulting in a fistulous tract into the pleural space. Such cases are often overlooked since children tend to present primarily with respiratory complaints; most diagnostic efforts then focus on the chest, despite the underlying pathology being abdominal. Our literature review yielded 21 pertinent publications, revealing that 68% of paediatric PPFs presented predominantly with respiratory symptoms such as large or recurrent, unilateral or bilateral pleural effusions, breathlessness, and cough. Fewer than 20% displayed abdominal manifestations including upper abdominal pain, vomiting, and weight reduction. Constitutional features like fever occurred in 24%. For instance, a case described by Zi Yin Shang in Frontiers in Paediatrics 2023 featured an 8-year-old with a two-week history of left-sided chest prominence without classic respiratory symptoms. Presentation with only chest bulging is extremely unusual. In this patient, it was postulated that the pleural effusion developed gradually after abdominal trauma, with compensatory function from the contralateral lung averting typical respiratory findings. The child's social situation led him to hide the history of abdominal injury. The initial workup focused on tuberculosis and malignancy, but ultimately a different diagnosis was made.

Similarly, in our patient, right-sided chest pain and recurrent pleural fluid accumulation predominated. Detailed re-evaluation uncovered a history of abdominal trauma, and MRCP findings implicated a pancreatic source. Hence, PPF may masquerade as a primary lung disease, potentially diverting physicians from the actual diagnosis without a thorough patient history and targeted imaging. The accumulation of pleural fluid in PPF generally results from external trauma or infection causing pancreatic duct or pseudocyst rupture. If the anterior pancreatic surface ruptures, fluid collects in the abdominal cavity, leading to ascites. With posterior rupture, fluid leaks retroperitoneally, typically tracking upwards into the mediastinum through the diaphragmatic hiatuses and subsequently entering the pleural cavity—ultimately forming a pancreatic fistula, diverting secretions into the thoracic compartment.

In adult patients, chronic pancreatitis from alcohol use is the leading cause of PPF. Uchiyama et al provided the most comprehensive national review, compiling 113 Japanese cases—99% related to alcoholic pancreatitis, with respiratory symptoms noted in 68%. Contrastingly, genetic aetiologies are more prominent in children, where variants in PRSS1, the cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator, SPINK1, and chymotrypsin C genes are implicated, especially PRSS1 mutations like R122H and N291. If paediatric pancreatitis is recurrent or chronic, clinicians should thoroughly assess family history and consider hereditary pancreatitis.

Diagnosis is usually delayed, as children undergo extensive pulmonary evaluation before a pancreatic etiology is considered. Pleural fluid amylase values ≥1000 IU/L are indicative of PPF, but differential diagnosis should include tuberculosis, esophageal perforation, and pulmonary or pancreatic neoplasms—these can also yield elevated pleural fluid amylase. Measuring pancreatic-type amylase in pleural fluid helps to differentiate PPF from these other conditions, which typically present with salivary-type amylase. Amylase isoenzyme testing may be employed as part of the diagnostic algorithm. MRCP detects the fistula and outlines ductal anatomy in 70%–80% of cases. While CT imaging effectively demonstrates pancreatic calcifications, ductal dilation, or pseudocysts, it only delineates the fistulous tract in 33%–47% of cases.
