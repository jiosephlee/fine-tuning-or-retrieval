Title: Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood

Hans Vaish, Vipin Vaish, Shalini Suri, Vasudha Tomar

Abstract:

Summary: An older child developed recurrent right-sided pleural effusions, accompanied by chest pain and respiratory difficulties that resolved briefly following aspiration. The pleural aspirate repeatedly appeared dark red. Ongoing effusions necessitated insertion of a right intercostal drain. The medical history revealed recent abdominal trauma involving a bicycle handlebar. Computed tomography of the thorax demonstrated right-sided patchy lung consolidation, hydropneumothorax, and a multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right paraesophageal area toward the pancreatic tail. Both the serum and pleural fluid showed increased amylase and lipase levels. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography identified pancreatopleural fistulas on both sides (predominantly right-sided). Management included keeping the patient nil per oral, provision of total parenteral nutrition, and administration of Octreotide. After 10 days, pleural fluid output lessened, permitting removal of the chest drain, and the patient was subsequently discharged.

Background:

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is a very uncommon condition characterized by repeated, large, and occasionally hemorrhagic pleural effusions. This complication arises in the context of acute or chronic pancreatitis, as well as pancreatic injury. Its clinical presentation is often misleading, as patients typically exhibit respiratory symptoms caused by recurring pleural effusions, leading clinicians to focus on thoracic causes and consequently postponing the recognition of the pancreatic origin. To date, only 47 pediatric cases of PPF have been documented, compared to over 300 reported occurrences in adult patients.

Case presentation:

A pediatric patient presented to the outpatient clinic with his mother, reporting a one-week history of breathing difficulties and right-sided chest discomfort. The mother described a progressive worsening of symptoms over the course of seven days. There was no background of consanguinity. She reported prior exposure to tuberculosis from the child’s grandfather, who had been treated for pulmonary Koch's. The dietary assessment indicated the child often ate processed and calorie-dense foods, with limited intake of fresh produce or well-balanced home-cooked dishes. The patient weighed 44 kg, had a height of 112 cm, resulting in a BMI of 35. Upon clinical assessment, the child’s respiratory rate was 20 per minute with evidence of mild tachypnoea. Right-sided chest movements were diminished. Percussion revealed stony dullness in the right subscapular, infra-axillary, and axillary areas. Vocal fremitus was markedly decreased on the right compared to the left. Reduced breath sounds were also noted in the right subscapular and axillary regions. There were no clinical signs of pallor, cyanosis, icterus, clubbing, or lymphadenopathy. The abdominal examination was benign with no hepatosplenomegaly. The child was alert and oriented to person, place, and time. Cardiovascular exam showed tachycardia without murmurs. Peripheral oxygen saturation was measured at 88%–89% in room air, and the heart rate was 110 bpm. Owing to tachypnoea and respiratory compromise, the patient was admitted to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit for supplemental oxygen at 5 l/min via mask. A chest radiograph was requested and, after drawing blood cultures, empirical therapy with piperacillin-tazobactam was commenced. Imaging showed a right-sided pleural effusion with blunting of the right costophrenic angle (figure 1) (Table 2). Bedside lung ultrasound demonstrated approximately one liter of pleural fluid (figure 2) (Table 2). Under sterile technique, thoracentesis was performed on the right side, and about one liter of dark red fluid was withdrawn. The tachypnoea resolved post-procedure and oxygen saturation increased to 95%–98% on room air. Chest pain improved and oxygen therapy was discontinued within 24 hours. The patient was transferred to the general ward the following day and subsequently discharged. The pleural fluid was submitted for analysis. Six days later, the patient was readmitted with similar complaints—right-sided chest pain, tachypnoea, and dyspnoea. He was again moved to the PICU; chest radiography again revealed a right-sided pleural effusion. Repeat thoracentesis yielded roughly one liter of dark red pleural fluid. Pleural fluid adenosine deaminase (ADA) was mildly increased (Table 1), and with a history of tuberculosis exposure, empirical antitubercular therapy was initiated. Five days after this, the child was admitted a third time, again presenting with right-sided chest pain and respiratory difficulty. On re-evaluation, the child reported a history of blunt trauma to the abdomen from a bicycle handle one month earlier. Prior to intercostal drainage (ICD) insertion, a contrast-enhanced chest CT was obtained, showing patchy airspace consolidation in the right lung, right hydropneumothorax, and an elongated, multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right pleural space at the paraoesophageal level to the upper left retroperitoneal area, reaching the pancreatic tail (Table 2). An ICD was placed, draining approximately one liter of dark red pleural fluid (figure 3). The combination of CT findings, dark pleural aspirate, and trauma history suggested a pancreaticopleural fistula (PPF). Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) was ordered that day and identified pancreatitis with a tubular, cystic fluid collection in the distal body and tail of the pancreas extending retroperitoneally and ascending via the oesophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity. A narrow tract to the left pleural cavity was also noted. The collection communicated with the distal pancreatic duct, which showed narrowing proximally. MRCP supported the diagnosis of PPF (figures 4-6) (Table 1). Amylase and lipase levels in serum and pleural fluid were assessed; the pleural fluid contained markedly elevated amylase (6118 U/L) and lipase (7211 U/L) (Table 2), consistent with PPF. Antitubercular therapy was discontinued, and the patient was made nil per oral with total parenteral nutrition. Octreotide infusion was initiated at 30 µg/kg/day (range 10–40 µg/kg/day). Differential diagnosis:

The patient presented with chest pain and pleural effusion, along with a documented abdominal injury sustained one month earlier. All other potential etiologies for pleural effusion were excluded aside from post-traumatic pancreatitis. The child’s history was negative for fever, persistent cough, and weight loss. Although there was reported household exposure to Koch’s disease and a mild increase in pleural fluid ADA, GeneXpert testing for MTB returned negative, thus eliminating pulmonary Koch’s from consideration. The absence of fever and lack of neutrophilic exudate in the pleural fluid excluded empyema. Pleural fluid rich in amylase may be associated with malignancy (including lung cancer, leukemia, lymphoma, or metastatic neoplasms); nevertheless, malignancy was dismissed due to lack of fever, absence of weight loss, normal hematological results, normal peripheral smear, and pleural fluid cytology showing no malignant cells. Amylase-rich pleural fluid can also result from Boerhaave’s syndrome (esophageal perforation); however, this diagnosis was excluded, as the child maintained normal bowel movements and there was no history of vomiting or chest pain. Acquired diaphragmatic hernia was not supported by imaging, which showed no abdominal organs in the thoracic cavity. Hepatic hydrothorax was excluded since liver function and imaging were unremarkable, and there was no history of chronic liver disease. Normal renal studies and imaging ruled out renal disease or hydronephrosis.

Treatment:

Humidified oxygen delivered via mask was administered for respiratory support in the PICU. The patient underwent ultrasound-guided thoracocentesis on two occasions, with approximately one liter of blood-tinged pleural fluid evacuated each time. Empirical antitubercular therapy was initiated but discontinued after the diagnosis was established through MRCP. During the third hospitalization, an intercostal drain (ICD) was placed on the right side under strict aseptic technique, yielding another liter of blood-stained pleural fluid, which was collected in the ICD bag. Due to the rarity of PPF in pediatric cases and the absence of standardized management guidelines, treatment approaches are informed by case reports. Initially, a conservative strategy was pursued: the patient was kept nil per os and received an Octreotide infusion at a dose of 30 µg/kg/day (ranging from 10–40 µg/kg/day). Total parenteral nutrition and pancreatic enzyme supplementation were initiated. Over the ensuing ten days, the volume of drainage from the ICD gradually declined and eventually ceased. The ICD was subsequently removed, and the child was discharged from hospital care.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following hospital discharge, the child has remained asymptomatic. He attends monthly follow-up appointments with me. Since leaving the hospital, there have been no further incidents of recurrent pleural effusions (S17). He has adjusted his lifestyle, adhering to a balanced diet and consistent exercise regimen as recommended. The child has resumed normal routines, including regular school attendance, and is able to participate in vigorous activities such as running and playing without limitations.

Discussion:

PPF represents an exceptionally rare complication of pancreatic disorders in children. To date, only 47 cases involving the paediatric population have been reported. This condition may occur in the context of acute or chronic pancreatitis, or following paediatric trauma. The precise frequency in children remains undetermined, but overall, PPF complicates approximately 0.4% of pancreatitis cases. Mutations in the serine peptidase inhibitor Kazal type 1 (SPINK1) and PRSS1 genes are frequently implicated, accounting for as much as 30% of chronic pancreatitis in paediatric patients. Blunt trauma to the abdomen constitutes a significant non-genetic factor, comprising 32% in the 25-case analysis by Yang et al. Idiopathic occurrences persist, potentially owing to unrecognized genetic or ductal anomalies. Pancreatic fluid originating from a damaged pancreatic duct or pseudocyst can track through anatomical diaphragmatic hiatuses—most commonly the aortic or oesophageal hiatus—or, less frequently, via direct trans-diaphragmatic erosion, forming a fistulous connection to the pleural space. These cases are often overlooked, as presentation predominantly involves thoracic symptoms, prompting investigations for pulmonary disease while the actual pathology lies within the abdomen. Literature review identified 21 pertinent reports, 68% of which described respiratory manifestations such as large, recurring unilateral or bilateral pleural effusions, dyspnoea, and cough. Fewer than 20% demonstrated abdominal signs like epigastric pain, vomiting, or weight loss, and 24% exhibited systemic features including fever. Zi Yin Shang, in Frontiers in Paediatrics 2023, described an 8-year-old boy presenting solely with left-sided chest protrusion persisting for 2 weeks—an extraordinarily rare initial symptom. In this case, pleural effusion likely developed gradually after abdominal trauma, and adaptive compensation by the healthy lung permitted the patient to adjust without typical respiratory symptoms such as cough or dyspnoea. The child, subjected to bullying at school, concealed his abdominal trauma. Evaluation focused initially on tuberculosis and malignancy for hemorrhagic pleural effusion, but the diagnosis ultimately diverged. Similarly, our patient experienced recurrent pulmonary complaints—chest pain and repeated right-sided pleural collections. Detailed history-taking eventually uncovered prior abdominal trauma, and MRCP guided identification of pancreatic involvement. Consequently, PPF can mimic pulmonary disease and may mislead clinicians, highlighting the importance of comprehensive history and appropriate imaging.

Fluid accumulation in PPF arises following external trauma or infection-induced rupture of the pancreatic duct or pseudocyst, causing leakage of pancreatic fluid. Anterior ruptures result in ascites, as fluid enters the abdominal cavity, whereas posterior ruptures permit fluid to track into the retroperitoneum, ascend along least resistance, enter the mediastinum through the aortic or oesophageal hiatus, and penetrate the pleura to form an effusion. The internal pancreatic fistula effectively acts as a conduit, channeling secretions into the pleural space. In adults, chronic pancreatitis due to alcohol predominates in the etiology of PPF. Uchiyama et al carried out the most extensive country-specific review, reporting 113 cases in the Japanese literature, with 99% associated with chronic alcoholic pancreatitis and 68% showing chest symptoms. Contrastingly, in children, hereditary pancreatitis prevails, attributed to mutations in PRSS1, CF transmembrane regulator, SPINK1, and chymotrypsin C genes; key mutations include R122H and N291 located in exons 3 and 2, respectively, of PRSS1. For recurrent or chronic paediatric pancreatitis, in-depth family history and consideration of genetic causes are imperative.

Diagnosis is frequently delayed because patients often undergo extensive pulmonary evaluation prior to consideration of pancreatic involvement. A pleural fluid amylase concentration ≥1000 IU/L is diagnostic of PPF. Differential diagnoses such as tuberculosis, oesophageal rupture, and lung or pancreatic neoplasms should be excluded, as these conditions may also exhibit elevated pleural fluid amylase. The amylase subtype differentiates PPF from other entities: in PPF, the enzyme is pancreatic in origin, whereas other conditions typically feature salivary-type amylase. Pleural fluid amylase typing can further aid diagnosis. MRCP yields sensitivity of 70–80% for detecting the fistula and assessing duct anatomy. CT identifies calcifications, pseudocysts, and ductal dilatation, but visualizes fistulas in only 33–47% of cases. Endoscopic cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) is the gold standard for therapeutic decision-making; pediatric fistula visualization rates range from 38% to 78% via ERCP.

PPF management encompasses medical, endoscopic, and surgical modalities. Due to its rarity, standardized treatment guidelines are lacking.
