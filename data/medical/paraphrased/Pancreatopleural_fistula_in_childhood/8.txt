Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood

Hans Vaish, Vipin Vaish, Shalini Suri, Vasudha Tomar

Abstract:

Summary: A preadolescent male exhibited repeated right-sided pleural effusions, accompanied by chest pain and respiratory distress, with only transient relief following aspiration procedures. The pleural aspirate consistently appeared dark red. Persistent effusions necessitated the insertion of a right intercostal drain. Further history taking uncovered a recent episode of abdominal trauma involving a bicycle handlebar. Chest computed tomography identified patchy consolidation in the right lung, hydropneumothorax, and a multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right paraesophageal area to the pancreatic tail. Laboratory tests revealed that both serum and pleural fluid amylase and lipase levels were raised. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography verified the presence of bilateral pancreatopleural fistulas, with greater involvement on the right side. Management consisted of bowel rest, total parenteral nutrition, and administration of Octreotide. Within ten days, the pleural drainage diminished, the chest tube was removed, and the patient was ultimately discharged.

Background:

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) constitutes a notably uncommon condition, characterized by recurrent, substantial, and occasionally hemorrhagic pleural effusions. PPF has been documented as a complication arising from acute pancreatitis, chronic pancreatitis, and pancreatic injury. The clinical challenge in diagnosing PPF arises from its predominant manifestation as respiratory distress due to repeated pleural effusions, prompting clinicians to focus on thoracic causes while overlooking the pancreatic origin of the problem, which subsequently delays accurate diagnosis. In the paediatric population, only 47 instances of PPF have been described, in contrast to over 300 reported adult cases.

Case presentation:

A young boy presented to the outpatient department, accompanied by his mother, with complaints of dyspnea and right-sided thoracic pain persisting for one week. According to the mother, his respiratory discomfort had been progressively worsening throughout this period. There was no history of consanguinity in the family. Notably, the child had a reported history of exposure to tuberculosis via his grandfather, who had pulmonary Koch's disease and had received complete treatment. Dietary assessment indicated a frequent intake of processed, high-calorie foods, with a deficiency in fresh produce and nutritionally balanced home meals. The patient weighed 44 kg, measured 112 cm in height, yielding a body mass index of 35.

On physical assessment, the respiratory rate was 20/min, and mild tachypnea was noted. There was a reduction in chest expansion on the right side. Percussion demonstrated stony dullness over the right subscapular, right infra-axillary, and axillary areas. Vocal fremitus was significantly decreased on the right in comparison to the left side. Likewise, breath sounds were diminished in these same regions. There was no evidence of pallor, cyanosis, icterus, clubbing, or lymphadenopathy. The abdomen was soft without hepatosplenomegaly. The child was fully alert and oriented. Examination of the cardiovascular system revealed tachycardia but no murmurs. Room air oxygen saturation was recorded at 88%–89%, and the heart rate was 110 /min.

Due to tachypnea and respiratory distress, the patient was transferred to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit and commenced on oxygen therapy at 5 L/min via mask. A chest X-ray was performed and, following the collection of blood cultures, empirical therapy with piperacillin-tazobactam was initiated. The chest radiograph displayed a right-sided pleural effusion with blunting of the right costophrenic angle (figure 1) (Table 2). A point-of-care lung ultrasound estimated approximately one liter of pleural effusion (figure 2) (Table 2). Thoracentesis under sterile conditions yielded about one liter of dark red pleural fluid. Following the procedure, the child’s tachypnea resolved, and oxygen saturation improved to 95%–98% on room air. Chest pain subsided and oxygen support was discontinued within 24 hours. He was transferred to the general ward the next day and eventually discharged. Pleural fluid samples were sent for further evaluation.

However, the patient was readmitted six days later, exhibiting recurrent right-sided chest pain, tachypnea, and dyspnea. He was transferred once again to the PICU, where chest X-ray revealed another right-sided pleural effusion. Repeated thoracentesis produced approximately one liter of similarly colored dark red pleural fluid. Pleural fluid Adenosine Deaminase (ADA) levels were modestly elevated (Table 1), and given his prior TB contact, empirical anti-tubercular therapy was started. The child required hospitalization a third time after five more days due to recurring right chest pain and respiratory difficulty.

At this juncture, a more detailed history uncovered a recent incident of blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle, which had occurred a month before presentation. A contrast-enhanced CT of the chest, performed prior to intercostal drainage (ICD) placement, revealed patchy right lung air space consolidation, right-sided hydropneumothorax, and a multi-loculated, lobulated, hypodense collection extending from the right pleural cavity down to the left retroperitoneal space, reaching the pancreatic tail (Table 2). An ICD was placed, draining around one liter of dark red pleural fluid (figure 3). The CT findings, combined with the appearance of the pleural fluid and the history of trauma, suggested a diagnosis of Pancreaticopleural Fistula (PPF). To further investigate, Magnetic Resonance Cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) was performed the same day. MRCP identified pancreatitis with a tubular cystic collection in the distal body and tail of the pancreas, extending posteriorly within the retroperitoneum and tracking through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity, with a narrow channel also noted to the left pleural space. The collection was in continuity with the distal pancreatic duct, with narrowing observed proximal to the communication. These MRCP findings were consistent with PPF (figures 4–6) (Table 1). Serum and pleural fluid amylase and lipase were also analyzed, revealing substantially elevated concentrations of amylase (6118 U/L) and lipase (7211 U/L) in pleural fluid (Table 2), corroborating the PPF diagnosis.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient exhibited pleural effusion and chest pain, with a clear record of abdominal trauma occurring one month prior. All etiologies for pleural effusion apart from post-traumatic pancreatitis were excluded. The child’s clinical history lacked any episodes of fever, prolonged cough, or weight reduction. Although there was a documented history of exposure to Koch’s within the family and the pleural fluid ADA showed a slight increase, GeneXpert testing for MTB returned negative, effectively excluding pulmonary Koch’s. The absence of fever and a lack of neutrophilic exudate in the pleural fluid made empyema unlikely. Malignancies, such as lung cancer, leukaemia, lymphoma, or metastatic tumors, are known to cause pleural fluid with high amylase levels; nevertheless, malignancy was discounted due to the absence of fever and weight loss, along with normal findings in blood tests and peripheral smear. Cytological analysis of the pleural fluid did not detect malignant cells. Boerhaave’s syndrome (esophageal perforation), another potential cause of amylase-enriched pleural effusion, was also excluded based on normal bowel movements and absence of chest pain or vomiting. Acquired diaphragmatic hernia was considered unlikely, given that imaging did not reveal any abdominal organs herniated into the thoracic cavity. Hepatic hydrothorax was excluded as liver function tests and imaging results were unremarkable, and there was no prior history suggestive of chronic liver disease. Lastly, normal results from renal imaging and function tests eliminated renal pathology or hydronephrosis as potential causes.

Treatment:

In the PICU, the patient received respiratory support through humidified oxygen administered by mask. Ultrasound-guided thoracocentesis was performed on two occasions, each time yielding approximately one litre of pleural fluid with blood present. Empirical antitubercular therapy was initiated, but discontinued after a definitive diagnosis was established via MRCP. Upon the patient's third hospital admission, an intercostal drain (ICD) was inserted on the right side under strict aseptic conditions, and roughly another litre of blood-tinged pleural fluid was collected in the ICD bag. Because PPF is a rare condition in paediatric patients and lacks established management protocols, the approach was informed by existing case reports. A conservative strategy was employed initially: the patient was kept nil per oral, and an infusion of Octreotide was commenced at a dosage of 30 µg/kg/day (range: 10–40 µg/kg/day). Total parenteral nutrition and pancreatic enzyme supplementation were also initiated. Over a 10-day period, the volume of fluid draining from the ICD diminished progressively and ultimately ceased. The ICD was then removed, and the patient was subsequently discharged from the hospital.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following hospital discharge, the patient has remained asymptomatic. He continues to see me for scheduled monthly follow-up visits. Since his discharge, there have not been any further incidents of recurrent pleural effusions (S17). He has adopted lifestyle modifications, including adherence to a balanced diet and routine physical activity, as per medical recommendations. The child has resumed his normal daily routines and attends school consistently. He is able to engage in vigorous physical activities, such as running and playing, without experiencing any issues.

Discussion:

PPF represents a particularly uncommon and rare complication related to pancreatic disease in the paediatric demographic. To date, only 47 paediatric instances of PPF have been reported in the literature. The condition may arise following either acute or chronic pancreatitis or after abdominal trauma in children. Its precise incidence in paediatric patients remains undetermined; nonetheless, it is recognized to complicate about 0.4% of overall pancreatitis cases. Notably, mutations in the serine peptidase inhibitor Kazal type 1 (SPINK1) and PRSS1 genes are present in as many as 30% of paediatric chronic pancreatitis cases. Non-hereditary triggers such as blunt abdominal trauma are also substantial, accounting for 32% of cases in the 25-patient series reported by Yang et al. Cases without identifiable cause continue to be documented, likely reflecting underdiagnosed genetic or ductal abnormalities. 

Pancreatic fluid associated with PPF can originate from a compromised pancreatic duct or pseudocyst, tracking superiorly through normal diaphragmatic hiatuses—most commonly the aortic or oesophageal hiatus—or, less frequently, by direct diaphragmatic erosion into the pleural space, thereby forming a fistulous tract. Frequently, these cases escape detection because symptoms often involve the thorax, leading to a workup focused on pulmonary etiologies, although the underlying problem is abdominal. A literature review uncovered 21 pertinent publications, among which 68% of the paediatric cases had predominantly respiratory complaints such as extensive, recurrent unilateral or bilateral pleural effusions, shortness of breath, and coughing. Fewer than 20% exhibited abdominal manifestations such as epigastric pain, vomiting, or unintentional weight loss. Constitutional signs like fever were noted in 24% of cases. In a 2023 case report by Zi Yin Shang in Frontiers in Paediatrics, an 8-year-old boy presented solely with a visible, bulging left chest wall for two weeks—an exceedingly rare primary symptom. Here, the effusion likely accrued over time post-abdominal trauma, and preserved respiratory function in the contralateral lung allowed for adaptation, explaining the absence of typical symptoms like dyspnoea or cough. Further complicating the history, the patient had concealed prior trauma due to social factors, and underwent extensive tuberculosis and malignancy investigations before the true diagnosis was reached. Similarly, in our case, recurrent thoracic manifestations such as chest pain and repeated right-sided pleural fluid accumulations were present, and the history of abdominal injury only surfaced after meticulous history-taking, with MRCP findings ultimately directing attention to the pancreatic origin. Hence, PPF can easily be misdiagnosed as a primary pulmonary disease—a phenomenon that underscores the need for comprehensive history and appropriate imaging.

Pathogenesis in PPF typically involves external trauma or bacterial infection precipitating a rupture of either the pancreatic duct or pseudocyst, with ensuing leakage of pancreatic secretions. If the rupture occurs at the anterior pancreatic surface, fluid disseminates into the peritoneal cavity, producing ascites. Posterior ruptures, conversely, channel fluid into the retroperitoneum, from where it may ascend, traverse the diaphragmatic hiatus into the mediastinum, and eventually enter the pleural space. In this setting, the fistulous tract functions much like a leaking conduit, directing pancreatic effluent into the chest cavity. In adults, alcohol use is the predominant risk factor for chronic pancreatitis and resultant PPF. Uchiyama et al’s large review of Japanese cases (n=113) demonstrated that chronic alcoholic pancreatitis accounted for 99% of adult cases, with thoracic symptoms evident in 68% of patients. Conversely, paediatric chronic pancreatitis is mostly associated with genetic mutations (e.g., PRSS1, CFTR, SPINK1, chymotrypsin C), with representative pathogenic variants such as R122H and N291 in PRSS1. Whenever paediatric chronic pancreatitis is suspected, a detailed familial history and consideration of hereditary aetiology are imperative.

Diagnosis is commonly postponed, as patients typically undergo exhaustive pulmonary evaluation before pancreatic involvement is recognized. An elevated pleural fluid amylase level (≥1000 IU/L) is highly specific for PPF. However, differential diagnoses such as tuberculosis, oesophageal rupture, and malignant neoplasms (pulmonary or pancreatic) should be ruled out, as these can also present with raised pleural amylase levels, albeit usually of salivary origin. Amylase typing in the pleural fluid can aid in establishing the diagnosis of PPF.
