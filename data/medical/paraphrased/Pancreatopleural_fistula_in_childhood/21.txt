Title: Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood

Hans Vaish, Vipin Vaish, Shalini Suri, Vasudha Tomar

Abstract:

Summary: An older boy developed repeated right-sided pleural effusions accompanied by chest pain and difficulty breathing, which temporarily resolved following aspiration of the fluid. The pleural aspirates were persistently dark red in appearance. Due to frequent accumulation, a right intercostal drain was inserted. The clinical history included recent abdominal trauma caused by a bicycle handlebar. Chest CT revealed right-sided areas of patchy consolidation, a hydropneumothorax, and a multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right paraesophageal area to the pancreatic tail. Amylase and lipase levels were increased in both serum and pleural fluid samples. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography identified pancreatopleural fistulas on both sides, more pronounced on the right. Management included nil by mouth, total parenteral nutrition, and Octreotide administration. Within 10 days, drainage lessened, the chest tube was taken out, and the patient was discharged.

Background:

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is a very uncommon condition, characterized by repeated episodes of large, hemorrhagic pleural effusions. It occurs in patients with acute or chronic pancreatitis as well as following pancreatic trauma. The diagnosis can be challenging, since the clinical manifestations are primarily due to recurrent pleural fluid accumulation and respiratory compromise, while the underlying disease involves the pancreas. This focus on pulmonary symptoms can lead to delays in identifying the true source of pathology. In the pediatric population, only 47 cases of PPF have been documented, compared to over 300 cases reported in adults.

Case presentation:

A boy presented to my outpatient clinic, accompanied by his mother, with complaints of shortness of breath and pain on the right side of his chest persisting for one week. The mother reported a gradual worsening of these symptoms over the seven days. There was no history of parental consanguinity. She mentioned that the child had been in contact with his grandfather, who previously had pulmonary Koch’s disease and received appropriate treatment. The child’s diet was characterized by frequent intake of processed, high-calorie foods and limited consumption of fresh produce or nutritionally balanced meals prepared at home. He weighed 44 kg, measured 112 cm in height, and had a BMI of 35. On clinical assessment, the child’s respiratory rate was 20/min, indicating mild tachypnea, and he exhibited decreased movement of the right side of his chest. Percussion revealed stony dullness over the right subscapular, infra-axillary, and axillary areas. Vocal fremitus was markedly reduced on the right compared to the left. Breath sounds were diminished in these same regions. There were no signs of pallor, cyanosis, icterus, clubbing, or lymphadenopathy. The abdomen was soft, with no evidence of hepatosplenomegaly. The child was alert and properly oriented. The cardiovascular exam showed tachycardia but no murmurs. Oxygen saturation was 88%–89% on room air, with a heart rate of 110/min. Due to tachypnea and respiratory compromise, the child was transferred to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit and started on oxygen via mask at 5 l/min. A chest radiograph was obtained, and empirical antibiotic therapy with piperacillin-tazobactam commenced after blood cultures were drawn. Chest X-ray revealed a right-sided pleural effusion with blunting of the right costophrenic angle (figure 1) (Table 2). Bedside lung ultrasound showed pleural effusion estimated at approximately one litre (figure 2) (Table 2). Right-sided thoracocentesis was performed under sterile conditions, yielding about one litre of dark red pleural fluid. The tachypnea resolved after fluid removal, with oxygen saturation on room air rising to 95%–98%. Chest pain improved and the child discontinued oxygen support within 24 hours. He was moved to the general ward the following day and subsequently discharged. Pleural fluid was sent for laboratory analysis. Six days later, the child was readmitted with recurrent right-sided chest pain, tachypnea, and shortness of breath. He was again transferred to the PICU, where a repeat chest X-ray demonstrated a right-sided pleural effusion. Another thoracocentesis was performed, draining roughly one litre of dark red fluid. Pleural fluid Adenosine Deaminase (ADA) was mildly elevated (Table 1), and due to the positive history of Koch’s exposure, antitubercular therapy was started empirically. After five more days, the child was admitted a third time for similar complaints of right-sided chest pain and dyspnea. Upon revisiting the history with the child, he disclosed experiencing blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle one month prior. A contrast-enhanced chest CT was performed before insertion of an intercostal drain (ICD). CT findings showed patchy airspace consolidation in the right lung, right-sided hydropneumothorax, and an elongated, lobulated, multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right pleural space and tracking from the right paraoesophageal area to the upper left retroperitoneum reaching the pancreatic tail (Table 2). An ICD was placed, draining about one litre of dark red pleural fluid (figure 3). The combination of CT results, dark red pleural aspirate, and prior abdominal trauma suggested a diagnosis of pancreaticopleural fistula (PPF). Same-day MRCP was performed, revealing pancreatitis with a tubular cystic collection at the distal body and tail of the pancreas extending posteriorly in the retroperitoneum, then moving superiorly through the oesophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity. There was also a narrow connection to the left pleural space. The collection was communicating with the distal pancreatic duct and there was narrowing of the duct proximal to the connection. MRCP findings indicated PPF (figures 4–6) (Table 1). Serum and pleural fluid were tested for amylase and lipase. Pleural fluid analysis demonstrated markedly increased amylase (6118 U/L) and lipase (7211 U/L) (Table 2), supporting the diagnosis of PPF. Differential diagnosis:

The patient exhibited pleural effusion and chest pain, with a clear history of abdominal trauma occurring one month prior. Alternative causes for pleural effusion were excluded except for post-traumatic pancreatitis. The child lacked any history of fever, prolonged cough, or weight reduction. Although there was known exposure to Koch’s disease within the family and pleural fluid ADA was moderately raised, GeneXpert testing for MTB yielded negative results, thereby excluding pulmonary Koch’s. There were no indications of fever or neutrophilic exudate within the pleural fluid, which eliminated the possibility of empyema. Malignancy—such as lung cancer, leukemia, lymphoma, or metastatic tumors—can also present with elevated amylase in pleural fluid; nonetheless, the absence of fever and weight loss, alongside normal blood counts and a normal peripheral smear, argued against malignancy. Furthermore, pleural fluid cytology did not detect any malignant cells. Boerhaave’s syndrome (esophageal perforation), another cause of amylase-rich pleural fluid, was excluded since the child maintained normal bowel movements and had no history of chest pain or vomiting. Acquired diaphragmatic hernia was discounted because imaging did not reveal abdominal organs within the thoracic cavity. Hepatic hydrothorax was also excluded as liver function tests and liver imaging were normal, with no previous history of chronic liver disease. Renal involvement or hydronephrosis was ruled out owing to unremarkable renal imaging and function tests.

Treatment:

The patient received humidified oxygen via mask for respiratory support in the PICU. Thoracocentesis was performed twice under ultrasound guidance, with approximately one litre of blood-tinged pleural fluid aspirated on each occasion. Antitubercular therapy was initiated empirically but discontinued upon confirmation of diagnosis through MRCP. During the third hospital admission, an intercostal drain (ICD) was inserted on the right side under sterile conditions, resulting in another litre of blood-stained pleural fluid drained into the ICD collection bag. Pediatric pancreaticopleural fistula (PPF) is a rare condition, and there are no established treatment protocols; management is primarily informed by published case reports. Initially, a conservative approach was chosen: the child was kept nil per os and started on an octreotide infusion at a dose of 30 µg/kg/day (range: 10–40 µg/kg/day). Total parenteral nutrition and pancreatic enzyme supplementation were commenced. Over a period of ten days, the volume of fluid output from the ICD gradually decreased and eventually ceased. The ICD was then removed, and the patient was subsequently discharged.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following hospital discharge, the patient has remained asymptomatic. He continues to attend scheduled monthly follow-up appointments with me. No further instances of recurrent pleural effusion have occurred post-discharge (S17). He has implemented lifestyle changes and currently maintains a balanced diet and engages in regular physical activity per recommendations. The patient has resumed his normal routine, is consistently going to school, and is able to perform vigorous activities such as running and playing without limitations.

Discussion:

PPF represents an exceptionally rare and unusual complication stemming from pancreatic disorders in paediatric patients. To date, only 47 paediatric cases of PPF have been described in the literature. It has been documented in association with both acute and chronic pancreatitis as well as following trauma in children. The precise rate of occurrence in the paediatric population remains undefined; however, PPF is known to complicate approximately 0.4% of pancreatitis cases overall. Mutations in the serine peptidase inhibitor Kazal type 1 (SPINK1) and PRSS1 genes are frequently implicated, accounting for up to 30% of paediatric chronic pancreatitis. Additionally, blunt abdominal trauma serves as a significant non-genetic trigger, making up 32% of cases in Yang et al’s 25-patient cohort. Instances labeled as idiopathic may reflect undetected genetic or ductal variations. Pancreatic secretions can migrate from a ruptured duct or pseudocyst through naturally existing diaphragmatic passages—typically the aortic or oesophageal hiatus, or less frequently via direct erosion of the diaphragm—leading to the formation of a fistula connecting the pancreas with the pleural cavity. Many cases are not identified promptly because clinical presentation is predominantly respiratory, prompting an evaluation for pulmonary disorders rather than abdominal pathology. A review of published reports revealed 21 relevant articles, with 68% of cases initially manifesting as respiratory symptoms such as large or recurrent unilateral or bilateral pleural effusions, dyspnoea, and cough. Fewer than 20% exhibited abdominal complaints like epigastric pain, vomiting, or weight loss. Constitutional symptoms including fever occurred in 24% of patients. Zi Yin Shang (Frontiers in Paediatrics 2023) described an instance of PPF in an 8-year-old male who presented solely with a protruding left chest for two weeks; cases exhibiting chest bulging as the primary sign are exceedingly uncommon. In that particular case, pleural effusion likely accumulated progressively post-abdominal trauma, with compensatory mechanisms of the unaffected lung enabling adaptation and masking typical respiratory signs such as cough or shortness of breath. Moreover, the patient concealed a history of trauma due to bullying. Diagnostic workup focused initially on tuberculosis and cancer given the bloody pleural fluid, but eventually diverged towards pancreatic etiology. Similarly, in our experience, the patient reported recurrent chest pain and repeated right-sided pleural fluid accumulation. Only after serial interviews and MRCP findings was abdominal trauma discovered, and a pancreatic source identified. Thus, this disorder frequently presents with a “pulmonary mask,” potentially obscuring the actual diagnosis if careful history-taking and appropriate imaging are not performed.

The mechanism of fluid accumulation in PPF involves disruption of the pancreatic duct or a pseudocyst, either by external trauma or infection, resulting in leakage of pancreatic secretions. Anterior duct rupture leads to peritoneal fluid accumulation (ascites), whereas posterior disruption allows pancreatic fluid to track into the retroperitoneum, ascend through the diaphragmatic hiatus (aortic or oesophageal), and eventually breach the pleural cavity to produce a pleural effusion. The internal fistula essentially acts as a conduit, diverting pancreatic fluid into the chest. In adults, alcoholism is the most common underlying cause of chronic pancreatitis associated with PPF. The comprehensive Japanese review by Uchiyama et al documented 113 cases, identifying chronic alcoholic pancreatitis as the underlying pathology in 99% and reporting chest-related symptoms in 68% of these patients. Conversely, paediatric chronic pancreatitis is most often attributed to genetic factors, including mutations in the PRSS1, CF transmembrane regulator, SPINK1, and chymotrypsin C genes. Specifically, R122H and N291 mutations in exon 3 and exon 2, respectively, of PRSS1 are characteristic. For children with recurrent or chronic pancreatitis, a thorough family history and consideration for hereditary pancreatitis are indicated.

Delays in diagnosis are frequent since the majority undergo extensive pulmonary investigations before pancreatic disease is suspected. Pleural fluid amylase concentrations equal to or exceeding 1000 IU/L are diagnostic for PPF. However, elevated amylase levels in pleural fluid may also be found in tuberculosis, esophageal perforation, and malignancies affecting the lung or pancreas, all of which must be excluded. The determination of pancreatic-type amylase distinguishes PPF from these conditions, as they generally display salivary-type amylase. Amylase isoenzyme typing in pleural fluid should therefore be incorporated into the workup for suspected PPF. MRCP provides 70%–80% sensitivity in visualizing both the fistula and ductal anatomy.
