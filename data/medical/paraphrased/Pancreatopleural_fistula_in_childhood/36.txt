Title: Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood

Hans Vaish, Vipin Vaish, Shalini Suri, Vasudha Tomar

Abstract:

Summary: An older male child experienced repeated right-sided pleural effusions, accompanied by chest pain and difficulty breathing; these symptoms briefly subsided following aspiration. The pleural fluid consistently appeared dark red in color. Because the effusions recurred, a right intercostal drain was inserted. Review of medical history uncovered a recent incident of abdominal trauma caused by a bicycle handle. Chest computed tomography revealed patchy consolidation on the right side, hydropneumothorax, and a multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right paraesophageal area to the pancreatic tail. Elevated amylase and lipase levels were detected in both serum and pleural fluid samples. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography identified bilateral pancreatopleural fistulas, more prominent on the right. Management included nil per os, total parenteral nutrition, and Octreotide. Over ten days, pleural drainage reduced, the chest tube was withdrawn, and the patient was discharged home.

Background:

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is a highly uncommon condition characterized by repeated, large, and often blood-tinged pleural effusions. This complication is seen in patients with acute or chronic pancreatitis, as well as those who have suffered pancreatic trauma. The clinical presentation frequently mimics respiratory disorders due to ongoing pleural effusions, while the underlying problem originates from the pancreas, which can lead to a delay in diagnosis as clinicians focus on thoracic causes. To date, only 47 instances of PPF have been documented in pediatric patients, in contrast to over 300 cases reported in adults.

Case presentation:

A child presented to the outpatient clinic with his mother, who reported that he had been experiencing difficulty breathing and right-sided chest pain for one week. According to the mother, the respiratory symptoms had worsened over the past seven days. There was no reported history of consanguinity. The mother stated that the child had a history of contact with his grandfather, who had pulmonary Koch's disease and had completed appropriate treatment. The child's diet consisted mainly of processed, high-calorie foods, with minimal consumption of fresh fruits, vegetables, or balanced home-cooked meals. On evaluation, his weight was 44 kg, his height measured 112 cm, and his body mass index was 35.

Physical examination revealed a respiratory rate of 20 breaths per minute, with evidence of mild tachypnea. Chest movement on the right side was diminished. Percussion elicited a stony dull note over the right subscapular, infra-axillary, and axillary regions. Vocal fremitus was markedly decreased on the right compared to the left. Breath sounds were similarly reduced in the right subscapular, infra-axillary, and axillary areas. There was no evidence of pallor, cyanosis, icterus, clubbing, or lymphadenopathy. The abdomen was soft and without hepatosplenomegaly. The child was alert and oriented to time, place, and person. Cardiac examination indicated tachycardia but no murmurs. Oxygen saturation was between 88% and 89% on room air, with a heart rate of 110 bpm.

Given the tachypnea and respiratory distress, the child was transferred to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit and commenced on oxygen therapy via mask at 5 liters per minute. A chest X-ray was obtained, and empirical piperacillin-tazobactam was initiated after obtaining blood cultures. Imaging revealed a right-sided pleural effusion with blunting of the right costophrenic angle (figure 1, Table 2). Bedside lung ultrasonography identified a pleural effusion measuring approximately one liter (figure 2, Table 2). Right-sided thoracentesis was performed under strict aseptic technique, yielding around one liter of dark red pleural fluid. Following drainage, tachypnea resolved and oxygen saturation on room air improved to 95%-98%. The child's chest pain subsided, and he was able to discontinue supplemental oxygen within 24 hours. He was moved to the general ward the following day and subsequently discharged. The aspirated pleural fluid was sent for analysis.

Six days later, the child was readmitted with recurrent right-sided chest pain, tachypnea, and breathing difficulty. He was admitted to the PICU again, where a repeat chest X-ray demonstrated another right-sided pleural effusion. Repeat thoracentesis resulted in aspiration of about one liter of dark red fluid. Pleural fluid Adenosine Deaminase (ADA) was mildly elevated (Table 1) and, in the context of positive exposure to Koch's, empirical antitubercular therapy was started. The patient was again hospitalized five days later with similar symptoms of right-sided chest pain and respiratory distress.

A more detailed history was obtained, during which the child disclosed that he had suffered blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle one month earlier. Before considering intercostal drainage (ICD), a contrast-enhanced chest CT was performed. The scan revealed patchy airspace consolidation in the right lung with right-sided hydropneumothorax, and a lobulated, multiloculated hypodense fluid collection tracking from the right pleural cavity, along the right paraoesophageal area, and extending retroperitoneally up to the tail of the pancreas (Table 2). An ICD was inserted, and roughly one liter of dark red pleural fluid was drained through it (figure 3). The CT findings, combined with the history of dark red pleural fluid and preceding abdominal trauma, suggested a diagnosis of pancreaticopleural fistula (PPF).

MRCP was conducted the same day, demonstrating pancreatitis with a tubular, cystic collection in the distal body and tail of the pancreas extending posteriorly in the retroperitoneum, tracking upward via the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity. There was also a narrow communication with the left pleural cavity, and the collection communicated with the distal pancreatic duct, which appeared narrowed proximal to the communication. MRCP supported the diagnosis of PPF (figures 4–6; Table 1). Serum and pleural fluid levels of amylase and lipase were measured. Pleural fluid analysis showed markedly raised amylase (6118 U/L) and lipase (7211 U/L) (Table 2), further substantiating the PPF diagnosis.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient arrived with pleural effusion and chest pain, following a confirmed abdominal trauma sustained one month prior. All etiologies for pleural effusion, aside from post-traumatic pancreatitis, were excluded. The child had no complaints of fever, persistent cough, or weight loss. Although there was a documented history of Koch’s contact within the family and pleural fluid ADA was slightly raised, GeneXpert testing for MTB returned negative, thereby excluding pulmonary Koch’s. Absence of fever and lack of neutrophilic exudate in the pleural fluid eliminated the possibility of empyema. While malignancies such as lung cancer, leukemia, lymphoma, and metastatic tumors may produce amylase-rich pleural fluid, malignancy was ruled out based on no fever, no weight loss, unremarkable blood findings, and a normal peripheral smear. Cytology of pleural fluid revealed no malignant cells. Boerhaave’s syndrome (esophageal perforation), another cause of amylase-rich pleural fluid, was discounted as the patient exhibited normal bowel movements and no history of vomiting or chest pain. Acquired diaphragmatic hernia was excluded, given that imaging did not show abdominal organs within the thoracic cavity. Hepatic hydrothorax was not considered as liver function tests and imaging were unremarkable and there was no previous history of chronic liver disease. Finally, normal renal imaging and functions ruled out renal pathology or hydronephrosis.

Treatment:

In the pediatric intensive care unit, the patient received humidified oxygen delivered by mask. Thoracocentesis was performed twice under ultrasound guidance, each time aspirating approximately one liter of hemorrhagic pleural fluid. Empirical antitubercular therapy was initiated but discontinued upon diagnostic confirmation by MRCP. During a third hospitalization, an intercostal drainage (ICD) tube was placed on the right side under aseptic conditions, and again about one liter of blood-tinged pleural fluid was collected in the drainage bag. As pancreatico-pleural fistula (PPF) is an uncommon condition in children with no established management protocols, treatment decisions relied on existing case reports. The initial approach was conservative; the patient was kept nil per os and commenced on an Octreotide infusion at a dose of 30 µg/kg/day (dose range: 10–40 µg/kg/day). Total parenteral nutrition and pancreatic enzyme supplementation were also initiated. Over the subsequent 10 days, the fluid output via ICD steadily declined and eventually ceased. The ICD was subsequently removed, and the patient was discharged.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following hospital discharge, the child has remained asymptomatic. He continues to attend scheduled monthly follow-up appointments with me. Since his discharge, he has not experienced any further episodes of recurrent pleural effusions (S17). He has adopted lifestyle changes, including adhering to a balanced diet and engaging in regular physical activity as recommended. The patient has resumed normal daily routines and is consistently attending school. He is also able to participate in vigorous activities such as running and playing without limitation.

Discussion:

PPF represents a highly unusual and rarely encountered complication of pancreatic pathology among pediatric patients. To date, only 47 pediatric cases of PPF have been reported in the literature. The condition may be associated with acute or chronic pancreatitis or occur as a result of pediatric trauma. While the precise prevalence in children is unknown, PPF complicates approximately 0.4% of pancreatitis cases overall. Mutations in serine peptidase inhibitor Kazal type 1 (SPINK1) and PRSS1 genes are notable contributors, linked to up to 30% of pediatric chronic pancreatitis cases. Blunt trauma to the abdomen also serves as a significant non-genetic cause, comprising 32% of cases within the cohort analyzed by Yang et al. Idiopathic cases are continually reported, likely owing to yet undetected genetic or ductal abnormalities. Pancreatic fluid may migrate from a damaged duct or pseudocyst through naturally occurring diaphragmatic openings—most commonly the aortic or esophageal hiatus—or, less frequently, by direct erosion through the diaphragm, creating a fistulous tract connecting to the pleural cavity. A majority of these cases escape detection since presentations often involve thoracic symptoms, and diagnostic efforts are typically focused on identifying pulmonary disease, although the underlying cause resides in the abdomen. Our review found 21 pertinent publications, where respiratory manifestations—such as massive or recurrent unilateral/bilateral pleural effusions, breathlessness, and cough—were present in 68% of patients. Less than one fifth displayed abdominal complaints (epigastric pain, vomiting, weight loss). Constitutional symptoms (e.g., fever) occurred in 24% of cases. The report by Zi Yin Shang in Frontiers in Pediatrics (2023) described PPF in an 8-year-old male presenting solely with a bulging left chest for two weeks. Such cases, where chest wall protrusion is the initial sign, are exceedingly unusual. In that patient, pleural effusion likely developed gradually after abdominal trauma, and the unaffected lung’s compensatory function allowed for adaptation, preventing typical respiratory symptoms such as cough or breathlessness. Additionally, the child intentionally withheld information regarding prior abdominal trauma due to bullying at school. This case was initially investigated with tuberculosis and malignancy tests for hemorrhagic pleural effusion, but ultimately was diagnosed differently. In our own patient, the clinical picture included recurrent pulmonary symptoms with right-sided chest pain and repeated pleural effusions. Discovery of the abdominal trauma emerged after thorough history-taking, and MRCP pointed toward a pancreatic cause. Consequently, PPF may masquerade as a pulmonary disorder, potentially leading physicians astray unless comprehensive history and appropriate imaging are utilized. In PPF, fluid accumulation stems from external trauma or infection, resulting in rupture of the pancreatic duct or a pseudocyst and leakage of pancreatic fluid. An anterior rupture results in fluid entering the peritoneal cavity and forming ascites, while a posterior rupture allows leakage into the retroperitoneum; from there, fluid ascends through the path of least resistance via the aortic or esophageal hiatus into the mediastinum and subsequently through the pleura, causing pleural effusion. The fistulous tract diverts pancreatic secretions into the thoracic cavity, behaving like a leaky pipe. Among adults, chronic pancreatitis leading to PPF is most commonly related to alcohol consumption. Uchiyama et al compiled an extensive single-country review of 113 Japanese cases, noting that 99% were attributable to chronic alcoholic pancreatitis and 68% displayed chest symptoms. Conversely, pediatric cases are more often rooted in genetic factors. Mutations in the PRSS1, cystic fibrosis transmembrane regulator, SPINK1, and chymotrypsin C genes are established causes of hereditary pancreatitis, with PRSS1 exon 3 (R122H) and exon 2 (N291) mutations being particularly representative. When pediatric pancreatitis recurs or appears chronic, meticulous family history should be obtained to assess for hereditary etiology. Diagnosis is often delayed because most children are subjected to extensive pulmonary evaluation before the pancreatic origin is suspected. Pathognomonic for PPF is pleural fluid amylase ≥1000 IU/L. It is crucial to rule out tuberculosis, esophageal perforation, and malignancies affecting the lung or pancreas, as these also elevate pleural fluid amylase levels. However, the amylase in pleural fluid from PPF is pancreatic type, while other conditions (discussed above) feature salivary type amylase. Amylase typing in pleural fluid can aid in the diagnostic process for PPF. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) enables visualization of the fistula and ductal architecture with a sensitivity of 70%–80%.
