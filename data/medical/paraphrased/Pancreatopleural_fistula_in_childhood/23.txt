Title: Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood

Hans Vaish, Vipin Vaish, Shalini Suri, Vasudha Tomar

Abstract:

Summary: An older child presented with repeated right-sided pleural effusions accompanied by chest pain and breathing difficulties, which improved briefly following thoracentesis. The pleural fluid consistently appeared dark red. Given persistent effusions, a right intercostal drain was inserted. The clinical history included recent abdominal trauma caused by impact with bicycle handlebars. Chest computed tomography revealed right-sided patchy consolidation, hydropneumothorax, and a multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right paraesophageal space toward the pancreatic tail. Elevated levels of amylase and lipase were detected in both serum and pleural fluid samples. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography demonstrated bilateral pancreatopleural fistulas, with greater involvement on the right. Management included keeping the patient nil per os, initiating total parenteral nutrition, and administering Octreotide. Over ten days, drainage from the chest tube diminished, enabling removal of the tube, and the child was subsequently discharged.

Background:

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is a highly uncommon condition characterized by recurrent, large, and often blood-tinged pleural effusions. It has been identified in individuals with acute or chronic pancreatitis as well as in those who have sustained pancreatic injury. Diagnosis can be challenging because the typical presentation involves respiratory symptoms secondary to persistent pleural effusions, leading clinicians to initially search for causes within the thoracic cavity rather than the pancreas, which can result in a delay in identifying the true source. In the pediatric population, only 47 cases of PPF have been documented, while there have been over 300 reported cases among adults.

Case presentation:

A young patient was brought to the outpatient clinic by his mother, presenting with a one-week history of breathing difficulty and pain on the right side of the chest. According to the mother, the severity of symptoms had steadily increased over the past seven days. There was no reported history of parental consanguinity. The mother reported that the child had been in close contact with his grandfather, who previously had pulmonary tuberculosis and had completed appropriate treatment. Dietary assessment revealed that the child's diet was high in processed and calorie-dense foods but deficient in fresh fruits, vegetables, and well-balanced, home-prepared meals. His measured weight was 44 kg, height 112 cm, and body mass index calculated at 35. On physical examination, the patient was mildly tachypneic with a respiratory rate of 20 breaths per minute. Chest expansion was diminished on the right side. Percussion elicited stony dullness in the right subscapular, infra-axillary, and axillary areas. Vocal fremitus was notably reduced on the right compared to the left. Breath sounds were similarly diminished in the right subscapular, infra-axillary, and axillary regions. The examination was negative for pallor, cyanosis, jaundice, digital clubbing, and lymphadenopathy. The abdomen was soft with no palpable liver or spleen enlargement. The child was alert and oriented to time, place, and person. Cardiovascular exam indicated tachycardia without murmurs. Oxygen saturation was recorded at 88–89% on room air, and heart rate was 110 beats per minute. Due to signs of respiratory distress and increased breathing effort, the patient was admitted to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit and began receiving oxygen via mask at a rate of 5 L/min. A chest radiograph was performed, and empirical piperacillin-tazobactam was initiated after collecting blood cultures. Imaging revealed a right-sided pleural effusion with blunting of the right costophrenic angle (figure 1) (Table 2). A point-of-care lung ultrasound confirmed a pleural fluid collection of approximately one liter (figure 2) (Table 2). A right-sided pleural aspiration was performed aseptically, yielding about one liter of dark red fluid. Following thoracocentesis, tachypnea improved and room air oxygen saturation rose to 95–98%. The patient's chest pain abated, and oxygen was discontinued within 24 hours. He was transferred to the general ward the next day and subsequently discharged. The aspirated pleural fluid was sent for analysis. Six days later, the patient was re-admitted with similar symptoms—right-sided chest pain, tachypnea, and dyspnea. He was readmitted to the PICU, where chest X-ray again showed a right-sided pleural effusion. Another thoracocentesis removed approximately one liter of dark red pleural fluid. Pleural fluid adenosine deaminase (ADA) was modestly elevated (Table 1), and given the positive history of tuberculosis exposure, empiric antitubercular therapy was initiated. After five days, the child required admission for a third episode with recurrent symptoms of right-sided chest pain and breathing difficulty. On further history, the child disclosed experiencing blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle one month prior. Before considering intercostal drainage (ICD), a contrast-enhanced chest CT scan was requested. The scan demonstrated patchy consolidation in the right lung, right-sided hydropneumothorax, and an elongated, multiloculated hypodense collection originating in the right pleural space, tracking from the right paraesophageal region to the upper left retroperitoneal space, extending to the pancreatic tail (Table 2). An ICD was placed, evacuating around one liter of dark red pleural fluid (figure 3). In light of the CT findings, the pleural fluid characteristics, and the trauma history, a diagnosis of pancreaticopleural fistula (PPF) was suspected. MRCP performed the same day demonstrated features of pancreatitis with a tubular cystic collection at the distal pancreatic body and tail, extending posteriorly in the retroperitoneum and ascending through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity; there was also a narrow communication with the left pleural space. The collection was connected to the distal pancreatic duct, which showed proximal ductal narrowing. The MRCP findings supported PPF as the diagnosis (figures 4-6) (Table 1). Amylase and lipase levels were obtained from serum and pleural fluid. Analysis showed markedly elevated pleural fluid amylase (6118 U/L) and lipase (7211 U/L) (Table 2), corroborating the diagnosis. Differential diagnosis:

The patient presented with pleural effusion accompanied by chest pain and had a documented episode of abdominal trauma one month prior. Excluding other etiologies of pleural effusion, post-traumatic pancreatitis remained as a consideration. There was no history of fever, prolonged cough, or weight reduction. Although the child had a family history of Koch’s contact and pleural fluid ADA levels were slightly raised, GeneXpert testing for MTB yielded negative results. Therefore, pulmonary Koch’s was excluded. The absence of fever and lack of neutrophilic exudate in the pleural fluid also excluded empyema. Malignant conditions (including lung cancer, leukaemia, lymphoma, or metastatic tumors) can produce pleural fluid with elevated amylase, but no fever, no weight loss, a normal hematological profile, and an unremarkable peripheral smear ruled out malignancy. Cytological examination of the pleural fluid did not detect any malignant cells. Boerhaave’s syndrome, which can present with amylase-rich pleural effusion due to esophageal perforation, was ruled out since the patient had normal bowel movements and no episodes of chest pain or vomiting. Acquired diaphragmatic hernia was discounted as imaging did not show abdominal organs within the thoracic cavity. Hepatic hydrothorax was excluded owing to normal liver function tests and imaging, along with no history suggestive of chronic liver disease. Renal imaging and function tests were normal, thereby excluding renal disease or hydronephrosis.

Treatment:

The patient received humidified oxygen via mask in the PICU for respiratory support. Ultrasound-guided thoracocentesis was performed twice, with approximately one liter of blood-tinged pleural fluid aspirated on each occasion. Empirical antitubercular therapy was initiated but discontinued once the diagnosis was confirmed by MRCP. During the third hospital admission, an intercostal drain (ICD) was placed under strict aseptic conditions on the right side, resulting in drainage of about one liter of blood-tinged pleural fluid into the ICD bag. Since PPF is a rare and atypical condition in pediatric patients, standardized treatment protocols are not available and management relies on case reports. Initial conservative therapy included withholding oral intake and starting an infusion of Octreotide at a dose of 30 µg/kg/day (range: 10–40 µg/kg/day). Total parenteral nutrition and pancreatic enzyme supplementation were also initiated. Over the following 10 days, the volume of fluid draining from the ICD progressively decreased until it ceased. The ICD was then removed, and the patient was subsequently discharged.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following his hospital discharge, the child has remained asymptomatic. He continues to attend scheduled monthly follow-up appointments with me. Since leaving the hospital, he has not experienced any further pleural effusion recurrences (S17). He has adopted lifestyle changes, including maintaining a balanced diet and engaging in routine physical activity as recommended. He has resumed his normal routines, including regular school attendance, and is able to participate in vigorous activities such as running and playing without limitation.

Discussion:

Panpleuroperitoneal fistula (PPF) is a highly uncommon complication associated with pancreatic disorders in children. To date, only 47 paediatric cases of PPF have been described in the literature. It is observed following both acute and chronic pancreatitis, as well as traumatic injury in paediatric patients. While its precise frequency among children remains undetermined, PPF complicates approximately 0.4% of all pancreatitis cases. Genetic mutations in the serine peptidase inhibitor Kazal type 1 (SPINK1) and PRSS1 are significant contributors, representing up to 30% of paediatric chronic pancreatitis cases. Blunt abdominal trauma is also a major non-genetic cause, accounting for 32% of the 25 subjects reviewed by Yang et al. Cases without identifiable cause persist, possibly due to underrecognized genetic or ductal abnormalities. Pancreatic fluid may leak from a ruptured duct or pseudocyst, traversing diaphragmatic hiatuses—most frequently the aortic or oesophageal hiatus, and rarely via direct erosion—establishing a fistulous tract to the pleural cavity. Many instances are overlooked because they manifest predominantly with thoracic symptoms, leading clinicians to focus on respiratory investigations when the true pathology arises from the abdomen. Through literature review, 21 pertinent articles were found; 68% of reported patients exhibited mainly respiratory features, such as large, repeated unilateral or bilateral pleural effusions, breathlessness, and coughing. Fewer than 20% experienced abdominal complaints including pain in the epigastrium, vomiting, and weight loss. General symptoms like fever appeared in 24% of cases. A case described by Zi Yin Shang in Frontiers in Paediatrics (2023) involved an 8-year-old male presenting solely with left-sided chest protrusion for two weeks; presentations with bulging chest as an initial symptom are exceedingly rare. In this instance, the effusion gradually formed following abdominal trauma; the child's unaffected lung compensated, masking typical respiratory signs like cough and shortness of breath. Furthermore, the child concealed a history of abdominal trauma due to bullying, delaying diagnosis. Initial investigations pursued tuberculosis and malignancy for the hemorrhagic pleural effusion, but eventual evaluation led to a different diagnosis. In our own experience, the patient experienced recurring chest pain and repeated right-sided pleural fluid accumulation. The antecedent abdominal trauma emerged only after repeated history taking, with MRCP findings confirming pancreatic disease. Therefore, PPF can mimic pulmonary disease, potentially diverting clinicians unless careful history and appropriate imaging are obtained.

The mechanism of fluid accumulation in PPF involves external trauma or infection triggering rupture of the pancreatic duct or pseudocyst, with consequent leakage. When the rupture occurs anteriorly, fluid enters the peritoneal cavity, causing ascites; posterior rupture allows fluid to migrate retroperitoneally along the path of least resistance, ascend via the aortic or oesophageal hiatus to the mediastinum, and ultimately perforate the pleura, creating pleural effusion. This internal fistula effectively diverts pancreatic secretions into the chest. In adults, chronic pancreatitis leading to PPF is predominantly attributed to alcohol abuse. The comprehensive review by Uchiyama et al, cataloguing 113 Japanese cases, demonstrated that 99% were related to chronic alcohol-induced pancreatitis, with 68% manifesting chest-related symptoms. In contrast, paediatric chronic pancreatitis usually arises from genetic factors. Established hereditary causes include mutations in PRSS1, CF transmembrane regulator, SPINK1, and chymotrypsin C genes. Specifically, mutations R122H and N291 in exons 3 and 2 of PRSS1 are the most characteristic. For recurring or persistent paediatric pancreatitis, family history and consideration of hereditary etiology are recommended.

Diagnosis of PPF is often delayed, as most children undergo exhaustive pulmonary assessment before pancreatic involvement is suspected. Pleural fluid amylase above 1000 IU/L is a diagnostic hallmark. Alternative diagnoses such as tuberculosis, oesophageal rupture, and neoplasms of lung or pancreas must also be excluded since they can elevate amylase levels in pleural fluid. In PPF, pleural fluid contains pancreatic-type amylase, in contrast to salivary-type amylase seen in other conditions, making amylase isoenzyme typing a helpful diagnostic tool. MRCP demonstrates 70–80% sensitivity for visualizing the fistula and ductal anatomy. CT scans identify calcifications, pseudocysts, and ductal dilation but can visualize the fistula in only 33–47% of cases. Endoscopic cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) remains the definitive modality for therapeutic planning, with fistula visualization rates in paediatric patients ranging from 38% to 78%.
