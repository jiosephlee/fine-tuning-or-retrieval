Title: Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood

Hans Vaish, Vipin Vaish, Shalini Suri, Vasudha Tomar

Abstract:

A preadolescent male presented with repeated right-sided pleural effusions, chest discomfort, and difficulty breathing, all of which improved temporarily following thoracentesis. The aspirated pleural fluid consistently appeared dark red in color. Owing to recurrent effusions, a right intercostal drain was inserted. Clinical history revealed that he had sustained abdominal injury from a bicycle handlebar recently. Computed tomography of the chest identified patchy consolidation on the right, a hydropneumothorax, and a multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right paraesophageal area to the pancreatic tail. Amylase and lipase concentrations were elevated in both the serum and the pleural fluid. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography demonstrated bilateral pancreatopleural fistulas, more pronounced on the right. Management included withholding oral intake, providing total parenteral nutrition, and administering Octreotide. Over a span of ten days, pleural drainage diminished, the chest tube was subsequently withdrawn, and the patient was released from hospital care.

Background:

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is a very uncommon condition characterized by recurrent, large-volume, hemorrhagic pleural effusions. It occurs in the context of acute or chronic pancreatitis, as well as following pancreatic injury. The diagnosis can be challenging because patients typically present with symptoms of respiratory compromise secondary to repeated pleural effusions, yet the underlying cause originates in the pancreas. This often results in delayed recognition, as the clinical focus may remain on identifying a thoracic cause. In the pediatric population, only 47 instances of PPF have been documented, compared to more than 300 cases reported in adults.

Case presentation:

A child presented to the outpatient clinic with his mother, who reported that he had experienced breathing difficulties and right-sided chest pain for one week, symptoms which had progressively worsened during this period. There was no history of parental consanguinity. The mother mentioned exposure to an individual with pulmonary tuberculosis, specifically the child’s grandfather, who had completed appropriate treatment. Dietary assessment revealed frequent intake of processed, high-calorie foods and low consumption of fresh fruits, vegetables, or well-balanced home meals. The child weighed 44 kg and had a height of 112 cm, yielding a BMI of 35. 

Physical examination showed a respiratory rate of 20 breaths per minute with mild tachypnoea. Diminished right-sided chest movement was noted, and percussion produced a stony dull note over the right subscapular, infra-axillary, and axillary areas. Vocal fremitus was markedly reduced on the right compared to the left. Decreased breath sounds were also observed in the same right-sided regions. There were no signs of pallor, cyanosis, jaundice, clubbing, or lymphadenopathy. The abdomen was soft and without hepatosplenomegaly. The child remained alert and fully oriented. Cardiovascular evaluation found tachycardia but no murmurs. Oxygen saturation on ambient air was 88–89%, and heart rate measured 110 beats per minute. 

Due to respiratory distress and tachypnoea, the child was admitted to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit (PICU) and received supplemental oxygen at 5 L/min via mask. A chest radiograph was ordered, and empirical antibiotic therapy with piperacillin-tazobactam was initiated after blood samples were taken for culture. Chest X-ray demonstrated a right pleural effusion with obliteration of the right costophrenic angle (figure 1; Table 2). Bedside lung ultrasound estimated the pleural effusion to be approximately one litre in volume (figure 2; Table 2). Thoracocentesis was performed aseptically on the right side, yielding around one litre of dark red pleural fluid. Following this procedure, the tachypnoea resolved, and the patient’s oxygen saturation improved to 95–98% on room air. Chest pain subsided, and oxygen therapy was discontinued within 24 hours. The child was transferred to the ward and subsequently discharged. The pleural aspirate was sent for laboratory analysis.

Six days later, the patient was readmitted with similar symptoms—right-sided chest pain, tachypnoea, and respiratory difficulty—and again required PICU admission. Repeat chest radiograph revealed another right-sided pleural effusion. A second thoracocentesis removed approximately one litre of dark red fluid. Pleural fluid adenosine deaminase (ADA) was modestly increased (Table 1), and given the history of contact with tuberculosis, empirical anti-tubercular therapy was started.

However, a third admission occurred five days later with recurrent right-sided chest pain and dyspnoea. A more detailed history revealed that the child had sustained blunt trauma to the abdomen from a bicycle handle a month earlier. Prior to placing an intercostal drain (ICD), a contrast-enhanced chest CT was performed, which identified patchy areas of consolidation in the right lung, right-sided hydropneumothorax, and an elongated, multiloculated, hypodense collection extending from the right pleural space at the right paraoesophageal level through the retroperitoneum to the tail of the pancreas (Table 2). An ICD was inserted and one litre of dark red pleural fluid was drained (figure 3). The imaging findings, in combination with the pleural aspirate appearance and abdominal trauma history, raised suspicion for a pancreaticopleural fistula (PPF).

Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) was conducted the same day, which demonstrated features of pancreatitis with a tubular cystic collection involving the distal body and tail of the pancreas, tracking posteriorly in the retroperitoneum and extending superiorly through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity. Communication with the left pleural cavity was also evident. The collection connected to the distal pancreatic duct, which appeared narrowed upstream from this point. MRCP findings were consistent with a diagnosis of PPF (figures 4–6; table 1). Measurements of amylase and lipase in serum and pleural fluid were requested. Pleural fluid analysis showed significantly elevated amylase (6118 U/L) and lipase (7211 U/L) (table 2), confirming PPF. Anti-tubercular therapy was discontinued, and the patient was placed nil per oral and supported with total parenteral nutrition. Differential diagnosis:

The patient developed pleural effusion accompanied by chest pain, and had a confirmed episode of abdominal trauma one month prior. Aside from post-traumatic pancreatitis, alternative etiologies for pleural effusion had been excluded. The child lacked any history suggestive of fever, prolonged cough, or loss of weight. Although there was documented exposure to Koch's in the family and the pleural fluid showed a mild increase in ADA levels, GeneXpert testing for MTB returned negative results. Consequently, pulmonary Koch’s was excluded. Additionally, neither fever nor a neutrophilic exudate was present in the pleural fluid, discounting empyema. Malignancies—including lung cancer, leukemia, lymphoma, and metastatic tumors—can exhibit pleural fluid enriched in amylase; however, absence of fever and weight loss, together with a normal complete blood count and peripheral smear, made malignancy unlikely. Cytology of the pleural fluid likewise did not demonstrate malignant cells. Boerhaave’s syndrome (esophageal perforation) may also cause amylase-rich pleural fluid, but this was considered improbable given normal bowel movements and no prior chest pain or vomiting. Acquired diaphragmatic hernia was excluded since imaging failed to show abdominal organs within the thoracic cavity. Hepatic hydrothorax was considered unlikely as liver imaging and function tests were unremarkable, and there was no history of chronic liver disease. Lastly, renal imaging and renal function tests were within normal limits, thereby excluding renal pathology or hydronephrosis.

Treatment:

The patient received humidified oxygen via mask for respiratory support in the pediatric intensive care unit (PICU). Thoracocentesis was performed twice using ultrasound guidance, and each time approximately one liter of blood-tinged pleural fluid was obtained. Empirical antitubercular therapy was initiated, but discontinued once the diagnosis was established using MRCP. During the third hospitalization, an intercostal drain (ICD) was placed on the right side with strict aseptic technique, resulting in drainage of another liter of blood-stained pleural fluid into the ICD collection bag. Since pancreatico-pleural fistula (PPF) is uncommon in children and no standardized treatment protocols exist, management is typically guided by case report evidence. Initially, conservative management was chosen: the child was kept nil per oral and commenced on Octreotide infusion at a dose of 30 µg/kg/day (range 10–40 µg/kg/day). The patient also received total parenteral nutrition and pancreatic enzyme supplementation. Over the ensuing 10 days, the volume of fluid drained from the ICD progressively lessened and ultimately ceased. The ICD was subsequently removed, and the patient was discharged from the hospital.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following his discharge, the child has remained asymptomatic. He continues to see me for scheduled monthly follow-up appointments. Since leaving the hospital, there have been no further occurrences of pleural effusions (S17). He has adopted recommended lifestyle changes, including a balanced diet and consistent physical activity. He has resumed his normal routine, attends school consistently, and is able to participate in strenuous physical activities such as running and playing without limitations.

Discussion:

PPF is an exceedingly rare complication associated with pancreatic disorders in children. To date, only 47 paediatric cases have been reported in the literature. PPF can occur secondary to both acute and chronic pancreatitis or following paediatric trauma, but its precise incidence in this demographic remains undetermined, though it complicates approximately 0.4% of pancreatitis cases overall. Genetic mutations including Serine peptidase inhibitor Kazal type 1 (SPINK1) and PRSS1 are particularly notable, comprising up to 30% of chronic pancreatitis in children. Blunt trauma to the abdomen is another significant non-genetic trigger, representing 32% within Yang et al.’s 25-patient series. Idiopathic presentations continue to arise, potentially reflecting unrecognised genetic or ductal abnormalities.

Pancreatic fluid may escape from a disrupted pancreatic duct or pseudocyst, traversing natural openings in the diaphragm such as the aortic or oesophageal hiatus, or occasionally through direct erosion, ultimately establishing a fistulous pathway to the pleural space. Many cases are overlooked because patients primarily display thoracic symptoms, leading clinicians to focus on pulmonary causes rather than abdominal origins. A literature review yielded 21 pertinent reports, showing 68% of paediatric PPF cases had predominant respiratory signs including large, recurrent unilateral or bilateral pleural effusions, dyspnoea, and cough. Abdominal manifestations—epigastric pain, vomiting, and weight loss—were reported in fewer than 20%, while constitutional symptoms like fever were seen in 24% of cases. Zi Yin Shang’s 2023 report in Frontiers in Paediatrics documented an 8-year-old presenting only with a protruding left chest over two weeks. Cases in which chest bulging is the initial symptom are especially uncommon. Here, pleural effusion was presumed to progress gradually after abdominal trauma, with the unaffected lung maintaining respiratory function, allowing the child to adapt and thus lack typical symptoms like cough or dyspnoea. Moreover, the child had concealed prior abdominal trauma due to bullying at school. Evaluation for tuberculosis and malignancy was conducted because of haemorrhagic pleural fluid, but ultimately, a different diagnosis was reached.

In the presented case, the patient experienced recurrent pulmonary complaints—chest pain and repeated right-sided pleural effusions. History of abdominal trauma only surfaced after persistent questioning, and MRCP guided diagnostic consideration toward a pancreatic etiology. Consequently, PPF can present as a ‘pulmonary mask,’ diverting the clinician’s attention away from the abdominal source unless a thorough history and appropriate imaging are obtained. Fluid accumulation in PPF stems from external trauma or infection, precipitating rupture of the pancreatic duct or pseudocyst and resulting in pancreatic fluid leak. Rupture at the anterior pancreas leads to ascites, whereas posterior rupture enables leakage into the retroperitoneum and eventual passage through the diaphragm into the mediastinum, then through the pleura, causing pleural effusion. The internal fistula diverts pancreatic secretion into the chest.

Chronic pancreatitis in adults most frequently arises from alcohol consumption as the underlying cause for PPF. Uchiyama et al. provided the largest single-nation review, compiling 113 cases from Japanese sources, finding 99% linked to chronic alcohol-induced pancreatitis; chest symptoms were seen in 68% of those patients. In contrast, paediatric chronic pancreatitis is predominantly hereditary, with mutations in PRSS1, CFTR, SPINK1, and chymotrypsin C genes playing leading roles. Notably, R122H and N291 mutations within exons 3 and 2 of PRSS1 are prominent. When pancreatitis in a child recurs or becomes chronic, clinicians should conduct an extensive family history and assess for hereditary forms.

Diagnosis of PPF is typically delayed because most patients undergo extensive pulmonary evaluation before its pancreatic origin is considered. A pleural fluid amylase concentration ≥1000 IU/L is diagnostic for PPF. However, elevated pleural amylase can also be found in cases of tuberculosis, oesophageal perforation, and malignancy affecting the lung or pancreas. PPF can be differentiated from such conditions by detecting pancreatic-type amylase in pleural fluid, whereas other conditions, which also elevate amylase, usually present with salivary-type amylase. Amylase typing of pleural fluid is a recommended part of PPF workup.

MRCP is able to visualise the fistula and ductal anatomy with 70–80% sensitivity, while CT reliably demonstrates pancreatic calcification, pseudocysts, or duct dilation but reveals the fistula in only 33–47% of cases. Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) remains the preferred modality for therapeutic decision-making, with paediatric fistula detection rates via ERCP ranging from 38% to 78%.
