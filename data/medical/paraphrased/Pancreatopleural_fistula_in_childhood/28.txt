Title: Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood

Hans Vaish, Vipin Vaish, Shalini Suri, Vasudha Tomar

Abstract:

Summary: An older boy presented with repeated episodes of right-sided pleural effusions, associated with chest pain and difficulty breathing, which showed temporary improvement following aspiration. The pleural aspirate consistently appeared dark red. Given the persistence of effusions, a right intercostal drain was inserted. His history included recent blunt abdominal injury from a bicycle handlebar. Chest CT revealed patchy consolidation on the right side, hydropneumothorax, and a multiloculated hypodense lesion extending from the right paraesophageal area toward the pancreatic tail. Elevated amylase and lipase levels were found in both his serum and pleural fluid samples. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography demonstrated bilateral pancreatopleural fistulas, more pronounced on the right. Management included nil per oral, total parenteral nutrition, and administration of Octreotide. Within 10 days, drainage gradually decreased, the chest tube was discontinued, and the patient was discharged.

Background:

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is a very uncommon condition characterized by repeated, large-volume, often hemorrhagic pleural effusions. This entity occurs in the context of acute or chronic pancreatitis as well as following pancreatic injury. Clinically, PPF often poses a diagnostic challenge because patients primarily manifest with respiratory compromise secondary to ongoing pleural effusions, yet the underlying disorder originates from the pancreas; as a result, initial diagnostic efforts are frequently directed toward identifying a thoracic cause, contributing to delays in establishing the correct diagnosis. In the pediatric population, only 47 cases of PPF have been documented, in contrast to more than 300 cases reported among adults.

Case presentation:

A child presented to the outpatient clinic with his mother, reporting difficulty breathing and right-sided chest pain persisting for one week. The mother described progressive worsening of the symptoms over seven days. There was no history of consanguinity. She noted previous contact with Koch’s disease, as the child’s grandfather had pulmonary Koch's and received appropriate treatment. Dietary history indicated frequent intake of processed, high-calorie foods, with scarce consumption of fresh fruits, vegetables, and balanced home-cooked meals. The child weighed 44 kg, measured 112 cm in height, and had a body mass index of 35.

On assessment, the child’s respiratory rate was 20/min with mild tachypnea. Reduced movement was noted in the right chest. Percussion was stony dull in the right subscapular, right infra-axillary, and axillary regions. Vocal fremitus was substantially diminished on the right compared to the left. Breath sounds were decreased over the right subscapular, infra-axillary, and axillary areas. Examination showed no evidence of pallor, cyanosis, icterus, clubbing, or lymphadenopathy. The abdomen was soft without hepatosplenomegaly. The child was alert and oriented to time, place, and person. The cardiovascular exam revealed tachycardia but no murmurs. Oxygen saturation was 88%–89% on ambient air with a heart rate of 110/min.

Given the tachypnea and respiratory distress, the child was transferred to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit, started on oxygen via mask at 5 l/min, and a chest X-ray was requested. Empirical antibiotics (piperacillin-tazobactam) were initiated after sending blood cultures. Chest X-ray demonstrated right-sided pleural effusion with blunting of the right costophrenic angle (figure 1) (Table 2). Bedside lung ultrasound estimated pleural effusion volume around one litre (figure 2) (Table 2). Right-sided pleural effusion was aspirated under aseptic technique, retrieving approximately one litre of dark red fluid. Post-thoracocentesis, tachypnea resolved and oxygen saturation on room air improved to 95%–98%. Chest pain also subsided, and oxygen support was discontinued within 24 hours. He was transferred to the ward the following day and subsequently discharged. Aspirated pleural fluid was sent for analysis.

Six days later, the child was re-admitted with recurrence of right-sided chest pain, tachypnea, and dyspnea. Once again, he was moved to PICU, and chest X-ray revealed another right-sided pleural effusion. Repeat thoracocentesis yielded approximately one litre of dark red pleural fluid. Pleural fluid adenosine deaminase (ADA) was mildly elevated (Table 1). Given the history of exposure to Koch’s disease, antitubercular medications were started empirically.

After five days, he was admitted for a third time with similar complaints of right-sided chest pain and breathing difficulty. Re-evaluation of history revealed that the child had sustained blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle a month prior. Contrast-enhanced chest CT was ordered prior to insertion of an intercostal drainage (ICD), revealing patchy air space consolidations in the right lung, right-sided hydropneumothorax, and an elongated, multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right pleural space at the paraesophageal level into the left-sided retroperitoneum reaching the pancreatic tail (Table 2). An ICD was placed, and one litre of dark red pleural fluid was drained (figure 3). CT findings, along with dark red pleural aspirate and history of trauma, raised suspicion for a pancreaticopleural fistula (PPF). MRCP performed the same day showed pancreatitis with tubular cystic collection in the distal body and tail of the pancreas, which extended posteriorly in the retroperitoneum and then tracked superiorly through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity. There was also a narrow communication with the left pleural cavity. The collection communicated with the distal pancreatic duct, with upstream duct narrowing. MRCP confirmed the diagnosis of PPF (figures 4–6, Table 1).

Serum and pleural fluid amylase and lipase tests were ordered, and pleural fluid analysis revealed highly elevated amylase (6118 U/L) and lipase (7211 U/L) levels (Table 2), corroborating the diagnosis of PPF. Antitubercular drugs were discontinued, and the patient was placed on nil per os status and total parenteral nutrition. An octreotide infusion was initiated at 30 µg/kg/day (range: 10–40 µg/kg/day), along with pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy

Differential diagnosis:

The patient was admitted with pleural effusion and thoracic pain, following an abdominal injury sustained one month prior. After consideration, causes of pleural effusion other than post-traumatic pancreatitis were excluded. The child denied any episodes of fever, persistent cough, or unintended weight loss. There was a known family contact with Koch’s, and pleural fluid ADA showed a slight elevation; however, the GeneXpert test for Mycobacterium tuberculosis was negative. As a result, pulmonary Koch’s was excluded. No fever was present and the pleural fluid lacked a neutrophilic exudate, thereby excluding empyema. Although amylase-enriched pleural fluid can be seen in malignancy (including lung cancer, leukemia, lymphoma, and metastatic tumors), the child’s lack of fever, stable weight, unremarkable blood profile, and normal peripheral smear excluded these possibilities. Pleural fluid cytology found no evidence of malignancy either. Amylase-rich pleural fluid may also be present in Boerhaave’s syndrome (esophageal perforation); however, this was excluded as the child had normal bowel movements and no complaints of chest pain or vomiting. Acquired diaphragmatic hernia was ruled out because chest imaging showed no intra-abdominal organs in the thoracic cavity. Hepatic hydrothorax was eliminated as liver function tests and imaging results were normal, and there was no history suggestive of chronic liver disease. Normal findings from renal imaging and laboratory tests excluded renal pathology or hydronephrosis.

Treatment:

Humidified oxygen administered via mask was provided for respiratory support in the PICU. The patient underwent two sessions of ultrasound-guided thoracocentesis, during which approximately one litre of blood-tinged pleural fluid was extracted each time. Antitubercular therapy was started empirically but discontinued after confirming the diagnosis by MRCP. During the third hospital admission, a right-sided intercostal drain (ICD) was placed under aseptic conditions, with another litre of blood-stained pleural fluid collected in the ICD bag. Due to the rarity and infrequency of PPF in paediatric patients, standardized treatment protocols are lacking and management is largely informed by individual case reports. Initially, a conservative approach was taken: oral intake was withheld, and octreotide infusion was initiated at a dose of 30 µg/kg/day (range 10–40 µg/kg/day). The patient received total parenteral nutrition as well as pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy. Over the course of 10 days, the volume of fluid drained from the ICD steadily declined, ultimately ceasing. The ICD was then removed, and the child was discharged from the hospital.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following hospital discharge, the child has remained asymptomatic. He continues to attend scheduled monthly follow-up visits. No additional instances of recurrent pleural effusions have been observed since his discharge (S17). He has adopted lifestyle changes, incorporating a balanced diet and consistent exercise as recommended. He has resumed normal daily routines, is going to school consistently, and is able to participate in vigorous activities such as running and playing without limitation.

Discussion:

PPF is an exceedingly uncommon complication related to pancreatic disorders within the paediatric population. To date, only 47 paediatric cases of PPF have been reported. This condition may arise in acute or chronic pancreatitis, as well as following trauma in children. The precise frequency in paediatric patients remains undetermined, though it is estimated to complicate approximately 0.4% of pancreatitis cases overall. Genetic mutations such as SPINK1 and PRSS1 are significant contributors, accounting for about 30% of paediatric chronic pancreatitis cases. Non-genetic triggers include blunt abdominal trauma, responsible for 32% of cases in a 25-child series analyzed by Yang et al. Idiopathic cases continue to be reported, possibly due to unidentified genetic or ductal anomalies. Pancreatic fluid may escape from a compromised pancreatic duct or pseudocyst, traversing natural diaphragmatic passages (most commonly the aortic or oesophageal hiatus), or less frequently through direct erosion of the diaphragm, forming a fistulous communication to the pleural space.

Many cases remain undetected because children often present predominantly with thoracic symptoms, leading clinicians to pursue pulmonary diagnoses while the primary disease is abdominal. A review of the literature yielded 21 pertinent publications, with 68% of paediatric cases manifesting chiefly respiratory complaints—such as significant and recurrent unilateral or bilateral pleural effusions, dyspnoea, and cough. Fewer than 20% described abdominal symptoms including epigastric pain, vomiting, or weight loss, and about 24% exhibited constitutional features such as fever. Zi Yin Shang’s report in Frontiers in Paediatrics (2023) highlighted an instance of PPF in an 8-year-old boy whose sole presentation was a left-sided chest protrusion lasting two weeks; presentations with chest bulging as the initial sign are notably rare. In this case, the pleural effusion apparently developed incrementally after abdominal trauma. The intact function of the other lung enabled gradual compensation, so typical respiratory signs (e.g., cough, dyspnoea) were absent. Furthermore, the child concealed the history of trauma, having experienced bullying at school. Hemorrhagic pleural effusion prompted investigations for tuberculosis and cancer, but the true diagnosis diverged.

In the case described here, the patient similarly experienced recurrent pulmonary issues, including chest pain and repeated accumulation of pleural fluid on the right side. Only after several in-depth history-taking sessions and MRCP did abdominal trauma become apparent, steering the diagnostic process toward pancreatic disease. Therefore, PPF may act as a 'Pulmonary mask', misleading clinicians unless thorough histories and appropriate imaging are obtained.

PPF fluid collection occurs due to trauma or infection, leading to rupture of the pancreatic duct or pseudocyst and subsequent leakage of pancreatic secretions. If rupture happens anteriorly, fluid accumulates in the peritoneal cavity, causing ascites; if posterior, it tracks through the retroperitoneal space, moving upwards via the path of least resistance into the mediastinum through the aortic or oesophageal hiatus and ultimately perforating the pleura, resulting in pleural effusion. The internal fistula creates a conduit through which pancreatic fluid is diverted into the thorax. In adults, chronic pancreatitis due to alcohol consumption is the predominant cause of PPF. The largest review, by Uchiyama et al, compiled 113 cases from Japanese literature and established that 99% were linked to alcoholic chronic pancreatitis, with thoracic symptoms occurring in 68% of cases. Conversely, in children, genetic factors are more commonly implicated, such as PRSS1, CFTR, SPINK1, and chymotrypsin C gene mutations, with exon 3 (R122H) and exon 2 (N291) mutations of PRSS1 being most notable. Recurrence or chronicity in paediatric pancreatitis necessitates comprehensive family history assessment and consideration of hereditary pancreatitis.

Diagnostic delays are typical, as patients frequently undergo extensive evaluation for respiratory conditions before pancreatic etiology is suspected. Pleural fluid amylase concentrations ≥1000 IU/L are considered definitive for PPF. However, tuberculosis, oesophageal perforation, and malignancies involving the lung or pancreas must be ruled out as these conditions also elevate pleural fluid amylase. Differentiating pancreatic-type amylase from salivary-type amylase is essential, and amylase isoform typing should be included in the diagnostic approach for suspected PPF. MRCP demonstrates 70%–80% sensitivity for identifying the fistula and ductal anatomy, while CT imaging reliably detects pancreatic calcifications, pseudocysts, or ductal dilation but only visualizes the fistula in 33%–47% of cases. ERCP remains the gold standard for planning therapeutic intervention, with paediatric fistula visualization rates ranging from 38% to 78%.
