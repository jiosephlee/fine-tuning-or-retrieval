Title: Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood

Hans Vaish, Vipin Vaish, Shalini Suri, Vasudha Tomar

Abstract:

Summary: An older boy was admitted with repeated right-sided pleural effusions, chest pain, and breathing difficulties, which were temporarily relieved by fluid aspiration. The pleural aspirate repeatedly appeared dark red. Due to persistent effusions, a right-sided intercostal chest tube was inserted. His history was notable for a recent episode of abdominal trauma from striking his abdomen on bicycle handlebars. Chest CT imaging identified patchy consolidation in the right lung, hydropneumothorax, and a multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right paraesophageal area down to the pancreatic tail. Amylase and lipase levels were elevated in both serum and pleural fluid. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography demonstrated bilateral pancreatopleural fistulas, more prominent on the right. Management included making the patient nil per os, providing total parenteral nutrition, and administering Octreotide. Over ten days, fluid output lessened, allowing chest tube removal, and the patient was ultimately discharged.

Background:

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is an exceptionally uncommon condition characterized by repeated, large-volume, and often hemorrhagic pleural effusions. It has been documented in the context of acute or chronic pancreatitis as well as following pancreatic injury. The diagnosis can be challenging, as patients usually manifest with symptoms of respiratory compromise secondary to ongoing pleural effusions, which may divert clinical attention toward identifying a thoracic etiology rather than recognizing the underlying pancreatic origin, thus causing delays in diagnosis. To date, only 47 pediatric cases have been described, in comparison with over 300 documented instances in the adult population.

Case presentation:

A pediatric patient presented to the outpatient clinic accompanied by his mother, reporting a one-week history of breathing difficulty and right-sided chest pain. According to the mother, the child’s respiratory discomfort had progressively worsened over the past seven days. There was no known history of parental consanguinity. The mother described a previous household exposure to tuberculosis (Koch’s contact), as the child's grandfather had pulmonary tuberculosis for which he received appropriate treatment. The child's dietary pattern included frequent consumption of processed, high-calorie foods, with limited fresh fruits, vegetables, or well-balanced home-cooked meals. The patient’s weight was 44 kg, his height was 112 cm, and he had a body mass index of 35. On physical assessment, the child’s respiratory rate was measured at 20/min, with mild tachypnea noted. Chest movement on the right was diminished. Percussion revealed stony dullness over the right subscapular, right infra-axillary, and axillary regions. Vocal fremitus was notably decreased on the right compared to the left. Diminished breath sounds were also detected in the right subscapular, right infra-axillary, and axillary regions. There were no findings of pallor, cyanosis, jaundice, clubbing, or lymphadenopathy. The abdomen was soft with no evidence of hepatosplenomegaly. The patient was alert and oriented to time, place, and person. Cardiovascular assessment showed tachycardia without murmurs. Oxygen saturation was found to be 88%–89% on room air, with a heart rate of 110/min. Due to tachypnea and respiratory distress, the patient was admitted to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit and given supplemental oxygen via face mask at 5 L/min. A chest X-ray was obtained, and empirical antibiotics (piperacillin-tazobactam) were initiated after drawing blood cultures. Radiography demonstrated a right-sided pleural effusion with blunting of the right costophrenic angle (figure 1) (Table 2). Point-of-care lung ultrasonography estimated the effusion volume at approximately one litre (figure 2) (Table 2). Under sterile technique, right-sided thoracocentesis was performed, yielding roughly one litre of dark red pleural fluid. The patient’s tachypnea resolved after drainage, and oxygen saturation improved to 95%–98% on room air. The chest pain abated, and oxygen therapy was discontinued within 24 hours. The child was subsequently transferred to the general ward the following day and discharged. The aspirated pleural fluid was submitted for laboratory analysis. However, six days later, the patient was readmitted with recurrence of right-sided chest pain, tachypnea, and respiratory difficulty. Admission to the PICU followed, and chest X-ray again demonstrated a right-sided pleural effusion. Repeat thoracocentesis was performed, extracting about one litre of dark red pleural fluid. Pleural fluid adenosine deaminase (ADA) was mildly elevated (Table 1), and in light of the tuberculosis exposure, empirical antitubercular treatment was initiated. Nevertheless, after another five days, the child was hospitalized a third time with similar symptoms of right-sided chest pain and dyspnea. Upon re-evaluating the history, the patient disclosed experiencing blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle a month earlier. A contrast-enhanced chest CT was performed prior to intercostal drainage (ICD) placement. CT imaging revealed patchy areas of airspace consolidation in the right lung, right-sided hydropneumothorax, and an elongated, lobulated, multiloculated hypodense collection stretching from the right pleural space, extending from the right paraesophageal region to the upper left retroperitoneum, reaching the pancreatic tail (Table 2). An ICD was inserted, and approximately one litre of dark red pleural fluid was evacuated via the drainage system (figure 3). These radiological features, together with the appearance of the aspirate and the history of trauma, raised suspicion for a pancreaticopleural fistula (PPF). Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) was performed the same day, which demonstrated pancreatitis with a tubular cystic collection involving the distal pancreatic body and tail, tracking posteriorly in the retroperitoneum and ascending through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity. There was a narrow communication to the left pleural cavity as well. The fluid collection was contiguous with the distal pancreatic duct, which was narrowed proximal to the connection. The MRCP findings were consistent with PPF (figures 4-6) (Table 1). Serum and pleural fluid amylase and lipase tests were sent. Differential diagnosis:

The patient exhibited pleural effusion and chest pain, with a clear account of sustaining abdominal trauma one month prior. All etiologies of pleural effusion aside from post-traumatic pancreatitis were excluded. The patient had no record of fever, prolonged cough, or weight loss. Despite a family history of contact with tuberculosis (Koch’s), and a mildly increased pleural fluid ADA, GeneXpert testing for MTB was negative, thereby excluding pulmonary tuberculosis. The absence of fever and the lack of neutrophilic exudate in the pleural fluid also helped to exclude empyema. Malignancies, including those of the lung, leukemia, lymphoma, or metastatic tumors, may produce pleural fluid rich in amylase; nevertheless, no malignancy was suspected owing to the lack of fever or weight loss, along with a normal hematologic profile and peripheral smear. Moreover, pleural fluid cytology was negative for malignant cells. Amylase-rich pleural fluid is also seen in Boerhaave’s syndrome (esophageal perforation), but this was considered unlikely as the child maintained normal bowel movements and did not experience chest pain or vomiting. An acquired diaphragmatic hernia was dismissed because imaging did not demonstrate abdominal organs within the thoracic cavity. Hepatic hydrothorax was excluded based on normal liver function studies and liver imaging, in addition to the absence of chronic liver disease in the medical history. Lastly, renal pathology or hydronephrosis was ruled out due to normal findings on renal imaging and renal function assessments.

Treatment:

In the pediatric intensive care unit, the patient received humidified oxygen via face mask for respiratory support. Thoracocentesis was performed under ultrasound guidance on two occasions, yielding approximately one liter of blood-tinged pleural fluid each time. Antitubercular therapy was initiated empirically but discontinued following confirmation of the diagnosis through MRCP. During the third hospitalization, an intercostal drain (ICD) was placed on the right side under strict aseptic conditions, and another one liter of blood-stained pleural fluid was evacuated into the ICD collection bag. Pancreaticopleural fistula (PPF), being exceptionally rare in children, has no standardized management protocols; treatment approaches are largely informed by isolated case reports. A conservative approach was initially chosen, keeping the patient nil per os and starting an Octreotide infusion at a dose of 30 µg/kg/day (range: 10–40 µg/kg/day). The patient was also started on total parenteral nutrition and pancreatic enzyme supplements. Over the course of 10 days, the volume of pleural fluid drainage from the ICD steadily declined and eventually ceased. The ICD was then removed, and the patient was discharged home.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following hospital discharge, the patient has experienced no recurrence of symptoms. He attends scheduled monthly follow-up appointments with me. Since his discharge, there have been no additional instances of pleural effusion (S17). The patient has adopted recommended lifestyle changes, including a balanced diet and consistent exercise. He has resumed his routine activities and is consistently attending school. He is also able to engage in vigorous physical activities such as running and playing without limitations.

Discussion:

Pancreaticopleural fistula (PPF) is a particularly rare and uncommon sequela of pancreatic pathology in children. To date, only 47 pediatric instances of PPF have been documented. PPF may arise in the context of both acute and chronic pancreatitis or following traumatic injury. The actual frequency of this complication in pediatric patients remains undefined, but PPF occurs in approximately 0.4% of all pancreatitis cases. Genetic alterations, specifically in the serine peptidase inhibitor Kazal type 1 (SPINK1) and PRSS1 genes, are significant contributors—accounting for as much as 30% of childhood chronic pancreatitis. Blunt trauma to the abdomen is also a key acquired risk factor, as it represented 32% of cases among 25 children reviewed by Yang et al. There are still cases for which no cause is identified, potentially reflecting unrecognized genetic or ductal variants.

Pancreatic fluid from a disrupted duct or pseudocyst may migrate into the thoracic cavity through natural openings in the diaphragm, like the aortic or esophageal hiatuses, or less frequently via direct erosion through the diaphragm, thus forming a fistulous channel to the pleural space. The majority of cases are overlooked, as most children manifest with chest symptoms, prompting exhaustive pulmonary investigations, whereas the true origin is intra-abdominal. Our literature review identified 21 relevant case reports, 68% of which involved primarily respiratory signs such as large, recurrent, unilateral or bilateral pleural effusions, shortness of breath, and cough. Fewer than 20% had digestive complaints like epigastric pain, vomiting, or unintentional weight loss. Approximately one-quarter (24%) presented with constitutional symptoms such as fever.

Zi Yin Shang’s 2023 report in Frontiers in Paediatrics highlighted an 8-year-old presenting only with a left-sided chest bulge of two weeks’ duration; initial presentation as chest swelling is exceptionally unusual. In this particular case, the pleural effusion developed gradually after abdominal trauma. Because the unaffected lung compensated, the child tolerated the evolving effusion without developing classic respiratory symptoms such as cough or dyspnea. Complicating the history, the patient, who was experiencing bullying, intentionally concealed the prior abdominal injury. The diagnostic workup focused on tuberculosis and malignancy as causes for hemorrhagic pleural fluid, before an alternative etiology was determined.

Similarly, our patient experienced repeated chest complaints and ongoing right-sided pleural effusions. Only after multiple detailed history attempts did the episode of abdominal trauma become apparent, and magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) further indicated a pancreatic origin. As such, this disorder may mimic lung disease and distract clinicians unless careful history-taking and targeted imaging are employed.

The pathogenesis of fluid accumulation in PPF involves rupture of the pancreatic duct or a pseudocyst from external trauma or infection, leading to leakage of pancreatic fluid. If the rupture is anterior, ascites develops; if posterior, the fluid dissects retroperitoneally, ascends through the diaphragmatic hiatuses into the mediastinum, then into the pleural space to cause effusion. The fistulous tract diverts pancreatic fluid into the chest, analogous to a leaking conduit. In adult populations, chronic pancreatitis secondary to alcohol consumption most commonly results in PPF. Uchiyama et al performed a comprehensive Japanese review of 113 cases, with 99% linked to chronic alcoholic pancreatitis and 68% of patients initially manifesting with chest complaints.

In contrast, genetic etiologies are the main cause of chronic pancreatitis leading to PPF in the pediatric population. Well-characterized mutations causing hereditary pancreatitis include those in the PRSS1, CFTR, SPINK1, and chymotrypsin C genes—particularly R122H and N291 in the PRSS1 gene’s exon 3 and exon 2, respectively. Therefore, repeated or chronic episodes of pancreatitis in children warrant thorough family assessment and consideration of hereditary forms.

Diagnosis is typically delayed since the clinical presentation mimics pulmonary disease, prompting unnecessary pulmonary investigations before pancreatic disease is suspected. An amylase concentration of ≥1000 IU/L in pleural fluid is pathognomonic for PPF. However, conditions such as tuberculosis, esophageal perforation, or neoplasm involving the lung or pancreas may also present with elevated pleural amylase. Identification of pancreatic-type amylase in the fluid distinguishes PPF; in contrast, alternative conditions show the salivary-type. Amylase isoenzyme typing may therefore be a critical component in the diagnostic evaluation of suspected PPF.
