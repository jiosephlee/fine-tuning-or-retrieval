Title: Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood

Hans Vaish, Vipin Vaish, Shalini Suri, Vasudha Tomar

Abstract:

Summary: An older male child experienced multiple episodes of right-sided pleural effusions accompanied by chest pain and respiratory distress, with temporary symptom relief following each aspiration. The aspirated pleural fluid consistently appeared dark red. Due to the recurrent nature of the effusions, a right-sided intercostal drain was inserted. Past medical history included a recent abdominal injury involving a bicycle handlebar. Chest CT revealed right-sided patchy lung consolidation, hydropneumothorax, and a multiloculated, hypodense collection extending from the right paraesophageal space toward the tail of the pancreas. Laboratory analysis demonstrated elevated amylase and lipase levels in both serum and pleural fluid. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) identified pancreatopleural fistulas on both sides, more pronounced on the right. Management included withholding oral intake, administration of total parenteral nutrition, and therapy with Octreotide. Over ten days, pleural fluid output declined, allowing for chest tube removal, and the child was subsequently discharged.

Background:

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) represents a highly uncommon condition characterized by recurrent, large-volume, and sometimes hemorrhagic pleural effusions. It has been identified in association with acute pancreatitis, chronic pancreatitis, as well as pancreatic injury. The clinical presentation may be misleading, as patients primarily exhibit symptoms of respiratory compromise caused by persistent pleural effusions, while the underlying abnormality originates in the pancreas. This often results in delayed diagnosis, as the initial clinical focus is commonly directed toward detecting a thoracic source of pathology. To date, there have been only 47 reported pediatric cases of PPF, whereas in adults the number exceeds 300 documented instances.

Case presentation:

A child presented to the outpatient clinic with his mother, reporting a one-week history of breathing difficulty and right-sided chest pain. The mother described a progressive worsening of symptoms over seven days. There was no consanguinity in the family. She reported a history of contact with tuberculosis (Koch’s contact), as the child's grandfather had been treated for pulmonary Koch's. The dietary history revealed frequent intake of processed, high-calorie foods with minimal consumption of fresh fruits, vegetables, and nutritionally balanced home-cooked meals. The child weighed 44 kg, stood 112 cm tall, and had a BMI of 35. On physical examination, he had a respiratory rate of 20 per minute, with mild tachypnea. Decreased chest movements were observed on the right side. Percussion produced a stony dull note in the right subscapular, right infra-axillary, and axillary regions. Vocal fremitus was notably diminished on the right compared to the left. Decreased breath sounds were noted over the same right-sided regions. There was no evidence of pallor, cyanosis, icterus, clubbing, or lymphadenopathy. The abdominal exam was soft without organomegaly. The patient was alert, oriented to time, place, and person. The cardiovascular examination found tachycardia, but no murmurs. His oxygen saturation was 88–89% on room air, and his heart rate was 110 per minute. Given his tachypnea and respiratory distress, he was admitted to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit and commenced on oxygen by mask at 5 L/min. A chest X-ray was obtained and empiric antibiotics (piperacillin-tazobactam) were initiated after obtaining blood cultures. The chest X-ray demonstrated a right-sided pleural effusion with blunting of the right costophrenic angle (figure 1) (Table 2). Point-of-care lung ultrasound identified a right pleural effusion of approximately one liter (figure 2) (Table 2). Under aseptic precautions, a right-sided thoracocentesis was performed, yielding about one liter of dark red pleural fluid. Post-thoracocentesis, the child’s tachypnea subsided and his oxygen saturation improved to 95–98% on room air. His chest pain decreased, and he was weaned off supplemental oxygen within 24 hours. He was transferred to the general ward the following day and later discharged. The pleural fluid sample was sent for analysis.

Six days post-discharge, the child was readmitted for recurrent right-sided chest pain, tachypnea, and dyspnea. He was transferred again to the PICU. Repeat chest X-ray showed a right-sided pleural effusion. Another thoracocentesis was done, again draining about one liter of dark red fluid. Pleural fluid Adenosine Deaminase (ADA) was slightly raised (Table 1), and in view of the positive history of Koch’s contact, antitubercular treatment was started empirically.

Five days later, he was admitted for a third time, with recurring right-sided chest pain and respiratory distress. Further review of the patient’s history revealed an episode of blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle one month prior. A contrast-enhanced CT chest was obtained prior to intercostal drain (ICD) insertion, showing patchy right lung airspace consolidation, right-sided hydropneumothorax, and a large elongated, lobulated, multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right pleural space, tracking from the right paraesophageal level through the upper left retroperitoneum to the tail of the pancreas (Table 2). An ICD was placed, draining about one liter of dark red pleural fluid (figure 3). These CT findings, alongside dark red pleural aspirate and history of abdominal trauma, raised suspicion for a pancreaticopleural fistula (PPF). Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) was obtained the same day, revealing features of pancreatitis with a tubular cystic collection at the distal pancreatic body and tail extending into the retroperitoneum and superiorly through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural space. There was also a narrow tract to the left pleural space. The collection was noted to communicate with the distal pancreatic duct, with ductal narrowing seen proximal to the connection. These findings on MRCP (figures 4-6) (Table 1) supported a PPF diagnosis. Serum and pleural fluid were analyzed for amylase and lipase, and the pleural fluid demonstrated marked elevations—amylase 6118 U/L, lipase 7211 U/L (Table 2)—corroborating the diagnosis of PPF. Differential diagnosis:

The patient exhibited pleural effusion accompanied by chest pain, with a confirmed episode of abdominal trauma occurring one month prior. After considering post-traumatic pancreatitis, alternative etiologies for the pleural effusion were excluded. The child’s history did not include fever, persistent cough, or weight loss. There was a documented familial exposure to Koch’s, and the pleural fluid ADA level was slightly raised; nevertheless, GeneXpert testing for MTB returned a negative result. Consequently, pulmonary Koch’s was excluded as a diagnosis. The absence of fever and a lack of neutrophilic predominance in the pleural fluid discounted empyema. Although amylase-rich pleural fluid may be associated with malignancy (including primary pulmonary cancers, leukemia, lymphoma, or metastatic disease), malignancy was considered unlikely due to the absence of fever and weight loss, along with normal findings on peripheral blood analysis and smear. Cytological evaluation of pleural fluid did not demonstrate malignant cells. Boerhaave’s syndrome (esophageal perforation), another potential source of elevated amylase in pleural effusion, was ruled out, given the patient’s normal stool passage and no history of chest pain or vomiting. Acquired diaphragmatic hernia was dismissed because chest imaging revealed no intra-abdominal organs within the thoracic cavity. Hepatic hydrothorax was not considered, as liver function tests and imaging studies were within normal limits, and there was no clinical evidence of chronic liver disease. Renal involvement, including hydronephrosis, was excluded based on normal renal imaging and function tests.

Treatment:

In the pediatric intensive care unit, the patient received humidified oxygen therapy administered via face mask. Ultrasound-guided thoracocentesis was performed on two occasions, with approximately one liter of hemorrhagic pleural fluid withdrawn each time. Antitubercular medications were started empirically but discontinued once a definitive diagnosis was achieved through MRCP. During the third hospitalization, an intercostal drainage (ICD) tube was inserted on the right side with strict aseptic technique, resulting in drainage of another liter of blood-tinged pleural fluid into the ICD collection bag. Due to the rarity of pleuroperitoneal fistula (PPF) in children and the absence of standardized treatment protocols, management is guided by published case reports. Initially favoring conservative therapy, the patient was made nil per os and commenced on an Octreotide infusion at a dose of 30 µg/kg/day (with a typical range of 10–40 µg/kg/day). The patient also received total parenteral nutrition and pancreatic enzyme supplements. Over the next 10 days, the ICD fluid output progressively decreased and eventually ceased. The ICD was then removed, and the child was subsequently discharged.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following hospital discharge, the child has remained asymptomatic. He continues to attend monthly follow-up appointments with me. Since leaving the hospital, there have been no further occurrences of recurrent pleural effusions (S17). He has adopted recommended lifestyle changes, including a balanced diet and consistent exercise. The patient has resumed normal daily routines and attends school without interruption. He is also able to engage in vigorous physical activities, such as running and playing, without limitation.

Discussion:

PPF is a highly uncommon and rare complication associated with pancreatic disorders in children. To date, only 47 paediatric cases of PPF have been reported in the literature. PPF can occur secondary to both acute and chronic pancreatitis or result from trauma in the paediatric population. The precise frequency in children is not established; overall, PPF occurs in approximately 0.4% of pancreatitis cases. Mutations in Serine peptidase inhibitor Kazal type 1 (SPINK1) and PRSS1 are significant contributors, seen in up to 30% of paediatric chronic pancreatitis. Non-genetic causes such as blunt abdominal trauma are also notable, representing 32% of a 25-patient paediatric cohort reviewed by Yang et al. Instances of PPF without identifiable cause (idiopathic) persist and may relate to unrecognized genetic or ductal differences.

Pancreatic secretions may escape from a disrupted duct or pseudocyst and travel through natural diaphragmatic openings—most commonly the aortic or oesophageal hiatus, or less frequently via direct trans-diaphragmatic extension—thereby forming a fistula with the pleural space. The majority of cases are overlooked due to initial presentation with thoracic manifestations; consequently, diagnostic efforts tend to focus on pulmonary disease, while the actual pathology originates intra-abdominally. Review of 21 pertinent reports found that 68% of cases exhibited predominantly respiratory complaints—such as large, recurring unilateral or bilateral pleural effusions, shortness of breath, and cough. Fewer than 20% displayed abdominal issues (e.g., epigastric pain, vomiting, weight loss) while constitutional features like fever occurred in 24% of patients.

A recent report by Zi Yin Shang in Frontiers in Paediatrics 2023 outlined PPF in an 8-year-old boy presenting solely with left chest protrusion over 2 weeks—a very atypical initial sign. In that instance, pleural fluid appeared to accumulate progressively after abdominal trauma, with the unaffected lung compensating such that the classic respiratory symptoms were absent. Additionally, psychosocial factors played a role, as the boy, experiencing bullying, concealed the history of trauma. Workup for haemorrhagic pleural effusion included tuberculosis and oncological investigations, but ultimately, the diagnosis was unrelated.

Similarly, our case involved a patient with persistent pulmonary complaints, namely chest pain and repeat right-sided pleural effusions. Eliciting a trauma history required multiple interviews, and ultimately, MRCP findings pointed towards an underlying pancreatic condition. Thus, PPF may masquerade as primary thoracic disease and risk misdirecting clinicians unless detailed historical assessment and suitable imaging are performed.

The mechanism underlying fluid collection in PPF involves external injury or infection leading to rupture of the pancreatic duct or pseudocyst and consequent leakage of pancreatic fluid. Rupture on the anterior pancreatic surface leads to ascites, while posterior rupture results in leakage to the retroperitoneal space, where fluid can ascend via least resistance through the diaphragm (aorta or oesophageal hiatus) and breach the pleura, yielding a pleural effusion. The resultant internal pancreatic fistula permits pancreatic fluid to escape into the thoracic cavity, behaving like a conduit.

In adults, chronic pancreatitis due to alcohol is the main etiology for PPF. The most extensive national review, performed by Uchiyama et al., identified 113 Japanese cases; 99% were linked to alcoholic chronic pancreatitis, with chest symptoms occurring in 68% of instances. By contrast, hereditary causes predominate among children. Mutations of PRSS1, the cystic fibrosis transmembrane regulator, SPINK1, and chymotrypsin C genes are implicated, notably the R122H and N291 mutations (exons 3 and 2, respectively) in PRSS1. Recurrent or chronic pancreatitis in a child warrants a thorough family history and evaluation for hereditary pancreatitis.

PPF is often diagnosed late, as the focus is typically on pulmonary investigations before pancreatic involvement is considered. Pathognomonic for PPF is pleural fluid amylase ≥1000 IU/L. However, differential diagnoses such as tuberculosis, oesophageal perforation, and pulmonary or pancreatic malignancies, which may also yield high pleural fluid amylase, should be ruled out. Pancreatic-type (rather than salivary-type) amylase in the pleural fluid helps distinguish PPF. Amylase isoenzyme analysis of the pleural fluid may aid in diagnosis.

MRCP demonstrates a sensitivity of 70%–80% in identifying fistula and ductal anatomy. CT scans are valuable for detecting pancreatic calcification, pseudocyst, or ductal dilatation, but visualise the fistula itself in only 33%–47% of cases.
