Title: Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood

Hans Vaish, Vipin Vaish, Shalini Suri, Vasudha Tomar

Abstract:

Summary: An older child presented with recurrent right pleural effusions accompanied by chest pain and difficulty breathing, with temporary relief following fluid aspiration. The drained pleural fluid consistently appeared dark red in color. Ongoing effusions warranted placement of a right-sided intercostal drain. On review, he had a history of recent abdominal trauma caused by a bicycle handlebar. Chest computed tomography revealed patchy consolidation on the right side, a hydropneumothorax, and a multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right paraesophageal area toward the pancreatic tail. Both the serum and pleural fluid demonstrated elevated levels of amylase and lipase. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography established the presence of bilateral pancreatopleural fistulas (more prominent on the right). Management included keeping the patient nil per os, administration of total parenteral nutrition, and Octreotide. After 10 days, the volume of drainage diminished, the chest tube was withdrawn, and the patient was discharged.

Background:

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is a highly uncommon condition characterized by repeated, large, and often hemorrhagic pleural effusions. It may occur in the context of acute or chronic pancreatitis, as well as following pancreatic injury. The condition can be diagnostically challenging because patients typically exhibit symptoms of respiratory compromise resulting from recurrent pleural fluid accumulation, while the underlying problem actually originates in the pancreas. This can lead to a delayed diagnosis, since clinical evaluation is often focused on potential thoracic causes. In the pediatric population, only 47 cases of PPF have been documented, in contrast to more than 300 cases reported among adults.

Case presentation:

A young patient presented to my outpatient clinic, accompanied by his mother, with a one-week history of dyspnea and right-sided chest pain. The mother reported that the child’s respiratory discomfort had worsened steadily over seven days. There was no history of consanguinity. The mother also reported household exposure to pulmonary tuberculosis ("Koch's contact") via the child’s grandfather, who had received proper treatment for the illness. Dietary assessment indicated frequent ingestion of processed and high-calorie foods, with insufficient intake of fresh produce and balanced homemade meals. The child's weight was 44 kg, height was 112 cm, and calculated body mass index was 35.

On physical exam, the child’s respiratory rate was mildly elevated at 20 breaths/min, and he appeared tachypneic. Right-sided chest movements were diminished. Percussion produced a stony dull note in the right subscapular, infra-axillary, and axillary regions. Vocal fremitus was markedly decreased on the right compared to the left, and breath sounds were similarly reduced over these right-sided regions. There was no evidence of pallor, cyanosis, jaundice, digital clubbing, or lymphadenopathy. The abdomen was soft and non-tender, without hepatosplenomegaly. Neurologically, the child was alert and fully oriented. Cardiovascular assessment showed tachycardia, but no murmurs were detected. Oxygen saturation on room air ranged from 88% to 89%, and heart rate was 110 beats per minute.

Given the respiratory distress and tachypnea, the patient was admitted to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit, where supplemental oxygen was initiated at 5 liters per minute via mask. Chest radiography was performed, and empiric antibiotics (piperacillin-tazobactam) were started after sending blood for cultures. Imaging demonstrated a right-sided pleural effusion with obliteration of the right costophrenic angle (figure 1, Table 2). Bedside lung ultrasonography indicated approximately one liter of pleural fluid on the right side (figure 2, Table 2). Thoracocentesis was performed under sterile technique, yielding approximately one liter of dark red pleural fluid. The patient’s tachypnea resolved following the procedure, with oxygen saturation improving to 95%–98% on room air. Chest pain improved, and he was weaned off oxygen support within 24 hours. He was then transferred to the ward and subsequently discharged. The pleural fluid sample was sent for laboratory analysis.

However, six days later, the child was readmitted with recurrence of right-sided chest pain, tachypnea, and breathing difficulty. He was again managed in the PICU, and chest radiography once more revealed a right-sided pleural effusion. Repeat thoracocentesis yielded about one liter of similarly dark red pleural fluid. Pleural fluid adenosine deaminase (ADA) was slightly increased (Table 1), and given the known history of exposure to tuberculosis, empirical antitubercular therapy was begun. Five days later, the patient returned for the third time with the same symptoms of right-sided chest pain and respiratory distress. At this point, further history was obtained; the child revealed having sustained blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handlebar one month prior. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography of the chest was conducted prior to intercostal drain (ICD) insertion. CT imaging showed patchy right lung consolidation, a right hydropneumothorax, and a multiloculated, elongated, hypodense collection tracking from the right pleural space at the paraoesophageal region down to the upper left retroperitoneum and extending to the tail of the pancreas (Table 2). An ICD was placed, which drained about one liter of dark red pleural fluid (figure 3). Correlating the CT findings, the appearance of pleural fluid, and the trauma history, a pancreaticopleural fistula (PPF) was considered. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) performed on the same day revealed features of pancreatitis and a tubular cystic collection involving the distal body and tail of the pancreas, extending posteriorly into the retroperitoneum, and tracking superiorly through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity. A narrow tract to the left pleural cavity was also observed. The collection communicated with the distal pancreatic duct, which was narrowed proximally to the site of communication. MRCP supported the diagnosis of PPF (figures 4–6, Table 1). Amylase and lipase levels were measured in both serum and pleural fluid. Pleural fluid analysis demonstrated markedly elevated amylase (6118 U/L) and lipase (7211 U/L) (Table 2), consistent with PPF. Antitubercular therapy was discontinued

Differential diagnosis:

The patient arrived with pleural effusion and chest pain, having a documented incident of abdominal trauma one month prior. After excluding other etiologies for pleural effusion, post-traumatic pancreatitis remained as the probable cause. The child lacked symptoms such as fever, persistent cough, or weight loss. Although there was a family history of Koch’s contact and the pleural fluid adenosine deaminase (ADA) was mildly raised, GeneXpert testing for Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB) returned negative results, thereby excluding pulmonary Koch’s. Empyema was considered but dismissed due to the absence of fever and lack of a neutrophilic exudate in the pleural fluid. Malignancies—including those of the lung, leukemia, lymphoma, or metastatic tumors—could also produce pleural fluid high in amylase; however, these were excluded based on the lack of fever and weight loss, a normal blood count, unremarkable peripheral smear, and pleural fluid cytology showing no malignant cells. Boerhaave’s syndrome (esophageal rupture) may result in amylase-rich pleural fluid, but was excluded as the child had normal stool passage and no complaints of chest pain or vomiting. Acquired diaphragmatic hernia was ruled out since imaging did not show any abdominal organs present in the thoracic cavity. Hepatic hydrothorax was considered but eliminated given normal liver function tests and liver imaging, as well as no history of chronic liver disease. Renal pathology or hydronephrosis were excluded as renal imaging and function tests were normal.

Treatment:

In the PICU, the patient received respiratory support through humidified oxygen administered by mask. Ultrasound-guided thoracocentesis was performed twice, each time yielding approximately one liter of blood-tinged pleural fluid. Empirical antitubercular therapy was initiated but discontinued after MRCP confirmed the diagnosis. Upon the third admission, a right-sided intercostal drain (ICD) was placed with strict aseptic technique, resulting in the drainage of an additional one liter of blood-stained pleural fluid into the ICD collection bag. As pleuro-pancreatic fistula (PPF) is both rare and infrequently seen among paediatric cases, definitive treatment protocols are absent. Management generally relies on published case reports. Conservative therapy was selected initially; the child was made nil per oral and commenced on an Octreotide infusion at a dose of 30 µg/kg/day (range 10–40 µg/kg/day). Total parenteral nutrition and pancreatic enzyme supplementation were started. Over the ensuing 10 days, ICD output progressively diminished until cessation. The ICD was removed, and the child was discharged from hospital care.

Outcome and follow-up:

Since leaving the hospital, the patient has remained asymptomatic. He continues to see me for routine monthly follow-up appointments. No additional instances of pleural effusion have occurred following discharge (S17). He has implemented recommended lifestyle modifications, maintaining a balanced diet and engaging in regular physical activity. He has resumed normal daily routines and is consistently attending school. He is able to participate in physically demanding activities such as running and playing without limitation.

Discussion:

PPF is a highly uncommon and seldom-seen complication of pancreatic disorders in children. To date, there have been only 47 paediatric cases of PPF recorded. The condition may arise following acute or chronic pancreatitis, or in association with paediatric trauma. Its precise prevalence among children is undetermined, but it complicates approximately 0.4% of all pancreatitis cases. Mutations in the serine peptidase inhibitor Kazal type 1 (SPINK1) and PRSS1 genes are significant, representing up to 30% of paediatric chronic pancreatitis cases. Blunt trauma to the abdomen is another significant non-genetic precipitating factor, accounting for 32% in the cohort studied by Yang et al. Cases of unknown cause persist and may result from underappreciated genetic or ductal anomalies.

Pancreatic fluid originating from a disrupted duct or pseudocyst may reach the pleural cavity by traversing natural diaphragmatic openings, most commonly the aortic or oesophageal hiatus, or, less often, through direct erosion of the diaphragm, thus establishing a fistulous pathway to the pleura. These instances are frequently overlooked, as most patients present with thoracic complaints, prompting exhaustive searches for pulmonary disease while the true origin is abdominal. A literature review identified 21 pertinent publications, in which 68% of paediatric PPF cases exhibited primary respiratory symptoms such as substantial or recurrent unilateral or bilateral pleural effusions, shortness of breath, and cough. Fewer than 20% reported abdominal signs including epigastric pain, vomiting, or weight loss. Constitutional symptoms, like fever, were noted in 24%. Zi Yin Shang’s report in Frontiers in Paediatrics (2023) details PPF in an 8-year-old with only a left chest bulge for 2 weeks; cases where chest bulging is the presenting symptom are extremely unusual. Here, pleural effusion is thought to have developed gradually post abdominal trauma, with compensatory function of the healthy lung permitting gradual adaptation and masking typical symptoms such as cough or dyspnoea. Moreover, the child, having experienced bullying, concealed the abdominal trauma history. This patient was initially investigated for tuberculosis and malignancy due to haemorrhagic pleural effusion, but the diagnosis ultimately proved otherwise.

Similarly, our patient experienced recurrent pulmonary issues in the form of chest pain and right-sided pleural effusions. The abdominal trauma history was revealed only after repeated interviews, and MRCP findings indicated underlying pancreatic disease. Therefore, this pathology may disguise itself with predominant pulmonary signs, potentially misleading clinicians unless thorough history and appropriate imaging are obtained.

PPF fluid results from extrinsic forces or infection causing rupture of the pancreatic duct or pseudocyst and subsequent leakage of pancreatic contents. Anterior duct rupture leads to intra-abdominal leakage and ascites, whereas posterior rupture releases fluid into the retroperitoneal space, which ascends via natural diaphragmatic openings and breaches the pleura, resulting in effusion. An internal pancreatic fistula serves as a conduit, channeling pancreatic secretions to the thorax.

In adults, chronic pancreatitis and subsequent PPF are largely associated with alcohol use. Uchiyama et al reviewed Japanese literature, documenting 113 cases, noting 99% linked to chronic alcoholic pancreatitis and chest manifestations in 68%. Conversely, paediatric chronic pancreatitis is mainly attributable to genetic defects. Well-described causative mutations include those in the PRSS1, CFTR, SPINK1, and chymotrypsin C genes. In particular, the R122H and N291 mutations in exons 3 and 2, respectively, of PRSS1 are most characteristic. For recurrent or chronic paediatric pancreatitis, a complete family history and consideration of hereditary pancreatitis are warranted.

Delays in diagnosis are typical, as most patients initially undergo comprehensive pulmonary evaluation before the pancreatic source is suspected. A pleural fluid amylase ≥1000 IU/L is diagnostic of PPF. Nevertheless, other diagnoses with elevated pleural fluid amylase — such as tuberculosis, oesophageal perforation, or malignancy involving the lung or pancreas — must be ruled out. PPF features pancreatic-type amylase in pleural fluid, whereas other conditions usually show the salivary-type; amylase isoenzyme typing assists in confirming the diagnosis.

MRCP offers a sensitivity of 70%–80% in visualising fistulas and ductal anatomy. CT reliably displays pancreatic calcifications, pseudocysts, or duct dilation, but demonstrates the fistulous tract only in 33%–47%. Endoscopic cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) is the gold standard for therapeutic assessment, with fistula detection rates in paediatrics ranging from 38% to 78%.
