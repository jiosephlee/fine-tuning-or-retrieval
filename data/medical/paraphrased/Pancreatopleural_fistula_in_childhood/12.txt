Title: Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood

Hans Vaish, Vipin Vaish, Shalini Suri, Vasudha Tomar

Abstract:

Summary: A preadolescent boy was admitted with multiple episodes of right-sided pleural effusion accompanied by chest pain and breathing difficulties, which showed only temporary relief following aspiration. The pleural fluid had a persistent dark red color. Due to ongoing effusions, a right intercostal drainage tube was inserted. The patient's medical history included a recent bicycle handlebar injury to the abdomen. Chest CT revealed patchy consolidation on the right, hydropneumothorax, and a multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right paraesophageal area to the pancreatic tail. Elevated levels of amylase and lipase were detected in both serum and pleural fluid. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography identified bilateral pancreatopleural fistulas, more pronounced on the right. Management consisted of keeping the patient nil per oral, providing total parenteral nutrition, and administering Octreotide. Within 10 days, drainage decreased, allowing for removal of the chest tube, and the patient was subsequently discharged.

Background:

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is a very uncommon condition, characterized by recurrent, large, and blood-tinged pleural effusions. It may develop in the setting of acute or chronic pancreatitis, as well as following pancreatic trauma. The diagnosis may be challenging, as patients typically exhibit symptoms of respiratory compromise related to persistent pleural effusions, while the underlying issue originates in the pancreas. This often leads to delays in identifying the correct diagnosis, as investigations primarily focus on potential thoracic causes. In the pediatric population, only 47 instances of PPF have been documented, compared to over 300 reported adult cases.

Case presentation:

A young boy was brought to the outpatient clinic by his mother, who reported that he had been experiencing shortness of breath and right-sided chest pain for one week. According to her, his respiratory distress had gradually worsened over the past seven days. There was no record of consanguinity in the family. The mother reported a history of Koch’s contact, as the child’s grandfather had been diagnosed with pulmonary tuberculosis and received appropriate treatment. The child's diet mainly consisted of processed and calorie-rich foods, with minimal fresh fruits, vegetables, or well-balanced home-cooked meals. His weight was measured at 44 kg, height at 112 cm, resulting in a body mass index (BMI) of 35.

On physical assessment, the child’s respiratory rate was 20 breaths per minute, showing mild tachypnea. Chest movement was reduced on the right, and percussion revealed stony dullness in the right subscapular, infra-axillary, and axillary areas. Vocal fremitus was markedly decreased on the right compared to the left. Auscultation showed diminished breath sounds in the same right-sided regions. There were no clinical signs of pallor, cyanosis, icterus, clubbing, or enlarged lymph nodes. Abdominal examination showed a soft, non-tender abdomen without evidence of hepatosplenomegaly. The child was alert, fully oriented, and remained hemodynamically stable apart from tachycardia, with no audible murmurs. His oxygen saturation was noted to be 88%–89% on room air, and his heart rate was 110 beats per minute. Due to persistent tachypnea and respiratory compromise, the child was admitted to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit (PICU), where oxygen therapy was initiated at 5 L/min via mask. Empiric antibiotics (piperacillin-tazobactam) were started following the collection of blood cultures, and a chest X-ray was performed, which showed a right-sided pleural effusion with obscured right costophrenic angle (figure 1) (Table 2). Bedside lung ultrasound confirmed the presence of approximately one litre of pleural effusion on the right (figure 2) (Table 2).

Under strict aseptic technique, therapeutic aspiration of the right pleural effusion was performed, yielding about one litre of dark red fluid. After thoracocentesis, the child’s tachypnea resolved and his oxygen saturation on room air increased to 95%–98%. Chest pain subsided, and oxygen supplementation was discontinued within 24 hours. He was transferred to the general ward the following day and subsequently discharged. The aspirated pleural fluid was sent for diagnostic analysis.

Six days later, the child was readmitted with recurrent right-sided chest pain, tachypnea, and dyspnea. He was once again moved to the PICU, where repeat chest radiography demonstrated another right-sided pleural effusion. A second thoracocentesis was performed, again draining approximately one litre of dark red pleural fluid. The pleural fluid adenosine deaminase (ADA) level was mildly elevated (Table 1), and considering the prior history of Koch’s contact, antitubercular therapy was initiated empirically.

Five days afterward, he was admitted for a third time, presenting with similar right-sided chest pain and respiratory difficulty. On reevaluation, the child disclosed a history of blunt abdominal trauma caused by a bicycle handle one month before symptom onset. A contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) scan of the chest was obtained prior to insertion of an intercostal drainage (ICD) tube. Imaging showed patchy air space consolidation in the right lung, right-sided hydropneumothorax, and a large, elongated, multiloculated, hypodense collection extending from the right pleural cavity, along the right paraesophageal area, and into the left-sided retroperitoneum near the tail of the pancreas (Table 2). An ICD was placed, draining around one litre of dark red pleural fluid (figure 3). The combination of the CT findings, the appearance of the pleural fluid, and the recent trauma suggested a pancreaticopleural fistula (PPF). Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) was performed the same day, revealing evidence of pancreatitis and a tubular cystic fluid collection originating from the distal body and tail of the pancreas, tracking posteriorly through the retroperitoneum and superiorly via the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural space. A narrow connection to the left pleural space was also present. The collection was in communication with the distal pancreatic duct, which appeared narrowed proximal to the fistula. Differential diagnosis:

The patient arrived with pleural effusion and chest pain, with a clear antecedent of abdominal trauma occurring one month earlier. Following the diagnosis of post-traumatic pancreatitis, alternative etiologies for the pleural effusion were excluded. The child did not report fever, persistent cough, or weight loss. Although there was a family history of contact with a tuberculosis case and the pleural fluid adenosine deaminase (ADA) was modestly increased, GeneXpert testing for Mycobacterium tuberculosis was negative, allowing exclusion of pulmonary tuberculosis. The absence of fever and lack of a neutrophilic exudate in the pleural fluid discounted empyema. While certain malignancies (including pulmonary neoplasms, leukemia, lymphoma, and metastases) may present with amylase-enriched pleural effusion, features such as no history of fever, no weight loss, unremarkable blood tests, and a normal peripheral smear argued against malignancy. Cytological examination of the pleural fluid also failed to identify malignant cells. Amylase-rich pleural effusions can occur in Boerhaave’s syndrome (esophageal perforation); however, this was excluded as the patient continued normal bowel movements and reported no chest pain or vomiting. Imaging did not reveal any abdominal organs herniating into the chest, thereby ruling out acquired diaphragmatic hernia. Hepatic hydrothorax was deemed unlikely due to normal liver function assessments, unremarkable hepatic imaging, and absence of any chronic liver disease history. Normal findings from renal imaging and preserved renal function tests excluded renal pathology, including hydronephrosis.

Treatment:

In the pediatric intensive care unit, the patient received humidified oxygen therapy via facemask. Thoracocentesis, guided by ultrasound, was performed twice, each occasion yielding approximately one liter of blood-tinged pleural fluid. Antitubercular medications were initiated empirically but were discontinued once magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) confirmed the diagnosis. Upon a third admission, an intercostal drainage (ICD) tube was aseptically inserted on the right side, with another liter of blood-tinged pleural effusion drained into the ICD collection bag. Pancreaticopleural fistula (PPF), being rare in children, has no standardized treatment protocol, so management is typically guided by individual case reports. An initial conservative approach was taken: the patient was made nil per os and commenced on an Octreotide infusion at a dose of 30 µg/kg/day (within the range of 10–40 µg/kg/day). Total parenteral nutrition and pancreatic enzyme supplementation were instituted. Over the subsequent 10 days, drainage from the ICD gradually lessened and eventually ceased. The ICD was subsequently removed, and the child was discharged from hospital care.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following hospital discharge, the patient has remained asymptomatic. He attends scheduled monthly follow-up appointments with me. Since leaving the hospital, there have been no further incidents of recurrent pleural effusions (S17). The patient has adjusted his lifestyle and now maintains a balanced diet along with consistent exercise as recommended. He has resumed his normal daily routines and attends school without interruption. He is able to participate in vigorous physical activities, including running and playing, without experiencing any limitations.

Discussion:

PPF is a highly uncommon complication arising from pancreatic pathology in children. To date, only 47 pediatric PPF cases have been documented. It may develop following acute or chronic pancreatitis or after childhood trauma. The true prevalence in pediatric patients is not established, but PPF complicates roughly 0.4% of pancreatitis cases overall. Genetic alterations, including serine peptidase inhibitor Kazal type 1 (SPINK1) and PRSS1 mutations, play a significant role, representing up to 30% of pediatric chronic pancreatitis cases. Blunt trauma to the abdomen is another noteworthy acquired trigger, seen in 32% of the 25 pediatric patients reviewed by Yang et al. Idiopathic cases continue to be described, likely reflecting unrecognized genetic or ductal anomalies. In PPF, pancreatic fluid from a ruptured duct or pseudocyst may migrate along the natural diaphragmatic openings, usually the aortic or esophageal hiatus, or less commonly directly erode through the diaphragm, thereby establishing a fistulous connection with the pleural space. These cases often go undiagnosed, as they typically present with thoracic symptoms and the diagnostic process frequently focuses on pulmonary diseases, despite the abdominal source of the problem. A literature review found 21 relevant publications, with 68% of patients exhibiting primarily respiratory complaints such as large, recurrent unilateral or bilateral pleural effusions, dyspnea, and cough. Fewer than 20% reported abdominal symptoms (e.g., epigastric pain, vomiting, weight loss), and constitutional symptoms like fever were seen in 24% of cases. In the case discussed by Zi Yin Shang (Frontiers in Paediatrics 2023), an 8-year-old boy presented solely with progressive left-sided chest swelling over two weeks—a presentation rarely reported. For this child, the pleural effusion likely developed gradually after trauma, with preserved compensatory function of the unaffected lung permitting adaptation and masking typical respiratory features such as cough or breathlessness. Additionally, the child concealed the history of trauma due to bullying. Initial workup targeted tuberculosis and malignancy as explanations for hemorrhagic effusion, but further evaluation yielded a different diagnosis. In our own case, recurrent chest pain and repeated right pleural effusions were prominent. Only through persistent history-taking did a trauma history emerge, and MRCP was instrumental in pinpointing pancreatic disease. Consequently, this condition can be misleading by mimicking a primarily pulmonary disorder, making thorough history-taking and targeted imaging critical for accurate diagnosis. Fluid accumulation in PPF arises from duct rupture or pseudocyst caused by trauma or infection. Anterior duct ruptures result in pancreatic fluid leakage into the abdominal cavity (causing ascites), whereas posterior ruptures allow fluid to migrate retroperitoneally, tracking cephalad via the aortic or esophageal hiatus, passing into the mediastinum, and breaking into the pleural cavity. Essentially, an internal pancreatic fistula acts as a conduit diverting secretions into the thoracic space. In adults, alcohol-induced chronic pancreatitis is the leading cause of PPF. The comprehensive Japanese review by Uchiyama et al recorded 113 cases, with 99% linked to alcoholic pancreatitis and respiratory symptoms observed in 68%. By contrast, hereditary forms dominate in pediatric chronic pancreatitis, with pathogenic variants in PRSS1, SPINK1, CFTR, and chymotrypsin C among the most frequently encountered. The PRSS1 gene’s R122H and N291 mutations (exon 3 and exon 2, respectively) are particularly significant. When a child develops recurrent or chronic pancreatitis, it is imperative to elicit a detailed family history and consider hereditary causes. Diagnosis of PPF is often delayed, as extensive pulmonary investigations usually precede suspicion of pancreatic etiology. A pleural fluid amylase concentration of ≥1000 IU/L is considered diagnostic for PPF. Nonetheless, conditions like tuberculosis, esophageal perforation, and thoracic or pancreatic tumors should be excluded since they too may elevate pleural fluid amylase. Notably, pancreatic isoenzyme identification differentiates PPF from other causes with elevated amylase, as other conditions show predominance of salivary-type amylase. Isoenzyme analysis can therefore be diagnostic in suspected cases. MRCP yields fistula or ductal anatomy in 70–80% of cases, while CT identifies pancreatic calcifications, pseudocysts, or ductal dilation, though it visualizes the fistula itself in only 33–47%. ERCP continues to serve as the gold standard for both diagnosis and therapy planning, with reported pediatric fistula visualization rates from 38% to 78%. PPF management includes medical, endoscopic, and surgical approaches. Standardized protocols are lacking due to PPF’s rarity. Initial management is medical if no ductal abnormality exists.
