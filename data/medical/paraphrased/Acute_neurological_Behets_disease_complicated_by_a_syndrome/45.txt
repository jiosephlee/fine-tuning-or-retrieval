Title: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

Eiichi Kakehi, Yukako Matsumoto, Shigehisa Sakurai, Kazuhiko Kotani

Abstract:

Summary: We describe an uncommon instance of acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) identified simultaneously with systemic Behçet’s disease (BD), meeting criteria for definite BD, and complicated by the development of syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). A male patient in his 60s presented with fever, headache, and mucocutaneous manifestations such as oral aphthous ulcers, folliculitis-like eruptions, genital ulceration, and hyponatraemia. The diagnosis of BD was established based on mucocutaneous features. Brain MRI findings were unremarkable, while cerebrospinal fluid analysis demonstrated aseptic meningitis, corroborating the diagnosis of acute neuro-BD. Laboratory assessment satisfied criteria for SIADH. Treatment with colchicine alone resulted in resolution of mucocutaneous lesions, neurological symptoms, and hyponatraemia, without the administration of corticosteroids or additional immunosuppressive agents. This case indicates a potential relationship between acute neuro-BD and SIADH, highlighting the necessity of recognizing both neurological and endocrine abnormalities in patients with recent-onset BD.

Background:

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a persistent inflammatory condition affecting multiple organ systems, marked by recurring oral and genital ulcers, uveitis, and cutaneous manifestations. Neurological manifestations, known as neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), are seen in approximately 10%–20% of cases and generally appear years after BD is first identified. The acute presentation of neuro-BD commonly includes meningoencephalitis and brain parenchymal lesions, often localized to the brainstem. While neuro-BD is more frequently diagnosed subsequent to the initial BD diagnosis, simultaneous identification of both conditions is rare. In such circumstances, it is important for clinicians to evaluate for both mucocutaneous signs and neurological issues, such as headache, behavioral or psychiatric disturbances, and localized neurological deficits.

The syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) represents a frequent etiology of hyponatremia and is usually secondary to central nervous system (CNS) inflammation, neoplastic processes, or the influence of certain drugs. Due to the inflammatory aspect of acute neuro-BD, individuals affected may be predisposed to SIADH. While associations between SIADH and the use of agents like cyclophosphamide in BD have been noted, the incidence of SIADH in the context of acute neuro-BD in the absence of pharmacologic provocations, attributable exclusively to CNS inflammation, remains insufficiently established.

We describe an uncommon case involving concurrent diagnoses of BD and acute neuro-BD complicated by SIADH. This report underscores the need to recognize SIADH as a possible complication in acute neuro-BD, especially in patients exhibiting vague neurologic complaints and hyponatremia.

Case presentation:

A man of Asian descent in his 60s arrived at the emergency department after experiencing fever, headache, general malaise, diminished appetite, continuous hiccups, and joint pain localized to the shoulders and ankles over the preceding three days. Additionally, he reported having oral aphthous ulcers.

His past medical history was notable for type 2 diabetes mellitus, which was untreated due to his limited awareness of the diagnosis and insufficient adherence to recommended dietary and exercise regimens. There was no significant family history, and no relatives had been diagnosed with BD. He drank alcohol on occasion, was a non-smoker, and had not recently traveled or been exposed to animals or sick persons. His occupation involved metalworking, and he had not participated in recent sexual activity.

Upon assessment, he was awake but showed signs of tiredness. His vital signs were recorded as follows: temperature at 38.1°C, blood pressure measuring 135/73 mm Hg, heart rate at 119 beats per minute with a regular rhythm, respiratory rate of 19 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation at 96% while breathing room air. The physical exam identified oral aphthae and rashes resembling folliculitis on his face and torso (see figure 1A, B, and C). Cardiopulmonary and abdominal examinations yielded normal results. Manual muscle testing indicated no weakness in either the upper or lower limbs. No peripheral oedema was observed, and clinically, his volume status was judged to be euvolemic. He was hospitalized for additional investigations.

Investigations:

Initial laboratory assessment showed pronounced hyponatraemia (124 mmol/L), increased creatine kinase (1349 U/L), raised aspartate aminotransferase (77 U/L), alanine aminotransferase (64 U/L), C-reactive protein (CRP) at 180 mg/L (reference <3 mg/L), and erythrocyte sedimentation rate at 70 mm/hour (reference <15 mm/hour), while renal function remained within normal limits. The patient was also found to be hyperglycaemic (plasma glucose 14.2 mmol/L) with a high HbA1c (8.2%), indicative of inadequate glycaemic regulation. Testing for hepatitis B and C was negative.

Hormonal assessments demonstrated thyroid-stimulating hormone (1.11 mIU/L; reference 0.50–5.00 mIU/L), free T4 (1.25 ng/dL; reference 0.90–1.70 ng/dL), and serum cortisol (42 µg/dL; reference >6 µg/dL) all within normal reference intervals. Serum osmolality was decreased (267 mOsm/kg; reference <270 mOsm/kg), and urine osmolality was substantially elevated (900 mOsm/kg; reference >300 mOsm/kg), alongside an increased urinary sodium concentration (88 mmol/L; reference >20 mmol/L), which signified impaired renal water clearance. Despite presenting with hypo-osmolar hyponatraemia, antidiuretic hormone (ADH) levels were raised (22.4 pg/mL). Electrocardiogram was normal, and imaging including chest X-ray and CT scans of the head, chest, and abdomen showed no abnormalities. Collectively, these results were compatible with a diagnosis of SIADH.

By the third day, his headache and vertigo had intensified. Despite the absence of neck rigidity or involvement of cranial nerves, inflammation within the CNS was considered. MRI of the brain demonstrated no significant changes affecting the hypothalamus or other areas (figure 2). Analysis of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) showed a mildly elevated opening pressure (17 cmH₂O), increased protein concentration (0.97 g/L), and slight lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL). Cultures and testing of the CSF for bacterial and mycobacterial organisms, as well as HSV, VZV, and cryptococcal antigen, yielded negative results. Cytology of the CSF revealed an absence of malignant cells.

Additional work-up was undertaken to investigate possible underlying systemic disorders. Evaluation for sarcoidosis included lysozyme at 5.1 µg/mL (reference range 5–10) and angiotensin-converting enzyme at 11.4 U/L (reference 7–25), both within normal limits. Screening for hematologic malignancies indicated elevated soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) at 704 U/mL (reference 122–496 U/mL), and testing for human T-cell leukemia virus type 1 antibody was negative.

On the fifth day, he developed a painful ulceration on the scrotum (figure 1D). This, along with the presence of oral aphthae and folliculitis-like skin eruptions, raised suspicion for a systemic variant of BD. The pathergy test was negative. An eye examination ruled out uveitis. He tested positive for HLA-B51.

Differential diagnosis:

Upon admission, the presence of fever, headache, general malaise, and hyponatremia indicated the possibility of a central nervous system (CNS) infection or a systemic inflammatory process. Viral meningoencephalitis, particularly that due to HSV or VZV, was initially presumed. Nevertheless, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) studies were negative for both viral antigens and antibodies, thereby excluding these etiologies. Consideration was also given to bacterial meningitis, but the lack of neutrophilic predominance, only mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, and negative CSF cultures led to its exclusion. Tuberculous meningitis was discounted based on negative acid-fast staining and tuberculosis DNA testing.

Given the neurological symptoms, a primary CNS neoplasm was evaluated. However, brain MRI was unremarkable, and CSF cytology did not detect malignant cells. While the soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) concentration was slightly elevated, this isolated result did not substantiate a diagnosis of CNS lymphoma.

Potential autoimmune conditions were investigated; however, the absence of relevant systemic symptoms and negative screening for autoantibodies argued against this category. Although neurosarcoidosis was included in the differential—considering that early disease may have a normal MRI—subsequent laboratory findings rendered this unlikely. The slight increase in sIL-2R was deemed a non-specific observation.

Ultimately, the diagnosis of definitive Behçet’s disease (BD) was made based on the constellation of fever and fatigue, oral aphthae (two points), genital aphthosis (two points), and a folliculitis-like rash (one point), fulfilling five points according to the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (with a threshold of four or more for definite diagnosis). Notably, genital aphthosis is considered highly specific for BD, providing strong support for this diagnosis. After excluding infectious, neoplastic, and autoimmune origins, the final diagnosis was acute neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), as evidenced by CNS manifestations in the setting of mucocutaneous involvement.

Treatment:

On the first day of hospitalization, clinicians considered an underlying inflammatory disorder, though the precise cause was not determined. Empirical treatment with cefepime commenced after obtaining blood cultures, aiming to cover possible bacterial infection in the absence of a defined infectious site.

By the third day, worsening headache and vertigo indicated possible central nervous system inflammation or infection. Empiric acyclovir therapy was provided but subsequently discontinued when herpes simplex virus and varicella zoster virus encephalitis were excluded by diagnostic testing.

On the eighth day, colchicine therapy began after confirming both systemic Behçet’s disease (BD) and acute neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD). Oral corticosteroid ointment was prescribed for aphthous oral lesions, while genital ulcers and skin lesions were treated with silver sulfadiazine cream. Syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) was handled conservatively via fluid restriction (<800 mL/day), regular serum sodium surveillance, and preventing overly rapid sodium correction; neither hypertonic saline nor vasopressin receptor antagonists were necessary.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following initiation of colchicine, the patient's fever subsided and his appetite improved the next day. Over the subsequent two weeks, symptoms such as vertigo, general malaise, hiccups, and mucocutaneous lesions steadily diminished. By day 16, serum sodium reached normal values at 136 mmol/L, and ADH levels declined to 1.8 pg/mL. Levels of myogenic enzymes also normalized.

Notably, neither systemic corticosteroids nor other immunosuppressive agents were administered during therapy. After a hospitalization lasting one month—during which acute phase recovery was accomplished, hyponatraemia was closely observed and managed, and physical rehabilitation was provided—the patient was discharged. Colchicine treatment was maintained for around four years, with no relapse of BD or neurological manifestations during this period. Once BD had stabilized, biguanide therapy was introduced, resulting in consistently good glycaemic control thereafter.

Discussion:

This report details the concurrent identification of systemic BD and acute neuro-BD, a combination that is infrequently encountered in clinical practice. The case further illustrates that acute neuro-BD may be accompanied by SIADH. The remission of mucocutaneous lesions, neurological deficits, and hyponatraemia following treatment with colchicine alone implies a possible direct relationship among BD, neuro-BD, and SIADH. In individuals with BD who develop fever or headache together with hyponatraemia, acute neuro-BD should be considered as a possible etiology.

This case is significant for the simultaneous diagnosis of both systemic BD and acute neuro-BD, as evidenced by the presence of mucocutaneous involvement and central nervous system symptoms. Typically, severe organ involvement such as CNS lesions becomes apparent approximately four years after BD is first diagnosed, and initial neurological presentations are relatively rare, accounting for fewer than 5% of cases. Only a small subset, about 10%, experience headaches that are directly attributable to neuro-BD. As such, diagnosing neuro-BD at presentation, as in this case, poses substantial difficulty. Acute neuro-BD most often manifests as a self-limiting meningoencephalitis, sometimes accompanied by focal neurological deficits. In this patient, brain MRI did not detect abnormalities at the regions usually affected by BD, including the hypothalamus. Acute neuro-BD can present as aseptic meningitis in the absence of brain parenchymal involvement, and a normal MRI, especially during the early phase, does not rule out neuro-BD. The absence of specific MRI findings complicated the diagnostic assessment. Although HLA-B51 testing was performed, it did not aid in confirming the diagnosis, consistent with international BD guidelines which emphasize clinical presentation over HLA-B51 status. This case underscores the need to consider neuro-BD in patients with mucocutaneous signs who develop vague neurological symptoms.

At the outset of the differential diagnosis, infection was considered the leading cause for central nervous system inflammation, due to the presence of unexplained fever, neurological manifestations, and hyponatremia; nonetheless, no causative infectious agent was identified. Alternative etiologies such as connective tissue disorders and neoplastic processes were also assessed, incorporating sarcoidosis and hematological malignancies into the differential list. As part of a comprehensive initial workup, autoimmune serologies, as well as serum ACE and soluble IL-2 receptor (sIL-2R) levels, were obtained as screening tests. These investigations were conducted selectively to exclude certain conditions, rather than being prompted by a high pre-test clinical probability. No supporting evidence from the clinical presentation, imaging, or cerebrospinal fluid analysis was found for these diseases, and the laboratory findings did not aid in reaching a definitive diagnosis. It is recognized that serum ACE lacks high sensitivity for detecting sarcoidosis, and while sIL-2R may assist in the assessment of malignant lymphoma, minor increases lack specificity and may merely signal an underlying inflammatory process. In this instance, the absence of genital ulcerations during early symptom presentation complicated the suspicion for Behçet’s disease (BD), necessitating a broader differential approach. This case underscores the significance of targeted diagnostic testing, advocating that such assessments should only be pursued in the presence of clinical indicators and their results should be interpreted judiciously.

While drug-induced SIADH has been documented in individuals with BD treated with agents like cyclophosphamide, the connection between acute neuro-BD and SIADH remains largely unclear. Neuro-BD involves inflammation within the central nervous system, which may result in abnormal regulation of ADH secretion. Research has demonstrated that interleukin-6 (IL-6), a proinflammatory cytokine commonly elevated in neuroinflammatory conditions, correlates with increased ADH production. In BD, atypical activation of monocytes drives spontaneous IL-6 release, and heightened IL-6 concentrations have been observed in the cerebrospinal fluid of those experiencing acute neuro-BD. These observations imply that IL-6-driven inflammation could play a role in SIADH development in this scenario. Although IL-6 was not assessed in the serum or CSF for this patient, the significant rise in serum CRP may signify an ongoing IL-6-mediated inflammatory process. SIADH is linked to several disorders, such as CNS inflammation, pulmonary illnesses, malignancies, and some pharmaceuticals; however, the data from this case do not permit a definitive causal relationship to be drawn between SIADH and acute neuro-BD. The timing noted in this report may indicate a general response to CNS inflammation, as opposed to a mechanism unique to the disease.

This case underscores the possible complications arising from hyponatraemia due to SIADH within the context of acute neuro-BD. Previous literature has indicated that persistent hiccups can be a significant clinical indicator for severe hyponatraemia caused by SIADH associated with central nervous system inflammation, such as meningitis. Furthermore, creatine kinase (CK) was assessed during the initial workup for fever, arthralgia, and general fatigue, all symptoms suggestive of inflammatory or metabolic muscle injury, revealing rhabdomyolysis—a well-documented but frequently neglected outcome of severe hyponatraemia that is often not routinely evaluated. While these manifestations are recognized in medical practice, their clinical significance in cases of profound hyponatraemia tends to be underestimated. Healthcare professionals should recognize that seemingly unrelated presentations, including hiccups or rhabdomyolysis, may point to underlying disturbances in electrolyte balance, particularly amid neuroinflammatory disorders.

Corticosteroids represent the primary therapy for acute neuro-BD, with most individuals showing a positive response. Nonetheless, in instances featuring only meningoencephalitis-like symptoms, spontaneous remission has occasionally been documented. In this patient, colchicine as a single agent led to improvement in both mucocutaneous and neurological symptoms, in addition to correcting hyponatraemia. The anti-inflammatory mechanism of colchicine involves the suppression of neutrophil chemotaxis and activation, along with inhibition of cytokine production, specifically IL-1β and IL-6. While colchicine is commonly prescribed to address mucocutaneous features of BD, systematic reviews indicate that it does not benefit those with acute neuro-BD, who usually require immunosuppressive medications for optimal disease control. Published reports note that colchicine has generally served as a supplement to corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants, rather than as monotherapy. Although the possibility of spontaneous improvement in this patient cannot be ruled out, our observations indicate that colchicine might represent a potential therapeutic approach for acute neuro-BD and associated complications like SIADH, especially when corticosteroids are not suitable or cannot be administered.

Conclusion:

We reported an uncommon presentation of Behçet's disease (BD) with simultaneous onset of acute neuro-BD, where the clinical course was further complicated by the development of SIADH. This case underscores the possibility of SIADH emerging as a complication in acute neuro-BD, emphasizing the necessity for clinicians to recognize this association. Treatment with colchicine alone resulted in a prompt resolution of mucocutaneous lesions, neurological deficits, and hyponatraemia, indicating that colchicine could serve as an effective initial therapy for early neuro-BD. For patients with BD presenting with ambiguous central nervous system symptoms such as fever and headache, together with hyponatraemia, clinicians should strongly consider acute neuro-BD in the context of BD complicated by SIADH.
