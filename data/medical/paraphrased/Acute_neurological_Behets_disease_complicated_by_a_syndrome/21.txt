Title: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

Eiichi Kakehi, Yukako Matsumoto, Shigehisa Sakurai, Kazuhiko Kotani

Abstract:

Summary: We describe an uncommon presentation of acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) occurring simultaneously with systemic Behçet’s disease (BD), meeting criteria for definite BD and complicated by syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). A male in his sixth decade developed fever, headache, and mucocutaneous signs such as oral aphthae, folliculitis-like eruptions, genital ulcers, along with hyponatraemia. BD was diagnosed based on his mucocutaneous findings. Brain MRI revealed no abnormalities; cerebrospinal fluid studies demonstrated aseptic meningitis, further supporting acute neuro-BD. Laboratory analyses fulfilled diagnostic criteria for SIADH. Treatment with colchicine alone resolved the patient’s mucocutaneous and neurological symptoms as well as hyponatraemia, without administration of corticosteroids or additional immunosuppressive agents. This report highlights a potential connection between acute neuro-BD and SIADH and emphasizes the necessity of recognizing simultaneous neurological and endocrine features in individuals newly diagnosed with BD.

Background:

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a persistent, multisystemic inflammatory condition defined by repeated occurrences of oral and genital ulcers, uveitis, and skin manifestations. Neurological manifestations, termed neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), affect roughly 10%–20% of individuals with BD and commonly arise several years following the initial BD diagnosis. Acute neuro-BD most often appears as meningoencephalitis along with parenchymal lesions, frequently affecting the brainstem region. While simultaneous presentation is infrequent, it is unusual for BD and neuro-BD to be diagnosed at the same time. In these situations, it is essential for clinicians to evaluate both mucocutaneous signs and neurological findings such as headache, behavioral alterations, psychiatric disturbances, and focal neurological deficits.

The syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) represents a recognized etiology of hyponatremia, typically stemming from central nervous system (CNS) inflammatory processes, neoplasms, or certain pharmacological agents. Owing to the inflammatory processes involved in acute neuro-BD, affected individuals may be predisposed to developing SIADH. Although SIADH has previously been documented in BD patients treated with agents like cyclophosphamide, the incidence of SIADH solely attributable to CNS inflammation from acute neuro-BD, independent of drug exposure, is not well established.

We present an uncommon case involving the simultaneous diagnosis of BD and acute neuro-BD, complicated further by the development of SIADH. This case underscores the need to recognize SIADH as a possible complication of acute neuro-BD, especially in patients who manifest with hyponatremia and ambiguous CNS-related symptoms.

Case presentation:

A man of Asian descent in his 60s arrived at the emergency department after experiencing three days of fever, headache, fatigue, diminished appetite, continuous hiccups, and joint pain localized to the shoulders and ankles. He additionally reported having mouth ulcers.

His past medical history was significant for type 2 diabetes mellitus, though he was not on medication due to a lack of understanding about his illness and insufficient attention to dietary and exercise recommendations. His family history did not reveal cases of BD. Social history revealed occasional alcohol intake, no smoking, no recent travel, and no known contacts with animals or individuals who were ill. He was employed in the metalworking field and denied recent sexual activity.

On assessment, the patient was oriented but showed signs of fatigue. His measured vital signs included a temperature of 38.1°C, blood pressure of 135/73 mm Hg, regular heart rate of 119 beats per minute, respiratory rate of 19 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation of 96% while breathing room air. Physical examination identified oral aphthae and rash resembling folliculitis on his face and trunk (see figure 1A, B, and C). The cardiopulmonary and abdominal exams did not yield any notable findings. Manual muscle testing indicated there was no weakness in either the upper or lower limbs. No peripheral oedema was detected, and his volume status was clinically assessed to be euvolemic. He was admitted to hospital for additional investigation.

Investigations:

Initial laboratory assessments showed significant hyponatremia at 124 mmol/L, along with raised levels of creatine kinase (1349 U/L), aspartate aminotransferase (77 U/L), alanine aminotransferase (64 U/L), C-reactive protein (CRP) at 180 mg/L (reference range <3 mg/L), and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (70 mm/hour; reference <15 mm/hour), while renal function remained within normal limits. The patient was also noted to have hyperglycemia (plasma glucose 14.2 mmol/L) and an increased HbA1c of 8.2%, indicative of suboptimal glucose management. Testing for hepatitis B and C returned negative.

Hormonal investigations revealed thyroid-stimulating hormone within the normal interval (1.11 mIU/L; reference 0.50–5.00 mIU/L), free T4 at 1.25 ng/dL (reference 0.90–1.70 ng/dL), and serum cortisol measuring 42 µg/dL (reference >6 µg/dL). Serum osmolality was decreased at 267 mOsm/kg (reference <270 mOsm/kg) and urine osmolality was elevated at 900 mOsm/kg (reference >300 mOsm/kg), with urinary sodium at 88 mmol/L (reference >20 mmol/L), pointing to defective water clearance. In spite of low serum osmolality and hyponatremia, antidiuretic hormone (ADH) was increased at 22.4 pg/mL. Electrocardiogram showed no abnormalities, and imaging studies including chest X-ray and computed tomography scans of the head, chest, and abdomen were all normal. Collectively, these results were consistent with SIADH.

On the third day, his headache and vertigo intensified. Despite the absence of neck rigidity or signs of cranial nerve dysfunction, central nervous system inflammation was considered. MRI of the brain did not show notable abnormalities in the hypothalamus or other regions (figure 2). Analysis of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) demonstrated a slightly elevated opening pressure (17 cmH₂O), higher protein levels (0.97 g/L), and a mild increase in lymphocytes (11 cells/µL). Cultures and tests of CSF for bacterial and mycobacterial organisms, herpes simplex virus (HSV), varicella zoster virus (VZV), and cryptococcal antigen returned negative results. Cytological examination of the CSF revealed no malignant cells.

Additional testing was undertaken to investigate possible underlying systemic conditions. Evaluation for sarcoidosis showed that lysozyme (5.1 µg/mL; reference range 5–10) and angiotensin-converting enzyme (11.4 U/L; reference range 7–25) were within normal limits. Screening for hematological malignancy yielded elevated soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) (704 U/mL; reference 122–496 U/mL), and human T-cell leukemia virus type 1 antibody was negative.

By day 5, a painful ulcer developed on his scrotum (figure 1D). Along with the presence of oral aphthae and folliculitis-like skin eruptions, these findings pointed toward a systemic presentation of BD. He showed no pinprick hypersensitivity. Ophthalmologic assessment found no evidence of uveitis. The HLA-B51 test was positive.

Differential diagnosis:

Upon initial evaluation, the patient’s presentation of fever, headache, generalized malaise, and hyponatremia indicated the possibility of a central nervous system infection or a systemic inflammatory process. Viral meningoencephalitis, especially from HSV or VZV, was initially considered. Nonetheless, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analyses were negative for viral antigens and antibodies, effectively ruling out these viral etiologies. Bacterial meningitis was also contemplated but excluded due to the absence of neutrophil predominance in the CSF, presence of mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, and negative CSF cultures. Tuberculous meningitis was ruled out because both acid-fast bacilli stains and TB DNA testing yielded negative results.

Given the neurologic findings, a primary central nervous system tumor was another diagnostic consideration. However, brain MRI revealed no abnormalities, and CSF cytology did not show any malignant cells. While the patient demonstrated a mild elevation in sIL-2R, this finding alone was not diagnostic of CNS lymphoma.

Autoimmune etiologies were also investigated but dismissed on the basis of negative screening for autoantibodies and lack of hallmark systemic manifestations. Although neurosarcoidosis was included in the differential, as early MRI scans may appear normal, further laboratory evidence did not support this diagnosis. The slight increase in sIL-2R was interpreted as nonspecific.

A definitive diagnosis of Behçet’s disease (BD) was established by assessing his clinical presentation: he exhibited fever and fatigue in conjunction with oral aphthous ulcers (two points), genital aphthosis (two points), and a folliculitis-like skin eruption (one point). According to the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease, a sum of five points (with four or more required for diagnosis) confirmed BD. Genital aphthosis, notably, is a key diagnostic feature of BD, adding weight to the diagnosis. Once infectious, malignant, and autoimmune causes were discounted, an acute neuro-Behçet's disease diagnosis was made, attributed to his CNS symptoms occurring with concurrent mucocutaneous involvement.

Treatment:

On the first day of hospitalization, clinicians suspected an inflammatory disorder, though the precise cause was not identified. Empirical treatment with cefepime commenced after obtaining blood cultures to address a possible bacterial infection, despite no definite site of infection.

By the third day, the patient experienced increased headache and vertigo, raising concerns for central nervous system infection or inflammation. Empiric acyclovir was prescribed but later discontinued once investigations excluded HSV and VZV encephalitis.

On day eight, colchicine therapy was initiated following the confirmed diagnoses of systemic Behçet’s disease (BD) and acute neuro-BD. Topical corticosteroid ointment was used to treat oral aphthae. Genital ulcers and skin lesions received silver sulfadiazine cream. Syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) was managed with conservative fluid restriction (less than 800 mL per day), close daily surveillance of serum sodium, and prevention of rapid sodium correction. Neither hypertonic saline nor vasopressin receptor antagonists were needed.

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient's fever subsided, and his appetite improved within a day of initiating colchicine therapy. Over the subsequent two weeks, his complaints—including vertigo, persistent malaise, hiccups, and mucocutaneous manifestations—showed progressive improvement. By day 16, his serum sodium concentration had corrected to 136 mmol/L, while ADH levels decreased to 1.8 pg/mL. Myogenic enzyme measurements also normalized.

Notably, systemic corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive agents were not administered during his treatment course. The patient was discharged following a one-month hospitalization, a period in which the acute phase resolved, hyponatremia was meticulously monitored and addressed, and rehabilitation was undertaken to address physical deconditioning. Colchicine was continued for roughly four years post-discharge, during which no recurrence of BD or neurological symptoms was observed. Following stabilization of his BD, biguanide treatment was started, resulting in satisfactory glycemic control thereafter.

Discussion:

This case presented a concurrent diagnosis of systemic Behçet’s disease (BD) and acute neuro-BD, a clinical rarity. It further illustrated that acute neuro-BD can be associated with the development of SIADH. Improvement in mucocutaneous manifestations, neurological symptoms, and hyponatraemia following treatment with colchicine alone raises the possibility of a direct link among BD, neuro-BD, and SIADH. Clinicians should contemplate acute neuro-BD as a potential underlying factor when patients with BD manifest fever or headache together with hyponatraemia.

The case is remarkable due to the simultaneous identification of systemic BD and acute neuro-BD, evidenced by mucocutaneous involvement and central nervous system pathology. Ordinarily, significant organ involvement such as CNS abnormalities arises approximately four years after BD is first diagnosed, and initial presentations with neurological symptoms are rare, observed in fewer than 5% of cases. Only a small proportion of patients, about 10%, experience headaches attributable to neuro-BD. Thus, diagnosing neuro-BD at onset posed considerable difficulties in this situation. Acute neuro-BD typically manifests as meningoencephalitis that resolves on its own, with or without localized neurological impairment. In this patient, MRI of the brain failed to detect any lesions at usual BD sites, including the hypothalamic region. Acute neuro-BD can appear as aseptic meningitis absent any parenchymal brain lesions, and a normal MRI, especially in early disease stages, does not rule out the diagnosis. This absence of imaging abnormalities contributed further to diagnostic complexity. Although HLA-B51 was tested, it did not aid in confirming the diagnosis. This is consistent with international BD diagnostic guidelines, which emphasize clinical features and do not mandate HLA-B51 testing. This report underscores the necessity of considering neuro-BD as a differential diagnosis when individuals with mucocutaneous lesions exhibit ambiguous neurological signs.

At the outset of the differential diagnosis, infection was considered the leading cause of CNS inflammation given the presentation of unexplained fever, neurological manifestations, and hyponatraemia; nonetheless, no causative microorganism was detected. Other potential etiologies of CNS inflammation, such as connective tissue disorders and malignancies—including sarcoidosis and hematologic cancers—were also included in the diagnostic workup. Consequently, a broad initial investigation was conducted, which encompassed the measurement of autoimmune serological markers, serum ACE, and sIL-2R as screening tools. These evaluations were undertaken in a limited, exclusionary fashion rather than being prompted by strong clinical indicators. No evidence from clinical assessment, imaging, or CSF analysis supported any of these alternative diagnoses, and the above investigations did not substantially impact the diagnostic outcome. Serum ACE lacks adequate sensitivity for sarcoidosis, and while sIL-2R can aid in the workup for malignant lymphoma, minor elevations lack specificity and may simply indicate inflammation. In the present case, the initial lack of genital ulcers complicated the consideration of BD, necessitating an extensive differential. This case underlines the necessity of avoiding unfocused testing, stressing that investigations should be reserved for instances with supporting clinical indications and must be interpreted judiciously.

While drug-induced SIADH has been described in individuals with Behçet’s disease (BD) undergoing treatment with agents such as cyclophosphamide, the relationship between acute neuro-BD and SIADH remains inadequately defined. Neuro-BD is marked by inflammation within the central nervous system (CNS), which could play a role in the dysregulation of ADH secretion. Multiple reports indicate that interleukin-6 (IL-6), a proinflammatory cytokine commonly elevated during neuroinflammatory conditions, has been linked to increased ADH production. In BD, abnormal activation of monocytes results in spontaneous IL-6 secretion, and raised IL-6 concentrations have been identified in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) of patients experiencing acute neuro-BD. These observations propose that IL-6-driven inflammation may contribute to the development of SIADH in such cases. Although IL-6 values were not assessed in either serum or CSF for the current patient, the substantially increased serum CRP level may point toward an underlying inflammatory response mediated by IL-6. SIADH has been associated with a variety of disorders, including CNS inflammation, pulmonary conditions, malignancies, and certain drugs; however, the data in this case do not permit confirmation of a direct causal relationship between SIADH and acute neuro-BD. The temporal correlation noted here may indicate a non-specific effect of CNS inflammation rather than a mechanism unique to the disease.

This case further demonstrates the possible complications arising from hyponatraemia due to SIADH in the context of acute neuro-BD. Earlier studies have highlighted that persistent hiccups can be a significant clinical indicator of severe hyponatraemia resulting from SIADH associated with central nervous system inflammation, such as meningitis. Additionally, creatine kinase (CK) was assessed as part of the initial workup for fever, arthralgia, and generalized fatigue—symptoms potentially linked to inflammatory or metabolic injury to muscle—and this assessment revealed rhabdomyolysis, a known but frequently neglected consequence of severe hyponatraemia that is not routinely checked for. Although these clinical findings are established, they are commonly underestimated in practice. It is important for clinicians to recognize that symptoms like hiccups or rhabdomyolysis, which may appear unrelated, can indicate underlying electrolyte imbalances, particularly in patients with neuroinflammatory disorders.

Corticosteroids are typically the primary therapy for acute neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), with most cases showing a positive response. Nonetheless, some reports indicate that spontaneous improvement may occur when presentation is limited to meningoencephalitis-like symptoms. In this instance, administration of colchicine alone led to resolution of both mucocutaneous and neurological manifestations, as well as correction of hyponatraemia. Colchicine's anti-inflammatory properties result from its capacity to inhibit neutrophil migration and activation and to suppress production of cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-6. While colchicine is frequently prescribed for mucocutaneous involvement in BD, evidence from systematic reviews indicates it is generally ineffective for acute neuro-BD, which typically necessitates immunosuppressive therapy for optimal management. Indeed, published cases have utilized colchicine in combination with corticosteroids or additional immunosuppressive agents, rather than as standalone therapy. Although the possibility of spontaneous resolution cannot be dismissed in this situation, these observations raise the possibility that colchicine monotherapy could be beneficial in acute neuro-BD and related complications, including SIADH, especially in patients for whom corticosteroid treatment is inadvisable or not possible.

Conclusion:

We reported an uncommon instance in which Behçet's disease (BD) and acute neuro-BD were diagnosed simultaneously, with SIADH occurring as a complication of the neuro-BD. This case underscores the possibility that SIADH may arise secondary to acute neuro-BD, emphasizing the necessity for clinicians to be aware of this relationship. Administration of colchicine alone resulted in prompt resolution of mucocutaneous lesions, neurological symptoms, and hyponatraemia, indicating colchicine may serve as an effective early treatment for neuro-BD. In individuals with BD who exhibit non-specific central nervous system manifestations such as fever and headache together with hyponatraemia, clinicians should remain vigilant for acute neuro-BD when SIADH is present as a complicating factor.
