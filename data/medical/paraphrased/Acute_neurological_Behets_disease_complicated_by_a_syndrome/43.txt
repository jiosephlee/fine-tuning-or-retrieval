Title: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

Eiichi Kakehi, Yukako Matsumoto, Shigehisa Sakurai, Kazuhiko Kotani

Abstract:

Summary: We describe an uncommon presentation of acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) occurring simultaneously with systemic Behçet’s disease (BD), meeting criteria for definite BD, complicated by syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). The patient, a male in his 60s, exhibited fever, headache, and mucocutaneous features, such as oral aphthae, folliculitis-like eruptions, genital ulcerations, and hyponatraemia. BD diagnosis was established based on the mucocutaneous symptoms. Magnetic resonance imaging of the brain revealed no significant findings, while cerebrospinal fluid examination showed aseptic meningitis, providing further evidence for acute neuro-BD. Laboratory evaluations satisfied diagnostic criteria for SIADH. Treatment with colchicine alone led to resolution of mucocutaneous and neurological symptoms as well as normalization of serum sodium, without the use of corticosteroids or additional immunosuppressive agents. This report indicates a potential link between acute neuro-BD and SIADH and highlights the need to recognize concomitant neurological and endocrine complications in individuals newly diagnosed with BD.

Background:

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a persistent, multisystem inflammatory condition marked by recurrent oral and genital ulceration, uveitis, and dermatological manifestations. Neurological involvement, identified as neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), develops in around 10%–20% of cases, typically manifesting several years following the primary BD diagnosis. Acute neuro-BD generally presents with meningoencephalitis and lesions within the brain parenchyma, with a predilection for the brainstem. While it is more usual for neuro-BD to occur as a later complication, it is uncommon for both BD and neuro-BD to be detected at the same time. In these situations, healthcare providers must evaluate both mucocutaneous and neurological findings, such as headaches, personality or behavioural changes, psychiatric manifestations, and localized neurological deficits.

Syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) is a recognized etiology of hyponatraemia and frequently arises due to central nervous system (CNS) inflammation, neoplasia, or exposure to specific medications. Owing to the inflammatory features of acute neuro-BD, affected individuals may be susceptible to SIADH development. While cases of SIADH have been documented in BD patients treated with agents like cyclophosphamide, its emergence solely due to CNS inflammation from acute neuro-BD, without medication-related precipitating factors, is not well established.

Here, we describe an unusual presentation in which BD and acute neuro-BD were diagnosed simultaneously, further complicated by the onset of SIADH. This report underscores the necessity of recognizing SIADH as a possible complication of acute neuro-BD, especially in those who exhibit unspecific neurological symptoms and hyponatraemia.

Case presentation:

A male of Asian descent in his 60s arrived at the emergency department, experiencing fever, headache, general fatigue, reduced appetite, ongoing hiccups, and joint pain localized to the shoulders and ankles over the past three days. He additionally noted the presence of oral aphthous ulcers.

His medical background was notable for type 2 diabetes mellitus, which remained untreated due to his limited understanding of the diagnosis and nonadherence to recommended dietary and exercise regimens. His family history showed no evidence of BD, and there were no affected relatives. He reported occasional alcohol consumption, denied smoking, recent travel, contact with animals, or exposure to ill persons. He was employed in the metalworking sector and had not had recent sexual encounters.

Upon assessment, the patient was conscious but showed signs of fatigue. His vital parameters were as follows: temperature measured at 38.1°C, blood pressure recorded at 135/73 mm Hg, regular heart rate of 119 bpm, respiratory rate of 19 per minute, and oxygen saturation at 96% breathing ambient air. Inspection revealed oral aphthae and rashes resembling folliculitis distributed across the face and trunk (figure 1A, B, and C). Examination of the heart, lungs, and abdomen did not reveal any abnormalities. Muscle strength testing demonstrated intact muscle power in both upper and lower limbs. There was an absence of peripheral edema, with clinical assessment indicating euvolemia. The patient was hospitalized for further investigation.

Investigations:

Initial laboratory assessments demonstrated pronounced hyponatraemia (124 mmol/L), increased creatine kinase (1349 U/L), aspartate aminotransferase (77 U/L), and alanine aminotransferase (64 U/L), along with a significantly raised C-reactive protein (180 mg/L; reference range <3 mg/L) and an elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate (70 mm/hour; reference <15 mm/hour), while renal function remained within normal limits. The patient was also noted to have hyperglycaemia (plasma glucose 14.2 mmol/L) and a raised HbA1c of 8.2%, indicating suboptimal glucose control. Hepatitis B and C serologies were negative.

Endocrine workup showed thyroid-stimulating hormone (1.11 mIU/L; reference range 0.50–5.00 mIU/L), free T4 (1.25 ng/dL; reference range 0.90–1.70 ng/dL), and serum cortisol (42 µg/dL; reference >6 µg/dL) all within normal parameters. Serum osmolality was decreased (267 mOsm/kg; reference <270 mOsm/kg), while urine osmolality was markedly increased (900 mOsm/kg; reference >300 mOsm/kg) together with elevated urine sodium (88 mmol/L; reference >20 mmol/L), suggesting reduced water elimination. In the presence of hypo-osmolar hyponatraemia, antidiuretic hormone (ADH) levels were high (22.4 pg/mL). Electrocardiogram results were normal, and imaging including chest X-ray and CT scans of the head, chest, and abdomen revealed no abnormalities. These combined findings were indicative of SIADH.

By the third day, his headache and vertigo had intensified. Although neck rigidity and cranial nerve deficits were absent, central nervous system inflammation was considered. Brain MRI demonstrated no notable lesions involving the hypothalamus or other regions (figure 2). Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) evaluation showed a mildly elevated opening pressure of 17 cmH₂O, increased protein at 0.97 g/L, and a slight lymphocytic pleocytosis of 11 cells/µL. CSF cultures and testing for bacterial and mycobacterial pathogens, as well as HSV, VZV, and cryptococcal antigen, yielded negative results. Cytological analysis of the CSF indicated no evidence of malignant cells.

Additional assessments were carried out to investigate potential systemic disorders. Workup for sarcoidosis included lysozyme (5.1 µg/mL; reference 5–10) and angiotensin-converting enzyme (11.4 U/L; reference 7–25), both within normal limits. Hematological malignancy screening detected elevated soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) at 704 U/mL (reference 122–496 U/mL), while the human T-cell leukaemia virus type 1 antibody was negative.

On the fifth day, he developed a painful ulcer on the scrotum (figure 1D). Coupled with oral aphthous ulcers and folliculitis-like skin eruptions, these signs indicated systemic Behçet's Disease (BD). There was no pathergy reaction on pinprick testing. Eye examination did not show uveitis. HLA-B51 was found to be positive.

Differential diagnosis:

Upon presentation, the coexistence of fever, headache, general malaise, and hyponatremia indicated the possibility of a central nervous system infection or a systemic inflammatory condition. Initial suspicion centered on viral meningoencephalitis, notably due to HSV or VZV. Nonetheless, cerebrospinal fluid analysis returned negative results for viral antigens and antibodies, thereby ruling out these etiologies. Bacterial meningitis was contemplated but discounted, as there was no evidence of neutrophilic predominance, only slight lymphocytic pleocytosis, and CSF cultures were negative. Tuberculous meningitis was also excluded, with both acid-fast staining and TB DNA assays yielding negative results.

Given the neurologic manifestations, a primary central nervous system malignancy was part of the differential. Nevertheless, no abnormalities were identified on brain MRI, and CSF cytological analysis did not demonstrate malignant cells. Although there was a slight increase in sIL-2R concentration, this was not sufficient to support CNS lymphoma as the diagnosis.

The possibility of autoimmune conditions was investigated, but these were dismissed due to the lack of typical systemic symptoms and negative autoantibody screenings. Neurosarcoidosis remained a consideration, since early MRI may not display abnormalities, yet subsequent laboratory assessments diminished the likelihood of this disorder. The mildly raised sIL-2R was interpreted as a non-specific finding.

A conclusive diagnosis of Behçet’s disease (BD) was ultimately made, based on clinical signs including fever, fatigue, oral aphthae (awarding two points), genital aphthosis (two points), and a folliculitis-like rash (one point), giving the patient five points in total according to the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (with a score of ≥4 being diagnostic). Notably, genital aphthosis is recognized as a specific sign of BD, which, along with the other manifestations, strongly indicated this diagnosis. Having systematically ruled out infectious, neoplastic, and other autoimmune etiologies, an acute neuro-Behçet’s diagnosis was established, based on central nervous system involvement alongside mucocutaneous findings.

Treatment:

On the first day of hospitalization, clinicians considered an inflammatory disorder, though the underlying cause remained unidentified. Empiric treatment with cefepime was started after obtaining blood cultures, aiming to cover possible bacterial pathogens despite no clear site of infection.

By the third day, the patient experienced increased headache and vertigo, raising concerns for central nervous system inflammation or infection. Acyclovir was initiated empirically but was later discontinued when laboratory testing excluded HSV and VZV encephalitis.

On day eight, colchicine therapy commenced after systemic BD and acute neuro-BD were diagnosed. Topical corticosteroid ointment was applied to address oral aphthae. Genital and skin lesions received treatment with silver sulfadiazine cream. SIADH was addressed with fluid restriction (under 800 mL/day), frequent monitoring of serum sodium levels, and preventing rapid sodium correction. There was no need for hypertonic saline or vasopressin receptor antagonists.

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient’s fever subsided, and his appetite increased the day after beginning colchicine therapy. Over the next two weeks, his clinical manifestations—including vertigo, overall malaise, hiccups, and mucocutaneous eruptions—gradually resolved. On day 16, his serum sodium concentration was restored to 136 mmol/L, and antidiuretic hormone (ADH) levels decreased to 1.8 pg/mL. Myogenic enzyme values also normalized.

Notably, systemic corticosteroids and other immunosuppressive agents were not administered throughout the treatment period. He was discharged following a hospital stay of one month, during which resolution of the acute phase occurred, hyponatraemia was diligently monitored and treated, and rehabilitation addressed the related loss of physical capability. Colchicine was maintained for roughly four years, during which neither Behçet’s disease (BD) nor neurologic signs recurred. After BD remained stable, biguanide medication was started, leading to sustained satisfactory glycaemic management.

Discussion:

This case report describes a patient who was concurrently diagnosed with both systemic Behçet’s disease (BD) and acute neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), a clinical presentation that is infrequently seen. Additionally, this case illustrates that acute neuro-BD can present with concomitant syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). Resolution of the patient’s mucocutaneous lesions, neurological symptoms, and hyponatraemia following treatment with colchicine monotherapy points to a potential direct link among BD, neuro-BD, and SIADH. Consequently, when patients with BD present with fever or headache and accompanying hyponatraemia, clinicians should recognize acute neuro-BD as a possible diagnosis.

The case is significant in that both systemic BD and acute neuro-BD were identified simultaneously, with supporting evidence including mucocutaneous involvement and central nervous system symptoms. Ordinarily, major organ complications, such as CNS involvement, develop on average four years after BD is first diagnosed, and it is unusual—seen in fewer than 5% of cases—for neurological symptoms to appear at disease onset. Moreover, only a small subset—about 10%—of patients develop headaches attributable to neuro-BD itself. As such, initially identifying neuro-BD in this patient posed a substantial challenge. The acute form of neuro-BD most often manifests as a transient meningoencephalitis, sometimes associated with focal neurological deficits. In this case, brain MRI did not demonstrate abnormalities in characteristic BD locations, including the hypothalamic area. Acute neuro-BD can present as aseptic meningitis in the absence of parenchymal brain lesions, and a normal MRI—especially in the initial disease stages—does not eliminate neuro-BD from consideration. The absence of imaging abnormalities further complicated making the diagnosis. Although the patient was tested for HLA-B51, the result did not aid in confirming the diagnosis, which is consistent with international BD guidelines that focus on clinical criteria rather than HLA-B51 status. Overall, this report underscores the necessity of considering neuro-BD when individuals with mucocutaneous manifestations develop vague neurological complaints.

At the outset of the differential diagnosis, infection was considered the most likely etiology for CNS inflammation due to the presentation of unexplained fever, neurological manifestations, and hyponatraemia; nonetheless, no definitive infectious agent was detected. Alternative origins of CNS inflammation, such as connective tissue disorders and malignancies—including sarcoidosis and hematologic cancers—were also evaluated. Consequently, an extensive initial workup included screening assays for autoimmune antibodies, serum ACE, and sIL-2R. These investigations were selectively ordered to exclude potential causes, rather than driven by strong clinical suspicion. Ultimately, no supporting clinical, radiological, or CSF evidence for these conditions was identified, and the laboratory results obtained did not significantly aid in reaching the definitive diagnosis. It should be noted that ACE exhibits limited sensitivity for detecting sarcoidosis, and while sIL-2R levels may assist in assessing for malignant lymphoma, slight increases are nonspecific and may indicate general inflammatory activity. In this scenario, the lack of genital ulcers in the early disease course rendered the diagnosis of BD equivocal, necessitating a wide-ranging differential. This approach underlines the significance of judicious use of diagnostic tests, reinforcing that investigations should be guided by relevant clinical findings and interpreted carefully.

While SIADH resulting from medications, such as cyclophosphamide, has been documented in individuals with BD, the link between acute neuro-BD and SIADH is still not well elucidated. Neuro-BD involves inflammatory processes within the central nervous system, which could potentially impact ADH regulation. Research has indicated that interleukin-6 (IL-6), a proinflammatory cytokine commonly elevated in neuroinflammatory conditions, correlates with increased ADH secretion. In BD, dysregulated monocyte activity promotes spontaneous production of IL-6, and heightened IL-6 concentrations have been observed in the CSF during acute neuro-BD episodes. These observations imply that inflammation driven by IL-6 may be a factor in the development of SIADH in this context. Although IL-6 was not assessed in the serum or CSF in the current case, significantly raised serum CRP might serve as a marker for an IL-6–driven inflammatory reaction. SIADH can arise in association with a wide range of conditions, including CNS inflammation, lung pathology, neoplasms, and certain pharmacologic agents; nevertheless, the data presented here do not confirm a direct causative connection between SIADH and acute neuro-BD. The chronological relationship noted may signify a general response to CNS inflammation, rather than one specific to neuro-BD pathology.

This case further demonstrates the possible adverse outcomes associated with hyponatraemia resulting from SIADH in acute neuro-BD. Earlier reports have noted that persistent hiccups can be a significant clinical indicator of severe hyponatraemia due to SIADH secondary to central nervous system inflammation, such as meningitis. Additionally, we included creatine kinase testing in our initial workup for fever, arthralgia, and general fatigue—symptoms that may stem from inflammatory or metabolic muscle injury—and discovered rhabdomyolysis, a recognized but frequently overlooked complication of severe hyponatraemia that is not routinely screened for. Although these features are established, they remain frequently underrecognized consequences of marked hyponatraemia. It is important for clinicians to recognize that seemingly unrelated findings, like hiccups or rhabdomyolysis, can be indicative of underlying electrolyte imbalances, especially within the context of neuroinflammatory disorders.

Corticosteroids represent the standard initial therapy for acute neuro-BD, with the majority of patients showing a positive response. Nevertheless, there have been reports of spontaneous improvement in patients whose symptoms are limited to a meningoencephalitis-like presentation. In the case discussed, colchicine used alone led to improvement in both mucocutaneous and neurological symptoms, as well as in hyponatraemia. Colchicine possesses anti-inflammatory properties through the inhibition of neutrophil movement and activation, as well as the suppression of cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-6. While colchicine is commonly prescribed for mucocutaneous symptoms of BD, evidence from systematic reviews indicates that it is not effective for managing acute neuro-BD, and that immunosuppressive drugs are typically necessary to achieve sufficient disease control. In published cases, colchicine has most often been utilized in conjunction with corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive agents, rather than by itself. Even though spontaneous remission cannot be ruled out in the present instance, the observations imply that colchicine might be a valuable therapeutic alternative for acute neuro-BD and associated conditions like SIADH, especially in situations where corticosteroid use is not feasible or is contraindicated.

Conclusion:

We reported an uncommon instance where both Behçet's disease (BD) and acute neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) were identified simultaneously, with SIADH serving as a complicating factor of the neuro-BD. This case underscores that SIADH can arise secondary to acute neuro-BD, emphasizing the need for clinicians to recognize this possible relationship. Treatment with colchicine alone resulted in prompt resolution of mucocutaneous signs, neurological symptoms, and hyponatraemia, indicating that colchicine may be a viable early intervention for neuro-BD. Clinicians should be vigilant for acute neuro-BD in BD patients who develop non-specific central nervous system symptoms, such as fever and headache, especially when hyponatraemia is present due to SIADH.
