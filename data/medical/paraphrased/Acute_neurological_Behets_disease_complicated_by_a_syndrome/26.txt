Title: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

Eiichi Kakehi, Yukako Matsumoto, Shigehisa Sakurai, Kazuhiko Kotani

Abstract:

Summary: We describe an uncommon case in which acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) was identified simultaneously with systemic Behçet’s disease (BD), classified as definite BD, and was complicated by the occurrence of syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). The patient, a male in his 60s, exhibited symptoms such as fever, headache, as well as mucocutaneous findings including oral aphthae, folliculitis-like eruptions, genital ulcerations, and hyponatraemia. The diagnosis of BD was made based on these mucocutaneous features. Magnetic resonance imaging of the brain did not reveal any abnormalities, while cerebrospinal fluid evaluation indicated aseptic meningitis, which corroborated the diagnosis of acute neuro-BD. Diagnostic criteria for SIADH were fulfilled based on laboratory findings. Treatment with colchicine alone resulted in resolution of both mucocutaneous and neurological symptoms, as well as correction of hyponatraemia, without administration of corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive therapy. This report highlights a potential link between acute neuro-BD and SIADH, emphasizing the need to consider both neurological and endocrine complications when evaluating patients with newly established BD.

Background:

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a persistent, multisystem inflammatory condition marked by recurrent oral and genital ulcerations, uveitis, and various skin manifestations. Neurological involvement, termed neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), is observed in roughly 10%–20% of cases and most commonly develops years following the initial diagnosis. The acute form of neuro-BD frequently manifests as meningoencephalitis with parenchymal brain lesions, predominantly affecting the brainstem. Simultaneous diagnosis of BD and neuro-BD is uncommon, as delayed onset tends to be the norm. When they do occur together, it is important to evaluate for both mucocutaneous signs and neurological findings such as headaches, alterations in behavior, psychiatric disturbances, or localized neurological deficits.

Syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) is a recognized etiology of hyponatremia, often arising due to central nervous system (CNS) inflammation, malignancy, or certain pharmacologic agents. The inflammatory processes inherent to acute neuro-BD may predispose patients to developing SIADH. While SIADH has been described in association with BD patients receiving therapies such as cyclophosphamide, cases where SIADH is provoked solely by CNS inflammation in the context of acute neuro-BD—without medication-related precipitating factors—remain insufficiently defined.

Herein, we present an unusual case involving simultaneous diagnoses of BD and acute neuro-BD, complicated by SIADH. This report underscores the need to recognize SIADH as a potential complication of acute neuro-BD, especially in individuals with vague CNS manifestations and documented hyponatremia.

Case presentation:

A man of Asian descent in his 60s arrived at the emergency department exhibiting a three-day duration of fever, headache, general fatigue, decreased appetite, continuous hiccups, and joint pain in the shoulders and ankles. He also mentioned having oral aphthae.

His past medical history revealed type 2 diabetes mellitus, which remained untreated owing to his limited understanding of the disease and a lack of appropriate nutritional or exercise practices. There was no relevant family history, as no relatives had BD. He occasionally drank alcohol, was not a smoker, had no recent travel history, and had not encountered animals or people who were ill. His occupation was in the metalworking sector and he had not participated in recent sexual activity.

Upon assessment, the patient was awake but showed signs of tiredness. Recorded vital parameters included a temperature of 38.1°C, blood pressure measuring 135/73 mm Hg, a regular heart rate of 119 bpm, respiratory rate at 19 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation of 96% while breathing ambient air. Physical assessment identified aphthous ulcers in the mouth and folliculitis-like eruptions present on his facial and truncal regions (see figure 1A, B, and C). Findings from cardiovascular, pulmonary, and abdominal examinations were normal. Muscle strength evaluated manually in both upper and lower limbs revealed no deficits. No peripheral edema was detected, and his fluid balance was judged clinically as euvolemic. He was subsequently admitted for additional diagnostic investigation.

Investigations:

Initial laboratory evaluation demonstrated significant hyponatremia (124 mmol/L), elevated levels of creatine kinase (1349 U/L), aspartate aminotransferase (77 U/L), alanine aminotransferase (64 U/L), C-reactive protein (CRP) (180 mg/L; reference, <3 mg/L), and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (70 mm/hour; reference, <15 mm/hour), with renal function remaining within normal parameters. Additionally, hyperglycemia was observed (plasma glucose 14.2 mmol/L), alongside a raised HbA1c of 8.2%, reflecting suboptimal glycemic management. Hepatitis B and C serologic markers tested negative.

Endocrine assessment indicated thyroid-stimulating hormone within the reference range (1.11 mIU/L; reference, 0.50–5.00 mIU/L), normal free T4 (1.25 ng/dL; reference, 0.90–1.70 ng/dL), and elevated serum cortisol (42 µg/dL; reference, >6 µg/dL). Serum osmolality was reduced (267 mOsm/kg; reference, <270 mOsm/kg), while urine osmolality was markedly raised (900 mOsm/kg; reference, >300 mOsm/kg) and urinary sodium was increased (88 mmol/L; reference, >20 mmol/L), which was indicative of compromised water clearance. Despite the presence of hypo-osmolar hyponatremia, antidiuretic hormone (ADH) level was found to be elevated (22.4 pg/mL). Electrocardiogram (ECG) appeared normal, and imaging including chest X-ray and CT scans of the head, chest, and abdomen did not reveal any abnormalities. Collectively, these results were consistent with the diagnosis of SIADH.

On the third day, his headache and vertigo intensified. Despite the absence of neck rigidity or signs of cranial nerve dysfunction, central nervous system inflammation was considered. MRI imaging of the brain did not identify notable abnormalities within the hypothalamus or elsewhere (figure 2). Analysis of cerebrospinal fluid showed a slightly elevated opening pressure (17 cmH₂O), increased protein content (0.97 g/L), and mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL). Microbiological tests of the CSF—including cultures and assays for bacterial and mycobacterial pathogens, HSV, VZV, and cryptococcal antigen—were negative. Cytological examination of the CSF revealed no evidence of malignancy.

Additional evaluations were undertaken to detect any underlying systemic conditions. For sarcoidosis, lysozyme (5.1 µg/mL; reference range 5–10) and angiotensin-converting enzyme (11.4 U/L; reference range 7–25) levels were within normal limits. Investigations for hematologic malignancies showed a raised soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) (704 U/mL; reference 122–496 U/mL), and tests for human T-cell leukemia virus type 1 antibody were negative.

By day 5, he developed a painful ulcer on the scrotum (figure 1D). Along with oral aphthous ulcers and rashes resembling folliculitis, these manifestations indicated a systemic type of BD. The patient showed no pinprick hypersensitivity. Eye examination did not reveal uveitis. HLA-B51 tested positive.

Differential diagnosis:

On admission, the presence of fever, headache, malaise, and hyponatremia raised suspicion for a central nervous system infection or systemic inflammatory condition. Viral meningoencephalitis, especially due to HSV or VZV, was initially presumed; nevertheless, cerebrospinal fluid analysis was negative for viral antigens and antibodies, thereby excluding these etiologies. Bacterial meningitis was also entertained, yet this diagnosis was dismissed because there was no neutrophilic predominance, only a mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, and CSF cultures yielded no bacterial growth. Tuberculous meningitis was considered but was ruled out following negative acid-fast staining and tuberculosis DNA assays.

Consideration was given to primary CNS malignancy due to the neurological symptoms; however, cranial MRI demonstrated no abnormalities, and CSF cytology failed to show any malignant cells. The soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) level was mildly elevated, but this alone did not suffice for diagnosing CNS lymphoma.

Autoimmune conditions were also assessed and subsequently excluded based on the absence of hallmark systemic manifestations and negative autoantibody screening. Neurosarcoidosis was included in the differential, recognizing that MRI findings may be unremarkable in early stages, but later laboratory evaluation rendered this diagnosis improbable. The slight elevation of sIL-2R was considered a non-specific change.

Ultimately, the patient was diagnosed with definite Behçet’s disease, in light of symptoms such as fever, fatigue, two points for oral aphthae, two points for genital aphthosis, and one point for a folliculitis-like eruption—reaching five points per the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (where a score of four or above confirms the diagnosis). Genital aphthosis, in particular, is a specific indicator of Behçet’s disease, lending strong diagnostic support. After ruling out infectious, malignant, and autoimmune etiologies, a final diagnosis of acute neuro-Behçet's disease was made, with central nervous system involvement appearing alongside mucocutaneous manifestations.

Treatment:

On the first day of hospitalization, clinicians suspected the presence of an underlying inflammatory disorder, though the specific cause could not be determined. Blood cultures were obtained, and the patient was empirically treated with cefepime to address a possible bacterial infection, despite no definitive site of infection being identified.

By the third day, the patient reported worsening headache and vertigo, raising concerns for central nervous system infection or inflammation. Empirical acyclovir therapy was initiated, but subsequently discontinued when laboratory studies excluded both HSV and VZV encephalitis.

On the eighth day, colchicine therapy commenced after a diagnosis of systemic Behçet’s disease (BD) and acute neuro-BD was established. Topical corticosteroid ointment was used to manage oral aphthae. Silver sulfadiazine cream was applied to genital and skin lesions. SIADH was treated non-pharmacologically with a fluid restriction of less than 800 mL per day, regular monitoring of serum sodium, and prevention of overly rapid sodium correction. There was no indication for hypertonic saline or vasopressin receptor antagonist administration.

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient’s fever subsided and his appetite increased one day following the initiation of colchicine. Over the subsequent two weeks, his symptoms, including vertigo, general malaise, hiccups, and mucocutaneous lesions, progressively lessened. By day 16, serum sodium returned to normal at 136 mmol/L, and ADH levels dropped to 1.8 pg/mL. Levels of myogenic enzymes also normalized.

Notably, the treatment course did not involve systemic corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive agents. After a hospitalization lasting one month—during which he recovered from the acute phase, had careful management and monitoring of hyponatremia, and received rehabilitation for diminished physical functioning—the patient was discharged. Colchicine therapy was continued for about 4 years, during which neither BD nor neurological symptoms recurred. Once BD was under control, biguanide therapy was started, resulting in sustained good glycemic control.

Discussion:

In this report, both systemic BD and acute neuro-BD were identified concurrently—a combination that is rarely observed clinically. It further illustrates that acute neuro-BD can be associated with SIADH. The fact that colchicine monotherapy led to the improvement of mucocutaneous manifestations, neurological symptoms, and hyponatraemia may indicate a direct link among BD, neuro-BD, and SIADH. Clinicians should keep acute neuro-BD in the differential diagnosis when patients with BD present with fever or headache accompanied by hyponatraemia.

What distinguishes this case is the simultaneous detection of systemic BD and acute neuro-BD, supported by both mucocutaneous findings and involvement of the central nervous system. Generally, significant organ complications like CNS lesions tend to appear about 4 years following initial BD diagnosis, and neurological features at first presentation are rare, occurring in less than 5% of individuals. Furthermore, only about 10% of patients report headaches specifically attributable to neuro-BD, making early recognition of neuro-BD in this patient particularly difficult. The typical presentation of acute neuro-BD is a transient meningoencephalitis, which may or may not include focal neurological signs. In this case, brain MRI failed to show distinct changes at conventional BD sites, such as the hypothalamus. Acute neuro-BD can present as aseptic meningitis in the absence of parenchymal lesions, and a normal brain MRI—especially in early disease—does not rule out the condition. The lack of MRI findings complicated the assessment. HLA-B51 testing did not help in confirming the diagnosis, which is consistent with international criteria for BD that emphasize clinical assessment over genetic testing. This case underscores the need to suspect neuro-BD in patients showing mucocutaneous signs together with vague neurological complaints.

At the outset of our differential diagnosis, we prioritized infectious etiologies for the CNS inflammation, given the coexistence of unexplained fever, neurological manifestations, and hyponatraemia, though no causative pathogen was detected. We expanded our considerations to encompass other sources of CNS inflammation, such as connective tissue disorders and neoplastic processes, including sarcoidosis and hematological cancers. Consequently, our initial broad assessment included screening for autoimmune markers, serum ACE, and sIL-2R. These investigations were conducted selectively, guided by an exclusionary strategy rather than significant clinical suspicion. Ultimately, clinical, imaging, and CSF findings failed to support these conditions, and the screening did not substantially aid the diagnostic outcome. It is noteworthy that ACE lacks sensitivity for sarcoidosis, and while sIL-2R can assist in evaluating lymphoma, slight increases are nonspecific and may simply indicate inflammation. The lack of genital ulcers during the early phase further obscured a diagnosis of BD, necessitating a wider differential. This experience underscores the necessity for targeted testing and cautions clinicians to reserve such investigations for cases backed by relevant clinical evidence, interpreting results judiciously.

While SIADH related to drug therapy has been documented in patients with BD treated with agents like cyclophosphamide, the relationship between SIADH and acute neuro-BD is not well elucidated. Neuro-BD involves inflammation of the central nervous system, which could potentially lead to inappropriate ADH release. Research has demonstrated that interleukin-6 (IL-6), a proinflammatory cytokine commonly elevated in neuroinflammatory conditions, correlates with increased secretion of ADH. In BD, atypical activation of monocytes results in the spontaneous production of IL-6, and raised IL-6 levels have been identified in the cerebrospinal fluid of patients experiencing acute neuro-BD. These observations raise the possibility that inflammation mediated by IL-6 may play a role in the development of SIADH in this context. In the current case, although serum or CSF IL-6 concentrations were not measured, the notably high serum CRP value could indicate an IL-6-driven inflammatory process. SIADH is associated with several clinical scenarios, such as CNS inflammation, pulmonary diseases, malignancy, and various medications; however, the evidence from this case does not allow for confirmation of a direct causative association between SIADH and acute neuro-BD. The chronology observed might simply indicate a general response to CNS inflammation, rather than a mechanism unique to this disease.

This case further demonstrates the possible complications associated with hyponatraemia resulting from SIADH in the context of acute neuro-BD. Prior literature has indicated that persistent hiccups can act as an important indicator of severe hyponatraemia due to SIADH linked to central nervous system inflammation, such as meningitis. Moreover, as part of our initial workup for fever, arthralgia, and generalized fatigue—which can suggest inflammatory or metabolic muscle injury—we assessed CK levels, leading to the identification of rhabdomyolysis. Although rhabdomyolysis is a recognized consequence of profound hyponatraemia, it often goes unrecognized and is not routinely considered. These findings represent well-documented but frequently underrecognized clinical consequences of significant hyponatraemia. Physicians should recognize that symptoms seemingly unrelated, such as hiccups or rhabdomyolysis, may signal underlying electrolyte abnormalities, especially in settings of neuroinflammation.

Corticosteroids represent the primary therapy for acute neuro-BD, with the majority of patients showing a good clinical response. Nevertheless, spontaneous recovery has been documented in instances presenting exclusively with meningoencephalitis-like manifestations. In this particular case, colchicine used as a single agent was associated with improvement in mucocutaneous and neurological manifestations, as well as correction of hyponatraemia. Colchicine mediates anti-inflammatory actions through inhibition of neutrophil chemotaxis and activation, and through the downregulation of cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-6. Despite its common use for mucocutaneous involvement in BD, meta-analyses have indicated that colchicine lacks efficacy for acute neuro-BD, necessitating the use of immunosuppressive drugs to achieve sufficient control. Indeed, the literature describes colchicine mainly as an add-on to corticosteroids or additional immunosuppressants rather than a single-agent regimen. Although the possibility of spontaneous resolution cannot be ruled out in this patient, our observations indicate that colchicine could represent a potential treatment choice for acute neuro-BD and associated conditions, like SIADH, particularly when corticosteroids are inappropriate or cannot be administered.

Conclusion:

We reported an uncommon case involving both Behçet's disease (BD) and acute neuro-Behçet's disease (neuro-BD), diagnosed simultaneously, where SIADH presented as a complication of neuro-BD. This case illustrates that SIADH can arise in the context of acute neuro-BD, emphasizing the need for healthcare providers to recognize this possible association. Administration of colchicine alone resulted in swift resolution of mucocutaneous symptoms, neurological signs, and hyponatraemia, indicating that colchicine may serve as an effective treatment option during the early stages of neuro-BD. When patients with BD experience non-specific central nervous system symptoms, such as fever and headache, alongside hyponatraemia, clinicians should have a heightened index of suspicion for acute neuro-BD in cases where SIADH is also present.
