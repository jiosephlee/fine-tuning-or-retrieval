Title: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

Eiichi Kakehi, Yukako Matsumoto, Shigehisa Sakurai, Kazuhiko Kotani

Abstract:

Summary: We describe an unusual instance of acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) identified simultaneously with systemic Behçet’s disease (BD), fulfilling the criteria for definite BD, and further complicated by syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). A male patient in his 60s presented with symptoms including fever, headache, and a range of mucocutaneous findings such as oral aphthae, folliculitis-like eruptions, genital ulcers, as well as hyponatraemia. The mucocutaneous presentations prompted a diagnosis of BD. Magnetic resonance imaging of the brain was unremarkable; however, analysis of cerebrospinal fluid indicated aseptic meningitis, thereby corroborating a diagnosis of acute neuro-BD. Further laboratory investigation satisfied criteria for SIADH. Treatment with colchicine alone resulted in the resolution of mucocutaneous, neurological, and electrolyte disturbances, without the administration of corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive therapies. This case highlights a potential relationship between acute neuro-BD and SIADH, and emphasises the clinical significance of identifying simultaneous neurological and endocrine complications in individuals with recent-onset BD.

Background:

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a persistent, systemic inflammatory condition distinguished by recurrent episodes of oral and genital ulceration, uveitis, and various cutaneous manifestations. Neurological complications, classified as neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), arise in about 10%–20% of cases and most often manifest several years following the primary diagnosis of BD. Acute neuro-BD is generally associated with presentations such as meningoencephalitis and inflammatory parenchymal lesions within the brain, with the brainstem being a frequent site of involvement. Although a delayed presentation is typical, simultaneous identification of both BD and neuro-BD is infrequent. In such scenarios, it is crucial for clinicians to thoroughly evaluate both the mucocutaneous signs and the neurological profile, including symptoms like headaches, neuropsychiatric disturbances, behavioral changes, and localized neurological deficits.

The syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) is a recognized etiology of hyponatraemia and is frequently triggered by central nervous system (CNS) inflammatory processes, neoplastic diseases, or the use of certain pharmacological agents. Due to the underlying inflammatory mechanisms in acute neuro-BD, these patients may possess an increased susceptibility to SIADH. Although SIADH has been observed in individuals with BD undergoing therapy with drugs such as cyclophosphamide, there remains a lack of clarity regarding its emergence in the absence of such pharmacological interventions—specifically when attributable to CNS inflammation resulting from acute neuro-BD.

In this report, we describe an uncommon presentation where BD and acute neuro-BD were diagnosed simultaneously, and the clinical course was complicated by the onset of SIADH. This case underscores the necessity of including SIADH in the differential diagnosis when managing patients with acute neuro-BD, especially among those exhibiting nonspecific central nervous system symptoms together with hyponatraemia.

Case presentation:

A man of Asian descent in his 60s arrived at the emergency department reporting a 3-day duration of fever, headache, fatigue, decreased appetite, ongoing hiccups, and joint pain localized to his shoulders and ankles. Additionally, he noted the presence of oral aphthous ulcers.

His past medical history was notable for type 2 diabetes mellitus, though he was not on medication due to limited understanding of his disease and insufficient adherence to dietary or exercise guidelines. There was no significant family history, with no cases of BD among his relatives. He occasionally drank alcohol, did not use tobacco, had no recent travel, and no recent contact with animals or ill individuals. Professionally, he worked in the metalworking field and reported no recent sexual intercourse.

Upon assessment, the patient was conscious but showed signs of tiredness. Recorded vital measurements included a temperature of 38.1°C, blood pressure at 135/73 mm Hg, heart rate of 119 beats per minute (regular rhythm), respiratory rate of 19 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation at 96% breathing ambient air. On physical examination, oral aphthous ulcers were noted along with folliculitis-like eruptions on his face and trunk (figure 1A, B, and C). Cardiopulmonary and abdominal exams yielded no notable findings. Muscle strength assessed through manual muscle testing was intact in both upper and lower limbs. There was no evidence of peripheral edema, and clinical evaluation indicated a euvolemic state. The patient was hospitalized for more comprehensive assessment.

Investigations:

The initial laboratory assessment demonstrated significant hyponatraemia ($124\ \mathrm{mmol/L}$), along with elevated levels of creatine kinase ($1349\ \mathrm{U/L}$), aspartate aminotransferase ($77\ \mathrm{U/L}$), alanine aminotransferase ($64\ \mathrm{U/L}$), C-reactive protein ($180\ \mathrm{mg/L}$; reference $<3\ \mathrm{mg/L}$), and erythrocyte sedimentation rate ($70\ \mathrm{mm/hour}$; reference $<15\ \mathrm{mm/hour}$). Renal function remained within normal parameters. Additionally, the patient exhibited hyperglycaemia, with a plasma glucose of $14.2\ \mathrm{mmol/L}$ and an elevated HbA1c of $8.2\%$, pointing to suboptimal glycaemic management. Tests for hepatitis B and C returned negative.

Endocrine analysis showed thyroid-stimulating hormone at $1.11\ \mathrm{mIU/L}$ (reference, $0.50–5.00\ \mathrm{mIU/L}$), free T4 at $1.25\ \mathrm{ng/dL}$ (reference, $0.90–1.70\ \mathrm{ng/dL}$), and serum cortisol at $42\ \mathrm{\mu g/dL}$ (reference, $>6\ \mathrm{\mu g/dL}$), all within reference intervals. Serum osmolality was decreased ($267\ \mathrm{mOsm/kg}$; reference, $<270\ \mathrm{mOsm/kg}$), while urine osmolality was elevated ($900\ \mathrm{mOsm/kg}$; reference, $>300\ \mathrm{mOsm/kg}$), and urinary sodium concentration was also high ($88\ \mathrm{mmol/L}$; reference, $>20\ \mathrm{mmol/L}$), indicative of disrupted water excretion. Although the patient had hypo-osmolar hyponatraemia, antidiuretic hormone (ADH) was found to be increased ($22.4\ \mathrm{pg/mL}$). Electrocardiography showed no abnormalities, and imaging—including chest X-ray and CT scans of the head, chest, and abdomen—was unremarkable. Collectively, these findings were consistent with a diagnosis of SIADH.

By the third day, the patient's headache and vertigo had intensified. Despite the absence of neck rigidity or evidence of cranial nerve dysfunction, clinicians considered the possibility of central nervous system (CNS) inflammation. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain did not identify any remarkable changes affecting the hypothalamus or other neural structures (see figure 2). Lumbar puncture revealed cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) with a mildly increased opening pressure (17 cmH₂O), elevated protein concentration (0.97 g/L), and a slight lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL). All CSF cultures and molecular testing for bacterial and mycobacterial organisms, herpes simplex virus (HSV), varicella zoster virus (VZV), and cryptococcal antigen were negative. Furthermore, cytological assessment of the CSF showed no evidence of malignancy.

Additional diagnostic work-up was undertaken to investigate the possibility of systemic illnesses. Evaluation for sarcoidosis demonstrated that both lysozyme (5.1 µg/mL; reference range 5–10) and angiotensin-converting enzyme (11.4 U/L; reference range 7–25) levels were within normal limits. Screening for hematologic malignancy included measurement of soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) (704 U/mL; reference 122–496 U/mL), which was elevated, while testing for human T-cell leukaemia virus type 1 antibody was negative.

On the fifth day, the patient developed a painful ulcer on the scrotum (figure 1D). Taken together with the presence of oral aphthous lesions and eruptions resembling folliculitis, these signs were indicative of systemic Behçet’s disease (BD). There was no observed hypersensitivity reaction to needle prick. Ocular examination did not reveal uveitis. Human leukocyte antigen B51 (HLA-B51) testing returned positive.

Differential diagnosis:

Upon initial assessment, the presence of fever, headache, general malaise, and hyponatraemia indicated the possibility of either a central nervous system (CNS) infection or a systemic inflammatory condition. Viral meningoencephalitis, particularly due to HSV or VZV, was the primary consideration. Nevertheless, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis yielded negative results for both viral antigens and antibodies, thereby discounting these etiologies. Similarly, bacterial meningitis was contemplated but excluded because CSF analysis demonstrated a lack of neutrophilic predominance, only a mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, and negative culture results. Tuberculous meningitis was dismissed based on negative acid-fast bacillus staining and tuberculosis DNA testing.

Given the neurological presentation, a primary CNS neoplasm was also considered. Yet, brain MRI revealed no significant abnormalities, and CSF cytological analysis identified no malignant cells. The observation of a slight elevation in sIL-2R was acknowledged, but this alone was inadequate to substantiate a diagnosis of CNS lymphoma.

Autoimmune disorders were systematically assessed; however, these were ruled out on the basis of absent hallmark systemic features and negative results for screening autoantibodies. Neurosarcoidosis remained part of the differential diagnosis due to the possibility of normal MRI findings in its early phase, but subsequent laboratory findings rendered this diagnosis unlikely. The mild increase in sIL-2R was regarded as non-specific.

A definitive diagnosis of Behçet’s Disease (BD) was made in this patient, based on clinical manifestations such as fever, fatigue, oral aphthae (two points), genital aphthosis (two points), and a folliculitis-like rash (one point). According to the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease, a score of five (≥4 points required for diagnosis) was assigned. Genital aphthosis is especially regarded as highly specific for BD, which further reinforced the clinical suspicion. Following comprehensive exclusion of infectious, malignant, and autoimmune origins, acute neuro-Behçet’s Disease was diagnosed in the context of CNS involvement and mucocutaneous symptoms.

Treatment:

On the first day of hospitalization, clinicians considered the possibility of an underlying inflammatory disorder, although its precise origin could not be determined. Cefepime was started empirically after obtaining blood cultures to address a potential bacterial source, even though no definitive infectious site was identified.

By the third day, the patient experienced increased severity of headache and vertigo, raising suspicion for central nervous system inflammation or infection. Empiric acyclovir treatment was initiated but subsequently stopped when HSV and VZV encephalitis were excluded by diagnostic tests.

On the eighth day, colchicine therapy commenced following confirmation of systemic BD and acute neuro-BD. Oral aphthous ulcers were managed with a topical corticosteroid ointment. Genital ulcers and skin lesions were treated with silver sulfadiazine cream. SIADH was addressed using conservative measures, including fluid restriction to less than 800 mL per day, meticulous daily monitoring of serum sodium concentrations, and avoidance of rapid sodium correction. The use of hypertonic saline or vasopressin receptor antagonists was not necessary.

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient experienced resolution of fever and improvement in appetite within one day of initiating colchicine therapy. Over the subsequent two weeks, his clinical manifestations—including vertigo, general malaise, persistent hiccups, and mucocutaneous lesions—progressively ameliorated. By the sixteenth day, his serum sodium had stabilized at 136 mmol/L and ADH concentrations declined to 1.8 pg/mL. Additionally, myogenic enzyme values normalized.

Notably, the therapeutic regimen did not include systemic corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive agents. The patient was discharged following a one-month hospital stay, during which acute symptoms subsided, careful management of hyponatraemia was ensured, and rehabilitation addressed diminished physical capabilities. Colchicine treatment was maintained for roughly four years, during which neither BD flare-ups nor neurological complications recurred. Subsequent to disease stabilization, biguanide medication was introduced, resulting in effective glycaemic management.

Discussion:

In this case, systemic BD and acute neuro-BD were identified concurrently, a clinical presentation that is rarely observed. Moreover, the occurrence of SIADH as a complication of acute neuro-BD was also documented. The improvement of mucocutaneous signs, neurological symptoms, and hyponatraemia following colchicine monotherapy suggests a potential direct link among BD, neuro-BD, and SIADH. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for acute neuro-BD in BD patients who develop fever or headache in conjunction with hyponatraemia.

The case is particularly significant because the diagnoses of both systemic BD and acute neuro-BD were established simultaneously, with clinical evidence of mucocutaneous and central nervous system involvement. Typically, major organ manifestations such as CNS lesions emerge several years after BD is first diagnosed; neurological features at initial presentation are uncommon and seen in fewer than 5% of individuals. Only about 10% of patients report headaches that can be attributed specifically to neuro-BD, making early identification difficult. Acute neuro-BD usually presents as a transient meningoencephalitis, potentially with focal neurological signs. In this instance, brain MRI did not show abnormalities at the typical sites affected in BD, such as the hypothalamus. It should be noted that acute neuro-BD may manifest as aseptic meningitis without evident parenchymal brain lesions, and a normal MRI early in the disease course does not exclude the diagnosis, thereby complicating evaluation. Although HLA-B51 testing was carried out, it did not assist in reaching the diagnosis, consistent with international BD diagnostic criteria that prioritize clinical manifestations over HLA-B51 status. This case underscores the necessity of considering neuro-BD in patients with mucocutaneous symptoms who develop non-specific neurological complaints.

During the early stages of forming a differential diagnosis, our primary focus was on infection as the potential source of CNS inflammation, given the presence of an unexplained fever, neurological manifestations, and hyponatraemia. Despite this, we were unable to identify a definitive pathogen. Alternative etiologies, such as connective tissue disorders and neoplastic processes—including sarcoidosis and haematologic malignancies—were also taken into account. Accordingly, our initial, broad diagnostic workup included the assessment of autoimmune serological markers, serum ACE, and sIL-2R, using these primarily as screening tools within an exclusionary framework rather than due to any compelling clinical suspicion.

These tests were selectively employed to help rule out possibilities rather than to confirm a working diagnosis. Ultimately, neither clinical presentation, imaging, nor CSF analysis yielded evidence in favor of these conditions, and these ancillary studies did not contribute significantly to establishing the diagnosis. Notably, ACE demonstrates suboptimal sensitivity for detecting sarcoidosis, while sIL-2R, although potentially useful in assessing malignant lymphoma, tends to be nonspecifically elevated in various inflammatory conditions. In this scenario, the lack of genital ulceration at symptom onset complicated the early consideration of BD, thereby broadening our differential. This case exemplifies the necessity of refraining from indiscriminate diagnostic testing and underscores that such investigations should be guided by substantiated clinical findings and their results carefully contextualized.

While SIADH induced by drugs such as cyclophosphamide has been documented in individuals with BD, the relationship between acute neuro-BD and SIADH is still not well defined. Neuro-BD is marked by inflammation within the CNS, which could potentially disrupt the normal regulation of ADH secretion. Prior investigations have indicated that interleukin-6 (IL-6), a proinflammatory cytokine commonly found at elevated concentrations in neuroinflammatory diseases, correlates with increased ADH secretion. In the context of BD, abnormal monocyte activation drives spontaneous IL-6 synthesis, with increased IL-6 levels identified in the CSF of those experiencing acute neuro-BD episodes. These observations point toward a possible contribution of IL-6-driven inflammation to the development of SIADH in this clinical scenario. Despite the absence of IL-6 measurements in serum or CSF in the current case, the substantial rise in serum CRP could be indicative of an underlying inflammatory state mediated by IL-6. Although SIADH has a broad spectrum of etiologies—including CNS inflammation, respiratory illnesses, cancer, and specific pharmacological agents—the evidence presented here does not permit a direct attribution of SIADH to acute neuro-BD. Instead, the timing of SIADH onset in this patient may represent a general inflammatory response in the CNS, as opposed to a mechanism unique to the underlying disease.

This case further highlights the range of possible complications associated with hyponatraemia due to SIADH occurring during episodes of acute neuro-BD. Notably, prior studies have indicated that persistent hiccups can act as a significant clinical indicator of severe hyponatraemia linked to SIADH as a consequence of central nervous system inflammation, such as in cases of meningitis. Furthermore, during the initial assessment of fever, arthralgia, and general fatigue—symptoms that may suggest inflammatory or metabolic muscle injury—creatine kinase (CK) levels were evaluated, leading to the identification of rhabdomyolysis. Although rhabdomyolysis is a documented consequence of severe hyponatraemia, it is frequently neglected during routine investigations. Both symptoms represent established, though commonly underrecognized, clinical outcomes of marked hyponatraemia. It is important for clinicians to recognize that signs seemingly unrelated, including hiccups or rhabdomyolysis, may actually signify electrolyte imbalances, particularly in the setting of neuroinflammatory disorders.

Corticosteroids represent the standard therapeutic approach for acute neuro-BD, typically eliciting a favourable clinical response. Nonetheless, cases restricted to meningoencephalitis-like presentations have occasionally undergone spontaneous remission. In this report, monotherapy with colchicine led to notable improvement in both mucocutaneous and neurological manifestations, as well as in hyponatraemia. Colchicine mediates anti-inflammatory effects primarily through the inhibition of neutrophil migration and activation, alongside the suppression of cytokine synthesis, specifically targeting IL-1β and IL-6. While colchicine is frequently employed for managing mucocutaneous symptoms in BD, evidence from systematic reviews indicates limited efficacy in acute neuro-BD, generally necessitating the use of immunosuppressive agents for satisfactory disease management. Previous case reports typically describe colchicine as a supplementary agent alongside corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive medications, rather than as a sole therapy. Although the possibility of spontaneous remission in this patient cannot be dismissed, our observations point to colchicine as a potentially beneficial therapeutic strategy for acute neuro-BD and its associated complications such as SIADH, especially in scenarios where corticosteroid administration is inappropriate or not feasible.

Conclusion:

We presented an unusual instance involving the simultaneous onset of BD and acute neuro-BD, with SIADH emerging as a complication of the neurological involvement. This report underscores that SIADH can manifest secondary to acute neuro-BD, emphasizing the necessity for healthcare providers to recognize this possible relationship. Administration of colchicine alone produced prompt resolution of both mucocutaneous and neurological symptoms, as well as correction of hyponatraemia, indicating its potential utility as an initial treatment strategy for neuro-BD. Clinicians should consider acute neuro-BD, particularly in BD patients exhibiting vague central nervous system symptoms such as fever and headache alongside hyponatraemia, especially when SIADH is present.
