Title: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

Eiichi Kakehi, Yukako Matsumoto, Shigehisa Sakurai, Kazuhiko Kotani

Abstract:

Summary: We describe an uncommon instance of acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) that was identified at the same time as systemic Behçet’s disease (BD), meeting criteria for definite BD and complicated by syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). A male patient in his 60s presented with fever, headache, and mucocutaneous manifestations such as oral aphthae, folliculitis-like eruptions, genital ulcers, and hyponatraemia. The diagnosis of BD was established based on the mucocutaneous signs. While brain MRI did not reveal any abnormalities, cerebrospinal fluid evaluation demonstrated aseptic meningitis, which supported the diagnosis of acute neuro-BD. Laboratory studies fulfilled the criteria for SIADH. Treatment with colchicine alone resulted in resolution of the mucocutaneous lesions, neurological symptoms, and hyponatraemia, without requiring corticosteroids or additional immunosuppressive therapies. This case indicates a potential connection between acute neuro-BD and SIADH, emphasizing the need for awareness of both neurological and endocrine features in patients with newly identified BD.

Background:

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a persistent, multisystem inflammatory condition marked by the repeated occurrence of oral and genital ulcers, uveitis, and cutaneous manifestations. Neurological manifestations, termed neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), are seen in approximately 10%–20% of cases and usually emerge several years following the initial diagnosis. Acute forms of neuro-BD typically manifest as meningoencephalitis accompanied by parenchymal lesions in the brain, most often affecting the brainstem. While a delayed presentation is common, simultaneous diagnosis of BD and neuro-BD is infrequent. In such presentations, clinicians should evaluate for both mucocutaneous manifestations and neurological symptoms such as headache, behavioral disturbances, psychiatric features, and focal neurological deficits.

Syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) is a recognized etiology of hyponatremia, frequently secondary to central nervous system (CNS) inflammation, neoplasms, or specific medications. Given the inflammatory profile of acute neuro-BD, patients may have an elevated risk of developing SIADH. SIADH has been described in BD patients treated with agents like cyclophosphamide; however, its development purely due to CNS inflammation in acute neuro-BD, without drug-related factors, is not well established.

Here, we present an uncommon case where BD and acute neuro-BD were identified simultaneously and were further complicated by the development of SIADH. This case emphasizes the necessity of including SIADH in the differential diagnosis for patients with acute neuro-BD presenting with non-specific neurological complaints and hyponatremia.

Case presentation:

A man of Asian descent in his 60s came to the emergency department with a 3-day duration of fever, headache, generalized fatigue, poor appetite, ongoing hiccups, and joint pain involving both shoulders and ankles. Additionally, he noted the presence of oral aphthous ulcers.

His past medical history was significant for type 2 diabetes mellitus, for which he was not under any therapeutic regimen due to limited awareness of his diagnosis and the absence of appropriate dietary or exercise practices. There was no notable family history, including Behçet’s disease (BD) among relatives. He drank alcohol occasionally, denied smoking, and had no recent travel history or exposures to animals or ill individuals. He was employed in metalworking and had not had recent sexual intercourse.

Upon assessment, the patient was conscious but showed signs of fatigue. His vital signs recorded included a temperature of 38.1°C, blood pressure measured at 135/73 mm Hg, a regular heart rate of 119 beats per minute, respiratory rate of 19 breaths per minute, and an oxygen saturation level of 96% while breathing ambient air. The physical exam identified oral aphthae as well as rash resembling folliculitis present on the face and trunk (see figure 1A, B, and C). No abnormal findings were noted during cardiovascular, pulmonary, or abdominal evaluation. Manual testing of muscle strength demonstrated intact strength in both upper and lower limbs. There was no evidence of peripheral edema, and clinical assessment indicated a euvolemic volume status. The patient was admitted for additional diagnostic workup.

Investigations:

The initial laboratory findings demonstrated significant hyponatraemia (124 mmol/L) and raised levels of creatine kinase (1349 U/L), aspartate aminotransferase (77 U/L), alanine aminotransferase (64 U/L), C-reactive protein (CRP) (180 mg/L; reference range <3 mg/L), and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (70 mm/hour; reference <15 mm/hour), while renal function remained within normal limits. The patient also exhibited hyperglycaemia, with a plasma glucose measurement of 14.2 mmol/L, and an elevated HbA1c at 8.2%, reflecting suboptimal glucose regulation. Serological testing for hepatitis B and C was negative.

Assessment of endocrine function showed thyroid-stimulating hormone at 1.11 mIU/L (reference: 0.50–5.00 mIU/L), free T4 at 1.25 ng/dL (reference: 0.90–1.70 ng/dL), and serum cortisol at 42 µg/dL (reference: >6 µg/dL), all within reference ranges. The serum osmolality was decreased at 267 mOsm/kg (reference: <270 mOsm/kg) and urine osmolality was increased at 900 mOsm/kg (reference: >300 mOsm/kg), along with elevated urine sodium at 88 mmol/L (reference: >20 mmol/L), indicative of defective water clearance. Despite the presence of hypo-osmolar hyponatraemia, antidiuretic hormone (ADH) levels were heightened at 22.4 pg/mL. Electrocardiogram findings were normal, and imaging—including chest X-ray and CT scans of the head, chest, and abdomen—did not reveal any abnormalities. These collective results were consistent with a diagnosis of SIADH.

By the third day, his headache and vertigo had become more severe. While there was no neck stiffness or involvement of the cranial nerves, central nervous system (CNS) inflammation was considered likely. MRI of the brain demonstrated no notable abnormalities affecting the hypothalamus or other brain regions (figure 2). Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) evaluation showed a mildly raised opening pressure (17 cmH₂O), elevated protein concentration (0.97 g/L), and modest lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL). CSF cultures, along with diagnostic tests for bacterial and mycobacterial agents, herpes simplex virus (HSV), varicella zoster virus (VZV), and cryptococcal antigen, were all negative. Cytological analysis of the CSF excluded the presence of malignant cells.

Additional testing was undertaken to identify potential systemic diseases. Assessment for sarcoidosis found lysozyme at 5.1 µg/mL (reference range 5–10) and angiotensin-converting enzyme at 11.4 U/L (reference 7–25), both within normal limits. Hematological malignancy screening showed a soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) value of 704 U/mL (reference, 122–496 U/mL), and the human T-cell leukemia virus type 1 antibody was negative.

On the fifth day, a painful ulcer developed on his scrotum (figure 1D). Along with oral aphthous ulcers and rashes resembling folliculitis, these symptoms pointed towards a systemic manifestation of BD. He did not demonstrate a pinprick hypersensitivity. Eye examination showed no evidence of uveitis. HLA-B51 testing was positive.

Differential diagnosis:

Upon initial evaluation, the presence of fever, headache, generalized malaise, and hyponatremia raised suspicion for either a central nervous system infection or a systemic inflammatory condition. Viral meningoencephalitis, with HSV or VZV as probable etiologies, was initially considered. Nevertheless, cerebrospinal fluid analysis returned negative results for both viral antigens and antibodies, thereby eliminating these viral causes. Bacterial meningitis was also contemplated but dismissed owing to the lack of neutrophilic predominance, the presence of only mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, and negative CSF cultures. Tuberculous meningitis was ruled out after acid-fast stain and tuberculosis DNA assays produced negative results.

Given the neurological manifestations, primary CNS malignancy was among the differentials. Brain MRI, however, showed no pathological findings, and CSF cytology did not detect malignant cells. Although soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) was marginally increased, this alone did not substantiate CNS lymphoma.

Autoimmune disorders were investigated as potential causes, but were discounted due to the absence of typical systemic features and negative autoantibody screening. Neurosarcoidosis was considered because it can present with normal MRI scans at onset; however, later laboratory data rendered this diagnosis improbable. The slightly raised sIL-2R was interpreted as an unspecific laboratory finding.

The diagnosis of definite Behçet’s disease (BD) in this patient was established based on clinical manifestations such as fever, fatigue, oral aphthae (two points), genital aphthosis (two points), and a rash resembling folliculitis (one point). According to the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease, the cumulative score reached five points (a score of four or above denotes definitive BD). Notably, genital aphthosis serves as a specific sign of BD and further supports the diagnosis. Infectious, neoplastic, and autoimmune etiologies were systematically excluded, leading to a final diagnosis of acute neuro-Behçet’s disease, characterized by central nervous system involvement along with mucocutaneous symptoms.

Treatment:

On the first day of hospitalization, clinicians suspected an underlying inflammatory disorder, but the precise cause could not be determined. Empirical treatment with cefepime was started following blood culture collection to address a possible bacterial infection, even though no clear infectious source had been identified.

By day 3, the patient experienced increased headache and vertigo, raising concerns for central nervous system infection or inflammation. Empiric acyclovir therapy was initiated but subsequently discontinued when HSV and VZV encephalitis were excluded based on diagnostic testing.

On day 8, colchicine was prescribed after establishing a diagnosis of systemic Behçet’s disease (BD) with acute neuro-BD. Aphthous lesions in the oral cavity were managed with topical corticosteroid ointment, while genital and skin lesions received silver sulfadiazine cream. SIADH was treated conservatively through restricting fluid intake to less than 800 mL per day, performing daily serum sodium measurements, and avoiding rapid sodium correction. Administration of hypertonic saline and vasopressin receptor antagonists was not necessary.

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient's fever subsided and his appetite increased one day after initiating colchicine therapy. Over the next two weeks, his clinical manifestations—including vertigo, general malaise, hiccups, and mucocutaneous lesions—gradually resolved. On day 16, his serum sodium levels normalized to 136 mmol/L, and ADH concentrations decreased to 1.8 pg/mL. Levels of myogenic enzymes also returned to the reference range.

Notably, systemic corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive agents were not administered during the course of treatment. After a hospital stay lasting one month—which allowed for recovery from the acute episode, close monitoring and management of hyponatraemia, and rehabilitation targeting decreased physical performance—the patient was discharged. Colchicine therapy was maintained for about four years, during which there were no further episodes of BD or neurological symptoms. Once BD had stabilized, biguanide was started, resulting in effective glycaemic control thereafter.

Discussion:

This report describes a case where systemic Behçet’s disease (BD) and acute neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) were diagnosed concurrently, a presentation that is seldom seen clinically. The case further illustrates that acute neuro-BD may be accompanied by the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). The improvement of mucocutaneous involvement, neurological symptoms, and hyponatraemia after initiating colchicine monotherapy indicates a potential direct link between BD, neuro-BD, and SIADH. For BD patients exhibiting fever or headache in association with hyponatraemia, clinicians should include acute neuro-BD in the differential diagnosis.

A distinguishing feature of this case is the coincident identification of both systemic BD and acute neuro-BD, based on the presence of mucocutaneous manifestations and central nervous system (CNS) abnormalities. Major organ involvement such as CNS pathology generally appears approximately four years after BD is first diagnosed; it is uncommon for neurological symptoms to be present at onset, as they occur in fewer than 5% of cases. Only about 10% of patients experience headaches attributable specifically to neuro-BD. Therefore, early detection of neuro-BD, as demonstrated in this patient, posed a significant diagnostic challenge. Typically, acute neuro-BD presents as a meningoencephalitis that resolves without intervention, with or without localized neurological deficits. Brain MRI in this individual failed to show characteristic changes at common sites for BD lesions, such as the hypothalamic region. In some cases, acute neuro-BD manifests as aseptic meningitis without discernible brain parenchymal lesions, and MRI findings can be unremarkable, especially during initial disease phases; this does not rule out the diagnosis. The absence of imaging abnormalities complicated diagnostic efforts. Testing for HLA-B51 was carried out but did not assist in diagnosis, consistent with international diagnostic criteria for BD, which place emphasis on clinical manifestations rather than HLA-B51 status. This case emphasizes the importance of considering neuro-BD in patients with BD who develop mucocutaneous findings and non-specific neurological complaints.

During the early phase of our differential diagnosis, infection was initially favored as the etiology of CNS inflammation due to the patient's unexplained fever, neurological manifestations, and hyponatremia, though no definitive infectious agent was found. We additionally evaluated other potential causes of CNS inflammation, including autoimmune connective tissue disorders and malignancies such as sarcoidosis and hematologic cancers. As a component of a comprehensive workup, we ordered autoimmune serologies, serum angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE), and soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) assays as preliminary screening tests. These investigations were conducted selectively and primarily to rule out other etiologies, rather than from high clinical suspicion. Clinical assessment, imaging studies, and CSF analysis yielded no evidence substantiating these conditions, and the results did not significantly aid our diagnostic conclusions. It is worth noting that serum ACE demonstrates limited sensitivity for detecting sarcoidosis, and while sIL-2R can be of value in assessing for malignant lymphoma, mild elevations are not specific and may simply indicate generalized inflammation. In this scenario, the lack of genital ulcers during the early presentation rendered Behçet’s disease an uncertain consideration, necessitating a wide-ranging differential diagnosis. This diagnostic pathway underscores the necessity for judicious test selection, advocating that clinicians restrict such investigations to cases supported by relevant clinical indicators and interpret findings carefully.

While drug-induced SIADH has been documented in individuals with BD treated with agents like cyclophosphamide, the connection between acute neuro-BD and SIADH remains unclear. Neuro-BD is defined by central nervous system inflammation, which may play a role in abnormal ADH regulation. Multiple reports indicate that interleukin-6 (IL-6), a proinflammatory cytokine often elevated in neuroinflammatory conditions, correlates with increased ADH secretion. In BD, inappropriate monocyte activation prompts spontaneous IL-6 release, and heightened IL-6 concentrations have been found in the CSF of those experiencing acute neuro-BD. This evidence implies that IL-6–driven inflammation could be implicated in SIADH development in this context. Although IL-6 levels were not assessed in either serum or CSF in the current case, the substantially raised serum CRP level may suggest an IL-6–related inflammatory process. SIADH has been linked to a variety of etiologies, including CNS inflammation, respiratory illnesses, neoplasia, and specific pharmacologic agents, but the current data are insufficient to establish a direct causal relationship between SIADH and acute neuro-BD. The observed temporal relationship in this case may indicate a generalized response to CNS inflammation rather than a mechanism unique to neuro-BD.

This case demonstrates the possible adverse effects of hyponatraemia due to SIADH in acute neuro-BD. Prior publications have highlighted that persistent hiccups can be a significant clinical indicator of severe hyponatraemia resulting from SIADH secondary to central nervous system inflammation, such as meningitis. Furthermore, creatine kinase (CK) was assessed during the initial workup for fever, arthralgia, and general fatigue—findings which might point to muscle injury of either inflammatory or metabolic origin—and this evaluation uncovered rhabdomyolysis. Rhabdomyolysis is a known, though frequently neglected, complication of severe hyponatraemia that is not regularly sought in practice. While these manifestations are documented, their clinical relevance in the context of marked hyponatraemia is often underestimated. It is important for clinicians to recognize that seemingly unrelated symptoms like hiccups or rhabdomyolysis could signify hidden disturbances in electrolyte balance, especially within neuroinflammatory disease states.

Corticosteroids remain the cornerstone of acute neuro-BD management, with most individuals exhibiting positive responses. Nonetheless, spontaneous improvement has been documented in instances where only meningoencephalitis-like manifestations are present. In this report, the use of colchicine alone led to amelioration of mucocutaneous and neurological symptoms, as well as correction of hyponatraemia. Colchicine’s anti-inflammatory action involves impeding neutrophil movement and activity, and decreasing cytokine production such as IL-1β and IL-6. While colchicine is frequently utilized for treating mucocutaneous symptoms of BD, evidence from systematic reviews indicates a lack of efficacy for acute neuro-BD, for which immunosuppressive drugs are generally necessary to achieve sufficient disease management. Historically, colchicine has been administered in combination with corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive agents, rather than as single-agent therapy. Although the possibility of spontaneous remission cannot be eliminated in this patient, the observed results indicate that colchicine could represent a viable therapeutic alternative for acute neuro-BD and associated conditions such as SIADH, particularly when corticosteroids are unsuitable or contraindicated.

Conclusion:

We reported an uncommon presentation of BD and acute neuro-BD diagnosed at the same time, with SIADH complicating the neuro-BD course. This case illustrates that SIADH can develop as a consequence of acute neuro-BD, underscoring the need for clinicians to recognize this possible relationship. Treatment with colchicine alone resulted in swift resolution of mucocutaneous signs, neurological symptoms, and hyponatraemia, indicating that colchicine may serve as an effective initial therapy for neuro-BD. For BD patients exhibiting vague CNS manifestations such as fever and headache alongside hyponatraemia, clinicians should be vigilant for acute neuro-BD, especially in cases where SIADH is present.
