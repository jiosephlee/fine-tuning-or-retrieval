Title: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

Eiichi Kakehi, Yukako Matsumoto, Shigehisa Sakurai, Kazuhiko Kotani

Abstract:

Summary: We describe an uncommon presentation of acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) manifesting simultaneously with systemic Behçet’s disease (BD), meeting criteria for definite BD, and further complicated by the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). A male patient in his sixties presented with fever, headache, and mucocutaneous findings including oral aphthous ulcers, folliculitis-like skin eruptions, genital ulcers, and hyponatraemia. BD was diagnosed based on the observed mucocutaneous manifestations. Although brain MRI was unremarkable, cerebrospinal fluid analysis demonstrated aseptic meningitis, in line with acute neuro-BD. Laboratory studies also fulfilled the diagnostic criteria for SIADH. Treatment with colchicine alone resulted in the resolution of both mucocutaneous and neurological symptoms, as well as hyponatraemia, with no requirement for corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive agents. This report highlights a potential link between acute neuro-BD and SIADH and emphasizes the need to identify simultaneous neurological and endocrine symptoms in patients with a new diagnosis of BD.

Background:

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a chronic inflammatory condition affecting multiple organ systems, distinguished by recurring oral and genital ulcers, uveitis, and dermatologic manifestations. Involvement of the nervous system, known as neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), is observed in roughly 10%–20% of cases and usually emerges several years following the initial diagnosis of BD. Acute neuro-BD most commonly presents with meningoencephalitis and parenchymal central nervous system lesions, often localised to the brainstem. While a delayed presentation is typical, simultaneous diagnosis of both BD and neuro-BD is uncommon. In such instances, clinicians must evaluate both mucocutaneous findings and neurological presentations such as headaches, alterations in behaviour, psychiatric disturbances, and focal neurological signs.

The syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) is a recognized etiology of hyponatraemia, frequently associated with inflammation of the central nervous system, malignancies, or certain pharmacologic agents. Due to the underlying inflammation in acute neuro-BD, patients may have an elevated risk of developing SIADH. Although instances of SIADH have been documented in BD patients treated with cyclophosphamide, its occurrence solely due to central nervous system inflammation from acute neuro-BD—without drug-related factors—remains insufficiently clarified.

We describe an unusual case where BD and acute neuro-BD were identified at the same time, with SIADH as a complicating factor. This report underscores the need to include SIADH in the differential diagnosis when acute neuro-BD presents, especially in individuals with ambiguous central nervous system findings accompanied by hyponatraemia.

Case presentation:

A man of Asian descent in his 60s arrived at the emergency department following three days of fever, headache, fatigue, anorexia, ongoing hiccups, and joint pain involving his shoulders and ankles. He additionally noted the presence of oral aphthous ulcers.

His past medical history was notable for type 2 diabetes mellitus, which remained untreated due to his limited understanding of the disease and his lack of adherence to appropriate diet and exercise regimens. His family history was non-contributory, with no known cases of BD among his relatives. The patient drank alcohol on occasion, was a non-smoker, had no recent travel history, and reported no exposure to animals or ill individuals. He was employed in metalworking and denied any recent sexual activity.

On assessment, the patient was conscious but showed signs of tiredness. His vital parameters were as follows: temperature of 38.1°C, blood pressure measured at 135/73 mm Hg, regular heart rate of 119 beats per minute, respiratory rate of 19 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation at 96% while breathing ambient air. The physical exam identified oral aphthous ulcers and rashes resembling folliculitis present on both the face and trunk (see figure 1A, B, and C). No abnormalities were noted during the cardiopulmonary or abdominal evaluations. Manual muscle testing indicated full strength in all extremities. Peripheral edema was not detected, and clinical evaluation suggested he was euvolemic. The patient was subsequently hospitalized for further assessment.

Investigations:

The initial laboratory assessment demonstrated significant hyponatremia (124 mmol/L), increased creatine kinase (1349 U/L), elevated aspartate aminotransferase (77 U/L) and alanine aminotransferase (64 U/L), as well as a markedly raised C-reactive protein (CRP) at 180 mg/L (reference, <3 mg/L) and an erythrocyte sedimentation rate of 70 mm/hour (reference, <15 mm/hour). Renal function was within normal limits. The patient exhibited hyperglycemia (plasma glucose 14.2 mmol/L) and a high HbA1c of 8.2%, suggesting suboptimal glycemic control. Tests for hepatitis B and C were negative.

Evaluation of endocrine function showed a thyroid-stimulating hormone of 1.11 mIU/L (reference, 0.50–5.00 mIU/L), free T4 at 1.25 ng/dL (reference, 0.90–1.70 ng/dL), and serum cortisol of 42 µg/dL (reference, >6 µg/dL), all within normal parameters. The serum osmolality was decreased (267 mOsm/kg; reference, <270 mOsm/kg), while urine osmolality was high (900 mOsm/kg, reference, >300 mOsm/kg), accompanied by elevated urinary sodium (88 mmol/L; reference, >20 mmol/L), indicative of defective water clearance. Although the patient had hypo-osmolar hyponatremia, antidiuretic hormone (ADH) was raised at 22.4 pg/mL. Electrocardiogram findings were unremarkable, as were chest X-ray and CT scans of the head, chest, and abdomen. Collectively, these results were consistent with a diagnosis of syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH).

On the third day, the patient experienced an intensification of both headache and vertigo. Despite the absence of neck rigidity or cranial nerve deficits, inflammation of the central nervous system was considered. Magnetic resonance imaging of the brain did not demonstrate any significant lesions in the hypothalamus or elsewhere (see figure 2). Analysis of the cerebrospinal fluid showed a mildly increased opening pressure (17 cmH₂O), elevated protein concentration (0.97 g/L), and a slight lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL). Microbiological tests for bacterial and mycobacterial species, herpes simplex virus (HSV), varicella zoster virus (VZV), and cryptococcal antigen in the CSF were all negative. Cytological assessment of the CSF revealed no evidence of malignant cells.

Further workup to assess for systemic conditions included sarcoidosis investigations, which showed normal lysozyme (5.1 µg/mL; reference range 5–10) and angiotensin-converting enzyme levels (11.4 U/L; reference range 7–25). Testing for hematologic malignancy detected elevated levels of soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) at 704 U/mL (reference range 122–496 U/mL), and screening for human T-cell leukaemia virus type 1 antibody was negative.

By day 5, the patient developed a painful ulcer on the scrotum (figure 1D). Together with oral aphthous lesions and folliculitis-like eruptions, these manifestations indicated a systemic variant of BD. The patient did not demonstrate pathergy to a pinprick. Ophthalmologic assessment revealed no evidence of uveitis. HLA-B51 testing was positive.

Differential diagnosis:

Upon admission, the presence of fever, headache, general malaise, and hyponatremia raised suspicion for a central nervous system infection or systemic inflammatory process. Viral meningoencephalitis, especially due to HSV or VZV, was the initial clinical suspicion. Nonetheless, cerebrospinal fluid analyses were negative for viral antigens and antibodies, essentially ruling out these etiologies. Bacterial meningitis was similarly considered but discounted owing to the lack of neutrophil predominance, a modest lymphocytic pleocytosis, and negative CSF cultures. Tuberculous meningitis was also eliminated from consideration following negative acid-fast bacilli staining and tuberculosis DNA testing.

The potential for a primary CNS neoplasm was assessed because of the neurological symptoms, yet brain MRI was unremarkable and CSF cytology did not detect any malignant cells. While sIL-2R levels showed a mild increase, this result alone was inadequate to confirm CNS lymphoma.

The evaluation also encompassed autoimmune conditions; however, these were ruled out due to a lack of characteristic systemic clinical features and negative autoantibody screening. Neurosarcoidosis remained a consideration since MRI may appear normal at early stages, though subsequent laboratory data rendered this diagnosis improbable. The modest rise in sIL-2R was interpreted as non-specific.

The diagnosis of definite Behçet’s Disease (BD) was made in light of clinical findings, namely fever and fatigue, oral aphthae (two points), genital aphthosis (two points), and a folliculitis-like skin eruption (one point). Utilizing the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease, the cumulative score reached five points (≥4 supports definitive diagnosis). Notably, genital aphthosis is highly specific for BD and lent strong support to the diagnosis. Following thorough exclusion of infectious, neoplastic, and autoimmune etiologies, a final diagnosis of acute neuro-Behçet’s Disease was determined based on CNS involvement concurrent with mucocutaneous manifestations.

Treatment:

On the first day of hospitalization, clinicians considered an underlying inflammatory disorder, though its precise cause was not identified. After obtaining blood cultures, empirical cefepime therapy was begun to address a possible bacterial infection, despite no localized infectious source being evident.

By the third day, the patient's headache and vertigo had intensified, raising concerns for central nervous system inflammation or infection. Empirical acyclovir was initiated but discontinued once testing excluded HSV and VZV encephalitis.

On day 8, treatment with colchicine was initiated following confirmation of systemic Behçet's disease (BD) and acute neuro-Behçet's disease (neuro-BD). Oral aphthous ulcers received topical corticosteroid ointment, while genital ulcers and skin lesions were treated with silver sulfadiazine cream. Syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) was handled conservatively using fluid restriction to less than 800 mL per day, close daily monitoring of serum sodium, and avoidance of rapid sodium correction. The use of hypertonic saline or vasopressin receptor antagonists was not necessary.

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient’s fever subsided and his appetite increased the day following the initiation of colchicine. Over the next two weeks, vertigo, general malaise, hiccups, and mucocutaneous lesions showed progressive improvement. By the sixteenth day, his serum sodium had returned to normal at 136 mmol/L, and ADH levels had fallen to 1.8 pg/mL. Levels of myogenic enzymes normalized as well.

Significantly, no systemic corticosteroids or alternative immunosuppressive agents were administered during his treatment. He was discharged after a one-month hospitalization, during which he recovered from the acute episode, hyponatraemia was closely observed and managed, and he underwent rehabilitation for his diminished physical function. Colchicine treatment was maintained for roughly four years, with no relapses of Behçet’s disease (BD) or neurological complications recorded during that period. Once his BD was controlled, biguanide therapy was started, resulting in consistently effective glycaemic management.

Discussion:

This report describes a rare clinical presentation, with both systemic Behçet’s disease (BD) and acute neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) being identified at the same time. The case further illustrates that acute neuro-BD may be associated with the development of syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). The improvement in mucocutaneous signs, neurological symptoms, and hyponatraemia observed after initiating colchicine monotherapy suggests a direct connection between BD, neuro-BD, and SIADH. For patients diagnosed with BD who develop fever or headache in conjunction with hyponatraemia, clinicians should have a high index of suspicion for underlying acute neuro-BD.

This report is distinguished by the concurrent identification of systemic BD and acute neuro-BD, evidenced by the coexistence of mucocutaneous lesions and central nervous system (CNS) involvement. Generally, major organ complications such as CNS pathology tend to develop roughly four years following the initial diagnosis of BD, while neurological manifestations at disease onset are rare, occurring in less than 5% of cases. Additionally, only about 10% of patients report headaches directly attributable to neuro-BD. As a result, initial identification of neuro-BD in this context was particularly difficult. The typical presentation of acute neuro-BD is a self-limited meningoencephalitic process, which may or may not involve focal neurological signs. In the present patient, MRI of the brain did not reveal abnormalities within characteristic BD regions, such as the hypothalamus. Acute neuro-BD can present in the form of aseptic meningitis without evidence of parenchymal lesions, and an unremarkable early MRI does not rule out the diagnosis, further hindering timely recognition. Although testing for HLA-B51 was carried out, it did not aid in diagnosis, consistent with international criteria for BD that emphasize clinical findings over genetic markers like HLA-B51. This case underscores the need to consider neuro-BD in patients exhibiting mucocutaneous findings alongside vague neurological symptoms.

At the outset of our differential diagnosis, we gave precedence to infection as the etiology of CNS inflammation, owing to the coexistence of fever of unknown origin, neurological manifestations, and hyponatraemia; despite this, no causative infectious agent was detected. Additional considerations for CNS inflammation encompassed connective tissue disorders and neoplastic processes, with sarcoidosis and hematologic malignancies featuring in the differential. Consequently, we conducted initial broad screening that included autoimmune serology, serum ACE, and sIL-2R measurements. These assessments were pursued on a limited basis as part of a process of exclusion rather than in response to strong clinical indicators. No clinical, imaging, or CSF evidence was identified to support these alternative diagnoses, and the results of these ancillary tests did not substantially inform the final diagnostic outcome. It is notable that ACE is insufficiently sensitive for sarcoidosis, while sIL-2R, although potentially useful for malignant lymphoma assessment, can yield mild elevations in various inflammatory conditions and thus lacks specificity. The absence of genital ulcers early in the patient’s clinical course introduced ambiguity regarding BD, necessitating a wide-ranging differential diagnosis. This approach to diagnosis underscores the need for selective testing guided by clinical features, and for cautious interpretation of results, rather than reliance on indiscriminate screening.

While drug-induced SIADH has previously been observed in individuals with BD treated with agents such as cyclophosphamide, the relationship between acute neuro-BD and SIADH is not well elucidated. Neuro-BD involves inflammation of the central nervous system, which could potentially lead to abnormal regulation of ADH secretion. Prior research has demonstrated that interleukin-6 (IL-6), a proinflammatory cytokine commonly elevated in neuroinflammatory diseases, correlates with heightened ADH secretion. In BD, atypical monocyte activation results in spontaneous IL-6 synthesis, and increased concentrations of IL-6 have been found in the cerebrospinal fluid of patients experiencing acute neuro-BD. These data imply that IL-6-driven inflammation might have a role in the development of SIADH in this context. Although IL-6 concentrations were not determined in either the serum or CSF in the present case, the notably increased serum CRP level may indicate a systemic inflammatory process potentially mediated by IL-6. Although SIADH can accompany various diseases, such as central nervous system inflammation, pulmonary pathology, malignancies, and some medications, current findings do not permit a definitive causal relationship between SIADH and acute neuro-BD to be concluded. The time correlation noted in this patient could represent a generalized reaction to CNS inflammation rather than a process unique to neuro-BD.

This case further highlights the possible complications associated with hyponatraemia due to SIADH in the context of acute neuro-Behçet’s disease. Prior literature has noted that persistent hiccups may act as a valuable clinical indicator of severe hyponatraemia resulting from SIADH secondary to central nervous system inflammation, such as in meningitis. As part of the initial assessment for fever, arthralgia, and generalized fatigue—which could stem from inflammatory or metabolic muscle injury—we assessed creatine kinase, uncovering rhabdomyolysis, a recognized but frequently missed complication of significant hyponatraemia that is not typically included in routine evaluation. Although these clinical features are well described, their significance is commonly underestimated in cases of profound hyponatraemia. Clinicians should recognize that seemingly unrelated symptoms like hiccups or rhabdomyolysis may point to underlying electrolyte imbalances, especially when neuroinflammatory disease is present.

Corticosteroids are recognized as the primary therapy for acute neuro-Behçet’s disease, with most cases showing a positive clinical response. Nonetheless, there have been descriptions of spontaneous remission in individuals with meningoencephalitis-like presentations. In this case, monotherapy with colchicine was associated with improvements in both mucocutaneous and neurological manifestations, as well as normalization of sodium levels. Colchicine mediates anti-inflammatory actions through suppression of neutrophil chemotaxis and activity, as well as by inhibiting cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-6. While colchicine is commonly administered for mucocutaneous involvement in Behçet’s disease, systematic reviews have concluded that it lacks efficacy for acute neuro-Behçet’s and typically, additional immunosuppressive drugs are necessary for proper management. Indeed, previous reports have described colchicine as adjunctive to corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants, rather than being used as a sole agent. Although the possibility of spontaneous remission in this patient cannot be ruled out, these observations indicate that colchicine may represent a potential therapeutic alternative for acute neuro-Behçet’s and associated conditions like SIADH, especially in cases where corticosteroids are either unsuitable or contraindicated.

Conclusion:

We report an uncommon presentation in which both Behçet’s disease (BD) and acute neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) were diagnosed at the same time, and where the course was further complicated by the development of syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). This case underscores that SIADH can arise as a complication of acute neuro-BD, emphasizing the necessity for clinicians to recognize this possible link. Administration of colchicine as a single agent resulted in prompt resolution of both mucocutaneous and neurologic symptoms as well as correction of hyponatraemia, indicating that colchicine may serve as an effective initial therapy in early-stage neuro-BD. In individuals with BD exhibiting non-specific central nervous system complaints such as headache and fever together with hyponatraemia, clinicians should have a heightened suspicion for acute neuro-BD, especially when SIADH coexists.
