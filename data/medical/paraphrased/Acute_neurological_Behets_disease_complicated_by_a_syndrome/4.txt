Acute neurological Behçet’s disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

Eiichi Kakehi, Yukako Matsumoto, Shigehisa Sakurai, Kazuhiko Kotani

Abstract:

Summary: We describe an uncommon presentation of acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) diagnosed simultaneously with systemic Behçet’s disease (BD), meeting the criteria for definite BD, and further complicated by the occurrence of syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). The patient, a man in his 60s, exhibited symptoms including fever, headache, hyponatraemia, and mucocutaneous involvement such as oral aphthae, folliculitis-like skin eruptions, and genital ulcers. The diagnosis of BD was established based on these mucocutaneous signs. Neuroimaging with brain MRI yielded normal results, while cerebrospinal fluid analysis identified aseptic meningitis, thus substantiating a diagnosis of acute neuro-BD. Laboratory evaluation fulfilled SIADH diagnostic criteria. The administration of colchicine alone resulted in remission of mucocutaneous, neurological, and biochemical (hyponatraemia) manifestations, eliminating the necessity for corticosteroid or additional immunosuppressive therapy. This report highlights a potential link between acute neuro-BD and SIADH, emphasizing the need for clinicians to be vigilant for simultaneous neurological and endocrine complications in cases of newly identified BD.

Background:

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a persistent inflammatory condition affecting multiple organ systems, most notably presenting with repeated oral and genital ulcers, uveitis, and cutaneous manifestations. Neurological complications, termed neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), develop in roughly 10%–20% of cases, generally manifesting several years subsequent to the initial BD diagnosis. The acute form of neuro-BD is predominantly characterized by meningoencephalitis and parenchymal lesions, frequently localized to the brainstem region. Although concurrent diagnosis of BD and neuro-BD is uncommon—most instances are identified sequentially—simultaneous presentation requires clinicians to evaluate both mucocutaneous findings and neurological symptoms, such as headache, behavioral disturbances, psychiatric complaints, and focal neurological impairments.

Syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) represents a prominent etiology of hyponatraemia, commonly precipitated by central nervous system (CNS) inflammation, neoplastic processes, or specific pharmaceuticals. Given the underlying CNS inflammatory processes in acute neuro-BD, there is a plausible risk for the emergence of SIADH in these patients. While prior documentation links SIADH with BD cases treated with agents like cyclophosphamide, the incidence of SIADH due solely to CNS inflammation associated with acute neuro-BD—absent drug-induced factors—remains insufficiently characterized.

Here, we describe an exceptional instance in which BD and acute neuro-BD were diagnosed simultaneously, with the additional complication of SIADH. This case underscores the necessity of considering SIADH as a possible clinical manifestation in acute neuro-BD, particularly in individuals who exhibit non-specific CNS symptoms coupled with hyponatraemia.

Case presentation:

A man of Asian descent in his 60s arrived at the emergency department, complaining of a 3-day period marked by fever, headache, general fatigue, diminished appetite, unrelenting hiccups, and joint pain localized to his shoulders and ankles. Additionally, he described the presence of oral aphthous ulcers.

His past medical history was notable for type 2 diabetes mellitus, which remained unmanaged due to his limited awareness of the diagnosis and a lack of adherence to recommended lifestyle modifications regarding diet and physical activity. The patient’s family history did not reveal any instances of BD among his relatives. Socially, he was an occasional alcohol consumer, did not use tobacco, and reported no recent travel, animal contact, or interactions with ill persons. He was employed in metalworking and had not had recent sexual encounters.

Upon assessment, the patient was conscious yet exhibited signs of tiredness. His recorded vital signs included a body temperature of 38.1°C, blood pressure measuring 135/73 mm Hg, a regular heart rate of 119 beats per minute, a respiratory rate of 19 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation of 96% while breathing ambient air. The physical examination identified oral aphthous ulcers and folliculitis-like lesions on his facial and truncal regions (figure 1A, B and C). No abnormalities were noted on cardiopulmonary or abdominal examination. Manual muscle testing demonstrated intact muscle strength in both upper and lower limbs. There was no evidence of peripheral edema, and clinical evaluation indicated he was euvolemic. He was hospitalized to enable further diagnostic assessment.

Investigations:

The initial set of laboratory investigations demonstrated pronounced hyponatraemia (124 mmol/L) alongside raised creatine kinase (1349 U/L), aspartate aminotransferase (77 U/L), alanine aminotransferase (64 U/L), C-reactive protein (CRP) at 180 mg/L (reference, <3 mg/L), and erythrocyte sedimentation rate at 70 mm/hour (reference, <15 mm/hour), while renal function remained within normal parameters. Additionally, the patient exhibited hyperglycaemia, as evidenced by a plasma glucose of 14.2 mmol/L, and a HbA1c value of 8.2%, reflecting suboptimal glycaemic regulation. Testing for hepatitis B and C returned negative results.

Further assessment of endocrine status showed thyroid-stimulating hormone within the normal range (1.11 mIU/L; reference, 0.50–5.00 mIU/L), free T4 at 1.25 ng/dL (reference, 0.90–1.70 ng/dL), and a normal serum cortisol concentration (42 µg/dL; reference, >6 µg/dL). There was evidence of hypo-osmolality in serum (267 mOsm/kg; reference, <270 mOsm/kg) and a simultaneously increased urine osmolality (900 mOsm/kg; reference, >300 mOsm/kg) with elevated urinary sodium (88 mmol/L; reference, >20 mmol/L), indicative of compromised water clearance. Despite the presence of hypo-osmolar hyponatraemia, the antidiuretic hormone (ADH) was inappropriately high (22.4 pg/mL). Electrocardiogram (ECG), chest radiography, and computed tomography of the head, chest, and abdomen yielded no pathological findings. Collectively, these results were compatible with a diagnosis of SIADH.

By the third day, the patient's headache and vertigo had intensified. Despite the absence of nuchal rigidity or signs of cranial nerve dysfunction, central nervous system inflammation was considered. Magnetic resonance imaging of the brain did not demonstrate significant pathological findings in the hypothalamic region or elsewhere (figure 2). Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis showed a mildly increased opening pressure (17 cmH₂O), elevated protein concentration (0.97 g/L), and a slight lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL). Microbiological assays of the CSF, including cultures and assessments for bacterial, mycobacterial organisms, herpes simplex virus (HSV), varicella zoster virus (VZV), and cryptococcal antigen, returned negative results. Cytological assessment did not detect malignant cells in the CSF.

Additional diagnostic workup was undertaken to rule out systemic disorders. Evaluation for sarcoidosis found lysozyme (5.1 µg/mL; reference 5–10) and angiotensin-converting enzyme concentrations (11.4 U/L; reference 7–25) to be within reference ranges. Hematological malignancy was explored by measuring soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) (704 U/mL; reference, 122–496 U/mL); human T-cell leukaemia virus type 1 antibody was negative.

On the fifth day, the patient developed a painful ulcer on the scrotum (figure 1D). In conjunction with oral aphthous lesions and rashes resembling folliculitis, these features supported the diagnosis of a systemic variant of BD. There was no evidence of pathergy to a pinprick. Ophthalmologic evaluation did not reveal uveitis. The HLA-B51 antigen was detected.

Differential diagnosis:

Upon initial evaluation, the simultaneous presence of fever, headache, general malaise, and hyponatraemia was indicative of a potential central nervous system (CNS) infection or an underlying systemic inflammatory process. Viral meningoencephalitis—especially due to HSV or VZV—was the primary clinical suspicion. Nevertheless, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analyses returned negative for both viral antigens and antibodies, effectively ruling out these pathogens. Consideration was also given to bacterial meningitis, but this was excluded based on the absence of neutrophilic dominance, the presence of only mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, and negative CSF culture results. Tuberculous meningitis was dismissed owing to negative outcomes for both acid-fast bacilli staining and tuberculosis DNA assays.

The possibility of a primary CNS tumor was evaluated because of the neurological presentation. Yet, brain MRI produced normal results, and no malignant cells were identified on CSF cytology. Although soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) levels were found to be modestly raised, this isolated elevation was insufficient evidence to affirm a diagnosis of CNS lymphoma.

Screening for autoimmune disorders was performed but did not yield supporting evidence, given the lack of defining systemic clinical features and negative autoantibody assays. Neurosarcoidosis was considered, acknowledging that early-stage MRI imaging may be non-revealing, but subsequent laboratory workup rendered this diagnosis improbable. The slight rise in sIL-2R was interpreted as non-specific.

The diagnosis of definite Behçet's Disease (BD) was ultimately made, based on the constellation of clinical symptoms: fever, fatigue, oral aphthous ulcers (two points), genital aphthosis (two points), and a folliculitis-like eruption (one point). The patient thus attained a total score of five under the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (where a score ≥4 supports the diagnosis). Genital aphthosis, in particular, is highly indicative of BD, strongly supporting this diagnosis. Following the exclusion of infectious, neoplastic, and autoimmune conditions, the patient was diagnosed with acute neuro-Behçet’s Disease, given CNS manifestations in association with mucocutaneous involvement.

Treatment:

Upon admission (day 1), clinicians considered the possibility of an underlying inflammatory disorder, although its precise cause could not be determined at that time. After obtaining blood cultures, broad-spectrum antibiotics (cefepime) were empirically prescribed to cover potential bacterial pathogens, even though there was no clear evidence of a focal infection.

By day 3, the patient's headache and vertigo had intensified, raising concerns for inflammation or infection within the central nervous system. Empirical acyclovir treatment was initiated to address possible viral etiologies but was discontinued when diagnostic testing excluded HSV and VZV encephalitis.

On day 8, following a diagnosis of systemic BD and acute neuro-BD, colchicine therapy was commenced. Topical corticosteroid ointment was applied to manage oral aphthous ulcers, while silver sulfadiazine cream was utilized for genital and skin lesions. For SIADH, conservative management consisted of restricting fluid intake to less than 800 mL per day, closely monitoring serum sodium concentrations, and ensuring sodium correction was not performed rapidly. Neither hypertonic saline nor vasopressin receptor antagonists were necessary.

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient's fever subsided and his appetite returned the day following the initiation of colchicine. Over the next two weeks, there was a progressive improvement in symptoms such as vertigo, general fatigue, hiccups, and mucocutaneous manifestations. By the 16th day, his serum sodium levels had reached a normal value of 136 mmol/L, and ADH concentrations had fallen to 1.8 pg/mL. Myogenic enzyme levels also normalized during this period.

Notably, systemic corticosteroids and other immunosuppressive medications were not administered during his treatment course. After a hospitalization of one month—during which acute symptoms resolved, hyponatraemia was actively managed, and physical rehabilitation was carried out to address deconditioning—the patient was discharged. Colchicine was continued for roughly four years post-discharge, with neither a relapse of BD nor a return of neurological symptoms throughout this period. Once BD stabilization was achieved, biguanide therapy was introduced, resulting in sustained glycaemic control.

Discussion:

This case is distinguished by the concurrent identification of systemic BD and acute neuro-BD, an occurrence that is infrequently encountered in clinical practice. It also illustrates that SIADH may arise as a complication during acute neuro-BD. The improvement of mucocutaneous, neurological, and electrolyte abnormalities after administration of colchicine alone points toward a potential direct link among BD, neuro-BD, and SIADH. In BD patients manifesting fever or headache in conjunction with hyponatraemia, physicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for acute neuro-BD as a possible etiology.

A particular feature of this report is the concomitant recognition of systemic BD alongside acute neuro-BD, based on the presence of both mucocutaneous lesions and central nervous system involvement. Normally, substantial organ complications, including CNS pathology, emerge approximately four years post-initial BD diagnosis, and presentations with neurological symptoms at onset are seen in less than 5% of cases. Headache attributable directly to neuro-BD is present in only around 10% of patients. Consequently, diagnosing neuro-BD at presentation, as occurred here, presents a significant challenge. Acute neuro-BD is classically characterized by a self-limited meningoencephalitic syndrome, either with or without localized neurological impairment. In this case, MRI of the brain did not reveal pathology at the conventional BD predilection sites such as the hypothalamic region. It is noteworthy that acute neuro-BD can manifest as aseptic meningitis even in the absence of structural brain lesions, and an unremarkable brain MRI, especially early in the disease process, cannot rule out the diagnosis. The absence of imaging abnormalities thus made diagnostic confirmation more difficult. While HLA-B51 genetic testing was undertaken, it did not assist in diagnosis, which is in accordance with international BD criteria that prioritize clinical assessment over HLA-B51 status. This case underscores the necessity of evaluating for neuro-BD in patients exhibiting mucocutaneous involvement alongside atypical or unexplained neurological complaints.

During the early stage of the differential diagnosis, we focused on infection as a potential etiology for CNS inflammation, prompted by the presence of fever of unknown origin, neurological manifestations, and hyponatraemia; nonetheless, no causative pathogen was detected. Alternative sources of CNS inflammation, such as connective tissue disorders and malignancy—including both sarcoidosis and haematologic cancers—were also incorporated into our differential considerations. Consequently, we conducted an initial comprehensive workup that included assessments of autoimmune serological markers, as well as serum ACE and sIL-2R levels, primarily as part of a screening strategy rather than owing to a high index of clinical suspicion. These tests were employed selectively as part of an exclusion process. Ultimately, neither clinical assessment, radiological imaging, nor CSF analysis supported any of these alternative diagnoses, and the ancillary investigations did not play a decisive role in reaching the final diagnosis. It should be noted that ACE is not particularly sensitive for identifying sarcoidosis, and while sIL-2R can be useful in evaluating malignant lymphoma, only minor elevations were noted, which are not specific and may merely signify an ongoing inflammatory response. Notably, the absence of genital ulcerations at symptom onset complicated the recognition of BD, thus necessitating an expansive differential diagnosis. This diagnostic journey underscores the risks of unselective diagnostic testing and reinforces the need for clinicians to pursue targeted investigations aligned with clinical indications, interpreting their results judiciously.

While cases of drug-induced SIADH have been described in individuals with BD treated with agents such as cyclophosphamide, the link between acute neuro-BD and the onset of SIADH is not well characterized. Neuro-BD involves inflammation within the central nervous system, which could play a role in abnormal antidiuretic hormone (ADH) regulation. Evidence from multiple investigations indicates that interleukin-6 (IL-6)—a proinflammatory cytokine commonly elevated during neuroinflammatory processes—is related to enhanced ADH secretion. In the context of BD, dysregulated monocyte activity is known to drive constitutive IL-6 production, and increased IL-6 concentrations have been identified in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) of patients undergoing acute neuro-BD episodes. These observations raise the possibility that IL-6–driven inflammation is implicated in SIADH development under these conditions. In this report, direct measurement of IL-6 in either serum or CSF was not performed; however, the substantially increased serum C-reactive protein (CRP) level may indirectly indicate the presence of IL-6–mediated inflammatory processes. SIADH can occur in association with a variety of disorders, such as CNS inflammatory states, respiratory disease, neoplasms, and particular pharmacotherapies. Despite the temporal relationship noted here, the current evidence does not support a definitive causal relationship between SIADH and acute neuro-BD. Rather, the observed temporal correlation may point to a more general response to CNS inflammation rather than a pathogenesis unique to the underlying disease.

This case further demonstrates the range of complications associated with hyponatraemia resulting from SIADH in the context of acute neuro-BD. Prior literature has noted that persistent hiccups may be a significant indicator of severe hyponatraemia due to SIADH secondary to CNS inflammation, such as meningitis. Furthermore, our assessment included measuring CK levels in response to symptoms like fever, arthralgia, and general fatigue—manifestations that can indicate either inflammatory or metabolic muscle injury. This approach led to the identification of rhabdomyolysis, which, while recognized, is a frequently missed complication of severe hyponatraemia that is not typically screened for in routine evaluations. These symptoms, though described in the literature, are frequently underestimated in clinical practice as important sequelae of significant hyponatraemia. It is essential for clinicians to consider that seemingly unrelated presentations, such as hiccups or rhabdomyolysis, may point to underlying electrolyte imbalances, especially amid neuroinflammatory diseases.

Corticosteroids remain the cornerstone therapy for acute neuro-BD, with the majority of patients experiencing significant clinical improvement. Nevertheless, there are documented instances of spontaneous remission, particularly among patients whose manifestations are restricted to meningoencephalitis-like presentations. In this report, the administration of colchicine as a single agent led to improvement in both mucocutaneous and neurological symptoms, as well as correction of hyponatraemia. Colchicine mediates its anti-inflammatory properties by inhibiting neutrophil trafficking and activation and by downregulating the synthesis of cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-6. Although colchicine is extensively utilized to manage mucocutaneous lesions in BD, evidence from systematic reviews indicates its lack of efficacy in acute neuro-BD, for which immunosuppressive agents are typically necessary to achieve sufficient disease suppression. Indeed, previous cases have reported the use of colchicine only as an adjunct to corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants, and rarely as monotherapy. While the possibility of natural remission in our case cannot be definitively ruled out, our observations raise the possibility that colchicine could serve as a viable therapeutic alternative for acute neuro-BD and associated complications like SIADH, especially in situations where corticosteroid use is not feasible or is contraindicated.

Conclusion:

We reported an uncommon presentation involving the simultaneous onset of BD and acute neuro-BD, further complicated by the development of SIADH. This instance underscores the possibility of SIADH arising secondary to acute neuro-BD, emphasizing the necessity for clinicians to recognize this potential link. Treatment with colchicine alone resulted in prompt resolution of both mucocutaneous and neurological symptoms as well as correction of hyponatraemia, indicating that colchicine could serve as an effective initial therapy in neuro-BD. When encountering BD patients with non-specific central nervous system complaints such as fever and headache in conjunction with hyponatraemia, physicians should strongly consider the possibility of acute neuro-BD coexisting with SIADH.
