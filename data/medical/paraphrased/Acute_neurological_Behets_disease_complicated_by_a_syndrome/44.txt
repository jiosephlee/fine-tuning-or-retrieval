Title: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

Eiichi Kakehi, Yukako Matsumoto, Shigehisa Sakurai, Kazuhiko Kotani

Abstract:

Summary: We present an unusual occurrence of acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) diagnosed simultaneously with systemic Behçet’s disease (BD), meeting criteria for definite BD, and further complicated by syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). A male in his sixth decade arrived with fever, headache, and mucocutaneous findings including oral aphthous ulcers, folliculitis-like eruptions, genital ulcerations, and hyponatraemia. Diagnosis of BD was established based on these mucocutaneous signs. Magnetic resonance imaging of the brain was unremarkable; however, cerebrospinal fluid assessment identified aseptic meningitis, substantiating acute neuro-BD. Laboratory evaluation satisfied diagnostic requirements for SIADH. Monotherapy with colchicine resulted in complete improvement of mucocutaneous manifestations, neurological symptoms, and hyponatraemia, obviating the necessity for corticosteroids or additional immunosuppressive agents. This report indicates a potential link between acute neuro-BD and SIADH, highlighting the need to identify overlapping neurological and endocrine features in patients with new-onset BD.

Background:

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a persistent, multisystem inflammatory condition marked by repeated oral and genital ulcers, uveitis, and cutaneous manifestations. Neurological involvement, termed neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), affects roughly 10%–20% of BD cases and most often develops several years after the initial BD diagnosis. The acute phase of neuro-BD generally manifests as meningoencephalitis accompanied by parenchymal lesions in the brain, with the brainstem frequently implicated. Although it is more typical for neuro-BD to present after a delay, it is uncommon for both BD and neuro-BD to be identified at the same time. In such scenarios, thorough evaluation of both mucocutaneous signs and neurological manifestations, such as headaches, changes in behavior, psychiatric disturbances, and focal neurologic deficits, is warranted.

The syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) represents a frequent etiology of hyponatraemia and commonly arises in the context of central nervous system (CNS) inflammation, neoplasms, or the use of specific medications. Given the inflammatory characteristics of acute neuro-BD, these patients may be predisposed to SIADH. While SIADH has been described in BD patients undergoing therapies like cyclophosphamide, its incidence in cases devoid of drug-related triggers and attributed solely to acute neuro-BD–associated CNS inflammation remains uncertain.

Here, we present an unusual instance where both BD and acute neuro-BD were identified simultaneously, complicated by SIADH. This report underscores the need to consider SIADH as a possible complication of acute neuro-BD, especially in individuals with ambiguous CNS presentations and concurrent hyponatraemia.

Case presentation:

A man of Asian descent in his 60s arrived at the emergency department, reporting three days of fever, headache, general fatigue, reduced appetite, ongoing hiccups, and joint pain localized to his shoulders and ankles. He additionally noted the presence of oral aphthae.

He had a prior diagnosis of type 2 diabetes mellitus, but was not undergoing any treatment, owing to a limited understanding of his disease and a lack of adherence to appropriate diet and exercise routines. There was no notable family history; specifically, no relatives with BD. He drank alcohol occasionally, was a non-smoker, had not recently traveled, and denied exposure to animals or individuals who were ill. His occupation was in metalworking, and he had not recently been sexually active.

Upon evaluation, the patient was conscious but showed signs of tiredness. His recorded vital signs were: temperature at 38.1°C, blood pressure measured at 135/73 mm Hg, heart rate of 119 bpm (regular rhythm), respiratory rate of 19 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation at 96% breathing ambient air. Physical assessment identified oral aphthous ulcers as well as folliculitis-like eruptions over the facial and truncal areas (see figure 1A, B, and C). Cardiopulmonary and abdominal examinations yielded no abnormal findings. Manual testing of muscle strength did not detect any weakness in either the upper or lower limbs. There was an absence of peripheral edema, and clinical evaluation suggested the patient was euvolemic. He was hospitalized for additional workup.

Investigations:

The patient's initial laboratory studies demonstrated significant hyponatraemia with a sodium concentration of 124 mmol/L. Creatine kinase was raised at 1349 U/L, as were aspartate aminotransferase and alanine aminotransferase at 77 U/L and 64 U/L, respectively. The C-reactive protein level was elevated to 180 mg/L (reference, <3 mg/L), and the erythrocyte sedimentation rate measured 70 mm/hour (reference, <15 mm/hour), while renal function remained within normal limits. Hyperglycaemia was observed, with a plasma glucose level of 14.2 mmol/L and HbA1c measured at 8.2%, indicative of suboptimal glycaemic regulation. Testing for hepatitis B and C returned negative results.

Endocrine investigation showed thyroid-stimulating hormone at 1.11 mIU/L (reference, 0.50–5.00 mIU/L), free T4 at 1.25 ng/dL (reference, 0.90–1.70 ng/dL), and serum cortisol at 42 µg/dL (reference, >6 µg/dL), all within standard limits. Serum osmolality was reduced (267 mOsm/kg; reference, <270 mOsm/kg) and urine osmolality was notably high (900 mOsm/kg, reference, >300 mOsm/kg), accompanied by a raised urinary sodium concentration of 88 mmol/L (reference, >20 mmol/L), suggesting compromised water clearance. Although hyponatraemia was present in a hypo-osmolar context, antidiuretic hormone (ADH) levels were increased to 22.4 pg/mL. The ECG appeared normal, and imaging via chest X-ray as well as CT scans of the head, chest, and abdomen yielded no abnormalities. Collectively, these results were consistent with a diagnosis of SIADH.

By the third day, his headache and vertigo had intensified. Despite the absence of neck rigidity or cranial nerve deficits, central nervous system inflammation was considered. MRI of the brain showed no noteworthy changes in the hypothalamic region or other areas (figure 2). Analysis of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) demonstrated a slightly raised opening pressure (17 cmH₂O), elevated protein concentration (0.97 g/L), and a mild increase in lymphocytes (11 cells/µL). Cultures and diagnostic assays for bacterial and mycobacterial organisms, as well as tests for herpes simplex virus (HSV), varicella zoster virus (VZV), and cryptococcal antigen, yielded negative results. CSF cytology did not identify any malignant cells.

Additional work-up was carried out to investigate possible systemic disorders. Evaluation for sarcoidosis showed lysozyme levels within normal limits (5.1 µg/mL; reference range 5–10) and angiotensin-converting enzyme levels were also normal (11.4 U/L; reference 7–25). Screening for hematological malignancies found an elevated soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) (704 U/mL; reference, 122–496 U/mL), and human T-cell leukemia virus type 1 antibody was negative.

On the fifth day, he developed a painful ulcer on the scrotum (figure 1D). Together with oral aphthae and skin lesions resembling folliculitis, these manifestations indicated a systemic variant of BD. There was no pinprick hypersensitivity. Eye examination showed no evidence of uveitis. Testing for HLA-B51 was positive.

Differential diagnosis:

Upon admission, the patient’s constellation of fever, headache, generalized malaise, and hyponatremia raised concerns for a central nervous system (CNS) infection or a systemic inflammatory condition. Initial suspicion centered on viral meningoencephalitis, particularly due to HSV or VZV. Nonetheless, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) studies were negative for both viral antigens and antibodies, effectively ruling these out. Consideration was also given to bacterial meningitis, but the diagnosis was deemed unlikely in view of the lack of neutrophil predominance, only mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, and negative CSF cultures. Tuberculous meningitis was discounted based on negative results for acid-fast staining and tuberculosis DNA assays.

The potential for a primary CNS malignancy was evaluated due to the neurological manifestations; however, MRI of the brain revealed no abnormalities, and CSF cytological examination found no evidence of malignant cells. Although the soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) was slightly elevated, this did not, in isolation, suffice to diagnose CNS lymphoma.

Autoimmune disorders were also contemplated but were eliminated from consideration owing to the absence of hallmark systemic features and negative autoantibody screenings. Neurosarcoidosis remained part of the differential since early imaging can be unremarkable, but subsequent laboratory findings reduced its likelihood. The mildly increased sIL-2R was interpreted as a non-specific marker.

A diagnosis of definite Behçet’s disease (BD) was made based on the clinical picture: fever, fatigue, oral aphthae (two points), genital aphthosis (two points), and a folliculitis-like eruption (one point), summing to five points according to the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (a score of four or more indicates a definite diagnosis). Genital aphthosis, notably, is recognized as a specific sign of BD and was a pivotal finding. With infectious, malignant, and autoimmune etiologies excluded, the patient was ultimately diagnosed with acute neuro-Behçet’s disease, considering CNS symptoms arising in the setting of mucocutaneous manifestations.

Treatment:

On the first day of hospitalization, clinicians suspected an underlying inflammatory disorder, though the precise cause was undetermined. Empirical cefepime therapy commenced after obtaining blood cultures, aiming to address possible bacterial infection even though no clear infectious source was identified.

By day three, the patient’s headache and vertigo had intensified, raising concern for central nervous system inflammation or infection. Acyclovir was prescribed empirically and then discontinued after HSV and VZV encephalitis were excluded based on testing.

On day eight, colchicine therapy was introduced subsequent to the diagnoses of systemic Behçet’s disease (BD) and acute neuro-BD. Topical corticosteroid ointment was applied to oral aphthous ulcers. Genital ulcers and skin lesions were treated using silver sulfadiazine cream. Syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) was managed conservatively by restricting fluid intake to less than 800 mL per day, closely monitoring daily serum sodium levels, and avoiding overly rapid sodium correction. Neither hypertonic saline nor vasopressin receptor antagonists was necessary.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following initiation of colchicine, the patient's fever subsided and his appetite improved within a day. Over the next two weeks, his clinical manifestations—including vertigo, general malaise, hiccups, and mucocutaneous lesions—progressively ameliorated. By the 16th day, serum sodium had returned to normal at 136 mmol/L, and ADH concentrations decreased to 1.8 pg/mL. Additionally, levels of myogenic enzymes normalized.

Notably, systemic corticosteroids and other immunosuppressive agents were not utilized throughout the treatment. After spending one month in the hospital, during which resolution of the acute phase occurred, careful management and monitoring of hyponatraemia took place, and rehabilitation was implemented to address physical deterioration, the patient was discharged. Colchicine treatment was sustained for roughly four years, during which no relapses of BD or neurological symptoms were observed. Once BD remained stable, biguanide therapy was introduced, resulting in continued effective glycaemic control.

Discussion:

This case describes the rare occurrence of systemic Behçet’s disease (BD) and acute neuro-BD being diagnosed at the same time. It further illustrates that acute neuro-BD may be associated with SIADH. The resolution of skin and mucosal lesions, neurologic symptoms, and hyponatraemia after colchicine monotherapy implies a potential direct relationship among BD, neuro-BD, and SIADH. In patients with BD who develop fever or headache along with hyponatraemia, acute neuro-BD should be considered as a possible etiological factor.

What makes this case distinctive is the concurrent identification of both systemic BD and acute neuro-BD, evidenced by mucocutaneous signs and central nervous system involvement. Generally, involvement of major organs, including CNS lesions, tends to develop approximately 4 years after BD is initially diagnosed, and presentation with neurological symptoms is unusual, noted in less than 5% of cases. Headaches directly related to neuro-BD occur in only about 10% of individuals. As a result, diagnosing neuro-BD at onset was particularly difficult in this patient. Acute neuro-BD is most often characterized by a self-limiting meningoencephalitis, sometimes accompanied by focal neurological impairments. In this case, brain MRI failed to show specific abnormalities in typical BD locations, such as the hypothalamus. Acute neuro-BD may manifest as aseptic meningitis without parenchymal lesions, and a normal brain MRI, especially early in the disease course, does not rule out neuro-BD. The absence of radiologic findings added to the diagnostic complexity. Although HLA-B51 typing was conducted, it did not assist in confirming the diagnosis, consistent with global BD diagnostic criteria, which prioritize clinical assessment and do not require HLA-B51. This case emphasizes the need to consider neuro-BD when patients with mucocutaneous symptoms develop vague neurological complaints.

During the initial differential diagnostic assessment, infection was considered the primary cause of CNS inflammation due to the presence of fever of unknown origin, neurological deficits, and hyponatraemia; nevertheless, we were unable to identify a causative organism. Other potential etiologies for CNS inflammation, such as connective tissue disorders and malignancy, including sarcoidosis and hematologic cancers, were also entertained in the differential. Consequently, as part of a comprehensive preliminary workup, we obtained autoimmune serological markers, as well as serum ACE and sIL-2R, as screening tests. These evaluations were performed selectively and served an exclusionary role rather than being guided by a strong clinical suspicion for those conditions. No supportive clinical, imaging, or CSF evidence was observed for these diagnoses, and these assessments did not ultimately aid in establishing the final diagnosis. It is important to note that the sensitivity of ACE for sarcoidosis is limited, and although sIL-2R can assist in the workup of malignant lymphoma, minor elevations lack specificity and can reflect general inflammatory processes. In this case, the lack of genital ulcers in the early phase of illness made the diagnosis of BD less certain, necessitating a broad differential. This approach to diagnosis underscores the value of restrained and rational testing, illustrating that ancillary studies should only be undertaken when clinical indications are present and that their results should be interpreted judiciously.

While SIADH due to medication has been documented in patients with Behçet's disease (BD) receiving agents such as cyclophosphamide, the relationship between acute neuro-BD and SIADH is still not well defined. Neuro-BD involves central nervous system inflammation, which may play a role in abnormal ADH secretion. Prior research has demonstrated that interleukin-6 (IL-6), a proinflammatory cytokine commonly found at elevated levels in neuroinflammatory diseases, correlates with increased ADH production. In the context of BD, abnormal monocyte activity results in spontaneous IL-6 secretion, and heightened concentrations of IL-6 have been reported in the cerebrospinal fluid of individuals during acute neuro-BD episodes. These observations imply that IL-6–driven inflammation could contribute to SIADH in this context. Despite the fact that serum and CSF IL-6 levels were not assessed in the current patient, the significant elevation of serum CRP may be indicative of an IL-6–associated inflammatory process. Although SIADH is linked with a range of underlying etiologies such as CNS inflammation, lung diseases, cancers, and particular medications, the current data do not definitively establish a direct causative relationship between SIADH and acute neuro-BD. The timing of events seen in this case may represent a generalized response to CNS inflammation rather than a mechanism unique to the disease.

This case demonstrates the risks associated with hyponatraemia resulting from SIADH in acute neuro-BD. Prior literature has noted that persistent hiccups can be an important indicator of severe hyponatraemia caused by SIADH due to central nervous system inflammation, including meningitis. As part of our initial workup for fever, arthralgia, and general fatigue, we checked CK levels to evaluate for inflammatory or metabolic muscle injury; this assessment uncovered rhabdomyolysis, a recognized but frequently overlooked consequence of profound hyponatraemia, which is not commonly screened for. Although these findings are established, they are often underestimated clinical consequences of severe hyponatraemia. Clinicians should recognize that symptoms such as hiccups or rhabdomyolysis, even if seemingly unrelated, may signal underlying electrolyte imbalances, especially in patients with neuroinflammatory diseases.

Corticosteroids remain the cornerstone of management for acute neuro-BD, with the majority of patients achieving a positive response. Nonetheless, instances of spontaneous remission have been documented in patients whose presentation is limited to meningoencephalitis-like features. In the current case, administration of colchicine alone led to improvements in both mucocutaneous and neurological symptoms, as well as correction of hyponatraemia. Colchicine’s anti-inflammatory properties are mediated by its ability to inhibit neutrophil migration and activation, and by the suppression of cytokine synthesis such as IL-1β and IL-6. Although colchicine is frequently employed to treat mucocutaneous lesions in BD, systematic reviews indicate that it lacks efficacy for acute neuro-BD, necessitating immunosuppressive therapies for appropriate disease management. Notably, the literature describes colchicine being used as an adjunct alongside corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants, rather than as monotherapy. While the possibility of spontaneous improvement cannot be ruled out for this patient, the observations suggest that colchicine might represent a viable therapeutic approach for acute neuro-BD and associated complications like SIADH, especially in scenarios where corticosteroids are contraindicated or not preferred.

Conclusion:

We reported an unusual presentation involving simultaneous diagnosis of BD and acute neuro-BD, in which SIADH developed as a complication of the neuro-BD. This observation emphasizes that SIADH can arise as a consequence of acute neuro-BD, underscoring the necessity for clinicians to recognize this potential link. Treatment with colchicine alone resulted in swift resolution of mucocutaneous findings, neurological symptoms, and hyponatraemia, indicating colchicine as a feasible early treatment option for neuro-BD. In individuals with BD exhibiting vague CNS manifestations—such as fever and headache—alongside hyponatraemia, clinicians should strongly consider acute neuro-BD complicated by SIADH.
