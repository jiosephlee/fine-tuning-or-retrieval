Title: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

Eiichi Kakehi, Yukako Matsumoto, Shigehisa Sakurai, Kazuhiko Kotani

Abstract:

Summary: We present an unusual case in which acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) was identified at the same time as systemic Behçet’s disease (BD), meeting criteria for definite BD, and accompanied by syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). The patient, a man in his 60s, arrived with fever, headache, and mucocutaneous signs including oral aphthous ulcers, folliculitis-like eruptions, genital lesions, and low sodium levels. BD was diagnosed based on the presence of mucocutaneous features. Brain MRI revealed no pathology, while cerebrospinal fluid analysis indicated aseptic meningitis, supporting the diagnosis of acute neuro-BD. Laboratory assessment confirmed that SIADH criteria were fulfilled. Treatment with colchicine alone was effective in resolving both mucocutaneous and neurological symptoms, as well as hyponatraemia, without the necessity for corticosteroids or additional immunosuppressive agents. This report points to a potential relationship between acute neuro-BD and SIADH, highlighting the importance of recognizing simultaneous neurological and endocrine involvement in patients with recently diagnosed BD.

Background:

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a persistent, multisystem inflammatory condition marked by recurrent oral and genital ulcerations, uveitis, and various dermatological manifestations. Neurological complications, known as neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), develop in roughly 10%–20% of BD patients and usually arise years following the initial presentation. The acute form of neuro-BD most frequently manifests as meningoencephalitis with brain parenchymal involvement, predominantly affecting the brainstem. Concurrent diagnosis of BD and neuro-BD is uncommon, as neuro-BD most often presents after BD has been established. In these rare simultaneous presentations, both mucocutaneous signs and neurological findings—such as headaches, alterations in behavior, psychiatric disturbances, and focal neurological impairments—should be thoroughly evaluated.

The syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) represents a frequent etiology of hyponatraemia and is frequently linked to central nervous system (CNS) inflammation, neoplasms, or particular drug therapies. Acute neuro-BD’s inflammatory process may predispose patients to SIADH. While SIADH has previously been observed in individuals with BD undergoing treatment with agents like cyclophosphamide, its development purely from CNS inflammation associated with acute neuro-BD, independent of medication exposure, has not been well established.

Herein, we describe an unusual case in which BD and acute neuro-BD were identified simultaneously, complicated by SIADH. This case underscores the need to include SIADH in the differential for acute neuro-BD patients who exhibit unexplained CNS symptoms accompanied by hyponatraemia.

Case presentation:

A man of Asian descent in his 60s came to the emergency department after experiencing three days of fever, headache, general fatigue, decreased appetite, ongoing hiccups, and joint pain involving the shoulders and ankles. He also complained of oral aphthous ulcers.

His past medical history was notable for type 2 diabetes mellitus, for which he was not receiving any therapy due to poor awareness of his diagnosis and inadequate adherence to dietary or exercise guidelines. There was no family history of BD, and none of his relatives were similarly affected. He occasionally drank alcohol, was a non-smoker, and denied recent travel or contact with animals or ill persons. His occupation was in the metalworking field, and he had not had recent sexual encounters.

Upon assessment, the patient was conscious but exhibited signs of tiredness. His vital measurements included a temperature of 38.1°C, blood pressure of 135/73 mm Hg, regular heart rate at 119 bpm, respiratory rate of 19 per minute, and oxygen saturation at 96% while breathing ambient air. The physical examination noted oral aphthae and rashes resembling folliculitis on his face and torso (figure 1A, B and C). Both cardiac and pulmonary examinations, as well as abdominal evaluation, were without abnormal findings. Manual muscle testing showed no weakness in either the upper or lower limbs. Peripheral edema was absent, and clinical assessment of his volume status indicated euvolemia. He was hospitalized for additional diagnostic workup.

Investigations:

The initial laboratory investigations demonstrated significant hyponatremia (124 mmol/L), increased creatine kinase (1349 U/L), aspartate aminotransferase (77 U/L), alanine aminotransferase (64 U/L), markedly elevated C-reactive protein (180 mg/L; reference, <3 mg/L), and a raised erythrocyte sedimentation rate (70 mm/hour; reference, <15 mm/hour), with renal function remaining within normal limits. The patient was also found to have hyperglycemia (plasma glucose 14.2 mmol/L) and a high HbA1c of 8.2%, indicative of poor blood sugar management. Hepatitis B and C serologies were negative.

Assessment of endocrine parameters showed that thyroid-stimulating hormone (1.11 mIU/L; reference, 0.50–5.00 mIU/L), free T4 (1.25 ng/dL; reference, 0.90–1.70 ng/dL), and serum cortisol (42 µg/dL; reference, >6 µg/dL) were all within reference ranges. Serum osmolality was decreased (267 mOsm/kg; reference, <270 mOsm/kg), while urine osmolality was significantly elevated (900 mOsm/kg; reference, >300 mOsm/kg), alongside increased urinary sodium (88 mmol/L; reference, >20 mmol/L), findings which indicate disrupted water excretion. Even though the patient had hypo-osmolar hyponatremia, antidiuretic hormone (ADH) was raised (22.4 pg/mL). Electrocardiogram results were unremarkable, as were chest radiographs and CT scans of the head, chest, and abdomen. Collectively, these results were consistent with a diagnosis of SIADH.

By the third day, the patient experienced worsening headache and vertigo. Despite the absence of neck rigidity or cranial nerve involvement, central nervous system inflammation was considered. MRI of the brain showed no significant changes in the hypothalamus or other areas (figure 2). Cerebrospinal fluid analysis indicated a slightly raised opening pressure (17 cmH₂O), elevated protein (0.97 g/L), and mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL). Cultures and assays for bacterial, mycobacterial pathogens, HSV, VZV, and cryptococcal antigen in the CSF returned negative results. Cytological assessment of the CSF did not identify any malignant cells.

Additional workup to assess for systemic conditions was undertaken. Evaluation for sarcoidosis showed normal lysozyme (5.1 µg/mL; reference range 5–10) and angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) levels (11.4 U/L; reference 7–25). Investigations for hematologic malignancy included soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R), which was elevated at 704 U/mL (reference, 122–496 U/mL), with negative results for human T-cell leukemia virus type 1 antibody.

On the fifth day, a painful ulcer appeared on his scrotum (figure 1D). Combined with oral aphthous ulcers and folliculitis-like skin lesions, these findings were indicative of a systemic variant of BD. There was no response to pinprick hypersensitivity testing. Ocular examination showed no evidence of uveitis. HLA-B51 was detected.

Differential diagnosis:

Upon arrival, the combination of fever, headache, generalized malaise, and hyponatremia pointed toward a possible central nervous system infection or a systemic inflammatory process. Initial suspicion centered on viral meningoencephalitis, specifically from HSV or VZV. Yet, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis did not identify viral antigens or antibodies, effectively dismissing these diagnoses. Bacterial meningitis was also contemplated but discounted due to the lack of neutrophilic predominance, the presence of only mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, and negative CSF cultures. The possibility of tuberculous meningitis was ruled out as acid-fast staining and tuberculosis DNA analysis were negative.

A primary central nervous system neoplasm was another consideration given the neurologic manifestations. Nevertheless, brain MRI demonstrated no pathology, and CSF cytology was void of malignant cells. Although serum soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) levels were slightly elevated, this did not suffice to substantiate CNS lymphoma as a diagnosis.

Evaluation for autoimmune conditions yielded negative results based on both the lack of typical systemic features and unremarkable screening for autoantibodies. Neurosarcoidosis remained on the list of potential diagnoses, since MRI can be unremarkable early in its course; however, later laboratory findings argued against this condition. The slight elevation in sIL-2R was interpreted as non-specific.

This individual was ultimately diagnosed with definite Behçet's disease (BD), based on clinical characteristics such as fever, fatigue, oral aphthae (two points), genital aphthosis (two points), and a folliculitis-like skin eruption (one point). Per the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease, a sum of five points (with four or more signifying definite BD) was recorded. Notably, genital aphthosis is recognized as a particularly specific manifestation of BD, further reinforcing the diagnosis. After ruling out infectious, neoplastic, and autoimmune etiologies, a diagnosis of acute neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) was determined, reflecting central nervous system involvement in the setting of accompanying mucocutaneous signs.

Treatment:

On the first day of hospitalization, clinicians suspected an underlying inflammatory disorder, though the precise cause was not identified. Broad-spectrum antibiotics with cefepime were started empirically after obtaining blood cultures to address a possible bacterial infection, even though no distinct infectious source was found.

By the third day, the patient experienced increased headache and vertigo, raising concerns for central nervous system inflammation or infection. Empirical treatment with acyclovir was begun, but it was discontinued after laboratory evaluation excluded HSV and VZV encephalitis.

On day eight, colchicine therapy was initiated following the diagnosis of systemic Behçet’s disease and acute neuro-Behçet’s disease. Aphthous lesions in the oral cavity were managed with topical corticosteroid ointment. Genital ulcerations and skin lesions were treated using silver sulfadiazine cream. SIADH management consisted of fluid restriction (<800 mL/day), meticulous daily tracking of serum sodium levels, and avoiding rapid sodium correction. Neither hypertonic saline nor vasopressin receptor antagonists were deemed necessary.

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient’s fever subsided and his appetite increased within one day of initiating colchicine therapy. Over the subsequent two weeks, his remaining symptoms—including vertigo, general malaise, persistent hiccups, and mucocutaneous manifestations—showed steady improvement. By the sixteenth day, his serum sodium reached a normal level of 136 mmol/L, while ADH concentrations decreased to 1.8 pg/mL. Myogenic enzyme values also normalized.

Notably, systemic corticosteroids or alternative immunosuppressive agents were not administered during his treatment. Following a hospitalization period of one month, in which acute symptoms were managed, hyponatremia was closely monitored and addressed, and rehabilitation for physical deconditioning was undertaken, the patient was discharged. Colchicine was maintained for about four years, during which neither Behçet’s disease nor neurological signs recurred. Once Behçet’s disease was stable, biguanide therapy began, resulting in effective maintenance of glycemic control.

Discussion:

This report describes a unique presentation involving the concurrent diagnosis of systemic Behçet’s disease (BD) and acute neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), an uncommon clinical circumstance. The case further illustrates that acute neuro-BD may be accompanied by syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). Improvement of mucocutaneous lesions, neurological signs, and hyponatraemia after the initiation of colchicine monotherapy raises the possibility of a direct relationship between BD, neuro-BD, and SIADH. For BD patients presenting with fever or headache and concomitant hyponatraemia, acute neuro-BD should be included in the differential diagnosis.

This scenario is distinguished by the concurrent onset of systemic BD and acute neuro-BD, substantiated by both mucocutaneous and central nervous system (CNS) features. In most instances, involvement of major organs such as the CNS emerges approximately 4 years after BD is initially diagnosed; presenting with neurological features at disease onset is rare, seen in fewer than 5% of cases. Headache attributable directly to neuro-BD is reported in only about 10% of patients. These factors contributed to the difficulty in identifying neuro-BD at the outset in this patient. Typically, acute neuro-BD manifests as a meningoencephalitic syndrome that is often self-limited, with or without focal neurological symptoms. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain in this case did not disclose any distinctive changes at common sites for BD lesions, including the hypothalamus. Acute neuro-BD can present clinically as aseptic meningitis in the absence of brain parenchymal changes, and a normal brain MRI early in the course does not rule out the diagnosis; this further complicated identification of the condition. Although the patient underwent HLA-B51 genotyping, the result was not contributory to diagnosis, consistent with international classification guidelines for BD, which emphasize clinical findings rather than HLA-B51 status. This case underlines the necessity to consider neuro-BD when non-specific neurological complaints arise in patients exhibiting mucocutaneous features.

At the outset of evaluating the differential diagnosis, we favored infection as the underlying cause of CNS inflammation, given the presence of unexplained fever, neurologic manifestations, and hyponatraemia; nevertheless, no particular pathogen was detected. Alternative etiologies of CNS inflammation were also entertained, such as connective tissue disorders and malignancies, with sarcoidosis and hematologic malignancy included in our differential. Accordingly, we undertook an initial, broad workup that involved testing for autoimmune serological markers, serum ACE, and sIL-2R as screening tools. These tests were carried out in a limited capacity within an exclusion-based strategy, rather than owing to a significant clinical suspicion. There was no evidence from clinical assessment, imaging, or CSF analysis to support these diagnoses, and the findings from these investigations had little impact on arriving at the definitive diagnosis. Since ACE testing offers poor sensitivity for sarcoidosis and sIL-2R may be of value in assessing malignant lymphoma, small increases in sIL-2R are non-specific and can simply indicate ongoing inflammation. In this patient, the lack of genital ulceration at the initial presentation rendered a diagnosis of BD ambiguous, necessitating a comprehensive differential. This approach underscores the need to avoid routine, non-selective testing and reinforces the principle that such evaluations should be undertaken only when justified by relevant clinical indicators, with careful interpretation of their results.

While drug-induced SIADH has previously been documented in individuals with BD undergoing treatment with agents such as cyclophosphamide, the connection between acute neuro-BD and SIADH remains unclear. Neuro-BD involves inflammatory processes within the central nervous system, which could play a role in abnormal regulation of ADH secretion. Multiple investigations have indicated that interleukin-6 (IL-6), a proinflammatory cytokine often elevated in neuroinflammatory conditions, is linked with enhanced ADH release. In BD, atypical activation of monocytes leads to spontaneous IL-6 synthesis, and increased IL-6 levels have been observed in the cerebrospinal fluid of patients experiencing acute neuro-BD. These observations imply that IL-6-driven inflammation may be a contributing factor to SIADH in this clinical context. In the current case, IL-6 concentrations in serum and CSF were not assessed; however, the significantly raised serum CRP level may indicate an underlying inflammatory process mediated by IL-6. SIADH is related to several etiologies, including CNS inflammation, lung diseases, malignancies, and certain pharmaceuticals. Nevertheless, the evidence presented here is insufficient to confirm a direct causative relationship between SIADH and acute neuro-BD. The chronological correlation noted may represent a general response to CNS inflammation rather than a mechanism specific to this disease.

This case highlights the possible adverse outcomes associated with hyponatraemia induced by SIADH during acute neuro-BD. Prior publications have noted that persistent hiccups can provide a key clinical indication of marked hyponatraemia stemming from SIADH linked to CNS inflammation, such as meningitis. Furthermore, as part of the initial assessment for fever, arthralgia, and general fatigue—symptoms potentially indicative of inflammatory or metabolic muscle injury—we obtained CK levels, which identified rhabdomyolysis. This is a documented but frequently neglected consequence of severe hyponatraemia, as it is not commonly evaluated. These symptoms are recognized but often insufficiently considered effects of profound hyponatraemia. It is important for clinicians to recognize that seemingly unrelated findings, like hiccups or rhabdomyolysis, may signal underlying electrolyte abnormalities, especially within the context of neuroinflammatory disorders.

Corticosteroids remain the primary therapeutic approach for acute neuro-BD, with the majority of patients exhibiting positive responses. Nevertheless, some cases limited to meningoencephalitis-like presentations have shown spontaneous improvement. In this particular instance, administration of colchicine alone led to amelioration of mucocutaneous and neurological features, as well as correction of hyponatraemia. Colchicine mediates its anti-inflammatory action through inhibition of neutrophil migration and activation and by downregulating cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-6. Although colchicine is commonly used to manage mucocutaneous lesions in BD, evidence from systematic reviews indicates that colchicine is not effective for acute neuro-BD, and that adequate disease management typically necessitates the use of immunosuppressive therapies. Previous reports describe colchicine utilized in combination with corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive agents, rather than as a standalone treatment. While spontaneous remission cannot be definitively excluded in the present case, the observations suggest that colchicine could represent a potential therapeutic alternative for acute neuro-BD and associated complications such as SIADH, especially in situations where corticosteroid treatment is contraindicated or not preferred.

Conclusion:

We reported a unique instance in which Behçet’s disease (BD) and acute neuro-BD were identified at the same time, and the course was further complicated by the development of SIADH. This case underscores that SIADH can emerge as a complication of acute neuro-BD, emphasizing the need for healthcare professionals to recognize this possible connection. Treatment with colchicine alone resulted in rapid resolution of mucocutaneous and neurological symptoms, as well as correction of hyponatraemia, indicating that colchicine could be a viable initial therapy for early neuro-BD. When patients with BD exhibit non-specific central nervous system symptoms—such as fever and headache—together with hyponatraemia, clinicians should be vigilant for the presence of acute neuro-BD associated with SIADH.
