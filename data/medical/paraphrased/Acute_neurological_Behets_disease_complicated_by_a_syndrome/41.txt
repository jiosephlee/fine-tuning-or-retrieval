Title: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

Eiichi Kakehi, Yukako Matsumoto, Shigehisa Sakurai, Kazuhiko Kotani

Abstract:

Summary: We describe an uncommon case involving acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) diagnosed simultaneously with systemic Behçet’s disease (BD), meeting criteria for definite BD, and complicated by the development of syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). A male patient in his sixties presented with fever, headache, and mucocutaneous symptoms, including oral aphthae, folliculitis-like eruptions, genital ulcers, and hyponatraemia. Diagnosis of BD was established on the basis of the mucocutaneous findings. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain revealed no abnormalities, and analysis of cerebrospinal fluid demonstrated features consistent with aseptic meningitis, supporting the diagnosis of acute neuro-BD. Laboratory evaluations satisfied the criteria for SIADH. Treatment with colchicine alone resulted in improvement of mucocutaneous manifestations, neurologic symptoms, and hyponatraemia, without requiring corticosteroids or additional immunosuppressive therapy. This report highlights a potential link between acute neuro-BD and SIADH, and emphasizes the need to be vigilant for both neurologic and endocrine complications in patients who are newly diagnosed with BD.

Background:

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a persistent, multisystemic inflammatory condition marked by episodic oral and genital ulcerations, uveitis, and cutaneous manifestations. Neurological complications, collectively termed neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), are observed in approximately 10%–20% of individuals and generally arise several years following the initial BD diagnosis. Acute presentations of neuro-BD typically involve meningoencephalitis with parenchymal lesions predominantly affecting the brainstem. While neuro-BD more frequently appears after a delay, it is unusual for BD and neuro-BD to be diagnosed at the same time. In instances where they coincide, it is important for healthcare providers to evaluate both mucocutaneous signs and neurological presentations such as cephalalgia, changes in behavior, psychiatric manifestations, and localized neurological deficits.

The syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) represents a common etiological factor in cases of hyponatraemia, frequently resulting from inflammation within the central nervous system (CNS), neoplasms, or certain medications. Due to the inflammatory component of acute neuro-BD, patients may face a heightened risk for the development of SIADH. While SIADH has been observed in BD individuals undergoing therapies including cyclophosphamide, its emergence solely attributable to CNS inflammation associated with acute neuro-BD—independent of pharmacological inducers—remains not well established.

We describe an uncommon case wherein both BD and acute neuro-BD were identified at the same time, with SIADH as a complication. This case underscores the necessity of recognizing SIADH as a possible feature of acute neuro-BD, especially in patients with hyponatraemia and vague CNS complaints.

Case presentation:

A man of Asian descent in his 60s arrived at the emergency department after experiencing fever, headache, malaise, reduced appetite, continuous hiccups, and joint pain in the shoulders and ankles for three days. He also noted the presence of oral aphthae.

His past medical history included type 2 diabetes mellitus, which was untreated due to limited awareness of his illness and a lack of adherence to recommended dietary or exercise practices. There was no relevant family history, and no relatives with BD. He occasionally drank alcohol, was a non-smoker, had not recently traveled, nor had contact with animals or sick persons. He worked in metalworking and had not had recent sexual encounters.

During evaluation, the patient was awake but showed signs of fatigue. His vital signs were as follows: temperature measured at 38.1°C, blood pressure recorded at 135/73 mm Hg, heart rate was regular at 119 bpm, respiratory rate counted at 19 per minute, and oxygen saturation was 96% while breathing ambient air. Physical assessment identified the presence of oral aphthae and rashes resembling folliculitis on the face and trunk (see figure 1A, B, and C). Examination of the heart, lungs, and abdomen did not yield any significant findings. Muscle strength testing revealed no deficits in either upper or lower limbs. No peripheral oedema was noted, and he was clinically judged to be euvolemic. He was subsequently hospitalized for additional workup.

Investigations:

Initial laboratory evaluation demonstrated significant hyponatraemia at 124 mmol/L, alongside increased creatine kinase (1349 U/L), aspartate aminotransferase (77 U/L), alanine aminotransferase (64 U/L), markedly raised C-reactive protein (CRP) at 180 mg/L (reference, <3 mg/L), and elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate at 70 mm/hour (reference, <15 mm/hour), while renal function remained within normal limits. The patient exhibited hyperglycaemia with a plasma glucose of 14.2 mmol/L and a raised HbA1c of 8.2%, indicating inadequate glycaemic management. Testing for hepatitis B and C was negative.

Assessment of endocrine parameters revealed thyroid-stimulating hormone at 1.11 mIU/L (reference, 0.50–5.00 mIU/L), free T4 at 1.25 ng/dL (reference, 0.90–1.70 ng/dL), and serum cortisol at 42 µg/dL (reference, >6 µg/dL), all within normal reference ranges. Serum osmolality was decreased at 267 mOsm/kg (reference, <270 mOsm/kg), while urine osmolality was markedly elevated at 900 mOsm/kg (reference, >300 mOsm/kg), and urinary sodium was high at 88 mmol/L (reference, >20 mmol/L), reflecting defective water clearance. Despite hypo-osmolar hyponatraemia, antidiuretic hormone (ADH) was increased to 22.4 pg/mL. Electrocardiogram findings were normal, and imaging studies, including chest X-ray and CT of the head, chest, and abdomen, revealed no abnormalities. Collectively, these results were consistent with a diagnosis of SIADH.

On the third day, his headache and vertigo intensified. Despite the absence of neck rigidity or involvement of cranial nerves, central nervous system inflammation was considered. Magnetic resonance imaging of the brain did not show notable changes in the hypothalamus or other areas (figure 2). Cerebrospinal fluid evaluation demonstrated a slightly elevated opening pressure (17 cmH₂O), increased protein concentration (0.97 g/L), and a mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL). Cultures and diagnostic tests for bacterial and mycobacterial organisms, as well as herpes simplex virus (HSV), varicella zoster virus (VZV), and cryptococcal antigen, returned negative results. Cytological assessment of the CSF did not reveal any malignant cells.

Additional testing was carried out to search for underlying systemic pathology. Assessment for sarcoidosis showed a lysozyme of 5.1 µg/mL (reference range 5–10) and an angiotensin-converting enzyme level of 11.4 U/L (reference range 7–25), both within normal limits. Evaluation for hematologic malignancy revealed an elevated soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) at 704 U/mL (reference, 122–496 U/mL), while testing for human T-cell leukaemia virus type 1 antibody was negative.

On the fifth day, a painful ulcer developed on his scrotum (figure 1D). Together with oral aphthae and rash resembling folliculitis, these clinical features raised suspicion for a systemic form of Behçet’s Disease (BD). Pinprick hypersensitivity was not present. Eye examination demonstrated no signs of uveitis. HLA-B51 was positive.

Differential diagnosis:

Upon admission, the presence of fever, headache, systemic malaise, and hyponatremia indicated the possibility of a central nervous system infection or a systemic inflammatory condition. Initial consideration was given to viral meningoencephalitis, most notably from HSV or VZV; however, cerebrospinal fluid studies yielded negative results for viral antigens and antibodies, thereby ruling out these pathogens. Bacterial meningitis was also entertained as a diagnosis but was dismissed due to the lack of neutrophil predominance, the presence of only mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, and sterile CSF cultures. Evaluation for tuberculous meningitis was negative as both acid-fast bacilli staining and tuberculosis DNA tests did not show evidence of infection.

A primary malignancy of the CNS was another diagnostic consideration in light of the neurological manifestations; however, brain MRI was unremarkable and CSF cytology did not detect malignant cells. Although a slight elevation in sIL-2R was observed, it was insufficient by itself to justify a diagnosis of CNS lymphoma.

Assessment for autoimmune conditions did not reveal supporting evidence, as the patient lacked typical systemic features and all screening autoantibody assays were negative. Neurosarcoidosis was also kept in the differential diagnosis, acknowledging that early-stage imaging can be unremarkable; nevertheless, further laboratory studies did not substantiate this disorder. The marginally increased sIL-2R was regarded as a non-specific laboratory abnormality.

The diagnosis of definite Behçet’s disease (BD) was ultimately made based on the clinical presentation, which included fever and fatigue, oral aphthous lesions (two points), genital aphthosis (two points), and a folliculitis-like eruption (one point). Following the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease, the patient accrued a score of five points (definite disease is ≥4 points). Particularly, the identification of genital aphthosis is highly characteristic of BD, and this collection of findings was strongly suggestive. After comprehensive exclusion of infectious, neoplastic, and autoimmune etiologies, a diagnosis of acute neuro-Behçet’s disease was confirmed due to neurological manifestations in association with mucocutaneous involvement.

Treatment:

On the first day after admission, clinicians suspected the presence of an inflammatory disorder, though its precise origin was indeterminate. Empirical treatment with cefepime was commenced after obtaining blood cultures, aiming to address a possible bacterial etiology despite no definitive infectious source being identified.

By day 3, the patient's headache and vertigo intensified, raising concern for central nervous system inflammation or infection. Empirical acyclovir therapy was provided but later discontinued once laboratory tests excluded HSV and VZV encephalitis.

Colchicine was introduced on day 8, following the diagnoses of systemic Behçet's disease (BD) and acute neuro-BD. Topical corticosteroid ointment was used for oral aphthous ulcers, while silver sulfadiazine cream was applied to genital ulcers and skin lesions. Management of SIADH consisted of conservative fluid restriction (<800 mL/day), vigilant daily assessment of serum sodium levels, and avoidance of rapid sodium correction; hypertonic saline and vasopressin receptor antagonists were deemed unnecessary.

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient experienced resolution of fever and improved appetite the day following the initiation of colchicine. Over the next two weeks, vertigo, general malaise, hiccups, and mucocutaneous lesions gradually subsided. By the sixteenth day, his serum sodium was restored to 136 mmol/L, ADH levels dropped to 1.8 pg/mL, and myogenic enzyme values normalized.

Notably, systemic corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive agents were not administered throughout the course of therapy. After a one-month hospitalization, during which he recovered from the acute phase, received vigilant management of hyponatraemia, and underwent rehabilitation for reduced physical function, the patient was discharged. Colchicine treatment was maintained for roughly four years, and during this period, there was no relapse of BD or emergence of neurological manifestations. Once BD was stabilized, biguanide therapy was introduced, resulting in sustained effective glycaemic control.

Discussion:

This report describes a rare clinical presentation in which systemic BD and acute neuro-BD were diagnosed concurrently. It further illustrates that SIADH can arise as a complication in the setting of acute neuro-BD. The prompt improvement of mucocutaneous and neurological symptoms, as well as hyponatraemia, following treatment with colchicine monotherapy points toward a possible direct relationship among BD, neuro-BD, and SIADH. Thus, when encountering a patient with BD who develops fever or headache in association with hyponatraemia, clinicians should include acute neuro-BD in the differential diagnosis.

The case is distinguished by the co-occurrence of systemic BD and acute neuro-BD, evidenced by both mucocutaneous manifestations and central nervous system involvement. Usually, major organ involvement, such as lesions within the CNS, presents approximately 4 years after the initial BD diagnosis, with neurological symptoms at disease onset being infrequent—observed in less than 5% of cases. Only about 10% of patients experience headaches attributable directly to neuro-BD. This rarity contributed to the diagnostic difficulty of neuro-BD at presentation in this patient. Acute neuro-BD generally manifests as a self-limited meningoencephalitis and may or may not be associated with focal neurological signs. In this case, neuroimaging with brain MRI did not show abnormalities at standard BD sites, including the hypothalamic region. Acute neuro-BD can appear as aseptic meningitis even in the absence of parenchymal lesions, and a normal MRI during the early disease phase does not eliminate neuro-BD as a diagnosis. The absence of radiological abnormalities added to the complexity of diagnosis. HLA-B51 testing was conducted but did not aid in making the diagnosis, which is consistent with international criteria for BD that do not mandate HLA-B51 testing and focus predominantly on clinical presentation. This case underscores the need to consider neuro-BD in patients displaying mucocutaneous lesions who also develop non-specific neurological complaints.

During the early stage of the differential diagnosis, infection was considered the primary potential etiology of CNS inflammation due to the concurrent presentation of unexplained fever, neurological manifestations, and hyponatraemia; nonetheless, no definitive infectious agent was detected. Other sources of CNS inflammation were evaluated, such as connective tissue disorders and neoplastic processes, including sarcoidosis and hematological malignancies. Accordingly, a broad assessment was pursued, which incorporated measurement of autoimmune serological markers, serum ACE, and sIL-2R, primarily for screening purposes. These studies were selected to systematically rule out conditions rather than based on a high pre-test probability informed by clinical findings. Ultimately, there were no supportive data from clinical assessment, imaging, or cerebrospinal fluid analyses to indicate these diagnoses, and the ancillary investigations did not significantly aid in establishing the final diagnosis. The sensitivity of ACE for sarcoidosis is known to be low, and although sIL-2R can be useful in the assessment of malignant lymphoma, mild elevations lack specificity and can simply indicate non-specific inflammation. In the current case, the initial absence of genital ulceration rendered a diagnosis of BD unclear, prompting extensive consideration of other possibilities. This approach underscores the need to avoid unselective diagnostic testing and reinforces that investigations should be pursued when clinically indicated, with results interpreted judiciously.

While drug-induced SIADH has been documented among individuals with BD who are treated with agents such as cyclophosphamide, the link between acute neuro-BD and SIADH is not well characterized. Neuro-BD is distinguished by inflammation within the central nervous system, which might result in altered secretion of ADH. Multiple investigations have indicated that interleukin-6 (IL-6), a proinflammatory cytokine commonly elevated in neuroinflammatory diseases, correlates with increased ADH secretion. In BD, dysregulated activation of monocytes triggers spontaneous production of IL-6, with high levels of IL-6 found in the cerebrospinal fluid of patients undergoing acute neuro-BD episodes. These observations imply that IL-6-driven inflammatory processes may play a role in the development of SIADH under these circumstances. Although IL-6 was not measured in serum or CSF in this particular case, the pronounced rise in serum CRP may signal an underlying IL-6-related inflammatory activity. While SIADH may occur in conjunction with a variety of conditions, such as CNS inflammation, lung disorders, malignancies, and some medications, the evidence here does not establish a definitive causal relationship between SIADH and acute neuro-BD. The timing of SIADH onset in this instance may indicate a generalized reaction to CNS inflammation, rather than a mechanism unique to the disease.

This case demonstrates the possible complications arising from hyponatraemia due to SIADH in acute neuro-BD. Prior publications have indicated that persistent hiccups can be a significant clinical indicator of severe hyponatraemia caused by SIADH as a result of CNS inflammation, such as meningitis. Furthermore, during the initial assessment for fever, arthralgia, and general fatigue—which may be attributable to inflammatory or metabolic muscle injury—we measured CK levels and discovered rhabdomyolysis. This is a known but frequently neglected complication associated with severe hyponatraemia that is not routinely assessed. Although these clinical features are recognized, their association with profound hyponatraemia is often overlooked. It is important for healthcare professionals to recognize that symptoms like hiccups or rhabdomyolysis, which may initially seem unrelated, can indicate underlying disturbances in electrolyte balance, especially in patients with neuroinflammatory diseases.

Corticosteroids remain the primary therapy for acute neuro-BD, with the majority of patients showing a good response. Nonetheless, spontaneous improvement has been documented in cases that manifest only with meningoencephalitis-like features. In the current case, treatment with colchicine alone resulted in improvement of mucocutaneous symptoms, neurological findings, and hyponatraemia. Colchicine’s anti-inflammatory properties arise from its inhibition of neutrophil migration and activation, as well as suppression of cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-6. While colchicine is commonly prescribed for mucocutaneous lesions in BD, evidence from systematic reviews indicates that it is not effective in acute neuro-BD, for which immunosuppressive medications are typically necessary to achieve disease control. Previous reports describe colchicine being used in combination with corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants rather than alone. Although spontaneous remission cannot be ruled out in this instance, the results suggest colchicine could serve as a beneficial therapy for acute neuro-BD and associated complications like SIADH, especially when corticosteroids are contraindicated or not preferred.

Conclusion:

We report a rare instance of simultaneous diagnosis of BD and acute neuro-BD, in which the patient developed SIADH as a complication of neuro-BD. This case highlights that SIADH can manifest secondary to acute neuro-BD and underscores the need for clinicians to be mindful of this possible relationship. Administration of colchicine alone resulted in swift resolution of mucocutaneous findings, neurological deficits, and hyponatraemia, indicating that colchicine may serve as an effective treatment option during the initial stage of neuro-BD. For BD patients presenting with vague central nervous system symptoms like headache and fever along with hyponatraemia, clinicians should strongly consider acute neuro-BD complicated by SIADH in their differential diagnosis.
