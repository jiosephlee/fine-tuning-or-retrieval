Title: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

Eiichi Kakehi, Yukako Matsumoto, Shigehisa Sakurai, Kazuhiko Kotani

Abstract:

Summary: We present an uncommon instance of acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) that was identified at the same time as systemic Behçet’s disease (BD), meeting criteria for definite BD, and was further complicated by the development of syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). A male in his 60s exhibited fever, headache, and mucocutaneous manifestations such as oral aphthous ulcers, folliculitis-like skin eruptions, genital ulcerations, and low serum sodium levels. The BD diagnosis was made on the basis of the mucocutaneous symptoms. Magnetic resonance imaging of the brain did not demonstrate any abnormalities, while analysis of cerebrospinal fluid was indicative of aseptic meningitis, which was consistent with acute neuro-BD. Additional laboratory assessments fulfilled the criteria for SIADH. Treatment with colchicine alone resulted in improvement of mucocutaneous, neurological, and electrolyte abnormalities without the use of corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive agents. This report indicates a potential link between acute neuro-BD and SIADH and highlights the need to consider both neurological and endocrine presentations in individuals newly diagnosed with BD.

Background:

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a persistent, multisystem inflammatory condition marked by the recurrence of oral and genital ulcerations, uveitis, and cutaneous manifestations. Neurological involvement, termed neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), affects approximately 10%–20% of individuals with BD and generally arises several years following the initial diagnosis. The acute form of neuro-BD most frequently manifests as meningoencephalitis along with parenchymal lesions, most often involving the brainstem. Although neuro-BD commonly develops after a delay, the simultaneous diagnosis of BD and neuro-BD is uncommon. In these instances, clinicians should thoroughly evaluate both the mucocutaneous features and neurological presentations such as headaches, altered behavior, psychiatric manifestations, and focal neurological deficits.

The syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) represents a recognized etiology of hyponatraemia, often resulting from inflammation within the central nervous system (CNS), malignancy, or specific medications. Owing to the inflammatory process seen in acute neuro-BD, patients may be susceptible to SIADH. While SIADH has been described among BD patients receiving agents like cyclophosphamide, its development in the setting of acute neuro-BD absent medication triggers, solely attributable to CNS inflammation, is not well established.

We present an unusual instance of simultaneous diagnosis of BD and acute neuro-BD complicated by SIADH. This report underscores the necessity of considering SIADH in the differential for acute neuro-BD, especially in patients with unexplained CNS findings and hyponatraemia.

Case presentation:

A man of Asian descent in his 60s arrived at the emergency department, reporting three days of fever, headache, widespread fatigue, reduced appetite, continuous hiccups, and joint pain involving both shoulders and ankles. He additionally noted the presence of oral aphthous ulcers.

His past medical history was notable for type 2 diabetes mellitus, for which he was not receiving therapy due to limited understanding of his diagnosis and a lack of adherence to dietary or exercise recommendations. There was no notable family history, and no known family members with Behçet’s disease (BD). He drank alcohol occasionally, did not use tobacco, had no recent travel history, and denied exposure to animals or ill individuals. He was employed in the metalworking sector and reported no recent sexual contacts.

On physical assessment, the patient was awake but demonstrated signs of tiredness. Vital parameters included a temperature of 38.1°C, blood pressure measured at 135/73 mm Hg, regular heart rate of 119 beats per minute, respiratory rate of 19 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation at 96% while breathing ambient air. Inspection noted the presence of oral aphthous ulcers and folliculitis-like eruptions on both the face and trunk (figure 1A, B, and C). Examinations of the cardiovascular, pulmonary, and abdominal systems were within normal limits. Manual muscle testing showed preserved strength without deficit in the upper and lower limbs. Peripheral edema was absent, and clinical evaluation of volume status indicated euvolemia. The patient was subsequently hospitalized for additional workup.

Investigations:

Initial laboratory assessment showed significant hyponatremia (124 mmol/L), increased creatine kinase (1349 U/L), aspartate aminotransferase (77 U/L), alanine aminotransferase (64 U/L), C-reactive protein (CRP) at 180 mg/L (reference <3 mg/L), and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) at 70 mm/hour (reference <15 mm/hour), while renal function was within normal limits. The patient was also noted to have hyperglycemia (plasma glucose 14.2 mmol/L) and a raised HbA1c of 8.2%, signifying inadequate glycemic control. Testing for hepatitis B and C was negative.

Endocrine workup demonstrated normal values for thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH: 1.11 mIU/L; reference, 0.50–5.00 mIU/L), free T4 (1.25 ng/dL; reference, 0.90–1.70 ng/dL), and serum cortisol (42 µg/dL; reference, >6 µg/dL). Serum osmolality was reduced (267 mOsm/kg; reference, <270 mOsm/kg), whereas urine osmolality was high (900 mOsm/kg; reference, >300 mOsm/kg), with elevated urine sodium (88 mmol/L; reference, >20 mmol/L), all suggestive of compromised water clearance. Despite the presence of hypo-osmolar hyponatremia, antidiuretic hormone (ADH) level was increased (22.4 pg/mL). Electrocardiogram showed no abnormalities, and imaging including chest X-ray and CT scans of the head, chest, and abdomen did not reveal any pathology. Collectively, these results were consistent with a diagnosis of SIADH.

By the third day, the patient experienced an intensification of headache and vertigo. Despite the absence of neck rigidity or involvement of cranial nerves, central nervous system inflammation was considered. Magnetic resonance imaging of the brain did not demonstrate notable pathology in the hypothalamus or other cerebral regions (see figure 2). Analysis of cerebrospinal fluid showed a slightly increased opening pressure (17 cmH₂O), elevated protein concentration (0.97 g/L), and a mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL). Microbiological cultures and diagnostic assays for bacterial and mycobacterial organisms, as well as for herpes simplex virus (HSV), varicella zoster virus (VZV), and cryptococcal antigen, yielded negative results. Cytological assessment of the CSF revealed no evidence of malignancy.

Additional workup was conducted to exclude systemic disorders. Evaluation for sarcoidosis demonstrated lysozyme at 5.1 µg/mL (reference: 5–10) and angiotensin-converting enzyme at 11.4 U/L (reference: 7–25), both within the normal range. Hematological screening showed an elevated soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) at 704 U/mL (reference: 122–496 U/mL), with negative results for antibodies against human T-cell leukemia virus type 1.

On the fifth day, the patient developed a painful ulceration on the scrotum (figure 1D). Taken together with the presence of oral aphthous ulcers and folliculitis-like skin lesions, these signs indicated a systemic presentation of Behçet’s disease (BD). The patient did not have a positive pathergy reaction. Ocular examination showed no evidence of uveitis. Testing for HLA-B51 was positive.

Differential diagnosis:

Upon initial assessment, the concurrent presence of fever, headache, general malaise, and hyponatremia raised suspicion for either a central nervous system (CNS) infection or a systemic inflammatory process. Viral meningoencephalitis, specifically due to HSV or VZV, was considered as a primary diagnosis. Nevertheless, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) assays did not detect any viral antigens or antibodies, thereby ruling out this etiology. The potential for bacterial meningitis was also explored; however, the lack of a neutrophilic predominance, only mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, and negative CSF cultures led to its exclusion. Tuberculous meningitis was discounted in light of negative acid-fast staining and tuberculosis DNA testing.

Consideration was also given to primary malignancy of the CNS owing to the patient’s neurological manifestations, but brain MRI revealed no abnormalities, and cytological analysis of CSF was negative for malignant cells. Although there was a slight elevation in soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) levels, this was insufficient to support a CNS lymphoma diagnosis by itself.

Autoimmune etiologies were investigated and subsequently eliminated due to a lack of hallmark systemic symptoms and negative results on screening autoantibody panels. Neurosarcoidosis was considered within the differential diagnosis since MRI findings may remain unremarkable in initial stages; however, further laboratory evaluation did not support this diagnosis. The modest increase in sIL-2R was interpreted as a non-specific laboratory abnormality.

The definitive diagnosis of Behçet’s disease (BD) was made based on clinical criteria: the patient presented with fever and fatigue, oral aphthae (two points), genital aphthosis (two points), and a folliculitis-like rash (one point). According to the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease, a cumulative score of five points (with a threshold of ≥4 points required for a definite diagnosis) confirmed BD. Notably, genital aphthosis is highly specific to BD, strongly pointing towards this diagnosis. With other infectious, neoplastic, and autoimmune processes systematically excluded, acute neuro-Behçet's disease was diagnosed based on CNS involvement occurring alongside mucocutaneous findings.

Treatment:

Upon admission (day 1), clinicians considered a possible underlying inflammatory disorder, though the precise cause was undetermined. Empiric cefepime was begun following collection of blood cultures to address a suspected bacterial process, even though no specific site of infection was identified.

By day 3, the patient experienced increased headache and vertigo, raising concerns for central nervous system involvement, either inflammatory or infectious. Empiric acyclovir therapy was started, but later stopped once HSV and VZV encephalitis had been excluded through diagnostic testing.

On day 8, after systemic BD with acute neuro-BD was diagnosed, colchicine therapy commenced. Topical corticosteroid ointment was used to address the oral aphthae, while silver sulfadiazine cream was applied to both genital ulcers and skin lesions. Management of SIADH consisted of restricting fluid intake to less than 800 mL per day and closely observing daily serum sodium levels, ensuring that sodium correction was not too rapid. Administration of hypertonic saline and vasopressin receptor antagonists was not necessary.

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient's fever subsided and his appetite returned the day following initiation of colchicine. Over the next two weeks, he experienced gradual improvement in symptoms such as vertigo, general malaise, hiccups, and mucocutaneous lesions. On day 16, serum sodium had risen to 136 mmol/L, and ADH had declined to 1.8 pg/mL. Myogenic enzyme values also normalized.

Notably, systemic corticosteroids and other immunosuppressive agents were not administered at any point. After a hospital stay of one month—during which acute symptoms resolved, hyponatraemia was closely managed, and rehabilitation addressed his diminished physical function—the patient was discharged. Colchicine treatment was maintained for roughly four years, during which neither BD flare-ups nor neurological symptoms reappeared. Once his BD was stable, he was started on biguanide therapy, resulting in continued satisfactory glycaemic control.

Discussion:

This report describes a rare clinical presentation in which systemic BD and acute neuro-BD were identified at the same time. It further illustrates that acute neuro-BD may be associated with SIADH. The improvement of mucocutaneous and neurological symptoms, as well as hyponatraemia, after starting colchicine monotherapy, suggests a potential direct relationship among BD, neuro-BD, and SIADH. In BD patients who develop fever or headache in conjunction with hyponatraemia, clinicians should keep acute neuro-BD in the differential diagnosis.

The case stands out due to the concurrent detection of systemic BD and acute neuro-BD, indicated by both mucocutaneous involvement and central nervous system findings. In most instances, significant organ complications such as CNS lesions typically develop about four years after the initial BD diagnosis, and fewer than 5% of individuals exhibit neurological symptoms at the outset. Furthermore, only about 10% of patients have headaches attributable to neuro-BD. These factors contributed to the diagnostic difficulty encountered. Acute neuro-BD generally appears as a transient meningoencephalitis, potentially with or without localized neurological signs. For this patient, MRI of the brain failed to reveal abnormalities at common sites for BD-related lesions, including the hypothalamic region. Acute neuro-BD can sometimes manifest as aseptic meningitis without evidence of parenchymal involvement on imaging, and a normal early MRI does not preclude the diagnosis. The absence of neuroimaging abnormalities further challenged the diagnosis in this case. HLA-B51 analysis was also performed but was not diagnostically useful. This finding is consistent with international BD guidelines, which prioritize clinical assessment over HLA-B51 testing for diagnosis. The case underscores the need to suspect neuro-BD in patients with mucocutaneous BD symptoms who also present with vague neurological complaints.

At the outset of our differential diagnosis, we gave precedence to infectious etiologies for CNS inflammation due to the presence of an unexplained fever, neurological manifestations, and hyponatraemia; nevertheless, no definitive pathogen was identified. Additional differential considerations encompassed other inflammatory conditions affecting the CNS, such as connective tissue disorders and neoplastic diseases, with sarcoidosis and hematologic malignancies included in our assessment. Consequently, our initial workup involved broad screening tests including autoimmune serologies, serum ACE, and sIL-2R, which we ordered in a limited fashion as part of a strategy aimed at exclusion rather than because of strong clinical indications. No clinical, imaging, or CSF evidence was identified to support these diagnoses, and the aforementioned laboratory studies ultimately did not contribute meaningfully to establishing the final diagnosis. It is recognized that ACE has poor sensitivity for detecting sarcoidosis, and while sIL-2R can be useful in evaluating possible malignant lymphoma, minor elevations are nonspecific and may simply indicate inflammation. In this instance, the lack of genital ulceration at symptom onset introduced uncertainty regarding the diagnosis of BD, prompting us to consider a wider range of potential causes. This case illustrates the pitfalls of unwarranted diagnostic testing and reinforces the principle that such investigations should be performed only when clearly supported by clinical evidence and should be interpreted judiciously.

While drug-induced SIADH has previously been described in individuals with BD who are treated with agents such as cyclophosphamide, the connection between acute neuro-BD and SIADH is not well elucidated. Neuro-BD involves central nervous system inflammation, which could potentially lead to inappropriate ADH secretion. Multiple investigations have demonstrated that interleukin-6 (IL-6), a proinflammatory cytokine commonly increased in neuroinflammatory diseases, is linked to enhanced ADH release. In the context of BD, abnormal activation of monocytes results in spontaneous IL-6 generation, and raised IL-6 concentrations have been found in the cerebrospinal fluid of patients during acute neuro-BD episodes. These observations imply that IL-6–driven inflammation may have a role in the development of SIADH under these circumstances. In this particular case, IL-6 concentrations in serum or CSF were not assessed; however, the significantly increased serum CRP might indicate an underlying inflammatory process mediated by IL-6. Although SIADH has associations with a range of conditions such as CNS inflammation, pulmonary disorders, malignancies, and certain pharmaceuticals, the present data do not provide definitive evidence for a causal association between SIADH and acute neuro-BD. The temporal concurrence described here may indicate a general reaction to CNS inflammation as opposed to a mechanism unique to the disease.

This case further demonstrates the possible complications arising from hyponatraemia due to SIADH occurring during acute neuro-BD. Persistent hiccups have previously been described as a significant clinical indicator of severe hyponatraemia resulting from SIADH associated with CNS inflammation, such as in meningitis. As part of the initial assessment for fever, arthralgia, and generalized fatigue—symptoms that can indicate inflammatory or metabolic muscle injury—creatine kinase was measured, revealing rhabdomyolysis. This condition is a recognized, yet frequently underdiagnosed, consequence of significant hyponatraemia, and is not often actively sought. These symptoms, while well-documented, are frequently underestimated outcomes of marked hyponatraemia. It is important for clinicians to recognize that symptoms such as hiccups or rhabdomyolysis, which may appear unrelated, can be signs of electrolyte disturbances, especially amidst neuroinflammatory processes.

Corticosteroids remain the cornerstone of therapy for acute neuro-BD, with most individuals exhibiting favorable responses. Nonetheless, there have been descriptions of cases with isolated meningoencephalitis-like presentations resolving without intervention. In this instance, treatment with colchicine alone led to improvements in both mucocutaneous and neurological manifestations, as well as correction of hyponatraemia. Colchicine’s anti-inflammatory action involves the inhibition of neutrophil migration and activation, along with the reduction of cytokine production such as IL-1β and IL-6. While colchicine is routinely employed for mucocutaneous symptoms in BD, evidence from systematic reviews indicates it does not provide benefit for acute neuro-BD, with immunosuppressive medications usually necessary to achieve adequate disease management. Reported literature also shows colchicine is most often combined with corticosteroids or additional immunosuppressants, rather than used alone. Although the possibility of spontaneous improvement in this case cannot be ruled out, these observations indicate that colchicine could be considered for acute neuro-BD and associated issues like SIADH, especially when steroid therapy is not preferable or is contraindicated.

Conclusion:

We presented an uncommon instance in which Behçet's disease (BD) and acute neuro-BD were diagnosed at the same time, with neuro-BD complicated by syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). This report illustrates that SIADH can develop as a secondary complication of acute neuro-BD, emphasizing the necessity for clinicians to recognize this possible link. Treatment with colchicine alone resulted in swift resolution of both mucocutaneous and neurological symptoms as well as hyponatraemia, indicating that colchicine might serve as an effective therapy in the initial stages of neuro-BD. In cases where patients with BD develop non-specific central nervous system signs like headache and fever in the presence of hyponatraemia, healthcare providers should have an elevated level of suspicion for acute neuro-BD accompanied by SIADH.
