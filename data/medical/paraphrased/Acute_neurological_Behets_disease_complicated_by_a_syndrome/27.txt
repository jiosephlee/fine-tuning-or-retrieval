Title: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

Eiichi Kakehi, Yukako Matsumoto, Shigehisa Sakurai, Kazuhiko Kotani

Abstract:

Summary: This report describes an uncommon presentation of acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) that was identified simultaneously with systemic Behçet’s disease (BD), meeting criteria for definite BD, and was further complicated by the development of syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). A male patient in his 60s arrived with fever, headache, and mucocutaneous signs such as oral aphthous ulcers, folliculitis-like eruptions, genital ulcers, and hyponatraemia. The clinical diagnosis of BD was made due to characteristic mucocutaneous features. Imaging with brain MRI showed no evidence of abnormality, while cerebrospinal fluid analysis identified aseptic meningitis, consistent with a diagnosis of acute neuro-BD. Laboratory evaluation confirmed the presence of SIADH according to established criteria. Treatment with colchicine alone resulted in complete remission of mucocutaneous findings, neurologic symptoms, and hyponatraemia, with no requirement for corticosteroids or additional immunosuppressive therapy. This case highlights a possible link between acute neuro-BD and SIADH and emphasizes the need to consider simultaneous neurological and endocrine involvement in patients newly diagnosed with BD.

Background:

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a persistent, multisystem inflammatory condition marked by recurrent oral and genital ulcers, uveitis, and various skin manifestations. Neurological complications, termed neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), arise in roughly 10%–20% of individuals and tend to occur years following the original BD diagnosis. Acute neuro-BD is generally characterized by meningoencephalitis and lesions within brain parenchyma, frequently affecting the brainstem. Although simultaneous onset of BD and neuro-BD is rare—delayed neurological involvement being more typical—clinicians should be vigilant for concurrent disease, evaluating both mucocutaneous signs and neurological complaints such as headache, behavioral alterations, psychiatric manifestations, and focal neurologic deficits.

Syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) represents a prevalent etiology of hyponatraemia and is commonly attributed to central nervous system (CNS) inflammation, malignancy, or exposure to specific medications. Given the underlying inflammation in acute neuro-BD, these patients may be susceptible to developing SIADH. While SIADH has been noted among BD patients undergoing therapies like cyclophosphamide, the development of SIADH exclusively due to CNS inflammation from acute neuro-BD without other pharmacological incitants has yet to be fully defined.

We describe an unusual instance of simultaneous BD and acute neuro-BD diagnosis complicated by SIADH. This report underscores the necessity of recognizing SIADH as a possible clinical feature of acute neuro-BD, particularly in cases where individuals present with vague neurological symptoms and hyponatraemia.

Case presentation:

A man of Asian descent in his sixties arrived at the emergency department complaining of three days of fever, headache, fatigue, decreased appetite, ongoing hiccups, and joint pain involving his shoulders and ankles. He also experienced aphthous ulcers in the mouth.

His past medical history was notable for type 2 diabetes mellitus, though he was not on any therapy due to minimal awareness of his diagnosis and poor adherence to dietary and exercise recommendations. There was no relevant family history, as none of his relatives had BD. He drank alcohol occasionally but did not smoke, had not recently traveled, nor had exposure to animals or ill individuals. His occupation was in metalworking, and he had not participated in sexual activity recently.

During assessment, the patient was alert yet seemed tired. His recorded vitals included a temperature of 38.1°C, blood pressure measuring 135/73 mm Hg, regular heart rate of 119 beats per minute, respiratory rate of 19 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation of 96% on ambient air. Physical exam demonstrated oral aphthae along with rashes resembling folliculitis present on his face and trunk (see figure 1A, B and C). The cardiopulmonary and abdominal examinations showed no abnormal findings. Manual muscle testing confirmed intact strength in both upper and lower limbs. No peripheral edema was observed, and clinical assessment indicated euvolemic status. He was hospitalized for further diagnostic workup.

Investigations:

Initial laboratory evaluation showed pronounced hyponatraemia at 124 mmol/L, increased creatine kinase of 1349 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase at 77 U/L, and alanine aminotransferase at 64 U/L. C-reactive protein was elevated to 180 mg/L (reference <3 mg/L), while erythrocyte sedimentation rate was 70 mm/hour (reference <15 mm/hour). Renal function remained within normal limits. The patient exhibited hyperglycaemia, with plasma glucose measured at 14.2 mmol/L, alongside an HbA1c of 8.2%, signifying suboptimal glycaemic regulation. Markers for hepatitis B and C were negative.

Endocrine assessment demonstrated thyroid-stimulating hormone at 1.11 mIU/L (reference range, 0.50–5.00 mIU/L), free T4 of 1.25 ng/dL (reference, 0.90–1.70 ng/dL), and serum cortisol at 42 µg/dL (reference, >6 µg/dL), all within reference ranges. Serum osmolality was reduced at 267 mOsm/kg (reference, <270 mOsm/kg), while urine osmolality was notably increased at 900 mOsm/kg (reference, >300 mOsm/kg), and urinary sodium concentration was elevated at 88 mmol/L (reference, >20 mmol/L), indicative of defective water excretion. Despite hypo-osmolar hyponatraemia, antidiuretic hormone was measured at 22.4 pg/mL, above normal levels. Electrocardiogram was unremarkable, as were chest X-ray and computed tomography scans of the head, chest, and abdomen. Collectively, these results were consistent with a diagnosis of SIADH.

By the third day, his headache and vertigo became more severe. Despite the absence of neck stiffness and cranial nerve symptoms, central nervous system inflammation was considered. Magnetic resonance imaging of the brain showed no significant abnormalities affecting the hypothalamus or other regions (figure 2). Cerebrospinal fluid analysis demonstrated slightly increased opening pressure (17 cmH₂O), elevated protein concentration (0.97 g/L), and a mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL). CSF cultures and tests for bacterial and mycobacterial organisms, herpes simplex virus (HSV), varicella zoster virus (VZV), and cryptococcal antigen returned negative results. CSF cytological examination did not identify malignant cells.

Additional workup was undertaken to rule out systemic conditions. Evaluation for sarcoidosis showed normal lysozyme (5.1 µg/mL; reference range 5–10) and angiotensin-converting enzyme values (11.4 U/L; reference range 7–25). Assessment for hematological malignancy included soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) measured at 704 U/mL (reference 122–496 U/mL); testing for human T-cell leukaemia virus type 1 antibody was negative.

By day 5, a painful ulcer developed on the scrotum (figure 1D). The presence of oral aphthae and rash resembling folliculitis supported the diagnosis of a systemic form of BD. He showed no evidence of hyperreactivity to pinprick. Eye examination did not reveal uveitis. The HLA-B51 allele was detected.

Differential diagnosis:

Upon initial assessment, the concurrent presentation of fever, headache, generalized malaise, and hyponatraemia pointed toward either an infection of the central nervous system or a systemic inflammatory condition. Viral meningoencephalitis, particularly from HSV or VZV, was the leading consideration. Nevertheless, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis did not detect viral antigens or antibodies, thereby effectively ruling out these viral etiologies. Bacterial meningitis was also contemplated but was discounted due to the lack of a neutrophil-dominant response in the CSF, only slight lymphocytic pleocytosis, and negative culture results. Additionally, tuberculous meningitis was ruled out on the basis of negative acid-fast staining and absence of tuberculosis DNA on CSF testing.

Given the patient’s neurological symptoms, a primary central nervous system neoplasm was evaluated. However, brain MRI was unremarkable, and CSF cytological analysis revealed no evidence of malignant cells. Although a slight elevation in sIL-2R was noted, this by itself did not substantiate a diagnosis of CNS lymphoma.

Autoimmune conditions were systematically reviewed but dismissed due to the lack of corresponding systemic clinical features and negative results on autoantibody screens. Neurosarcoidosis was also considered as early MRI can appear normal; however, later laboratory assessments made this diagnosis less probable, and the moderately increased sIL-2R was viewed as nonspecific.

Ultimately, the patient’s constellation of clinical manifestations—fever and fatigue, oral aphthae (two points), genital aphthosis (two points), and a folliculitis-like eruption (one point)—fulfilled criteria for definite Behçet's Disease (BD), reaching a total of five points as per the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (where a score of ≥4 signifies a definite diagnosis). Genital aphthosis, in particular, is considered a distinguishing feature of BD and supported this diagnosis strongly. With infectious, neoplastic, and autoimmune causes excluded, an acute neuro-Behçet’s Disease (neuro-BD) diagnosis was made, characterized by central nervous system manifestations accompanying mucocutaneous lesions.

Treatment:

On the first day of hospitalization, clinicians considered a possible underlying inflammatory disorder, but the precise cause could not be determined. Cefepime was given empirically after blood cultures were obtained, aiming to treat a possible bacterial infection even though no clear source of infection was identified.

By the third day, the patient experienced increased headache and vertigo, raising suspicion for central nervous system inflammation or infection. Empiric acyclovir therapy was started but later withdrawn once HSV and VZV encephalitis were excluded through testing.

On day 8, colchicine therapy commenced after confirming the diagnosis of systemic Behçet's disease (BD) with acute neuro-Behçet's disease (neuro-BD). Topical corticosteroid ointment was used for oral aphthous ulcers, while silver sulfadiazine cream was applied to genital ulcers and skin lesions. SIADH management involved conservative fluid restriction (less than 800 mL daily), meticulous daily tracking of serum sodium levels, and prevention of rapid sodium correction. Neither hypertonic saline nor vasopressin receptor antagonists were utilized.

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient’s fever subsided, and his appetite returned the day after initiating colchicine. Over the next two weeks, his symptoms—including vertigo, generalized malaise, hiccups, and mucocutaneous lesions—improved progressively. On day 16, serum sodium normalized to 136 mmol/L, and ADH dropped to 1.8 pg/mL. Myogenic enzyme values also reverted to normal ranges.

Notably, systemic corticosteroids and other immunosuppressive agents were not administered throughout his treatment. Following a one-month hospital stay—during which he recovered from the acute phase, hyponatraemia was closely observed and managed, and rehabilitation addressed reduced physical functioning—he was discharged. Colchicine treatment was maintained for approximately four years, and no relapse of Behçet’s disease or neurologic manifestations occurred during this period. Once his Behçet’s disease stabilized, biguanide therapy was initiated, resulting in ongoing effective glycaemic management.

Discussion:

This report describes a case in which systemic Behçet's disease (BD) and acute neuro-BD were diagnosed concurrently—a clinical presentation that is distinctly uncommon. Furthermore, the development of syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) in the context of acute neuro-BD was observed. Resolution of cutaneous and oral symptoms, neurological disturbances, and hyponatraemia after administration of colchicine as monotherapy implies there may be a direct link among BD, neuro-BD, and SIADH. When individuals with BD exhibit fever or headache along with hyponatraemia, acute neuro-BD should be included in the differential diagnosis.

This case is significant because both systemic BD and acute neuro-BD were identified simultaneously, as evidenced by mucocutaneous lesions and involvement of the central nervous system. In most instances, organ involvement such as CNS pathology appears an average of four years after BD is first diagnosed; neurological symptoms at onset are rare and found in less than 5% of cases. Moreover, headaches directly associated with neuro-BD are noted in only about 10% of patients. As a result, the early identification of neuro-BD in this case was particularly challenging. Acute neuro-BD characteristically manifests as a transient meningoencephalitis, sometimes with focal neurologic findings and sometimes without. In this patient, MRI of the brain did not reveal abnormalities at classic sites for BD involvement, including the hypothalamus. Notably, acute neuro-BD may be characterized by aseptic meningitis in the absence of brain parenchymal lesions, and a normal MRI—especially at disease onset—does not eliminate the diagnosis. The absence of imaging findings further complicated efforts to reach a diagnosis. HLA-B51 testing was conducted but proved unhelpful for diagnosis, reflecting international diagnostic criteria for BD, which prioritize clinical features over HLA-B51 status. This case emphasizes that neuro-BD should be considered whenever patients with mucocutaneous features of BD develop nonspecific neurological complaints.

During the initial differential diagnostic workup, we prioritized infectious etiologies for CNS inflammation due to the combination of unexplained fever, neurological manifestations, and hyponatraemia, though no specific pathogen could be identified. Alternative causes such as connective tissue disorders and malignancy—including sarcoidosis and hematologic cancers—were also considered. Accordingly, we conducted broad screening tests including autoimmune serological panels, serum ACE, and sIL-2R, employing these as exclusionary measures in the absence of strong clinical indications. Ultimately, there was no supporting evidence from clinical presentation, imaging, or CSF analysis for these conditions, and the results of these tests did not significantly aid in establishing the final diagnosis. While serum ACE is not highly sensitive for sarcoidosis, sIL-2R can assist in assessing malignant lymphoma, but modest elevations are nonspecific and may simply indicate inflammatory activity. Because genital ulcers were absent early in the disease course, diagnosing BD was challenging, necessitating an expansive differential. This experience underscores the necessity of judicious test ordering—clinicians should restrict these investigations to situations backed by relevant clinical findings and interpret them carefully.

While medication-induced SIADH, including cases involving cyclophosphamide in patients with BD, has been documented, the connection between acute neuro-BD and SIADH remains unclear. Neuro-BD presents with inflammation of the central nervous system, which could potentially result in abnormal ADH secretion. Multiple studies have indicated that interleukin-6 (IL-6), a proinflammatory cytokine often found at high levels in neuroinflammatory conditions, correlates with increased ADH secretion. In BD, inappropriate activation of monocytes leads to spontaneous IL-6 synthesis, and elevated IL-6 concentrations have been identified in the cerebrospinal fluid of individuals experiencing acute neuro-BD. These observations imply that IL-6-driven inflammation may play a role in the development of SIADH within this context. Although this case did not include serum or CSF measurement of IL-6, the significantly raised serum CRP could be indicative of an inflammatory process involving IL-6. While SIADH is linked to a variety of conditions such as central nervous system inflammation, pulmonary disorders, malignancies, and some pharmaceuticals, the data from this case do not definitively establish a causal relationship between SIADH and acute neuro-BD. The observed temporal relationship may simply represent a general response to CNS inflammation rather than a neuro-BD–specific mechanism.

This case further demonstrates the possible complications arising from hyponatraemia due to SIADH in acute neuro-BD. Past literature has noted that ongoing hiccups can act as a significant clinical indicator of severe hyponatraemia attributed to SIADH secondary to central nervous system inflammation, such as in cases of meningitis. Moreover, CK levels were assessed during the initial workup for fever, arthralgia, and general fatigue—symptoms that might be linked to either inflammatory or metabolic myopathy—leading to the identification of rhabdomyolysis. This condition represents a known, though frequently missed, complication of profound hyponatraemia that is not typically evaluated for in such scenarios. These findings highlight clinical effects of marked hyponatraemia that are recognized but often not fully appreciated. Physicians should remain vigilant that symptoms which appear unrelated, including persistent hiccups or rhabdomyolysis, could be manifestations of underlying electrolyte abnormalities, especially when associated with neuroinflammatory diseases.

Corticosteroids are regarded as the primary therapy for acute neuro-BD, with the majority of patients showing a positive response. Nonetheless, instances of spontaneous recovery have been documented, especially when presentation is limited to meningoencephalitis-like features. In this report, monotherapy with colchicine led to improvements in mucocutaneous and neurological manifestations, as well as correction of hyponatraemia. The anti-inflammatory properties of colchicine arise from its ability to block neutrophil migration and activation, along with inhibiting the production of cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-6. While colchicine is commonly prescribed for managing mucocutaneous symptoms in BD, systematic reviews indicate that it does not effectively treat acute neuro-BD, with immunosuppressive medications typically necessary for sufficient disease management. In published literature, colchicine has typically been administered alongside corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants rather than as a sole agent. Although the potential for spontaneous remission cannot be ruled out in this patient, these observations raise the possibility that colchicine could represent a therapeutic alternative for acute neuro-BD and complications like SIADH, especially in cases where corticosteroids are not suitable or contraindicated.

Conclusion:

We reported an unusual instance where BD and acute neuro-BD were identified simultaneously, with SIADH emerging as a complication of neuro-BD. This case underscores that SIADH can develop in the context of acute neuro-BD, emphasizing the necessity for clinicians to recognize this potential link. Treatment with colchicine alone resulted in prompt resolution of mucocutaneous and neurological symptoms as well as hyponatraemia, indicating that colchicine could be an effective therapeutic strategy during the initial stage of neuro-BD. When patients with BD present with vague central nervous system manifestations, such as fever and headache, alongside hyponatraemia, clinicians should consider the possibility of acute neuro-BD in BD cases complicated by SIADH.
