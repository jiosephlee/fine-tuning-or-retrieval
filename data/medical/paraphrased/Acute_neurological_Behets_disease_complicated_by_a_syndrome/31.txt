Title: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

Eiichi Kakehi, Yukako Matsumoto, Shigehisa Sakurai, Kazuhiko Kotani

Abstract:

Summary: We describe an uncommon instance of acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) identified simultaneously with systemic Behçet’s disease (BD), meeting criteria for definite BD, and further complicated by syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). A male in his 60s presented with fever, headache, and mucocutaneous symptoms including oral aphthae, folliculitis-like eruptions, genital ulcers, and hyponatraemia. BD was diagnosed on the basis of these mucocutaneous signs. Brain MRI was unremarkable, while analysis of cerebrospinal fluid demonstrated aseptic meningitis, consistent with acute neuro-BD. Laboratory investigations confirmed SIADH diagnostic criteria. The patient's mucocutaneous symptoms, neurological issues, and hyponatraemia resolved following colchicine monotherapy, with no requirement for corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive agents. This report highlights a potential link between acute neuro-BD and SIADH, emphasizing the need to be vigilant for simultaneous neurological and endocrine symptoms in individuals newly diagnosed with BD.

Background:

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a persistent, multisystem inflammatory condition marked by recurring oral and genital ulcers, uveitis, and skin manifestations. Neurological complications, known as neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), affect roughly 10%–20% of individuals and commonly emerge years after BD is first identified. Acute neuro-BD frequently manifests as meningoencephalitis with brain parenchymal lesions, most often involving the brainstem. While neuro-BD typically appears later in the disease, simultaneous diagnoses of BD and neuro-BD are uncommon. In such presentations, evaluation should include both mucocutaneous signs and neurological features, such as headache, changes in behavior, psychiatric manifestations, and focal neurological impairments.

The syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) represents a recognized cause of hyponatraemia, often secondary to central nervous system (CNS) inflammation, malignancies, or specific medications. Because of the inflammatory nature of acute neuro-BD, individuals with this condition may be predisposed to developing SIADH. SIADH has been described in the setting of BD patients treated with agents like cyclophosphamide; however, its presence without medication triggers—attributable solely to CNS inflammation from acute neuro-BD—remains uncertain.

Here, we describe an unusual presentation in which both BD and acute neuro-BD were established simultaneously and complicated by SIADH. This report emphasizes the need to consider SIADH as a possible complication of acute neuro-BD, especially when encountering patients with undifferentiated CNS manifestations and hyponatraemia.

Case presentation:

A man of Asian descent in his 60s arrived at the emergency department with a three-day duration of fever, headache, generalized fatigue, diminished appetite, continuous hiccups, and joint pain involving his shoulders and ankles. He also noted the presence of oral aphthous ulcers.

His past medical history was notable for type 2 diabetes mellitus, for which he was not undergoing therapy due to minimal awareness of his diagnosis and a lack of appropriate diet or exercise. There was no family history of Behçet's disease, and no other significant hereditary conditions were reported. He drank alcohol on occasion, did not smoke, had no history of recent travel, and denied contact with animals or ill individuals. His occupation was in the metalworking field, and he had not participated in sexual activity recently.

On assessment, he was conscious but showed signs of tiredness. His vital signs included a temperature of 38.1°C, blood pressure measured at 135/73 mm Hg, a regular heart rate of 119 beats per minute, respiratory rate of 19 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation at 96% while breathing ambient air. Physical exam identified oral aphthous ulcers and rashes resembling folliculitis on his face and trunk (figure 1A, B, and C). No abnormal findings were noted during cardiac, pulmonary, or abdominal evaluations. Manual muscle strength testing demonstrated intact strength in both upper and lower limbs. Peripheral edema was absent, and his volume status appeared euvolemic based on clinical assessment. The patient was hospitalized for additional investigation.

Investigations:

Initial laboratory assessment demonstrated significant hyponatremia (124 mmol/L) and raised levels of creatine kinase (1349 U/L), aspartate aminotransferase (77 U/L), alanine aminotransferase (64 U/L), C-reactive protein (CRP) at 180 mg/L (reference range, <3 mg/L), and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) at 70 mm/hour (reference, <15 mm/hour), all with preserved renal function. Additionally, the patient exhibited hyperglycemia (plasma glucose 14.2 mmol/L) along with an elevated HbA1c of 8.2%, indicative of inadequate glycemic control. Screening for hepatitis B and C was negative.

Hormonal testing showed normal thyroid-stimulating hormone levels (1.11 mIU/L; reference range, 0.50–5.00 mIU/L), free thyroxine (1.25 ng/dL; reference, 0.90–1.70 ng/dL), and serum cortisol (42 µg/dL; reference, >6 µg/dL). Serum osmolality was decreased (267 mOsm/kg; reference, <270 mOsm/kg), whereas urine osmolality was markedly increased (900 mOsm/kg; reference, >300 mOsm/kg), alongside elevated urinary sodium (88 mmol/L; reference, >20 mmol/L), reflecting defective renal water elimination. Although serum sodium was low and osmolality decreased, antidiuretic hormone (ADH) concentrations were elevated at 22.4 pg/mL. The electrocardiogram appeared normal, and imaging studies including chest X-ray and CT scans of the head, chest, and abdomen showed no abnormalities. The constellation of these findings pointed toward a diagnosis of SIADH.

By the third day, his headache and vertigo intensified. Despite the absence of neck rigidity and cranial nerve deficits, central nervous system inflammation was considered. Magnetic resonance imaging of the brain did not demonstrate notable abnormalities in the hypothalamus or other regions (figure 2). Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) examination showed a mildly raised opening pressure (17 cmH₂O), elevated protein (0.97 g/L), and mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL). CSF cultures, along with tests for bacterial, mycobacterial pathogens, herpes simplex virus (HSV), varicella zoster virus (VZV), and cryptococcal antigen, yielded negative results. Cytological assessment of the CSF did not detect malignant cells.

Additional assessments were carried out to investigate potential systemic diseases. Sarcoidosis workup included lysozyme (5.1 µg/mL; reference range 5–10) and angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) (11.4 U/L; reference range 7–25), both of which were within normal limits. Screening for hematologic malignancy revealed an elevated soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) (704 U/mL; reference, 122–496 U/mL) but was negative for human T-cell leukaemia virus type 1 antibody.

On day 5, he developed a painful ulcer in the scrotum (figure 1D). The presence of oral aphthae and folliculitis-like skin lesions supported suspicion for systemic Behçet’s disease (BD). There was no pinprick hypersensitivity. Eye evaluation showed absence of uveitis. HLA-B51 testing was positive.

Differential diagnosis:

Upon presentation, the concurrence of fever, headache, generalized malaise, and hyponatremia raised suspicion for central nervous system infection or a systemic inflammatory condition. Viral meningoencephalitis, especially due to HSV or VZV, was initially considered. Nonetheless, cerebrospinal fluid analysis was negative for both viral antigens and antibodies, thereby ruling out these etiologies. Bacterial meningitis was also contemplated; however, the diagnosis was dismissed in view of the lack of neutrophilic dominance, only mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, and negative CSF cultures. The possibility of tuberculous meningitis was excluded, as both acid-fast staining and tuberculosis DNA assays yielded negative outcomes.

Given the neurologic manifestations, a primary malignancy of the CNS was also taken into account. Nevertheless, MRI of the brain revealed no pathologic findings, and CSF cytology did not demonstrate the presence of malignant cells. Although a modest elevation in sIL-2R was detected, this alone did not substantiate a diagnosis of CNS lymphoma.

Consideration was also given to autoimmune conditions, but these were eliminated based on the absence of hallmark systemic symptoms and negative results on screening for autoantibodies. Neurosarcoidosis was among the differential diagnoses because early-stage disease can feature an unremarkable MRI; however, subsequent laboratory evaluation diminished the likelihood of this diagnosis. The observed slight increase in sIL-2R was interpreted as non-specific.

A definitive diagnosis of Behçet’s disease (BD) was established in this patient by virtue of clinical manifestations including fever and fatigue, two episodes of oral aphthae (counting for two points), genital aphthosis (adding another two points), and a rash resembling folliculitis (one point). His cumulative score on the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease was five points (where a score of four or higher denotes definite disease). Notably, genital aphthosis is regarded as a characteristic feature of BD and, when present, strongly supports this diagnosis. Upon excluding infectious, malignant, and autoimmune processes, the conclusion was made that the patient was experiencing acute neuro-Behçet’s disease, as evidenced by CNS involvement in the context of mucocutaneous lesions.

Treatment:

On the first day of hospitalization, clinicians considered the possibility of an underlying inflammatory disorder, though its precise cause was not identified. Cefepime was started empirically following the collection of blood cultures, aiming to address a possible bacterial infection despite no obvious infectious source.

By the third day, the patient experienced increased headache and vertigo, raising concerns for central nervous system infection or inflammation. Acyclovir was given empirically, but later discontinued once HSV and VZV encephalitis were excluded by diagnostic testing.

On day 8, the patient began colchicine therapy after being diagnosed with systemic Behçet's disease (BD) and acute neuro-Behçet's disease. Oral aphthous ulcers were managed using topical corticosteroid ointment, while genital and skin lesions were treated with silver sulfadiazine cream. SIADH was addressed conservatively through restriction of fluid intake to less than 800 mL daily, regular monitoring of serum sodium, and prevention of rapid correction. Administration of hypertonic saline and vasopressin receptor antagonists was not necessary.

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient's fever subsided and his appetite returned the day following the initiation of colchicine. Over the next two weeks, symptoms such as vertigo, general malaise, hiccups, and mucocutaneous lesions gradually abated. On day 16, his serum sodium concentration had normalized to 136 mmol/L, and ADH levels had dropped to 1.8 pg/mL. Levels of myogenic enzymes also reverted to normal.

Notably, systemic corticosteroids and other immunosuppressive agents were not employed throughout his treatment. After a one-month hospitalization, during which he recovered from the acute phase, hyponatraemia was monitored and addressed, and rehabilitative interventions supported improvement in physical function, the patient was discharged. Colchicine was continued for roughly four years, during which no relapse of BD or neurological symptoms occurred. Once BD reached stability, biguanide therapy was started, resulting in effective glycaemic control thereafter.

Discussion:

This report describes the concurrent identification of systemic Behçet’s disease (BD) and acute neuro-BD, an uncommon clinical occurrence. It further illustrates that acute neuro-BD may be associated with the development of SIADH. The improvement of mucocutaneous involvement, neurological deficits, and hyponatraemia following colchicine treatment alone indicates a potential direct link between BD, neuro-BD, and SIADH. Clinicians should be alert to the possibility of acute neuro-BD in individuals with BD who develop fever or headache in the context of hyponatraemia.

The case is distinguished by the concurrent detection of both systemic BD, evidenced by mucocutaneous lesions, and CNS manifestations attributable to acute neuro-BD. Ordinarily, significant organ complications such as CNS involvement arise approximately four years after BD is initially diagnosed, and neurological symptoms at presentation are rare, observed in under 5% of cases. Moreover, only around 10% of patients experience headaches directly related to neuro-BD, which made the early suspicion of neuro-BD particularly difficult in this instance. Acute neuro-BD typically manifests as self-limited meningoencephalitis, sometimes accompanied by focal neurological symptoms. In the present case, brain MRI did not demonstrate any lesions in characteristic sites affected by BD, including the hypothalamic region. Acute neuro-BD can manifest as aseptic meningitis even when there are no visible parenchymal lesions on imaging, and the absence of MRI abnormalities, especially at disease onset, does not rule out the diagnosis. The absence of radiological evidence further complicated the evaluation. HLA-B51 testing, although conducted, did not assist in diagnosing BD, which is consistent with international diagnostic standards that prioritize clinical assessment over genetic testing. This case underscores the necessity of considering neuro-BD in the differential when a patient with mucocutaneous BD features develops unexplained neurological symptoms.

During the initial differential diagnosis, we gave precedence to infectious etiologies for CNS inflammation due to the patient's combination of unexplained fever, neurological manifestations, and hyponatremia; nevertheless, no causative pathogen was detected. Additional possible sources of CNS inflammation, such as connective tissue disorders, neoplasms, sarcoidosis, and hematologic malignancies, were also included in the differential. As part of a comprehensive early assessment, autoimmune serologies, serum ACE, and sIL-2R were obtained as preliminary screening tests. These were ordered in a targeted yet limited fashion as part of a process of elimination, rather than because of any significant clinical suspicion. Ultimately, neither clinical presentation, imaging, nor CSF findings substantiated these considerations, and the laboratory tests did not significantly aid the diagnosis. ACE demonstrates low sensitivity for sarcoidosis, and although sIL-2R can assist in identifying malignant lymphoma, slight elevations are not specific and may be associated with general inflammation. The lack of genital ulcerations at the onset contributed to diagnostic uncertainty surrounding BD and necessitated an extensive differential. This case underscores the necessity of thoughtful test selection and suggests that ancillary investigations should only be pursued based on relevant clinical signs, with results interpreted judiciously.

While SIADH due to medications such as cyclophosphamide has been documented in patients with Behçet’s disease (BD), the connection between acute neuro-BD and SIADH is not well understood. Neuro-BD involves inflammation of the central nervous system (CNS), which may disrupt regulation of antidiuretic hormone (ADH) secretion. Research has indicated that interleukin-6 (IL-6), a pro-inflammatory cytokine commonly elevated in disorders involving neuroinflammation, is linked to increased ADH release. In BD, abnormal activation of monocytes results in spontaneous IL-6 secretion, and elevated concentrations of IL-6 have been observed in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) of individuals with acute neuro-BD. These observations suggest that IL-6–driven inflammation could be implicated in the development of SIADH in this context. Although IL-6 concentrations were not assessed in the serum or CSF for the current case, the substantially increased serum C-reactive protein (CRP) might indicate a concomitant inflammatory response mediated by IL-6. SIADH has been associated with various causes, including CNS inflammation, lung disease, malignancy, and certain drugs; however, based on the current findings, a definitive causative relationship between SIADH and acute neuro-BD cannot be confirmed. The chronological occurrence seen here may represent a generalized reaction to CNS inflammation rather than a mechanism unique to the disease.

This case demonstrates the possible complications associated with hyponatraemia induced by SIADH in acute neuro-BD. Prior literature has indicated that ongoing hiccups may provide a significant clinical indication of severe hyponatraemia arising from SIADH secondary to central nervous system inflammation, such as meningitis. Furthermore, during the initial assessment for fever, arthralgia, and general fatigue—symptoms that may originate from inflammatory or metabolic muscle injury—we included CK measurement, which identified rhabdomyolysis. Rhabdomyolysis is a known but frequently neglected consequence of severe hyponatraemia that is not typically screened for. Although these clinical features are documented, their relevance in the context of marked hyponatraemia is often underestimated. It is important for clinicians to recognize that seemingly unrelated symptoms, such as hiccups or rhabdomyolysis, may signal underlying electrolyte abnormalities, especially when neuroinflammatory diseases are present.

Corticosteroids are regarded as the primary therapeutic option for acute neuro-BD, with the majority of patients exhibiting a positive response. Nevertheless, cases that present only with meningoencephalitis-like symptoms have been documented to resolve spontaneously. In this instance, colchicine used alone was associated with improvement in mucocutaneous and neurological manifestations, as well as correction of hyponatraemia. Colchicine has anti-inflammatory properties, functioning by inhibiting neutrophil migration and activation, and by reducing cytokine production such as IL-1β and IL-6. Although colchicine is commonly prescribed for mucocutaneous involvement in BD, systematic reviews have indicated that it is not effective in acute neuro-BD and that immunosuppressive medications are generally needed for sufficient disease control. Indeed, prior reports have described colchicine in combination with corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants rather than as monotherapy. Although the possibility of spontaneous resolution cannot be ruled out in this situation, these findings imply that colchicine could serve as an alternative treatment for acute neuro-BD and associated complications like SIADH, especially when corticosteroids are not preferred or are contraindicated.

Conclusion:

We reported an unusual presentation involving concurrent diagnosis of BD and acute neuro-BD, with SIADH developing as a complication of the neuro-BD. This case illustrates that SIADH can arise as a sequela of acute neuro-BD, underlining the necessity for clinicians to recognize this possible relationship. Administration of colchicine as a single agent resulted in prompt resolution of mucocutaneous signs, neurological deficits, and hyponatraemia, indicating that colchicine might be an effective early treatment for neuro-BD. For patients with BD who exhibit vague central nervous system symptoms, such as fever and headache, alongside hyponatraemia, clinicians should remain vigilant for acute neuro-BD associated with SIADH.
