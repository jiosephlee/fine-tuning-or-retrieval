Title: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

Eiichi Kakehi, Yukako Matsumoto, Shigehisa Sakurai, Kazuhiko Kotani

Abstract:

Summary: This paper presents an unusual instance of acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) identified simultaneously with systemic Behçet’s disease (BD), fulfilling the criteria for definite BD, and further complicated by syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). A male patient in his sixties arrived with symptoms of fever, headache, and mucocutaneous lesions—namely, oral aphthae, folliculitis-like eruptions, genital ulcers, and hyponatraemia. Diagnosis of BD was established on the basis of his mucocutaneous findings. While brain MRI yielded no pathological findings, analysis of the cerebrospinal fluid indicated aseptic meningitis, supporting acute neuro-BD. Additional laboratory results met criteria for SIADH. Treatment with colchicine alone resulted in the complete remission of the patient’s mucocutaneous and neurological symptoms, as well as normalization of sodium levels, without resorting to corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive therapies. This report highlights a potential link between acute neuro-BD and SIADH, emphasizing the necessity to consider both neurological and endocrine complications at the time of initial BD diagnosis.

Background:

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a persistent, multisystemic inflammatory condition marked by episodic oral and genital ulcerations, uveitis, and cutaneous manifestations. Neurological involvement—termed neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD)—arises in roughly 10%–20% of cases, generally manifesting years after the initial identification of BD. Acute neuro-BD most frequently manifests as meningoencephalitis accompanied by parenchymal brain abnormalities, with a predilection for the brainstem. While it is more usual for neurological features to develop later, it is uncommon for BD and neuro-BD to be diagnosed at the same time. In such situations, comprehensive evaluation of both mucocutaneous signs and neurological manifestations—including cephalalgia, personality alterations, psychiatric disturbances, and localized neurological deficits—is crucial.

Syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) constitutes a frequent etiology of hyponatraemia, often secondary to central nervous system (CNS) inflammation, neoplasms, or specific pharmacotherapies. Given the intense inflammatory activity associated with acute neuro-BD, these individuals may be predisposed to SIADH. Although there are accounts of SIADH developing in BD patients undergoing agents such as cyclophosphamide, evidence supporting SIADH arising exclusively from CNS inflammatory mechanisms—without medication-related triggers—in acute neuro-BD remains insufficiently documented.

We present an unusual instance in which BD and acute neuro-BD were simultaneously diagnosed, with the course complicated by SIADH. This case underscores the necessity of including SIADH in the differential diagnosis when evaluating patients with acute neuro-BD who exhibit hyponatraemia and vague CNS symptomatology.

Case presentation:

A man of Asian descent in his sixties arrived at the emergency department experiencing three days of fever, headache, general malaise, loss of appetite, ongoing hiccups, and joint pain localized to his shoulders and ankles. He also mentioned the presence of oral aphthous ulcers.

The patient’s medical background was notable for type 2 diabetes mellitus; however, he was not on medication due to limited awareness about his illness and insufficient attention to dietary and exercise recommendations. There was no significant family history, as none of his relatives had been diagnosed with BD. His social history revealed occasional alcohol consumption, no tobacco use, no recent travel, and no contact with animals or ill individuals. His occupation was in the metalworking sector, and he denied any recent sexual encounters.

Upon evaluation, the patient was conscious but showed signs of tiredness. His vital parameters were as follows: temperature measured at 38.1°C, blood pressure at 135/73 mm Hg, heart rate of 119 beats per minute with regular rhythm, respiratory rate at 19 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation at 96% while breathing ambient air. On physical inspection, there were oral aphthous ulcers and folliculitis-like lesions located on the face and trunk (see figure 1A, B, and C). Assessment of the cardiopulmonary and abdominal systems was within normal limits. Manual testing of muscle strength in both upper and lower limbs demonstrated no evidence of weakness. There were no signs of peripheral edema, and clinical evaluation indicated a euvolemic state. The patient was hospitalized for additional assessment.

Investigations:

Upon admission, laboratory investigations demonstrated significant hyponatraemia (124 mmol/L), markedly increased creatine kinase (1349 U/L), as well as raised levels of aspartate aminotransferase (77 U/L), alanine aminotransferase (64 U/L), C-reactive protein (CRP) (180 mg/L; reference, <3 mg/L), and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (70 mm/hour; reference, <15 mm/hour), while renal function parameters remained within normal limits. Additionally, the patient exhibited hyperglycaemia (plasma glucose 14.2 mmol/L) and a high HbA1c (8.2%), reflecting inadequate glycaemic management. Screening for hepatitis B and C returned negative results.

Assessment of endocrine status indicated that thyroid-stimulating hormone (1.11 mIU/L; reference, 0.50–5.00 mIU/L), free T4 (1.25 ng/dL; reference, 0.90–1.70 ng/dL), and serum cortisol (42 µg/dL; reference, >6 µg/dL) were within normal reference ranges. Serum osmolality was reduced (267 mOsm/kg; reference, <270 mOsm/kg), whereas urine osmolality was substantially increased (900 mOsm/kg; reference, >300 mOsm/kg), accompanied by elevated urinary sodium (88 mmol/L; reference, >20 mmol/L), indicating compromised renal water clearance. Notably, despite the presence of hypo-osmolar hyponatraemia, antidiuretic hormone (ADH) concentration was increased (22.4 pg/mL). Electrocardiogram findings were unremarkable, as were imaging studies including chest X-ray and CT scans of the head, chest, and abdomen. Collectively, these results were consistent with a diagnosis of SIADH.

By the third day, the patient’s headache and vertigo had intensified. Despite the absence of nuchal rigidity or cranial nerve deficits, there was concern for central nervous system inflammation. Magnetic resonance imaging of the brain did not show any notable abnormalities affecting the hypothalamus or other regions (figure 2). Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) evaluation demonstrated a slightly elevated opening pressure (17 cmH₂O), increased protein content (0.97 g/L), and a modest lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL). Microbiological studies of the CSF, including cultures and assays for bacterial and mycobacterial organisms, as well as HSV, VZV, and cryptococcal antigen, all yielded negative results. CSF cytology excluded the presence of malignant cells.

Additional diagnostic work-up was undertaken to identify possible underlying systemic illnesses. Markers for sarcoidosis, including lysozyme (5.1 µg/mL; reference 5–10) and angiotensin-converting enzyme (11.4 U/L; reference 7–25), were within normal limits. Hematological evaluation demonstrated an elevated soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) (704 U/mL; reference, 122–496 U/mL), but the patient tested negative for human T-cell leukaemia virus type 1 antibody.

By day 5, a painful ulceration developed on the scrotum (figure 1D). Alongside concurrent oral aphthae and rashes resembling folliculitis, this clinical picture was indicative of a systemic manifestation of BD. There was no evidence of hypersensitivity following a pinprick test. Ocular assessment did not detect any signs of uveitis. HLA-B51 testing was positive.

Differential diagnosis:

Upon admission, the constellation of symptoms—fever, headache, general malaise, and hyponatraemia—raised suspicion for either a central nervous system infection or a systemic inflammatory condition. Viral meningoencephalitis, especially from HSV or VZV, was initially considered likely. Nevertheless, cerebrospinal fluid analysis failed to detect viral antigens or antibodies, effectively ruling out these viral etiologies. Bacterial meningitis was also entertained as a differential diagnosis, but was excluded because of the absence of neutrophilic predominance, only slight lymphocytic pleocytosis, and negative CSF cultures. Tuberculous meningitis was similarly deemed unlikely, given negative results for acid-fast staining and tuberculosis DNA assays.

A primary CNS neoplasm was contemplated owing to the neurological presentation, but both the brain MRI and CSF cytology returned normal, providing no evidence for malignancy. Though a slight elevation in sIL-2R was detected, this laboratory finding was not sufficient by itself to confirm CNS lymphoma.

Autoimmune processes were also systematically assessed and excluded, as there were neither typical systemic manifestations nor positive findings on autoantibody screens. Neurosarcoidosis remained a remote possibility, given that MRI may initially appear normal in such cases; however, laboratory findings did not support this diagnosis, and the mildly increased sIL-2R level was ultimately interpreted as nonspecific.

The diagnosis of definite BD was based on the clinical picture, which featured fever, fatigue, oral aphthae (scoring two points), genital aphthosis (scoring two points), and a folliculitis-like rash (scoring one point), totaling five points as per the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (with a threshold of ≥4 points required). Genital aphthosis, in particular, is considered a highly specific manifestation of BD and lends strong support to this diagnosis. Following comprehensive exclusion of infectious, neoplastic, and autoimmune causes, the patient was diagnosed with acute neuro-BD, supported by the concurrence of CNS symptoms and mucocutaneous involvement.

Treatment:

Upon admission (day 1), clinicians suspected an underlying inflammatory disorder, though its precise origin was indeterminate. Empirical antimicrobial treatment with cefepime was commenced after blood cultures were obtained, addressing the possibility of a bacterial infection despite the lack of a clearly identifiable infectious site.

By day 3, the patient experienced increasing severity of headache and vertigo, raising concern for possible CNS infection or inflammation. Acyclovir therapy was empirically initiated but subsequently discontinued when laboratory testing excluded HSV and VZV encephalitis.

On day 8, colchicine therapy was introduced subsequent to diagnoses of systemic BD and acute neuro-BD. Management of oral aphthous ulcers involved topical corticosteroid ointment, while genital ulcers and cutaneous manifestations were addressed with silver sulfadiazine cream. SIADH was treated with conservative measures including restriction of fluid intake to less than 800 mL/day, close daily monitoring of serum sodium, and prevention of rapid sodium correction. The use of hypertonic saline and vasopressin receptor antagonists was not deemed necessary.

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient's fever subsided and his appetite increased the day following initiation of colchicine therapy. Over the next two weeks, his various symptoms—including vertigo, malaise, hiccups, and mucocutaneous lesions—showed gradual improvement. By the sixteenth day, his serum sodium had normalized at 136 mmol/L, and ADH concentrations had dropped to 1.8 pg/mL. Levels of myogenic enzymes also returned to within normal limits.

Notably, no systemic corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive agents were administered throughout his treatment course. The patient was discharged after one month in the hospital, during which the acute phase was resolved, hyponatraemia was vigilantly tracked and addressed, and rehabilitation was provided to address his physical deconditioning. Colchicine therapy was maintained for nearly four years without relapse of BD or recurrence of neurological manifestations. After stabilization of his BD, biguanide therapy was introduced, resulting in sustained good glycaemic control.

Discussion:

This case presented the uncommon concurrent diagnosis of systemic BD and acute neuro-BD, underscoring a rare clinical scenario. Additionally, it showed that acute neuro-BD can be associated with SIADH. The resolution of mucocutaneous lesions, neurological symptoms, and hyponatraemia after colchicine monotherapy suggests there may be a direct relationship among BD, neuro-BD, and SIADH. In individuals with BD who develop fever or headache in conjunction with hyponatraemia, acute neuro-BD should be considered as a possible etiology.

A key point in this case was the concurrent identification of both systemic BD and acute neuro-BD, supported by mucocutaneous findings and central nervous system involvement. Major organ manifestations, such as CNS involvement, most often emerge approximately four years post-initial BD diagnosis, and neurological symptoms at first presentation are infrequent, appearing in less than 5% of cases. Direct headache attribution to neuro-BD is found in only about 10% of affected patients. Consequently, recognizing neuro-BD at disease onset was especially challenging in this instance. Acute neuro-BD typically manifests as a transient meningoencephalitis, which may or may not be accompanied by focal neurological signs. In this patient, brain MRI was unremarkable, failing to reveal changes in characteristic regions affected by BD, such as the hypothalamus. Acute neuro-BD can manifest as aseptic meningitis in the absence of brain parenchymal lesions, and a normal brain MRI—especially in early disease—does not eliminate the possibility of neuro-BD, complicating the diagnostic process further. Although HLA-B51 analysis was conducted, it did not aid diagnosis, consistent with international BD guidelines, which prioritize clinical findings and do not mandate HLA-B51. Ultimately, this case emphasizes the need to maintain a high index of suspicion for neuro-BD in BD patients with mucocutaneous symptoms who present with ambiguous neurological complaints.

At the outset of the differential diagnosis process, we initially focused on infectious etiologies for the CNS inflammation, prompted by the co-occurrence of unexplained fever, neurological manifestations, and hyponatraemia; nonetheless, no definitive infectious agent was detected. We subsequently broadened the differential to include additional inflammatory causes such as connective tissue disorders and malignancies, with specific attention to sarcoidosis and haematologic cancers. Consequently, a comprehensive initial assessment was undertaken, including assays for autoimmune serological markers, serum ACE, and sIL-2R, primarily to exclude possible diagnoses rather than as a response to strong clinical suspicion. These tests were thus ordered with a narrow intent as part of a strategy centered on ruling out certain conditions. Ultimately, there was no supporting evidence—clinical, radiological, or within CSF studies—affirming these potential diagnoses, and these ancillary tests did not materially assist in reaching a conclusive diagnosis. The diagnostic value of ACE for sarcoidosis remains limited due to its low sensitivity, and while sIL-2R can sometimes aid in the workup for malignant lymphoma, slight elevations lack specificity and generally indicate non-specific inflammatory activity. In this patient's scenario, the absence of genital ulceration early in the disease course complicated the consideration of BD, necessitating an expanded differential. This diagnostic trajectory underscores the necessity for judicious use of ancillary testing, reminding clinicians to reserve such investigations for situations with adequate clinical justification and to interpret findings with discernment.

While drug-induced SIADH has been documented in individuals with BD undergoing treatment with agents like cyclophosphamide, the link between acute neuro-BD and the development of SIADH has not been clearly elucidated. Neuro-BD involves inflammation within the central nervous system, a process that could potentially lead to abnormal ADH regulation. Prior research indicates that interleukin-6 (IL-6)—a proinflammatory cytokine commonly elevated in neuroinflammatory states—can drive increased ADH secretion. In the context of BD, monocyte dysregulation triggers spontaneous IL-6 production, and heightened concentrations of IL-6 have been observed in the cerebrospinal fluid of those experiencing acute neuro-BD. These observations imply that inflammation mediated by IL-6 may play a role in the pathogenesis of SIADH in this clinical scenario. Although this case report did not assess IL-6 levels in serum or CSF, the presence of a substantially increased serum CRP could serve as an indirect marker of an IL-6–related inflammatory process. Despite SIADH being linked to multiple conditions such as central nervous system inflammation, pulmonary disorders, neoplasms, and various pharmacological agents, the current evidence is insufficient to definitively establish causality between SIADH and acute neuro-BD. The sequence of events documented here may be indicative of a general response to CNS inflammation, rather than a mechanism unique to neuro-BD.

This case further demonstrates the range of complications that can arise from SIADH-induced hyponatraemia in acute neuro-BD. Prior literature has highlighted that persistent hiccups may provide a valuable clinical indication of severe hyponatraemia secondary to SIADH resulting from central nervous system inflammation, such as that seen in meningitis. Moreover, our assessment included measuring CK to investigate nonspecific symptoms such as fever, arthralgia, and generalized fatigue, given their potential link to inflammatory or metabolic muscle injury; this led to the detection of rhabdomyolysis. Although rhabdomyolysis is a well-documented, it is frequently neglected complication of marked hyponatraemia and is seldom specifically tested for. These clinical features, while recognized, are often insufficiently emphasized as significant sequelae of profound hyponatraemia. It is essential for clinicians to recognize that symptoms seemingly unrelated to electrolyte imbalance—including hiccups and rhabdomyolysis—may, in fact, signal hidden disturbances in electrolyte homeostasis, especially in the context of neuroinflammatory disease.

Corticosteroids remain the cornerstone therapy for acute neuro-BD, with the majority of patients exhibiting a positive therapeutic response. Nonetheless, there are documented instances of spontaneous improvement in patients whose clinical presentation is limited to meningoencephalitis-like features. In the case described here, administration of colchicine alone led to amelioration of both mucocutaneous and neurological manifestations, in addition to correcting hyponatraemia. Colchicine’s anti-inflammatory properties are mediated through the inhibition of neutrophil motility and activation, as well as the suppression of cytokine synthesis, particularly IL-1β and IL-6. Although colchicine is routinely prescribed to manage the mucocutaneous symptoms of BD, evidence from systematic reviews indicates that it does not have significant efficacy in treating acute neuro-BD, where immunosuppressants are typically necessary to achieve sufficient disease control. Indeed, the literature generally describes colchicine being used as an adjunctive agent alongside corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive medications, rather than as the sole therapy. While it is possible that the observed improvements in this case could be attributed to spontaneous recovery, these observations imply that colchicine might represent a viable therapeutic alternative for acute neuro-BD and associated complications such as SIADH, especially in scenarios where corticosteroid use is not feasible or is contraindicated.

Conclusion:

We reported an uncommon presentation of BD alongside simultaneous onset of acute neuro-BD, where SIADH emerged as a complication of neuro-BD. This case underscores that SIADH can arise secondary to acute neuro-BD, emphasizing the necessity for clinicians to recognize this possible link. Treatment with colchicine alone resulted in swift resolution of mucocutaneous lesions, neurological deficits, and hyponatraemia, indicating colchicine's potential as an effective early intervention for neuro-BD. For BD patients exhibiting vague central nervous system symptoms—such as fever and headache—accompanied by hyponatraemia, clinicians should remain vigilant for the possibility of acute neuro-BD complicated by SIADH.
