Title: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

Eiichi Kakehi, Yukako Matsumoto, Shigehisa Sakurai, Kazuhiko Kotani

Abstract:

Summary: We present an unusual case involving acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) that was identified simultaneously with systemic Behçet’s disease (BD), classified as definite BD, and further complicated by syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). The patient, a man in his 60s, arrived with fever, headache, and various mucocutaneous lesions such as oral aphthae, rashes resembling folliculitis, genital ulcers, as well as hyponatraemia. The diagnosis of BD was established based on mucocutaneous findings. Although brain MRI was unremarkable, cerebrospinal fluid analysis indicated aseptic meningitis, aligning with an acute neuro-BD diagnosis. Laboratory investigations satisfied the criteria for SIADH. Treatment with colchicine alone resolved the patient’s mucocutaneous and neurological symptoms along with normalization of sodium levels, eliminating the requirement for corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive agents. This report indicates a potential connection between acute neuro-BD and SIADH, highlighting the need to recognize both neurological and endocrine features in patients newly diagnosed with BD.

Background:

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a persistent, multisystem inflammatory condition marked by recurrent oral and genital ulcerations, uveitis, and cutaneous manifestations. Neurological complications, termed neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), are observed in about 10%–20% of cases and typically arise several years post-initial diagnosis. The acute form of neuro-BD often presents as meningoencephalitis and involves parenchymal lesions within the central nervous system, most frequently affecting the brainstem region. While it is more usual for neuro-BD to manifest after a delay, the simultaneous identification of BD and neuro-BD is uncommon. In such scenarios, practitioners should evaluate both dermatologic and neurologic findings, including cephalgia, changes in behavior, psychiatric disturbances, and focal neurological impairments.

The syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) represents a frequent etiology of hyponatraemia and is generally secondary to inflammation of the CNS, neoplasia, or specific pharmaceuticals. Due to the inflammatory basis of acute neuro-BD, patients may have an increased susceptibility to SIADH. Reports have described SIADH in BD patients under medications like cyclophosphamide; however, the occurrence of SIADH solely attributable to CNS inflammation from acute neuro-BD—absent any pharmacological contributors—remains uncertain.

We present a rare instance in which BD and acute neuro-BD were diagnosed simultaneously, complicated by the development of SIADH. This case underscores the necessity to recognize SIADH as a possible clinical feature of acute neuro-BD, especially in individuals who exhibit ambiguous CNS complaints and low sodium levels.

Case presentation:

A man of Asian descent in his 60s arrived at the emergency department after experiencing three days of fever, headache, generalized fatigue, decreased appetite, continuous hiccups, and joint pain localized to the shoulders and ankles. He additionally mentioned having oral aphthous ulcers.

His past medical history was notable for type 2 diabetes mellitus, which was unmanaged due to his limited understanding of the condition and lack of adherence to recommended dietary and exercise regimens. There was no notable family history, as no relatives were known to have BD. He reported occasional alcohol use, denied smoking, and had no recent history of travel or contact with sick individuals or animals. He was employed in the metalworking sector and reported no recent sexual activity.

Upon assessment, the patient was conscious but showed signs of tiredness. His vital parameters were as follows: temperature measured at 38.1°C, blood pressure recorded at 135/73 mm Hg, heart rate at 119 beats per minute with a regular rhythm, respiratory rate at 19 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation of 96% while breathing room air. The physical examination identified oral aphthous ulcers and rash resembling folliculitis present on both his face and trunk (see figure 1A, B, and C). Cardiopulmonary and abdominal evaluations were within normal limits. Manual muscle testing demonstrated intact strength in both the upper and lower limbs. No peripheral edema was noted, and clinical assessment indicated he was euvolemic. He was admitted for additional diagnostic workup.

Investigations:

Initial laboratory evaluations demonstrated significant hyponatraemia (124 mmol/L), elevated creatine kinase (1349 U/L), increased aspartate aminotransferase (77 U/L) and alanine aminotransferase (64 U/L), as well as a markedly raised C-reactive protein (CRP) concentration (180 mg/L; reference, <3 mg/L) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (70 mm/hour; reference, <15 mm/hour). Renal function was within normal limits. The patient exhibited hyperglycaemia (plasma glucose 14.2 mmol/L), along with an increased HbA1c (8.2%), consistent with inadequate glycaemic management. Markers for hepatitis B and C were negative.

Endocrine testing indicated normal thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH, 1.11 mIU/L; reference, 0.50–5.00 mIU/L), free thyroxine (T4, 1.25 ng/dL; reference, 0.90–1.70 ng/dL), and serum cortisol (42 µg/dL; reference, >6 µg/dL) levels. Serum osmolality was decreased (267 mOsm/kg; reference, <270 mOsm/kg), while urine osmolality was notably high (900 mOsm/kg; reference, >300 mOsm/kg) and urinary sodium excretion was elevated (88 mmol/L; reference, >20 mmol/L), indicating a disturbance in water clearance. In spite of hypo-osmolar hyponatraemia, antidiuretic hormone (ADH) level was increased (22.4 pg/mL). Electrocardiogram appeared normal, and imaging studies including chest X-ray and CT scans of the head, chest, and abdomen revealed no abnormalities. The constellation of findings was consistent with a diagnosis of SIADH.

By the third hospital day, the patient experienced increasing severity of headache and vertigo. Despite the absence of nuchal rigidity or cranial nerve deficits, central nervous system (CNS) inflammation was considered. MRI of the brain showed no notable pathology affecting the hypothalamus or other cerebral areas (see figure 2). Examination of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) demonstrated a mildly raised opening pressure (17 cmH₂O), elevated protein concentration (0.97 g/L), and a modest lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL). CSF cultures, as well as assays for bacterial and mycobacterial organisms, herpes simplex virus (HSV), varicella zoster virus (VZV), and cryptococcal antigen, returned negative results. Cytological assessment of the CSF did not reveal malignant cells.

Additional diagnostic workup was undertaken to investigate the possibility of systemic disease. Assessment for sarcoidosis included lysozyme levels of 5.1 µg/mL (reference range 5–10) and angiotensin-converting enzyme at 11.4 U/L (reference range 7–25), both within normal limits. Hematologic malignancy screening identified elevated soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) at 704 U/mL (reference, 122–496 U/mL), but testing for human T-cell leukemia virus type 1 antibody was negative.

By the fifth day, he developed a painful ulcer on the scrotum (see figure 1D). Along with the presence of oral aphthous ulcers and folliculitis-like eruptions, these manifestations pointed toward a systemic variant of BD. He did not demonstrate a pathergy reaction to needle prick. Eye examination found no evidence of uveitis. HLA-B51 testing was positive.

Differential diagnosis:

Upon admission, the presence of fever, headache, general malaise, and hyponatremia indicated a possible central nervous system (CNS) infection or a systemic inflammatory process. The leading suspicion was viral meningoencephalitis, specifically due to HSV or VZV. Nonetheless, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis for viral antigens and antibodies returned negative, effectively ruling out these viral etiologies. Bacterial meningitis was also contemplated; however, this was discounted owing to the lack of neutrophilic dominance, only a mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, and negative CSF cultures. Tuberculous meningitis was eliminated as a diagnosis as both acid-fast bacilli stains and tuberculosis DNA testing were negative.

Consideration was given to a primary CNS neoplasm in light of the neurological manifestations. However, no abnormalities were seen on brain MRI, and CSF cytology was negative for malignancy. The slight increase in serum sIL-2R did not meet the threshold to support a diagnosis of CNS lymphoma.

Screening for autoimmune disorders yielded no supportive features, with both characteristic systemic manifestations and autoantibody screening tests being negative. Neurosarcoidosis was briefly considered since initial MRI scans can appear normal; however, further laboratory findings made this diagnosis unlikely. The modestly elevated sIL-2R level was thus interpreted as non-specific.

The clinical diagnosis of definitive Behçet’s disease (BD) was made, based on the patient’s presentation of fever and fatigue, two instances of oral aphthae (two points), two occurrences of genital aphthosis (two points), and a rash resembling folliculitis (one point). As per the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease, the patient accumulated five points (where ≥4 confirms the diagnosis). Genital aphthosis, in particular, is recognized as a distinguishing sign of BD, thereby strongly supporting this diagnosis. With alternative infectious, neoplastic, and autoimmune explanations excluded, acute neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) was diagnosed, characterized by CNS involvement alongside mucocutaneous symptoms.

Treatment:

On the first day of hospitalization, clinicians suspected an underlying inflammatory process; however, the specific cause had yet to be identified. Empirical cefepime therapy was started following collection of blood cultures, aiming to cover possible bacterial pathogens in the absence of a definitive source of infection.

By the third day, worsening headache and vertigo were noted, raising concern for involvement of the CNS by either an inflammatory or infectious process. Acyclovir was given empirically but discontinued after laboratory evaluation excluded HSV and VZV encephalitis.

On day eight, colchicine therapy commenced subsequent to diagnoses of systemic Behçet’s disease (BD) and acute neuro-BD. Topical corticosteroid ointment was used to manage oral aphthous ulcers, while silver sulfadiazine cream addressed both genital and skin lesions. Syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) was treated with conservative measures, including fluid restriction to less than 800 mL per day, meticulous daily serum sodium monitoring, and prevention of rapid sodium correction. Hypertonic saline and vasopressin receptor antagonists were not utilized.

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient’s fever subsided, and his appetite returned the day after beginning colchicine therapy. Over the next two weeks, his symptoms—including vertigo, general malaise, hiccups, and mucocutaneous lesions—progressively improved. By day 16, his serum sodium had reached a normal value of 136 mmol/L, and ADH concentration had declined to 1.8 pg/mL. Levels of myogenic enzymes also normalized.

Notably, the patient did not receive systemic corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive agents throughout treatment. After a one-month hospital stay—during which acute-phase recovery was achieved, hyponatraemia was closely observed and managed, and rehabilitation addressed diminished physical capacity—the patient was discharged. Colchicine was continued for roughly four years, during which there were no recurrences of BD or neurological manifestations. Once his BD was under control, biguanide therapy was started, leading to sustained adequate glycaemic control.

Discussion:

This case features the concurrent identification of systemic BD and acute neuro-BD, an infrequent clinical occurrence. It further illustrates that acute neuro-BD may be accompanied by SIADH. The improvement in mucocutaneous signs, neurological symptoms, and hyponatraemia following treatment with colchicine monotherapy raises the possibility of a direct linkage among BD, neuro-BD, and SIADH. When patients diagnosed with BD present with fever or headache and are found to have hyponatraemia, clinicians should evaluate for acute neuro-BD as a potential underlying etiology.

The distinctive aspect of this case lies in the simultaneous diagnosis of both systemic BD and acute neuro-BD, as indicated by both mucocutaneous involvement and central nervous system pathology. Ordinarily, significant organ complications such as CNS lesions develop several years—on average, four—after BD is first identified, with neurological involvement as an initial manifestation occurring in fewer than 5% of patients. Only about 10% of individuals with neuro-BD report headaches that are attributable to the neurological involvement itself. As a result, identifying neuro-BD at the onset was intrinsically challenging in this patient. Acute neuro-BD tends to manifest as self-limited meningoencephalitis, which may or may not include focal neurological signs. In this instance, MRI scans of the brain did not detect abnormalities in conventional sites of BD involvement, such as the hypothalamic area. Acute neuro-BD may present in the form of aseptic meningitis even without detectable parenchymal lesions, and a normal MRI—especially in the early disease stages—does not preclude the diagnosis. The absence of imaging findings complicated the diagnostic process further. Although HLA-B51 testing was conducted, it did not aid in confirming the diagnosis; this is consistent with the international BD diagnostic criteria, which are predicated on clinical findings rather than HLA-B51 status. This case underscores the significance of considering neuro-BD in patients who have mucocutaneous manifestations along with vague neurological complaints.

At the outset of the differential diagnosis, we placed emphasis on infectious etiologies for CNS inflammation, given the presence of unexplained fever, neurological manifestations, and hyponatraemia, but ultimately did not detect any causative pathogens. Alternative sources of CNS inflammation were also explored, such as connective tissue disorders and malignancies; sarcoidosis and hematologic malignancies were included in our considerations. Thus, our initial comprehensive assessment involved testing for autoimmune serological markers, as well as measuring serum ACE and soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) as part of preliminary screening. These investigations were applied in a restricted fashion, serving primarily to rule out conditions rather than being guided by definitive clinical indications. No supporting clinical, imaging, or cerebrospinal fluid evidence for these diseases was identified, and the test results did not significantly aid the diagnostic process. The sensitivity of ACE for detecting sarcoidosis is limited, while sIL-2R may assist in the assessment of malignant lymphoma, but slight elevations lack specificity and can simply signal inflammation. In this scenario, the initial lack of genital ulceration rendered the diagnosis of BD uncertain, necessitating a broad range of differential diagnoses. This case underlines the pitfalls of non-targeted laboratory testing and stresses that such evaluations should be reserved for instances with relevant clinical indications and interpreted judiciously.

While drug-induced SIADH has been documented in individuals with BD who are treated with agents like cyclophosphamide, the link between acute neuro-BD and SIADH is not well clarified. Neuro-BD is marked by inflammation within the central nervous system, which could lead to abnormal secretion of ADH. Multiple reports have demonstrated that interleukin-6 (IL-6), a proinflammatory cytokine often elevated in neuroinflammatory diseases, is correlated with enhanced ADH production. In Behçet’s disease, dysregulated activation of monocytes results in spontaneous IL-6 release, and raised IL-6 concentrations have been found in the cerebrospinal fluid of patients experiencing acute neuro-BD. These observations imply that IL-6-driven inflammation may play a role in the development of SIADH in this context. In this particular patient, although IL-6 concentrations in serum or CSF were not evaluated, the significantly increased serum CRP may indicate an inflammatory process mediated by IL-6. SIADH occurs in connection with a variety of underlying conditions, such as inflammation of the CNS, malignancies, pulmonary disorders, and the use of specific drugs. Nonetheless, the data from this case are insufficient to firmly establish a direct causal relationship between SIADH and acute neuro-BD. The observed temporal relationship could instead suggest a nonspecific reaction to CNS inflammation, rather than a process unique to neuro-BD.

This case further demonstrates the possible complications associated with hyponatraemia due to SIADH occurring alongside acute neuro-BD. Previous literature has indicated that persistent hiccups may be an important clinical indicator of significant hyponatraemia arising from SIADH secondary to central nervous system inflammation, such as meningitis. Moreover, creatine kinase was assessed during the initial workup for fever, arthralgia, and general fatigue—symptoms which could be suggestive of inflammatory or metabolic insult to the muscles—and the results showed rhabdomyolysis, a known but frequently missed consequence of marked hyponatraemia that is not typically assessed. Although these findings are recognized sequelae, they often go underappreciated in clinical practice. It is important for clinicians to recognize that symptoms not obviously related—such as hiccups or rhabdomyolysis—might signal an underlying electrolyte imbalance, especially in patients with neuroinflammatory diseases.

Corticosteroids represent the cornerstone of therapy for acute neuro-BD, with the majority of patients exhibiting positive responses. Nevertheless, cases characterized only by meningoencephalitis-like features have been documented to resolve without intervention. In the case described here, treatment with colchicine alone led to improvement in both mucocutaneous and neurological symptoms, as well as correction of hyponatraemia. Colchicine acts as an anti-inflammatory by blocking neutrophil chemotaxis and activation, and by decreasing the production of cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-6. While colchicine is commonly administered for mucocutaneous involvement in BD, evidence from systematic reviews indicates it does not benefit patients with acute neuro-BD, for whom immunosuppressive drugs are generally necessary for sufficient disease management. Indeed, in previously published cases, colchicine has typically been administered alongside corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive agents, rather than used alone. Though a spontaneous remission cannot be definitively ruled out in this instance, these observations raise the possibility that colchicine may be an effective option in managing acute neuro-BD and associated complications such as SIADH, especially in circumstances where corticosteroids cannot be employed or are contraindicated.

Conclusion:

We report an uncommon presentation of Behçet's disease (BD) occurring simultaneously with acute neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), where neuro-BD was further complicated by the development of syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). This observation underscores that SIADH can emerge as a sequela of acute neuro-BD and emphasizes the need for clinicians to recognize this possible link. Administration of colchicine as monotherapy resulted in rapid resolution of mucocutaneous and neurological symptoms as well as normalization of sodium levels, indicating colchicine might serve as an effective initial treatment strategy in early neuro-BD. For patients with BD who exhibit vague central nervous system symptoms such as headache and fever in the setting of hyponatraemia, clinicians should strongly consider the possibility of acute neuro-BD complicated by SIADH.
