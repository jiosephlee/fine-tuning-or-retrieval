Title: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

Eiichi Kakehi, Yukako Matsumoto, Shigehisa Sakurai, Kazuhiko Kotani

Abstract:

Summary: We describe an unusual presentation of acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) occurring simultaneously with systemic Behçet’s disease (BD), which met the criteria for definite BD and was further complicated by syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). A male patient in his 60s arrived with fever, headache, and mucocutaneous symptoms, including oral aphthae, folliculitis-like eruptions, genital ulcerations, and hyponatraemia. BD was diagnosed based on the mucocutaneous features. Magnetic resonance imaging of the brain revealed no abnormalities; however, analysis of cerebrospinal fluid demonstrated findings consistent with aseptic meningitis, supporting an acute neuro-BD diagnosis. Laboratory investigations confirmed SIADH according to diagnostic criteria. The patient’s mucocutaneous and neurological symptoms, as well as hyponatraemia, resolved following treatment with colchicine alone, with no need for corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive agents. This case indicates a potential link between acute neuro-BD and SIADH and highlights the need to recognize the simultaneous appearance of neurological and endocrine symptoms in those newly diagnosed with BD.

Background:

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a chronic inflammatory condition affecting multiple organ systems, primarily defined by recurrent oral and genital ulcerations, uveitis, and cutaneous manifestations. Neurological complications, collectively termed neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), are observed in roughly 10%–20% of BD cases and generally arise several years following the initial BD diagnosis. In its acute presentation, neuro-BD commonly manifests as meningoencephalitis and parenchymal lesions within the brain, with a predilection for the brainstem. While a delayed onset is typical, simultaneous presentation of BD and neuro-BD at initial diagnosis is uncommon. In these instances, healthcare providers should carefully evaluate for both mucocutaneous signs and neurological complaints, such as headache, behavioral or psychiatric disturbances, and localized neurological deficits.

Syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) is a recognized etiology of hyponatremia, frequently precipitated by central nervous system (CNS) inflammation, neoplasms, or exposure to specific pharmaceuticals. Due to the inflammatory profile of acute neuro-BD, patients are potentially susceptible to developing SIADH. Although SIADH has been described in BD patients treated with agents like cyclophosphamide, cases where SIADH arises exclusively from CNS inflammation secondary to acute neuro-BD, absent any medication-related triggers, have not been clearly characterized.

We present an unusual instance of simultaneous BD and acute neuro-BD diagnosis, further complicated by the development of SIADH. This report underscores the need to recognize SIADH as a possible clinical feature of acute neuro-BD, particularly in individuals with CNS symptoms that lack specificity accompanied by hyponatremia.

Case presentation:

A man of Asian descent in his 60s arrived at the emergency department with complaints of fever, headache, general fatigue, reduced appetite, persistent hiccups, and joint pain involving the shoulders and ankles over the preceding 3 days. He additionally noted the presence of oral aphthous ulcers.

His past medical history was notable for type 2 diabetes mellitus, though he was untreated due to limited understanding of the disease and lack of adherence to diet or exercise recommendations. There was no significant family history, particularly regarding BD. He reported occasional alcohol intake, denied tobacco use, recent travel, or contact with sick persons or animals. He was employed in the metalworking sector and reported no recent sexual activity.

During assessment, the patient was alert but showed signs of fatigue. Recorded vital signs included a temperature of 38.1°C, blood pressure of 135/73 mm Hg, heart rate of 119 beats per minute (regular), respiratory rate of 19 per minute, and oxygen saturation at 96% while breathing room air. Inspection identified oral aphthae as well as folliculitis-like eruptions on the face and trunk (figure 1A, B, and C). Findings from the cardiopulmonary and abdominal examinations were within normal limits. Manual muscle testing demonstrated no weakness in either the upper or lower limbs. No peripheral oedema was detected, and clinical evaluation indicated a euvolemic state. The patient was hospitalized for further investigation.

Investigations:

Initial laboratory evaluation demonstrated significant hyponatraemia (124 mmol/L), increased creatine kinase (1349 U/L), aspartate aminotransferase (77 U/L), alanine aminotransferase (64 U/L), elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) at 180 mg/L (reference range <3 mg/L), and erythrocyte sedimentation rate of 70 mm/hour (reference <15 mm/hour), while renal function remained within normal limits. Hyperglycaemia was noted (plasma glucose 14.2 mmol/L), accompanied by a high HbA1c (8.2%), reflecting suboptimal glycaemic management. Testing for hepatitis B and C yielded negative results.

Assessment of endocrine function revealed thyroid-stimulating hormone at 1.11 mIU/L (reference 0.50–5.00 mIU/L), free T4 of 1.25 ng/dL (reference 0.90–1.70 ng/dL), and serum cortisol at 42 µg/dL (reference >6 µg/dL), all within reference intervals. Serum osmolality was decreased (267 mOsm/kg; reference <270 mOsm/kg), whereas urine osmolality was increased (900 mOsm/kg; reference >300 mOsm/kg), and urinary sodium was raised (88 mmol/L; reference >20 mmol/L), indicating defective water elimination. Despite the presence of hypo-osmolar hyponatraemia, antidiuretic hormone (ADH) was found to be elevated (22.4 pg/mL). Electrocardiogram was normal, and imaging studies including chest X-ray and CT scans of the head, chest, and abdomen did not show any abnormalities. Collectively, these data were indicative of SIADH.

On the third day, the patient's headache and vertigo intensified. Despite the absence of neck rigidity or involvement of cranial nerves, central nervous system (CNS) inflammation was considered. MRI of the brain showed no notable anomalies in the hypothalamus or other brain areas (figure 2). Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) studies demonstrated a mildly raised opening pressure (17 cmH₂O), elevated protein concentration (0.97 g/L), and slight lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL). Testing for bacterial, mycobacterial, HSV, VZV, and cryptococcal antigens in the CSF returned negative, as did cultures. CSF cytology detected no malignancy.

Additional work-up was initiated to rule out systemic illnesses. Evaluation for sarcoidosis produced normal results for lysozyme (5.1 µg/mL; reference range: 5–10) and angiotensin-converting enzyme (11.4 U/L; reference range: 7–25). Testing for hematological malignancy yielded elevated soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) levels (704 U/mL; reference, 122–496 U/mL), and human T-cell leukemia virus type 1 antibody was negative.

By day 5, a painful ulcer had developed on his scrotum (figure 1D). Taken together with oral aphthae and skin eruptions resembling folliculitis, these manifestations pointed toward a systemic variant of BD. No pinprick hypersensitivity was present. Ophthalmological assessment did not show evidence of uveitis. HLA-B51 testing was positive.

Differential diagnosis:

Upon admission, the concurrence of fever, headache, malaise, and hyponatremia raised suspicion for an infection of the central nervous system or a systemic inflammatory condition. Initial consideration centered on viral meningoencephalitis, especially due to herpes simplex virus (HSV) or varicella zoster virus (VZV). Nevertheless, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis was negative for both viral antigens and antibodies, thereby ruling out these viral etiologies. Bacterial meningitis was another possibility; however, its diagnosis was discounted because there was no predominance of neutrophils in the CSF, only a slight lymphocytic pleocytosis, and all CSF cultures were negative. Tuberculous meningitis was similarly ruled out in view of negative acid-fast bacilli staining and tuberculosis DNA assay results.

A primary central nervous system neoplasm was considered as an explanation for the neurological manifestations. This was dismissed owing to a normal brain MRI and lack of malignant cells on CSF cytology. Although the soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) level was modestly elevated, this alone did not support the diagnosis of CNS lymphoma.

Autoimmune disorders were investigated and ultimately discounted because the patient did not display specific systemic symptoms, and autoantibody screening was negative. Neurosarcoidosis remained in the differential diagnosis—given that magnetic resonance imaging findings can initially be unremarkable—but further laboratory testing did not support this diagnosis. The slightly raised sIL-2R was interpreted as a nonspecific finding.

A definitive diagnosis of Behçet’s disease (BD) was reached on the basis of clinical features: fever, fatigue, oral aphthae (two points), genital aphthosis (two points), and a folliculitis-like skin eruption (one point), adding up to five points per the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (with four or more points required for a definitive diagnosis). Genital aphthosis, in particular, is recognized as a specific indicator of BD, strongly supporting this diagnosis. After systematically excluding infectious, neoplastic, and autoimmune origins, a diagnosis of acute neuro-Behçet’s disease was made, characterized by CNS involvement alongside mucocutaneous features.

Treatment:

On the first day of hospitalization, clinicians considered the possibility of an underlying inflammatory disorder, though its precise cause was not yet determined. Empiric treatment with cefepime was begun after obtaining blood cultures, aiming to address a possible bacterial infection, despite the lack of a clear infectious source.

By the third day, the patient’s headache and dizziness had intensified, raising concerns about central nervous system inflammation or infection. Acyclovir was given empirically but subsequently discontinued once diagnostic tests excluded herpes simplex virus and varicella-zoster virus encephalitis.

On the eighth day, colchicine therapy was initiated following the diagnoses of systemic Behçet’s disease (BD) and acute neuro-BD. Oral aphthae were managed with topical corticosteroid ointment, while genital ulcers and skin lesions were treated using silver sulfadiazine cream. Syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) was addressed through conservative measures: restricting fluid intake to less than 800 mL per day, closely monitoring daily sodium levels, and avoiding rapid sodium correction. The use of hypertonic saline and vasopressin receptor antagonists was unnecessary.

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient’s fever subsided and his appetite returned the day following initiation of colchicine therapy. Over the next two weeks, symptoms such as vertigo, general malaise, hiccups, and mucocutaneous manifestations progressively lessened. By day 16, his serum sodium had normalized to 136 mmol/L, and ADH concentration decreased to 1.8 pg/mL. Muscle enzyme levels also returned within normal limits.

Notably, systemic corticosteroids and additional immunosuppressants were not administered throughout the course of treatment. After a month-long hospitalization—in which he recovered from the acute episode, received vigilant monitoring and management of his hyponatremia, and underwent rehabilitation for physical deconditioning—the patient was discharged. Colchicine was maintained for around four years with no recurrence of Behçet's disease or neurological symptoms during this period. Once his Behçet's disease remained stable, biguanide treatment was commenced, resulting in effective glycaemic control thereafter.

Discussion:

This patient presented with both systemic BD and acute neuro-BD diagnosed concurrently, a scenario seldom reported in clinical practice. The case further revealed that acute neuro-BD may be accompanied by SIADH. The complete improvement of mucocutaneous and neurological symptoms as well as the correction of hyponatraemia following colchicine alone implies a potential direct correlation between BD, neuro-BD, and SIADH. Clinicians should be aware of acute neuro-BD as an underlying diagnosis in BD patients exhibiting fever or headache in conjunction with hyponatraemia.

This report is significant for the concurrent identification of systemic BD and acute neuro-BD, as indicated by mucocutaneous and central nervous system manifestations. Ordinarily, significant organ involvement—such as CNS pathology—emerges approximately four years after the initial diagnosis of BD, and neurological symptoms as an early presentation are infrequent, affecting less than 5% of cases. Additionally, only about 10% of patients develop headaches attributable to neuro-BD. Thus, early detection of neuro-BD in this patient posed considerable challenges. Acute neuro-BD is most frequently observed as a transient meningoencephalitic process, with or without focal neurologic symptoms. In this instance, the brain MRI was unremarkable for characteristic BD-related lesions, such as those involving the hypothalamus. Acute neuro-BD can manifest as aseptic meningitis even in the absence of observable brain parenchymal abnormalities, and a normal MRI—especially at the outset—does not rule out the condition. The absence of radiological abnormalities made diagnosis more difficult. Although HLA-B51 analysis was undertaken, it did not aid in confirming the diagnosis. This observation is consistent with international BD diagnostic criteria, which prioritize clinical findings rather than HLA-B51 status. This case underscores the need for clinicians to consider neuro-BD in patients who have mucocutaneous symptoms and nonspecific neurological complaints.

At the outset of our differential diagnosis, infection was considered the leading potential cause of CNS inflammation, given the patient's unexplained fever, neurological manifestations, and hyponatremia, although no definitive pathogen was identified. Alternative etiologies for CNS inflammation, such as connective tissue disorders and neoplastic conditions, were also evaluated, and sarcoidosis as well as hematologic malignancies were specifically included in the differential. Thus, as part of an initial comprehensive workup, we conducted serological testing for autoimmune markers, as well as assessments of serum ACE and sIL-2R, utilizing these assays as exclusionary screening tools rather than being guided by robust clinical suspicion. No clinical presentation, imaging, or CSF analyses substantiated these diagnoses, and these ancillary investigations did not ultimately influence the diagnostic outcome. It should be noted that ACE demonstrates limited sensitivity in diagnosing sarcoidosis, and while sIL-2R can be helpful in considering malignant lymphoma, slight increases are nonspecific and may simply represent a generalized inflammatory state. In this scenario, the lack of genital ulcers in the early disease course rendered a definitive diagnosis of BD questionable and led us to consider a broad spectrum of differential diagnoses. The approach taken in this case underscores the necessity of limiting indiscriminate diagnostic testing, reinforcing the principle that such investigations should be based on relevant clinical findings and interpreted judiciously.

While SIADH triggered by medications such as cyclophosphamide has been documented in patients with BD, the relationship between acute neuro-BD and SIADH remains unclear. Neuro-BD involves inflammation within the central nervous system, which could potentially disrupt the regulation of ADH secretion. Research has indicated that interleukin-6 (IL-6), a proinflammatory cytokine commonly increased in neuroinflammatory conditions, is correlated with heightened ADH release. In BD, abnormal monocyte activation results in unprompted IL-6 production, and patients with acute neuro-BD have exhibited increased levels of IL-6 in their CSF. These observations suggest that inflammation driven by IL-6 might play a role in SIADH development in this context. Although IL-6 concentrations were not evaluated in the serum or CSF in the present case, the notably elevated serum CRP could serve as a marker for underlying IL-6–related inflammation. While SIADH is linked to a variety of disorders—including CNS inflammation, lung disease, cancer, and specific drugs—the evidence presented does not support a definitive causative connection between SIADH and acute neuro-BD. The timing observed in this instance may indicate a generalized response to CNS inflammation as opposed to a process unique to the disease.

This case further demonstrates the range of complications associated with hyponatraemia resulting from SIADH in acute neuro-BD. Earlier literature has noted that persistent hiccups can be a significant indicator of severe hyponatraemia due to SIADH related to CNS inflammation, such as meningitis. As part of the initial assessment for fever, arthralgia, and generalized fatigue—which may stem from inflammatory or metabolic muscle injury—we obtained CK levels, uncovering rhabdomyolysis. This is a recognized, yet frequently overlooked, consequence of severe hyponatraemia that is not routinely assessed. While these presentations are known, their clinical significance in the context of profound hyponatraemia is often underestimated. Clinicians should consider that seemingly unrelated symptoms, including hiccups or rhabdomyolysis, may signal underlying electrolyte imbalances, especially amid neuroinflammatory disorders.

Corticosteroids remain the standard therapy for acute neuro-Behçet's disease (neuro-BD), with most individuals experiencing positive outcomes. Nonetheless, spontaneous remission has been documented in instances where presentation was limited to meningoencephalitis-like symptoms. In this particular case, administration of colchicine as monotherapy led to improvements in mucocutaneous and neurological manifestations, along with resolution of hyponatraemia. Colchicine provides anti-inflammatory effects by preventing neutrophil movement and activation, and by inhibiting production of cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-6. While colchicine is commonly prescribed for mucocutaneous lesions in BD, evidence from systematic reviews indicates its lack of efficacy in acute neuro-BD, and that disease control typically necessitates immunosuppressants. Existing reports describe colchicine being given alongside corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive medications, rather than used alone. Though spontaneous recovery cannot be definitively ruled out in this situation, our observations raise the possibility that colchicine may serve as a valuable alternative for acute neuro-BD and associated complications like SIADH, especially when steroid use is inadvisable or contraindicated.

Conclusion:

We reported an unusual presentation of Behçet's disease (BD) and acute neuro-Behçet's disease diagnosed simultaneously, in which syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) occurred as a complication of neuro-BD. This case underscores that SIADH can emerge in the context of acute neuro-BD, emphasizing the need for clinicians to recognize this possible association. Treatment with colchicine alone resulted in prompt resolution of mucocutaneous manifestations, neurological symptoms, and hyponatraemia, indicating colchicine may offer an effective therapeutic choice in the initial stage of neuro-BD. For BD patients exhibiting non-specific central nervous system complaints, including fever and headache accompanied by hyponatraemia, clinicians should strongly consider acute neuro-BD, particularly when SIADH is present.
