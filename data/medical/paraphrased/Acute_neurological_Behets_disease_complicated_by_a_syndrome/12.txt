Title: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

Eiichi Kakehi, Yukako Matsumoto, Shigehisa Sakurai, Kazuhiko Kotani

Abstract:

Summary: We describe an uncommon instance of acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) diagnosed simultaneously with systemic Behçet’s disease (BD), meeting criteria for definite BD, and further complicated by the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). A male patient in his 60s presented with fever, headache, and mucocutaneous manifestations, which included oral aphthae, folliculitis-like eruptions, genital ulcers, and hyponatraemia. The mucocutaneous lesions supported a diagnosis of BD. Magnetic resonance imaging of the brain was unremarkable; however, cerebrospinal fluid examination indicated aseptic meningitis, aligning with acute neuro-BD. Laboratory data fulfilled the SIADH diagnostic criteria. Treatment with colchicine alone was sufficient to resolve mucocutaneous findings, neurologic symptoms, and hyponatraemia, obviating the requirement for corticosteroids or additional immunosuppressive therapies. This case highlights a potential link between acute neuro-BD and SIADH and emphasizes the need to consider both neurological and endocrine features in patients with newly identified BD.

Background:

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a persistent, multisystem inflammatory condition that presents with recurrent oral and genital ulcers, uveitis, and dermatological manifestations. Neurological complications, collectively termed neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), are observed in roughly 10%–20% of cases, typically arising several years following the initial BD diagnosis. Acute presentations of neuro-BD most often feature meningoencephalitis and parenchymal lesions, with the brainstem commonly affected. Concurrent diagnosis of both BD and neuro-BD at onset is unusual, as neuro-BD usually develops later. In instances of simultaneous presentation, it is important for clinicians to evaluate both mucocutaneous findings and neurological complaints, such as headache, behavioral disturbances, psychiatric features, and focal neurologic impairments.

Syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) constitutes a recognized etiology of hyponatraemia and is frequently attributable to central nervous system (CNS) inflammatory processes, neoplasms, or exposure to certain drugs. The inflammatory basis of acute neuro-BD suggests that these patients may be predisposed to SIADH. While SIADH has been documented in BD individuals receiving therapies like cyclophosphamide, there is limited evidence regarding SIADH developing exclusively due to CNS inflammation from acute neuro-BD in the absence of drug-related causes.

We describe an uncommon case in which BD and acute neuro-BD were identified simultaneously, further complicated by SIADH. This case underscores the necessity of recognizing SIADH as a possible complication of acute neuro-BD, especially among patients who present with both ambiguous CNS symptoms and hyponatraemia.

Case presentation:

A man of Asian descent in his 60s arrived at the emergency department after experiencing three days of fever, headache, general fatigue, decreased appetite, ongoing hiccups, and joint pain localized to his shoulders and ankles. He additionally described the presence of oral aphthous ulcers.

His past medical history was notable for type 2 diabetes mellitus, though he was not on any therapy due to minimal awareness of the diagnosis and lack of adherence to dietary or exercise recommendations. There was no significant family history, with no relatives diagnosed with BD. He reported infrequent alcohol use, was a non-smoker, had not recently traveled, and denied any contact with animals or individuals who were ill. He was employed in the metalworking sector and had not participated in recent sexual activity.

Upon assessment, the patient was conscious but showed signs of fatigue. Recorded vital signs included a temperature of 38.1°C, blood pressure measured at 135/73 mm Hg, heart rate of 119 beats per minute with a regular rhythm, respiratory rate of 19 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation of 96% while breathing ambient air. Physical examination identified oral aphthous ulcers and rash resembling folliculitis present on the face and trunk (refer to figure 1A, B, and C). The cardiopulmonary and abdominal examinations were without significant findings. Manual muscle strength testing did not demonstrate weakness in either the upper or lower limbs. There was no evidence of peripheral edema, and his clinical assessment indicated euvolemic status. He was hospitalized for additional workup.

Investigations:

Initial blood tests showed significant hyponatremia (124 mmol/L), raised creatine kinase (1349 U/L), increased aspartate aminotransferase (77 U/L), alanine aminotransferase (64 U/L), elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) at 180 mg/L (normal <3 mg/L), and a high erythrocyte sedimentation rate (70 mm/hour; reference <15 mm/hour), while renal function remained within normal limits. Additionally, the patient had hyperglycemia (plasma glucose 14.2 mmol/L) and a high HbA1c of 8.2%, pointing to inadequate glycemic control. Hepatitis B and C serologies were negative.

Hormonal testing showed thyroid-stimulating hormone at 1.11 mIU/L (normal range, 0.50–5.00 mIU/L), free T4 at 1.25 ng/dL (reference, 0.90–1.70 ng/dL), and serum cortisol at 42 µg/dL (reference, >6 µg/dL), all within standard parameters. The serum osmolality measured 267 mOsm/kg (reference, <270 mOsm/kg), which was low, whereas urine osmolality was increased (900 mOsm/kg; reference, >300 mOsm/kg), and urinary sodium was elevated at 88 mmol/L (reference, >20 mmol/L), indicating reduced water clearance. In the presence of hypo-osmolar hyponatremia, the antidiuretic hormone (ADH) concentration was raised at 22.4 pg/mL. Electrocardiogram findings were normal, and imaging studies—including chest X-ray and CT scans of the head, chest, and abdomen—showed no abnormalities. Altogether, these results were consistent with a diagnosis of SIADH.

On the third day, his headache and vertigo intensified. Despite the absence of neck stiffness or cranial nerve deficits, central nervous system (CNS) inflammation was considered. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain showed no notable abnormalities within the hypothalamus or other cerebral regions (see figure 2). Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) examination demonstrated a slightly raised opening pressure of 17 cmH₂O, elevated protein at 0.97 g/L, and mild lymphocytic pleocytosis with 11 cells/µL. Cultures and assays of the CSF for bacterial and mycobacterial organisms, herpes simplex virus (HSV), varicella zoster virus (VZV), and cryptococcal antigen were all negative. CSF cytology did not detect any malignant cells.

Additional diagnostic work-up was undertaken to assess for systemic diseases. Evaluation for sarcoidosis yielded a normal lysozyme concentration (5.1 µg/mL; reference range 5–10) and angiotensin-converting enzyme level (11.4 U/L; reference 7–25). Screening for hematologic malignancy showed an elevated soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) at 704 U/mL (reference 122–496 U/mL), with negative testing for human T-cell leukemia virus type 1 antibodies.

By the fifth day, the patient developed a painful ulcer in the scrotal region (figure 1D). Along with oral aphthous ulcers and folliculitis-like eruptions, these signs were indicative of a systemic type of Behçet’s disease (BD). There was no observed hypersensitivity to pinprick testing. An ophthalmology assessment showed no evidence of uveitis. Testing for HLA-B51 was positive.

Differential diagnosis:

Upon presentation, the concurrent presence of fever, headache, general malaise, and hyponatraemia raised concerns for a central nervous system infection or a systemic inflammatory process. Initial suspicion centered on viral meningoencephalitis, particularly from HSV or VZV, but cerebrospinal fluid studies failed to detect viral antigens or antibodies, effectively ruling these out. Bacterial meningitis was also contemplated but discounted due to the lack of neutrophil predominance, a mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, and negative CSF cultures. Tests for acid-fast bacilli and Mycobacterium tuberculosis DNA were negative, thus tuberculous meningitis was excluded.

Consideration was also given to a primary CNS neoplasm in light of the neurological manifestations; however, no abnormalities were seen on brain MRI, and CSF cytological evaluation did not identify malignant cells. Although serum sIL-2R levels showed a mild elevation, this single abnormality was insufficient to diagnose CNS lymphoma.

Autoimmune etiologies were investigated and dismissed, as the patient did not display characteristic systemic manifestations and autoantibody screenings returned negative. Neurosarcoidosis was included in the differential since MRI can appear normal in early disease; nonetheless, subsequent laboratory data made this diagnosis unlikely, and the modest sIL-2R elevation was interpreted as a non-specific result.

A definitive diagnosis of Behçet’s disease (BD) was established on clinical grounds, including febrile episodes, fatigue, oral aphthae (awarding two points), genital aphthosis (two points), and a folliculitis-like cutaneous eruption (one point). The patient’s cumulative score met the threshold for definite disease per the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (five points, with ≥4 indicating certainty). The presence of genital aphthosis is notably specific to BD, and, together with the other findings, strongly supports this diagnosis. Following exclusion of infectious, neoplastic, and autoimmune sources, a diagnosis of acute neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) was made, characterized by neurological symptoms accompanying mucocutaneous involvement.

Treatment:

On the first day of hospitalization, clinicians considered an underlying inflammatory disorder, though its precise cause was still indeterminate. Broad-spectrum antibiotic coverage with cefepime was begun empirically following the collection of blood cultures, in case of bacterial infection, despite a lack of a clear infectious source.

By the third day, the patient’s headache and vertigo intensified, raising concerns about central nervous system inflammation or infection. Empiric acyclovir therapy was initiated but subsequently stopped when HSV and VZV encephalitis were excluded by diagnostic testing.

On the eighth day, colchicine therapy commenced after systemic BD and acute neuro-BD were diagnosed. Oral aphthous ulcers were managed using a topical corticosteroid ointment, while genital and skin lesions were treated with silver sulfadiazine cream. The syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) was addressed conservatively by restricting fluid intake to under 800 mL per day, with vigilant monitoring of daily serum sodium levels and avoidance of overly rapid sodium correction. Hypertonic saline and vasopressin receptor antagonists were not deemed necessary.

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient's fever subsided, and his appetite improved within one day of beginning colchicine therapy. Over the next two weeks, symptoms such as vertigo, general malaise, hiccups, and mucocutaneous lesions gradually subsided. By day 16, his serum sodium had stabilized at 136 mmol/L, while ADH levels dropped to 1.8 pg/mL. Myogenic enzyme concentrations also normalized.

Notably, systemic corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive agents were not administered during his treatment. The patient was discharged following a one-month hospitalization, during which he recovered from the acute episode, received careful monitoring and management of his hyponatremia, and underwent rehabilitation to address his physical deconditioning. Colchicine treatment was maintained for around four years, during which there was no relapse of Behçet's disease (BD) or return of neurological issues. Once his BD reached stability, biguanide treatment was started, resulting in sustained effective glycemic control.

Discussion:

This report describes a rare clinical occurrence where systemic BD and acute neuro-BD were diagnosed concurrently. It further illustrates that acute neuro-BD may be associated with the development of SIADH. The improvement in mucocutaneous signs, neurological symptoms, and hyponatraemia following treatment with colchicine alone implies a potential direct relationship among BD, neuro-BD, and SIADH. When evaluating BD patients who present with fever or headache in conjunction with hyponatraemia, clinicians should remain alert to the possibility of underlying acute neuro-BD.

Of note, this case featured the concurrent identification of systemic BD and acute neuro-BD, evidenced by both mucocutaneous lesions and central nervous system symptoms. Typically, serious organ involvement like CNS lesions manifests about 4 years after BD is first diagnosed, and initial neurological signs are infrequent, seen in less than 5% of cases. Headaches attributable specifically to neuro-BD occur in only about 10% of affected patients. As such, detecting neuro-BD at onset was inherently difficult in this scenario. Acute neuro-BD usually manifests as a self-limited meningoencephalitis, with or without focal deficits. In the present case, brain MRI did not reveal any characteristic abnormalities at common BD lesion sites, including the hypothalamus. Acute neuro-BD can present as aseptic meningitis in the absence of parenchymal involvement, and a normal MRI—especially in early disease—does not exclude the diagnosis. The absence of typical radiologic findings added to the diagnostic challenges. Although HLA-B51 was evaluated, it did not assist in confirming the diagnosis, consistent with international BD guidelines that prioritize clinical manifestations and do not require HLA-B51 testing. This case underscores the necessity of including neuro-BD in the differential when patients with mucocutaneous features develop vague neurological complaints.

At the outset of our differential diagnosis, we focused on infection as the leading cause of CNS inflammation, given the presence of unexplained fever, neurological manifestations, and hyponatraemia; nonetheless, no specific infectious agent was identified. Additional etiologies for CNS inflammation, such as connective tissue disorders and neoplasms—including sarcoidosis and hematologic malignancies—were also considered in our differential. Consequently, we undertook a broad initial workup by assessing autoimmune serological markers, as well as measuring serum ACE and sIL-2R, primarily as screening tools. These tests were utilized selectively, guided by a process of exclusion instead of by a high degree of clinical suspicion. Ultimately, there were no supportive clinical, imaging, or CSF findings for these diagnoses, and the screening results did not significantly impact the eventual diagnosis. It should be noted that ACE testing lacks sensitivity for detecting sarcoidosis, and while sIL-2R can assist in the assessment of malignant lymphoma, modest increases are non-specific and may merely signal generalized inflammation. In the present case, the early absence of genital ulceration contributed to uncertainty around a diagnosis of BD and necessitated consideration of a wider differential. This approach to diagnosis underscores the need to avoid indiscriminate laboratory investigations, highlighting that such testing should only be pursued when substantiated by relevant clinical evidence and interpreted prudently.

While cases of drug-induced SIADH have been observed in patients with Behçet's disease (BD) treated with agents such as cyclophosphamide, the relationship between acute neuro-BD and SIADH remains unclear. Neuro-BD involves central nervous system inflammation, which may disrupt the regulation of ADH secretion. Multiple studies have demonstrated a link between interleukin-6 (IL-6)—a proinflammatory cytokine commonly elevated in neuroinflammatory conditions—and increased release of ADH. In BD, abnormal activation of monocytes can result in the spontaneous production of IL-6, and heightened IL-6 concentrations have been reported in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) of individuals experiencing acute neuro-BD. These observations raise the possibility that IL-6–driven inflammation could be a factor in the development of SIADH in this clinical context. Although serum and CSF IL-6 were not evaluated in the current case, the significant rise in serum C-reactive protein (CRP) may indicate an IL-6–related inflammatory process. SIADH is known to occur in association with a range of disorders, including CNS inflammation, lung disease, malignancies, and the use of certain drugs; however, the data from this case do not support establishing a direct causal association between SIADH and acute neuro-BD. The temporal relationship noted here may represent a general reaction to CNS inflammation rather than a specific pathogenic link to the disease itself.

This case further demonstrates the possible complications arising from hyponatraemia due to SIADH in acute neuro-BD. Past literature has indicated that persistent hiccups can be an important clinical sign of severe hyponatraemia resulting from SIADH associated with central nervous system inflammation, such as meningitis. Additionally, creatine kinase (CK) was included in the initial workup for fever, arthralgia, and generalized fatigue, symptoms that may point to muscle injury of inflammatory or metabolic origin. This investigation uncovered rhabdomyolysis, a recognized yet frequently missed consequence of significant hyponatraemia, which is not commonly assessed. These symptoms, while established, often escape attention as clinical sequelae of profound hyponatraemia. Healthcare providers should recognize that symptoms seemingly unrelated—such as persistent hiccups or the onset of rhabdomyolysis—may be indicative of underlying disturbances in electrolyte balance, especially within the context of neuroinflammatory disease.

Corticosteroids remain the primary therapeutic option for acute neuro-BD, with most cases showing a positive response. Nevertheless, there are reports of spontaneous recovery in patients whose only manifestations are meningoencephalitis-like symptoms. In this report, treatment with colchicine alone led to improvement in both mucocutaneous and neurological features, as well as in hyponatraemia. Colchicine mediates its anti-inflammatory properties by blocking neutrophil activation and migration and inhibiting cytokine production—including IL-1β and IL-6. While colchicine is commonly employed to manage mucocutaneous symptoms of BD, systematic reviews indicate it is generally ineffective for acute neuro-BD, and that disease control typically necessitates additional immunosuppressive therapy. Published case reports generally describe colchicine being used alongside corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants, not as monotherapy. Although it is not possible to rule out spontaneous resolution in the present case, these observations imply that colchicine could serve as a potential alternative for acute neuro-BD and associated complications such as SIADH, especially in circumstances where corticosteroids are unsuitable or contraindicated.

Conclusion:

We reported an uncommon presentation involving simultaneous diagnosis of BD and acute neuro-BD, with the additional complication of SIADH in the setting of neuro-BD. This case underscores that SIADH can arise as a secondary complication of acute neuro-BD, emphasizing the need for clinicians to be vigilant about this possible connection. Administration of colchicine alone resulted in a swift resolution of mucocutaneous lesions, neurological signs, and hyponatraemia, indicating that colchicine could serve as an effective early treatment option for neuro-BD. Clinicians should be alert to the possibility of acute neuro-BD in BD patients who develop nonspecific central nervous system symptoms such as fever and headache alongside hyponatraemia, particularly when SIADH is also present.
