Title: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

Eiichi Kakehi, Yukako Matsumoto, Shigehisa Sakurai, Kazuhiko Kotani

Abstract:

Summary: We describe an uncommon presentation of acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) occurring simultaneously with systemic Behçet’s disease (BD), categorized as definite BD and further complicated by syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). A male patient in his sixth decade arrived with fever, headache, and mucocutaneous symptoms, which included oral aphthous ulcers, folliculitis-like eruptions, genital lesions, and hyponatraemia. The diagnosis of BD was established based on these mucocutaneous signs. Brain magnetic resonance imaging was unremarkable, while cerebrospinal fluid analysis demonstrated aseptic meningitis, consistent with acute neuro-BD. Laboratory evaluation met diagnostic criteria for SIADH. Treatment with colchicine alone resulted in the resolution of mucocutaneous features, neurologic symptoms, and normalization of sodium levels, obviating the need for corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive agents. This report raises the possibility of a link between acute neuro-BD and SIADH, and highlights the necessity of early recognition of both neurological and endocrine involvement in newly diagnosed BD cases.

Background:

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a chronic inflammatory condition affecting multiple organ systems and is marked by recurrent ulceration of the mouth and genitals, uveitis, and dermatologic manifestations. Neurological manifestations, termed neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), appear in roughly 10%–20% of BD patients, often several years following the initial onset of disease. When neuro-BD develops acutely, it frequently manifests as meningoencephalitis and focal lesions within the brain parenchyma, most often impacting the brainstem. While neuro-BD generally emerges after a period of established BD, the simultaneous diagnosis of both BD and neuro-BD at initial presentation is rare. In such cases, it is crucial for clinicians to evaluate both mucocutaneous indicators and neurological complaints, such as headache, changes in behavior, psychiatric disturbances, or localized neurological deficits.

Syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) is a recognized etiology of hyponatraemia, typically secondary to inflammatory processes within the central nervous system (CNS), malignancies, or the administration of certain pharmaceuticals. The acute inflammatory state seen in neuro-BD may predispose patients to SIADH. While SIADH has been previously described in BD patients undergoing therapy with agents such as cyclophosphamide, its development solely due to CNS inflammation in the context of acute neuro-BD, without contributing pharmacological agents, has not been clearly documented.

Here, we present an unusual instance where a patient received simultaneous diagnoses of BD and acute neuro-BD, with the course further complicated by SIADH. This report emphasizes the need to consider SIADH as a potential feature of acute neuro-BD, especially in cases presenting with non-specific neurological symptoms accompanied by hyponatraemia.

Case presentation:

A man of Asian descent in his 60s came to the emergency department after experiencing fever, headache, general malaise, reduced appetite, persistent hiccups, and joint pain in both shoulders and ankles over the preceding three days. He additionally noted the presence of oral aphthae.

He had a prior diagnosis of type 2 diabetes mellitus but was not on any medications, owing to insufficient awareness of his illness and failure to adhere to recommended dietary or exercise routines. His family history was notable for the absence of Behçet's disease (BD) among relatives. He reported occasional alcohol intake, denied tobacco use, had not traveled recently, and had no contact with animals or individuals with illness. He worked in metalworking and had not recently been sexually active.

During assessment, the patient was conscious though notably tired. Recorded vital signs were as follows: temperature 38.1°C, blood pressure 135/73 mm Hg, heart rate 119 bpm (regular rhythm), respiratory rate 19/min, and oxygen saturation at 96% breathing ambient air. The physical exam identified oral aphthous ulcers and folliculitis-like lesions on the facial and truncal regions (see figure 1A, B, and C). Cardiac, pulmonary, and abdominal examinations did not yield abnormal findings. Manual muscle strength testing showed no weakness in either the upper or lower limbs. Peripheral edema was absent, and clinical evaluation determined the patient’s volume status to be euvolemic. The patient was hospitalized for additional assessment.

Investigations:

Initial laboratory analysis showed pronounced hyponatraemia (124 mmol/L), increased creatine kinase (1349 U/L), elevated levels of aspartate aminotransferase (77 U/L) and alanine aminotransferase (64 U/L), significantly raised C-reactive protein (CRP) at 180 mg/L (reference, <3 mg/L), and erythrocyte sedimentation rate at 70 mm/hour (reference, <15 mm/hour), with renal function within normal limits. Additionally, the patient exhibited hyperglycaemia (plasma glucose 14.2 mmol/L) alongside a raised HbA1c of 8.2%, reflecting poor long-term glycaemic control. Screening for hepatitis B and C markers yielded negative results.

Hormonal assessment demonstrated thyroid-stimulating hormone within the reference range (1.11 mIU/L; reference, 0.50–5.00 mIU/L), normal free T4 (1.25 ng/dL; reference, 0.90–1.70 ng/dL), and an appropriate serum cortisol level (42 µg/dL; reference, >6 µg/dL). Serum osmolality was decreased (267 mOsm/kg; reference, <270 mOsm/kg), while urine osmolality was markedly increased (900 mOsm/kg; reference, >300 mOsm/kg), together with high urinary sodium (88 mmol/L; reference, >20 mmol/L), indicative of defective water excretion. Despite the presence of hypo-osmolar hyponatraemia, antidiuretic hormone (ADH) levels were elevated (22.4 pg/mL). Electrocardiogram results were normal, and imaging studies including chest X-ray and CT scans of the head, chest, and abdomen revealed no abnormalities. Collectively, these findings were consistent with a diagnosis of SIADH.

By the third day, the patient experienced increased severity of headache and vertigo. While there was no evidence of neck rigidity or cranial nerve deficits, central nervous system inflammation was considered. MRI of the brain showed no notable pathology affecting the hypothalamus or other areas (figure 2). Examination of cerebrospinal fluid demonstrated slightly elevated opening pressure (17 cmH₂O), raised protein concentration (0.97 g/L), and a mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL). All cerebrospinal fluid cultures and assays for bacterial and mycobacterial organisms, as well as herpes simplex virus (HSV), varicella zoster virus (VZV), and cryptococcal antigen, were negative. CSF cytology did not reveal malignant cells.

Additional studies were undertaken to search for potential systemic illnesses. Sarcoidosis was assessed with a normal serum lysozyme (5.1 µg/mL; reference range 5–10) and angiotensin-converting enzyme (11.4 U/L; reference range 7–25). Hematologic malignancy screening showed a soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) of 704 U/mL (reference 122–496 U/mL), with a negative human T-cell leukemia virus type 1 antibody result.

On the fifth day, a painful ulcer developed on the scrotum (figure 1D). When considered alongside oral aphthous lesions and rashes resembling folliculitis, these manifestations indicated a systemic presentation of Behçet’s disease (BD). The patient did not have an exaggerated response to pinprick testing. Ophthalmological evaluation found no evidence of uveitis. HLA-B51 testing was positive.

Differential diagnosis:

At presentation, the patient’s symptoms of fever, headache, generalized fatigue, and hyponatremia were indicative of a potential central nervous system (CNS) infection or a systemic inflammatory disorder. An initial suspicion was viral meningoencephalitis, especially due to HSV or VZV; however, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis did not detect viral antigens or antibodies, essentially ruling out these causes. Bacterial meningitis was also contemplated, but dismissed owing to the lack of neutrophilic predominance, minimal lymphocytic pleocytosis, and sterile CSF cultures. Furthermore, tuberculous meningitis was discounted based on negative acid-fast staining and tuberculosis DNA assays.

Consideration was given to a primary CNS neoplasm due to the presence of neurologic symptoms. Nonetheless, MRI of the brain did not reveal any abnormalities, and cytological examination of the CSF did not show malignant cells. While the soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) was slightly raised, this isolated result did not substantiate a CNS lymphoma diagnosis.

The possibility of autoimmune conditions was assessed, but these were ruled out on the basis of unremarkable systemic features and negative autoantibody screenings. Neurosarcoidosis remained among the differentials, recognizing that early stages may show a normal MRI; nonetheless, subsequent laboratory findings rendered this diagnosis improbable. The minor increase in sIL-2R was ultimately regarded as non-specific.

A diagnosis of definite Behçet’s disease (BD) was reached, grounded on the clinical evidence of fever, malaise, oral aphthae (awarding two points), genital ulcers (two points), and a rash resembling folliculitis (one point). By the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease, he attained a cumulative score of five points (with a threshold of ≥4 confirming the diagnosis). Genital aphthosis, considered highly specific for BD, reinforced the clinical impression. After systematically eliminating infectious, neoplastic, and autoimmune processes, an acute neuro-Behçet’s disease diagnosis was made, integrating his neurological and mucocutaneous presentation.

Treatment:

On the first day of hospitalization, clinicians considered a possible underlying inflammatory disorder, though its origin could not be determined. Broad-spectrum antibiotic treatment with cefepime was commenced following the collection of blood cultures to address a potential bacterial etiology, even in the absence of a clearly identified site of infection.

By day 3, the patient’s headache and vertigo had intensified, raising concerns about inflammation or infection of the central nervous system. Acyclovir was given as empirical therapy; however, it was stopped once investigations excluded HSV and VZV encephalitis.

On the eighth day, colchicine therapy was initiated after systemic BD and acute neuro-BD were diagnosed. Oral aphthae were managed with a topical corticosteroid ointment, while genital ulcers and skin lesions were treated using silver sulfadiazine cream. Management of SIADH consisted of fluid restriction to less than 800 mL per day, daily monitoring of serum sodium levels, and precautionary avoidance of rapid sodium correction. Neither hypertonic saline nor vasopressin receptor antagonists were deemed necessary.

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient’s fever subsided and his appetite returned the day after initiating colchicine therapy. Over the next two weeks, there was a gradual improvement in his symptoms, which included vertigo, general malaise, hiccups, and mucocutaneous manifestations. By the sixteenth day, serum sodium levels had returned to normal at 136 mmol/L, and ADH concentrations had decreased to 1.8 pg/mL. Additionally, levels of myogenic enzymes normalized.

Notably, systemic corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive agents were not administered during his treatment course. The patient was discharged following a one-month hospital stay, during which time the acute phase was resolved, hyponatremia was meticulously managed and monitored, and rehabilitation was undertaken to address the decline in physical capacity. Colchicine was maintained for roughly four years without any relapse of BD or neurological complaints. Following stabilization of his BD, biguanide therapy was started, resulting in sustained glycemic control.

Discussion:

This report describes a unique instance where systemic BD and acute neuro-BD were diagnosed concurrently, a situation infrequently encountered in clinical practice. It further illustrates that acute neuro-BD may be associated with SIADH. The resolution of the patient's mucocutaneous signs, neurological symptoms, and hyponatraemia upon administration of colchicine monotherapy indicates a possible direct link among BD, neuro-BD, and SIADH. Clinicians should keep acute neuro-BD in mind when evaluating BD patients presenting with fever or headache alongside hyponatraemia.

The significance of this case lies in the identification of both systemic BD and acute neuro-BD at the same time, supported by the presence of mucocutaneous lesions and central nervous system involvement. Ordinarily, major organ involvement such as CNS lesions arises roughly 4 years following the initial diagnosis of BD, and initial presentation with neurological symptoms is rare, observed in less than 5% of individuals. Additionally, only about 10% of patients develop headaches that can be directly attributed to neuro-BD. Consequently, the early detection of neuro-BD in this scenario posed inherent challenges. Acute neuro-BD is most frequently characterized by a self-limited meningoencephalitis, sometimes accompanied by focal neurological deficits. In this case, brain MRI failed to reveal abnormalities at the regions typically affected by BD, including the hypothalamus. Acute neuro-BD may manifest as aseptic meningitis absent of parenchymal brain involvement, and a normal brain MRI—especially early in the disease process—does not rule out the diagnosis. This absence of radiological evidence added further complexity to making the diagnosis. Although HLA-B51 testing was undertaken, it did not provide diagnostic value, which is consistent with international BD guidelines that primarily depend on clinical criteria rather than HLA-B51 status. This case underscores the necessity of considering neuro-BD when patients exhibiting mucocutaneous findings present with non-specific neurological complaints.

At the outset of the differential diagnosis, we gave precedence to infection as a possible cause of CNS inflammation due to the simultaneous presence of unexplained fever, neurological manifestations, and hyponatraemia, but we were unable to detect any particular infectious agent. Alternative etiologies of CNS inflammation, such as connective tissue disorders and neoplastic processes—including sarcoidosis and hematologic malignancies—were also entertained as potential diagnoses. Consequently, we included autoimmune serological markers, serum ACE, and sIL-2R in our initial diagnostic work-up to screen for these conditions. These assessments were ordered selectively within an exclusion-based framework rather than out of strong clinical suspicion. There were no supportive clinical, imaging, or CSF findings for these conditions, and these laboratory studies ultimately did not contribute significantly to establishing a diagnosis. The sensitivity of serum ACE for sarcoidosis is low, and while sIL-2R levels can aid in evaluating malignant lymphoma, slight elevations are non-specific and may simply indicate an inflammatory response. In this instance, the lack of genital ulceration in the early symptomatic period complicated the diagnosis of BD, thus necessitating an extensive differential. This case underscores the need to refrain from indiscriminate diagnostic testing and reinforces that such investigations should be reserved for circumstances guided by specific clinical features, with careful interpretation of results.

While SIADH induced by medications, including cyclophosphamide, has been documented in individuals with BD, the connection between acute neuro-BD and SIADH is not well elucidated. Neuro-BD involves inflammation of the central nervous system, which could play a role in abnormal regulation of ADH secretion. Evidence from multiple studies indicates that interleukin-6 (IL-6), a proinflammatory cytokine commonly increased in neuroinflammatory conditions, promotes augmented ADH release. In BD, atypical monocyte activation results in spontaneous IL-6 synthesis, with heightened IL-6 concentrations found in the CSF of patients experiencing acute neuro-BD. These observations imply that IL-6–driven inflammation may be involved in SIADH in such cases. Although IL-6 was not assessed in either serum or CSF for the patient described here, the substantially raised serum CRP could signify an inflammatory process mediated by IL-6. SIADH occurs in association with several conditions, such as CNS inflammation, malignancy, pulmonary pathology, and particular drugs, yet the available evidence from this case does not permit confirmation of a direct causal relationship between SIADH and acute neuro-BD. The timing seen here may instead point to a general response to CNS inflammation rather than a mechanism specific to the disease.

This case demonstrates the potential complications associated with hyponatraemia due to SIADH in acute neuro-BD. Previous literature has indicated that persistent hiccups can be a significant clinical indicator of severe hyponatraemia resulting from SIADH secondary to CNS inflammation, such as meningitis. Furthermore, during the initial assessment for fever, arthralgia, and general fatigue—which may be attributable to inflammatory or metabolic muscle injury—we included CK measurement, which identified rhabdomyolysis. This is a well-recognized, though frequently neglected, complication of severe hyponatraemia and is not commonly screened for. Although these presentations are established, they are often underestimated as clinical consequences of marked hyponatraemia. It is important for clinicians to recognize that seemingly unrelated signs like hiccups or rhabdomyolysis could indicate hidden electrolyte abnormalities, particularly in patients with neuroinflammatory disorders.

Corticosteroids are regarded as the standard therapy for acute neuro-BD, with most individuals showing a positive response. Nonetheless, cases characterized by only meningoencephalitis-like manifestations have occasionally demonstrated spontaneous improvement. In this instance, treatment with colchicine alone led to resolution of both mucocutaneous and neurologic features, as well as hyponatraemia. Colchicine has anti-inflammatory properties through inhibition of neutrophil movement and activation, and through reduction of cytokine production including IL-1β and IL-6. While colchicine is commonly administered for mucocutaneous symptoms in BD, systematic reviews indicate it does not effectively manage acute neuro-BD, where immunosuppressive agents are typically necessary for sufficient control. Furthermore, in previously published cases, colchicine has typically been used together with corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive drugs rather than by itself. Although spontaneous remission cannot be definitively ruled out in this patient, our results indicate that colchicine could represent a potential therapeutic alternative for acute neuro-BD and associated complications such as SIADH, especially in situations where corticosteroids are not preferred or contraindicated.

Conclusion:

We reported an uncommon instance in which Behçet's disease (BD) and acute neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) were diagnosed simultaneously, with neuro-BD complicated by syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). This case illustrates that SIADH can arise as a complication during the acute phase of neuro-BD, underscoring the need for clinicians to recognize this possible association. Treatment with colchicine alone resulted in prompt resolution of mucocutaneous findings, neurological symptoms, and hyponatraemia, indicating that colchicine may be an effective initial therapy for neuro-BD. In BD patients exhibiting vague central nervous system complaints, such as fever and headache, along with hyponatraemia, practitioners should consider acute neuro-BD in the differential when SIADH is present.
