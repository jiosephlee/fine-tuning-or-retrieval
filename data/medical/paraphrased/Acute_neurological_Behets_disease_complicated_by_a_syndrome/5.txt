Acute neurological Behçet’s disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

Eiichi Kakehi, Yukako Matsumoto, Shigehisa Sakurai, Kazuhiko Kotani

Abstract:

Summary: This article presents an uncommon instance of acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) that was identified simultaneously with systemic Behçet’s disease (BD), meeting the criteria for definite BD, and further complicated by syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). The patient, a man in his 60s, exhibited fever, headache, and a range of mucocutaneous symptoms including oral aphthae, genital ulcerations, folliculitis-like eruptions, as well as hyponatraemia. Diagnosis of BD was established on the basis of these mucocutaneous signs. Although brain MRI revealed no abnormalities, cerebrospinal fluid examination was consistent with aseptic meningitis, substantiating the diagnosis of acute neuro-BD. Laboratory evaluation satisfied the diagnostic benchmarks for SIADH. Treatment with colchicine alone resulted in complete remission of the mucocutaneous, neurological, and electrolyte abnormalities, eliminating the requirement for corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive therapies. This case highlights a potential link between acute neuro-BD and SIADH, and emphasizes the necessity of recognizing coexisting neurological and endocrine complications at the time of BD diagnosis.

Background:

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a persistent, multisystem inflammatory condition marked by repeated episodes of oral and genital ulceration, uveitis, and cutaneous manifestations. Neurological complications, collectively termed neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), are observed in roughly 10%–20% of individuals and often manifest several years subsequent to the initial BD diagnosis. The acute form of neuro-BD commonly presents as meningoencephalitis accompanied by brain parenchymal lesions, frequently affecting the brainstem region. Concurrent presentation of BD and neuro-BD is rare, as neurological involvement typically appears later; nonetheless, when they occur together, clinicians must evaluate both cutaneous-mucosal features and neurological manifestations, such as headache, behavioral or psychiatric changes, and localized neurological deficits.

The syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) represents a recognized etiology of hyponatraemia, frequently arising secondary to central nervous system (CNS) inflammation, malignancies, or certain pharmaceutical agents. Given that acute neuro-BD is driven by inflammation, patients may be predisposed to SIADH development. While SIADH has occasionally been documented among BD patients treated with agents such as cyclophosphamide, the incidence of SIADH directly attributable to CNS inflammation from acute neuro-BD in the absence of medication triggers remains insufficiently characterized.

We describe an unusual case in which simultaneous diagnoses of BD and acute neuro-BD were complicated by the onset of SIADH. This case underscores the need to recognize SIADH as a potential complication in acute neuro-BD, particularly when evaluating patients who present with ambiguous CNS symptoms in conjunction with hyponatraemia.

Case presentation:

A man of Asian descent in his 60s arrived at the emergency department, describing three days of symptoms that included fever, headache, fatigue, diminished appetite, continuous hiccups, and joint pain involving both shoulders and ankles. Additionally, he mentioned the presence of oral aphthae.

His past medical history was significant for type 2 diabetes mellitus, though he was not on any treatment regimen due to a lack of understanding about his disease and insufficient adherence to dietary and exercise guidelines. There was no family history of BD among his relatives. Social history revealed that he drank alcohol infrequently, did not use tobacco, had no recent travel, and reported no contact with animals or people who were ill. Occupationally, he was employed in metalworking, and he denied any recent sexual activity.

Upon assessment, the patient was awake but showed signs of tiredness. Recorded vital parameters included a temperature of 38.1°C, blood pressure measuring 135/73 mm Hg, heart rate of 119 beats per minute (regular rhythm), respiratory rate of 19 breaths per minute, and an oxygen saturation of 96% while breathing room air. The physical examination was notable for oral aphthous ulcers and folliculitis-like eruptions present on both the facial and truncal regions (see figure 1A, B, and C). Cardiopulmonary and abdominal examinations did not yield any abnormal findings. Manual evaluation of muscle strength demonstrated full power in both the upper and lower limbs. No peripheral edema was detected, and clinical assessment indicated a euvolemic state. The patient was subsequently hospitalized for further diagnostic work-up.

Investigations:

Upon admission, laboratory investigations showed significant hyponatraemia (124 mmol/L) and raised levels of creatine kinase (1349 U/L), aspartate aminotransferase (77 U/L), alanine aminotransferase (64 U/L), C-reactive protein (CRP) (180 mg/L; reference, <3 mg/L), and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (70 mm/hour; reference, <15 mm/hour). Renal function remained within normal parameters. The patient exhibited hyperglycaemia, with a plasma glucose concentration of 14.2 mmol/L, as well as an HbA1c of 8.2%, signifying inadequate glycaemic regulation. Screening for hepatitis B and C was negative.

Endocrine testing showed thyroid-stimulating hormone (1.11 mIU/L; reference, 0.50–5.00 mIU/L), free T4 (1.25 ng/dL; reference, 0.90–1.70 ng/dL), and serum cortisol (42 µg/dL; reference, >6 µg/dL) all within standard ranges. The patient’s serum osmolality was decreased (267 mOsm/kg; reference, <270 mOsm/kg) whereas urine osmolality was markedly increased (900 mOsm/kg, reference, >300 mOsm/kg). Urinary sodium was also elevated (88 mmol/L; reference, >20 mmol/L), pointing toward a defect in free water clearance. Notably, antidiuretic hormone (ADH) was raised (22.4 pg/mL) despite the presence of hypo-osmolar hyponatraemia. Electrocardiogram findings were unremarkable, and imaging—including chest X-ray and CT scans of the head, chest, and abdomen—did not reveal any abnormalities. Collectively, these results were consistent with a diagnosis of SIADH.

By the third day, his headache and vertigo had intensified. Despite the absence of neck rigidity or cranial nerve deficits, central nervous system (CNS) inflammation was considered likely. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain demonstrated no notable lesions affecting the hypothalamus or any other cerebral regions (figure 2). Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis identified a mildly raised opening pressure (17 cmH₂O), elevated protein concentration (0.97 g/L), and a mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL). Cultures and assays of the CSF for bacterial and mycobacterial organisms, as well as for herpes simplex virus (HSV), varicella zoster virus (VZV), and cryptococcal antigen, were negative. Cytological examination of the CSF did not detect any malignant cells.

Additional diagnostic assessments were pursued to rule out systemic diseases. Evaluation for sarcoidosis showed a lysozyme level of 5.1 µg/mL (reference range: 5–10) and angiotensin-converting enzyme of 11.4 U/L (reference range: 7–25), both within normal limits. Screening for hematologic malignancy revealed an elevated soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) at 704 U/mL (reference, 122–496 U/mL), while testing for human T-cell leukaemia virus type 1 antibody was negative.

By the fifth day, he developed a painful ulceration on the scrotum (figure 1D). This, along with the presence of oral aphthae and folliculitis-like cutaneous lesions, pointed toward a systemic variant of BD. There was no pinprick hypersensitivity. Ophthalmic evaluation did not reveal any signs of uveitis. He was positive for HLA-B51.

Differential diagnosis:

At initial evaluation, the concurrence of fever, headache, general malaise, and hyponatraemia raised suspicion for either a central nervous system (CNS) infection or an underlying systemic inflammatory condition. Viral meningoencephalitis—especially etiologies such as HSV or VZV—was considered as the leading possibility. Yet, CSF analyses revealed no evidence of viral antigens or antibodies, ruling out these infectious agents. Bacterial meningitis was another differential, but was dismissed owing to the lack of neutrophilic dominance, only mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, and negative CSF cultures. Tuberculous meningitis was similarly discounted due to negative results from both acid-fast staining and tuberculosis DNA assays.

Primary CNS malignancy was also contemplated in light of the neurological presentation. Nevertheless, neuroimaging by brain MRI failed to detect pathological findings, and CSF cytological examination did not show malignant cells. Although there was a slight increase in sIL-2R, this isolated result was inadequate to confirm CNS lymphoma.

Assessment for autoimmune diseases yielded negative results as there were no typical systemic manifestations or serological markers on autoantibody screening. Neurosarcoidosis was tentatively considered since early-stage MRI can appear normal, but subsequent laboratory evaluation rendered this unlikely. The slight elevation in sIL-2R was ultimately interpreted as non-specific.

The clinical diagnosis was definite BD, grounded in characteristic clinical criteria: fever, fatigue, the presence of oral aphthae (two points), genital aphthosis (two points), and a folliculitis-like eruption (one point). Under the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease, these symptoms accumulated a total of five points, which surpasses the threshold (≥4) required for diagnosis. Notably, genital aphthosis is highly specific for BD, reinforcing this conclusion. Once infectious, neoplastic, and autoimmune etiologies were systematically ruled out, the patient was diagnosed with acute neuro-BD, characterized by CNS involvement against a backdrop of mucocutaneous findings.

Treatment:

Upon admission (day 1), clinicians considered an underlying inflammatory disorder, though its precise cause was uncertain. Empirical treatment with cefepime was commenced following the collection of blood cultures, aiming to address a possible bacterial infection even in the absence of an identifiable infection site.

By day 3, the patient’s headache and vertigo had intensified, raising concerns about potential CNS inflammation or infection. Acyclovir was empirically prescribed, but was subsequently discontinued when testing excluded HSV and VZV encephalitis.

On day 8, colchicine therapy was initiated after systemic BD and acute neuro-BD were diagnosed. Topical corticosteroid ointment was applied to treat the oral aphthous lesions. Genital ulcers and skin lesions were managed using silver sulfadiazine cream. SIADH was addressed through conservative means, specifically fluid intake restriction to less than 800 mL/day, close daily monitoring of serum sodium, and the avoidance of rapid sodium correction. There was no need for hypertonic saline or vasopressin receptor antagonists.

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient's fever subsided and his appetite increased the day after initiating colchicine therapy. Over the next two weeks, there was a gradual resolution of his clinical manifestations, such as vertigo, general malaise, hiccups, and mucocutaneous lesions. By the sixteenth day, his serum sodium had reached a normal value of 136 mmol/L, and his ADH concentration had decreased to 1.8 pg/mL. Levels of myogenic enzymes had also normalized.

Notably, neither systemic corticosteroids nor additional immunosuppressive agents were administered during the course of treatment. Following a one-month hospitalization—during which the patient recovered from the acute episode, received close monitoring and management of hyponatraemia, and underwent rehabilitation for impaired physical abilities—he was discharged. Colchicine was continued for around four years post-discharge, during which no relapses of BD or neurologic symptoms were observed. Once the patient's BD had stabilized, biguanide therapy was started, leading to effective maintenance of glycaemic control.

Discussion:

In this case, both systemic BD and acute neuro-BD were diagnosed concurrently, a scenario that is rarely reported. Furthermore, it illustrated that SIADH may occur as a complication of acute neuro-BD. Notably, the resolution of the patient’s mucocutaneous symptoms, neurological disturbances, and hyponatraemia following treatment with colchicine alone implies a potential direct link among BD, neuro-BD, and SIADH. Consequently, clinicians should remain alert to the possibility of acute neuro-BD as a cause when individuals with BD exhibit fever or headache along with hyponatraemia.

This particular case stands out due to the concomitant identification of systemic BD and acute neuro-BD, substantiated by the presence of both mucocutaneous involvement and central nervous system symptoms. Generally, major organ manifestations such as CNS involvement appear a median of four years post BD diagnosis, and neurologic symptoms at initial presentation are observed in fewer than 5% of BD cases. Moreover, only about 10% of patients develop headaches that are specifically attributable to neuro-BD, which contributed to the diagnostic complexity in this instance. Acute neuro-BD usually manifests as a self-limiting meningoencephalitis, which may or may not be accompanied by focal neurological signs. For this patient, brain MRI did not reveal characteristic changes in the usual locations affected by BD, such as the hypothalamus. It is important to recognize that acute neuro-BD can initially present as aseptic meningitis without evidence of parenchymal brain lesions, and early-stage disease may yield normal MRI findings, further complicating diagnosis. HLA-B51 analysis was undertaken, but it provided no additional value in reaching a diagnosis, consistent with current international BD criteria that emphasize clinical assessment over genetic testing. This report underscores the need to include neuro-BD in the differential diagnosis when individuals with established mucocutaneous manifestations of BD develop vague neurological symptoms.

At the outset of the differential diagnosis, our primary consideration was an infectious etiology for the CNS inflammation, prompted by the presentation of unexplained fever, neurological deficits, and hyponatraemia. Despite this suspicion, no definitive pathogen was identified. Alternative sources of CNS inflammation, such as connective tissue disorders and neoplastic conditions, were also assessed, and the differential diagnosis included entities like sarcoidosis and haematologic malignancies. Consequently, our initial comprehensive assessment encompassed screening with autoimmune serological assays, serum ACE, and sIL-2R measurements. These tests were utilized as part of a systematic exclusion process rather than owing to concrete clinical indicators for specific diseases. Ultimately, there was no supportive evidence from clinical examination, imaging, or cerebrospinal fluid analysis to suggest these conditions, and these screening tests did not substantially aid the diagnostic process. It should be noted that serum ACE is not highly sensitive for sarcoidosis, and while sIL-2R may contribute to the assessment of malignant lymphoma, minor elevations are nonspecific and generally represent an inflammatory state. In this scenario, the lack of genital ulceration at the initial stage of symptoms complicated the clinical suspicion for BD, necessitating a wider differential. This diagnostic trajectory underscores the pitfalls of indiscriminate laboratory testing and underscores the necessity for clinicians to order investigations judiciously, guided by relevant clinical features, and to interpret such findings with due caution.

While drug-induced SIADH has been documented in individuals with BD treated with agents such as cyclophosphamide, the relationship between acute neuro-BD and SIADH is still not well clarified. Neuro-BD involves inflammation of the CNS, which could potentially lead to abnormal ADH secretion. Multiple investigations have indicated that interleukin-6 (IL-6), a proinflammatory cytokine commonly upregulated in neuroinflammatory states, is linked to augmented ADH release. In BD, inappropriate activation of monocytes triggers constitutive IL-6 secretion, and heightened levels of IL-6 have been observed in the CSF of patients experiencing acute neuro-BD episodes. These observations raise the possibility that IL-6–driven inflammation might play a role in the development of SIADH in this context. Although direct measurement of IL-6 in the serum or CSF was not performed in this case, the notably increased serum CRP level may serve as an indirect indicator of an IL-6–mediated inflammatory state. Despite SIADH being reported in association with a broad spectrum of conditions—including CNS inflammation, respiratory diseases, malignancies, and use of certain drugs—the evidence in the present case does not support a definitive causal relationship between SIADH and acute neuro-BD. The chronological correlation noted here may represent a non-specific reaction to CNS inflammation, rather than a mechanism unique to the disease.

This case underscores the possible adverse outcomes associated with hyponatraemia resulting from SIADH in patients with acute neuro-BD. Literature has previously highlighted that persistent hiccups can represent a significant clinical indicator of profound hyponatraemia induced by SIADH due to central nervous system inflammation, including cases of meningitis. Furthermore, creatine kinase (CK) was evaluated as part of the initial assessment of fever, arthralgia, and general fatigue, symptoms potentially indicative of muscle injury from inflammatory or metabolic causes. This assessment revealed rhabdomyolysis, which, although recognized as a complication of severe hyponatraemia, is frequently neglected and not routinely assessed. These symptoms exemplify established but commonly overlooked clinical consequences of marked hyponatraemia. Healthcare providers should maintain a high index of suspicion for electrolyte disturbances—especially hyponatraemia—when encountering atypical symptoms such as hiccups or rhabdomyolysis in the setting of neuroinflammatory disease.

Corticosteroids represent the standard therapy for acute neuro-BD, with the majority of patients experiencing substantial improvement. Nonetheless, there have been instances of spontaneous remission, especially in cases presenting only with meningoencephalitis-like features. In the case under discussion, monotherapy with colchicine was effective in alleviating both mucocutaneous and neurological symptoms, as well as correcting hyponatraemia. The anti-inflammatory properties of colchicine are attributed to its capacity to impede neutrophil movement and activation, as well as its ability to downregulate cytokine production, notably IL-1β and IL-6. While colchicine is extensively utilized in managing mucocutaneous symptoms in BD, evidence from systematic reviews indicates it does not yield significant benefit in acute neuro-BD, where more potent immunosuppressive drugs are usually necessary for sufficient disease control. Indeed, in published literature, colchicine is typically employed alongside corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants, rather than as a single-agent regimen. Although the possibility of spontaneous improvement in this patient cannot be entirely discounted, the outcomes observed point to colchicine as a potentially viable treatment alternative for acute neuro-BD and associated complications such as SIADH, especially in cases where corticosteroid use is not appropriate or is contraindicated.

Conclusion:

We reported an uncommon presentation where BD and acute neuro-BD were simultaneously diagnosed, with SIADH emerging as a complication of the neuro-BD. This observation underscores that SIADH can develop in the setting of acute neuro-BD and emphasizes the need for clinicians to recognize this potential relationship. Treatment with colchicine alone resulted in prompt resolution of both mucocutaneous and neurological symptoms, as well as correction of hyponatraemia, indicating that colchicine could be an effective initial therapy for neuro-BD. For BD patients who exhibit nonspecific central nervous system manifestations, such as fever and headache alongside hyponatraemia, clinicians should consider the possibility of acute neuro-BD, particularly when SIADH is present.
