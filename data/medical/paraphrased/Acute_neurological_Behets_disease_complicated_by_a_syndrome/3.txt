Title: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

Eiichi Kakehi, Yukako Matsumoto, Shigehisa Sakurai, Kazuhiko Kotani

Abstract:

Summary: We describe an uncommon presentation of acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) identified simultaneously with systemic Behçet’s disease (BD)—meeting criteria for definite BD—and complicated by syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). A male patient in his 60s arrived with symptoms of fever, headache, and various mucocutaneous lesions, such as oral aphthae, rashes resembling folliculitis, genital ulcers, and laboratory-confirmed hyponatraemia. The mucocutaneous findings prompted a BD diagnosis. Although brain MRI was unremarkable, analysis of cerebrospinal fluid indicated aseptic meningitis, further substantiating acute neuro-BD. Laboratory evaluation satisfied the criteria for SIADH. Treatment with colchicine alone successfully resolved his mucocutaneous and neurological symptoms as well as the hyponatraemia, eliminating the necessity for corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive therapies. This report highlights a potential link between acute neuro-BD and SIADH and emphasizes the need to consider both neurological and endocrine involvement when assessing patients newly diagnosed with BD.

Background:

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a persistent inflammatory condition affecting multiple organ systems and is typified by the presence of recurrent oral and genital ulcerations, uveitis, and various dermatological manifestations. Neurological manifestations, termed neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), emerge in roughly 10%–20% of individuals with BD, frequently arising years after the initial disease onset. The acute phase of neuro-BD is characteristically associated with meningoencephalitis and parenchymal lesions within the brain, most notably implicating the brainstem region. While it is more common for neurological symptoms to manifest later in the disease course, it is uncommon for both BD and neuro-BD to be diagnosed at the same time. When this simultaneous presentation occurs, it is important for clinicians to systematically evaluate for both cutaneous-mucosal findings and neurological indicators such as headache, behavioural or psychiatric changes, and focal neurological impairments.

The syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) is a frequent etiology of hyponatraemia and is most often attributed to CNS inflammatory processes, neoplasms, or specific pharmacological agents. The inflammatory milieu characterizing acute neuro-BD may predispose patients to the development of SIADH. While SIADH has occasionally been described in BD patients undergoing treatment with agents like cyclophosphamide, the incidence of SIADH strictly due to acute neuro-BD–related CNS inflammation, absent any medication-induced factors, is yet to be clearly established.

Here, we describe an uncommon clinical scenario involving the concurrent diagnosis of BD and acute neuro-BD, which was further complicated by SIADH. This report underscores the need to consider SIADH as a possible complication in cases of acute neuro-BD, particularly when patients present with hyponatraemia in conjunction with vague or ambiguous neurological symptoms.

Case presentation:

A man of Asian descent in his sixties arrived at the emergency department, reporting three days of fever, headache, general malaise, anorexia, ongoing hiccups, and joint pain localized to the shoulders and ankles. Additionally, he noted the presence of oral aphthous ulcers.

The patient's medical background included type 2 diabetes mellitus, though he was not on any therapy for this condition, largely due to limited understanding of his diagnosis and a lack of adherence to recommended lifestyle modifications in diet or exercise. His family medical history revealed no known cases of BD. He consumed alcohol infrequently, was a non-smoker, and denied any recent travel, contact with animals, or exposure to sick individuals. Employment history revealed work in the metalworking sector, and he reported no recent sexual activity.

During assessment, the patient was conscious though exhibited signs of tiredness. His vital measurements included a temperature of 38.1°C, blood pressure at 135/73 mm Hg, regular pulse of 119 beats per minute, respiratory rate of 19 breaths per minute, and an oxygen saturation level of 96% while breathing ambient air. Upon physical inspection, oral aphthae were noted as well as rashes resembling folliculitis present on his face and trunk (figure 1A, B and C). No abnormalities were identified on examination of the cardiopulmonary or abdominal systems. Manual muscle testing showed preserved strength in both upper and lower limbs. There was an absence of peripheral oedema, and clinical evaluation indicated he was euvolemic. Hospital admission was undertaken to enable further diagnostic workup.

Investigations:

The initial laboratory assessment demonstrated significant hyponatraemia, with serum sodium measured at 124 mmol/L. There were increased levels of creatine kinase (1349 U/L), aspartate aminotransferase (77 U/L), alanine aminotransferase (64 U/L), C-reactive protein (180 mg/L; reference value, <3 mg/L), and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (70 mm/hour; reference, <15 mm/hour), while renal function remained within normal limits. The patient was additionally found to have hyperglycaemia (plasma glucose 14.2 mmol/L) and an HbA1c of 8.2%, pointing to inadequate control of blood glucose. Markers for hepatitis B and C returned negative.

Further endocrine analysis showed thyroid-stimulating hormone within the normal range (1.11 mIU/L; reference, 0.50–5.00 mIU/L), normal free T4 (1.25 ng/dL; reference, 0.90–1.70 ng/dL), and a serum cortisol of 42 µg/dL (reference, >6 µg/dL). Serum osmolality was reduced (267 mOsm/kg; reference, <270 mOsm/kg), while urine osmolality was markedly raised (900 mOsm/kg, reference, >300 mOsm/kg), accompanied by a high urinary sodium concentration (88 mmol/L; reference, >20 mmol/L), reflecting impaired ability to excrete water. In the context of hypo-osmolar hyponatraemia, antidiuretic hormone (ADH) levels were also elevated (22.4 pg/mL). Results from ECG, chest X-ray, and CT scans of the head, chest, and abdomen were all within normal parameters. Collectively, these data were consistent with a diagnosis of SIADH.

By the third day, his headache and vertigo had intensified. Despite the absence of neck rigidity or cranial nerve deficits, central nervous system (CNS) inflammation was considered as a possible cause. MRI of the brain did not demonstrate notable abnormalities affecting the hypothalamus or other regions (figure 2). Analysis of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) indicated a slightly elevated opening pressure (17 cmH₂O), increased protein concentration (0.97 g/L), and a mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL). Cultures and diagnostic tests on CSF for bacterial and mycobacterial organisms, herpes simplex virus (HSV), varicella zoster virus (VZV), and cryptococcal antigen were all negative. No malignant cells were identified in CSF cytology.

Additional testing was undertaken to assess for underlying systemic illnesses. Investigations for sarcoidosis showed normal lysozyme (5.1 µg/mL; reference 5–10) and angiotensin-converting enzyme levels (11.4 U/L; reference 7–25). A hematological malignancy screen showed an elevated soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) level (704 U/mL; reference, 122–496 U/mL), but human T-cell leukaemia virus type 1 antibody was negative.

By day 5, a painful ulcer developed on the scrotum (figure 1D). Together with the presence of oral aphthae and folliculitis-like eruptions, these features pointed towards a systemic manifestation of BD. The patient did not display pinprick hypersensitivity, and ophthalmological evaluation detected no evidence of uveitis. Testing revealed the presence of HLA-B51.

Differential diagnosis:

Upon initial assessment, the concurrence of fever, headache, general malaise, and hyponatraemia raised suspicion for a central nervous system infection or a systemic inflammatory disorder. Viral meningoencephalitis, particularly due to HSV or VZV, was considered the most probable etiology at first. Nonetheless, analysis of the cerebrospinal fluid showed neither viral antigens nor antibodies, which effectively discounted these infections. The possibility of bacterial meningitis was evaluated but dismissed given the lack of neutrophilic predominance, only a slight lymphocytic pleocytosis, and negative CSF cultures. Tuberculous meningitis was similarly ruled out due to negative findings on acid-fast staining and PCR for tuberculosis DNA.

Given the neurological presentation, a primary CNS neoplasm was also considered. However, brain MRI revealed no pathologic abnormalities, and cytological analysis of the CSF did not identify any malignant cells. Although a slight elevation in sIL-2R was noted, this isolated finding was not sufficient to support CNS lymphoma as a diagnosis.

Assessment for autoimmune diseases was undertaken, but these were excluded based on a lack of hallmark systemic features and negative autoantibody screening. Neurosarcoidosis was retained as a potential diagnosis since early disease may present with unremarkable MRI, but later laboratory data made this unlikely. The moderate increase in sIL-2R was interpreted as nonspecific.

Definite BD was diagnosed based on the clinical picture, which included fever and fatigue as well as mucocutaneous findings: oral aphthae (two points), genital aphthosis (two points), and a rash resembling folliculitis (one point). This patient’s cumulative score using the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease was five (where a score of four or higher is diagnostic). Of note, genital aphthosis is considered highly specific for BD, and these features robustly support the diagnosis. Following thorough exclusion of infectious, neoplastic, and autoimmune etiologies, a diagnosis of acute neuro-BD was confirmed, based on CNS manifestations associated with characteristic mucocutaneous symptoms.

Treatment:

Upon admission (day 1), clinicians considered an underlying inflammatory disorder, though its precise cause was indeterminate. Empirical cefepime therapy was commenced after drawing blood cultures to address a possible bacterial infection, even though no clear infectious source was identified.

By day 3, worsening headache and vertigo indicated possible inflammation or infection of the CNS. Acyclovir was initiated empirically but subsequently discontinued when diagnostic testing excluded both HSV and VZV encephalitis.

On day 8, treatment with colchicine was initiated after systemic BD and acute neuro-BD were diagnosed. Topical corticosteroid ointment was prescribed for oral aphthous ulcers. Silver sulfadiazine cream was used for the management of genital ulcers and cutaneous lesions. Management of SIADH consisted of conservative measures, including fluid restriction (<800 mL/day), rigorous daily monitoring of serum sodium, and ensuring that correction of sodium levels was gradual. Use of hypertonic saline and vasopressin receptor antagonists was not warranted.

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient’s fever subsided and his appetite increased within a day of commencing colchicine treatment. Over the next two weeks, his clinical symptoms—including vertigo, general malaise, persistent hiccups, and mucocutaneous manifestations—showed progressive resolution. By day 16, laboratory findings indicated serum sodium had returned to a normal value of 136 mmol/L, while ADH levels had decreased to 1.8 pg/mL. In addition, levels of myogenic enzymes normalized.

Notably, the management of his condition did not involve systemic corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive agents. Following a one-month period of hospitalization—which facilitated recovery from the acute phase, allowed for meticulous monitoring and correction of hyponatraemia, and included rehabilitation to address physical deconditioning—the patient was discharged. Colchicine was continued for around four years post-discharge, during which there were no observed recurrences of Behçet’s disease or neurologic manifestations. After stabilization of BD, biguanide therapy was introduced, resulting in sustained and effective glycaemic control.

Discussion:

This report presents a rare instance where systemic BD and acute neuro-BD were concurrently diagnosed. It further illustrates that SIADH can develop as a complication of acute neuro-BD. Notably, resolution of mucocutaneous symptoms, neurological deficits, and hyponatraemia was achieved through colchicine monotherapy, suggesting a potential direct link among BD, neuro-BD, and SIADH. For BD patients who exhibit fever or headache alongside hyponatraemia, acute neuro-BD should be considered as an underlying etiology.

The uniqueness of this case lies in the concurrent identification of both systemic BD and acute neuro-BD, as evidenced by the coexistence of mucocutaneous lesions and CNS involvement. Usually, severe organ involvement such as CNS pathology tends to occur approximately four years after BD is initially diagnosed, and presenting with neurological features at onset is infrequent—affecting less than 5% of cases. Only about 10% of individuals with BD develop headaches that are specifically attributable to neuro-BD, making initial detection challenging in this scenario. Acute neuro-BD is most often characterized by a self-limited meningoencephalitis, which may or may not be associated with focal neurological signs. In this case, MRI of the brain failed to reveal abnormalities in classic BD-affected regions, such as the hypothalamus. It is possible for acute neuro-BD to manifest as aseptic meningitis in the absence of parenchymal lesions, and a normal brain MRI early in the course of illness does not preclude the diagnosis. The absence of characteristic radiological findings introduced further complexity into the diagnostic evaluation. Although the patient underwent HLA-B51 genotyping, the result was not diagnostic, consistent with international BD diagnostic criteria, which prioritize clinical manifestations over genetic testing. This case underscores the importance of suspecting neuro-BD in patients with mucocutaneous involvement who present with non-specific neurological complaints.

At the outset of the differential diagnosis, our principal concern was infection as the etiology of CNS inflammation, due to the concurrent presence of unexplained fever, neurological manifestations, and hyponatraemia. Despite these findings, no causative pathogen could be identified. Our diagnostic considerations also encompassed alternative sources of CNS inflammation such as connective tissue disorders and malignancy; thus, sarcoidosis and haematologic malignancies were incorporated into our list of possible diagnoses. In line with this broad initial approach, we opted to screen for autoimmune disease markers, and evaluated serum ACE and sIL-2R levels. The decision to perform these assays was driven more by a process of elimination than by targeted clinical suspicion. Ultimately, neither the clinical presentation, imaging studies, nor CSF analysis offered support for these diagnoses, and the results of these investigations did not play a substantial role in establishing the final diagnosis. Notably, the diagnostic yield of ACE for sarcoidosis is modest, and while sIL-2R can aid in detecting malignant lymphoma, mild increases in sIL-2R are non-specific and may merely indicate an underlying inflammatory process. In this instance, the lack of genital ulcers in the initial phase complicated the suspicion for BD, necessitating consideration of a wide range of possible diagnoses. The process underscored the necessity for judicious use of diagnostic testing, underscoring that investigations should be directed by clinical indications and their interpretation approached prudently.

While SIADH resulting from pharmacological agents, such as cyclophosphamide, has been documented in individuals with BD, the relationship between acute neuro-BD and SIADH is not well elucidated. Neuro-BD features inflammatory activity within the CNS, potentially leading to disruptions in ADH regulation. Previous research has demonstrated that interleukin-6 (IL-6), a proinflammatory cytokine commonly upregulated in neuroinflammatory conditions, is implicated in promoting ADH secretion. In BD, excessive activation of monocytes can trigger spontaneous IL-6 production, with raised IL-6 concentrations identified in the CSF of those experiencing acute neuro-BD. These observations imply that IL-6–driven inflammation could play a role in SIADH under these circumstances. Although IL-6 measurements in the serum or CSF were not obtained in the current case, a significantly elevated serum CRP was observed, potentially indicating an IL-6–dependent inflammatory process. While SIADH can arise in diverse clinical contexts, including CNS inflammation, respiratory illness, malignancy, or medication use, the current evidence does not establish a definitive causal connection between SIADH and acute neuro-BD. The chronology seen in this case may simply indicate a generalized reaction to CNS inflammation, rather than a process unique to the underlying disease.

This case further demonstrates the possible complications arising from hyponatraemia due to SIADH in the context of acute neuro-BD. Prior literature has identified persistent hiccups as a significant clinical indicator of severe hyponatraemia related to SIADH resulting from CNS inflammation, including conditions such as meningitis. Furthermore, as part of our initial workup for fever, arthralgia, and generalized fatigue—symptoms potentially attributable to either inflammatory or metabolic muscle injury—we measured CK, which led to the identification of rhabdomyolysis. Although rhabdomyolysis is a recognized complication of profound hyponatraemia, it is frequently underdiagnosed because it is not routinely assessed. While these clinical presentations are documented in the literature, their significance in the context of profound hyponatraemia is often underestimated. It is important for clinicians to recognize that seemingly unrelated symptoms, including hiccups and rhabdomyolysis, may serve as indicators of underlying electrolyte imbalances, especially amid neuroinflammatory disease processes.

Corticosteroids are widely recognized as the primary therapy for acute neuro-BD, with the majority of patients exhibiting a favorable clinical response. Nonetheless, there have been instances where cases manifesting only with meningoencephalitis-like symptoms resolved spontaneously. In the case described here, administration of colchicine alone led to improvement in both mucocutaneous and neurological symptoms, as well as correction of hyponatraemia. The anti-inflammatory action of colchicine is attributed to its ability to impede neutrophil migration and activation, along with its capacity to downregulate cytokine production, notably IL-1β and IL-6. While colchicine is frequently prescribed for mucocutaneous involvement in BD, evidence from systematic reviews indicates that it does not effectively treat acute neuro-BD; rather, immunosuppressive agents are usually needed to achieve optimal disease management. Indeed, documented cases have typically combined colchicine with corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive medications, instead of employing colchicine as a standalone treatment. Although spontaneous remission cannot be completely ruled out in this instance, the observations here indicate that colchicine could represent a valuable therapeutic alternative for acute neuro-BD and associated complications, such as SIADH, especially in situations where corticosteroid treatment is either unsuitable or contraindicated.

Conclusion:

We reported an unusual instance where BD and acute neuro-BD were identified at the same time, with neuro-BD being further complicated by the presence of SIADH. This report underscores that SIADH can manifest as a complication of acute neuro-BD and emphasizes the necessity for clinicians to recognize this possible link. Treatment with colchicine alone resulted in swift resolution of both mucocutaneous and neurological symptoms, as well as hyponatraemia, indicating that colchicine might serve as an effective treatment during the initial stage of neuro-BD. When encountering BD patients who exhibit vague central nervous system complaints such as fever and headache together with hyponatraemia, practitioners should strongly consider acute neuro-BD complicated by SIADH in their differential diagnosis.
