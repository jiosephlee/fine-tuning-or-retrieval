Title: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

Eiichi Kakehi, Yukako Matsumoto, Shigehisa Sakurai, Kazuhiko Kotani

Abstract:

Summary: We describe an uncommon instance of acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) occurring simultaneously with systemic Behçet’s disease (BD), categorized as definite BD and complicated by syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). The patient, a male in his 60s, presented with fever, headache, and mucocutaneous abnormalities including oral aphthae, folliculitis-like eruptions, genital ulcers, and hyponatraemia. BD was diagnosed based on the mucocutaneous features. Brain MRI was unremarkable, while cerebrospinal fluid assessment indicated aseptic meningitis, confirming acute neuro-BD. Laboratory evaluation fulfilled the criteria for SIADH. Treatment with colchicine alone resulted in improvement of the mucocutaneous manifestations, neurological symptoms, and hyponatraemia, with no requirement for corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive agents. This report highlights a potential link between acute neuro-BD and SIADH, emphasizing the need to recognize overlapping neurological and endocrine manifestations in patients at initial diagnosis of BD.

Background:

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a persistent, multisystem inflammatory condition marked by recurrent oral and genital ulcerations, uveitis, and cutaneous manifestations. Neurological manifestations, termed neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), affect roughly 10%–20% of cases and often appear several years after BD is initially identified. The acute presentation of neuro-BD frequently consists of meningoencephalitis with parenchymal lesions, most often involving the brainstem. While simultaneous diagnosis is rare and delayed onset is more typical, clinicians should be mindful to evaluate both mucocutaneous signs and neurological symptoms—such as headaches, changes in behavior, psychiatric manifestations, and localized neurological deficits—when both BD and neuro-BD are identified together.

Syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) represents a frequent etiology of hyponatraemia and is commonly linked to central nervous system (CNS) inflammation, malignancies, or specific pharmacologic agents. Due to the inflammatory character of acute neuro-BD, there is a potential risk for SIADH development in these patients. Although SIADH has been described in BD patients treated with medications like cyclophosphamide, cases arising exclusively from CNS inflammation in acute neuro-BD and independent of drug effects remain insufficiently characterized.

Here, we describe a rare instance where both BD and acute neuro-BD were diagnosed at the same time, with the course further complicated by SIADH. This report emphasizes the need to consider SIADH as a possible complication of acute neuro-BD, especially in patients presenting with both general CNS complaints and hyponatraemia.

Case presentation:

A man of Asian descent in his 60s came to the emergency department with a 3-day duration of fever, headache, overall fatigue, decreased appetite, continuous hiccups, and joint pain in the shoulders and ankles. Additionally, he noted the presence of oral aphthous ulcers.

His past medical history was notable for type 2 diabetes mellitus, for which he was untreated due to limited knowledge of his disease and poor adherence to recommended dietary and exercise routines. There was no notable family history, including an absence of Behçet’s disease in relatives. He drank alcohol occasionally, did not use tobacco products, had not traveled recently, nor had contact with animals or people who were ill. He was employed in metalworking and had not recently been sexually active.

Upon assessment, the patient was conscious but showed signs of fatigue. Vital signs recorded included a temperature of 38.1°C, blood pressure measuring 135/73 mm Hg, heart rate of 119 beats per minute (regular rhythm), respiratory rate at 19 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation of 96% while breathing ambient air. The physical exam identified oral aphthae along with folliculitis-like eruptions present on the face and trunk (figure 1A, B, and C). Examination of the heart, lungs, and abdomen revealed no abnormalities. Manual muscle testing demonstrated intact strength in both upper and lower limbs. No peripheral edema was detected, and clinical assessment indicated that his volume status was euvolemic. He was admitted for additional diagnostic workup.

Investigations:

Initial laboratory evaluations showed pronounced hyponatraemia (124 mmol/L), elevated creatine kinase (1349 U/L), increased aspartate aminotransferase (77 U/L), raised alanine aminotransferase (64 U/L), high C-reactive protein (CRP) at 180 mg/L (reference <3 mg/L), and elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate (70 mm/hour; reference <15 mm/hour), while renal function remained within normal limits. Hyperglycaemia was observed (plasma glucose 14.2 mmol/L), accompanied by an HbA1c value of 8.2%, indicative of inadequate glycaemic management. Hepatitis B and C serologies were negative.

Assessment of endocrine parameters demonstrated normal values for thyroid-stimulating hormone (1.11 mIU/L; reference 0.50–5.00 mIU/L), free T4 (1.25 ng/dL; reference 0.90–1.70 ng/dL), and serum cortisol (42 µg/dL; reference >6 µg/dL). The patient exhibited decreased serum osmolality (267 mOsm/kg; reference <270 mOsm/kg), with increased urine osmolality (900 mOsm/kg; reference >300 mOsm/kg) and elevated urinary sodium (88 mmol/L; reference >20 mmol/L), signifying reduced ability to excrete free water. In spite of hypo-osmolar hyponatraemia, antidiuretic hormone (ADH) concentration was elevated at 22.4 pg/mL. Electrocardiogram findings were unremarkable, and imaging studies—including chest X-ray and CT scans of the head, chest, and abdomen—did not reveal abnormalities. Collectively, these results were consistent with SIADH diagnosis.

On the third day, the patient experienced increased severity of headache and vertigo. Although physical examination found neither neck rigidity nor signs of cranial nerve dysfunction, central nervous system inflammation was considered. MRI of the brain did not demonstrate notable abnormalities in the hypothalamus or other areas (figure 2). Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) evaluation showed a mildly increased opening pressure at 17 cmH₂O, elevated protein level of 0.97 g/L, and a slight lymphocytic pleocytosis of 11 cells/µL. Microbiological assessments of CSF—including cultures and testing for bacterial, mycobacterial, HSV, VZV, and cryptococcal antigen—were all negative. CSF cytology did not detect malignant cells.

Additional tests were conducted to investigate possible systemic diseases. Assessment for sarcoidosis found lysozyme levels at 5.1 µg/mL (reference 5–10) and angiotensin-converting enzyme at 11.4 U/L (reference 7–25), both within normal limits. Examination for hematological malignancies identified a raised soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) level at 704 U/mL (reference, 122–496 U/mL); human T-cell leukemia virus type 1 antibody was negative.

By the fifth day, he developed a painful ulcer on the scrotum (figure 1D). Coupled with the presence of oral aphthae and folliculitis-like skin eruptions, these signs indicated a systemic presentation of BD. There was no observed pinprick hypersensitivity. Eye evaluation revealed no evidence of uveitis. The HLA-B51 antigen was positive.

Differential diagnosis:

Upon admission, the constellation of fever, headache, generalized malaise, and hyponatremia raised suspicion for a central nervous system infection or a systemic inflammatory condition. Viral meningoencephalitis, notably herpes simplex virus (HSV) or varicella zoster virus (VZV), was initially presumed. Nonetheless, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis returned negative results for both viral antigens and antibodies, effectively ruling out these infections. Consideration was also given to bacterial meningitis; however, its likelihood was dismissed due to a lack of neutrophilic predominance, the presence of only a mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, and negative CSF culture findings. Tuberculous meningitis was excluded as acid-fast stain and tuberculosis DNA assays were negative.

The possibility of a primary malignancy of the CNS was evaluated due to the neurological symptoms, but brain MRI revealed no abnormalities and CSF cytology did not demonstrate malignant cells. Despite a slight elevation in soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) levels, this isolated finding did not suffice for diagnosing CNS lymphoma.

Consideration was also given to autoimmune conditions, but these were excluded based on the lack of specific systemic manifestations and negative autoantibody screening. Neurosarcoidosis was contemplated as a differential diagnosis, noting that early MRI may be normal; however, further laboratory evidence argued against it. The modestly increased sIL-2R was considered nonspecific.

Ultimately, the patient met criteria for definite Behçet’s disease (BD), based on clinical manifestations such as fever, fatigue, oral aphthae (two points), genital aphthosis (two points), and a folliculitis-like eruption (one point). According to the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease, a cumulative score of five points (with four points or more establishing the diagnosis) was achieved. Genital aphthosis is recognized as a distinctive marker of BD, thereby strongly indicating this condition. Following the exclusion of infectious, neoplastic, and autoimmune etiologies, a final diagnosis of acute neuro-Behçet’s disease was made, with central nervous system involvement in conjunction with mucocutaneous features.

Treatment:

On the first day of hospitalization, an underlying inflammatory process was suspected, though the definitive cause could not be determined. Empiric treatment with cefepime was begun after obtaining blood cultures, aiming to address a possible bacterial infection in the absence of a clear site of infection.

By the third day, the patient experienced intensified headache and vertigo, raising concerns regarding central nervous system inflammation or infection. Acyclovir was started as an empiric measure but discontinued once laboratory studies excluded both HSV and VZV encephalitis.

Colchicine was introduced on day 8 following the confirmed diagnoses of systemic Behçet's disease (BD) and acute neuro-Behçet's disease (neuro-BD). Topical corticosteroid ointment was applied to oral aphthous lesions, while silver sulfadiazine cream was utilized for genital ulcers and skin lesions. SIADH management consisted of conservative fluid restriction to less than 800 mL per day, close daily tracking of serum sodium, and avoidance of rapid correction. Neither hypertonic saline nor vasopressin receptor antagonists were necessary.

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient’s fever subsided and his appetite increased the day following initiation of colchicine. Over the subsequent two weeks, symptoms such as vertigo, general malaise, hiccups, and mucocutaneous lesions gradually resolved. By day 16, his serum sodium had corrected to 136 mmol/L and ADH levels fell to 1.8 pg/mL. Myogenic enzyme concentrations also normalized.

Notably, systemic corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive agents were not administered during the course of therapy. After a hospitalization lasting one month—during which he recovered from the acute phase, hyponatraemia was closely monitored and managed, and rehabilitation addressed the accompanying deterioration in physical function—the patient was discharged. Colchicine treatment was maintained for roughly four years, during which there was no relapse of Behçet’s disease (BD) or reappearance of neurologic manifestations. Following stabilization of his BD, biguanide medication was started, resulting in sustained optimal glycaemic control.

Discussion:

This report describes a rare clinical occurrence of concurrent systemic BD and acute neuro-BD diagnoses. It further illustrates that acute neuro-BD may be associated with SIADH. Improvement of mucocutaneous lesions, neurological signs, and hyponatraemia after colchicine monotherapy indicates a potential direct link among BD, neuro-BD, and SIADH. In individuals with BD who develop fever or headache together with hyponatraemia, acute neuro-BD should be considered as a possible etiology.

This presentation is significant due to the concurrence of systemic BD and acute neuro-BD, as evidenced by both mucocutaneous involvement and central nervous system features. Conventionally, major organ manifestations such as CNS pathology are observed around 4 years post-initial BD diagnosis, and neurological symptoms at initial presentation are rare, occurring in less than 5% of cases. Headache directly attributable to neuro-BD is noted in only about 10% of patients. Therefore, the early identification of neuro-BD in this scenario was particularly challenging. Acute neuro-BD typically manifests as a self-limited meningoencephalitis, which may occur with or without localized neurological symptoms. In this case, brain MRI failed to detect characteristic lesions in customary sites, including the hypothalamic area. Acute neuro-BD can appear as aseptic meningitis in the absence of parenchymal findings, and normal brain MRI, particularly in early disease, does not preclude the diagnosis. The absence of radiographic evidence added difficulty to confirming the diagnosis. Though HLA-B51 was assessed, this genetic marker did not assist with diagnostic clarification, in accordance with international BD criteria, which emphasize clinical manifestations over HLA-B51 status. This case underscores the necessity of considering neuro-BD in patients with BD who demonstrate mucocutaneous signs and unspecific neurological symptoms.

At the outset of our differential diagnosis, we chiefly considered infection as the cause of CNS inflammation due to the presence of fever of unknown origin, neurological manifestations, and hyponatraemia; nonetheless, no specific infectious agent was detected. Alternative etiologies of CNS inflammation, such as connective tissue disorders and neoplasms, were also evaluated, and conditions like sarcoidosis and hematologic malignancy were incorporated into the differential. Consequently, we included autoimmune serologic testing, as well as serum ACE and sIL-2R assays, as part of a comprehensive initial screening. These investigations were pursued in a targeted manner as part of a process of exclusion, rather than stemming from a strong clinical suspicion. No supportive findings emerged from the clinical evaluation, imaging, or CSF analysis for these conditions, and these tests did not significantly impact the final diagnosis. Given its limited sensitivity, ACE is not a definitive marker for sarcoidosis, and while sIL-2R can assist in assessing malignant lymphoma, modest elevations are not specific and may indicate inflammation more generally. In this instance, the lack of genital ulcers during the early phase of symptom development made Behçet’s disease a less clear consideration, resulting in an extensive differential diagnosis. The diagnostic approach employed here underscores the necessity of avoiding unselective testing and reinforces that investigations should be guided by relevant clinical indications and interpreted judiciously.

While SIADH resulting from medications, such as cyclophosphamide, has been documented in individuals with BD, the link between acute neuro-BD and SIADH is not well delineated. Neuro-BD involves central nervous system inflammation, which may influence abnormal antidiuretic hormone (ADH) secretion. Multiple studies have demonstrated that interleukin-6 (IL-6), a proinflammatory cytokine often increased in neuroinflammatory conditions, is correlated with enhanced ADH secretion. In Behçet disease, atypical monocyte activation results in spontaneous IL-6 release, and raised IL-6 concentrations have been observed in the cerebrospinal fluid of patients experiencing acute neuro-BD episodes. These observations imply that inflammation driven by IL-6 may play a role in the development of SIADH in this context. Although IL-6 levels were not assessed in serum or CSF in this patient, a substantially elevated serum C-reactive protein (CRP) value could be indicative of an IL-6–mediated inflammatory process. SIADH is linked to a variety of disorders, such as CNS inflammation, lung disease, cancer, and specific drugs; however, the available data do not permit confirmation of a direct causative relationship between SIADH and acute neuro-BD. The timing noted in this instance may represent a generalized response to CNS inflammation instead of a mechanism particular to the disease.

This case highlights possible complications associated with SIADH-induced hyponatraemia occurring during acute neuro-BD. Persistent hiccups have been previously documented as an important sign of significant hyponatraemia resulting from SIADH linked to central nervous system inflammation, such as meningitis. Furthermore, during our initial assessment for fever, arthralgia, and generalized fatigue—which could stem from inflammatory or metabolic muscle injury—we included CK testing, which identified rhabdomyolysis. Rhabdomyolysis is a recognized, though frequently overlooked, consequence of severe hyponatraemia that is not routinely screened for. Although these findings are established, their clinical relevance is often underestimated in the context of marked hyponatraemia. Healthcare providers should recognize that symptoms seemingly unrelated, like persistent hiccups or rhabdomyolysis, may signal an underlying electrolyte imbalance, especially amid neuroinflammatory disorders.

Corticosteroids represent the primary therapy for acute neuro-BD, with the majority of patients exhibiting a good clinical response. Nevertheless, there have been accounts of spontaneous improvement, particularly in instances with only meningoencephalitis-like features. In this particular patient, colchicine administered alone led to improvement in both mucocutaneous and neurological manifestations, in addition to resolving hyponatraemia. The anti-inflammatory properties of colchicine are attributed to its ability to impede neutrophil activation and migration, as well as reduce the synthesis of cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-6. While colchicine is commonly utilized for treating mucocutaneous symptoms in BD, comprehensive reviews indicate it lacks efficacy in managing acute neuro-BD, for which immunosuppressants are generally necessary for appropriate disease management. Reported cases describe colchicine as an adjunctive therapy alongside corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive medications, rather than as a single-agent regimen. Although spontaneous remission cannot be ruled out in this individual, the outcomes suggest that colchicine might serve as an alternative therapeutic option for acute neuro-BD and associated conditions like SIADH, especially when corticosteroid administration is not advisable or possible.

Conclusion:

We report a unique instance of Behçet's disease (BD) presenting simultaneously with acute neuro-BD, wherein neuro-BD was further complicated by the development of SIADH. This case illustrates that SIADH can manifest as a complication during the acute phase of neuro-BD, underscoring the need for clinicians to recognize this possible correlation. Administration of colchicine alone resulted in swift resolution of mucocutaneous signs, neurological symptoms, and hyponatraemia, indicating colchicine could serve as an effective early-stage therapy for neuro-BD. When patients with BD exhibit vague central nervous system symptoms—such as fever and headache—alongside hyponatraemia, practitioners should be vigilant for acute neuro-BD, particularly if SIADH is also present.
