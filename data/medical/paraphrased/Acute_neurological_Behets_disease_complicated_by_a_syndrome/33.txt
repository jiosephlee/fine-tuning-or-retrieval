Title: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

Eiichi Kakehi, Yukako Matsumoto, Shigehisa Sakurai, Kazuhiko Kotani

Abstract:

Summary: We describe an unusual case involving the simultaneous onset of acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) and systemic Behçet’s disease (BD), meeting criteria for definite BD and complicated by the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). A male patient in his 60s presented with fever, headache, and mucocutaneous signs, including oral aphthae, folliculitis-like eruptions, genital ulcers, and hyponatraemia. BD was diagnosed on the basis of these mucocutaneous manifestations. Although brain MRI was unremarkable, cerebrospinal fluid analysis indicated aseptic meningitis, consistent with acute neuro-BD. Laboratory findings fulfilled the diagnostic criteria for SIADH. The administration of colchicine alone resulted in remission of mucocutaneous lesions, neurological symptoms, and hyponatraemia, obviating the need for corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive agents. This case highlights a potential link between acute neuro-BD and SIADH and emphasizes the significance of identifying concomitant neurological and endocrine symptoms in patients newly diagnosed with BD.

Background:

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a persistent, multisystem inflammatory condition characterized by recurrent oral and genital ulcerations, uveitis, and cutaneous manifestations. Neurological complications, collectively termed neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), affect approximately 10%–20% of BD patients and usually appear several years following the initial disease diagnosis. Acute neuro-BD most often manifests as meningoencephalitis and focal brain lesions, frequently targeting the brainstem. While a delayed neurological onset is typical, simultaneous diagnosis of BD and neuro-BD is uncommon. In these scenarios, practitioners should thoroughly evaluate both mucocutaneous findings and neurological signs, such as headaches, behavioral disturbances, psychiatric features, and localized neurologic deficits.

The syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) is a recognized etiology of hyponatraemia and is commonly associated with central nervous system (CNS) inflammation, neoplastic processes, or certain pharmaceutical agents. Due to the inflammatory basis of acute neuro-BD, these patients may have an elevated risk for SIADH. Although reports exist of SIADH in BD individuals undergoing treatment with agents like cyclophosphamide, instances where SIADH develops exclusively due to CNS inflammation from acute neuro-BD, without pharmacologic provocation, have not been clearly established.

Herein, we describe an unusual presentation involving simultaneous onset of BD and acute neuro-BD, accompanied by SIADH. This case emphasizes the need to recognize SIADH as a potential complication of acute neuro-BD, especially in individuals displaying both general CNS symptoms and hyponatraemia.

Case presentation:

A man of Asian descent in his 60s arrived at the emergency department following a 3-day duration of fever, headache, generalized fatigue, diminished appetite, continuous hiccups, and joint pain involving both shoulders and ankles. Additionally, he noted the presence of oral aphthous ulcers.

His past medical history was significant for type 2 diabetes mellitus; however, he was not undergoing therapy due to limited disease awareness and inadequate adherence to dietary or exercise recommendations. There was no pertinent family history, including an absence of Behçet’s disease among relatives. He reported occasional alcohol intake, denied smoking, recent travel, or known contact with ill individuals or animals. His occupation was in metalworking, and he had no history of recent sexual encounters.

Upon evaluation, the patient was oriented but demonstrated signs of tiredness. His vitals included a body temperature of 38.1°C, blood pressure measured at 135/73 mm Hg, heart rate of 119 bpm (regular rhythm), respiratory rate of 19 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation at 96% while breathing ambient air. Examination identified oral aphthous lesions as well as folliculitis-like eruptions on the facial and trunk regions (figure 1A, B, and C). No abnormalities were found during cardiopulmonary and abdominal assessments. Strength testing with manual muscle examination showed no weakness in either the upper or lower limbs. No peripheral edema was observed, and his volume status was judged to be euvolemic on clinical grounds. The patient was hospitalized for additional investigation.

Investigations:

Initial blood tests showed significant hyponatraemia (124 mmol/L), raised creatine kinase (1349 U/L), aspartate aminotransferase (77 U/L), alanine aminotransferase (64 U/L), C-reactive protein (CRP) level (180 mg/L; normal, <3 mg/L), and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (70 mm/hour; normal, <15 mm/hour), while renal function remained normal. The patient was also found to have hyperglycaemia (plasma glucose 14.2 mmol/L) with an increased HbA1c at 8.2%, suggesting suboptimal control of blood sugar. Hepatitis B and C screening returned negative results.

Assessment of endocrine function demonstrated thyroid-stimulating hormone within the reference range (1.11 mIU/L; reference, 0.50–5.00 mIU/L), normal free T4 (1.25 ng/dL; reference, 0.90–1.70 ng/dL), and adequate serum cortisol levels (42 µg/dL; reference, >6 µg/dL). Serum osmolality was reduced (267 mOsm/kg; reference, <270 mOsm/kg), while urine osmolality was markedly increased (900 mOsm/kg, reference, >300 mOsm/kg); urinary sodium was also elevated (88 mmol/L; reference, >20 mmol/L), consistent with compromised water excretion. Despite the presence of hypo-osmolar hyponatraemia, antidiuretic hormone (ADH) levels were raised (22.4 pg/mL). Electrocardiogram findings were unremarkable, and imaging studies—including chest X-ray and CT scans of the head, chest, and abdomen—showed no abnormalities. Collectively, these results were consistent with a diagnosis of SIADH.

On the third day, his headache and vertigo became more severe. Despite the absence of neck stiffness or cranial nerve signs, central nervous system inflammation was suspected. MRI of the brain showed no significant abnormalities in the hypothalamus or other regions (figure 2). Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) examination demonstrated a slightly raised opening pressure (17 cmH₂O), elevated protein levels (0.97 g/L), and mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL). Tests for bacterial, mycobacterial, HSV, VZV, and cryptococcal antigen in the CSF were all negative, as were CSF cultures. Cytological analysis of the CSF did not identify any malignant cells.

Additional assessments were carried out to investigate potential systemic illnesses. Sarcoidosis was excluded by normal lysozyme levels (5.1 µg/mL; reference range 5–10) and angiotensin-converting enzyme values (11.4 U/L; reference range 7–25). Screening for hematologic malignancy revealed an increased soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) level (704 U/mL; reference, 122–496 U/mL), while human T-cell leukemia virus type 1 antibody testing was negative.

By day 5, a painful ulcer developed on his scrotum (figure 1D). Together with oral aphthae and rash resembling folliculitis, these features indicated a systemic form of BD. The patient showed no hypersensitivity to pinprick. Ocular assessment did not reveal any signs of uveitis. HLA-B51 testing was positive.

Differential diagnosis:

Upon admission, the presence of fever, headache, general malaise, and hyponatraemia raised suspicion for either a central nervous system infection or a systemic inflammatory process. Initially, viral meningoencephalitis—most notably due to HSV or VZV—was the leading consideration. Nevertheless, CSF analysis did not detect viral antigen or antibodies, thus ruling out these infections. The possibility of bacterial meningitis was addressed, but this was excluded on the grounds of no predominance of neutrophils, a minor degree of lymphocytic pleocytosis, and negative CSF cultures. Tuberculous meningitis was also considered but dismissed due to negative acid-fast bacilli staining and absence of tuberculosis DNA in the CSF.

A primary malignancy involving the CNS was entertained because of the neurological manifestations; however, brain MRI findings were unremarkable, and cytological examination of the CSF did not uncover malignant cells. Although the soluble IL-2 receptor (sIL-2R) level was found to be modestly increased, this was not sufficient on its own to diagnose CNS lymphoma.

An autoimmune etiology was also investigated and ruled out because there were no hallmark systemic features and autoantibody screens returned negative. Neurosarcoidosis was entertained in the differential, especially since early imaging may not reveal abnormalities, but subsequent laboratory investigations argued against this diagnosis. The mild sIL-2R elevation was interpreted as a non-specific laboratory finding.

A definitive diagnosis of Behçet's disease (BD) was made, supported by clinical presentation: fever and fatigue, oral aphthae (scoring two points), genital aphthosis (two points), and a folliculitis-like eruption (one point). According to the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease, this resulted in a total of five points (with four or more indicating definite BD). Genital aphthosis, in particular, is highly specific for BD and reinforced the diagnosis. With infectious, malignant, and autoimmune causes excluded, acute neuro-Behçet’s disease was ultimately diagnosed, based on central nervous system manifestations occurring alongside mucocutaneous features.

Treatment:

On the first day of hospitalization, clinicians suspected an underlying inflammatory disorder, but the precise cause was undetermined. Empiric treatment with cefepime was started after obtaining blood cultures, aiming to cover possible bacterial pathogens despite no clear infectious source.

By day 3, the patient's headache and vertigo had intensified, raising suspicion for inflammation or infection of the central nervous system. Acyclovir was given as an empiric antiviral but subsequently discontinued when laboratory investigations excluded herpes simplex virus and varicella-zoster virus encephalitis.

On day 8, colchicine therapy commenced in response to a diagnosis of systemic Behçet’s disease (BD) and acute neuro-BD. Oral corticosteroid ointment was used to address aphthous ulcers in the mouth. Silver sulfadiazine cream was applied for genital and skin lesions. Syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) was managed with conservative measures including restricted fluid intake (<800 mL/day), close daily surveillance of serum sodium levels, and deliberate avoidance of rapid sodium correction. Neither hypertonic saline nor vasopressin receptor antagonists were utilized.

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient's fever subsided, and his appetite increased the day following the initiation of colchicine. Over the next two weeks, symptoms such as vertigo, malaise, hiccups, and mucocutaneous manifestations progressively lessened. By day 16, his serum sodium returned to a normal value of 136 mmol/L, and ADH levels had declined to 1.8 pg/mL. Levels of myogenic enzymes were also within normal limits.

Notably, systemic corticosteroids or alternative immunosuppressive agents were not administered throughout his treatment course. After a hospital stay of 1 month—during which he recovered from the acute episode, hyponatraemia was closely tracked and managed, and rehabilitative support was given for physical deconditioning—the patient was discharged. Colchicine treatment continued for around 4 years, during which no relapse of BD or neurological issues was observed. Once his BD became stable, biguanide medication was started, resulting in sustained satisfactory glycaemic control.

Discussion:

In this case, systemic BD and acute neuro-BD were diagnosed concurrently, a scenario that is rarely observed in clinical practice. The case further illustrated that acute neuro-BD may be associated with the development of SIADH. Improvement of mucocutaneous lesions, neurological symptoms, and hyponatraemia following treatment with colchicine alone indicates a potential direct link among BD, neuro-BD, and SIADH. When BD patients exhibit fever or headache along with hyponatraemia, clinicians should suspect acute neuro-BD as a possible cause.

What distinguishes this case is the concurrent detection of both systemic BD and acute neuro-BD, demonstrated by the presence of mucocutaneous and central nervous system signs. Ordinarily, involvement of major organs such as the CNS typically develops about four years after the BD diagnosis, while neurological symptoms at onset are infrequent, appearing in less than 5% of cases. Additionally, only about 10% of individuals report headaches directly attributable to neuro-BD. As a result, the early identification of neuro-BD in this context was intrinsically difficult. Acute neuro-BD is most frequently manifested as a self-limiting meningoencephalitis, sometimes accompanied by focal neurological impairments. For this patient, brain MRI did not reveal characteristic BD lesions, including within the hypothalamus. It is possible for acute neuro-BD to present as aseptic meningitis without any parenchymal abnormalities, and a normal brain MRI in the early disease stage does not preclude the diagnosis. The absence of typical imaging findings complicated the diagnostic process further. Although HLA-B51 was evaluated, the results were not useful for the diagnosis, which is consistent with global BD criteria that prioritize clinical characteristics over HLA-B51 status. This report underscores the need to consider neuro-BD in patients with mucocutaneous involvement who present with vague neurological complaints.

At the outset of the diagnostic workup, infection was considered the most likely etiology for the patient's CNS inflammation due to the presence of persistent fever of unknown cause, neurological manifestations, and hyponatremia; nevertheless, no identifiable pathogen was detected. Alternative etiologies for CNS inflammation were also assessed, including autoimmune connective tissue disorders and neoplasms, with sarcoidosis and hematologic malignancies incorporated into the differential. Accordingly, an extensive preliminary assessment was carried out, including the evaluation of autoimmune serology, and measurement of serum ACE and sIL-2R as general screening measures. These investigations were selectively pursued primarily for exclusion purposes, not because of specific clinical concern. Ultimately, clinical assessment, imaging studies, and CSF analysis yielded no findings to support these alternative diagnoses, and the results of these screening tests were not substantially contributory to establishing the definitive diagnosis. It is noted that the sensitivity of serum ACE for sarcoidosis is limited, and while sIL-2R can provide diagnostic clues for malignant lymphoma, slight elevations are not specific and might instead reflect generalized inflammation. In the current case, the lack of genital ulceration during the early clinical course made Behçet’s disease (BD) less certain, necessitating an expansive differential. This approach to diagnosis underscores the necessity of judicious test selection and illustrates that investigations should be guided by the relevant clinical picture, with results interpreted judiciously.

While SIADH induced by medications such as cyclophosphamide has been documented in patients with BD, the relationship between acute neuro-BD and SIADH is not well defined. Neuro-BD involves inflammation within the central nervous system, which can potentially disrupt the regulation of ADH secretion. Multiple studies indicate that interleukin-6 (IL-6), a proinflammatory cytokine commonly elevated in neuroinflammatory conditions, is linked to increased ADH production. In BD, abnormal activation of monocytes results in spontaneous IL-6 synthesis, and increased IL-6 concentrations have been observed in the cerebrospinal fluid of individuals experiencing acute neuro-BD. These observations imply that IL-6–driven inflammation may play a role in the development of SIADH in such cases. Although IL-6 concentrations were not assessed in either serum or CSF for this patient, the significantly raised serum CRP might be indicative of an IL-6–related inflammatory process. SIADH is known to be associated with a variety of factors, including CNS inflammation, respiratory disease, cancer, and particular drugs; however, the findings in this case are insufficient to directly link SIADH with acute neuro-BD. The timing of SIADH in this patient may simply represent a general response to CNS inflammation, rather than a specific pathophysiological mechanism unique to neuro-BD.

This case further demonstrates the range of complications that may arise from hyponatraemia due to SIADH in acute neuro-BD. Prior literature has indicated that persistent hiccups can be a valuable clinical indicator of severe hyponatraemia induced by SIADH as a result of central nervous system inflammation, such as meningitis. During the initial workup for fever, arthralgia, and general fatigue—which might be attributed to muscle injury of either inflammatory or metabolic origin—we included creatine kinase measurement, which detected rhabdomyolysis. Although rhabdomyolysis is a recognized complication of significant hyponatraemia, it frequently goes unnoticed and is not routinely assessed. Such presentations, though recognized, are frequently underestimated in their relevance to severe hyponatraemia. Healthcare providers should recognize that symptoms appearing unrelated, such as persistent hiccups or rhabdomyolysis, may in fact indicate underlying electrolyte abnormalities, especially within the setting of neuroinflammatory disorders.

Corticosteroids are generally regarded as the primary therapy for acute neuro-BD, with most individuals experiencing a positive response. Nonetheless, there have been accounts of cases limited to meningoencephalitis-like presentations that resolved without intervention. In this report, administration of colchicine alone led to improvement in both mucocutaneous and neurological manifestations, along with correction of hyponatraemia. Colchicine’s anti-inflammatory action involves the inhibition of neutrophil migration and activity, as well as the suppression of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-6. While colchicine is commonly utilized for mucocutaneous symptoms in BD, evidence from systematic reviews indicates that it is not effective as monotherapy for acute neuro-BD, where immunosuppressive agents are usually necessary for sufficient disease control. Typically, colchicine has been combined with corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants, rather than used alone, in published cases. Although the possibility of spontaneous improvement cannot be ruled out for this patient, these findings imply that colchicine might serve as an alternative therapeutic option for acute neuro-BD and associated conditions like SIADH, especially when corticosteroids are not recommended or must be avoided.

Conclusion:

We reported an uncommon instance of Behçet's disease (BD) presenting simultaneously with acute neuro-BD, in which neuro-BD was further complicated by the development of SIADH. This case illustrates that SIADH can arise as a complication of acute neuro-BD, emphasizing the necessity for clinicians to recognize this possible association. Treatment with colchicine alone resulted in prompt resolution of mucocutaneous signs, neurological symptoms, and hyponatraemia, indicating that colchicine could serve as an effective therapeutic approach during the early stage of neuro-BD. When individuals with BD exhibit non-specific central nervous system manifestations, such as fever and headache, together with hyponatraemia, clinicians should consider the possibility of acute neuro-BD associated with SIADH.
