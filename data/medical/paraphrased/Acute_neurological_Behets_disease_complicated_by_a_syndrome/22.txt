Title: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

Eiichi Kakehi, Yukako Matsumoto, Shigehisa Sakurai, Kazuhiko Kotani

Abstract:

Summary: We present an uncommon instance of acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) simultaneously identified with systemic Behçet’s disease (BD), classified as definite BD, and complicated by the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). A male in his 60s exhibited fever, headache, and mucocutaneous symptoms, including oral aphthae, folliculitis-like eruptions, genital ulcers, and hyponatraemia. The BD diagnosis was established on the basis of mucocutaneous findings. Brain MRI was unremarkable, while cerebrospinal fluid analysis demonstrated aseptic meningitis, consistent with acute neuro-BD. SIADH was confirmed via laboratory criteria. The patient was treated with colchicine monotherapy, which resulted in the resolution of both mucocutaneous and neurological symptoms as well as hyponatraemia, without corticosteroid or additional immunosuppressive therapy. This report highlights a potential link between acute neuro-BD and SIADH and emphasizes the necessity of recognizing simultaneous neurologic and endocrine involvement in individuals newly diagnosed with BD.

Background:

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a persistent, multisystem inflammatory condition marked by repeated occurrences of oral and genital ulcers, as well as uveitis and dermatological findings. Neurological manifestations, termed neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), appear in about 10%–20% of individuals and often arise several years following the initial BD diagnosis. Acute forms of neuro-BD frequently manifest as meningoencephalitis accompanied by parenchymal lesions, predominantly affecting the brainstem. Simultaneous identification of both BD and neuro-BD is unusual, as neurological involvement typically emerges later; however, in such scenarios, clinicians should evaluate both cutaneous and neurological findings, including headaches, behavioral disturbances, psychiatric manifestations, and localized neurological deficits.

Syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) is a recognized etiology of hyponatraemia, commonly secondary to CNS inflammation, neoplasms, or certain medications. In light of the inflammatory process in acute neuro-BD, individuals may be predisposed to developing SIADH. Although SIADH has been documented in patients with BD receiving agents like cyclophosphamide, there is limited understanding of its development due to CNS inflammation alone in the context of acute neuro-BD, absent pharmacological influences.

Here, we describe an uncommon case in which both BD and acute neuro-BD were diagnosed simultaneously and complicated by SIADH. This report underscores the necessity of recognizing SIADH as a possible complication of acute neuro-BD, particularly among patients with general neurological symptoms and low sodium levels.

Case presentation:

A man of Asian descent in his 60s arrived at the emergency department, reporting three days of fever, headache, fatigue, decreased appetite, ongoing hiccups, and joint pain in the shoulders and ankles. He also noted the presence of oral aphthae.

His past medical history was notable for type 2 diabetes mellitus, which remained untreated due to his limited understanding of the disease and lack of adherence to recommended dietary and exercise routines. There was no significant family history, including no relatives with BD. He drank alcohol occasionally, was a non-smoker, had not traveled recently, and lacked exposure to animals or ill persons. He was employed in the metalworking field and had not participated in recent sexual activity.

Upon assessment, the patient was conscious but exhibited signs of tiredness. His vital measurements included a body temperature of 38.1°C, blood pressure recorded at 135/73 mm Hg, regular heart rate of 119 beats per minute, respiratory rate at 19 breaths per minute, and an oxygen saturation of 96% breathing room air. Physical inspection identified the presence of oral aphthae and rash resembling folliculitis on the face and trunk (refer to figure 1A, B, and C). There were no abnormal findings noted on the cardiopulmonary or abdominal examinations. Manual evaluation of muscle strength indicated the absence of weakness in both upper and lower limbs. Peripheral oedema was not observed, and the patient’s volume status was clinically determined to be euvolemic. He was hospitalized to allow for additional diagnostic assessment.

Investigations:

Initial laboratory evaluation demonstrated significant hyponatremia (124 mmol/L), increased creatine kinase (1349 U/L), aspartate aminotransferase (77 U/L), alanine aminotransferase (64 U/L), markedly elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) at 180 mg/L (reference range <3 mg/L), and a raised erythrocyte sedimentation rate (70 mm/hour; reference <15 mm/hour), while renal function remained within normal parameters. The patient exhibited hyperglycemia (plasma glucose 14.2 mmol/L) and an HbA1c of 8.2%, reflecting suboptimal glycemic regulation. Testing for hepatitis B and C was negative.

Assessment of endocrine function showed thyroid-stimulating hormone (1.11 mIU/L; reference, 0.50–5.00 mIU/L), free T4 (1.25 ng/dL; reference, 0.90–1.70 ng/dL), and serum cortisol (42 µg/dL; reference, >6 µg/dL) all within normal ranges. Serum osmolality was decreased (267 mOsm/kg; reference, <270 mOsm/kg), whereas urine osmolality was elevated (900 mOsm/kg; reference, >300 mOsm/kg), and urinary sodium excretion was high (88 mmol/L; reference, >20 mmol/L), which suggests reduced water elimination. Despite the presence of hypo-osmolar hyponatremia, the antidiuretic hormone (ADH) concentration was raised at 22.4 pg/mL. The electrocardiogram (ECG), chest X-ray, and computed tomography of the head, chest, and abdomen revealed no abnormalities. Collectively, these data were indicative of SIADH.

By the third day, the patient's headache and vertigo had intensified. While neck rigidity and cranial nerve deficits were absent, central nervous system inflammation was considered. MRI of the brain demonstrated no notable anomalies affecting the hypothalamus or other regions (figure 2). Examination of the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) showed a mildly raised opening pressure (17 cmH₂O), increased protein concentration (0.97 g/L), and moderate lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL). CSF cultures and assays for bacterial and mycobacterial organisms, herpes simplex virus (HSV), varicella zoster virus (VZV), and cryptococcal antigen all yielded negative results. Cytological assessment of the CSF found no evidence of malignant cells.

Additional assessments were undertaken to rule out systemic conditions. Evaluation for sarcoidosis included normal levels of lysozyme (5.1 µg/mL; reference 5–10) and angiotensin-converting enzyme (11.4 U/L; reference 7–25). Screening for haematological malignancy identified an elevated soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) at 704 U/mL (reference, 122–496 U/mL) while testing for human T-cell leukaemia virus type 1 antibodies was negative.

On the fifth day, a painful ulcer developed in the scrotal region (figure 1D). Together with the presence of oral aphthae and rashes resembling folliculitis, these features were indicative of a systemic variant of BD. There was no abnormal pinprick sensitivity. Ophthalmologic evaluation did not detect any uveitis. HLA-B51 testing returned positive.

Differential diagnosis:

Upon admission, the constellation of fever, headache, generalized malaise, and hyponatremia indicated the possibility of a central nervous system (CNS) infection or systemic inflammatory disorder. Initially, viral meningoencephalitis—especially HSV or VZV—was suspected. Nonetheless, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analyses were negative for viral antigens and antibodies, thus eliminating these viruses as causative agents. Bacterial meningitis was also considered but discounted due to the lack of neutrophil predominance, only mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, and negative CSF cultures. Testing for acid-fast bacilli and tuberculosis DNA in the CSF produced negative results, ruling out tuberculous meningitis.

The potential for primary CNS neoplasia was entertained due to the neurological presentation, yet MRI of the brain demonstrated no abnormalities and cytological examination of CSF did not detect malignant cells. Although the soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) level was slightly raised, this alone was inadequate to support a diagnosis of CNS lymphoma.

Autoimmune disorders were also investigated but dismissed, as no typical systemic manifestations were observed and screening for autoantibodies yielded negative results. Neurosarcoidosis was included in the differential diagnosis because MRI may appear normal during the early phase; however, further laboratory findings rendered this unlikely. The mildly elevated sIL-2R was considered nonspecific.

A definitive diagnosis of Behçet’s Disease (BD) was made based on clinical findings such as fever, fatigue, oral aphthae (worth two points), genital aphthosis (two points), and a folliculitis-like rash (one point). Using the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease, the patient’s total score was five points (≥4 indicates definite BD). Genital aphthosis, in particular, is recognized as a highly specific sign of BD, lending strong support to this diagnosis. With infectious, neoplastic, and autoimmune etiologies ruled out, the patient was ultimately diagnosed with acute neuro-Behçet’s disease, presenting with CNS manifestations alongside mucocutaneous features.

Treatment:

On the first day of hospitalization, clinicians suspected an underlying inflammatory disorder, though the precise cause was not determined. Cefepime was started empirically following blood culture collection, aiming to cover a possible bacterial infection, even in the absence of a clear infectious source.

By the third day, the patient’s headache and vertigo had intensified, raising concerns for central nervous system inflammation or infection. Acyclovir was given empirically, but later discontinued once investigations excluded HSV and VZV encephalitis.

On the eighth day, colchicine therapy was initiated after confirming systemic Behçet’s disease and acute neuro-Behçet’s disease. Oral aphthae were managed with topical corticosteroid ointment. Genital ulcers and skin lesions were treated using silver sulfadiazine cream. Syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) was addressed with conservative fluid restriction (<800 mL/day), close daily surveillance of serum sodium, and careful avoidance of rapid sodium correction. Hypertonic saline and vasopressin receptor antagonists were unnecessary.

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient's fever subsided and his appetite increased the day following the initiation of colchicine therapy. Over the ensuing two weeks, his symptoms—including vertigo, general malaise, hiccups, and mucocutaneous lesions—steadily improved. On day 16, his serum sodium normalized at 136 mmol/L, while ADH levels decreased to 1.8 pg/mL. Levels of myogenic enzymes also returned to reference ranges.

Notably, no systemic corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive agents were administered during the course of treatment. The patient was discharged after spending one month in the hospital, during which acute-phase recovery was achieved, hyponatraemia was closely monitored and treated, and rehabilitation was performed to address his reduction in physical capacity. Colchicine treatment was maintained for roughly four years, with no recurrence of BD or neurological manifestations observed during that period. Once BD was stabilized, biguanide therapy was initiated, resulting in sustained effective glycaemic control.

Discussion:

In this report, systemic BD and acute neuro-BD were diagnosed concurrently—a scenario seldom encountered in clinical practice. The case further underscores that acute neuro-BD may be accompanied by SIADH. The observed resolution of mucocutaneous, neurological, and electrolyte disturbances following colchicine monotherapy indicates a potential link among BD, neuro-BD, and SIADH. Clinicians are advised to suspect acute neuro-BD in BD patients who develop fever or headache in conjunction with hyponatraemia.

This presentation is significant due to the simultaneous identification of both systemic BD and acute neuro-BD, evidenced by characteristic mucocutaneous involvement and CNS pathology. Ordinarily, manifestations affecting major organs such as the CNS appear approximately 4 years after BD is first identified, with neurological symptoms at the time of initial presentation being rare (fewer than 5% of cases). Additionally, only a small subset of patients—around 10%—have headaches attributable specifically to neuro-BD, making the initial diagnosis particularly challenging. Acute neuro-BD is most frequently characterized by a self-limiting meningoencephalitis, which may occur with or without focal neurological deficits. In this instance, the patient’s brain MRI did not reveal any typical BD-associated abnormalities, including in the hypothalamic region. Acute neuro-BD may manifest as aseptic meningitis absent any parenchymal brain lesions, and a normal MRI during the early stage does not exclude the condition. The absence of diagnostic imaging findings further complicated reaching a definitive diagnosis. Although HLA-B51 typing was conducted, it provided no diagnostic value, which is consistent with international classification criteria for BD that prioritize clinical signs over genetic testing. This case illustrates the necessity of considering neuro-BD in the differential when individuals with BD and mucocutaneous symptoms present with unexplained neurological complaints.

At the outset of the differential diagnosis, infection was considered the leading possible etiology for CNS inflammation, due to the presence of unexplained fever, neurologic manifestations, and hyponatraemia; nonetheless, no causative organism was detected. Additional differential considerations encompassed other sources of CNS inflammation, such as connective tissue disorders and malignancies, specifically including sarcoidosis and hematologic malignancies. Accordingly, our initial broad diagnostic workup included assessment of autoimmune serological markers, serum ACE, and sIL-2R as part of screening investigations. These tests were utilized selectively in an exclusionary manner without strong suspicion from the clinical scenario. There was no supporting evidence from clinical evaluation, imaging, or CSF analysis to substantiate these diagnoses, and the results of these tests ultimately did not aid the final diagnosis. The sensitivity of ACE for sarcoidosis is suboptimal, and while sIL-2R might have some utility in evaluating malignant lymphoma, only minor increases are non-specific and could indicate generalized inflammation. In this instance, the lack of genital ulcers during early symptom development rendered Behçet’s disease (BD) less likely, leading us to a broader differential. This process demonstrates the need to refrain from excessive indiscriminate investigations and underscores that such testing should only be guided by pertinent clinical findings, with results interpreted prudently.

While SIADH caused by medications, such as cyclophosphamide in patients with BD, has been documented, the relationship between acute neuro-BD and SIADH remains largely unclear. Neuro-BD involves inflammation of the central nervous system, which could potentially lead to abnormal ADH secretion. Multiple studies have indicated that interleukin-6 (IL-6)—a pro-inflammatory cytokine commonly increased in neuroinflammatory diseases—is correlated with enhanced ADH production. In Behçet’s disease, abnormal activation of monocytes results in spontaneous synthesis of IL-6, with elevated IL-6 concentrations identified in the cerebrospinal fluid of individuals experiencing acute neuro-BD. These observations imply that IL-6-driven inflammation may play a role in the development of SIADH in this context. Although the present case did not include IL-6 measurements in either serum or CSF, a significantly increased serum CRP could be indicative of an IL-6-mediated inflammatory reaction. SIADH has been linked to a range of conditions such as central nervous system inflammation, lung diseases, cancers, and certain drug therapies. However, the current evidence does not permit a definite causal relationship between SIADH and acute neuro-BD to be determined. The timing of SIADH onset in this patient may represent a generic response to CNS inflammation rather than a mechanism unique to neuro-BD.

This case further highlights the range of complications that may arise from hyponatraemia due to SIADH within the context of acute neuro-BD. Persistent hiccups have previously been documented as a significant clinical indicator of marked hyponatraemia secondary to SIADH associated with central nervous system inflammation, such as meningitis. Additionally, creatine kinase (CK) was assessed during the initial workup for fever, arthralgia, and general fatigue—symptoms which could be attributed to either inflammatory or metabolic muscle injury—and this evaluation identified rhabdomyolysis. Rhabdomyolysis is a recognized but frequently missed consequence of severe hyponatraemia, as it is not commonly screened for. While these presentations are established, their clinical relevance in profound hyponatraemia is often underestimated. Health care providers should recognize that symptoms appearing unrelated, like hiccups or rhabdomyolysis, might indicate significant electrolyte imbalances, especially in the presence of neuroinflammatory disease.

Corticosteroids remain the primary therapy for acute neuro-BD, with most patients exhibiting a positive clinical response. Nonetheless, there are documented instances of spontaneous recovery in patients presenting only with meningoencephalitis-like manifestations. In the current report, colchicine used alone alleviated both mucocutaneous and neurologic symptoms, as well as hyponatraemia. Colchicine mediates anti-inflammatory effects by blocking neutrophil activity and migration and inhibiting cytokine production, notably IL-1β and IL-6. While colchicine is commonly employed to manage mucocutaneous symptoms in BD, evidence from systematic reviews indicates its lack of efficacy for acute neuro-BD, emphasizing the need for immunosuppressive therapy for sufficient disease control. Previously published cases have described colchicine as a supplemental agent alongside corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive drugs, rather than as the sole treatment. Although the possibility of spontaneous improvement cannot be dismissed in this instance, these observations imply that colchicine may represent a viable treatment alternative for acute neuro-BD and related complications such as SIADH, particularly when corticosteroids are not preferred or contraindicated.

Conclusion:

We presented an uncommon instance in which Behçet’s disease (BD) and acute neuro-BD were diagnosed at the same time, with neuro-BD complicated by SIADH. This report illustrates that SIADH can manifest as a complication during the acute phase of neuro-BD, emphasizing the necessity for clinicians to recognize this possible relationship. Treatment with colchicine alone resulted in rapid resolution of mucocutaneous signs, neurological symptoms, and hyponatraemia, indicating that colchicine could be an effective treatment approach in the initial stages of neuro-BD. For BD patients exhibiting non-specific central nervous system symptoms such as fever and headache alongside hyponatraemia, physicians should maintain a heightened suspicion for acute neuro-BD when SIADH is also present.
