Title: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

Eiichi Kakehi, Yukako Matsumoto, Shigehisa Sakurai, Kazuhiko Kotani

Abstract:

Summary: We describe an unusual instance of acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) identified at the same time as systemic Behçet’s disease (BD), fulfilling criteria for definite BD and accompanied by syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). A male patient in his 60s presented with fever, headache, and mucocutaneous symptoms such as oral aphthae, folliculitis-like skin lesions, genital ulcers, and hyponatraemia. BD was diagnosed based on these mucocutaneous findings. Although brain MRI was unremarkable, cerebrospinal fluid analysis indicated aseptic meningitis, which supported a diagnosis of acute neuro-BD. Laboratory assessment met the criteria for SIADH. Administration of colchicine alone resulted in resolution of the patient's mucocutaneous and neurological symptoms as well as correction of hyponatraemia, eliminating the need for corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive therapy. This report highlights a potential link between acute neuro-BD and SIADH, emphasizing the significance of identifying simultaneous neurological and endocrine involvement in patients with newly diagnosed BD.

Background:

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a persistent inflammatory condition affecting multiple organ systems, typically presenting with recurrent oral and genital ulcerations, eye inflammation (uveitis), and dermatological manifestations. Neurological involvement, known as neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), is observed in around 10%–20% of cases, usually manifesting years following the original diagnosis. Acute neuro-BD commonly features meningoencephalitis and brain lesions, most frequently affecting the brainstem region. While neuro-BD often appears after BD has been established, concurrent diagnosis of both is unusual. When this occurs, clinicians should evaluate for both skin and mucosal findings as well as neurological complaints, including headache, changes in behavior, psychiatric symptoms, and localized neurological deficits.

The syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) is a recognized etiology of hyponatraemia, typically triggered by central nervous system (CNS) inflammatory processes, neoplasms, or particular drugs. The inflammatory processes underlying acute neuro-BD may predispose patients to developing SIADH. While there have been documented instances of SIADH occurring in BD patients undergoing treatment with agents such as cyclophosphamide, instances arising independently of such therapies and directly linked to CNS inflammation from acute neuro-BD have not been clearly established.

We present an uncommon case involving simultaneous diagnosis of BD and acute neuro-BD, complicated by the development of SIADH. This case underlines the need to consider SIADH among the possible complications of acute neuro-BD, especially in those who exhibit non-specific CNS manifestations in conjunction with hyponatraemia.

Case presentation:

A man of Asian descent in his 60s arrived at the emergency department reporting three days of fever, headache, generalized fatigue, reduced appetite, ongoing hiccups, and joint pain localized to his shoulders and ankles. He additionally described the presence of oral aphthous ulcers.

His past medical history was notable for type 2 diabetes mellitus, though he was untreated due to a lack of awareness about his illness and insufficient adherence to recommended lifestyle modifications involving diet and exercise. There was no relevant family history, and no relatives had Behçet’s disease (BD). He reported occasional alcohol intake, denied tobacco use, recent travel, animal exposure, or contact with sick individuals. He was employed in metalworking and had not had sexual activity recently.

During assessment, the patient was alert, though noticeably tired. His vital signs included a temperature of 38.1°C, blood pressure measuring 135/73 mm Hg, a regular heart rate of 119 beats per minute, respiratory rate at 19 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation at 96% on ambient air. The physical examination showed the presence of oral aphthae as well as folliculitis-like lesions on his face and trunk (figure 1A, B and C). Findings from the cardiopulmonary and abdominal evaluations were normal. Manual muscle strength testing did not reveal any weakness in either the upper or lower limbs. No peripheral edema was noted, and volume status was clinically deemed euvolemic. He was hospitalized for further assessment.

Investigations:

Initial laboratory assessments demonstrated significant hyponatraemia (124 mmol/L), increased creatine kinase (1349 U/L), aspartate aminotransferase (77 U/L), alanine aminotransferase (64 U/L), elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) (180 mg/L; reference, <3 mg/L), and a raised erythrocyte sedimentation rate (70 mm/hour; reference, <15 mm/hour), while renal function remained within normal limits. Hyperglycaemia (plasma glucose 14.2 mmol/L) and a high HbA1c (8.2%) were also observed, suggestive of suboptimal glycaemic control. Tests for hepatitis B and C were negative.

Endocrinological studies indicated that thyroid-stimulating hormone (1.11 mIU/L; reference, 0.50–5.00 mIU/L), free T4 (1.25 ng/dL; reference, 0.90–1.70 ng/dL), and serum cortisol (42 µg/dL; reference, >6 µg/dL) were all within reference ranges. The patient exhibited a decreased serum osmolality (267 mOsm/kg; reference, <270 mOsm/kg) and a high urine osmolality (900 mOsm/kg; reference, >300 mOsm/kg), accompanied by an increased urinary sodium concentration (88 mmol/L; reference, >20 mmol/L), indicative of disrupted water clearance. In the presence of hypo-osmolar hyponatraemia, antidiuretic hormone (ADH) levels were found to be elevated (22.4 pg/mL). Electrocardiogram results were normal, and imaging studies including chest X-ray and CT scans of the head, chest, and abdomen showed no abnormalities. Collectively, these results were consistent with SIADH.

By day 3, his headache and vertigo had intensified. Despite the absence of neck stiffness or cranial nerve deficits, central nervous system inflammation was considered. Brain MRI showed no notable abnormalities involving the hypothalamus or other areas (figure 2). Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) evaluation demonstrated a mildly elevated opening pressure (17 cmH₂O), increased protein concentration (0.97 g/L), and a slight lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL). Microbiological assays, including CSF cultures and tests for bacterial, mycobacterial, herpes simplex virus (HSV), varicella zoster virus (VZV), and cryptococcal antigen, were all negative. CSF cytology excluded malignancy, with no malignant cells detected.

Additional assessments were undertaken to rule out systemic conditions. Sarcoidosis workup showed lysozyme at 5.1 µg/mL (reference range 5–10) and angiotensin-converting enzyme at 11.4 U/L (reference range 7–25), both within normal limits. Evaluation for hematologic malignancy identified an elevated soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) at 704 U/mL (reference range 122–496 U/mL) and a negative test for human T-cell leukemia virus type 1 antibody.

On the fifth day, a painful ulcer developed in his scrotum (figure 1D). Along with oral aphthous ulcers and skin eruptions resembling folliculitis, these symptoms indicated a systemic variant of BD. He lacked pathergy reaction to pinprick. Eye examination did not show any uveitis. HLA-B51 testing was positive.

Differential diagnosis:

Upon admission, the presence of fever, headache, generalized fatigue, and hyponatremia raised concern for either a central nervous system infection or a systemic inflammatory process. Initial diagnostic considerations included viral meningoencephalitis, specifically due to HSV or VZV. Nonetheless, cerebrospinal fluid analysis showed no evidence of viral antigens or antibodies, thereby ruling out these etiologies. The possibility of bacterial meningitis was also investigated, but it was deemed unlikely given the lack of neutrophilic predominance, the presence of only mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, and negative CSF cultures. Tuberculous meningitis was similarly excluded because acid-fast bacilli staining and PCR for tuberculosis DNA returned negative results.

Given the neurological symptoms, a primary CNS neoplasm was another differential diagnosis. Nevertheless, MRI of the brain did not reveal any abnormalities, and CSF cytology was negative for malignant cells. Although there was a slight increase in sIL-2R, this isolated finding did not substantiate a diagnosis of CNS lymphoma.

Potential autoimmune etiologies were also systematically assessed; however, the absence of typical systemic manifestations and negative autoantibody panels did not support this. Neurosarcoidosis was included in the differential since early-stage disease may not be evident on MRI, though subsequent laboratory assessment rendered this diagnosis improbable. The modestly raised sIL-2R was interpreted as a non-specific result.

A definitive diagnosis of Behçet's disease (BD) was reached based on the clinical constellation of fever, fatigue, oral aphthous ulcers (scoring two points), genital aphthosis (two points), and a folliculitis-like rash (one point), which summed to five points per the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (a score of four or more indicates definite disease). Notably, genital aphthosis is a characteristic finding in BD, lending additional support to this diagnosis. Following exclusion of infectious, malignant, and autoimmune processes, acute neuro-Behçet’s disease was diagnosed, reflecting CNS involvement in a patient with corresponding mucocutaneous features.

Treatment:

On the first day of hospitalization, clinicians suspected an underlying inflammatory disorder, though the specific cause remained undetermined. After obtaining blood cultures, empirical treatment with cefepime was started to cover for possible bacterial infection, even though there was no clear infectious source.

By the third day, the patient's headache and vertigo had intensified, raising concerns for central nervous system inflammation or infection. Empirical acyclovir therapy was commenced, but subsequently discontinued once diagnostic tests excluded HSV and VZV encephalitis.

On day eight, colchicine was prescribed after the patient was diagnosed with systemic BD and acute neuro-BD. Topical corticosteroid ointment was used to address oral aphthous ulcers. Genital and skin lesions were treated with silver sulfadiazine cream. SIADH was managed noninvasively through fluid restriction to less than 800 mL per day, daily serum sodium monitoring, and avoidance of rapid sodium correction; neither hypertonic saline nor vasopressin receptor antagonists were administered.

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient’s fever subsided and his appetite returned the day after initiating colchicine treatment. Over the subsequent two weeks, he experienced gradual improvement in vertigo, general malaise, hiccups, and mucocutaneous lesions. By the sixteenth day, his serum sodium concentration reached 136 mmol/L, and ADH levels dropped to 1.8 pg/mL. Levels of myogenic enzymes also normalized.

Of note, systemic corticosteroids and other immunosuppressive agents were not administered during therapy. After a one-month hospitalization—which included resolution of the acute phase, vigilant monitoring and management of hyponatraemia, and rehabilitation to address decreased physical capacity—the patient was discharged. Colchicine was continued for nearly four years, during which neither BD nor neurological symptoms re-emerged. Once BD was controlled, biguanide therapy was started and stable glycaemic control has since been maintained.

Discussion:

This report describes a rare instance of the concurrent diagnosis of systemic Behçet’s disease (BD) and acute neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD). It further illustrates that acute neuro-BD may be complicated by the development of the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). The resolution of the patient's mucocutaneous lesions, neurological symptoms, and hyponatraemia after administration of colchicine alone supports a potential direct link between BD, neuro-BD, and SIADH. Consequently, in patients with BD who present with fever or headache along with hyponatraemia, clinicians should be alert to the possibility of acute neuro-BD as an underlying etiology.

The simultaneous identification of systemic BD and acute neuro-BD in this patient is particularly significant, as evidenced by the presence of both mucocutaneous and central nervous system (CNS) manifestations. Usually, severe organ involvement such as CNS disease emerges a median of four years post-BD diagnosis, and presenting with neurological features is relatively uncommon—reported in under 5% of cases. Furthermore, only about 10% of individuals experience headaches directly attributed to neuro-BD, which rendered initial recognition in this case inherently difficult. Acute neuro-BD most often presents as a self-limited meningoencephalitis, with or without localized neurological deficits. In this situation, brain MRI did not demonstrate characteristic changes in typical BD-affected regions, such as the hypothalamus. Acute neuro-BD can manifest as aseptic meningitis in the absence of parenchymal lesions, and unremarkable brain imaging, especially early in the disease process, does not preclude the diagnosis. The absence of specific MRI findings introduced additional challenges to confirming the diagnosis. Although HLA-B51 typing was performed, it was not contributory to diagnostic determination, consistent with internationally recognized BD classification criteria, which are primarily based on clinical presentation rather than genetic testing. This case underscores the necessity of considering neuro-BD when patients with mucocutaneous signs develop vague neurological complaints.

At the outset of the differential diagnosis, we placed primary emphasis on infectious etiologies for CNS inflammation due to the concurrence of unexplained fever, neurological manifestations, and hyponatraemia; nevertheless, no specific infectious agent was identified. Alternative causes of CNS inflammation were also evaluated, including connective tissue disorders and neoplastic processes, with both sarcoidosis and haematologic malignancy considered among possible diagnoses. Consequently, as part of an initial comprehensive workup, we assessed autoimmune serological markers as well as measured serum ACE and sIL-2R for screening purposes. These investigations were conducted in a focused manner intended to rule out possibilities rather than driven by strong clinical indication. No supporting evidence for these conditions was observed clinically, radiologically, or in CSF analysis, and the results of these tests did not contribute significantly to establishing the diagnosis. Notably, ACE is known to have limited sensitivity for detecting sarcoidosis, and while sIL-2R can be useful in assessing malignant lymphoma, minor elevations lack specificity and may simply indicate underlying inflammation. In this patient, the lack of genital ulcers early in the disease course rendered Behçet’s disease a less clear diagnosis, necessitating a broad diagnostic approach. This case underscores the need to avoid nonspecific testing and illustrates the principle that such investigations should be guided by relevant clinical presentations and interpreted carefully.

While cases of drug-induced SIADH have been documented in BD patients taking agents such as cyclophosphamide, the link between acute neuro-BD and SIADH remains largely unclear. Neuro-BD is marked by inflammation within the central nervous system, which could potentially disrupt normal ADH regulation. Multiple studies have demonstrated that interleukin-6 (IL-6), a proinflammatory cytokine often elevated during neuroinflammatory conditions, is related to increased secretion of ADH. In BD, abnormal activation of monocytes prompts spontaneous IL-6 release, and heightened IL-6 concentrations have been found in the cerebrospinal fluid of individuals experiencing acute neuro-BD. These observations imply that IL-6–driven inflammation may play a role in the development of SIADH under these circumstances. Despite IL-6 levels not being assessed in serum or CSF for this particular case, the significantly raised serum CRP may indicate an IL-6–mediated inflammatory state. SIADH is associated with a broad range of etiologies, including CNS inflammation, pulmonary pathology, malignancies, and certain pharmacotherapies; however, the evidence presented here does not confirm a direct causal relationship between SIADH and acute neuro-BD. The chronological correlation seen in this case may represent a general response to CNS inflammation rather than a mechanism unique to neuro-BD.

This case demonstrates the possible complications arising from hyponatraemia induced by SIADH within the context of acute neuro-BD. Past literature has noted that ongoing hiccups may act as a significant clinical indicator of severe hyponatraemia associated with SIADH secondary to central nervous system inflammation, such as meningitis. As part of the initial workup for fever, arthralgia, and general fatigue—which can indicate inflammatory or metabolic injury to muscle—we evaluated CK levels, leading to the identification of rhabdomyolysis, an established but frequently neglected complication of profound hyponatraemia that is rarely systematically assessed. While these findings are documented, their clinical significance is often underrecognized. It is important for clinicians to recognize that symptoms which seem unrelated, like hiccups or rhabdomyolysis, may actually signify underlying disturbances in electrolytes, especially in patients with neuroinflammatory disorders.

Corticosteroids remain the primary therapeutic option for acute neuro-BD, with most individuals showing favorable responses. Nonetheless, cases presenting only with meningoencephalitis-like symptoms have demonstrated spontaneous improvement. In this particular instance, colchicine administered as monotherapy led to amelioration of both mucocutaneous and neurological manifestations, as well as resolution of hyponatraemia. The anti-inflammatory properties of colchicine are attributed to its ability to block neutrophil migration and activation, and to inhibit cytokine synthesis—including IL-1β and IL-6. Although colchicine is frequently employed to manage mucocutaneous symptoms in BD, systematic reviews indicate its lack of efficacy for acute neuro-BD, with immunosuppressants generally needed to achieve sufficient disease management. Existing reports illustrate that colchicine has typically been utilized in combination with corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive agents, rather than alone. While it is possible that spontaneous improvement occurred in this case, these observations imply that colchicine could be considered as a therapeutic option for acute neuro-BD and associated issues like SIADH, especially when corticosteroid administration is unwarranted or contraindicated.

Conclusion:

We reported an uncommon instance of Behçet’s disease (BD) with simultaneous onset of acute neuro-BD, complicated by the presence of syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). This case underscores that SIADH can develop secondary to acute neuro-BD, emphasizing the necessity for clinicians to recognize this potential relationship. Administration of colchicine alone resulted in prompt resolution of both mucocutaneous lesions and neurological symptoms, as well as correction of hyponatraemia, indicating that colchicine could be an effective treatment during the initial phase of neuro-BD. In individuals with BD exhibiting vague central nervous system complaints such as fever and headache together with hyponatraemia, physicians should have a strong index of suspicion for acute neuro-BD complicated by SIADH.
