Title: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

Eiichi Kakehi, Yukako Matsumoto, Shigehisa Sakurai, Kazuhiko Kotani

Abstract:

Summary: We describe an uncommon presentation of acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) simultaneously identified with systemic Behçet’s disease (BD), categorized as definite BD, and complicated by syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). A male patient in his 60s developed fever, headache, and mucocutaneous findings such as oral aphthae, folliculitis-like eruptions, genital ulcers, and hyponatraemia. The diagnosis of BD was established based on these mucocutaneous features. Magnetic resonance imaging of the brain did not reveal any lesions, whereas cerebrospinal fluid analysis indicated aseptic meningitis, which was compatible with acute neuro-BD. Laboratory assessments supported the presence of SIADH according to established diagnostic standards. Treatment with colchicine alone resulted in complete improvement of both mucocutaneous and neurological symptoms, as well as normalization of serum sodium, without requiring corticosteroids or additional immunosuppressive therapy. This report highlights a potential link between acute neuro-BD and SIADH, emphasizing the need to consider overlapping neurological and endocrine complications in individuals with recently diagnosed BD.

Background:

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a chronic, multisystem inflammatory condition defined by recurrent ulcers of the mouth and genitals, uveitis, and cutaneous lesions. Neurologic involvement, termed neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), develops in roughly 10%–20% of individuals, commonly manifesting several years post-initial BD diagnosis. The acute phase of neuro-BD is frequently characterised by meningoencephalitis and brain parenchymal lesions, with a predilection for the brainstem region. While delayed onset is more often observed, it is unusual for both BD and neuro-BD to be identified simultaneously. In such presentations, physicians should evaluate not only mucocutaneous findings but also neurological complaints, such as headache, behavioural alterations, psychiatric manifestations, and focal neurologic deficits.

The syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) represents a frequent etiology of hyponatraemia and is commonly linked to inflammatory processes within the central nervous system (CNS), neoplasms, or the use of specific medications. Owing to the inflammatory process associated with acute neuro-BD, these patients may have a predisposition for SIADH development. Although SIADH has been described in the context of BD patients undergoing treatment with agents such as cyclophosphamide, instances where SIADH is provoked exclusively by CNS inflammation in the course of acute neuro-BD—without pharmacological involvement—remain insufficiently defined.

Here, we present an uncommon case of simultaneous diagnoses of BD and acute neuro-BD, further complicated by SIADH. This report underscores the need to consider SIADH as a potential complication of acute neuro-BD, especially when evaluating patients with ambiguous CNS symptoms and hyponatraemia.

Case presentation:

A man of Asian descent in his 60s arrived at the emergency department after experiencing three days of fever, headache, general fatigue, anorexia, ongoing hiccups, and joint pain in his shoulders and ankles. He also noted the presence of oral aphthous ulcers.

His past medical history was notable for type 2 diabetes mellitus, though he was not undergoing therapy due to a lack of disease awareness and suboptimal adherence to recommended dietary and exercise regimens. There was no notable family history, with no relatives diagnosed with BD. He occasionally drank alcohol, did not smoke, and had not recently traveled, nor did he report animal contact or close exposure to ill persons. He was employed in the metalworking sector and had no recent history of sexual activity.

On assessment, the patient was awake but exhibited signs of fatigue. His vital parameters were as follows: temperature measured at 38.1°C, blood pressure recorded at 135/73 mm Hg, heart rate was 119 beats per minute with a regular rhythm, respiratory rate was 19 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation stood at 96% while breathing ambient air. Physical inspection identified aphthous lesions in the oral cavity and folliculitis-type eruptions present on both the face and trunk (refer to figure 1A, B, and C). Examination of the heart, lungs, and abdomen did not reveal any significant abnormalities. Manual muscle strength testing did not demonstrate any weakness in either the upper or lower limbs. No peripheral edema was detected, and clinical assessment indicated a euvolemic state. He was subsequently hospitalized for additional diagnostic workup.

Investigations:

Initial laboratory investigations demonstrated significant hyponatraemia (124 mmol/L), elevated levels of creatine kinase (1349 U/L), aspartate aminotransferase (77 U/L), alanine aminotransferase (64 U/L), a markedly increased C-reactive protein (CRP) concentration (180 mg/L; reference, <3 mg/L), and a raised erythrocyte sedimentation rate (70 mm/hour; reference, <15 mm/hour), with preserved renal function. Hyperglycaemia was observed (plasma glucose 14.2 mmol/L), alongside an HbA1c of 8.2%, reflective of suboptimal glycaemic control. Hepatitis B and C serologies were negative.

Endocrine assessment showed thyroid-stimulating hormone within normal limits (1.11 mIU/L; reference, 0.50–5.00 mIU/L), normal free T4 (1.25 ng/dL; reference, 0.90–1.70 ng/dL), and serum cortisol in a normal range (42 µg/dL; reference, >6 µg/dL). Serum osmolality was reduced (267 mOsm/kg; reference, <270 mOsm/kg), urine osmolality was elevated (900 mOsm/kg; reference, >300 mOsm/kg), and urinary sodium concentration was high (88 mmol/L; reference, >20 mmol/L), indicative of defective water clearance. In the setting of hypo-osmolar hyponatraemia, antidiuretic hormone (ADH) was increased (22.4 pg/mL). Electrocardiogram appeared normal, and imaging—including chest X-ray and CT scans of the head, chest, and abdomen—showed no abnormalities. Collectively, these results were consistent with the diagnosis of SIADH.

On the third day, the patient's headache and dizziness intensified. Despite the absence of neck rigidity or cranial nerve deficits, central nervous system inflammation was considered. Brain MRI demonstrated no significant findings in the hypothalamic region or other areas (see figure 2). Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis showed a slightly raised opening pressure (17 cmH₂O), elevated protein concentration (0.97 g/L), and mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL). Microbiological cultures and assays for bacterial and mycobacterial agents, along with herpes simplex virus (HSV), varicella zoster virus (VZV), and cryptococcal antigen, all yielded negative results. CSF cytology did not detect any malignant cells.

Additional tests were conducted to investigate possible systemic illnesses. Assessment for sarcoidosis revealed normal lysozyme levels (5.1 µg/mL; reference range 5–10) and angiotensin-converting enzyme values (11.4 U/L; reference range 7–25). Evaluation for hematologic malignancies included a measurement of soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) at 704 U/mL (reference range, 122–496 U/mL), and the human T-cell leukemia virus type 1 antibody test was negative.

On the fifth day, the patient developed a painful ulcer on the scrotum (see figure 1D). Together with oral aphthae and folliculitis-like skin lesions, these findings pointed to a systemic variant of BD. There was no evidence of hypersensitivity to pinprick. Eye examination did not show uveitis. The patient tested positive for HLA-B51.

Differential diagnosis:

Upon admission, the constellation of fever, headache, generalized malaise, and hyponatraemia raised suspicion for either central nervous system infection or a systemic inflammatory condition. Viral meningoencephalitis—most notably due to HSV or VZV—was initially suspected. Nevertheless, cerebrospinal fluid analysis yielded negative results for both viral antigens and antibodies, ruling out these etiologies. Consideration of bacterial meningitis was also dismissed following the absence of neutrophilic predominance, presence of only mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, and negative CSF cultures. Tuberculous meningitis was similarly excluded on the basis of negative acid-fast bacilli staining and tuberculosis DNA assays.

A primary CNS tumor was part of the differential due to neurological manifestations. Yet, MRI of the brain did not reveal abnormalities, and cytological examination of the CSF showed no evidence of malignant cells. The solitary mild increase in sIL-2R was not sufficient to support a diagnosis of CNS lymphoma.

Evaluation for autoimmune disorders was performed but these were excluded since characteristic systemic features were lacking and autoantibody screening returned negative. Neurosarcoidosis was also considered, given that normal MRI findings are not uncommon in early disease, but subsequent laboratory tests rendered this diagnosis improbable. The slightly increased sIL-2R concentration was interpreted as non-specific.

Ultimately, the patient fulfilled criteria for definite Behçet’s disease (BD) based on the presence of fever and fatigue, oral aphthae (two points), genital aphthosis (two points), and a rash resembling folliculitis (one point). Per the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease, the patient's cumulative score was five (≥4 is diagnostic). Notably, genital aphthosis is viewed as a specific feature of BD, supporting this diagnosis strongly. Having eliminated infectious, malignant, and autoimmune explanations, a final diagnosis of acute neuro-Behçet’s disease was made, characterized by CNS symptoms alongside mucocutaneous findings.

Treatment:

On the first day of hospitalization, clinicians considered a possible underlying inflammatory disorder, though its precise cause was not identified. After obtaining blood cultures, empiric antibiotic treatment with cefepime was commenced to address a possible bacterial process, despite the lack of a clear site of infection.

By the third day, the patient experienced intensified headache and vertigo, raising suspicion for central nervous system inflammation or infection. Empiric acyclovir was given; however, it was discontinued when HSV and VZV encephalitis were excluded through laboratory testing.

On day eight, colchicine therapy was initiated in response to the diagnosis of systemic Behçet’s disease (BD) and acute neuro-BD. Oral aphthae were addressed with topical corticosteroid ointment, while silver sulfadiazine cream was applied for the management of genital ulcers and skin lesions. The syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) was treated noninvasively by restricting fluid intake to under 800 mL per day, performing daily serum sodium assessments, and preventing rapid sodium correction. Administration of hypertonic saline or vasopressin receptor antagonists was deemed unnecessary.

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient's fever subsided, and his appetite returned the day following initiation of colchicine therapy. Over the next two weeks, there was progressive improvement in symptoms such as vertigo, generalized fatigue, hiccups, and mucocutaneous manifestations. By the 16th day, his serum sodium level had normalized to 136 mmol/L, with ADH concentrations decreasing to 1.8 pg/mL. Levels of myogenic enzymes also normalized.

Significantly, systemic corticosteroids and other immunosuppressive agents were not administered throughout his treatment course. After a hospitalization lasting one month, during which the acute episode resolved, hyponatremia was closely managed, and the patient received rehabilitation for physical deconditioning, he was discharged. Colchicine was maintained for about four years post-discharge, and during this time, there was no relapse of Behçet’s disease (BD) or neurological complaints. Once BD was controlled, biguanide therapy was started, resulting in effective glycemic control.

Discussion:

This report describes a case in which systemic Behçet’s disease (BD) and acute neuro-BD were both identified concurrently—an unusual clinical occurrence. The case further illustrates that SIADH can arise as a complication in the context of acute neuro-BD. Improvement of mucocutaneous lesions, neurological signs, and hyponatraemia solely with colchicine monotherapy suggests a potential direct link among BD, neuro-BD, and SIADH. Therefore, in BD patients who develop fever or headache together with hyponatraemia, clinicians should remain alert to the possibility of acute neuro-BD as the underlying etiology.

A distinguishing aspect of this case was the co-presentation of systemic BD and acute neuro-BD, evidenced by the presence of both mucocutaneous and central nervous system involvement. Ordinarily, serious organ involvement such as central nervous system disease arises about 4 years after initial BD diagnosis, and it is uncommon for neurological features to be present at onset, observed in less than 5% of BD patients. Additionally, only about 10% of individuals with BD will report headaches directly attributable to neuro-BD. Consequently, the early detection of neuro-BD in this setting was especially challenging. Acute neuro-BD often manifests as a transient form of meningoencephalitis and may include or exclude focal neurological symptoms. In this patient, brain MRI demonstrated no definitive abnormalities at customary BD-affected sites, including the hypothalamus. Acute neuro-BD may initially present as aseptic meningitis without evidence of parenchymal brain lesions, so a normal MRI in the early stages does not preclude the diagnosis. The absence of abnormal neuroimaging complicated diagnosis further. HLA-B51 analysis was conducted but did not aid in confirming BD, which is consistent with international diagnostic guidelines that prioritize clinical manifestations over HLA-B51 status. This case emphasizes the necessity of considering neuro-BD in patients with mucocutaneous manifestations who present with vague neurological complaints.

During the initial phase of our differential diagnosis, we gave precedence to infection as the underlying cause of CNS inflammation due to the coexistence of unexplained fever, neurological manifestations, and hyponatraemia, though no specific pathogen was identified. Other potential causes of CNS inflammation were also evaluated, including connective tissue disorders and malignancies, with sarcoidosis and haematologic cancer considered in our differential. As part of a comprehensive preliminary work-up, we ordered autoimmune serological tests, serum ACE, and sIL-2R as screening tools. These investigations were conducted selectively as a process of exclusion rather than on the basis of strong clinical suspicion. No clinical, radiological, or CSF evidence was found to support these possibilities, and the tests ultimately did not significantly aid in establishing the diagnosis. ACE has limited sensitivity for detecting sarcoidosis, and while sIL-2R may assist in assessing malignant lymphoma, modest increases are non-specific and may simply indicate general inflammation. In this patient, the lack of genital ulcers at symptom onset rendered BD diagnosis ambiguous, prompting consideration of a wide differential. This case illustrates the necessity to refrain from indiscriminate laboratory work-up and underscores that clinicians should pursue such evaluations only when warranted by relevant clinical signs and interpret the results judiciously.

While cases of drug-induced SIADH have been described in individuals with BD treated with agents such as cyclophosphamide, the connection between acute neuro-BD and SIADH is not well understood. Neuro-BD involves inflammation of the central nervous system, which may play a role in abnormal ADH secretion. Research indicates that interleukin-6 (IL-6), a proinflammatory cytokine commonly increased in neuroinflammatory diseases, is linked to enhanced ADH production. In Behçet disease, irregular activation of monocytes results in spontaneous IL-6 secretion, with elevated levels of IL-6 identified in the CSF of patients experiencing acute neuro-BD. This evidence implies that IL-6-driven inflammation could contribute to SIADH within this clinical context. Although IL-6 was not assessed in the serum or CSF for this patient, the substantially raised serum CRP may suggest a concurrent IL-6–associated inflammatory process. SIADH has been noted alongside a variety of conditions, including CNS inflammation, lung disorders, malignancies, and certain drugs; however, the data in this case cannot conclusively establish a causal relationship between SIADH and acute neuro-BD. The temporal proximity seen may indicate a general response to CNS inflammation rather than a mechanism unique to the disease.

This case highlights the possible complications associated with hyponatraemia resulting from SIADH during acute neuro-BD. Earlier publications have indicated that persistent hiccups can be a significant indicator of severe hyponatraemia due to SIADH linked to central nervous system inflammation, such as meningitis. Additionally, creatine kinase (CK) was assessed during the initial workup for fever, arthralgia, and general fatigue, symptoms that might signify inflammatory or metabolic myopathy, which led to the identification of rhabdomyolysis—a well-documented but frequently neglected consequence of profound hyponatraemia that is not typically screened for. Though these presentations are recognized, their association with severe hyponatraemia is often underestimated. It is important for clinicians to consider that seemingly unrelated clinical features like hiccups or rhabdomyolysis could be manifestations of underlying electrolyte imbalances, especially in patients with neuroinflammatory disorders.

Corticosteroids remain the primary therapy for acute neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), typically producing favorable responses in the majority of cases. Nonetheless, cases exhibiting only meningoencephalitis-like features have occasionally resolved spontaneously. In this instance, colchicine administered alone led to improvements in both mucocutaneous and neurological manifestations as well as hyponatraemia. Colchicine’s anti-inflammatory mechanism involves the inhibition of neutrophil migration and activation, along with the downregulation of cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-6. While colchicine is extensively employed to address mucocutaneous symptoms in BD, evidence from systematic reviews indicates its ineffectiveness in treating acute neuro-BD, with most patients necessitating immunosuppressive therapies to achieve sufficient disease control. Indeed, previous reports describe colchicine being used alongside corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants rather than as monotherapy. While the possibility of spontaneous remission cannot be dismissed in this case, these observations raise the possibility that colchicine could offer therapeutic benefit for acute neuro-BD and associated complications like SIADH, especially where corticosteroids are unsuitable or contraindicated.

Conclusion:

We report an uncommon instance where BD and acute neuro-BD were both identified at the same time, with SIADH serving as a complicating factor for neuro-BD. This case illustrates that SIADH can develop secondary to acute neuro-BD and underscores the necessity for clinicians to recognize this possible link. Treatment with colchicine alone resulted in swift resolution of mucocutaneous complaints, neurological symptoms, and hyponatraemia, indicating that colchicine might be an effective option during the initial stage of neuro-BD. For patients with BD who exhibit vague CNS signs such as fever and headache together with hyponatraemia, clinicians should strongly consider acute neuro-BD in BD cases complicated by SIADH.
