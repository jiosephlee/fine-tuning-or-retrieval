Title: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

Eiichi Kakehi, Yukako Matsumoto, Shigehisa Sakurai, Kazuhiko Kotani

Abstract:

Summary: We describe an uncommon presentation of acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) occurring at the same time as systemic Behçet’s disease (BD), meeting criteria for definite BD, and further complicated by the development of syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). A male patient in his sixties arrived with fever, headache, and mucocutaneous lesions that included oral aphthous ulcers, folliculitis-like eruptions, genital sores, and low sodium levels. BD was diagnosed on the basis of his mucocutaneous signs. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain appeared normal, while cerebrospinal fluid testing indicated aseptic meningitis, which supported a diagnosis of acute neuro-BD. Additional laboratory results satisfied criteria for SIADH. Treatment with colchicine alone resulted in remission of his skin and mucous membrane findings, neurological symptoms, and correction of hyponatraemia, without requiring corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive agents. This report highlights a potential link between acute neuro-BD and SIADH and emphasizes the need to identify simultaneous neurological and endocrine features in patients with a new diagnosis of BD.

Background:

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a persistent, multisystem inflammatory condition marked by recurrent ulcers in the mouth and genital area, as well as uveitis and dermatologic manifestations. Neurological complications, described as neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), affect around 10%–20% of individuals with BD, most often appearing years after the initial diagnosis. The acute phase of neuro-BD generally manifests as meningoencephalitis along with brain parenchymal lesions, most frequently involving the brainstem. Although it is more usual for neuro-BD to emerge after the initial BD diagnosis, simultaneous identification of both BD and neuro-BD is unusual. In such presentations, it is important for clinicians to evaluate for both cutaneous and mucosal symptoms as well as neurologic signs such as headaches, alterations in behavior, psychiatric disturbances, and localized neurologic deficits.

Syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) is a recognized contributor to hyponatraemia, often secondary to central nervous system (CNS) inflammation, neoplastic processes, or specific drugs. Due to the inflammatory pathology seen in acute neuro-BD, these individuals may be susceptible to SIADH. While SIADH has been reported among patients with BD undergoing therapy with agents like cyclophosphamide, its development solely in the setting of CNS inflammation from acute neuro-BD, without medication-induced effects, remains not well understood.

We present an unusual case featuring the simultaneous diagnosis of BD and acute neuro-BD, which was further complicated by the development of SIADH. This report underscores the necessity of recognizing SIADH as a possible complication of acute neuro-BD, especially in those presenting with ambiguous neurological symptoms and low serum sodium.

Case presentation:

A man of Asian descent in his 60s came to the emergency department after experiencing fever, headache, generalized malaise, decreased appetite, ongoing hiccups, and joint pain localized to the shoulders and ankles for three days. Additionally, he noted the presence of oral aphthae.

His past medical history was notable for type 2 diabetes mellitus, for which he was not on therapy due to low awareness of the condition and a lack of adherence to dietary or exercise recommendations. There was no notable family history, with no family members diagnosed with BD. He drank alcohol occasionally, did not smoke, and had not traveled recently or been in contact with animals or ill people. He was employed in the metalworking sector and had not had recent sexual contacts.

During evaluation, the patient was conscious but showed signs of tiredness. Vital parameters included a temperature of 38.1°C, blood pressure measuring 135/73 mm Hg, a regular pulse of 119 beats per minute, respiratory rate of 19 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation of 96% on ambient air. Examination identified oral aphthous ulcers and folliculitis-like eruptions distributed over the facial and truncal areas (figure 1A, B, and C). Assessments of the cardiovascular, pulmonary, and abdominal systems yielded normal results. Strength testing by manual muscle examination demonstrated intact muscle power in both upper and lower limbs. No peripheral edema was detected, and overall volume status was judged as euvolemic on clinical assessment. The patient was hospitalized to allow for additional diagnostic work-up.

Investigations:

Initial laboratory evaluation demonstrated significant hyponatremia (124 mmol/L), elevated creatine kinase (1349 U/L), increased aspartate aminotransferase (77 U/L) and alanine aminotransferase (64 U/L), as well as a raised C-reactive protein (CRP) at 180 mg/L (reference, <3 mg/L), and erythrocyte sedimentation rate at 70 mm/hour (reference, <15 mm/hour), with renal function remaining within normal limits. The patient exhibited hyperglycemia, with plasma glucose measured at 14.2 mmol/L and HbA1c at 8.2%, indicative of inadequate glycemic control. Screening for hepatitis B and C returned negative results.

Endocrine studies showed thyroid-stimulating hormone at 1.11 mIU/L (reference, 0.50–5.00 mIU/L), free T4 at 1.25 ng/dL (reference, 0.90–1.70 ng/dL), and a serum cortisol of 42 µg/dL (reference, >6 µg/dL), all within normal reference ranges. Serum osmolality was decreased at 267 mOsm/kg (reference, <270 mOsm/kg), whereas urine osmolality was markedly increased at 900 mOsm/kg (reference, >300 mOsm/kg), with urinary sodium concentration also elevated at 88 mmol/L (reference, >20 mmol/L), consistent with impaired water excretion. In the setting of hypo-osmolar hyponatremia, antidiuretic hormone (ADH) was found to be elevated at 22.4 pg/mL. Electrocardiogram (ECG), chest radiograph, and computed tomography imaging of the head, chest, and abdomen were all unremarkable. Collectively, these results were in keeping with a diagnosis of SIADH.

By the third day, the patient's headache and vertigo had intensified. Despite the absence of neck stiffness or cranial nerve deficits, central nervous system inflammation was considered. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain did not demonstrate notable abnormalities affecting the hypothalamus or other cerebral regions (see figure 2). Examination of the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) showed a mildly raised opening pressure (17 cmH₂O), increased protein concentration (0.97 g/L), and a slight lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL). Microbiological assessment of the CSF, including cultures and assays for bacterial, mycobacterial, herpes simplex virus (HSV), varicella zoster virus (VZV), and cryptococcal antigen, yielded negative results. CSF cytology was negative for malignant cells.

Additional diagnostic workup was undertaken to assess for possible systemic diseases. Testing for sarcoidosis indicated normal serum lysozyme (5.1 µg/mL; reference interval 5–10) and angiotensin-converting enzyme (11.4 U/L; reference range 7–25) values. Evaluation for hematological malignancies revealed an elevated soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) at 704 U/mL (reference, 122–496 U/mL), while testing for human T-cell leukemia virus type 1 antibody was negative.

By the fifth day, the patient developed a painful ulcerative lesion on the scrotum (see figure 1D). Along with the presence of oral aphthous ulcers and a folliculitis-like skin eruption, these features pointed toward a systemic type of Behçet's disease (BD). No hypersensitivity reaction was elicited with pinprick testing. Ophthalmologic assessment found no evidence of uveitis. HLA-B51 testing was positive.

Differential diagnosis:

At presentation, the constellation of fever, headache, malaise, and hyponatremia raised concern for either a central nervous system (CNS) infection or a systemic inflammatory condition. Viral meningoencephalitis, especially due to HSV or VZV, was the initial leading consideration. Nevertheless, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analyses were negative for both viral antigens and antibodies, thereby ruling out these etiologies. Bacterial meningitis was also entertained but dismissed given the lack of a neutrophilic predominance, presence of only a mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, and negative CSF cultures. Tuberculous meningitis was considered improbable due to negative acid-fast staining and TB DNA test results.

The possibility of a primary malignancy of the CNS was assessed in light of the neurological presentation. Yet, brain MRI yielded normal results, and no malignant cells were detected upon CSF cytological examination. Despite a mild increase in sIL-2R levels, this was not deemed adequate evidence to substantiate CNS lymphoma.

Evaluation for autoimmune conditions was undertaken, but ultimately excluded due to the absence of hallmark systemic manifestations and negative autoantibody screens. Neurosarcoidosis was part of the differential, particularly because early MRI may appear unremarkable, but subsequent laboratory findings reduced the likelihood of this diagnosis. The modestly increased sIL-2R concentration was considered a non-specific observation.

The final diagnosis was definite Behçet’s disease (BD), based on clinical criteria comprising fever, fatigue, two points for oral aphthae, two points for genital aphthosis, and one point for a folliculitis-like skin eruption. Using the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease, the total was five points (where a score of ≥4 establishes definite BD). Genital aphthosis, a distinguishing feature of BD, strongly supported the diagnosis. Following systematic exclusion of infectious, neoplastic, and autoimmune causes, acute neuro-Behçet’s disease was concluded, given CNS involvement alongside mucocutaneous symptoms.

Treatment:

Upon admission on day 1, clinicians considered an inflammatory disorder as a possible diagnosis, though its specific origin was not identified. Empirical cefepime was started after obtaining blood cultures, aiming to cover for a possible bacterial infection, even though a definitive infectious source was not established.

By day 3, the patient's headache and vertigo intensified, raising concerns for central nervous system involvement by either inflammation or infection. Empirical acyclovir treatment was initiated, but discontinued once diagnostic testing excluded HSV and VZV encephalitis.

On day 8, colchicine therapy commenced in response to newly confirmed systemic Behçet’s disease (BD) and acute neuro-BD. Oral aphthae were addressed with a topical corticosteroid ointment, while silver sulfadiazine cream was applied to genital and skin lesions. For SIADH, management included restricting fluid intake to less than 800 mL per day, vigilant daily assessment of serum sodium, and deliberate avoidance of rapid sodium correction. The use of hypertonic saline and vasopressin receptor antagonists was unnecessary.

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient's fever subsided, and his appetite returned the day following initiation of colchicine therapy. His symptoms—including vertigo, general malaise, hiccups, and mucocutaneous lesions—showed progressive improvement over the next two weeks. By the sixteenth day, his serum sodium normalized to 136 mmol/L, and ADH concentrations declined to 1.8 pg/mL. Myogenic enzyme values also normalized.

Of note, neither systemic corticosteroids nor other immunosuppressive agents were administered at any point during his treatment. After a one-month hospital admission—during which the acute phase was resolved, hyponatremia was closely observed and managed, and rehabilitation addressed his physical deconditioning—the patient was discharged. Colchicine therapy was maintained for nearly four years, with no relapses of BD or neurological manifestations during that interval. Subsequent to stabilization of his BD, biguanide treatment was started, resulting in sustained optimal glycemic control.

Discussion:

This report describes a rare clinical scenario where systemic BD and acute neuro-BD were diagnosed concurrently. It also illustrates that SIADH can complicate acute neuro-BD. The improvement of mucocutaneous signs, neurological symptoms, and hyponatraemia following colchicine monotherapy may indicate a direct link among BD, neuro-BD, and SIADH. In BD patients presenting with fever or headache alongside hyponatraemia, clinicians should be alert to the possibility of underlying acute neuro-BD.

The case is significant for the concurrent detection of systemic BD and acute neuro-BD, as evidenced by both mucocutaneous manifestations and CNS involvement. Ordinarily, major organ involvement such as CNS lesions arises approximately four years after initial BD diagnosis, while neurological symptoms at the onset are rare, occurring in less than 5% of cases. Direct headache attributable to neuro-BD is also infrequent, affecting around 10% of patients. Therefore, recognizing neuro-BD at the outset in this instance was notably difficult. Acute neuro-BD is most often characterized by a self-limited meningoencephalitis, which may be accompanied by focal neurological symptoms. In this patient, brain MRI did not demonstrate specific abnormalities in common BD lesion sites, such as the hypothalamic area. Acute neuro-BD can present as aseptic meningitis without evidence of parenchymal brain involvement, and a normal MRI scan, especially in the initial stages, does not rule out the diagnosis. The absence of radiological findings complicated the assessment further. While HLA-B51 testing was conducted, it did not assist in diagnosis, in accordance with international criteria for BD, which prioritize clinical features over HLA-B51 status. This case underscores the need to consider neuro-BD in patients displaying mucocutaneous features along with vague neurological complaints.

At the outset of the differential diagnosis, we gave precedence to infectious etiologies for CNS inflammation, as the patient exhibited fever of unknown origin, neurological manifestations, and hyponatraemia; despite this, no identifiable pathogen was detected. Additional considerations in the differential included other potential sources of CNS inflammation, such as connective tissue disorders, malignancies, sarcoidosis, and hematologic cancers. As part of a comprehensive initial assessment, we evaluated autoimmune serological markers, as well as serum ACE and soluble IL-2 receptor (sIL-2R) levels, utilizing these tests as part of a broad exclusionary strategy rather than in response to specific clinical indicators. No clinical, imaging, or CSF evidence was found to support these alternate diagnoses, and the ancillary testing did not significantly assist in determining the ultimate diagnosis. Serum ACE demonstrates suboptimal sensitivity for sarcoidosis, and while sIL-2R can assist in assessing malignant lymphoma, minor elevations are non-specific and may merely indicate general inflammation. In this patient's case, the lack of genital ulcers early in the clinical course complicated the diagnosis of BD, necessitating a wider differential. This case underscores the necessity to refrain from excessive indiscriminate testing and stresses that such diagnostic measures should be guided by relevant clinical presentations and interpreted judiciously.

While drug-induced SIADH has been observed in individuals with BD treated with agents like cyclophosphamide, the relationship between acute neuro-BD and SIADH remains insufficiently defined. Neuro-BD involves inflammation of the central nervous system, which may play a role in abnormal ADH secretion. Evidence from multiple studies indicates that interleukin-6 (IL-6), a proinflammatory cytokine commonly increased in neuroinflammatory diseases, correlates with augmented ADH release. In BD, dysregulated activation of monocytes results in spontaneous IL-6 secretion, with raised IL-6 concentrations found in the CSF of patients experiencing acute neuro-BD episodes. These data imply that IL-6–driven inflammation could contribute to the development of SIADH in this clinical context. Although IL-6 was not assayed in either serum or CSF in the present patient, a significantly increased serum CRP may indicate the presence of an IL-6–driven inflammatory process. SIADH has been linked to several disorders, such as CNS inflammation, respiratory illnesses, malignancies, and use of specific drugs; however, the current evidence does not confirm a direct cause-and-effect relationship between SIADH and acute neuro-BD. The observed chronological connection in this case may indicate a general reaction to CNS inflammation rather than a mechanism specific to this disease.

This case further demonstrates the possible complications arising from hyponatraemia due to SIADH associated with acute neuro-BD. Past case reports have indicated that persistent hiccups may be an important clinical indicator of severe hyponatraemia resulting from SIADH secondary to central nervous system inflammation, such as meningitis. Additionally, during the initial assessment for fever, arthralgia, and general fatigue, creatine kinase (CK) was measured to evaluate potential inflammatory or metabolic muscle injury, which uncovered rhabdomyolysis—a well-documented but frequently missed consequence of severe hyponatraemia that is not commonly assessed. These findings highlight recognized yet frequently underemphasized clinical consequences of significant hyponatraemia. Healthcare providers should recognize that seemingly unrelated symptoms, including hiccups and rhabdomyolysis, may signal underlying disturbances in electrolyte balance, especially within the context of neuroinflammatory disorders.

Corticosteroids are typically regarded as the primary therapy for acute neuro-BD, with the majority of patients demonstrating a positive response. Nonetheless, there have been accounts of self-limiting disease in individuals whose presentations were limited to meningoencephalitis-like features. In this report, use of colchicine alone led to improvement in mucocutaneous and neurological manifestations as well as resolution of hyponatraemia. Colchicine’s anti-inflammatory action results from suppression of neutrophil migration and activation, along with inhibition of cytokine production such as IL-1β and IL-6. Although colchicine is commonly administered for the mucocutaneous symptoms of BD, evidence from systematic reviews suggests it is not effective as sole therapy for acute neuro-BD, and that control of the disease typically necessitates additional immunosuppressive agents. Indeed, published reports indicate colchicine is generally used in conjunction with corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants rather than on its own. While spontaneous improvement cannot be completely ruled out in this instance, this experience raises the possibility that colchicine could represent a viable therapeutic alternative for acute neuro-BD and complications such as SIADH, especially in circumstances where corticosteroids are contraindicated or not recommended.

Conclusion:

We reported an unusual presentation of BD diagnosed simultaneously with acute neuro-BD, in which SIADH developed as a complication of the neurological involvement. This case underscores that SIADH can arise secondary to acute neuro-BD, emphasizing the need for clinicians to recognize this possible association. Administration of colchicine alone resulted in swift resolution of mucocutaneous findings, neurological deficits, and hyponatremia, indicating that colchicine monotherapy may be an effective early treatment strategy for neuro-BD. When patients with BD develop non-specific central nervous system symptoms, including fever and headache, alongside hyponatremia, physicians should consider acute neuro-BD, particularly in the context of coexisting SIADH.
