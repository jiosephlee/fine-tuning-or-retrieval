Title: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

Eiichi Kakehi, Yukako Matsumoto, Shigehisa Sakurai, Kazuhiko Kotani

Abstract:

Summary: We describe an uncommon presentation of acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) that was identified at the same time as systemic Behçet’s disease (BD), meeting criteria for definite BD, and was further complicated by syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). A male patient in his 60s developed fever, headache, and mucocutaneous findings such as oral aphthous ulcers, folliculitis-like eruptions, genital ulcers, and low serum sodium. The diagnosis of BD was made based on the cutaneous and mucosal features. Magnetic resonance imaging of the brain was unremarkable, while cerebrospinal fluid assessment demonstrated features of aseptic meningitis, supporting the diagnosis of acute neuro-BD. Laboratory evaluation fulfilled the criteria for SIADH. Treatment with colchicine alone resulted in remission of cutaneous, neurological, and biochemical abnormalities, obviating the need for corticosteroids or additional immunosuppressive therapy. This case highlights a potential relationship between acute neuro-BD and SIADH and emphasizes the need to consider overlapping neurological and endocrine symptoms in newly diagnosed BD patients.

Background:

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a persistent inflammatory disorder that affects multiple organ systems and is identified by the presence of recurrent oral and genital ulcers, uveitis, and dermatologic lesions. Neurological manifestations, termed neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), are seen in roughly 10%–20% of individuals and often appear several years following the initial BD diagnosis. Acute neuro-BD frequently manifests as meningoencephalitis with parenchymal lesions, most often impacting the brainstem region. While a delayed presentation is typical, simultaneous diagnoses of BD and neuro-BD are unusual. In such presentations, it is important for clinicians to evaluate both mucocutaneous signs and neurological symptoms such as headache, behavioral disturbances, psychiatric complaints, and localized neurological deficits.

Syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) is a recognized etiology of hyponatraemia, frequently linked to central nervous system (CNS) inflammation, neoplasms, or the use of certain medications. Due to the inflammatory processes seen in acute neuro-BD, affected individuals may have an increased susceptibility to SIADH. Although cases of SIADH have been described in BD patients undergoing therapies like cyclophosphamide, the incidence of SIADH due exclusively to CNS inflammation from acute neuro-BD, absent pharmacologic causes, is not well documented.

We describe an unusual instance in which both BD and acute neuro-BD were identified at the same time, with the additional complication of SIADH. This case underscores the need to consider SIADH as a possible complication of acute neuro-BD, especially among patients presenting with vague CNS findings and low serum sodium.

Case presentation:

A man of Asian descent in his 60s arrived at the emergency department reporting a 3-day duration of fever, headache, generalized weakness, decreased appetite, continuous hiccups, and joint pain in the shoulders and ankles. He additionally mentioned experiencing oral aphthous ulcers.

His past medical history was notable for type 2 diabetes mellitus, which remained untreated due to his limited understanding of the condition and lack of adherence to recommended lifestyle modifications. There was no significant family history, including any relatives with BD. Social history revealed occasional alcohol intake, no tobacco use, absence of recent travel, and no contact with animals or individuals who were ill. His occupation involved work in the metalworking field, and he had not engaged in sexual activity recently.

On assessment, the patient was conscious but showed signs of tiredness. Recorded vital signs included a body temperature of 38.1°C, blood pressure measured at 135/73 mm Hg, a regular pulse of 119 beats per minute, respiratory rate at 19 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation at 96% while breathing ambient air. Physical examination demonstrated oral aphthous ulcers and rash resembling folliculitis on the face and trunk (see figure 1A, B, and C). Cardiopulmonary and abdominal examinations yielded no significant abnormalities. Manual testing did not reveal muscle weakness in either upper or lower limbs. There was no evidence of peripheral edema, and clinical evaluation suggested a euvolemic fluid status. The patient was admitted for additional diagnostic workup.

Investigations:

Initial laboratory evaluation demonstrated significant hyponatremia (124 mmol/L) and raised levels of creatine kinase (1349 U/L), aspartate aminotransferase (77 U/L), alanine aminotransferase (64 U/L), C-reactive protein (180 mg/L; reference range <3 mg/L), and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (70 mm/hour; reference range <15 mm/hour), while renal function was within normal limits. The patient was also found to have hyperglycemia (plasma glucose 14.2 mmol/L) and an elevated HbA1c of 8.2%, suggestive of suboptimal glycaemic regulation. Tests for hepatitis B and C serology were negative.

Endocrine assessment indicated thyroid-stimulating hormone within the reference range (1.11 mIU/L; reference 0.50–5.00 mIU/L), normal free T4 (1.25 ng/dL; reference 0.90–1.70 ng/dL), and serum cortisol (42 µg/dL; reference >6 µg/dL). The patient's serum osmolality was low (267 mOsm/kg; reference <270 mOsm/kg), while urine osmolality was elevated (900 mOsm/kg; reference >300 mOsm/kg), with urinary sodium also increased (88 mmol/L; reference >20 mmol/L), indicating a deficit in water excretion. Antidiuretic hormone (ADH) was increased (22.4 pg/mL) despite the presence of hypo-osmolar hyponatremia. Electrocardiogram was unremarkable, and imaging with chest X-ray and computed tomography scans of the head, chest, and abdomen were all normal. Collectively, these results were indicative of syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH).

By the third day, the patient's headache and vertigo became more severe. Despite the absence of neck rigidity or signs involving cranial nerves, inflammation of the central nervous system was considered. MRI of the brain did not demonstrate notable abnormalities affecting the hypothalamus or other regions (figure 2). Analysis of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) showed a slightly elevated opening pressure at 17 cmH₂O, protein level of 0.97 g/L, and a mild lymphocytic pleocytosis at 11 cells/µL. Microbiological evaluation of CSF, including cultures and diagnostic tests for bacteria, mycobacteria, herpes simplex virus (HSV), varicella zoster virus (VZV), and cryptococcal antigen, were all negative. No malignant cells were detected on CSF cytology.

Additional workup aimed at identifying systemic disorders was conducted. Testing for sarcoidosis demonstrated lysozyme within the normal range (5.1 µg/mL; reference 5–10) and angiotensin-converting enzyme levels also within reference (11.4 U/L; reference 7–25). A workup for hematologic malignancies showed elevated soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) at 704 U/mL (reference, 122–496 U/mL), and human T-cell leukemia virus type 1 antibody testing was negative.

By day 5, the patient developed a painful ulcer on the scrotum (figure 1D). Together with the presence of oral aphthae and folliculitis-like skin eruptions, this raised suspicion for systemic Behçet's disease (BD). He did not display a pathergy reaction to a pinprick. Eye examination did not identify uveitis. HLA-B51 testing was positive.

Differential diagnosis:

On admission, the presence of fever, headache, generalized malaise, and hyponatremia pointed toward either a central nervous system (CNS) infection or a systemic inflammatory condition. An initial suspicion of viral meningoencephalitis—particularly due to herpes simplex virus (HSV) or varicella zoster virus (VZV)—was raised, but analysis of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) revealed no viral antigens or antibodies, ruling out these etiologies. Consideration was also given to bacterial meningitis; however, the diagnosis was excluded given the lack of neutrophilic dominance in the CSF, the presence of only mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, and negative CSF cultures. Tests for acid-fast bacilli and tuberculosis DNA were negative, excluding tuberculous meningitis.

A primary CNS malignancy was also contemplated due to the neurological manifestations, yet brain MRI showed no abnormalities, and cytological examination of the CSF did not detect malignant cells. Although soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) was slightly elevated, this alone was insufficient for diagnosing CNS lymphoma.

Autoimmune conditions were systematically investigated, but these were discounted owing to both a lack of characteristic systemic manifestations and negative screening for autoantibodies. Neurosarcoidosis remained in the differential, since early MRI findings may be unremarkable; however, follow-up laboratory results rendered this diagnosis unlikely. The minor elevation of sIL-2R was considered a non-specific observation.

This case met the criteria for a definitive diagnosis of Behçet’s disease (BD) based on clinical presentation, including fever and malaise, the presence of oral aphthae (two points), genital aphthosis (two points), and a folliculitis-like eruption (one point). Per the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease, this yielded a total of five points (a score of four or higher confirms the diagnosis). Notably, genital aphthosis is recognized as a specific marker for BD, and this combination of findings strongly supports BD. Once infectious, malignant, and autoimmune causes were excluded, acute neuro-BD was diagnosed, characterized by CNS involvement alongside mucocutaneous symptoms.

Treatment:

On the first day after admission, clinicians considered the possibility of an underlying inflammatory disorder, though the specific cause was uncertain. Cefepime was started empirically for presumed bacterial infection following the collection of blood cultures, despite no clear infectious source being identified.

By the third day, the patient experienced increasing headache and vertigo, raising concern for central nervous system involvement from either inflammation or infection. Empirical acyclovir therapy was given, but was later stopped when diagnostic tests excluded herpes simplex virus and varicella-zoster virus encephalitis.

On the eighth day, colchicine therapy was begun after systemic Behçet's disease (BD) with acute neurological involvement (neuro-BD) was diagnosed. Topical corticosteroid ointment was prescribed for oral aphthous ulcers. Silver sulfadiazine cream was used to treat genital and cutaneous ulcers. Syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) was addressed conservatively with fluid intake restricted to less than 800 mL per day, close daily monitoring of serum sodium, and measures to avoid rapid sodium correction. There was no need to administer hypertonic saline or vasopressin receptor antagonists.

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient’s fever subsided and his appetite returned the day after initiating colchicine therapy. Over the subsequent two weeks, there was a gradual resolution of his symptoms, including vertigo, general malaise, hiccups, and mucocutaneous lesions. By day 16, serum sodium had corrected to 136 mmol/L, and ADH concentrations declined to 1.8 pg/mL. Levels of myogenic enzymes also normalized.

It is noteworthy that systemic corticosteroids and other immunosuppressive agents were not employed during his treatment course. The patient was discharged after one month of hospitalization, during which acute phase recovery occurred, hyponatremia was closely observed and treated, and rehabilitative support addressed his diminished physical function. Colchicine was maintained for roughly four years without any recurrence of Behçet’s disease or neurologic symptoms during that period. Once his Behçet’s disease was stabilized, biguanide therapy was started, resulting in effective ongoing glycemic control.

Discussion:

This report describes a case where both systemic BD and acute neuro-BD were identified concurrently, a situation infrequently encountered in clinical practice. Furthermore, the patient developed SIADH as a complication of acute neuro-BD. The improvement of mucocutaneous complaints, neurological symptoms, and hyponatraemia after treatment with colchicine alone raises the possibility of a direct link between BD, neuro-BD, and SIADH. Thus, for BD patients presenting with fever or headache together with hyponatraemia, acute neuro-BD should be considered in the differential diagnosis.

The present case is distinguished by the concurrent diagnosis of systemic BD and acute neuro-BD, as evidenced by both mucocutaneous involvement and central nervous system pathology. Major organ manifestations such as CNS lesions are usually observed roughly 4 years after the first diagnosis of BD, and neurological features at presentation are rare, occurring in under 5% of cases. Headaches attributable to neuro-BD are also seen in only about 10% of individuals. Consequently, making the initial diagnosis of neuro-BD in this instance posed a considerable challenge. Acute neuro-BD is most frequently characterized by a transient meningoencephalitic episode, sometimes accompanied by focal neurological impairment. In this patient, brain MRI failed to demonstrate abnormalities in the usual BD target sites, including the hypothalamus. It is recognized that acute neuro-BD can present as aseptic meningitis without overt parenchymal involvement, and a normal MRI in early disease does not exclude neuro-BD, adding further complexity to the diagnostic workup. Although HLA-B51 was evaluated, the result did not aid in diagnosis, consistent with international BD diagnostic standards, which prioritize clinical signs and do not mandate HLA-B51 positivity. This experience underscores the necessity of suspecting neuro-BD in cases where individuals exhibit mucocutaneous features alongside vague neurological symptoms.

At the outset of our differential diagnosis, infectious causes of CNS inflammation were considered most likely due to the presentation of unexplained fever, neurological deficits, and hyponatraemia; nonetheless, no definite infectious agent was detected. Additional considerations included other etiologies of CNS inflammation, such as connective tissue disorders and malignancy, with sarcoidosis and hematologic malignancy specifically included in our differential. Accordingly, our initial comprehensive work-up comprised screening tests for autoantibodies, and assessments of serum ACE and sIL-2R levels. These evaluations were carried out more to rule out possibilities than based on definitive clinical evidence. Ultimately, neither clinical presentation, imaging, nor CSF analysis supported these diagnoses, and the screening investigations did not facilitate reaching the eventual diagnosis. It is important to note that serum ACE is an insensitive marker for sarcoidosis, and while sIL-2R may aid in identifying malignant lymphoma, modest elevations lack specificity and can indicate general inflammation. In this situation, the lack of genital ulcers early in the disease course complicated the diagnosis of BD, necessitating consideration of a wider spectrum of conditions. This case illustrates the risk of widespread, non-targeted testing and reinforces the need for clinicians to base diagnostic investigations on clear clinical indications and to interpret such results judiciously.

While SIADH triggered by medications has been documented in BD patients treated with agents such as cyclophosphamide, the link between acute neuro-BD and SIADH is not well defined. Neuro-BD is marked by inflammation within the central nervous system, which may play a role in abnormal antidiuretic hormone secretion. Multiple reports have demonstrated that interleukin-6 (IL-6), a proinflammatory cytokine commonly elevated in neuroinflammatory conditions, is linked to greater ADH production. In Behçet’s disease, dysregulated monocyte activation results in spontaneous IL-6 synthesis, and high concentrations of IL-6 have been identified in the cerebrospinal fluid of those experiencing acute neuro-BD episodes. Such observations imply a possible role of IL-6–driven inflammation in the development of SIADH in this context. In the current case, although neither serum nor CSF IL-6 was quantified, a significantly raised serum C-reactive protein (CRP) level might suggest an underlying inflammatory process related to IL-6. SIADH can arise in association with numerous underlying factors, including CNS inflammatory states, malignancy, lung disorders, and specific pharmacotherapies; therefore, the current data do not permit confirmation of a direct causative association between SIADH and acute neuro-BD. The observed timing in this report could indicate a general reaction to central nervous system inflammation, as opposed to a pathophysiology uniquely attributable to neuro-BD.

This case further demonstrates the possible complications associated with hyponatraemia resulting from SIADH in acute neuro-BD. Prior literature has indicated that persistent hiccups can be a valuable clinical indicator of severe hyponatraemia due to SIADH linked to central nervous system inflammation, including meningitis. During the initial assessment for fever, arthralgia, and general fatigue, creatine kinase (CK) levels were obtained to evaluate potential inflammatory or metabolic muscle injury; this led to the identification of rhabdomyolysis, a recognized but frequently neglected complication of severe hyponatraemia that is not routinely assessed. Such findings represent well-documented, though often underestimated, clinical effects of profound hyponatraemia. Clinicians should maintain vigilance for seemingly unrelated symptoms, such as hiccups or rhabdomyolysis, which may be indicative of underlying electrolyte imbalances, especially in the presence of neuroinflammatory disease.

Corticosteroids are typically the first-line therapy for acute neuro-BD, with most individuals demonstrating a positive response. Nonetheless, there are documented instances where meningoencephalitis-like presentations have subsided spontaneously without intervention. In this case, administration of colchicine alone led to improvement in both mucocutaneous and neurologic symptoms, as well as correction of hyponatraemia. The anti-inflammatory properties of colchicine are attributed to its ability to inhibit neutrophil activation and migration, and to reduce the production of cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-6. While colchicine is commonly employed to treat mucocutaneous manifestations in BD, evidence from systematic reviews indicates its ineffectiveness for managing acute neuro-BD, with immunosuppressive therapy often necessary for sufficient control. Reported cases typically describe colchicine used as an adjunct to corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive drugs, not as a stand-alone treatment. Although it is possible that spontaneous improvement occurred in this instance, the observed outcomes raise the possibility that colchicine could serve as a therapeutic alternative in acute neuro-BD and associated complications like SIADH, particularly in situations where corticosteroids are inappropriate or contraindicated.

Conclusion:

We reported an uncommon instance of Behçet's disease (BD) occurring simultaneously with acute neuro-Behçet's disease (neuro-BD), in which the neuro-BD was further complicated by syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). This case underscores that SIADH can present as a complication during acute neuro-BD, emphasizing the need for clinicians to recognize this possible relationship. Administration of colchicine alone resulted in a prompt resolution of mucocutaneous lesions, neurological symptoms, and hyponatraemia, indicating that colchicine may serve as an effective initial treatment for neuro-BD. For individuals with BD who develop vague central nervous system complaints—such as fever and headache—together with hyponatraemia, physicians should strongly consider the likelihood of acute neuro-BD coexisting with SIADH.
