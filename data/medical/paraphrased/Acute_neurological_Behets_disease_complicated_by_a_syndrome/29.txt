Title: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

Eiichi Kakehi, Yukako Matsumoto, Shigehisa Sakurai, Kazuhiko Kotani

Abstract:

Summary: We describe an uncommon presentation of acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) identified simultaneously with definite systemic Behçet’s disease (BD), further complicated by the development of syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). A male in his 60s was admitted with fever, headache, and mucocutaneous symptoms such as oral aphthous ulcers, folliculitis-like skin eruptions, genital ulceration, and hyponatraemia. The mucocutaneous findings were instrumental in diagnosing BD. Although brain MRI demonstrated no pathology, cerebrospinal fluid analysis revealed aseptic meningitis, consistent with acute neuro-BD. Additional laboratory evaluation established the criteria for SIADH. Treatment with colchicine alone successfully resolved the patient’s mucocutaneous lesions, neurological symptoms, and hyponatraemia, obviating the requirement for corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive agents. This case highlights a potential correlation between acute neuro-BD and SIADH and emphasizes the need for heightened awareness of simultaneous neurological and endocrine manifestations in those newly diagnosed with BD.

Background:

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a persistent, multisystemic inflammatory condition marked by recurrent oral and genital ulcers, uveitis, and cutaneous manifestations. Involvement of the nervous system—termed neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD)—is observed in roughly 10%–20% of cases, most often arising several years post-BD diagnosis. The acute form of neuro-BD is typically characterized by meningoencephalitis and parenchymal lesions, with a predilection for the brainstem. While delayed neurological presentation is more usual, simultaneous identification of both BD and neuro-BD is infrequent. Under these circumstances, clinicians must evaluate for both mucocutaneous findings and neurologic manifestations such as headaches, changes in behavior, psychiatric disturbances, and localized neurological deficits.

Syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) represents a common etiology for hyponatraemia and is frequently associated with CNS inflammation, malignancies, or specific pharmacologic agents. Since acute neuro-BD has an inflammatory CNS involvement, patients may be susceptible to developing SIADH. Previous reports have linked SIADH with BD, particularly in patients treated with drugs like cyclophosphamide; however, SIADH occurring solely as a result of acute neuro-BD-related CNS inflammation, independent of medication use, has not been clearly documented.

Here, we describe a unique case featuring the simultaneous diagnosis of BD and acute neuro-BD, complicated by SIADH. This case underscores the need to recognize SIADH as a possible complication of acute neuro-BD, especially in individuals exhibiting ambiguous CNS symptoms alongside hyponatraemia.

Case presentation:

A man of Asian descent in his 60s arrived at the emergency department with a 3-day duration of fever, headache, generalized weakness, decreased appetite, ongoing hiccups, and joint pain localized to his shoulders and ankles. He additionally noted the presence of oral aphthous ulcers.

His past medical history was notable for type 2 diabetes mellitus, which was untreated due to his limited understanding of the disease and his failure to adhere to recommended dietary or exercise regimens. There was no relevant family history of BD among his relatives. He drank alcohol on occasion, denied tobacco use, had not traveled recently, nor had he come into contact with animals or individuals who were ill. He was employed in the metalworking sector and reported no recent sexual activity.

During the assessment, the patient was awake but showed signs of tiredness. Recorded vital signs included a temperature of 38.1°C, blood pressure measured at 135/73 mm Hg, a heart rate of 119 beats per minute with a regular rhythm, a respiratory rate of 19 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation at 96% while breathing ambient air. On physical examination, oral aphthae were observed, along with folliculitis-like eruptions on both the facial area and torso (see figure 1A, B, and C). There were no significant findings on cardiopulmonary and abdominal exams. Manual testing of muscle strength detected no weakness in either the upper or lower limbs. Peripheral oedema was absent, and clinical evaluation indicated a euvolemic fluid status. The patient was subsequently hospitalized for additional investigation.

Investigations:

Initial laboratory evaluations showed significant hyponatraemia (124 mmol/L), raised creatine kinase (1349 U/L), aspartate aminotransferase (77 U/L), alanine aminotransferase (64 U/L), C-reactive protein (CRP) at 180 mg/L (reference <3 mg/L), and erythrocyte sedimentation rate of 70 mm/hour (reference <15 mm/hour), with renal function remaining within normal limits. Hyperglycaemia was identified (plasma glucose 14.2 mmol/L) alongside an elevated HbA1c of 8.2%, reflecting suboptimal glucose control. Hepatitis B and C serological markers returned negative.

Endocrinological assessment demonstrated thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) at 1.11 mIU/L (reference range 0.50–5.00 mIU/L), free T4 at 1.25 ng/dL (reference range 0.90–1.70 ng/dL), and serum cortisol measured at 42 µg/dL (reference >6 µg/dL), all within standard limits. Serum osmolality was reduced (267 mOsm/kg; reference <270 mOsm/kg), while urine osmolality was elevated (900 mOsm/kg, reference >300 mOsm/kg) with high urinary sodium (88 mmol/L; reference >20 mmol/L), indicating compromised water clearance. Although the patient had hypo-osmolar hyponatraemia, antidiuretic hormone (ADH) level was elevated (22.4 pg/mL). The ECG was unremarkable, and imaging studies including chest X-ray and CT scans of the head, chest, and abdomen did not reveal significant findings. Collectively, these results were indicative of SIADH.

On the third day, his headache and vertigo intensified. Although there was an absence of neck rigidity or cranial nerve deficits, inflammation within the central nervous system was considered. Brain MRI showed no notable abnormalities involving the hypothalamus or other brain regions (figure 2). Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) assessment demonstrated a slightly increased opening pressure (17 cmH₂O), elevated protein concentration (0.97 g/L), and mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL). Results from CSF cultures and assays for bacterial and mycobacterial pathogens, along with herpes simplex virus (HSV), varicella zoster virus (VZV), and cryptococcal antigen testing, were all negative. Cytology of the CSF did not reveal any malignant cells.

Additional examinations were performed to investigate potential systemic conditions. Testing related to sarcoidosis showed normal lysozyme (5.1 µg/mL; reference range 5–10) and angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) concentrations (11.4 U/L; reference range 7–25). Evaluation for haematological malignancy indicated soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) levels at 704 U/mL (reference 122–496 U/mL), with a negative result for human T-cell leukaemia virus type 1 antibody.

On the fifth day, he developed a painful ulcer on his scrotum (figure 1D). When considered alongside oral aphthae and rashes resembling folliculitis, these manifestations were indicative of a systemic presentation of BD. He did not demonstrate hypersensitivity following a pinprick. Eye examination showed no evidence of uveitis. HLA-B51 was found to be positive.

Differential diagnosis:

Upon admission, the presence of fever, headache, general malaise, and hyponatraemia raised suspicion for either a central nervous system infection or a systemic inflammatory process. Initially, viral meningoencephalitis due to HSV or VZV was considered; nonetheless, cerebrospinal fluid assays were negative for both viral antigens and antibodies, thus ruling out these etiologies. The possibility of bacterial meningitis was entertained but dismissed based on the lack of neutrophilic dominance, only slight lymphocytic elevation, and negative CSF cultures. Tuberculous meningitis was also excluded, given negative acid-fast staining and tuberculosis DNA test results.

A primary CNS tumor was contemplated due to the neurological symptoms present; yet, no abnormalities were observed on brain MRI, and CSF cytology was clear of malignant cells. Although sIL-2R was found to be modestly increased, this alone was not sufficient to diagnose CNS lymphoma.

Consideration was given to autoimmune disorders; however, these were ruled out due to the absence of specific systemic clinical manifestations and negative results on autoantibody screenings. Neurosarcoidosis was included among possible diagnoses, since initial MRI findings may be normal, but subsequent laboratory evidence was inconsistent with this diagnosis. The slightly elevated sIL-2R was interpreted as non-specific.

A definitive diagnosis of Behçet’s Disease (BD) was made based on clinical signs: fever and fatigue, oral aphthae (scoring two points), genital aphthosis (two points), and a folliculitis-like eruption (one point). Under the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease, the patient accumulated five points (≥4 meets the threshold for definitive diagnosis). Notably, genital aphthosis is recognized as a specific marker for BD, further supporting this diagnosis. After systematically excluding infectious, neoplastic, and autoimmune pathologies, acute neuro-BD was diagnosed, marked by CNS involvement alongside mucocutaneous features.

Treatment:

On the first day of hospitalization, a possible inflammatory disorder was considered, though the precise cause was not determined. Broad-spectrum antibiotics, specifically cefepime, were started empirically after obtaining blood cultures, with the aim of covering a bacterial infection despite no evident source.

By the third day, worsening headache and dizziness raised suspicion for central nervous system infection or inflammation. Acyclovir was given as an empirical antiviral treatment, but was stopped once laboratory evaluations excluded herpes simplex virus and varicella zoster virus encephalitis.

On the eighth day, colchicine was initiated after systemic Behçet's disease (BD) and acute neuro-Behçet's disease (neuro-BD) were diagnosed. Oral aphthae were managed using topical corticosteroid ointment, while genital and skin ulcers were treated with silver sulfadiazine cream. Syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) was addressed conservatively with fluid restriction to less than 800 mL per day, vigilant daily monitoring of serum sodium, and precautions to avoid rapid correction of sodium levels. There was no need for hypertonic saline or vasopressin receptor antagonists.

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient's fever subsided and his appetite increased the day following initiation of colchicine therapy. Over the next two weeks, his clinical manifestations—including vertigo, malaise, hiccups, and mucocutaneous lesions—showed progressive improvement. By day 16, his serum sodium level had returned to 136 mmol/L, and antidiuretic hormone (ADH) concentrations declined to 1.8 pg/mL. Myogenic enzyme values normalized as well.

Notably, no systemic corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive agents were administered during treatment. After a hospital stay of one month, during which he recovered from the acute illness, hyponatraemia was closely monitored and addressed, and rehabilitative support was provided to counteract physical deterioration, the patient was discharged. Colchicine was continued for roughly four years, during which no recurrence of Behçet's disease (BD) or neurological issues was observed. Once BD was stable, biguanide therapy began, resulting in satisfactory glycaemic management.

Discussion:

This report describes a case where both systemic Behçet's disease (BD) and acute neuro-Behçet's disease (neuro-BD) were identified at the same time, a clinical occurrence that is infrequently reported. It also illustrates that acute neuro-BD may be accompanied by SIADH. The resolution of cutaneous and oral lesions, neurological abnormalities, and hyponatraemia after initiation of colchicine monotherapy implies a direct connection among BD, neuro-BD, and SIADH. When individuals with BD develop symptoms such as fever or headache in conjunction with hyponatraemia, clinicians should remain alert to the possibility of underlying acute neuro-BD.

This case is significant as it features a concurrent diagnosis of systemic BD and acute neuro-BD, which was supported by the presence of mucocutaneous manifestations and central nervous system involvement. Ordinarily, major organ involvement, particularly CNS abnormalities, tends to occur about four years following the initial detection of BD, and it is uncommon for neurological symptoms to be observed at presentation, with less than 5% of patients exhibiting these findings. Only a small subset—approximately 10%—of cases have headaches that are directly attributable to neuro-BD. Consequently, identifying neuro-BD at disease onset in this patient was notably difficult. The acute form of neuro-BD most often appears as a transient meningoencephalitis, which may or may not be associated with localized neurological deficits. In this instance, MRI of the brain failed to reveal notable lesions in customary areas of BD involvement, including the hypothalamus. Acute neuro-BD can manifest as aseptic meningitis without clear parenchymal changes, and the absence of MRI abnormalities, especially early in the disease, does not rule out the diagnosis. This absence of characteristic imaging findings further hindered diagnostic certainty. Although HLA-B51 analysis was undertaken, the result was not instrumental in making the diagnosis, reflecting international criteria for BD, which focus on clinical evaluation and do not mandate HLA-B51 positivity. This report underscores the necessity of considering neuro-BD in patients with mucocutaneous features who develop vague neurological complaints.

At the outset of our differential diagnosis, infectious etiologies were prioritized as a potential source of central nervous system (CNS) inflammation due to the presence of unexplained fever, neurologic deficits, and hyponatremia; despite this, no causative pathogen was identified. Additional considerations included other sources of CNS inflammation, such as connective tissue disorders and neoplastic processes, with sarcoidosis and hematologic malignancies incorporated into the list of potential diagnoses. Consequently, we conducted a comprehensive screening by assessing autoimmune serological markers as well as serum angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) and soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) levels, not out of strong clinical suspicion but rather as part of a strategy aimed at excluding alternative possibilities. No supporting evidence for these diagnoses was found based on clinical presentation, imaging studies, or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis, and these particular tests had minimal impact on reaching the final diagnosis. It is important to note that ACE demonstrates suboptimal sensitivity for detecting sarcoidosis, and while sIL-2R may provide valuable information in assessing for malignant lymphoma, modest increases lack specificity and may simply indicate inflammatory activity. In this patient’s case, the initial absence of genital ulcers rendered the diagnosis of Behçet's disease (BD) less apparent, necessitating a broader differential. This approach underscores the critical need to avoid unnecessary investigations, emphasizing that clinicians should rely on pertinent clinical indicators when ordering such tests and interpret the findings prudently.

While SIADH induced by drugs has been documented in individuals with BD who are administered agents such as cyclophosphamide, the link between acute neuro-BD and SIADH has not been clearly elucidated. Neuro-BD involves inflammation of the central nervous system, which might play a role in disturbed ADH regulation. Research indicates that interleukin-6 (IL-6), a proinflammatory cytokine commonly heightened in neuroinflammatory conditions, can promote increased ADH secretion. In BD, abnormal activation of monocytes results in spontaneous IL-6 synthesis, and raised IL-6 concentrations have been identified in the CSF of patients experiencing acute neuro-BD episodes. These observations imply that inflammation driven by IL-6 may play a role in the development of SIADH in this clinical context. Although IL-6 was not evaluated in either the serum or CSF in this instance, a substantially elevated serum CRP could signify an underlying IL-6–mediated inflammatory response. SIADH can be related to numerous conditions such as CNS inflammation, respiratory diseases, cancers, and certain drugs, but the data presented here are insufficient to confirm a direct causal relationship between SIADH and acute neuro-BD. The timing of events in this patient may indicate a general response to CNS inflammation, rather than a specific disease mechanism.

This case underscores the possible complications associated with hyponatraemia due to SIADH in acute neuro-BD. Prior accounts have indicated that persistent hiccups can provide an important clinical indicator of severe hyponatraemia arising from SIADH linked to central nervous system inflammation, such as meningitis. Furthermore, creatine kinase was measured during the initial assessment for fever, arthralgia, and fatigue—symptoms that may suggest either inflammatory or metabolic muscle injury—and this testing disclosed rhabdomyolysis, an established yet frequently neglected consequence of severe hyponatraemia that is not routinely checked for. Although these clinical findings are recognized, they tend to be underestimated in practice. Healthcare professionals should remain cognizant that atypical symptoms, like hiccups or rhabdomyolysis, might signify an underlying electrolyte imbalance, especially within the setting of neuroinflammatory disease.

Corticosteroids are widely regarded as the primary therapy for acute neuro-BD, with most patients demonstrating a positive response. Nevertheless, spontaneous improvement has been noted in cases that exhibit only meningoencephalitis-like features. In this instance, treatment with colchicine alone led to improvement in both mucocutaneous and neurological manifestations, as well as resolution of hyponatraemia. Colchicine has anti-inflammatory properties, achieved through inhibition of neutrophil movement and activity, and by reducing cytokine production including IL-1β and IL-6. While colchicine is commonly administered for the management of mucocutaneous symptoms in BD, evidence from systematic reviews indicates its lack of efficacy in acute neuro-BD, which typically necessitates the use of immunosuppressive medications for sufficient disease management. In previously documented cases, colchicine has generally been combined with corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive agents rather than being used as monotherapy. Although the possibility of spontaneous remission cannot be eliminated in this patient, our observations imply that colchicine could serve as a viable therapeutic alternative for acute neuro-BD and associated complications such as SIADH, especially in situations where corticosteroids are contraindicated or undesirable.

Conclusion:

We reported an uncommon instance where Behçet’s disease (BD) and acute neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) were simultaneously diagnosed, with the development of SIADH further complicating the neuro-BD. This case illustrates that SIADH can manifest as a complication during acute neuro-BD episodes, underscoring the necessity for clinicians to recognize this possible correlation. Administration of colchicine alone resulted in prompt resolution of cutaneous and mucosal lesions, neurological symptoms, and hyponatraemia, indicating that colchicine may serve as an effective initial treatment option for early neuro-BD. Clinicians should remain alert to the possibility of acute neuro-BD associated with SIADH when BD patients display vague central nervous system symptoms such as fever and headache together with hyponatraemia.
