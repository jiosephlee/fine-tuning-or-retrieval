Acute neurological Behçet’s disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

Eiichi Kakehi, Yukako Matsumoto, Shigehisa Sakurai, Kazuhiko Kotani

Abstract:

Summary: This report details an uncommon instance of acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) identified simultaneously with systemic Behçet’s disease (BD), meeting the criteria for definite BD and presenting alongside syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). A male patient in his 60s exhibited fever, headache, and a range of mucocutaneous symptoms, such as oral aphthae, folliculitis-like skin eruptions, genital ulcers, and hyponatraemia. The diagnosis of BD was established based on these mucocutaneous features. Despite normal findings on brain MRI, analysis of cerebrospinal fluid indicated aseptic meningitis, which corroborated the diagnosis of acute neuro-BD. Laboratory evaluation substantiated the diagnosis of SIADH. Treatment with colchicine alone resulted in improvement of the mucocutaneous lesions, neurological symptoms, and hyponatraemia, without resorting to corticosteroids or additional immunosuppressive therapies. This case highlights a potential link between acute neuro-BD and SIADH, emphasizing the need for clinicians to be vigilant for simultaneous neurological and endocrine involvement when evaluating patients with recent BD diagnoses.

Background:

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a persistent, systemic inflammatory condition marked by recurring oral and genital ulcers, ocular inflammation (uveitis), and cutaneous manifestations. Neurological complications, classified as neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), arise in around 10%–20% of BD cases, most commonly manifesting years following the primary diagnosis. Acute presentations of neuro-BD are often characterized by meningoencephalitis alongside parenchymal lesions of the brain, particularly affecting the brainstem region. Concurrent diagnosis of BD and neuro-BD is infrequent, as neurological symptoms usually appear later in the disease course. When both are identified simultaneously, it is crucial for clinicians to evaluate both mucocutaneous signs and neurological indicators, such as headaches, behavioral or psychiatric disturbances, and localized neurological deficits.

The syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) constitutes a common etiology of hyponatraemia and frequently results from inflammation of the central nervous system (CNS), malignancies, or specific pharmacological agents. Since acute neuro-BD is associated with significant inflammation, patients are potentially susceptible to developing SIADH. Although there have been descriptions of SIADH in BD patients undergoing therapy with agents like cyclophosphamide, the incidence of SIADH directly attributable to CNS inflammation from acute neuro-BD, in the absence of exogenous pharmacologic triggers, remains insufficiently clarified.

In this report, we describe an unusual instance where BD and acute neuro-BD were simultaneously diagnosed, with the clinical course further complicated by SIADH. This case underscores the necessity of recognizing SIADH as a possible complication in patients with acute neuro-BD, especially when presenting with vague CNS symptoms coupled with hyponatraemia.

Case presentation:

A man of Asian descent in his 60s arrived at the emergency department after experiencing three days of fever, headache, overall fatigue, diminished appetite, continuous hiccups, and joint pain in his shoulders and ankles. He further described having developed oral aphthous ulcers.

The patient’s medical background was notable for type 2 diabetes mellitus, which remained untreated due to his insufficient awareness of the diagnosis and lack of adherence to recommended dietary and physical activity guidelines. His family medical history was not significant for BD, and there were no affected relatives. Socially, he reported infrequent alcohol consumption, no tobacco use, absence of recent travel, and no known contact with animals or individuals who were ill. His occupation was in metalworking, and he denied recent sexual encounters.

Upon assessment, the patient was conscious but displayed signs of tiredness. His recorded vital parameters included a temperature of 38.1°C, blood pressure at 135/73 mm Hg, heart rate measuring 119 beats per minute with a regular rhythm, respiratory rate at 19 breaths per minute, and an oxygen saturation of 96% while breathing ambient air. Physical inspection showed the presence of oral aphthae and rashes resembling folliculitis on both his face and torso (refer to figure 1A, B, and C). Examinations of the cardiovascular, pulmonary, and abdominal systems did not reveal any abnormalities. Manual muscle strength evaluation demonstrated normal muscle function in both upper and lower limbs. No peripheral edema was detected, and clinical judgment indicated a euvolemic state. The patient was subsequently hospitalized for additional diagnostic work-up.

Investigations:

The initial laboratory assessment demonstrated significant hyponatraemia (124 mmol/L), along with raised levels of creatine kinase (1349 U/L), aspartate aminotransferase (77 U/L), alanine aminotransferase (64 U/L), C-reactive protein (CRP) at 180 mg/L (reference, <3 mg/L), and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (70 mm/hour; reference, <15 mm/hour). Renal function tests remained within normal limits. The patient exhibited hyperglycaemia (plasma glucose 14.2 mmol/L) and a high HbA1c of 8.2%, reflecting suboptimal glycaemic management. Screening for hepatitis B and C returned negative.

Further endocrine workup showed that thyroid-stimulating hormone was 1.11 mIU/L (reference, 0.50–5.00 mIU/L), free T4 was 1.25 ng/dL (reference, 0.90–1.70 ng/dL), and serum cortisol was 42 µg/dL (reference, >6 µg/dL), all within the expected ranges. The serum osmolality was decreased (267 mOsm/kg; reference, <270 mOsm/kg), while urine osmolality was notably elevated (900 mOsm/kg; reference, >300 mOsm/kg), and urinary sodium was increased (88 mmol/L; reference, >20 mmol/L), indicative of a defect in water excretion. In the context of hypo-osmolar hyponatraemia, antidiuretic hormone (ADH) was found to be elevated (22.4 pg/mL). Electrocardiogram results were normal, and imaging studies including chest X-ray and CT scans of the head, chest, and abdomen were unremarkable. Collectively, these results were consistent with a diagnosis of SIADH.

By the third day, his symptoms of headache and vertigo had intensified. Despite the absence of neck rigidity or involvement of cranial nerves, central nervous system (CNS) inflammation remained a concern. MRI scans of the brain did not indicate any significant lesions in the hypothalamus or other brain structures (figure 2). Examination of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) demonstrated a slightly raised opening pressure (17 cmH₂O), elevated protein concentration (0.97 g/L), and a modest lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL). Microbiological analysis, including cultures for bacteria and mycobacteria, as well as PCR or antigen tests for herpes simplex virus (HSV), varicella zoster virus (VZV), and cryptococcus, were all negative. CSF cytology did not detect any malignant cells.

Additional diagnostic workup was undertaken to assess for possible systemic disorders. Evaluation for sarcoidosis included measurements of lysozyme (5.1 µg/mL; reference 5–10) and angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) (11.4 U/L; reference 7–25), both within normal reference intervals. Assessment for hematologic malignancies revealed an elevated soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) level (704 U/mL; reference 122–496 U/mL), while testing for human T-cell leukaemia virus type 1 antibodies was negative.

By day 5, the patient developed a painful ulcerative lesion on the scrotum (figure 1D). In conjunction with oral aphthous ulcers and eruptions resembling folliculitis, these clinical features pointed toward a systemic manifestation of BD. No pinprick hypersensitivity was observed. Ophthalmologic assessment found no evidence of uveitis. HLA-B51 testing returned positive.

Differential diagnosis:

Upon admission, the presence of fever, headache, malaise, and hyponatraemia raised concerns about a potential CNS infection or systemic inflammatory process. The initial diagnostic impression leaned toward viral meningoencephalitis, with HSV and VZV regarded as the primary pathogens. Nevertheless, CSF analyses for viral antigens and antibodies were negative, effectively dismissing this etiology. Bacterial meningitis was also contemplated but excluded due to a lack of neutrophilic dominance, only modest lymphocytic pleocytosis, and negative CSF cultures. Tuberculous meningitis was discounted in light of negative results for acid-fast staining and tuberculosis DNA testing.

Consideration was also given to primary CNS tumors, prompted by the neurological manifestations. Yet, brain MRI demonstrated no abnormalities and CSF cytological examination identified no malignant cells. Although sIL-2R was found to be slightly increased, this single observation was inadequate to diagnose CNS lymphoma.

Potential autoimmune etiologies were assessed but rejected in the absence of typical systemic symptoms and negative results from screening for autoantibodies. Neurosarcoidosis remained a theoretical possibility, as normal MRI imaging is sometimes seen in the disease’s early phases; however, subsequent laboratory findings did not support this diagnosis. The mild elevation in sIL-2R was ultimately viewed as a nonspecific laboratory finding.

A definitive diagnosis of BD was rendered, supported by clinical manifestations including fever, fatigue, oral aphthae (two points), genital aphthosis (two points), and a folliculitis-like eruption (one point). According to the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease, a total of five points was tallied (with a threshold of four points required for definite diagnosis). Genital aphthosis, in particular, is regarded as highly specific for BD, which lent strong credence to the diagnosis. After systematically ruling out infectious, neoplastic, and autoimmune origins, acute neuro-BD was diagnosed in light of CNS involvement coupled with characteristic mucocutaneous symptoms.

Treatment:

Upon admission (day 1), clinicians suspected the presence of an inflammatory disorder, though the precise underlying cause could not be identified. Broad-spectrum antimicrobial treatment with cefepime was commenced following collection of blood cultures, aiming to address a possible bacterial pathogen despite no clear source of infection being apparent.

By day 3, the patient’s symptoms of headache and vertigo intensified, raising concerns for central nervous system inflammation or infection. As a precaution, acyclovir was initiated but later withdrawn once testing excluded both HSV and VZV encephalitis.

On day 8, initiation of colchicine therapy occurred after the diagnoses of systemic BD and acute neuro-BD were confirmed. Topical corticosteroid ointment was used to manage oral aphthous ulcers. Treatment of genital and cutaneous lesions was accomplished with silver sulfadiazine cream. For SIADH, conservative management consisted of restricting fluid intake to less than 800 mL/day, with close daily monitoring of serum sodium levels to prevent rapid correction. Neither hypertonic saline nor vasopressin receptor antagonists were necessary during this period.

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient’s fever subsided, and his appetite returned the day following the initiation of colchicine therapy. Over the subsequent two weeks, his clinical manifestations—including vertigo, persistent fatigue, hiccups, and mucocutaneous lesions—showed progressive improvement. By the 16th day, his serum sodium had stabilized at 136 mmol/L, and antidiuretic hormone (ADH) levels decreased to 1.8 pg/mL. Additionally, his myogenic enzyme values normalized.

Notably, the treatment course did not involve systemic corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive medications. The patient was discharged after a hospitalization lasting one month, during which acute symptoms were resolved, hyponatraemia was closely managed, and rehabilitative interventions addressed physical deconditioning. Colchicine was maintained as ongoing therapy for around four years, during which time neither BD nor neurological issues reappeared. Once BD reached a stable state, biguanide medication was introduced, resulting in effective long-term glycaemic management.

Discussion:

This case report details a rare scenario in which systemic BD and acute neuro-BD were diagnosed concurrently. Furthermore, it illustrates that acute neuro-BD may be associated with SIADH as a complication. The complete resolution of mucocutaneous lesions, neurological symptoms, and hyponatraemia following colchicine monotherapy points toward a potential direct link between BD, neuro-BD, and SIADH. Consequently, in individuals with BD who exhibit fever or headache in conjunction with hyponatraemia, clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for acute neuro-BD as an underlying etiology.

The uniqueness of this case lies in the parallel identification of both systemic BD and acute neuro-BD, evidenced by both mucocutaneous involvement and central nervous system symptoms. Conventionally, major organ manifestations such as CNS pathology tend to emerge approximately four years after BD diagnosis, and neurological features at onset are infrequent, occurring in less than 5% of cases. Only a small fraction, around 10%, of BD patients develop headaches attributable directly to neuro-BD. Therefore, the initial diagnosis of neuro-BD in this instance was particularly challenging. Acute neuro-BD most frequently manifests as a transient meningoencephalitis that may or may not be associated with focal neurological deficits. In this patient, brain MRI failed to show characteristic BD-related lesions, including within the hypothalamic area. It is important to note that acute neuro-BD can present as aseptic meningitis in the absence of brain parenchymal lesions, and a normal brain MRI in the early phase does not rule out the condition. The absence of specific imaging findings further complicated the diagnosis. Although HLA-B51 analysis was conducted, it did not facilitate the diagnostic process. This outcome is consistent with international diagnostic criteria for BD, which prioritize clinical presentation over HLA-B51 status. This case underscores the necessity of considering neuro-BD in the differential diagnosis when patients with BD present with mucocutaneous findings accompanied by non-specific neurological complaints.

During the initial stage of differential diagnosis, we gave precedence to infectious etiologies for CNS inflammation due to the presence of an unexplained fever, neurological manifestations, and hyponatraemia; nonetheless, no definitive pathogen was identified. Alternative causes of CNS inflammation, such as connective tissue disorders and neoplastic processes, were also evaluated, with particular attention to sarcoidosis and hematologic malignancies. Consequently, a wide-ranging initial workup included testing for autoimmune serological markers, as well as serum ACE and sIL-2R, which were employed as preliminary screening tools. These investigations were conducted selectively and served as part of an exclusionary strategy rather than as a response to compelling clinical suspicion. Ultimately, neither clinical presentation, imaging, nor cerebrospinal fluid analysis supported these alternative diagnoses, and these ancillary investigations did not meaningfully aid in establishing the underlying cause. It is noteworthy that ACE levels are of low sensitivity for sarcoidosis, and while sIL-2R measurements may be beneficial in assessing malignant lymphoma, slight elevations are frequently nonspecific and may simply indicate a general inflammatory state. In this scenario, the lack of genital ulcers in the early symptomatic phase rendered a diagnosis of BD less apparent, thereby necessitating consideration of an extensive differential diagnosis. This approach to diagnosis illustrates the pitfalls of nonselective testing and underscores the principle that such diagnostic measures should be undertaken judiciously—only when warranted by supportive clinical evidence—and interpreted within the appropriate clinical context.

While drug-induced SIADH has previously been observed in individuals with BD treated with agents such as cyclophosphamide, the connection between acute neuro-BD and the development of SIADH has yet to be fully elucidated. Neuro-BD is marked by central nervous system inflammation, a process that may underlie abnormal ADH regulation. A number of investigations have demonstrated that interleukin-6 (IL-6)—a proinflammatory cytokine often elevated in neuroinflammatory diseases—is implicated in enhanced ADH secretion. In the context of BD, aberrant activation of monocytes results in unregulated IL-6 production, with increased concentrations of IL-6 documented in the CSF of those experiencing acute neuro-BD. This evidence raises the possibility that IL-6–driven inflammation plays a role in the onset of SIADH in such circumstances. Although IL-6 was not specifically assessed in the serum or CSF for the current patient, the notably high serum CRP level could serve as an indicator of an IL-6–mediated inflammatory reaction. SIADH is linked to multiple etiologies, such as CNS inflammation, lung diseases, cancer, and the use of specific drugs; thus, the data presented here cannot definitively establish a direct causative relationship between SIADH and acute neuro-BD. Instead, the close timing of these events in this case may indicate a generalized physiological response to CNS inflammation rather than a process unique to the underlying disease.

This case further demonstrates the possible complications arising from hyponatraemia due to SIADH occurring in the context of acute neuro-BD. Prior studies have indicated that persistent hiccups can be a significant clinical indicator of severe hyponatraemia resulting from SIADH associated with central nervous system inflammation, including cases of meningitis. Furthermore, as part of the initial assessment for symptoms such as fever, arthralgia, and general fatigue—which could be indicative of inflammatory or metabolic muscle injury—we assessed CK levels, which led to the identification of rhabdomyolysis. This condition is a recognised, though frequently underdiagnosed, consequence of severe hyponatraemia that is not routinely screened for. Although these clinical features are established manifestations of significant hyponatraemia, they are commonly under-recognised. It is important for clinicians to consider that symptoms such as hiccups or rhabdomyolysis, which may appear unrelated, could signal underlying electrolyte imbalances, especially when present with neuroinflammatory diseases.

Corticosteroids are widely regarded as the cornerstone therapy for acute neuro-BD, with the majority of individuals showing a positive therapeutic response. Nevertheless, there have been descriptions of spontaneous remission in patients whose clinical manifestations are limited to meningoencephalitis-like presentations. In this particular case, administration of colchicine as a single agent led to improvement in both mucocutaneous and neurological features, in addition to correcting hyponatraemia. The mechanism of colchicine involves anti-inflammatory action via the inhibition of neutrophil movement and activation, as well as downregulation of cytokine release, notably IL-1β and IL-6. While colchicine is commonly employed for addressing mucocutaneous lesions in BD, evidence from systematic reviews indicates its lack of efficacy in acute neuro-BD, for which immunosuppressive medications are typically considered necessary to achieve optimal disease control. Historically, colchicine has served as an adjunctive agent alongside corticosteroids or alternative immunosuppressants rather than as a standalone treatment. Though spontaneous improvement in this case cannot be conclusively ruled out, the results observed here imply that colchicine could represent a potential therapeutic option for acute neuro-BD and associated conditions like SIADH, especially in scenarios where the use of corticosteroids is inadvisable or contraindicated.

Conclusion:

We reported an uncommon instance of concurrent BD and acute neuro-BD, in which the patient developed SIADH as a complication of neuro-BD. This observation indicates that SIADH can arise in the context of acute neuro-BD, underscoring the need for clinicians to recognize this possible relationship. Administration of colchicine as a single agent resulted in prompt resolution of mucocutaneous manifestations, neurological symptoms, and hyponatraemia, implying that colchicine could serve as an effective initial treatment option for neuro-BD. For BD patients who exhibit vague central nervous system symptoms—including fever and headache—alongside hyponatraemia, practitioners should remain vigilant for acute neuro-BD in cases where SIADH is also present.
