Title: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

Eiichi Kakehi, Yukako Matsumoto, Shigehisa Sakurai, Kazuhiko Kotani

Abstract:

Summary: We present an unusual case involving acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), diagnosed simultaneously with systemic Behçet’s disease (BD) categorized as definite BD, further complicated by syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). A male patient in his 60s presented with fever, headache, and mucocutaneous features including oral aphthae, folliculitis-like skin eruptions, genital ulceration, and hyponatraemia. BD was diagnosed based on the mucocutaneous signs. Brain MRI was unremarkable, while cerebrospinal fluid analysis indicated aseptic meningitis, supporting acute neuro-BD. Laboratory testing verified SIADH according to its diagnostic criteria. The patient’s mucocutaneous and neurological symptoms as well as hyponatraemia resolved following colchicine monotherapy, without recourse to corticosteroids or additional immunosuppressive therapy. This case highlights a potential connection between acute neuro-BD and SIADH, emphasizing the necessity to identify simultaneous neurological and endocrine involvement in individuals newly diagnosed with BD.

Background:

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a persistent, multisystem inflammatory condition distinguished by recurring oral and genital ulcerations, uveitis, and cutaneous manifestations. Involvement of the nervous system, termed neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), is seen in about 10%–20% of individuals with BD and usually arises years after BD has initially been identified. Acute neuro-BD most often manifests as meningoencephalitis with parenchymal lesions in the brain, frequently affecting the brainstem region. While neuro-BD characteristically develops after a latency period, simultaneous diagnosis of both BD and neuro-BD is uncommon. In these presentations, healthcare providers must evaluate mucocutaneous findings alongside neurological manifestations such as headache, alterations in behavior, psychiatric disturbances, and focal neurological impairments.

The syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) is a recognized etiology of hyponatraemia and is often attributed to central nervous system (CNS) inflammation, neoplasia, or specific pharmacologic agents. Due to the inflammatory component inherent to acute neuro-BD, there is a potential for patients to develop SIADH. Though SIADH has been documented among BD cases treated with agents like cyclophosphamide, its presence purely due to CNS inflammatory processes in acute neuro-BD without medication-induced triggers remains poorly defined.

Here, we describe an uncommon occurrence in which a patient received concurrent diagnoses of BD and acute neuro-BD, complicated by the development of SIADH. This report underlines the need to recognize SIADH as a possible complication of acute neuro-BD, particularly in those who present with general CNS complaints and low serum sodium levels.

Case presentation:

A man of Asian descent in his sixties arrived at the emergency department following three days of fever, headache, pronounced fatigue, diminished appetite, ongoing hiccups, and joint pain involving his shoulders and ankles. He also described developing oral aphthous ulcers.

His past medical history was notable for type 2 diabetes mellitus, which remained untreated due to his poor understanding of the disease and lack of adherence to recommended dietary and physical activity guidelines. There was no significant family history, including no relatives with BD. He reported occasional alcohol intake, denied tobacco use, had not traveled recently, nor had contact with animals or individuals who were ill. Employed in metalworking, he also stated no recent sexual encounters.

Upon evaluation, the patient was conscious though noticeably tired. His vital signs included a temperature of 38.1°C, blood pressure measuring 135/73 mm Hg, heart rate at 119 beats per minute with regular rhythm, respiratory rate of 19 breaths per minute, and an oxygen saturation of 96% while breathing ambient air. Physical assessment identified the presence of oral aphthous ulcers as well as folliculitis-like eruptions distributed over the face and trunk (figure 1A, B, and C). No abnormalities were detected during cardiovascular, pulmonary, or abdominal examinations. Strength testing of both upper and lower limbs showed no muscle weakness. There was no evidence of peripheral edema, and his clinical volume status was deemed euvolemic. He was subsequently hospitalized for additional workup.

Investigations:

The initial laboratory assessment demonstrated significant hyponatraemia at 124 mmol/L, accompanied by increased creatine kinase (1349 U/L), aspartate aminotransferase (77 U/L), alanine aminotransferase (64 U/L), CRP (180 mg/L; reference, <3 mg/L), and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (70 mm/hour; reference, <15 mm/hour), with renal function remaining within normal limits. The patient was also found to have hyperglycaemia, with a plasma glucose level of 14.2 mmol/L, and a raised HbA1c at 8.2%, indicative of suboptimal glucose regulation. Negative results were obtained for hepatitis B and C markers.

Evaluation of endocrine parameters showed thyroid-stimulating hormone at 1.11 mIU/L (reference, 0.50–5.00 mIU/L), free T4 at 1.25 ng/dL (reference, 0.90–1.70 ng/dL), and serum cortisol at 42 µg/dL (reference, >6 µg/dL), all within normal ranges. Serum osmolality was reduced (267 mOsm/kg; reference, <270 mOsm/kg), whereas urine osmolality was notably high (900 mOsm/kg; reference, >300 mOsm/kg), and urine sodium was elevated (88 mmol/L; reference, >20 mmol/L), suggesting deficient water clearance. Although the patient presented with hypo-osmolar hyponatraemia, antidiuretic hormone (ADH) was found to be increased (22.4 pg/mL). Electrocardiogram was unremarkable, and imaging including chest X-ray, and CT scans of the head, chest, and abdomen did not reveal abnormalities. Collectively, these findings were consistent with SIADH.

On the third day, the patient’s headache and vertigo intensified. While he exhibited no neck rigidity or cranial nerve signs, central nervous system inflammation was considered. MRI of the brain showed no notable lesions affecting the hypothalamus or other areas (figure 2). Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) assessment demonstrated a slightly raised opening pressure (17 cmH₂O), elevated protein (0.97 g/L), and mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL). Cultures and tests for bacterial and mycobacterial organisms, along with herpes simplex virus (HSV), varicella zoster virus (VZV), and cryptococcal antigen, returned negative. Cytological analysis of the CSF did not detect malignant cells.

Additional workup was conducted to investigate potential systemic conditions. Evaluation for sarcoidosis revealed normal levels of lysozyme (5.1 µg/mL; reference range 5–10) and angiotensin-converting enzyme (11.4 U/L; reference range 7–25). Assessment for hematological malignancy showed an elevated soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) (704 U/mL; reference range 122–496 U/mL) and negative testing for human T-cell leukemia virus type 1 antibody.

On the fifth day, the patient developed a painful scrotal ulcer (figure 1D). Together with oral aphthae and a folliculitis-like rash, these features were consistent with systemic Behçet's disease (BD). He did not have a positive pinprick hypersensitivity response. Eye examination did not demonstrate uveitis. HLA-B51 was positive.

Differential diagnosis:

Upon admission, the coexistence of fever, headache, general malaise, and hyponatremia raised suspicion for a central nervous system (CNS) infection or a systemic inflammatory condition. The initial diagnostic consideration was viral meningoencephalitis, especially from herpes simplex virus (HSV) or varicella-zoster virus (VZV). Nonetheless, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analyses were negative for both viral antigens and antibodies, effectively ruling out these etiologies. The possibility of bacterial meningitis was assessed but subsequently dismissed due to the lack of neutrophil predominance, the presence of mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, and negative CSF culture results. Tuberculous meningitis was ruled out on the basis of negative acid-fast bacilli staining and tuberculosis DNA tests.

Primary CNS neoplasms were also contemplated in light of the patient’s neurological symptoms. However, brain MRI demonstrated no abnormalities, and CSF cytology did not detect malignant cells. Although there was a slight increase in the soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R), this isolated laboratory finding was not sufficient to confirm a diagnosis of CNS lymphoma.

Evaluations for autoimmune conditions were also undertaken, but these were dismissed because the patient lacked the typical systemic features and had negative results on screening tests for autoantibodies. Neurosarcoidosis was considered in the differential diagnosis since early-phase MRI scans may appear normal; however, subsequent laboratory findings diminished the likelihood of this diagnosis. The minor increase in sIL-2R was interpreted as non-specific.

A definitive diagnosis of Behçet’s disease (BD) was made based on the clinical presentation, which included fever, fatigue, the presence of oral aphthae (two points), genital aphthosis (two points), and a folliculitis-like rash (one point). According to the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease, the patient’s cumulative score of five (≥4 required for definitive diagnosis) confirmed the condition. Notably, genital aphthosis is a distinguishing characteristic of BD, reinforcing this diagnosis. After comprehensive exclusion of infectious, neoplastic, and autoimmune causes, the patient was ultimately diagnosed with acute neuro-Behçet’s disease, given his CNS manifestations in conjunction with mucocutaneous findings.

Treatment:

Upon admission on day 1, clinicians suspected an underlying inflammatory disorder, but the specific cause had not been determined. Empiric treatment with cefepime was commenced after obtaining blood cultures, aimed at covering potential bacterial pathogens despite no definitive infectious site being identified.

By day 3, the patient experienced increasing headache and vertigo, indicative of possible central nervous system inflammation or infection. Empiric acyclovir therapy was initiated; however, this was discontinued once testing excluded HSV and VZV encephalitis.

On day 8, colchicine therapy was begun after the diagnosis of systemic Behçet's disease (BD) and acute neuro-Behçet's disease (neuro-BD) was established. Oral aphthae were addressed with topical corticosteroid ointment, while genital ulcers and skin lesions were managed with silver sulfadiazine cream. Syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) was treated conservatively through restriction of fluid intake to less than 800 mL/day, frequent monitoring of serum sodium, and avoidance of rapid sodium correction. Neither hypertonic saline nor vasopressin receptor antagonists were needed.

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient experienced resolution of his fever and an improvement in appetite the day after starting colchicine. Over the next two weeks, vertigo, general malaise, hiccups, and mucocutaneous lesions all gradually subsided. By day 16, his serum sodium had normalized at 136 mmol/L, and ADH levels had fallen to 1.8 pg/mL. Myogenic enzyme concentrations likewise returned to reference ranges.

Notably, systemic corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive agents were not administered at any point during his treatment. After a one-month inpatient stay, during which he recovered from the acute phase, hyponatraemia was closely monitored and managed, and rehabilitation was undertaken to address reduced physical capabilities, he was discharged. Colchicine therapy was continued for roughly four years, during which there was no recurrence of Behçet’s disease (BD) nor any neurological symptoms. Once BD had stabilized, biguanide therapy was started, resulting in sustained glycaemic control.

Discussion:

This report describes a rare clinical occurrence: concurrent diagnoses of systemic BD and acute neuro-BD. Additionally, it illustrates that SIADH may arise as a complication of acute neuro-BD. Improvement in the patient’s mucocutaneous findings, neurological symptoms, and hyponatraemia after initiating colchicine monotherapy implies a direct link among BD, neuro-BD, and SIADH. Clinicians should be vigilant for acute neuro-BD in patients with BD who present with fever or headache in association with hyponatraemia.

The case is remarkable in that both systemic BD and acute neuro-BD were identified simultaneously, supported by evidence of mucocutaneous involvement and CNS symptoms. Ordinarily, major organ manifestations such as CNS pathology tend to manifest roughly 4 years following the onset of BD, with neurological symptoms at presentation being observed in less than 5% of patients. Headaches attributable to neuro-BD are seen in only about 10% of cases. Consequently, recognizing neuro-BD at initial presentation posed considerable diagnostic difficulty in this case. Classically, acute neuro-BD manifests as a self-limited meningoencephalitis, which may or may not include focal neurological deficits. For this patient, brain MRI did not reveal abnormalities at the conventional sites of BD involvement, such as the hypothalamic area. Acute neuro-BD can present as aseptic meningitis without evidence of parenchymal brain involvement, and a normal MRI early in the disease course does not preclude diagnosis. The absence of imaging abnormalities added complexity to the assessment. Although HLA-B51 was tested, it did not aid in diagnosing this case, consistent with current international BD criteria which prioritize clinical findings over genetic testing. This case underscores the necessity of considering neuro-BD when individuals with mucocutaneous symptoms also exhibit ambiguous neurological complaints.

At the outset of our differential diagnosis, infectious etiologies were given precedence as the cause of CNS inflammation, considering the patient presented with unexplained fever, neurological manifestations, and hyponatraemia; nevertheless, no definite pathogen was identified. We also evaluated additional inflammatory etiologies affecting the CNS, such as connective tissue disorders and malignancies, including sarcoidosis and hematologic cancers, as part of our differential considerations. Consequently, we obtained autoimmune serological panels, along with serum ACE and sIL-2R, as part of a comprehensive initial assessment. These tests were selected on a limited basis, more as exclusionary measures rather than due to strong clinical suspicion. No findings from clinical assessment, imaging studies, or CSF analysis substantiated these alternative diagnoses, and the laboratory results did not contribute substantially to the eventual diagnosis. It is noteworthy that ACE levels lack adequate sensitivity for detecting sarcoidosis, and although sIL-2R may provide relevant information for the assessment of malignant lymphoma, mild increases in sIL-2R are nonspecific and can simply indicate generalized inflammation. In this situation, the lack of genital ulceration in the early phase of symptom development complicated a straightforward diagnosis of BD, thereby broadening the range of possible diagnoses. This case underscores the necessity of avoiding indiscriminate use of diagnostic tests and reinforces that investigations should be guided by clear clinical indications and their interpretations approached with prudence.

While instances of SIADH triggered by medications, such as cyclophosphamide, have been documented in individuals with BD, the relationship between acute neuro-BD and SIADH remains unclear. Neuro-BD features inflammation within the central nervous system, which could play a role in abnormal ADH regulation. Multiple studies have demonstrated that interleukin-6 (IL-6)—a proinflammatory cytokine commonly elevated during neuroinflammatory conditions—correlates with enhanced ADH release. In BD, atypical monocyte activation produces spontaneous IL-6 secretion, and raised IL-6 levels have been observed in the cerebrospinal fluid of patients experiencing acute neuro-BD. This evidence raises the possibility that IL-6-driven inflammation may promote SIADH in such cases. Although serum or CSF IL-6 concentrations were not measured in this patient, significantly increased serum CRP levels could signify a response mediated by IL-6–related inflammation. Although SIADH can occur secondary to a range of disorders, such as CNS inflammation, lung disease, malignancy, and specific medications, data from this case do not permit establishing a direct causal relationship between SIADH and acute neuro-BD. The temporal correlation seen here may indicate a general reaction to CNS inflammation rather than a mechanism specifically linked to neuro-BD.

This case demonstrates the possible adverse effects of hyponatraemia resulting from SIADH in the context of acute neuro-BD. Prior publications have identified persistent hiccups as a significant indicator of severe hyponatraemia due to SIADH following CNS inflammation, such as meningitis. Furthermore, creatine kinase was included in our initial assessment for fever, arthralgia, and general fatigue—symptoms potentially linked to inflammatory or metabolic muscle injury—which revealed rhabdomyolysis. Although rhabdomyolysis is a well-documented but frequently overlooked consequence of profound hyponatraemia, it is not commonly considered in routine investigations. These presentations, while recognized, are often underestimated as clinical effects of severe hyponatraemia. It is important for clinicians to recognize that seemingly unrelated symptoms, such as hiccups or rhabdomyolysis, may indicate underlying electrolyte imbalances, particularly in cases of neuroinflammatory disorders.

Corticosteroids are typically the primary therapeutic approach for acute neuro-BD, with most patients showing a positive response. Nevertheless, there have been instances of spontaneous remission in cases limited to meningoencephalitis-like symptoms. In this particular patient, treatment with colchicine alone alleviated both mucocutaneous and neurologic manifestations, along with hyponatraemia. Colchicine’s anti-inflammatory properties stem from its ability to block neutrophil migration and activation, as well as to inhibit production of cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-6. Despite its widespread use for managing mucocutaneous symptoms in BD, systematic reviews indicate that colchicine is not effective for acute neuro-BD, and that immunosuppressive medications are generally necessary for effective disease control. Indeed, previous case reports describe colchicine being utilized as an adjunctive agent alongside corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive drugs, rather than as the sole therapy. Although the possibility of spontaneous improvement cannot be ruled out in this instance, these observations imply that colchicine might serve as a beneficial treatment alternative for acute neuro-BD and associated complications like SIADH, particularly when corticosteroids are either not preferred or contraindicated.

Conclusion:

We report an uncommon instance of BD with simultaneous onset of acute neuro-BD, complicated by SIADH. This case illustrates that SIADH can develop as a sequela of acute neuro-BD and underscores the need for clinicians to recognize this possible link. Treatment with colchicine alone resulted in prompt resolution of mucocutaneous lesions, neurological symptoms, and hyponatraemia, indicating that colchicine may serve as an effective initial therapy for neuro-BD. In patients with BD presenting with vague central nervous system complaints such as fever and headache accompanied by hyponatraemia, clinicians should be vigilant for acute neuro-BD, particularly when SIADH is present.
