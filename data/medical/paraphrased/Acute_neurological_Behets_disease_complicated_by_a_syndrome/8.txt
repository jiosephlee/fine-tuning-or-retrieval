Title: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

Eiichi Kakehi, Yukako Matsumoto, Shigehisa Sakurai, Kazuhiko Kotani

Abstract:

Summary: We describe an uncommon instance of acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) presenting simultaneously with systemic Behçet’s disease (BD), meeting criteria for definite BD, and further complicated by the development of syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). A male patient in his sixth decade exhibited fever, cephalalgia, and various mucocutaneous signs such as oral aphthous ulcers, rashes resembling folliculitis, genital ulcerations, and reduced serum sodium. The BD diagnosis was established on the basis of his mucocutaneous presentations. Although brain MRI appeared normal, cerebrospinal fluid examination indicated aseptic meningitis, thereby corroborating a diagnosis of acute neuro-BD. Subsequent laboratory evaluations satisfied the diagnostic requirements for SIADH. Treatment solely with colchicine resulted in complete resolution of both mucocutaneous and neurological symptoms, as well as correction of hyponatraemia, without necessitating corticosteroids or other forms of immunosuppression. This report highlights a potential link between acute neuro-BD and SIADH, and emphasizes the clinical significance of identifying concurrent neurological and endocrine features when evaluating new-onset BD.

Background:

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a persistent, multisystemic inflammatory condition marked by recurrent ulcerations in the oral and genital regions, uveitis, and various cutaneous manifestations. Neurological complications, collectively termed neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), emerge in about 10%–20% of individuals with BD, generally manifesting several years after the initial diagnosis. Acute neuro-BD often presents as meningoencephalitis accompanied by parenchymal brain lesions, with the brainstem being a frequent site of involvement. While it is more typical for neurological symptoms to appear after BD has been established, simultaneous diagnosis of BD and neuro-BD is unusual. When such concurrence arises, practitioners should thoroughly evaluate both the classic mucocutaneous findings and neurologic indicators such as headaches, behavioral disturbances, psychiatric symptoms, and localized neurological deficits.

Syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) represents a recognized etiology of hyponatraemia, commonly resulting from central nervous system (CNS) inflammation, neoplasms, or specific pharmacologic agents. Due to the pronounced inflammation in acute neuro-BD, individuals with this presentation may have an elevated risk for developing SIADH. Although SIADH has been documented among BD patients undergoing treatments like cyclophosphamide, it is not well established whether SIADH can occur solely as a consequence of CNS inflammation from acute neuro-BD in the absence of medication-induced triggers.

Here, we describe an unusual case featuring the simultaneous onset of BD and acute neuro-BD, complicated by the development of SIADH. This report underscores the necessity of recognizing SIADH as a possible complication of acute neuro-BD, especially in patients who exhibit non-specific neurological symptoms in conjunction with hyponatraemia.

Case presentation:

A man of Asian descent in his 60s arrived at the emergency department, describing a 3-day period marked by fever, headache, general fatigue, diminished appetite, constant hiccups, and joint pain localized to the shoulders and ankles. He further mentioned the presence of oral aphthous ulcers.

The patient’s past medical history was notable for type 2 diabetes mellitus, though he was not receiving any medical therapy due to insufficient knowledge of his diagnosis and poor adherence to dietary and exercise recommendations. His family history revealed no cases of BD. He reported infrequent alcohol intake, no tobacco use, no recent travel, and no exposure to animals or ill individuals. Professionally, he worked in the metalworking field and reported no recent sexual activity.

Upon assessment, the patient was conscious but showed signs of fatigue. His vital parameters were as follows: temperature of 38.1°C, blood pressure measured at 135/73 mm Hg, heart rate recorded at 119 beats per minute with a regular rhythm, respiratory rate of 19 breaths per minute, and an oxygen saturation of 96% while breathing ambient air. Physical examination identified the presence of oral aphthae and folliculitis-like lesions on both his face and trunk (see figure 1A, B, and C). Examination of the heart, lungs, and abdomen yielded no abnormal findings. Manual muscle strength assessment demonstrated preserved muscle power in both the upper and lower limbs. No peripheral edema was observed, and clinical evaluation indicated that he was euvolemic. The patient was admitted for additional diagnostic workup.

Investigations:

Initial blood investigations demonstrated significant hyponatraemia (124 mmol/L), with raised levels of creatine kinase (1349 U/L), aspartate aminotransferase (77 U/L), alanine aminotransferase (64 U/L), C-reactive protein (CRP) (180 mg/L; reference, <3 mg/L), and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (70 mm/hour; reference, <15 mm/hour); renal function was within normal parameters. Additionally, the patient exhibited hyperglycaemia (plasma glucose 14.2 mmol/L) and an increased HbA1c of 8.2%, suggestive of inadequate glycaemic regulation. Tests for hepatitis B and C were negative.

Further endocrine assessment showed thyroid-stimulating hormone (1.11 mIU/L; reference, 0.50–5.00 mIU/L), free T4 (1.25 ng/dL; reference, 0.90–1.70 ng/dL), and serum cortisol (42 µg/dL; reference, >6 µg/dL) all within normal reference ranges. Serum osmolality was found to be reduced (267 mOsm/kg; reference, <270 mOsm/kg), while urine osmolality was markedly elevated (900 mOsm/kg, reference, >300 mOsm/kg), as was urinary sodium (88 mmol/L; reference, >20 mmol/L), indicating a deficit in water excretion capacity. Despite the presence of hypo-osmolar hyponatraemia, antidiuretic hormone (ADH) concentrations were increased (22.4 pg/mL). Electrocardiogram, chest radiograph, and computed tomography scans of the head, chest, and abdomen revealed no abnormalities. Collectively, these laboratory and imaging results were compatible with a diagnosis of SIADH.

By the third day, the patient's headache and vertigo had intensified. Despite the absence of neck rigidity or signs implicating cranial nerves, there was suspicion of central nervous system inflammation. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain did not reveal any significant pathology within the hypothalamus or other cerebral areas (see figure 2). Examination of the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) indicated a mildly elevated opening pressure at 17 cmH₂O, an increased protein concentration of 0.97 g/L, and a modest lymphocytic pleocytosis, with 11 cells/µL. Microbiological analysis of the CSF, including cultures and tests for bacteria, mycobacteria, herpes simplex virus (HSV), varicella zoster virus (VZV), and cryptococcal antigen, returned negative results. Cytological assessment of the CSF excluded the presence of malignant cells.

Additional diagnostic workup aimed at identifying potential underlying systemic disorders was undertaken. Assessment for sarcoidosis showed lysozyme at 5.1 µg/mL (reference range 5–10) and angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) at 11.4 U/L (reference 7–25), both within normal ranges. Evaluation for possible hematological malignancy demonstrated a soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) level of 704 U/mL (reference 122–496 U/mL); tests for human T-cell leukemia virus type 1 antibodies were negative.

By day 5, the patient developed a painful ulceration on the scrotum (see figure 1D). Together with concomitant oral aphthous ulcers and folliculitis-like cutaneous eruptions, these manifestations were consistent with a systemic presentation of BD. He did not manifest any pinprick hypersensitivity. An ophthalmologic evaluation found no evidence of uveitis. Testing showed positivity for HLA-B51.

Differential diagnosis:

Upon initial evaluation, the constellation of fever, headache, generalized malaise, and hyponatraemia indicated the possibility of a central nervous system (CNS) infection or a systemic inflammatory process. The clinical team primarily suspected viral meningoencephalitis, especially from HSV or VZV. Nevertheless, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analyses failed to detect viral antigens or antibodies, thereby largely excluding this etiology. Consideration was also given to bacterial meningitis, but this was dismissed because there was no neutrophilic predominance in the CSF, only a mild increase in lymphocytes, and negative CSF cultures. Tuberculous meningitis was ruled out by negative acid-fast staining and tuberculosis DNA assays.

Given the neurological presentation, primary CNS malignancy was also contemplated. However, imaging with brain MRI did not demonstrate any abnormalities and CSF cytology found no evidence of malignant cells. The mildly increased soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) level was interpreted as non-specific and insufficient for the diagnosis of CNS lymphoma in the absence of further supporting data.

Autoimmune conditions were systematically assessed, but the lack of characteristic systemic features and negative autoantibody screens excluded these diagnoses. Neurosarcoidosis remained a consideration, since normal MRI findings do not eliminate the possibility, especially in early disease; however, subsequent laboratory investigations diminished the likelihood of this diagnosis, and the slightly elevated sIL-2R was considered non-diagnostic.

Ultimately, the patient met the criteria for definite Behçet’s disease (BD) based on clinical manifestations, which comprised fever, fatigue, oral aphthae (awarded two points), genital aphthosis (two points), and a folliculitis-like skin eruption (one point). With a total of five points—surpassing the four-point threshold of the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease—the diagnosis of BD was strongly supported, particularly due to the specificity of genital aphthosis for BD. After systematically excluding infectious, neoplastic, and other autoimmune conditions, the presence of CNS symptoms alongside mucocutaneous involvement led to a diagnosis of acute neuro-BD.

Treatment:

On the first day of hospitalization, clinicians considered an underlying inflammatory disorder, though the precise cause could not be identified. Broad-spectrum antimicrobial treatment with cefepime was commenced after obtaining blood cultures, aiming to address a possible bacterial infection even though a distinct site of infection was not apparent.

By the third day, there was a deterioration in the patient's headache and vertigo, pointing toward central nervous system involvement, either inflammatory or infectious in nature. Empirical therapy with acyclovir was given, but subsequently discontinued once laboratory investigations excluded both HSV and VZV encephalitis.

On the eighth day, colchicine therapy was introduced based on a new diagnosis of systemic BD and acute neuro-BD. Management of oral aphthous ulcers involved the use of topical corticosteroid ointment, while genital and skin lesions were treated with silver sulfadiazine cream. For SIADH, a conservative strategy was employed consisting of fluid intake restriction to less than 800 mL per day, daily monitoring of serum sodium levels, and measures to prevent overly rapid correction of sodium. Administration of hypertonic saline or vasopressin receptor antagonists was not necessary.

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient experienced resolution of his fever and an improvement in appetite the day following the initiation of colchicine. Over the subsequent two weeks, symptoms such as vertigo, general malaise, hiccups, and mucocutaneous lesions showed steady improvement. By the sixteenth day, his serum sodium had normalized at 136 mmol/L, and ADH levels had declined to 1.8 pg/mL. Additionally, myogenic enzyme concentrations returned to within the reference range.

Notably, systemic corticosteroids and other immunosuppressive agents were not employed during his treatment. After a hospital stay lasting one month—during which he recovered from the acute illness, hyponatraemia was closely monitored and managed, and rehabilitation addressed the reduction in physical capabilities—the patient was discharged. Colchicine therapy was maintained for approximately four years, during which no relapses of BD or neurological issues occurred. Once BD reached stabilization, biguanide therapy was started, resulting in sustained adequate glycaemic control.

Discussion:

In this instance, both systemic BD and acute neuro-BD were concurrently diagnosed, a notably uncommon clinical circumstance. Moreover, this case highlighted that acute neuro-BD may be accompanied by SIADH. The observed improvement in mucocutaneous, neurological, and biochemical (hyponatraemia) features following treatment with colchicine monotherapy points toward a possible direct relationship among BD, neuro-BD, and SIADH. Consequently, when a patient with BD develops fever or headache along with hyponatraemia, clinicians should be vigilant for the possibility of underlying acute neuro-BD.

A distinguishing feature of this case is the contemporaneous identification of systemic BD and acute neuro-BD, as evidenced by the presence of both mucocutaneous and central nervous system manifestations. Ordinarily, major organ involvement such as CNS pathology appears on average approximately four years after BD is first identified, and initial neurological presentations are relatively rare, occurring in fewer than 5% of cases. Headaches attributable to neuro-BD are also relatively uncommon, reported in roughly 10% of patients. Accordingly, making an initial diagnosis of neuro-BD in this scenario posed a particular diagnostic challenge. Acute neuro-BD most frequently manifests as a self-limiting meningoencephalitis, which can occur with or without focal deficits. In the present patient, MRI of the brain failed to show abnormalities in regions typically implicated in BD, such as the hypothalamus. It is noteworthy that acute neuro-BD may sometimes present solely as aseptic meningitis without accompanying parenchymal abnormalities, and normal brain MRI findings, especially in the early stages, do not rule out the diagnosis. The absence of radiographic abnormalities further compounded diagnostic difficulty. Although HLA-B51 typing was undertaken, it did not aid in reaching a diagnosis. This reflects current international BD classification criteria, which emphasize clinical rather than genetic findings, as HLA-B51 is not a requirement. This case underscores the need for clinicians to consider neuro-BD as a potential diagnosis when patients with cutaneous or mucosal features present with otherwise unexplained neurological symptoms.

At the outset of the differential diagnostic process, we primarily considered infection as the underlying cause of CNS inflammation, due to the presence of unexplained fever, neurological deficits, and hyponatraemia. Despite this, our investigations did not detect any particular infectious agent. We also explored alternative etiologies of CNS inflammation, such as connective tissue disorders and neoplastic conditions, with particular attention to sarcoidosis and haematologic malignancies. To facilitate an inclusive initial workup, we obtained autoimmune serological markers, serum ACE, and sIL-2R levels as part of a screening strategy. These assays were selected more for exclusionary purposes than for any pronounced clinical suspicion. Ultimately, there were no supportive clinical, imaging, or CSF findings for these disorders, and the results from these tests did not significantly inform our final diagnosis. Notably, serum ACE levels are only modestly sensitive for detecting sarcoidosis, and while sIL-2R might have value in assessing malignant lymphoma, slight elevations lack specificity and can arise from various inflammatory states. In our patient's presentation, the absence of genital ulcers early in the clinical course complicated the identification of BD, necessitating a wide-ranging differential diagnosis. This approach underscores the risks associated with indiscriminate testing, reinforcing that diagnostic investigations should be driven by concrete clinical findings and their results interpreted judiciously.

While cases of drug-induced SIADH in individuals with BD treated with agents such as cyclophosphamide have been documented, the link between acute neuro-BD and SIADH is not well defined. Neuro-BD involves inflammatory processes within the CNS, potentially leading to abnormalities in ADH regulation. Multiple studies indicate that interleukin-6 (IL-6)—a proinflammatory cytokine commonly increased in neuroinflammatory conditions—is implicated in enhanced ADH secretion. In the context of BD, irregular monocyte activity results in spontaneous production of IL-6, with raised IL-6 concentrations identified in the CSF of patients during acute neuro-BD episodes. These observations imply that inflammation driven by IL-6 could play a role in the pathogenesis of SIADH under such circumstances. Despite the absence of direct IL-6 measurements in serum or CSF for the current patient, the significantly increased serum CRP level may indirectly indicate an IL-6–associated inflammatory reaction. Although SIADH has been reported in conjunction with numerous conditions—such as CNS inflammation, pulmonary disorders, malignancy, and specific medications—the data from this case are insufficient to determine a direct causal association between SIADH and acute neuro-BD. The observed timing between these events might represent a general response to CNS inflammation instead of a neuro-BD–specific pathophysiological process.

This case further demonstrates the range of complications that can arise from hyponatraemia due to SIADH in acute neuro-BD. Previous studies have indicated that persistent hiccups may be a key clinical indicator of severe hyponatraemia associated with SIADH secondary to central nervous system inflammation, such as meningitis. As part of our initial assessment for fever, arthralgia, and general fatigue—which could result from either inflammatory or metabolic muscle injury—we also measured CK levels. This assessment identified rhabdomyolysis, a known but frequently neglected consequence of marked hyponatraemia that is not typically screened for in routine practice. Although these clinical features are recognized, they often do not receive sufficient attention as indicators of serious hyponatraemia. It is important for clinicians to recognize that symptoms seemingly unrelated—such as persistent hiccups or rhabdomyolysis—may actually signal underlying disturbances in electrolyte balance, particularly in patients with neuroinflammatory disease.

Corticosteroids remain the cornerstone therapy for acute neuro-BD, with most individuals experiencing a positive response to their use. Nonetheless, there are accounts of spontaneous recovery, particularly in instances characterized only by meningoencephalitis-like presentations. In the case discussed here, administration of colchicine alone led to improvement in mucocutaneous lesions, neurological deficits, and hyponatraemia. Colchicine mediates its anti-inflammatory properties primarily through the inhibition of neutrophil migration and activation, and by downregulating the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-6. While colchicine is commonly prescribed to manage mucocutaneous features of BD, evidence from systematic reviews indicates that it is not effective as a sole therapy for acute neuro-BD, and that effective disease management generally necessitates the use of immunosuppressive medications. Indeed, reports in the literature typically describe colchicine being used in combination with corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive drugs, rather than as monotherapy. Although the possibility of spontaneous symptom resolution in this patient cannot be ruled out, our results imply that colchicine could represent a viable therapeutic alternative for acute neuro-BD and associated complications, such as SIADH, especially in scenarios where corticosteroids are either inappropriate or contraindicated.

Conclusion:

We reported an uncommon presentation involving the simultaneous onset of BD and acute neuro-BD, complicated further by the development of SIADH. This case underscores the possibility of SIADH manifesting as a complication of acute neuro-BD, emphasizing the necessity for healthcare providers to be alert to this potential link. Administration of colchicine as a single agent resulted in swift resolution of mucocutaneous lesions, neurological deficits, and hyponatraemia, indicating that colchicine might serve as an effective initial therapy in early-stage neuro-BD. When encountering patients with BD who exhibit vague central nervous system symptoms—such as fever and headache—alongside hyponatraemia, clinicians should be particularly vigilant for acute neuro-BD in the setting of SIADH.
