Title: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

Eiichi Kakehi, Yukako Matsumoto, Shigehisa Sakurai, Kazuhiko Kotani

Abstract:

Summary: We present an uncommon instance of acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) identified simultaneously with systemic Behçet’s disease (BD), meeting criteria for definite BD, and further complicated by syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). A male in his 60s was admitted with symptoms of fever, headache, and mucocutaneous findings such as oral aphthous ulcers, folliculitis-like eruptions, genital sores, and hyponatraemia. The diagnosis of BD was established based on these mucocutaneous features. Magnetic resonance imaging of the brain did not reveal any abnormalities, while cerebrospinal fluid analysis was consistent with aseptic meningitis, lending support to a diagnosis of acute neuro-BD. Laboratory evaluation met the criteria for SIADH. Treatment with colchicine alone resulted in the improvement of both mucocutaneous and neurological symptoms as well as normalization of sodium levels, without the administration of corticosteroids or alternative immunosuppressive agents. This report highlights a potential link between acute neuro-BD and SIADH and emphasizes the need to consider simultaneous neurological and endocrine involvement in patients newly diagnosed with BD.

Background:

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a persistent inflammatory condition that affects multiple organ systems, typically presenting with recurring oral and genital ulcerations, uveitis, and cutaneous lesions. Neurological manifestations, known as neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), are observed in about 10%–20% of individuals with BD, most often emerging several years following the initial diagnosis. Acute forms of neuro-BD generally manifest with meningoencephalitis and parenchymal abnormalities predominantly affecting the brainstem. While neuro-BD usually develops after an established diagnosis of BD, simultaneous identification of both conditions is unusual. In such instances, clinicians should evaluate for both mucocutaneous signs and neurological manifestations such as cephalgia, behavioral or psychiatric disturbances, and focal neurologic deficits.

The syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) represents a recognized etiology of hyponatraemia, frequently linked to inflammation within the central nervous system (CNS), malignancy, or specific drug therapies. Because acute neuro-BD is characterized by CNS inflammation, patients may be predisposed to developing SIADH. There have been accounts of SIADH among BD patients treated with agents like cyclophosphamide; however, occurrences attributed solely to CNS inflammation from acute neuro-BD in the absence of medication triggers are not well documented.

Here, we present an uncommon case featuring the simultaneous onset of BD and acute neuro-BD, complicated by the development of SIADH. This report underscores the need to recognize SIADH as a possible complication of acute neuro-BD, especially when patients exhibit non-specific CNS complaints and are found to be hyponatraemic.

Case presentation:

A man of Asian descent in his 60s arrived at the emergency department after experiencing 3 days of fever, headache, generalized malaise, reduced appetite, ongoing hiccups, and arthralgia in both his shoulders and ankles. He additionally noted the presence of oral aphthae.

His past medical history was significant for type 2 diabetes mellitus, which was unmanaged due to a lack of awareness about the diagnosis and failure to follow appropriate diet or exercise routines. His family history revealed no instances of BD among relatives. The patient drank alcohol on occasion, did not use tobacco, had not traveled recently, and denied contact with animals or ill individuals. His occupation was in the metalworking sector, and he reported no recent sexual activity.

Upon assessment, the patient was conscious, though he showed signs of tiredness. His vital measurements were as follows: temperature of 38.1°C, blood pressure reading 135/73 mm Hg, heart rate of 119 bpm with a regular rhythm, respiratory rate of 19 per minute, and oxygen saturation at 96% while breathing ambient air. Inspection identified aphthous ulcers in the mouth and folliculitis-like lesions on his face and trunk (figure 1A, B, and C). Cardiopulmonary and abdominal examinations were within normal limits. Manual testing of muscle strength did not detect any weakness in either the upper or lower limbs. There was no evidence of peripheral edema, and he was clinically assessed to be euvolemic. He was subsequently admitted for additional diagnostic workup.

Investigations:

Initial bloodwork demonstrated significant hyponatraemia (124 mmol/L), increased creatine kinase (1349 U/L), aspartate aminotransferase (77 U/L), alanine aminotransferase (64 U/L), a markedly raised C-reactive protein (CRP) level (180 mg/L; normal <3 mg/L), and elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate (70 mm/hour; normal <15 mm/hour), while renal function remained within normal limits. The patient had hyperglycaemia (plasma glucose 14.2 mmol/L) and a high HbA1c (8.2%), reflecting suboptimal glucose regulation. Tests for hepatitis B and C returned negative.

Endocrine studies showed thyroid-stimulating hormone (1.11 mIU/L; normal 0.50–5.00 mIU/L), free T4 (1.25 ng/dL; normal 0.90–1.70 ng/dL), and serum cortisol (42 µg/dL; reference >6 µg/dL) all within normal ranges. Serum osmolality was decreased (267 mOsm/kg; reference <270 mOsm/kg) whereas urine osmolality was markedly raised (900 mOsm/kg; reference >300 mOsm/kg), alongside a high urine sodium (88 mmol/L; reference >20 mmol/L), indicating compromised renal free water clearance. In the context of hypo-osmolar hyponatraemia, the antidiuretic hormone (ADH) was found to be increased (22.4 pg/mL). Electrocardiogram results were unremarkable, and imaging including chest radiography and CT scans of the brain, chest, and abdomen did not reveal any abnormalities. Collectively, these results were indicative of syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH).

By the third day, the patient's headache and vertigo had intensified. Despite the absence of neck stiffness or signs of cranial nerve dysfunction, central nervous system (CNS) inflammation remained a consideration. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain showed no notable abnormalities involving the hypothalamus or other areas (figure 2). Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) studies demonstrated a mildly raised opening pressure of 17 cmH₂O, protein elevation to 0.97 g/L, and a mild lymphocytic pleocytosis with 11 cells/µL. Results from CSF cultures and molecular testing for bacterial, mycobacterial, herpes simplex virus (HSV), varicella zoster virus (VZV), and cryptococcal antigen were all negative. Cytological analysis of the CSF detected no evidence of malignancy.

Additional workup was conducted to exclude systemic conditions. Evaluation for sarcoidosis yielded normal lysozyme (5.1 µg/mL; reference range 5–10) and angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) (11.4 U/L; reference range 7–25) levels. Hematologic malignancy screening demonstrated elevated soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) at 704 U/mL (reference, 122–496 U/mL), while testing for human T-cell leukaemia virus type 1 antibody was negative.

On the fifth day, he developed a painful ulcer on the scrotum (figure 1D). Given the concurrent findings of oral aphthae and folliculitis-like skin eruptions, a systemic type of Behçet’s disease (BD) was suspected. There was no skin hyperreactivity to pinprick. Ophthalmologic assessment did not reveal uveitis. Testing for HLA-B51 was positive.

Differential diagnosis:

Upon initial evaluation, the constellation of fever, headache, general malaise, and hyponatremia raised concerns for either a central nervous system (CNS) infection or a systemic inflammatory condition. Viral meningoencephalitis, especially due to HSV or VZV, was the leading suspicion, but subsequent cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis did not detect viral antigens or antibodies, thereby excluding these etiologies. Bacterial meningitis was contemplated, but the CSF profile—marked by the lack of neutrophilic predominance, only mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, and negative cultures—argued against this diagnosis. Additionally, negative results from acid-fast bacilli staining and tuberculosis DNA assays ruled out tuberculous meningitis.

Given the presence of neurological deficits, primary CNS tumor was also considered. Nonetheless, brain MRI revealed no abnormalities, and CSF cytology found no evidence of malignant cells. The soluble IL-2 receptor (sIL-2R) was only modestly raised, which by itself did not substantiate a diagnosis of CNS lymphoma.

Autoimmune processes were investigated but dismissed due to the lack of definitive systemic symptoms and negative results on screening autoantibody tests. Neurosarcoidosis remained in the differential given that early MRI findings can be unremarkable; however, subsequent laboratory evaluations rendered this diagnosis improbable. The slight elevation of sIL-2R was interpreted as non-specific.

The patient fulfilled criteria for definite Behçet’s disease (BD) based on clinical presentation, including persistent fever and fatigue, recurrent oral ulcerations (two points), genital aphthae (two points), and a folliculitis-like cutaneous eruption (one point), totaling five points on the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease scale (a score of four or more confirming the diagnosis). Genital aphthosis, in particular, is considered a highly specific manifestation of BD, lending further support to this diagnosis. With infectious, neoplastic, and other autoimmune causes systematically excluded, a diagnosis of acute neuro-Behçet’s disease was made, given the neurological involvement in conjunction with mucocutaneous features.

Treatment:

On the first day of hospitalization, clinicians considered an underlying inflammatory disorder, though the specific cause was not determined. Broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy with cefepime was commenced following the collection of blood cultures, aiming to address a possible bacterial pathogen in the absence of an identifiable source of infection.

By the third day, the patient's headache and vertigo had intensified, raising concerns about central nervous system inflammation or infection. Acyclovir was given empirically but discontinued once laboratory testing excluded both HSV and VZV encephalitis.

On the eighth day, colchicine therapy was initiated following confirmation of systemic Behçet’s disease (BD) with acute neurological involvement (neuro-BD). Topical corticosteroid ointment was prescribed for oral aphthous ulcers. Genital and skin lesions were treated using silver sulfadiazine cream. The patient's syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) was addressed conservatively by restricting fluid intake to less than 800 mL per day, closely monitoring daily serum sodium levels, and avoiding overly rapid correction of sodium. Neither hypertonic saline nor vasopressin receptor antagonists were administered.

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient's fever subsided and his appetite returned the day following the initiation of colchicine therapy. During the subsequent two weeks, there was a gradual resolution of his symptoms, which included vertigo, general malaise, hiccups, and mucocutaneous lesions. By day 16, his serum sodium levels had normalized to 136 mmol/L, and his ADH levels had declined to 1.8 pg/mL. In addition, myogenic enzyme concentrations reverted to normal.

Notably, neither systemic corticosteroids nor other immunosuppressive agents were administered throughout treatment. After a one-month hospital stay, during which time he recovered from the acute illness, hyponatremia was closely observed and managed, and he received rehabilitation for his decreased physical capacity, the patient was discharged. Colchicine therapy was maintained for approximately four years without any relapse of Behçet’s disease (BD) or neurological manifestations in that period. Once BD was stabilized, biguanide therapy was started, resulting in sustained adequate glycaemic control.

Discussion:

This case report describes the concurrent identification of systemic Behçet's disease (BD) and acute neuro-Behçet's disease (neuro-BD), a clinical occurrence that is seldom seen. It further illustrates that SIADH can arise as a complication during the course of acute neuro-BD. The improvement of the patient’s mucocutaneous lesions, neurological symptoms, and hyponatraemia solely with colchicine therapy points toward a potential direct link among BD, neuro-BD, and SIADH. In cases where BD patients exhibit fever or headache in combination with hyponatraemia, clinicians should keep acute neuro-BD in the differential diagnosis.

A distinguishing feature of this case is the simultaneous onset of both systemic BD and acute neuro-BD, indicated by the coexistence of mucocutaneous involvement and central nervous system symptoms. Ordinarily, major organ manifestations such as CNS lesions are observed approximately four years after the initial BD diagnosis, and neurological signs are present at disease onset in less than 5% of patients. Headaches directly attributable to neuro-BD are reported in only about 10% of cases, which inherently increases the diagnostic difficulty for neuro-BD at presentation. Acute neuro-BD most often appears as a meningoencephalitis that is self-limited, which may be accompanied by focal neurological signs. In this case, brain MRI did not reveal characteristic abnormalities, including in the hypothalamus, where BD lesions are commonly found. Neuro-BD may occasionally manifest as aseptic meningitis absent of parenchymal changes, and a negative MRI—especially in the early stages—does not exclude the diagnosis. The absence of radiological features compounded the diagnostic challenge. Although HLA-B51 genetic testing was conducted, it was not helpful in confirming the diagnosis, consistent with international classification criteria for BD, which do not incorporate HLA-B51 and are based primarily on clinical evidence. This case underlines the need to consider neuro-BD in BD patients who have mucocutaneous signs along with vague neurological complaints.

During the initial steps of our differential diagnosis, infection was given precedence as a potential source of CNS inflammation due to the patient's presentation with an unexplained fever, neurological manifestations, and hyponatraemia; nonetheless, we could not detect any definitive infectious agent. We expanded our consideration to encompass other etiologies of CNS inflammation, such as connective tissue disorders and neoplastic processes, and included sarcoidosis and haematologic malignancies among possible causes. Accordingly, our broad preliminary workup involved checking autoimmune serological markers, serum ACE, and soluble IL-2 receptor (sIL-2R) as part of a general screening protocol. These assessments were carried out on a limited basis for exclusion, rather than being guided by a strong clinical suspicion for a particular disease. There were no supportive clinical, imaging, or CSF findings for these conditions, and these investigations ultimately did not influence the final diagnostic outcome. Serum ACE demonstrates low sensitivity for sarcoidosis, while sIL-2R can aid in assessing malignant lymphoma, but minor increases are nonspecific and often merely indicate inflammation. The lack of genital ulcers in the early disease phase complicated an initial diagnosis of BD, necessitating a wider differential. This approach underscores the necessity to avoid unnecessary laboratory testing and advises that clinicians base such investigations on pertinent clinical findings and interpret them judiciously.

While SIADH resulting from medication use has been documented in patients with Behçet’s disease (BD), particularly with agents like cyclophosphamide, the relationship between acute neuro-BD and SIADH is not well established. Neuro-BD features inflammation within the central nervous system, which may disturb normal antidiuretic hormone (ADH) regulation. Multiple reports have indicated that interleukin-6 (IL-6)—a proinflammatory cytokine that is commonly elevated in neuroinflammatory conditions—is linked to increased secretion of ADH. In BD, abnormal activation of monocytes results in spontaneous IL-6 production, and elevated concentrations of IL-6 have been observed in the cerebrospinal fluid of patients experiencing acute neuro-BD. These observations imply a possible contribution of IL-6–driven inflammation to SIADH in such scenarios. Although IL-6 measurements were not performed in the serum or CSF for this patient, the significantly raised serum CRP level could indicate an active IL-6–mediated inflammatory state. While SIADH has been connected to a variety of disorders, including CNS inflammation, pulmonary pathology, malignancy, and specific drugs, the evidence from this case does not permit establishing a direct causal relationship between SIADH and acute neuro-BD. The observed temporal correlation in this instance may merely represent a generalized reaction to CNS inflammation, rather than indicating a mechanism unique to the disease.

This case further demonstrates the possible complications arising from hyponatraemia due to SIADH in acute neuro-BD. Previous literature has indicated that persistent hiccups can be an important indicator of severe hyponatraemia attributable to SIADH from central nervous system inflammation, such as meningitis. Additionally, creatine kinase was assessed as part of the workup for fever, arthralgia, and generalized fatigue, symptoms potentially linked to inflammatory or metabolic muscle injury. This assessment identified rhabdomyolysis, a well-documented but frequently missed complication of severe hyponatraemia that is not part of routine evaluation. Although these clinical features are known, their significance in the context of profound hyponatraemia is often underestimated. It is important for clinicians to consider that seemingly unrelated presentations, including persistent hiccups or rhabdomyolysis, may be manifestations of underlying electrolyte imbalances, especially amid neuroinflammatory disease.

Corticosteroids represent the standard therapy for acute neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), with the majority of patients demonstrating a favorable clinical response. Nonetheless, there have been reports of spontaneous remission in patients whose presentation is limited to meningoencephalitis-like features. In the current patient, administration of colchicine alone led to improvement in both mucocutaneous and neurological manifestations, as well as correction of hyponatraemia. The anti-inflammatory action of colchicine is mediated through inhibition of neutrophil migration and activation, alongside suppression of cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-6. While colchicine is commonly employed to manage the mucocutaneous lesions of BD, evidence from systematic reviews indicates that it does not demonstrate efficacy in acute neuro-BD, where immunosuppressive medications are typically necessary to achieve effective disease control. Reportedly, colchicine has primarily served as an adjunct to corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants, rather than as the sole therapeutic agent. Although the possibility of spontaneous recovery in this patient cannot be ruled out, the observations suggest that colchicine may offer benefit in treating acute neuro-BD and associated complications like SIADH, especially in scenarios where corticosteroid therapy is contraindicated or not preferable.

Conclusion:

We reported an uncommon presentation involving simultaneous diagnosis of BD and acute neuro-BD, where SIADH served as a complication of the neuro-BD. This case illustrates that SIADH can emerge alongside acute neuro-BD, underscoring the necessity for clinicians to recognize this potential link. Administration of colchicine alone resulted in rapid resolution of both mucocutaneous and neurological symptoms, as well as correction of hyponatraemia, indicating that colchicine may be an effective initial treatment option for neuro-BD. For patients with BD who experience vague central nervous system symptoms—such as fever and headache—together with hyponatraemia, it is important for clinicians to strongly consider the possibility of acute neuro-BD in the context of SIADH.
