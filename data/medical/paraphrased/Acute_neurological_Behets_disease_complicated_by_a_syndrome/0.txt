Acute neurological Behçet’s disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

Eiichi Kakehi, Yukako Matsumoto, Shigehisa Sakurai, Kazuhiko Kotani

Abstract:

Summary: We describe an uncommon presentation of acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) that was simultaneously identified with systemic Behçet’s disease (BD), fulfilling the criteria for definite BD, and further complicated by syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). A male patient in his sixth decade developed fever, headache, and various mucocutaneous manifestations, such as oral aphthae, folliculitis-like eruptions, genital ulcers, and was noted to have hyponatraemia. The clinical and mucocutaneous evidence supported a diagnosis of BD. Despite a normal brain MRI, analysis of cerebrospinal fluid revealed aseptic meningitis, in line with acute neuro-BD. Laboratory investigations established the presence of SIADH. Treatment with colchicine alone resulted in complete remission of mucocutaneous signs, neurological symptoms, and normalization of sodium levels, obviating the need for corticosteroids or additional immunosuppressive therapies. This report highlights a potential link between acute neuro-BD and SIADH, emphasizing the necessity for clinicians to be vigilant for simultaneous neurological and endocrine disorders in newly identified BD patients.

Background:

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a persistent, multisystem inflammatory condition, marked by recurring oral and genital ulcers, uveitis, as well as various skin manifestations. Neurological complications, identified as neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), arise in about 10%–20% of individuals with BD and often appear several years subsequent to the initial diagnosis. Typically, acute neuro-BD manifests with meningoencephalitis and parenchymal brain lesions, which are most frequently located in the brainstem. Although neuro-BD tends to develop later, it is uncommon for both BD and neuro-BD to be diagnosed simultaneously. When simultaneous diagnosis occurs, clinicians must evaluate both mucocutaneous signs and neurological complaints, such as headaches, behavioral disturbances, psychiatric manifestations, and focal neurological deficits.

The syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) constitutes a common etiology of hyponatraemia and is frequently attributed to central nervous system (CNS) inflammation, malignancy, or use of specific medications. Owing to the inflammatory pathology characteristic of acute neuro-BD, these patients may face an increased risk of developing SIADH. While SIADH has been documented in association with BD patients undergoing therapies like cyclophosphamide, the occurrence of SIADH due solely to CNS inflammation in the context of acute neuro-BD, without any medication triggers, remains insufficiently established.

Here, we present an unusual instance in which BD and acute neuro-BD were identified at the same time, complicated by the onset of SIADH. This case underscores the need to recognize SIADH as a possible complication of acute neuro-BD, especially in patients exhibiting vague CNS symptoms and unexplained hyponatraemia.

Case presentation:

A man of Asian descent in his 60s arrived at the emergency department, reporting a 3-day duration of fever, headache, generalized fatigue, reduced appetite, ongoing hiccups, and joint pain localized to the shoulders and ankles. He also described experiencing oral aphthae.

His past medical history was notable for type 2 diabetes mellitus; however, he was not undergoing any medical management, largely due to a lack of awareness about his illness and failure to adhere to dietary or exercise recommendations. There was no significant family history, including no known instances of BD among his relatives. His alcohol intake was occasional, he was a non-smoker, and there were no recent episodes of travel, animal contact, or exposure to ill individuals. The patient was employed in the metalworking field and had not engaged in recent sexual activity.

During assessment, the patient was alert, though notably fatigued. Recorded vital parameters included a temperature of 38.1°C, blood pressure measuring 135/73 mm Hg, a regular heart rate of 119 beats per minute, respiratory rate at 19 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation of 96% while breathing ambient air. Physical evaluation identified oral aphthae and rashes resembling folliculitis distributed on the face and trunk (figure 1A, B, and C). Examinations of the cardiopulmonary and abdominal systems were without significant findings. Manual muscle testing showed preserved strength in both upper and lower limbs. No peripheral oedema was present, and clinical assessment indicated a euvolemic state. The patient was subsequently hospitalized for additional diagnostic workup.

Investigations:

The initial laboratory analysis showed significant hyponatraemia at 124 mmol/L, alongside raised levels of creatine kinase (1349 U/L), aspartate aminotransferase (77 U/L), alanine aminotransferase (64 U/L), C-reactive protein (CRP) at 180 mg/L (reference, <3 mg/L), and erythrocyte sedimentation rate of 70 mm/hour (reference, <15 mm/hour), while renal function remained within normal limits. The patient also exhibited hyperglycaemia (plasma glucose 14.2 mmol/L) and an increased HbA1c of 8.2%, indicating inadequate glycaemic management. Screening for hepatitis B and C returned negative.

Endocrine assessment demonstrated thyroid-stimulating hormone within normal range (1.11 mIU/L; reference, 0.50–5.00 mIU/L), free T4 of 1.25 ng/dL (reference, 0.90–1.70 ng/dL), and a serum cortisol level of 42 µg/dL (reference, >6 µg/dL). Serum osmolality was decreased at 267 mOsm/kg (reference, <270 mOsm/kg), while urine osmolality was elevated (900 mOsm/kg; reference, >300 mOsm/kg), accompanied by a high urinary sodium level (88 mmol/L; reference, >20 mmol/L), suggesting compromised water excretion. Despite the presence of hypo-osmolar hyponatraemia, antidiuretic hormone (ADH) was found to be raised (22.4 pg/mL). Electrocardiogram results were normal, and imaging studies including chest X-ray and CT scans of the head, chest, and abdomen revealed no abnormalities. Collectively, these findings were indicative of SIADH.

By the third day, the patient's headache and vertigo intensified. Although neck rigidity and cranial nerve dysfunction were absent, central nervous system (CNS) inflammation was considered. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain did not reveal any notable abnormalities affecting the hypothalamus or other cerebral regions (figure 2). Analysis of the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) showed a slightly increased opening pressure (17 cmH₂O), elevated protein concentration (0.97 g/L), and a modest lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL). Cultures and molecular diagnostics of the CSF for bacterial and mycobacterial organisms, as well as herpes simplex virus (HSV), varicella zoster virus (VZV), and cryptococcal antigen, returned negative results. Cytological assessment of the CSF demonstrated no evidence of malignant cells.

Additional assessments were undertaken to investigate possible systemic disorders. Workup for sarcoidosis indicated normal lysozyme levels (5.1 µg/mL; reference range 5–10) and angiotensin-converting enzyme levels (11.4 U/L; reference 7–25). Screening for hematological malignancy demonstrated an elevated soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) (704 U/mL; reference range, 122–496 U/mL), while testing for human T-cell leukaemia virus type 1 antibody was negative.

By the fifth day, the patient developed a painful ulceration of the scrotum (figure 1D). The concurrent presence of oral aphthous ulcers and folliculitis-like cutaneous lesions raised suspicion for a systemic presentation of BD. There was no pathergy reaction to skin pricking. Ocular evaluation showed no signs of uveitis. The patient tested positive for HLA-B51.

Differential diagnosis:

Upon initial assessment, the presence of fever, headache, general malaise, and hyponatraemia pointed toward either a central nervous system infection or a systemic inflammatory process. Viral meningoencephalitis, particularly due to HSV or VZV, was the primary concern. Nonetheless, cerebrospinal fluid analysis returned negative for viral antigens and antibodies, thereby eliminating this possibility. Bacterial meningitis was similarly contemplated but dismissed owing to a lack of neutrophilic dominance, only a mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, and sterile CSF cultures. Tuberculous meningitis was ruled out as acid-fast staining and tuberculosis DNA tests were negative.

Given the neurological symptoms, a primary CNS malignancy was another consideration. However, imaging via brain MRI revealed no abnormalities, and no malignant cells were identified in CSF cytology. Although the soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) level was modestly elevated, this by itself was insufficient to confirm CNS lymphoma.

Autoimmune disorders were systematically excluded on the basis of absent hallmark systemic manifestations and negative autoantibody screening tests. Neurosarcoidosis remained in the differential diagnosis as normal MRI findings can occur in its early stages, but further laboratory workup ultimately argued against it. The slight increase in sIL-2R was deemed non-specific.

The diagnosis of definite BD was established according to clinical presentation: fever and fatigue, oral aphthous ulcers (assigned two points), genital aphthosis (two points), and a folliculitis-like eruption (one point). He accumulated five points based on the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (with a threshold of four or more for a definitive diagnosis). Notably, the presence of genital aphthosis is highly specific for BD and was a strong indicator in this context. After thoroughly excluding infectious, neoplastic, and autoimmune etiologies, the diagnosis of acute neuro-BD was confirmed, characterized by CNS involvement in the presence of mucocutaneous manifestations.

Treatment:

Upon admission (day 1), clinicians suspected the presence of an underlying inflammatory disorder, though its precise cause was not identified. Empirical cefepime therapy commenced after obtaining blood cultures, in order to address a possible bacterial infection, despite the lack of an identifiable source.

By day 3, the patient’s headache and vertigo had intensified, raising concerns about possible CNS inflammation or infection. Empirical acyclovir therapy was started, but discontinued once laboratory results excluded HSV and VZV encephalitis.

On day 8, following the confirmed diagnosis of systemic BD and acute neuro-BD, colchicine therapy was introduced. Oral aphthous ulcers received topical corticosteroid ointment, while genital ulcers and skin lesions were managed with silver sulfadiazine cream. For SIADH, management consisted of fluid restriction to less than $800\,\mathrm{mL}/\mathrm{day}$, with vigilant daily serum sodium monitoring and caution to prevent overly rapid sodium correction. Treatment with hypertonic saline or vasopressin receptor antagonists was not necessary.

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient experienced resolution of his fever and an increase in appetite within a day of initiating colchicine therapy. Over the subsequent two weeks, there was gradual improvement in his symptoms, such as vertigo, general malaise, hiccups, and mucocutaneous manifestations. By the sixteenth day, his serum sodium concentration had returned to 136 mmol/L and ADH levels had dropped to 1.8 pg/mL. Additionally, levels of myogenic enzymes normalized.

Notably, the management plan did not include systemic corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive agents. The patient was discharged after spending one month in the hospital, a period during which the acute phase resolved, hyponatraemia was actively monitored and treated, and rehabilitation addressed his decreased physical capabilities. Colchicine was maintained for roughly four years, during which neither BD relapse nor neurological complications were observed. Upon stabilization of BD, biguanide therapy was started, resulting in sustained glycaemic control.

Discussion:

In this instance, both systemic BD and acute neuro-BD were concurrently identified, representing an unusual clinical occurrence. The case also illustrated that acute neuro-BD can be accompanied by SIADH. The improvement of mucocutaneous findings, neurological symptoms, and hyponatraemia following colchicine monotherapy indicates a potential direct link among BD, neuro-BD, and SIADH. When encountering patients with BD who develop fever or headache together with hyponatraemia, healthcare providers should entertain the possibility of acute neuro-BD as an underlying etiology.

The simultaneous detection of systemic BD and acute neuro-BD in this case, substantiated by the presence of mucocutaneous lesions and central nervous system involvement, is particularly noteworthy. Ordinarily, manifestations in major organs, such as CNS lesions, emerge on average about four years after a BD diagnosis, and neurological signs are initially present in fewer than 5% of individuals. Additionally, only around 10% of patients develop headaches attributable directly to neuro-BD, rendering early identification of neuro-BD particularly difficult in this context. Most frequently, acute neuro-BD manifests as a self-limiting meningoencephalitis, which may or may not be accompanied by focal neurological deficits. In this patient, MRI of the brain did not reveal any abnormalities at typical BD-affected regions, such as the hypothalamus. Acute neuro-BD is capable of presenting as aseptic meningitis in the absence of parenchymal brain involvement, and a normal brain MRI, especially during the initial stage, does not preclude the diagnosis. The absence of radiological evidence contributed to the diagnostic challenge in this scenario. Although testing for HLA-B51 was undertaken, it did not assist in diagnosis. This is consistent with international criteria for BD, which prioritize clinical findings and do not mandate HLA-B51 positivity. Ultimately, this case underscores the necessity of considering neuro-BD when patients exhibiting mucocutaneous symptoms also report non-specific neurological complaints.

During the initial steps of the differential diagnosis, our main consideration was an infectious etiology for the CNS inflammation, given the presence of unexplained fever, neurological manifestations, and hyponatraemia; nonetheless, no causative pathogen was identified. We expanded our differential to encompass other potential sources of CNS inflammation such as connective tissue disorders and neoplastic processes, specifically including sarcoidosis and haematologic malignancies. Consequently, our comprehensive preliminary workup incorporated autoimmune serological markers, as well as serum ACE and sIL-2R levels, as general screening measures. These investigations were undertaken largely to rule out possibilities rather than out of significant clinical suspicion. Ultimately, neither clinical evaluation, imaging, nor CSF analysis substantiated any of these conditions, and the ancillary tests did not significantly influence our diagnostic conclusions. The sensitivity of ACE for sarcoidosis remains suboptimal, and although sIL-2R might have utility in assessing malignant lymphoma, slight elevations lack specificity and can simply indicate a generic inflammatory response. In this instance, the absence of genital ulcers early in the disease course contributed to uncertainty regarding a diagnosis of BD and prompted an extensive differential approach. This case underscores the necessity of judicious test selection, reminding clinicians to reserve ancillary investigations for situations where they are clearly indicated by clinical context, and to interpret such results with discernment.

While drug-induced SIADH has previously been documented in individuals with BD treated with agents such as cyclophosphamide, the link between acute neuro-BD and the onset of SIADH is not well elucidated. Neuro-BD is defined by inflammatory processes within the CNS, which can potentially result in abnormal secretion of ADH. Evidence from multiple investigations has indicated that interleukin-6 (IL-6)—a proinflammatory cytokine commonly heightened in neuroinflammatory states—is implicated in the enhancement of ADH secretion. In the context of BD, dysregulated monocyte activity results in autonomous IL-6 generation, with increased IL-6 concentrations observed in the CSF of those experiencing acute neuro-BD episodes. This body of research has led to the hypothesis that IL-6–driven inflammation might be involved in the emergence of SIADH in this context. Despite the absence of direct IL-6 measurements in serum or CSF in the current report, the significantly raised serum CRP could indicate an underlying inflammatory process mediated by IL-6. SIADH is recognized as a complication of numerous conditions, encompassing CNS inflammation, pulmonary disorders, neoplasms, and select pharmacotherapies; however, the data presented here are insufficient to conclusively demonstrate a direct causative relationship between SIADH and acute neuro-BD. The temporal concurrence seen in this instance might simply represent a general reaction to CNS inflammation rather than indicating a mechanism specific to this disease entity.

This case further highlights the range of complications that may arise from hyponatraemia induced by SIADH, particularly in the context of acute neuro-BD. Prior literature has suggested that persistent hiccups could serve as a valuable clinical indicator of severe hyponatraemia stemming from SIADH associated with central nervous system inflammation, such as meningitis. Moreover, our initial diagnostic approach included assessing CK levels due to symptoms of fever, arthralgia, and generalized fatigue—findings that may be linked to inflammatory or metabolic muscle injury—and this testing uncovered rhabdomyolysis. Although rhabdomyolysis is a recognized complication of severe hyponatraemia, it is frequently missed in standard evaluations. These clinical presentations, while established, are frequently underestimated manifestations of marked hyponatraemia. It is important for healthcare providers to recognize that symptoms such as persistent hiccups or rhabdomyolysis, which may initially appear unrelated, could signal significant electrolyte imbalances, especially against a background of neuroinflammatory disease.

Corticosteroids remain the cornerstone therapy for acute neuro-BD, with the majority of patients showing a positive clinical response. Nonetheless, there are documented instances where patients exhibiting only meningoencephalitis-like features experience spontaneous remission. In the case discussed here, administration of colchicine alone resulted in improvements in both mucocutaneous and neurological manifestations, as well as correction of hyponatraemia. Colchicine mediates its anti-inflammatory activity through inhibition of neutrophil chemotaxis and activation, along with downregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-6. While colchicine is routinely employed for managing mucocutaneous symptoms in BD, meta-analyses indicate limited efficacy for acute neuro-BD, underscoring the need for immunosuppressants to achieve sufficient disease control. Previously, colchicine has mainly been administered in conjunction with corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive medications, rather than as sole therapy. Even though spontaneous improvement cannot be definitively ruled out in this instance, these observations imply that colchicine monotherapy may represent a viable therapeutic alternative for acute neuro-BD and associated complications like SIADH, especially in situations where corticosteroids are not recommended or are contraindicated.

Conclusion:

We presented an uncommon instance in which Behçet’s Disease (BD) and acute neuro-BD were diagnosed simultaneously, and where SIADH served as a complicating factor for the neuro-BD. This report underscores that SIADH can manifest as a consequence of acute neuro-BD, emphasizing the necessity for clinicians to recognize this potential link. Administration of colchicine alone resulted in swift resolution of mucocutaneous lesions, neurological deficits, and hyponatraemia, indicating that colchicine could be an effective initial treatment in early neuro-BD. For individuals with BD who develop vague central nervous system complaints such as fever and headache alongside hyponatraemia, it is imperative for healthcare providers to consider the possibility of acute neuro-BD complicated by SIADH.
