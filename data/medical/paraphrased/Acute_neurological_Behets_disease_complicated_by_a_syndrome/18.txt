Title: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

Eiichi Kakehi, Yukako Matsumoto, Shigehisa Sakurai, Kazuhiko Kotani

Abstract:

Summary: We describe an uncommon instance of acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) that was simultaneously identified with systemic Behçet’s disease (BD), meeting criteria for definite BD, and further complicated by syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). A male in his 60s presented with fever, headache, and mucocutaneous symptoms, including oral aphthae, folliculitis-like eruptions, genital ulcers, and hyponatraemia. The diagnosis of BD was established based on the mucocutaneous manifestations. Brain MRI was unremarkable; however, cerebrospinal fluid examination indicated aseptic meningitis, corroborating the diagnosis of acute neuro-BD. Laboratory assessment fulfilled criteria for SIADH. The patient’s mucocutaneous complaints, neurological deficits, and hyponatraemia resolved following treatment with colchicine alone, without the administration of corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive agents. This case highlights a potential link between acute neuro-BD and SIADH and emphasizes the need to recognize both neurological and endocrine features in individuals with newly diagnosed BD.

Background:

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a persistent, multisystem inflammatory condition marked by the recurrence of oral and genital ulcers, uveitis, and skin manifestations. Neurological complications, termed neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), affect about 10%–20% of individuals with BD and generally emerge several years following the initial diagnosis. Acute neuro-BD most frequently presents as meningoencephalitis and parenchymal brain involvement, with the brainstem being the most commonly affected region. Although the onset of neuro-BD usually occurs later, simultaneous diagnoses of BD and neuro-BD are uncommon. In such scenarios, it is important for clinicians to evaluate both mucocutaneous signs and neurological manifestations, which may include headaches, behavioral disturbances, psychiatric symptoms, and focal neurological deficits.

The syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) is a recognized cause of hyponatremia, most often linked to central nervous system (CNS) inflammation, malignancies, or exposure to certain medications. Due to the inflammatory characteristics of acute neuro-BD, patients are potentially susceptible to SIADH. While cases of SIADH have been documented in BD patients treated with medications such as cyclophosphamide, the occurrence of SIADH solely due to CNS inflammation from acute neuro-BD and in the absence of pharmacological triggers is not well established.

Here, we present a rare instance where BD and acute neuro-BD were simultaneously diagnosed, complicated by the development of SIADH. This report underscores the need to recognize SIADH as a possible complication of acute neuro-BD, particularly in patients presenting with general CNS complaints and hyponatremia.

Case presentation:

A man of Asian descent in his sixties arrived at the emergency department after experiencing three days of fever, headache, generalized weakness, reduced appetite, ongoing hiccups, and joint pain localized to his shoulders and ankles. Additionally, he described the presence of oral aphthous ulcers.

His past medical history was significant for type 2 diabetes mellitus, which remained untreated due to limited awareness of his diagnosis and inadequate dietary or exercise habits. There was no relevant family history, as no relatives had Behçet's disease (BD). He drank alcohol infrequently, did not smoke, and denied recent travel, animal contact, or close contact with sick individuals. He was employed in metalworking and had not been sexually active in the recent past.

During assessment, the patient was conscious but showed signs of fatigue. His vital parameters were as follows: temperature 38.1°C, blood pressure 135/73 mm Hg, heart rate 119 bpm and regular, respiratory rate 19 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation measured at 96% while breathing room air. On physical exam, aphthous ulcers were noted in the oral cavity along with folliculitis-like lesions on the face and torso (figure 1A, B, and C). Cardiopulmonary and abdominal examinations were within normal limits. Strength testing by manual muscle testing did not demonstrate weakness in either the upper or lower limbs. There was an absence of peripheral edema, and the patient was determined to be euvolemic upon clinical evaluation. He was subsequently hospitalized for further work-up.

Investigations:

Initial bloodwork showed significant hyponatremia (124 mmol/L), elevated creatine kinase (1349 U/L), increased aspartate aminotransferase (77 U/L) and alanine aminotransferase (64 U/L), a raised C-reactive protein (CRP) of 180 mg/L (reference <3 mg/L), and an erythrocyte sedimentation rate of 70 mm/hour (reference <15 mm/hour), while renal function remained within normal limits. The patient exhibited hyperglycemia, with a plasma glucose of 14.2 mmol/L and a HbA1c of 8.2%, pointing to suboptimal glycemic management. Testing for hepatitis B and C was negative.

Further endocrine assessment showed thyroid-stimulating hormone at 1.11 mIU/L (reference 0.50–5.00 mIU/L), free T4 at 1.25 ng/dL (reference 0.90–1.70 ng/dL), and serum cortisol of 42 µg/dL (reference >6 µg/dL), all within normal ranges. The serum osmolality measured low at 267 mOsm/kg (reference <270 mOsm/kg), urine osmolality was high at 900 mOsm/kg (reference >300 mOsm/kg), and the urinary sodium concentration was increased at 88 mmol/L (reference >20 mmol/L), suggesting defective water clearance. Despite hypo-osmolar hyponatremia, antidiuretic hormone (ADH) was elevated at 22.4 pg/mL. Electrocardiogram findings were unremarkable, and imaging including chest X-ray, as well as CT scans of the head, chest, and abdomen, did not reveal abnormalities. Collectively, these results were indicative of SIADH.

By the third day, his headache and vertigo had intensified. Despite the absence of neck rigidity or cranial nerve deficits, central nervous system inflammation was considered. Magnetic resonance imaging of the brain showed no notable findings in the hypothalamic region or elsewhere (figure 2). Lumbar puncture indicated a mildly raised opening pressure at 17 cmH₂O, with elevated protein levels (0.97 g/L) and a mild increase in lymphocytes (11 cells/µL) in the cerebrospinal fluid. Tests of CSF, including cultures and studies for bacterial and mycobacterial organisms, as well as herpes simplex virus, varicella zoster virus, and cryptococcal antigen, were negative. No malignant cells were identified on CSF cytology.

Additional workup was carried out to assess for potential systemic conditions. Sarcoidosis was ruled out with normal lysozyme (5.1 µg/mL; reference 5–10) and angiotensin-converting enzyme (11.4 U/L; reference 7–25) levels. Investigation for hematologic malignancy showed an elevated soluble interleukin-2 receptor (704 U/mL; reference range, 122–496 U/mL), but human T-cell leukemia virus type 1 antibody testing was negative.

On day 5, a tender ulcer developed on the scrotum (figure 1D). The occurrence of this ulcer, along with oral aphthous lesions and folliculitis-like eruptions, raised suspicion for a systemic variant of BD. There was no evidence of pathergy upon pinprick testing. An eye evaluation showed no signs of uveitis. The patient tested positive for HLA-B51.

Differential diagnosis:

Upon admission, the constellation of fever, headache, general malaise, and hyponatremia was indicative of a potential central nervous system infection or systemic inflammatory process. Initial diagnostic consideration centered on viral meningoencephalitis, notably due to HSV or VZV; however, cerebrospinal fluid analyses were negative for viral antigens and antibodies, thereby effectively ruling out these etiologies. Bacterial meningitis was also evaluated but was discounted based on findings that included an absence of neutrophilic predominance, only mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, and negative cultures from CSF. Tuberculous meningitis was also excluded, as acid-fast staining and tuberculosis DNA assays yielded negative results.

Given the neurological presentation, the possibility of a primary CNS malignancy was contemplated. Nonetheless, brain MRI failed to show any abnormalities, and cytological assessment of the CSF did not detect malignant cells. Although the soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) was mildly elevated, this parameter alone did not substantiate a diagnosis of CNS lymphoma.

Autoimmune conditions were also considered but subsequently ruled out, due to a lack of hallmark systemic features and negative autoantibody screening. Neurosarcoidosis was part of the differential because early MRI changes may be absent; however, follow-up laboratory data made this diagnosis unlikely. The slight increase in sIL-2R was deemed a non-specific observation.

Ultimately, the patient’s clinical presentation was consistent with a definitive diagnosis of Behçet’s disease (BD), as evidenced by recurrent fever and fatigue, oral aphthae (two points), genital aphthosis (two points), and a rash resembling folliculitis (one point), yielding a total of five points as per the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (a score of four or more establishes a definite diagnosis). Genital aphthosis, in particular, is highly indicative of BD, thus strongly supporting this diagnosis. With infectious, neoplastic, and autoimmune disorders excluded, a final diagnosis of acute neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) was determined, characterized by CNS manifestations in the setting of mucocutaneous lesions.

Treatment:

Upon admission (day 1), clinicians suspected the presence of an underlying inflammatory disorder, though its specific origin was indeterminate. Cefepime was empirically prescribed after obtaining blood cultures, aiming to address a possible bacterial cause even in the absence of a clear infectious source.

By the third day, the patient developed increased headache and vertigo, raising concerns for central nervous system inflammation or infection. Empirical acyclovir was started, but discontinued once laboratory evaluations excluded HSV and VZV encephalitis.

On day 8, initiation of colchicine therapy occurred after systemic Behçet's disease and acute neuro-Behçet's disease were diagnosed. Topical corticosteroid ointment was used to address oral aphthous ulcers, whereas silver sulfadiazine cream was applied to genital and skin lesions. The management of SIADH consisted of conservative fluid restriction (<800 mL/day), with daily monitoring of serum sodium and careful avoidance of rapid correction of sodium. Neither hypertonic saline nor vasopressin receptor antagonists were utilized.

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient's fever subsided and his appetite increased the day following initiation of colchicine therapy. During the subsequent two weeks, his symptoms—including vertigo, generalized malaise, hiccups, and mucocutaneous manifestations—steadily improved. By the sixteenth day, his serum sodium had returned to normal at 136 mmol/L, and ADH had decreased to 1.8 pg/mL. Levels of myogenic enzymes also normalized.

Notably, systemic corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive agents were not utilized at any stage of his treatment. Following a one-month hospital admission—during which acute phase recovery occurred, hyponatremia was meticulously monitored and addressed, and rehabilitation was conducted for his physical decline—the patient was discharged. Colchicine treatment was maintained for approximately four years, during which time neither BD nor neurological symptoms recurred. After stabilization of his BD, biguanide therapy was started, resulting in sustained satisfactory glycemic control.

Discussion:

This case featured the concurrent identification of systemic BD and acute neuro-BD, a rare clinical occurrence. It further illustrated that acute neuro-BD may be complicated by the development of SIADH. The observed improvement of mucocutaneous manifestations, neurological symptoms, and hyponatraemia after initiating colchicine monotherapy points to a possible direct relationship among BD, neuro-BD, and SIADH. Clinicians should entertain the possibility of acute neuro-BD in BD patients presenting with fever or headache in conjunction with hyponatraemia.

Of note, this case involved a simultaneous diagnosis of systemic BD and acute neuro-BD, substantiated by both mucocutaneous and central nervous system findings. Ordinarily, major organ involvement such as CNS lesions tends to develop approximately 4 years after BD is first identified, and the presence of neurological symptoms at onset is rare, occurring in less than 5% of cases. Only about 10% of affected individuals experience headaches directly attributable to neuro-BD. Consequently, initially diagnosing neuro-BD in this context was particularly challenging. Typically, acute neuro-BD manifests as a self-limited meningoencephalitis, which may or may not be associated with focal neurological signs. In this patient, brain MRI did not demonstrate abnormalities in classic regions affected in BD, including the hypothalamus. Acute neuro-BD may present with aseptic meningitis without parenchymal brain involvement, and a normal MRI early in the course does not rule out the disease. The absence of radiographic findings further hindered the diagnostic approach. Although HLA-B51 testing was completed, it did not aid in reaching a diagnosis. This corresponds to international BD diagnostic guidelines, which prioritize clinical presentation over HLA-B51 status. This report underscores the importance of considering neuro-BD when patients with mucocutaneous lesions develop vague neurological complaints.

At the outset of the differential diagnosis, infection was given primary consideration as the cause of CNS inflammation, prompted by the presentation of an unexplained fever, neurological manifestations, and hyponatraemia; despite this, no causative pathogen was found. Additional etiologies for CNS inflammation, including connective tissue disorders and neoplastic processes, were evaluated, and sarcoidosis as well as hematologic malignancies were included in the diagnostic workup. Consequently, we undertook a broad initial assessment by checking autoimmune serologic markers, serum ACE, and sIL-2R as part of our screening protocols. These investigations were conducted in a targeted manner with the aim of excluding certain diagnoses, rather than stemming from a strong clinical suspicion. No supporting evidence emerged from clinical presentation, imaging studies, or CSF analysis for these diseases, and the laboratory findings did not assist significantly in reaching a diagnosis. Serum ACE is known to have poor sensitivity for diagnosing sarcoidosis, while sIL-2R may aid in assessing malignant lymphoma; however, minimal increases lack specificity and may simply indicate an inflammatory process. In this instance, the lack of genital ulceration during the early course complicated the consideration of BD, necessitating a comprehensive diagnostic approach. This scenario underscores the risk associated with nonspecific testing and reinforces the necessity for clinicians to base such evaluations on pertinent clinical indications, as well as to interpret findings judiciously.

While cases of SIADH induced by medications such as cyclophosphamide have been documented in individuals with Behçet’s disease (BD), the link between acute neuro-BD and SIADH is not well established. Neuro-BD involves inflammation of the central nervous system, which could play a role in abnormal antidiuretic hormone (ADH) regulation. Evidence from several investigations indicates that interleukin-6 (IL-6), a proinflammatory cytokine commonly found at increased levels in neuroinflammatory conditions, is related to heightened ADH secretion. In BD, irregular activation of monocytes can lead to unchecked IL-6 production, and raised concentrations of IL-6 have been observed in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) of patients experiencing acute neuro-BD. Such observations imply a possible contribution of IL-6–driven inflammation to the development of SIADH in this context. Although IL-6 concentrations were not evaluated in the serum or CSF in this particular patient, the substantially increased serum CRP level might indicate an IL-6–related inflammatory process. SIADH can arise from multiple etiologies—including CNS inflammation, lung disorders, cancers, and specific drugs—but the evidence in this report is insufficient to definitively correlate SIADH with acute neuro-BD. The timing of symptom onset may simply represent a general consequence of CNS inflammation rather than a mechanism unique to this disease.

This case further demonstrates the possible adverse effects of hyponatraemia resulting from SIADH during acute neuro-BD. Prior literature has indicated that persistent hiccups may be a significant clinical indicator of severe hyponatraemia due to SIADH associated with CNS inflammation, including cases of meningitis. Furthermore, creatine kinase (CK) levels were assessed as part of the workup for fever, arthralgia, and general fatigue, symptoms that can be linked to inflammatory or metabolic injury to muscle. This testing identified rhabdomyolysis, which is a documented yet frequently neglected complication of severe hyponatraemia and not commonly screened for. While these clinical features are recognized, they remain frequently underrecognized consequences of marked hyponatraemia. It is important for clinicians to recognize that seemingly unrelated findings, like hiccups or rhabdomyolysis, may indicate underlying disturbances in electrolytes, especially when encountered in the setting of neuroinflammatory disease.

Corticosteroids are typically regarded as the cornerstone of therapy for acute neuro-BD, with the majority of patients showing a positive response. Nonetheless, there have been reports of cases limited to meningoencephalitis-like manifestations resolving without intervention. In this instance, administration of colchicine alone resulted in improvement of both mucocutaneous and neurological symptoms, along with correction of hyponatraemia. The anti-inflammatory mechanism of colchicine involves inhibition of neutrophil chemotaxis and activation, as well as suppression of cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-6. While colchicine is routinely utilized to manage mucocutaneous features of BD, systematic reviews indicate it is not effective for acute neuro-BD, for which immunosuppressants are typically necessary for effective management. Indeed, literature demonstrates that colchicine is most often given alongside corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive medications rather than on its own. While the possibility of spontaneous remission in this case cannot be ruled out, these observations imply that colchicine could represent a potential therapeutic alternative for acute neuro-BD and associated issues such as SIADH, especially in situations where steroid use is undesirable or contraindicated.

Conclusion:

We report a unique instance in which both BD and acute neuro-BD were diagnosed simultaneously, with SIADH presenting as a complicating factor in the context of neuro-BD. This report underscores that SIADH can develop as a complication during acute neuro-BD, emphasizing the necessity for healthcare professionals to recognize this potential link. Monotherapy with colchicine resulted in prompt resolution of mucocutaneous signs, neurologic symptoms, and hyponatraemia, indicating that colchicine could serve as an effective initial treatment strategy in early neuro-BD. For BD patients exhibiting general central nervous system complaints—such as fever and headache—alongside hyponatraemia, clinicians are advised to maintain a strong suspicion for acute neuro-BD complicated by SIADH.
