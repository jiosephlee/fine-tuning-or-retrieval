Title: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

Eiichi Kakehi, Yukako Matsumoto, Shigehisa Sakurai, Kazuhiko Kotani

Abstract:

Summary: We describe an uncommon presentation of acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) that was identified at the same time as systemic Behçet’s disease (BD), meeting criteria for definite BD, and was further complicated by the development of syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). A male patient in his 60s presented with fever, headache, and mucocutaneous signs including oral aphthous ulcers, folliculitis-like skin lesions, genital ulcers, and low sodium levels. The diagnosis of BD was established on the basis of these mucocutaneous features. Magnetic resonance imaging of the brain showed no abnormalities, while cerebrospinal fluid examination revealed aseptic meningitis, which was consistent with acute neuro-BD. Additional laboratory assessment met the established criteria for SIADH. Treatment with colchicine alone resulted in resolution of both the mucocutaneous and neurological symptoms as well as normalization of sodium levels, obviating the need for corticosteroids or additional immunosuppressive therapies. This report indicates a potential link between acute neuro-BD and SIADH, highlighting the need to consider simultaneous neurological and endocrine involvement in patients with a new diagnosis of BD.

Background:

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a persistent inflammatory condition affecting multiple organ systems, recognized by repeated episodes of oral and genital ulcerations, uveitis, and dermatological manifestations. Neurological complications, termed neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), develop in roughly 10%–20% of individuals and tend to arise several years post-initial BD diagnosis. Acute presentations of neuro-BD typically manifest as meningoencephalitis with parenchymal involvement, most frequently affecting the brainstem. Although it is more common for neurological symptoms to appear later, simultaneous diagnosis of BD and neuro-BD is uncommon. In such scenarios, it is essential for healthcare providers to evaluate both mucocutaneous indicators and neurological complaints, such as headaches, cognitive or behavioral disturbances, psychiatric symptoms, and localized neurological impairments.

The syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) is a recognized etiology of hyponatraemia, frequently resulting from central nervous system (CNS) inflammation, neoplasms, or exposure to particular drugs. Due to the inflammatory processes underlying acute neuro-BD, patients may be susceptible to SIADH. Although associations between SIADH and BD patients undergoing agents like cyclophosphamide have been documented, cases where SIADH arises exclusively from CNS inflammation associated with acute neuro-BD—without medication involvement—are not well established.

Herein, we describe an unusual case where BD and acute neuro-BD were identified at the same time, with subsequent development of SIADH. This report underscores the necessity of recognizing SIADH as a possible complication of acute neuro-BD, especially in individuals who develop non-specific neurological findings and hyponatraemia.

Case presentation:

A man of Asian descent in his 60s arrived at the emergency department following a 3-day period characterized by fever, headache, general malaise, decreased appetite, ongoing hiccups, and joint pain involving both his shoulders and ankles. He further described experiencing oral aphthous ulcers.

His past medical history was significant for type 2 diabetes mellitus, though he was not under any treatment due to limited disease awareness and a lack of proper dietary or exercise habits. There was no notable family history, and none of his relatives were diagnosed with BD. He drank alcohol on occasion, did not smoke, had no recent travel history, and reported no contact with animals or individuals who were ill. His occupation was in the metalworking field, and he had not had sexual activity recently.

Upon assessment, the patient was awake but showed signs of tiredness. His vital signs were as follows: temperature measured at 38.1°C, blood pressure recorded at 135/73 mm Hg, pulse rate at 119 beats per minute with a regular rhythm, respiratory rate of 19 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation at 96% while breathing ambient air. On physical examination, aphthous ulcers were noted in the oral cavity, along with folliculitis-like eruptions present on the facial and truncal areas (see figure 1A, B, and C). Cardiopulmonary and abdominal exams were within normal limits. Muscle strength, as evaluated by manual muscle testing, was preserved in both upper and lower limbs. There was an absence of peripheral edema, and clinical evaluation suggested a euvolemic state. The patient was hospitalized for additional diagnostic work-up.

Investigations:

Initial laboratory analysis showed significant hyponatraemia (124 mmol/L) alongside increased creatine kinase (1349 U/L), aspartate aminotransferase (77 U/L), alanine aminotransferase (64 U/L), C-reactive protein (CRP) (180 mg/L; reference range, <3 mg/L), and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (70 mm/hour; reference, <15 mm/hour), while renal function remained within normal parameters. The patient was also noted to have hyperglycaemia (plasma glucose 14.2 mmol/L) and a raised HbA1c of 8.2%, reflective of inadequate glycaemic management. Testing for hepatitis B and C was negative.

Endocrinological assessment demonstrated thyroid-stimulating hormone at 1.11 mIU/L (reference, 0.50–5.00 mIU/L), free T4 at 1.25 ng/dL (reference, 0.90–1.70 ng/dL), and serum cortisol at 42 µg/dL (reference, >6 µg/dL), all within normal limits. Serum osmolality was found to be decreased (267 mOsm/kg; reference, <270 mOsm/kg), in contrast to an elevated urine osmolality (900 mOsm/kg; reference, >300 mOsm/kg) and increased urinary sodium (88 mmol/L; reference, >20 mmol/L), suggesting an abnormality in water clearance. Notably, antidiuretic hormone (ADH) was elevated (22.4 pg/mL) in the setting of hypo-osmolar hyponatraemia. ECG, chest radiography, and computed tomography scans of the head, chest, and abdomen were all unremarkable. Collectively, these data were compatible with a diagnosis of SIADH.

By the third day, the patient's headache and vertigo had intensified. Despite the absence of neck stiffness or cranial nerve deficits, central nervous system (CNS) inflammation was considered. MRI of the brain showed no notable abnormalities within the hypothalamic area or elsewhere in the brain (figure 2). Analysis of the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) indicated a mildly raised opening pressure (17 cmH₂O), an elevated protein concentration (0.97 g/L), and slight lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL). Cultures and assays of the CSF were negative for bacterial and mycobacterial organisms as well as herpes simplex virus (HSV), varicella zoster virus (VZV), and cryptococcal antigen. No malignant cells were identified on CSF cytology.

Additional assessments were undertaken to detect possible systemic conditions. Testing for sarcoidosis demonstrated a normal lysozyme value (5.1 µg/mL; reference range 5–10) and a normal angiotensin-converting enzyme level (11.4 U/L; reference range 7–25). Evaluation for hematologic malignancy showed an increased soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) (704 U/mL; reference, 122–496 U/mL), while the human T-cell leukemia virus type 1 antibody was negative.

On the fifth day, he developed a painful ulcer on the scrotum (figure 1D). Coupled with oral aphthous ulcers and skin eruptions resembling folliculitis, these clinical features raised suspicion for systemic Behçet’s disease (BD). The pathergy reaction was negative. No signs of uveitis were found upon ophthalmologic assessment. HLA-B51 testing returned positive.

Differential diagnosis:

Upon admission, the concurrent presence of fever, headache, general malaise, and hyponatremia raised suspicion for either a central nervous system infection or a systemic inflammatory condition. Viral meningoencephalitis, particularly due to HSV or VZV, was the initial leading consideration. Nonetheless, cerebrospinal fluid studies for both viral antigen and antibody returned negative, thereby effectively ruling out these pathogens. Bacterial meningitis was also entertained as a potential diagnosis, but the lack of a neutrophilic predominance, presence of only mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, and negative CSF cultures argued against it. Furthermore, acid-fast staining and tuberculosis DNA testing in CSF were negative, thereby excluding tuberculous meningitis.

A primary CNS neoplasm remained on the differential because of ongoing neurological complaints; however, no abnormalities were detected on brain MRI, and CSF cytology was negative for malignant cells. The soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) level was only modestly raised, which on its own was not adequate to confirm CNS lymphoma.

Consideration was also given to autoimmune disorders, but these were dismissed due to a lack of typical systemic symptoms and negative results for screening autoantibody panels. Neurosarcoidosis was not excluded initially, particularly since early MRI may appear unremarkable, but later laboratory results diminished the probability of this diagnosis. The slight increase in sIL-2R was interpreted as non-specific.

The diagnosis of definite Behçet’s disease (BD) was established on the basis of clinical presentation, which included fever, fatigue, oral aphthae (two points), genital aphthosis (two points), and a folliculitis-like eruption (one point). Utilizing the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease, this patient achieved a score of five points, meeting the threshold for a definitive diagnosis (≥4). Notably, genital aphthosis represents a highly specific marker for BD, supporting the diagnosis. Following comprehensive exclusion of infectious, neoplastic, and autoimmune etiologies, a diagnosis of acute neuro-Behçet’s disease was made, characterized by CNS involvement associated with mucocutaneous symptoms.

Treatment:

On the first day of hospitalization, clinicians considered an underlying inflammatory disorder, though its precise cause remained unidentified. Broad-spectrum antibiotics with cefepime were started empirically after obtaining blood cultures, aiming to address a possible bacterial process in the absence of a clear site of infection.

By the third day, the patient’s headache and vertigo had intensified, raising concern for central nervous system inflammation or infection. Empiric acyclovir was given, but discontinued once investigations excluded HSV and VZV encephalitis.

On the eighth day, colchicine therapy commenced after systemic BD and acute neuro-BD were diagnosed. Topical corticosteroid ointment was prescribed for oral aphthous ulcers. Silver sulfadiazine cream was used to treat both genital ulcers and skin lesions. Syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) was managed with conservative measures, specifically fluid restriction to less than 800 mL per day, daily monitoring of serum sodium, and careful avoidance of rapid sodium correction. There was no need for hypertonic saline or vasopressin receptor antagonists.

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient's fever subsided and his appetite increased the day following the initiation of colchicine. Over the next two weeks, his symptoms—including vertigo, general malaise, hiccups, and mucocutaneous lesions—showed gradual improvement. By day 16, serum sodium had risen to a normal value of 136 mmol/L, while ADH concentration dropped to 1.8 pg/mL. Myogenic enzyme concentrations also normalized.

Notably, systemic corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive agents were not administered throughout his treatment. Following a one-month hospitalization—during which acute symptoms resolved, hyponatremia was closely monitored and addressed, and rehabilitation was implemented for physical deconditioning—the patient was discharged. Colchicine treatment continued for about four years, during which there was no recurrence of BD or neurological issues. After stabilization of his BD, biguanide therapy was commenced, and glycemic control has been successfully maintained since.

Discussion:

This report describes a unique instance where both systemic Behçet's disease (BD) and acute neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) were identified concurrently, an occurrence that is seldom encountered. Additionally, the case illustrates that acute neuro-BD may be associated with the development of SIADH. Improvement of mucocutaneous signs, neurological symptoms, and hyponatremia following treatment with colchicine alone indicates a potential direct link between BD, neuro-BD, and SIADH. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for acute neuro-BD in BD patients presenting with fever or headache alongside hyponatremia.

This particular case stands out due to the concurrent diagnosis of systemic BD and acute neuro-BD, as indicated by mucocutaneous involvement and central nervous system findings. Ordinarily, significant organ manifestations such as central nervous system lesions tend to appear approximately four years after BD is first diagnosed; neurological symptoms at presentation occur infrequently, affecting less than 5% of individuals. Headaches attributed specifically to neuro-BD affect only about 10% of cases. These factors contributed to the inherent difficulty in promptly diagnosing neuro-BD in this scenario. Typically, acute neuro-BD presents as a self-limited meningoencephalitis, sometimes accompanied by focal neurological impairments. In this patient, MRI of the brain was unremarkable, showing no typical BD-related lesions, including the hypothalamic area. Acute neuro-BD can manifest as aseptic meningitis without brain parenchymal involvement, and an initially normal brain MRI does not exclude the diagnosis, especially early in the disease process. The absence of radiological abnormalities further complicated diagnosis in this case. Although HLA-B51 analysis was carried out, it did not aid in confirming the diagnosis, which is consistent with international BD classification that emphasizes clinical manifestations over genetic markers. This case underscores the need to consider neuro-BD in patients with mucocutaneous findings who develop vague neurological complaints.

At the outset of our differential diagnosis, we initially favored infection as the etiology for CNS inflammation due to the presence of unexplained fever, neurologic manifestations, and hyponatremia; despite this, no definite pathogen was detected. Alternative etiologies such as connective tissue disorders and neoplastic processes were also considered, with sarcoidosis and hematological malignancies incorporated into our differential. Thus, our preliminary comprehensive assessment included screening for autoimmune serologies, along with measurement of serum ACE and sIL-2R. These investigations were utilized narrowly within a framework of ruling out conditions, rather than because of a high degree of suspicion for any particular disorder. Ultimately, neither clinical presentation, radiological evaluation, nor CSF analysis yielded evidence supportive of these conditions, and the diagnostic tests did not significantly aid in identifying the underlying disease. It is important to note that ACE exhibits limited sensitivity for diagnosing sarcoidosis, and while sIL-2R may assist in assessing malignant lymphoma, mild increases are non-specific and generally denote inflammatory activity. In this situation, the initial lack of genital ulceration complicated an early diagnosis of BD, resulting in an extended differential. This approach underlines the necessity to avoid excessive testing and underscores the recommendation that such investigations should be tailored to the clinical scenario and interpreted judiciously.

While drug-induced SIADH has been documented in individuals with BD treated with agents like cyclophosphamide, the link between SIADH and acute neuro-BD is not yet clearly elucidated. Neuro-BD is defined by inflammation within the central nervous system, which could potentially disrupt normal ADH regulation. Multiple reports have identified a relationship between increased levels of interleukin-6 (IL-6)—a proinflammatory cytokine commonly elevated in neuroinflammatory diseases—and enhanced ADH secretion. In BD, dysregulated activation of monocytes results in the autonomous production of IL-6, with heightened concentrations of this cytokine found in the cerebrospinal fluid of patients during acute neuro-BD episodes. These observations imply that IL-6–driven inflammation might play a role in the pathogenesis of SIADH under such circumstances. In this particular case, although IL-6 was not measured in either serum or CSF, the substantially increased serum C-reactive protein (CRP) level could indicate an underlying IL-6–related inflammatory process. SIADH has been linked to numerous etiologies, including but not limited to CNS inflammation, lung disorders, malignancies, and certain drugs; however, the evidence from this case is insufficient to definitively connect SIADH to acute neuro-BD. The chronological coincidence noted here may simply represent a nonspecific effect of CNS inflammation rather than a mechanism unique to the disease itself.

This case highlights possible complications associated with hyponatraemia due to SIADH occurring during acute neuro-BD. Prior studies have indicated that persistent hiccups can be a significant clinical indicator of severe hyponatraemia resulting from SIADH secondary to central nervous system inflammation, such as meningitis. Furthermore, as part of the initial workup for fever, arthralgia, and generalized fatigue—symptoms that may suggest inflammatory or metabolic muscle injury—we measured CK, which demonstrated rhabdomyolysis. This represents a recognized but frequently overlooked consequence of severe hyponatraemia that is not commonly assessed. These presentations, while documented, are often underestimated in their relevance to profound hyponatraemia. Physicians should recognize that symptoms like hiccups or rhabdomyolysis, which may seem unrelated, could point to underlying disturbances in electrolytes, particularly in patients with neuroinflammatory diseases.

Corticosteroids represent the standard initial therapy for acute neuro-BD, with most individuals exhibiting a positive clinical response. Nonetheless, some patients who present exclusively with symptoms akin to meningoencephalitis have experienced spontaneous symptom resolution. In this reported case, administration of colchicine alone resulted in improvement of both mucocutaneous and neurological features, as well as correction of hyponatraemia. Colchicine’s anti-inflammatory properties derive from its ability to impede neutrophil migration and activity, in addition to inhibiting cytokine synthesis, including IL-1β and IL-6. While colchicine is commonly employed in managing mucocutaneous lesions in BD, evidence from systematic reviews indicates that colchicine is not effective for the acute neurologic manifestations of BD, and typically, effective disease control relies upon immunosuppressive medications. Indeed, in published cases, colchicine has typically been combined with corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants, rather than given alone. Although spontaneous remission cannot be entirely ruled out in this situation, our observations raise the possibility that colchicine may offer benefit in treating acute neuro-BD and associated complications like SIADH, especially in scenarios where corticosteroids are unsuitable or cannot be used.

Conclusion:

We reported an uncommon presentation of BD occurring simultaneously with acute neuro-BD, where SIADH developed as a complication of neuro-BD. This case illustrates that SIADH can arise secondary to acute neuro-BD and underscores the necessity for clinicians to recognize this possible link. Treatment with colchicine alone resulted in prompt resolution of mucocutaneous and neurological symptoms, as well as correction of hyponatraemia, indicating that colchicine could serve as an effective initial treatment in the early stage of neuro-BD. For patients with BD who exhibit non-specific central nervous system complaints—such as fever and headache—alongside hyponatraemia, practitioners should be vigilant for acute neuro-BD when SIADH is also present.
