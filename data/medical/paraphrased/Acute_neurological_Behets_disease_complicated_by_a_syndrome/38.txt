Title: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

Eiichi Kakehi, Yukako Matsumoto, Shigehisa Sakurai, Kazuhiko Kotani

Abstract:

Summary: We describe an uncommon presentation of acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) appearing simultaneously with systemic Behçet’s disease (BD), which was classified as definite BD, and complicated by syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). The patient, a man in his 60s, was admitted with fever, headache, and mucocutaneous symptoms such as oral aphthae, folliculitis-like eruptions, genital ulcers, and hyponatraemia. Diagnosis of BD was made based on mucocutaneous features. Although brain MRI was unremarkable, cerebrospinal fluid examination indicated aseptic meningitis, consistent with acute neuro-BD. Laboratory analysis satisfied diagnostic criteria for SIADH. Treatment with colchicine alone resulted in resolution of mucocutaneous signs, neurological symptoms, and hyponatraemia, eliminating the need for corticosteroids or additional immunosuppressive therapy. This case points to a potential link between acute neuro-BD and SIADH, emphasizing the need to identify concurrent neurological and endocrine disorders in patients newly diagnosed with BD.

Background:

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a persistent, multisystem inflammatory condition defined by repeated episodes of oral and genital ulceration, uveitis, and cutaneous manifestations. Neurological manifestations, termed neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), affect around 10%–20% of individuals with BD and generally manifest years following the initial diagnosis. Acute neuro-BD is most often characterized by meningoencephalitis and lesions within the brain parenchyma, with the brainstem frequently involved. While simultaneous presentation is uncommon, BD and neuro-BD are seldom diagnosed at the same time. When this occurs, it is important for clinicians to evaluate both dermatological and neurological findings, such as cephalgia, alterations in behavior, psychiatric manifestations, and focal neurologic signs.

Syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) represents a recognized etiology for hyponatraemia, commonly resulting from central nervous system (CNS) inflammatory processes, neoplasia, or specific medications. Due to the inflammatory milieu of acute neuro-BD, individuals with this condition may be predisposed to developing SIADH. Although SIADH has been documented in association with BD patients undergoing therapies like cyclophosphamide, its development independent of medication exposure and as a direct consequence of CNS inflammation in acute neuro-BD is not well established.

Here, we present an unusual case featuring the simultaneous onset of BD and acute neuro-BD, further complicated by SIADH. This report underlines the need to recognize SIADH as a possible complication of acute neuro-BD, especially in patients who exhibit non-specific neurological complaints and hyponatraemia.

Case presentation:

A man of Asian descent in his 60s arrived at the emergency department reporting three days of fever, headache, generalized fatigue, decreased appetite, ongoing hiccups, and joint pain localized to the shoulders and ankles. He additionally described experiencing oral aphthous ulcers.

His past medical history was notable for type 2 diabetes mellitus; however, he was not on any therapy due to limited knowledge regarding his diagnosis and an absence of healthy diet or exercise routines. There was no significant family history, including no known relatives with BD. He reported infrequent alcohol intake, denied smoking, had no recent travel, and had not come into contact with animals or sick persons. His occupation was in the metalworking sector, and he had not had recent sexual contacts.

Upon evaluation, the patient was awake but showed signs of fatigue. His recorded vital signs were: temperature 38.1°C, blood pressure 135/73 mm Hg, regular heart rate at 119 beats per minute, respiratory rate of 19 per minute, and oxygen saturation at 96% on ambient air. Inspection identified oral aphthae and folliculitis-like eruptions on the face and trunk (figure 1A, B, and C). The cardiopulmonary and abdominal examinations were without notable findings. Manual muscle strength testing demonstrated normal strength in both upper and lower limbs. Peripheral edema was absent, and clinical assessment indicated euvolemia. The patient was admitted for additional diagnostic workup.

Investigations:

Initial laboratory assessment showed significant hyponatraemia at 124 mmol/L, raised creatine kinase measuring 1349 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase at 77 U/L, alanine aminotransferase at 64 U/L, increased C-reactive protein (CRP) at 180 mg/L (reference value <3 mg/L), and an erythrocyte sedimentation rate of 70 mm/hour (reference <15 mm/hour). Renal function was within normal limits. The patient exhibited hyperglycaemia (plasma glucose 14.2 mmol/L) and a high HbA1c of 8.2%, signifying inadequate glycaemic regulation. Results for hepatitis B and C markers were negative.

Endocrine studies showed thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) at 1.11 mIU/L (reference range 0.50–5.00 mIU/L), free T4 at 1.25 ng/dL (reference range 0.90–1.70 ng/dL), and serum cortisol at 42 µg/dL (reference range >6 µg/dL), all within normal parameters. Serum osmolality was reduced to 267 mOsm/kg (reference <270 mOsm/kg), while urine osmolality was markedly elevated at 900 mOsm/kg (reference >300 mOsm/kg), accompanied by increased urinary sodium at 88 mmol/L (reference >20 mmol/L), indicative of defective water elimination. Despite hypo-osmolar hyponatraemia, antidiuretic hormone (ADH) was elevated at 22.4 pg/mL. Electrocardiogram results were normal, as were findings from chest X-ray and CT scans of the head, chest, and abdomen. Collectively, these results were consistent with a diagnosis of SIADH.

By the third day, the patient experienced increased severity of headache and vertigo. Despite the absence of neck rigidity and cranial nerve deficits, central nervous system inflammation was considered. MRI of the brain demonstrated no significant lesions affecting the hypothalamus or other areas (figure 2). Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) evaluation showed a slightly raised opening pressure (17 cmH₂O), elevated protein concentration (0.97 g/L), and mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL). Microbiological assays, including cultures and tests for bacteria, mycobacteria, HSV, VZV, and cryptococcal antigen, returned negative results. Cytological assessment of CSF revealed no evidence of malignancy.

Additional workup was conducted to rule out underlying systemic conditions. Assessment for sarcoidosis yielded normal lysozyme (5.1 µg/mL; reference range 5–10) and angiotensin-converting enzyme levels (11.4 U/L; reference range 7–25). Investigation for hematologic malignancies showed elevated soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) (704 U/mL; reference range 122–496 U/mL), while serology for human T-cell leukemia virus type 1 antibody was negative.

On the fifth day, a painful ulcer developed on the scrotum (figure 1D). Alongside oral aphthous lesions and folliculitis-like skin eruptions, these manifestations indicated the possibility of a systemic type of BD. The patient had no pinprick hypersensitivity. Ocular assessment showed absence of uveitis. HLA-B51 testing was positive.

Differential diagnosis:

Upon admission, the constellation of fever, headache, generalized malaise, and hyponatraemia raised suspicion for either a central nervous system infection or an underlying systemic inflammatory condition. Viral meningoencephalitis, particularly due to HSV or VZV, was initially suspected. Nevertheless, cerebrospinal fluid analysis did not detect viral antigens or antibodies, thereby ruling out these etiologies. Bacterial meningitis was also considered, but was excluded owing to the lack of neutrophilic dominance in the CSF, only mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, and negative CSF culture results. Tuberculous meningitis was excluded as both acid-fast staining and tuberculosis DNA assays were negative.

The possibility of primary CNS malignancy was explored in light of the neurological manifestations. However, no abnormalities were identified on MRI of the brain, and CSF cytology revealed no malignant cells. Although there was a mild elevation in sIL-2R levels, this isolated finding did not substantiate a diagnosis of CNS lymphoma.

Potential autoimmune disorders were assessed, but the absence of typical systemic symptoms and negative autoantibody screens led to their exclusion. Neurosarcoidosis remained a consideration since early-stage MRI findings may be unremarkable, but subsequent laboratory results diminished the likelihood of this diagnosis. The mild increase in sIL-2R was deemed non-specific.

Definite BD was diagnosed for this patient based on clinical presentation, which included fever, fatigue, oral aphthae (allocated two points), genital aphthosis (allocated two points), and a folliculitis-like skin eruption (allocated one point). Based on the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease, he accrued a total of five points (where a score of four or higher designates definite disease). Notably, genital aphthosis is recognized as a distinctive indicator of BD, and the combination of findings was strongly supportive of this diagnosis. Following exclusion of infectious, malignant, and autoimmune etiologies, a definitive diagnosis of acute neuro-Behçet’s disease was made, characterized by CNS involvement concomitant with mucocutaneous symptoms.

Treatment:

Upon admission (day 1), clinicians suspected an underlying inflammatory process, though the precise cause was not determined. Empirical cefepime therapy was commenced after obtaining blood cultures, aimed at covering possible bacterial pathogens in the absence of a definitive site of infection.

By day 3, the patient exhibited increasing headache and vertigo, raising concern for central nervous system inflammation or infection. Acyclovir was provided empirically, but later discontinued once laboratory studies excluded HSV and VZV encephalitis.

On day 8, colchicine was prescribed after identifying systemic BD and acute neuro-BD. Oral aphthae were managed with a topical corticosteroid ointment, while silver sulfadiazine cream was applied to genital ulcers and skin lesions. SIADH was addressed by restricting fluids to less than 800 mL daily, closely monitoring serum sodium levels, and avoiding rapid sodium correction. The use of hypertonic saline or vasopressin receptor antagonists was unnecessary.

Outcome and follow-up:

Following initiation of colchicine, the patient’s fever subsided and his appetite increased the next day. Over the subsequent two weeks, his clinical manifestations—vertigo, generalized malaise, hiccups, and mucocutaneous lesions—showed gradual improvement. On day 16, his serum sodium level normalized at 136 mmol/L, and antidiuretic hormone (ADH) decreased to 1.8 pg/mL. Myogenic enzyme concentrations likewise returned to within normal limits.

Notably, neither systemic corticosteroids nor other immunosuppressive agents were administered during treatment. The patient was released after one month of hospitalization, during which he recovered from the acute episode, hyponatremia was closely observed and treated, and rehabilitation was provided to address diminished physical capability. Colchicine therapy continued for around four years; during this period, no relapse of Behçet’s disease (BD) or neurological symptoms occurred. After BD became stable, biguanide medication was introduced, resulting in sustained glycemic control.

Discussion:

This report describes an uncommon situation in which systemic Behçet’s disease (BD) and acute neuro-BD were identified at the same time. Additionally, it highlights that SIADH can occur as a complication of acute neuro-BD. The resolution of skin and mucosal symptoms, neurological signs, and low sodium levels after starting colchicine alone implies a potential direct link between BD, neuro-BD, and SIADH. Clinicians should be alert to the possibility of acute neuro-BD in patients with BD who develop fever or headache alongside hyponatraemia.

Simultaneous detection of systemic BD and acute neuro-BD in this patient was established by the presence of characteristic skin and mucosal changes as well as CNS involvement. Ordinarily, CNS manifestations and other major organ complications tend to emerge roughly four years following an initial BD diagnosis, while neurologic findings at onset are rare, reported in less than 5% of cases. Furthermore, only about 10% of individuals experience headaches attributable to neuro-BD. For these reasons, the diagnosis of neuro-BD at presentation in this case posed a particular challenge. The classic clinical course for acute neuro-BD includes self-limited meningoencephalitis, with or without localized neurologic deficits. In this instance, brain MRI did not reveal any abnormalities at expected sites, such as the hypothalamic region. It is recognized that acute neuro-BD may present as aseptic meningitis in the absence of brain parenchymal lesions, and normal neuroimaging—especially during early stages—does not rule out the diagnosis. This absence of MRI findings added further complexity to diagnosing the condition. Although HLA-B51 testing was conducted, it was not instrumental in establishing the diagnosis; this is consistent with international BD diagnostic criteria, which emphasize clinical features over genetic testing. This case underscores the need to contemplate neuro-BD in individuals who display mucocutaneous signs together with ambiguous neurological complaints.

At the outset of our differential diagnosis, we focused on infectious etiologies for CNS inflammation due to the patient’s unexplained fever, neurological manifestations, and hyponatraemia, but no specific infectious agent was identified. Additional potential causes of CNS inflammation, such as connective tissue disorders, malignancies, sarcoidosis, and hematologic cancers, were also included in our considerations. Consequently, we conducted screening tests, including autoimmune serologic markers, serum ACE, and sIL-2R, as part of our broad initial workup. These laboratory evaluations were utilized in a limited, exclusionary manner rather than being directed by a high clinical suspicion. There was an absence of supportive clinical features, imaging results, or CSF findings for these conditions, and these tests did not significantly aid in establishing the final diagnosis. Notably, serum ACE is not highly sensitive for detecting sarcoidosis, and while sIL-2R can have some value in investigating malignant lymphoma, minor increases lack specificity and may indicate general inflammatory activity. In the present case, the lack of genital ulcers at the onset of the patient’s illness created uncertainty regarding Behçet's disease, prompting us to maintain an extensive differential diagnosis. This process underlines the necessity of refraining from unnecessary testing and reinforces that diagnostic investigations should be performed when justified by pertinent clinical findings and interpreted carefully.

While SIADH triggered by medications, including cyclophosphamide, has been documented in individuals with BD, the relationship between SIADH and acute neuro-BD remains largely unclear. Neuro-BD involves inflammatory processes within the CNS, which could potentially lead to abnormal ADH regulation. Multiple studies indicate that interleukin-6 (IL-6), a proinflammatory cytokine commonly elevated during neuroinflammatory states, is linked to heightened ADH secretion. In BD, monocyte dysfunction results in spontaneous IL-6 production, and increased IL-6 concentrations have been identified in the CSF of patients during acute neuro-BD episodes. Such observations imply that IL-6–driven inflammation might play a role in SIADH development under these circumstances. Although IL-6 measurements were not available in serum or CSF in the current case, the significantly elevated serum CRP may serve as an indicator of an IL-6–mediated inflammatory process. SIADH has associations with numerous conditions—including CNS inflammation, pulmonary illnesses, malignancies, and certain pharmaceuticals—but current data from this case do not permit confirmation of a direct causative relationship between SIADH and acute neuro-BD. The timing of the events in this report might simply reflect a generalized response to CNS inflammation, rather than a mechanism unique to this disease.

This case further demonstrates the possible complications associated with hyponatraemia resulting from SIADH during acute neuro-BD. Persistent hiccups have previously been identified as a significant clinical indicator of severe hyponatraemia due to SIADH secondary to central nervous system inflammation, such as in cases of meningitis. Additionally, as part of the initial workup for fever, arthralgia, and general fatigue—which can be indicative of inflammatory or metabolic muscle injury—we assessed CK levels and discovered rhabdomyolysis, a recognized but frequently neglected complication of severe hyponatraemia that is not typically screened for. These clinical features are established but often under-recognized consequences of significant hyponatraemia. It is important for clinicians to recognize that symptoms seemingly unrelated to the primary presentation, including hiccups or rhabdomyolysis, may signal electrolyte imbalances, especially in patients with neuroinflammatory disorders.

Corticosteroids remain the primary therapy for acute neuro-BD, with most individuals showing a positive response. Nonetheless, there have been instances where cases characterized only by meningoencephalitis-like features have resolved without intervention. In this particular patient, sole treatment with colchicine led to improvement in both mucocutaneous and neurological manifestations as well as correction of hyponatraemia. Colchicine’s anti-inflammatory action involves blocking neutrophil migration and activation, and curbing cytokine release such as IL-1β and IL-6. While colchicine is frequently administered for mucocutaneous symptoms of BD, systematic reviews indicate that it lacks efficacy against acute neuro-BD, for which immunosuppressive agents are typically required for sufficient disease management. In previously published reports, colchicine has generally been used alongside corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants, not alone. Although the possibility of spontaneous remission cannot be discounted in this scenario, these observations raise the prospect that colchicine could serve as a therapeutic alternative in acute neuro-BD and related complications like SIADH, especially when corticosteroids are contraindicated or not suitable.

Conclusion:

We reported an unusual presentation where Behçet’s disease (BD) and acute neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) were identified at the same time, with neuro-BD being further complicated by the development of SIADH. This case highlights that SIADH can arise as a sequela of acute neuro-BD, underscoring the need for clinicians to be vigilant about this potential link. Treatment with colchicine alone resulted in a swift resolution of the mucocutaneous lesions, neurological symptoms, and hyponatraemia, indicating that colchicine may serve as an effective initial therapy for neuro-BD. For BD patients displaying non-specific central nervous system symptoms—including fever and headache—accompanied by hyponatraemia, clinicians should strongly consider the possibility of acute neuro-BD complicated by SIADH.
