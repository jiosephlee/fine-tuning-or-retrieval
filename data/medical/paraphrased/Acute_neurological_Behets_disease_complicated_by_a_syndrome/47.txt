Title: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

Eiichi Kakehi, Yukako Matsumoto, Shigehisa Sakurai, Kazuhiko Kotani

Abstract:

Summary: We present an uncommon case involving acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) simultaneously diagnosed with systemic Behçet’s disease (BD), classified as definite BD, and complicated by syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). The patient, a man in his 60s, exhibited fever, headache, and mucocutaneous manifestations such as oral aphthae, folliculitis-like skin lesions, genital ulcers, and hyponatraemia. The diagnosis of BD was established based on the mucocutaneous symptoms. Magnetic resonance imaging of the brain was unremarkable; however, cerebrospinal fluid analysis indicated aseptic meningitis, consistent with acute neuro-BD. Laboratory evaluation satisfied diagnostic criteria for SIADH. Treatment with colchicine alone resulted in improvement of mucocutaneous lesions, neurological signs, and correction of hyponatraemia, without utilizing corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive therapies. This case demonstrates a potential correlation between acute neuro-BD and SIADH and emphasizes the need to identify simultaneous neurologic and endocrine features in individuals with new-onset BD.

Background:

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a persistent, multisystem inflammatory condition marked by recurring oral and genital ulcerations, uveitis, and various skin manifestations. Neurological complications, described as neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), arise in roughly 10%–20% of BD patients and commonly appear several years following the original diagnosis. The acute form of neuro-BD generally manifests as meningoencephalitis accompanied by parenchymal brain lesions, with the brainstem being frequently affected. While neuro-BD most often occurs some time after the onset of BD, simultaneous diagnoses of BD and neuro-BD are rare. In these situations, it is essential for clinicians to evaluate both mucocutaneous findings and neurologic symptoms such as headache, behavioral disturbances, psychiatric issues, and focal neurological signs.

Syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) represents a recognized etiology of hyponatremia and is typically associated with central nervous system (CNS) inflammation, cancer, or certain drugs. Given that acute neuro-BD involves CNS inflammation, individuals affected by this condition could have an increased susceptibility to SIADH. Although SIADH has been documented in BD patients undergoing therapy with agents like cyclophosphamide, cases attributed exclusively to acute neuro-BD-related CNS inflammation, in the absence of medication triggers, remain uncertain.

Here, we present an unusual case involving the simultaneous diagnosis of BD and acute neuro-BD complicated by SIADH. This report underscores the need to consider SIADH as a possible complication of acute neuro-BD, especially in patients who exhibit nonspecific neurological symptoms alongside hyponatremia.

Case presentation:

A man of Asian descent in his 60s arrived at the emergency department after experiencing three days of fever, headache, generalized fatigue, decreased appetite, ongoing hiccups, and joint pain involving the shoulders and ankles. He also noted the presence of oral aphthous ulcers.

His past medical history was significant for type 2 diabetes mellitus, which was unmanaged due to his limited understanding of the disease and inadequate adherence to proper diet or exercise. There was no family history of Behçet’s disease (BD). He reported only occasional alcohol use, was a non-smoker, had not recently traveled, and denied contact with animals or individuals who were ill. He was employed in metalworking and had not participated in sexual activity in the recent past.

Upon assessment, the patient was awake but exhibited signs of tiredness. His vital parameters included a body temperature of 38.1°C, blood pressure measuring 135/73 mm Hg, a regular pulse of 119 beats per minute, a respiratory rate of 19 breaths per minute, and an oxygen saturation of 96% breathing ambient air. On physical inspection, oral aphthae were observed alongside folliculitis-like eruptions on both the facial and trunk regions (figure 1A, B, and C). Findings from the cardiopulmonary and abdominal examinations were within normal limits. Muscle strength testing did not show weakness in either the upper or lower limbs. There was no evidence of peripheral edema, and clinical evaluation indicated the patient was euvolemic. Hospital admission was undertaken for additional assessment.

Investigations:

Initial laboratory analysis demonstrated significant hyponatraemia (124 mmol/L), increased creatine kinase (1349 U/L), aspartate aminotransferase (77 U/L), alanine aminotransferase (64 U/L), C-reactive protein (CRP) (180 mg/L; reference, <3 mg/L), and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (70 mm/hour; reference, <15 mm/hour), while renal function remained within normal limits. The patient exhibited hyperglycaemia (plasma glucose 14.2 mmol/L) as well as an elevated HbA1c (8.2%), indicative of suboptimal glycaemic management. Screening for hepatitis B and C was negative.

Endocrine assessment showed thyroid-stimulating hormone at 1.11 mIU/L (reference range 0.50–5.00 mIU/L), free T4 at 1.25 ng/dL (reference range 0.90–1.70 ng/dL), and serum cortisol at 42 µg/dL (reference, >6 µg/dL), all within the normal range. Serum osmolality was decreased (267 mOsm/kg; reference, <270 mOsm/kg) whereas urine osmolality was elevated (900 mOsm/kg, reference, >300 mOsm/kg), with urinary sodium also increased (88 mmol/L; reference, >20 mmol/L), signifying defective water excretion. Notably, despite the presence of hypo-osmolar hyponatraemia, the antidiuretic hormone (ADH) concentration was raised (22.4 pg/mL). The patient’s ECG was unremarkable, and imaging—including chest X-ray and computed tomography of the head, chest, and abdomen—revealed no abnormalities. Collectively, these results were consistent with a diagnosis of SIADH.

By the third day, his headache and vertigo had intensified. Despite the absence of neck rigidity or signs of cranial nerve dysfunction, central nervous system inflammation was considered. MRI of the brain demonstrated no notable pathological changes in the hypothalamic area or other cerebral regions (see figure 2). Analysis of cerebrospinal fluid showed a slightly raised opening pressure of 17 cmH₂O, protein concentration of 0.97 g/L, and a mild lymphocytic pleocytosis at 11 cells/µL. Microbiological tests, including CSF cultures and assays for bacterial and mycobacterial organisms, HSV, VZV, and cryptococcal antigen, yielded negative results. No malignant cells were detected on CSF cytology.

Additional workup for possible underlying systemic conditions was pursued. Evaluation for sarcoidosis indicated normal lysozyme (5.1 µg/mL; reference range 5–10) and angiotensin-converting enzyme levels (11.4 U/L; reference range 7–25). Hematologic malignancy screening identified elevated soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) at 704 U/mL (reference, 122–496 U/mL), but human T-cell leukemia virus type 1 antibody testing was negative.

On the fifth day, a painful ulcer developed in the scrotum (figure 1D). Together with oral aphthae and folliculitis-like skin lesions, these findings were indicative of a systemic manifestation of BD. He showed no hypersensitivity to needle puncture. Eye evaluation found no evidence of uveitis. HLA-B51 testing was positive.

Differential diagnosis:

Upon admission, the presence of fever, headache, general malaise, and hyponatraemia raised the suspicion of a central nervous system infection or a systemic inflammatory disease. The initial differential favored viral meningoencephalitis, especially due to HSV or VZV infection. Nevertheless, cerebrospinal fluid analysis returned negative for both viral antigens and antibodies, thereby ruling out these viral etiologies. Consideration was also given to bacterial meningitis, but this was discounted in light of a lack of neutrophil-dominant pleocytosis, only mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, and sterile CSF cultures. The possibility of tuberculous meningitis was eliminated since both acid-fast staining and tuberculosis DNA assays were negative.

Given the neurological presentation, a primary CNS neoplasm was also suspected. However, neuroimaging via brain MRI revealed no abnormalities, and cytological analysis of the CSF did not detect malignant cells. Although soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) was found to be mildly increased, this isolated finding was not sufficient to confirm a diagnosis of CNS lymphoma.

Evaluation for autoimmune disorders was undertaken, but these were ruled out owing to unremarkable systemic signs and consistently negative results on autoimmune antibody screens. Neurosarcoidosis remained under consideration as early MRI can appear normal in this condition; however, further laboratory assessment subsequently argued against this diagnosis. The slight elevation of sIL-2R was interpreted as non-specific.

Ultimately, a diagnosis of definitive Behçet’s disease was made, based on clinical evidence including fever, fatigue, oral aphthae (scoring two points), genital aphthosis (two points), and a folliculitis-like eruption (one point). According to the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease, the patient accumulated a total of five points (with a score of four or greater being diagnostic). Notably, the presence of genital aphthosis is a characteristic marker for BD, supporting this diagnosis. Infectious, neoplastic, and autoimmune pathologies having been excluded, a final diagnosis of acute neuro-Behçet’s disease was established, characterized by CNS involvement in the setting of mucocutaneous symptoms.

Treatment:

Upon admission, an inflammatory disorder was suspected, though its precise origin could not be determined. Empirical treatment with cefepime was initiated following blood culture collection, aimed at covering possible bacterial pathogens even in the absence of a definitive site of infection.

By the third day, the patient experienced worsening headache and vertigo, raising concern for central nervous system inflammation or infection. Empirical acyclovir therapy was commenced, but later discontinued when laboratory evaluations excluded HSV and VZV encephalitis.

Colchicine therapy was initiated on day 8 after systemic Behçet’s disease (BD) and acute neuro-BD were diagnosed. Aphthous lesions in the oral cavity were managed using topical corticosteroid ointment. Genital ulcers and skin lesions received silver sulfadiazine cream. Syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) was addressed conservatively through restricting fluid intake to less than 800 mL per day, with diligent daily checks of serum sodium and avoidance of rapid sodium correction. There was no requirement for hypertonic saline or vasopressin receptor antagonist therapy.

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient's fever subsided, and his appetite increased the day following the initiation of colchicine therapy. Over the subsequent two weeks, symptoms such as vertigo, malaise, hiccups, and mucocutaneous lesions gradually diminished. By the sixteenth day, his serum sodium had returned to 136 mmol/L and ADH concentrations had dropped to 1.8 pg/mL. Levels of myogenic enzymes normalized as well.

Significantly, the management did not involve systemic corticosteroids or additional immunosuppressants. He was discharged after a hospital stay lasting one month, during which he recovered from the acute phase, hyponatremia was closely monitored and treated, and rehabilitation addressed his loss of physical function. Colchicine was continued for about four years, with no relapse of BD or neurologic manifestations in that period. Once his BD was stabilized, biguanide therapy was commenced, resulting in effective long-term glycemic control.

Discussion:

This report details the concurrent identification of systemic Behçet's disease (BD) and acute neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), an unusual presentation. The case further illustrates that acute neuro-BD may be associated with the development of the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). Improvement of mucocutaneous manifestations, neurological symptoms, and hyponatraemia following administration of colchicine monotherapy indicates a potential direct link between BD, neuro-BD, and SIADH. In BD patients who present with symptoms such as fever or headache and are found to be hyponatraemic, clinicians should include acute neuro-BD in the differential diagnosis.

A distinguishing aspect of this case is the synchronous recognition of systemic BD and acute neuro-BD, which was substantiated by the presence of mucocutaneous lesions and central nervous system symptoms. In most cases, significant organ involvement—such as CNS lesions—typically manifests about four years subsequent to the initial diagnosis of BD, while neurological symptoms at initial presentation are seen in less than 5% of patients. Headache directly attributed to neuro-BD is reported in only a small proportion—about 10%—of cases. Thus, the early identification of neuro-BD here was particularly difficult. The typical clinical picture of acute neuro-BD is that of self-limiting meningoencephalitis, potentially accompanied by or without localized neurological deficits. For this patient, brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) did not show abnormalities at the conventional sites for BD involvement, such as the hypothalamus. Acute neuro-BD can also appear as aseptic meningitis in the absence of parenchymal involvement, and an unremarkable brain MRI early in the illness does not rule out the diagnosis. The lack of imaging findings added to the diagnostic complexity. While HLA-B51 testing was conducted, it did not support the diagnosis, consistent with the international BD diagnostic criteria which do not include HLA-B51 as an essential component and are primarily reliant on clinical manifestations. This scenario underlines the necessity of considering neuro-BD in patients with mucocutaneous symptoms who present with vague neurological complaints.

At the outset of the differential diagnosis, we focused on infection as a likely etiology for CNS inflammation due to the presence of persistent fever of unknown origin, neurological manifestations, and hyponatraemia, yet no specific infectious agent was identified. Other possible sources of CNS inflammation, such as connective tissue disorders and neoplastic processes, were also entertained; sarcoidosis and hematologic malignancies were specifically included in our differential considerations. Consequently, our initial extensive assessment included screening tests such as autoimmune serological markers, serum ACE, and sIL-2R. These evaluations were selectively performed as part of an exclusion strategy, rather than arising from compelling clinical suspicion. There was a lack of clinical, imaging, or CSF evidence to substantiate these possible diagnoses, and the findings from these tests did not significantly inform our final conclusion. Notably, ACE possesses suboptimal sensitivity for sarcoidosis, and while sIL-2R can assist in assessing malignant lymphoma, mild elevations are non-specific and may simply indicate ongoing inflammatory responses. The lack of genital ulceration during early symptom presentation rendered Behçet’s disease (BD) an uncertain diagnosis, prompting us to maintain a broad scope in our differential diagnosis. This diagnostic pathway underscores the necessity of refraining from non-targeted investigations, stressing that such tests should be employed based on relevant clinical evidence and their results interpreted judiciously.

While drug-induced SIADH has been documented in individuals with BD taking agents like cyclophosphamide, the relationship between acute neuro-BD and SIADH remains largely unclear. Neuro-BD is marked by inflammation within the central nervous system, which may play a role in abnormal ADH secretion. Multiple investigations have demonstrated that interleukin-6 (IL-6), a proinflammatory cytokine commonly elevated in neuroinflammatory conditions, correlates with increased ADH release. In BD, irregular monocyte activity results in spontaneous production of IL-6, with heightened IL-6 concentrations identified in the cerebrospinal fluid of patients experiencing acute neuro-BD. These observations imply that IL-6-driven inflammation may contribute to SIADH in this context. Although IL-6 levels were not assessed in either serum or CSF in the current case, a substantially raised serum CRP may indicate an IL-6–related inflammatory reaction. Despite SIADH being associated with a range of disorders such as CNS inflammation, pulmonary pathology, malignancies, and certain drugs, the current data do not support a direct causal relationship between SIADH and acute neuro-BD. The chronological overlap described in this report could represent a generalized response to CNS inflammation rather than a mechanism uniquely tied to the disease.

This case highlights the possible adverse outcomes associated with hyponatraemia due to SIADH occurring during acute neuro-BD. Previous studies have noted that persistent hiccups can be a key clinical indicator of severe hyponatraemia arising from SIADH secondary to central nervous system inflammation, such as meningitis. As part of the initial work-up for fever, arthralgia, and general fatigue—which could stem from either inflammatory or metabolic muscle injury—we obtained CK levels and detected rhabdomyolysis, a documented yet frequently neglected complication of severe hyponatraemia that is not typically assessed. Although these presentations are established, their clinical significance in severe hyponatraemia is often underestimated. It is important for clinicians to recognize that symptoms that seem unrelated, including hiccups or rhabdomyolysis, may signal underlying electrolyte imbalances, especially in the setting of neuroinflammatory disease.

Corticosteroids remain the cornerstone for managing acute neuro-BD, with the majority of patients showing positive responses. Nonetheless, instances of spontaneous improvement have been noted in patients with meningoencephalitis-like presentations alone. In this case, the use of colchicine monotherapy resulted in amelioration of mucocutaneous and neurological manifestations, as well as correction of hyponatraemia. Colchicine’s anti-inflammatory properties stem from its ability to impede neutrophil migration and activation, while also inhibiting cytokine release, including IL-1β and IL-6. Although colchicine is commonly prescribed for mucocutaneous symptoms of BD, comprehensive reviews indicate its lack of efficacy for acute neuro-BD, with immunosuppressive medications typically necessary for sufficient disease management. Notably, prior cases have employed colchicine only in combination with corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive agents, rather than as sole therapy. Despite the possibility that spontaneous remission occurred, the present findings raise the prospect that colchicine could serve as a viable treatment for acute neuro-BD and associated complications such as SIADH, especially in situations where corticosteroids are unsuitable or contraindicated.

Conclusion:

We presented an uncommon instance of Behçet's disease (BD) and acute neuro-BD diagnosed simultaneously, complicated by SIADH. This report underscores that SIADH can arise as a complication of acute neuro-BD, emphasizing the need for clinicians to recognize this possible correlation. Treatment with colchicine alone resulted in swift resolution of mucocutaneous signs, neurological symptoms, and hyponatraemia, indicating colchicine may serve as an effective therapeutic approach during the initial stage of neuro-BD. For individuals with BD who develop non-specific central nervous system complaints such as fever and headache together with hyponatraemia, clinicians should remain vigilant for acute neuro-BD complicated by SIADH.
