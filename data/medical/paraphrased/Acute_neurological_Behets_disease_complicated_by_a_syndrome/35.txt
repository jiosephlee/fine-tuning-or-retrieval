Title: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

Eiichi Kakehi, Yukako Matsumoto, Shigehisa Sakurai, Kazuhiko Kotani

Abstract:

Summary: We describe an uncommon instance of acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) identified simultaneously with systemic Behçet’s disease (BD), classified as definite BD, and accompanied by syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). A male patient in his sixties presented with fever, headache, and mucocutaneous abnormalities such as oral aphthae, rashes resembling folliculitis, genital ulcers, and hyponatraemia. The diagnosis of BD was established based on his mucocutaneous signs. While brain MRI was unremarkable, cerebrospinal fluid examination indicated aseptic meningitis, thereby confirming acute neuro-BD. Laboratory evaluation met the criteria for SIADH. Treatment with colchicine alone resulted in remission of mucocutaneous and neurological symptoms, as well as correction of hyponatraemia, obviating the requirement for corticosteroids or additional immunosuppressive therapies. This report indicates a potential link between acute neuro-BD and SIADH and highlights the necessity of recognising simultaneous neurological and endocrine presentations in patients newly diagnosed with BD.

Background:

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a chronic inflammatory condition affecting multiple systems, and is marked by recurrent oral and genital ulcerations, uveitis, and various skin manifestations. Neurological complications, termed neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), arise in about 10%–20% of cases, generally manifesting several years after BD is first identified. Acute presentations of neuro-BD are typically characterised by meningoencephalitis and parenchymal lesions, often with brainstem involvement. Concurrent presentation of BD and neuro-BD is rare, as simultaneous onset is less typical. When this occurs, clinicians should evaluate both the mucocutaneous signs and neurological manifestations, such as headaches, changes in behavior, psychiatric disturbances, and focal neurological deficits.

Syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) is a recognized etiology of hyponatraemia, frequently attributed to inflammation of the central nervous system (CNS), neoplastic processes, or exposure to certain drugs. The acute inflammatory processes seen in neuro-BD may predispose individuals to SIADH. While SIADH has been observed in BD patients undergoing therapies like cyclophosphamide, its development in the setting of acute neuro-BD without pharmacological factors—and driven by CNS inflammation alone—has not been fully defined.

We present an unusual case where both BD and acute neuro-BD were identified at the same time, complicated by the development of SIADH. This report underscores the need to recognize SIADH as a potential complication of acute neuro-BD, especially in individuals with unexplained CNS complaints and low serum sodium.

Case presentation:

A man of Asian descent in his 60s arrived at the emergency department, reporting three days of fever, headache, fatigue, diminished appetite, ongoing hiccups, and joint pain involving both shoulders and ankles. He also noted having oral aphthae.

His past medical history was notable for type 2 diabetes mellitus, for which he was not undergoing any treatment, attributed to limited understanding of his illness and absence of proper dietary and exercise routines. There was no significant family history, including no relatives with BD. His social history revealed occasional alcohol intake, no tobacco use, no recent travel, and no contact with animals or sick persons. He worked in metal fabrication and had not participated in sexual activity recently.

Upon assessment, the patient was conscious but demonstrated signs of fatigue. Recorded vital parameters included a temperature of 38.1°C, blood pressure at 135/73 mm Hg, regular heart rate of 119 bpm, respiratory rate of 19 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation measuring 96% on ambient air. Physical examination identified oral aphthae as well as rashes resembling folliculitis on both the face and trunk (figure 1A, B, and C). Findings from the cardiopulmonary and abdominal examinations were normal. Manual muscle testing showed no evidence of weakness in either upper or lower limbs. No peripheral oedema was noted, and clinical evaluation suggested the patient was euvolemic. Hospital admission was arranged for additional diagnostic work-up.

Investigations:

The initial laboratory workup demonstrated significant hyponatraemia at 124 mmol/L, alongside increased creatine kinase (1349 U/L), aspartate aminotransferase (77 U/L), alanine aminotransferase (64 U/L), and C-reactive protein (CRP) measured at 180 mg/L (reference range <3 mg/L). The erythrocyte sedimentation rate was also elevated at 70 mm/hour (reference <15 mm/hour), while renal function remained within normal limits. Hyperglycaemia was identified with plasma glucose levels of 14.2 mmol/L and a raised HbA1c of 8.2%, signifying poor glycaemic control. Screening for hepatitis B and C yielded negative results.

Endocrine testing showed normal thyroid-stimulating hormone (1.11 mIU/L; reference 0.50–5.00 mIU/L), free T4 (1.25 ng/dL; reference 0.90–1.70 ng/dL), and serum cortisol (42 µg/dL; reference >6 µg/dL). The patient’s serum osmolality was decreased at 267 mOsm/kg (reference <270 mOsm/kg), while urine osmolality was elevated at 900 mOsm/kg (reference >300 mOsm/kg), and urinary sodium was high at 88 mmol/L (reference >20 mmol/L), findings indicative of impaired water excretion. Despite the hypo-osmolar hyponatraemia, antidiuretic hormone (ADH) level was raised at 22.4 pg/mL. The electrocardiogram was unremarkable, and imaging—including chest X-ray and CT scans of the head, chest, and abdomen—revealed no abnormalities. Collectively, these results were consistent with a diagnosis of SIADH.

On the third day, his headache and vertigo became more severe. While neck rigidity and cranial nerve deficits were absent, central nervous system inflammation was considered as a possible cause. MRI of the brain did not show notable abnormalities affecting the hypothalamus or other areas (figure 2). Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis indicated a slightly increased opening pressure (17 cmH₂O), elevated protein concentration (0.97 g/L), and a mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL). Cultures and tests for bacterial and mycobacterial organisms, herpes simplex virus (HSV), varicella zoster virus (VZV), and cryptococcal antigen in CSF all returned negative. CSF cytology was negative for malignant cells.

Additional workup was performed to look for systemic diseases. Sarcoidosis markers, including lysozyme (5.1 µg/mL; reference range 5–10) and angiotensin-converting enzyme (11.4 U/L; reference range 7–25), were within normal limits. Hematological malignancy screening showed a raised soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) (704 U/mL; reference range, 122–496 U/mL), but human T-cell leukemia virus type 1 antibody was negative.

By day 5, a painful ulcer developed on the scrotum (figure 1D). When considered alongside oral aphthous ulcers and folliculitis-like skin lesions, these manifestations were consistent with systemic Behçet’s disease (BD). The patient did not demonstrate hypersensitivity to a needle prick. Ophthalmologic evaluation did not reveal uveitis. HLA-B51 testing was positive.

Differential diagnosis:

Upon admission, the presence of fever, headache, generalized malaise, and hyponatraemia pointed toward either an infection involving the central nervous system or a systemic inflammatory condition. Viral meningoencephalitis, especially due to HSV or VZV, was initially suspected. Nevertheless, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) studies did not detect viral antigens or antibodies, effectively ruling out these viruses. Bacterial meningitis was another consideration, but was dismissed since there was no neutrophil predominance, only mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, and negative results on CSF cultures. Tuberculous meningitis was ruled out after acid-fast stain and tuberculosis DNA testing were both negative.

A primary malignancy of the central nervous system was contemplated because of neurological manifestations. However, the brain MRI was unremarkable, and no malignant cells were found on CSF cytology. Although sIL-2R was slightly above normal, this result alone did not provide sufficient evidence to diagnose CNS lymphoma.

Evaluation for autoimmune disorders was performed, but they were excluded due to the absence of hallmark systemic manifestations and negative autoantibody screens. Neurosarcoidosis remained among the possible diagnoses, as MRI may be unremarkable in initial stages, yet subsequent laboratory findings diminished this likelihood. The modest elevation of sIL-2R was interpreted as a nonspecific feature.

The diagnosis of definite Behçet’s disease (BD) was established on the basis of clinical presentation: fever, fatigue, oral aphthae (score: two points), genital aphthosis (score: two points), and a rash resembling folliculitis (score: one point). Using the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease, the total score reached five points (a score of four or greater confirms the diagnosis). Notably, genital aphthosis is considered a hallmark of BD, and its presence strongly supports this diagnosis. Having eliminated infectious, neoplastic, and autoimmune etiologies, acute neuro-Behçet's disease was concluded, as evidenced by CNS symptoms accompanied by mucocutaneous signs.

Treatment:

On the first day of hospitalization, clinicians suspected the presence of an inflammatory process, though the precise cause could not be determined. Blood cultures were obtained, and empirical cefepime was started to address a possible bacterial infection, in spite of lacking a localized infectious source.

By the third day, the patient’s headache and vertigo intensified, raising concerns for central nervous system inflammation or infection. Acyclovir was given empirically but was subsequently stopped after laboratory tests excluded HSV and VZV encephalitis.

On the eighth day, colchicine therapy commenced following the diagnosis of systemic Behçet’s disease (BD) and acute neuro-BD. Topical corticosteroid ointment was applied to treat oral aphthous lesions. Silver sulfadiazine cream was used for the management of genital ulcers and cutaneous manifestations. Syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) was addressed conservatively by restricting fluid intake to less than 800 mL per day, conducting regular monitoring of serum sodium, and preventing rapid correction of sodium levels. Neither hypertonic saline nor vasopressin receptor antagonists was needed.

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient's fever subsided, and his appetite returned the day following initiation of colchicine. Over the next two weeks, his symptoms—including vertigo, malaise, hiccups, and mucocutaneous lesions—showed gradual resolution. By day 16, serum sodium had reached a normal value of 136 mmol/L, with ADH concentrations falling to 1.8 pg/mL. Myogenic enzyme measurements also normalized.

Notably, systemic corticosteroids and other immunosuppressive agents were not administered throughout therapy. The patient was discharged after a one-month hospitalization, during which the acute illness resolved, hyponatremia was closely observed and managed, and rehabilitation addressed diminished physical capabilities. Colchicine treatment was maintained for roughly four years, during which neither Behçet's disease nor neurological symptoms recurred. Once BD was controlled, biguanide agents were introduced, resulting in sustained optimal glycemic regulation.

Discussion:

In this case, systemic BD and acute neuro-BD were both diagnosed concurrently—a presentation that is infrequently encountered. Additionally, this report highlights that acute neuro-BD may be associated with the development of SIADH. The resolution of hyponatraemia, along with the improvement in mucocutaneous and neurological symptoms following treatment with colchicine alone, indicates a possible direct link among BD, neuro-BD, and SIADH. Accordingly, in BD patients presenting with fever or headache and concurrent hyponatraemia, clinicians should consider the possibility of acute neuro-BD.

This case is significant due to the simultaneous identification of systemic and acute neuro-BD, as evidenced by both mucocutaneous involvement and central nervous system findings. Ordinarily, CNS involvement such as lesions manifests approximately four years after an initial diagnosis of BD, while neurological symptoms at initial presentation occur in less than 5% of cases. Headache directly attributable to neuro-BD is observed in only about 10% of affected individuals. Therefore, recognizing neuro-BD at initial presentation in this instance posed particular difficulty. The most frequent initial manifestation of acute neuro-BD is a meningoencephalitis with a typically self-limited course, sometimes accompanied by focal neurological deficits. In this case, brain MRI revealed no abnormalities at commonly involved sites in BD, including the hypothalamic region. It is known that acute neuro-BD can manifest as aseptic meningitis without evidence of parenchymal lesions on neuroimaging, and a normal MRI, particularly early in the disease, does not preclude a diagnosis. The absence of radiologic findings further complicated diagnostic efforts. Although HLA-B51 analysis was performed, it was not diagnostically contributory, which is consistent with established international BD diagnostic criteria that do not mandate HLA-B51 positivity and are based primarily on clinical presentation. This case underscores the need to consider neuro-BD in patients who exhibit mucocutaneous symptoms and non-specific neurological complaints.

During the early stages of differential diagnosis, we gave precedence to infection as the underlying cause of CNS inflammation, given the presence of unexplained fever, neurological manifestations, and hyponatraemia; nevertheless, no particular pathogen was identified. Additional etiologies such as connective tissue disorders and malignancies—including sarcoidosis and hematologic cancers—were also considered. Consequently, we pursued an initial comprehensive evaluation by assessing autoimmune serological markers, serum ACE, and sIL-2R as preliminary screenings. These investigations were conducted in a restricted fashion as part of a strategy to rule out conditions rather than from a strong clinical indication. There was no evidence from clinical presentation, imaging, or CSF analysis to support these diagnoses, and the results from these tests did not significantly contribute to the final diagnosis. While ACE testing is not highly sensitive for sarcoidosis, and sIL-2R may aid in diagnosing malignant lymphoma, minor increases are nonspecific and might indicate generalized inflammation. In this scenario, the lack of genital ulcers during early symptom development complicated the diagnosis of BD, necessitating a wider differential diagnosis. This approach underscores the importance of selective testing and suggests that such investigations should only be undertaken in the context of relevant clinical signs and interpreted carefully.

While drug-induced SIADH has been observed in individuals with BD who are treated with agents such as cyclophosphamide, the connection between acute neuro-BD and SIADH is not well elucidated. Neuro-BD involves central nervous system inflammation, which may play a role in abnormal ADH regulation. Research has demonstrated that interleukin-6 (IL-6), a proinflammatory cytokine commonly elevated in neuroinflammatory conditions, correlates with heightened ADH secretion. In Behçet’s disease, dysregulated monocyte activity results in spontaneous IL-6 release, and increased IL-6 concentrations have been identified in the cerebrospinal fluid of patients experiencing acute neuro-BD. These observations raise the possibility that IL-6–driven inflammation could underlie SIADH in this context. Although serum or CSF IL-6 levels were not determined in the current case, the significantly raised serum CRP may indicate an ongoing IL-6–associated inflammatory process. SIADH has been linked to a range of conditions such as CNS inflammation, lung disorders, malignancies, and certain medications; however, the data here are insufficient to conclusively establish a direct causative relationship between SIADH and acute neuro-BD. The timing of SIADH in this instance may represent a general response to CNS inflammation as opposed to a mechanism unique to neuro-BD.

This case demonstrates the range of complications that can arise from hyponatraemia due to SIADH occurring in acute neuro-BD. Prior literature indicates that persistent hiccups may provide a valuable diagnostic hint toward severe hyponatraemia resulting from SIADH secondary to central nervous system inflammation, such as meningitis. Furthermore, as part of our initial assessment of fever, arthralgia, and generalized fatigue—which could suggest inflammatory or metabolic injury to muscle tissue—we checked creatine kinase levels and identified rhabdomyolysis. This condition is a known, but frequently missed, consequence of significant hyponatraemia and is not typically sought during routine investigations. While these symptoms are recognized in relation to profound hyponatraemia, their clinical importance is often underestimated. Healthcare providers should recognize that seemingly unrelated presentations, like hiccups or rhabdomyolysis, may point to underlying disturbances in electrolytes, particularly in the presence of neuroinflammatory disorders.

Corticosteroids represent the standard approach for managing acute neuro-BD, with most individuals experiencing favorable outcomes. Nevertheless, in instances characterized only by meningoencephalitis-like symptoms, spontaneous improvement has been documented. In this particular case, treatment with colchicine alone led to amelioration of mucocutaneous and neurologic signs, in addition to correction of hyponatraemia. Colchicine mediates its anti-inflammatory properties by preventing neutrophil migration and activation and reducing cytokine release, including IL-1β and IL-6. Although colchicine is commonly employed for the management of mucocutaneous features in BD, systematic reviews have indicated its lack of efficacy in acute neuro-BD, highlighting the necessity for immunosuppressive medications to achieve appropriate disease control. Published reports describe colchicine primarily as a supplement to corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive agents, rather than as monotherapy. While the possibility of spontaneous remission in this case cannot be definitively ruled out, the results herein imply that colchicine may serve as a viable therapeutic option for acute neuro-BD and related complications such as SIADH, especially when corticosteroids are either not preferred or contraindicated.

Conclusion:

We report an uncommon instance where Behçet’s disease (BD) and acute neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) were simultaneously identified, with the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) presenting as a complication of neuro-BD. This case underscores that SIADH can emerge secondary to acute neuro-BD, emphasizing the need for clinicians to recognize this possible link. Administration of colchicine alone resulted in a prompt resolution of the patient’s mucocutaneous and neurological symptoms, as well as correction of hyponatraemia, indicating that colchicine may serve as an effective treatment option during the initial stages of neuro-BD. When evaluating individuals with BD who develop nonspecific central nervous system symptoms such as headache and fever together with hyponatraemia, healthcare providers should be alert to the possibility of acute neuro-BD associated with SIADH.
