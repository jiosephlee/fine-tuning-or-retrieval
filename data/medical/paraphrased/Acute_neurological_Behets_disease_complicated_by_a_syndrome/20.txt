Title: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

Eiichi Kakehi, Yukako Matsumoto, Shigehisa Sakurai, Kazuhiko Kotani

Abstract:

Summary: We present an unusual case involving simultaneous onset of acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) and systemic Behçet’s disease (BD)—meeting the criteria for definite BD—complicated by syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). The patient, a man in his 60s, exhibited fever, headache, and mucocutaneous symptoms such as oral aphthae, folliculitis-like eruptions, genital ulceration, and hyponatraemia. The diagnosis of BD was established on the basis of mucocutaneous findings. Although brain MRI demonstrated no pathological changes, cerebrospinal fluid examination indicated aseptic meningitis, lending support to acute neuro-BD. Diagnostic evaluation also fulfilled criteria for SIADH. The patient's mucocutaneous and neurological symptoms as well as his hyponatraemia resolved following colchicine treatment alone, without resorting to corticosteroids or additional immunosuppressive agents. This report highlights a potential connection between acute neuro-BD and SIADH, emphasizing the need to recognize overlapping neurological and endocrine features in individuals newly identified with BD.

Background:

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a persistent, multisystemic inflammatory condition marked by repeated oral and genital ulcerations, uveitis, and dermatological manifestations. Neurological complications, termed neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), are observed in around 10%–20% of cases and generally appear several years post initial BD diagnosis. Acute neuro-BD is most frequently seen as meningoencephalitis with parenchymal lesions in the brain, especially involving the brainstem region. Although neuro-BD more often manifests after the onset of BD, simultaneous diagnoses of both conditions are uncommon. When they occur together, it is crucial for healthcare providers to evaluate both cutaneous-mucosal and neurologic signs, such as headache, changes in behavior, psychiatric disturbances, and localized neurological impairments.

Syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) is recognized as a frequent etiology of hyponatraemia and is often secondary to central nervous system (CNS) inflammation, malignancies, or use of specific medications. In the context of acute neuro-BD's inflammatory component, there may be a predisposition to SIADH among these patients. While SIADH has been identified in individuals with BD undergoing therapies like cyclophosphamide, its development without medication triggers—and solely as a result of CNS inflammation in the setting of acute neuro-BD—remains insufficiently defined.

Here, we present an unusual case in which a patient was diagnosed simultaneously with BD and acute neuro-BD, further complicated by the onset of SIADH. This report underscores the necessity to consider SIADH in patients with acute neuro-BD, especially when non-specific CNS features and hyponatraemia are present.

Case presentation:

A man of Asian descent in his sixties arrived at the emergency department, reporting three days of fever, headache, generalized fatigue, decreased appetite, continuous hiccups, and joint pain located in the shoulders and ankles. He additionally noted the presence of oral aphthous ulcers.

His past medical history revealed type 2 diabetes mellitus, for which he was not undergoing any therapy, owing to his limited understanding of the condition and lack of proper diet or exercise habits. There was no relevant family history, and none of his relatives had been diagnosed with BD. He drank alcohol occasionally, was not a smoker, had not traveled recently, nor had contact with animals or sick individuals. He was employed in metalworking and had not had recent sexual intercourse.

Upon evaluation, the patient was awake but exhibited signs of tiredness. His recorded vital parameters included a temperature of 38.1°C, blood pressure measured at 135/73 mm Hg, a regular heart rate of 119 bpm, a respiratory rate of 19 per minute, and an oxygen saturation level of 96% while breathing ambient air. Physical assessment identified oral aphthae as well as rashes resembling folliculitis located on his face and trunk (figure 1A, B, and C). Examinations of the heart, lungs, and abdomen yielded no abnormal findings. Testing muscle strength manually found no deficits in either the upper or lower limbs. Peripheral edema was absent, and clinical evaluation suggested that his volume status was euvolemic. The patient was admitted for further diagnostic workup.

Investigations:

Initial laboratory evaluation showed significant hyponatraemia (124 mmol/L), raised creatine kinase (1349 U/L), increased aspartate aminotransferase (77 U/L), elevated alanine aminotransferase (64 U/L), high C-reactive protein (180 mg/L; reference, <3 mg/L), and an erythrocyte sedimentation rate of 70 mm/hour (reference, <15 mm/hour), with preserved renal function. Hyperglycaemia was observed (plasma glucose 14.2 mmol/L) alongside a heightened HbA1c of 8.2%, reflecting suboptimal glycaemic management. Testing for hepatitis B and C was negative.

Hormonal studies demonstrated thyroid-stimulating hormone within normal range (1.11 mIU/L; reference, 0.50–5.00 mIU/L), as well as free T4 (1.25 ng/dL; reference, 0.90–1.70 ng/dL) and serum cortisol (42 µg/dL; reference, >6 µg/dL). Serum osmolality was reduced (267 mOsm/kg; reference, <270 mOsm/kg), while urine osmolality was markedly increased (900 mOsm/kg, reference, >300 mOsm/kg) and urinary sodium was elevated (88 mmol/L; reference, >20 mmol/L), indicating disturbed water excretion. Despite hypotonic hyponatraemia, antidiuretic hormone (ADH) was found to be raised (22.4 pg/mL). Electrocardiography showed no abnormalities, and imaging with chest X-ray as well as head, chest, and abdominal CT scans yielded normal findings. These results were compatible with a diagnosis of SIADH.

On the third day, his headache and dizziness became more severe. Despite the absence of neck rigidity or involvement of cranial nerves, central nervous system inflammation was considered. Brain MRI did not show notable changes in the hypothalamus or other regions of the brain (figure 2). Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis demonstrated a mildly raised opening pressure (17 cmH₂O), elevated protein level (0.97 g/L), and a slight lymphocytic increase (11 cells/µL). CSF cultures and assays for bacterial and mycobacterial organisms, herpes simplex virus (HSV), varicella zoster virus (VZV), and cryptococcal antigen returned negative results. CSF cytology did not reveal any malignant cells.

Additional testing was conducted to assess for systemic disease. Evaluation for sarcoidosis yielded normal lysozyme (5.1 µg/mL; reference range 5–10) and angiotensin-converting enzyme (11.4 U/L; reference range 7–25) values. Hematological malignancy screening showed an elevated soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) (704 U/mL; reference 122–496 U/mL) and was negative for human T-cell leukemia virus type 1 antibody.

By the fifth day, he developed a painful ulcer on the scrotum (figure 1D). This, along with oral aphthae and skin lesions resembling folliculitis, pointed to a systemic variant of Behçet's disease (BD). There was no evidence of hypersensitivity to needle prick. Ophthalmological assessment did not detect uveitis. HLA-B51 was found to be positive.

Differential diagnosis:

Upon admission, the combination of fever, headache, malaise, and hyponatraemia indicated the possibility of either a central nervous system infection or an underlying systemic inflammatory process. Initially, viral meningoencephalitis—particularly due to HSV or VZV—was suspected. Nonetheless, analysis of CSF returned negative for both viral antigens and antibodies, thereby excluding these etiologies. Consideration was also given to bacterial meningitis, but this was dismissed due to the absence of neutrophilic dominance, presence of mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, and negative CSF cultures. Tuberculous meningitis was ruled out based on negative results for both acid-fast staining and tuberculosis DNA testing.

A primary CNS malignancy was also contemplated in light of the neurological presentation. However, brain MRI demonstrated no abnormalities and CSF cytology did not identify any malignant cells. The slight elevation in sIL-2R was considered, yet this was insufficient in itself to support CNS lymphoma as a diagnosis.

The possibility of autoimmune diseases was also assessed and ultimately discounted due to lack of distinctive systemic manifestations and negative results from screening autoantibody tests. Neurosarcoidosis remained in the differential, as early MRI findings may be unremarkable, but this diagnosis became less likely after reviewing further laboratory data. The minor increase in sIL-2R was interpreted as nonspecific.

The diagnosis of definite Behçet’s disease (BD) was established based on clinical findings of fever, fatigue, oral aphthae (two points), genital aphthosis (two points), and a folliculitis-like rash (one point). According to the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease, his cumulative score was five points (≥4 corresponds to a definite diagnosis). Genital aphthosis, in particular, is recognized as highly specific for BD, thereby reinforcing this clinical suspicion. After infectious, neoplastic, and autoimmune etiologies were excluded, a diagnosis of acute neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) was rendered, characterized by CNS involvement in conjunction with mucocutaneous symptoms.

Treatment:

On the first day of hospitalization, clinicians suspected the presence of an underlying inflammatory disorder; however, the precise cause was not yet identified. Empirical therapy with cefepime was commenced after obtaining blood cultures, aiming to address a possible bacterial infection, even though a clear infectious source was not found.

By the third day, the patient experienced intensified headache and vertigo, raising concern for inflammation or infection of the central nervous system. Empirical acyclovir was prescribed but subsequently stopped when testing excluded HSV and VZV encephalitis.

On the eighth day, colchicine therapy was initiated in light of the diagnoses of systemic BD and acute neuro-BD. Topical corticosteroid ointment was applied to manage oral aphthous ulcers. Genital ulcers and skin lesions were treated with silver sulfadiazine cream. SIADH was handled conservatively by restricting fluid intake to less than 800 mL per day, closely tracking daily serum sodium levels, and avoiding rapid sodium correction. Neither hypertonic saline nor vasopressin receptor antagonists were deemed necessary.

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient’s fever subsided, and his appetite returned the day following initiation of colchicine therapy. Over the subsequent two weeks, he experienced gradual improvement in symptoms such as vertigo, general malaise, hiccups, and mucocutaneous lesions. By day 16, serum sodium normalized to 136 mmol/L, and ADH concentration decreased to 1.8 pg/mL. Additionally, myogenic enzyme values returned to within normal limits.

Notably, systemic corticosteroids and other immunosuppressive agents were not administered during the course of treatment. The patient was discharged after a one-month hospitalization, during which acute phase recovery occurred, hyponatremia was closely observed and controlled, and rehabilitation was undertaken to address the decline in physical capacity. Colchicine was continued for around four years, during which there was no evidence of Behçet’s disease (BD) or neurological symptom recurrence. Following stabilization of BD, biguanide therapy was started, leading to sustained adequate glycemic control.

Discussion:

In this case, both systemic BD and acute neuro-BD were identified concurrently, an uncommon clinical occurrence. Additionally, the case illustrated that SIADH can develop as a complication of acute neuro-BD. Improvement in mucocutaneous symptoms, neurological signs, and hyponatraemia after starting colchicine monotherapy points to a potential direct relationship between BD, neuro-BD, and SIADH. Clinicians should consider acute neuro-BD in the differential diagnosis when encountering BD patients with fever or headache associated with hyponatraemia.

The case is distinguished by the concurrent detection of systemic BD and acute neuro-BD, with evidence provided by the presence of mucocutaneous lesions and central nervous system involvement. In most cases, significant organ involvement such as CNS lesions tend to develop roughly four years post-BD diagnosis, and initial neurological symptoms are observed in fewer than 5% of cases. Only around 10% of BD patients report headaches attributable to neuro-BD. As a result, early recognition of neuro-BD in this scenario was particularly difficult. Acute neuro-BD typically presents as a self-limited meningoencephalitis, occasionally accompanied by focal deficits. For this patient, brain MRI did not detect abnormalities in characteristic BD locations, such as the hypothalamus. Acute neuro-BD may manifest as aseptic meningitis in the absence of brain parenchymal lesions, and a normal MRI scan—especially during early disease—does not rule out this diagnosis. This lack of radiological evidence added to the diagnostic challenges. Although HLA-B51 testing was completed, it was not helpful in confirming the diagnosis, consistent with international BD diagnostic standards that prioritize clinical findings over HLA-B51 status. The case underscores the need to suspect neuro-BD in individuals with mucocutaneous manifestations who develop vague neurological complaints.

During the early stages of evaluating the differential diagnosis, infection was initially suspected as the cause of CNS inflammation due to the presence of unexplained fever, neurological manifestations, and hyponatraemia; nonetheless, no pathogen was identified. Other etiologies for CNS inflammation, such as connective tissue disorders and neoplastic processes, were also taken into account, with sarcoidosis and haematologic malignancy included in our differential. As a component of a comprehensive preliminary assessment, autoimmune serological markers, serum ACE, and sIL-2R levels were measured to screen for these conditions. These tests were ordered selectively within a framework of exclusion, rather than stemming from compelling clinical suspicion. Ultimately, we did not observe supporting clinical signs, radiological evidence, or CSF abnormalities for these conditions, and the laboratory results were not helpful in establishing the final diagnosis. Serum ACE has limited sensitivity when evaluating sarcoidosis, and although sIL-2R can assist in assessing malignant lymphoma, slight increases in its levels are non-specific and may simply indicate an inflammatory response. In this case, the lack of genital ulceration early in the clinical course made the diagnosis of BD less evident, necessitating an expanded differential. This diagnostic approach underscores the necessity of refraining from indiscriminate testing, and reinforces the principle that clinicians should pursue such investigations only in the presence of relevant clinical signs and should interpret findings carefully.

While cases of SIADH induced by medications, such as cyclophosphamide, have been documented in individuals with BD, the relationship between acute neuro-BD and SIADH remains incompletely elucidated. Neuro-BD involves inflammation within the central nervous system, which might play a role in abnormal ADH regulation. Multiple reports indicate that interleukin-6 (IL-6), a proinflammatory cytokine commonly elevated in neuroinflammatory conditions, promotes increased ADH secretion. In Behçet disease, dysregulated monocyte activity results in spontaneous IL-6 synthesis, and high IL-6 concentrations have been identified in the cerebrospinal fluid of patients experiencing acute neuro-BD. Collectively, these observations imply that IL-6–driven inflammatory processes could underlie SIADH in this context. In this case, although serum or CSF IL-6 levels were not obtained, significantly raised serum CRP values may signify an IL-6–dependent inflammatory state. While SIADH can arise from a variety of causes, such as CNS inflammation, pulmonary pathology, malignancies, and certain drugs, the evidence presented here does not establish a definitive causative connection between SIADH and acute neuro-BD. The chronological correlation found in this patient may represent a generalized response to CNS inflammation, rather than a distinct pathophysiologic mechanism specific to the disease.

This case demonstrates the possible adverse effects associated with hyponatraemia resulting from SIADH in acute neuro-BD. Prior literature has indicated that persistent hiccups may represent a significant clinical indicator of severe hyponatraemia induced by SIADH secondary to CNS inflammatory processes, such as meningitis. Furthermore, during the initial assessment of fever, arthralgia, and general fatigue—which can be attributed to muscle injury from inflammatory or metabolic causes—we obtained a CK measurement that uncovered rhabdomyolysis. Although rhabdomyolysis is a known complication of severe hyponatraemia, it is frequently neglected and not routinely evaluated. These presentations are recognized yet frequently underestimated consequences of marked hyponatraemia. Healthcare professionals should recognize that atypical manifestations, including hiccups or rhabdomyolysis, could signify underlying disturbances in electrolytes, especially in patients with neuroinflammatory disorders.

Corticosteroids are regarded as the primary therapy for acute neuro-BD, with most individuals demonstrating good clinical response. Nonetheless, spontaneous recovery has been documented in instances where patients present exclusively with meningoencephalitis-like features. In this particular case, treatment with colchicine alone resulted in improvement of both mucocutaneous and neurologic symptoms, as well as correction of hyponatremia. Colchicine acts through anti-inflammatory mechanisms, notably by preventing neutrophil migration and activation and inhibiting cytokine production, including IL-1β and IL-6. While colchicine is frequently employed for managing mucocutaneous manifestations in BD, systematic reviews have indicated that colchicine lacks efficacy in acute neuro-BD, for which immunosuppressive medications are generally necessary for effective disease management. Previously reported cases show that colchicine is typically administered in combination with corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive drugs, rather than as monotherapy. Although the possibility of spontaneous remission cannot be ruled out in this patient, the results suggest that colchicine may represent a potential therapeutic option for acute neuro-BD and associated complications such as SIADH, especially when corticosteroids are contraindicated or undesirable.

Conclusion:

We reported an uncommon instance in which Behçet’s disease (BD) and acute neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) were simultaneously diagnosed, with neuro-BD further complicated by the development of SIADH. This case illustrates that SIADH can emerge as a secondary complication in the setting of acute neuro-BD, emphasizing the necessity for clinicians to recognize this possible association. Treatment with colchicine alone resulted in a swift resolution of both mucocutaneous and neurological symptoms, as well as correction of hyponatraemia, indicating that colchicine could serve as a viable early treatment option for neuro-BD. For patients with BD who exhibit non-specific central nervous system symptoms, such as fever and headache, along with hyponatraemia, clinicians should consider the possibility of acute neuro-BD complicated by SIADH.
