Title: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

Eiichi Kakehi, Yukako Matsumoto, Shigehisa Sakurai, Kazuhiko Kotani

Abstract:

Summary: We present an uncommon instance of acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) occurring simultaneously with systemic Behçet’s disease (BD), classified as definite BD, complicated by the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). A male patient in his 60s arrived with fever, headache, and mucocutaneous signs including oral aphthae, folliculitis-like skin eruptions, genital ulceration, and hyponatraemia. BD was diagnosed on the basis of these mucocutaneous features. Magnetic resonance imaging of the brain demonstrated no abnormalities, while cerebrospinal fluid analysis identified aseptic meningitis, consistent with a diagnosis of acute neuro-BD. Laboratory findings met the criteria for SIADH. Treatment with colchicine alone resolved all mucocutaneous complaints, neurological symptoms, and hyponatraemia, eliminating the necessity for corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive agents. This case highlights a potential link between acute neuro-BD and SIADH, emphasizing the significance of identifying concurrent neurologic and endocrine disturbances in patients newly diagnosed with BD.

Background:

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a persistent inflammatory condition affecting multiple organ systems, typified by the recurrence of oral and genital ulcerations, uveitis, and cutaneous manifestations. Neurological involvement, identified as neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), is observed in about 10%–20% of cases and typically emerges years following the initial BD diagnosis. Acute neuro-BD is frequently characterized by meningoencephalitis and focal brain lesions, most often affecting the brainstem. While simultaneous onset is uncommon, BD and neuro-BD are infrequently identified at the same time. In such situations, healthcare providers must evaluate both mucocutaneous signs as well as neurological manifestations, such as headache, changes in behavior, psychiatric disturbances, and focal neurological impairments.

The syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) represents a recognized etiology of hyponatraemia, most commonly linked to inflammation within the central nervous system (CNS), neoplasms, or particular medications. Owing to the inflammatory processes underlying acute neuro-BD, affected individuals may be predisposed to SIADH. Although SIADH has been previously documented in BD patients undergoing treatment with agents like cyclophosphamide, the development of SIADH solely due to CNS inflammation without pharmacological contributions in acute neuro-BD has not been clearly established.

Here, we describe an unusual presentation in which both BD and acute neuro-BD were diagnosed simultaneously, complicated by the development of SIADH. This case emphasizes the need to recognize SIADH as a possible complication of acute neuro-BD, especially in those presenting with non-specific neurological complaints and low serum sodium levels.

Case presentation:

A man of Asian descent in his 60s arrived at the emergency department after experiencing fever, headache, general fatigue, reduced appetite, persistent hiccups, and joint pain in his shoulders and ankles over the past three days. He additionally noted the presence of oral aphthae.

His past medical history was notable for type 2 diabetes mellitus, although he was not undergoing any treatment as he had limited awareness of his diagnosis and did not adhere to appropriate dietary or exercise regimens. There was no significant family history, and none of his relatives had been diagnosed with BD. He consumed alcohol sporadically, did not smoke, had no history of recent travel, and reported no contact with animals or ill persons. Employed in the metalworking field, he had not engaged in sexual activity recently.

Upon assessment, the patient was conscious but exhibited signs of fatigue. His recorded vital parameters included a temperature of 38.1°C, blood pressure measuring 135/73 mm Hg, heart rate of 119 beats per minute (regular rhythm), respiratory rate of 19 per minute, and oxygen saturation at 96% while breathing ambient air. On physical evaluation, oral aphthous ulcers and folliculitis-like eruptions were noted on his face and torso (figure 1A, B, and C). Findings from the examination of the cardiopulmonary and abdominal systems were normal. Manual muscle testing indicated that muscle strength in both upper and lower limbs was intact. Peripheral edema was absent, and the clinical assessment confirmed he was euvolemic. He was hospitalized for additional diagnostic workup.

Investigations:

The patient's initial blood work showed significant hyponatraemia with a sodium level of 124 mmol/L. There were raised levels of creatine kinase at 1349 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase at 77 U/L, alanine aminotransferase at 64 U/L, C-reactive protein at 180 mg/L (reference value <3 mg/L), and erythrocyte sedimentation rate at 70 mm/hour (reference value <15 mm/hour), all while renal function remained within normal limits. Laboratory analysis also demonstrated hyperglycaemia (plasma glucose 14.2 mmol/L) and a high HbA1c of 8.2%, indicative of inadequate glycaemic control. Serologic testing for hepatitis B and C returned negative.

Endocrine assessment showed thyroid-stimulating hormone at 1.11 mIU/L (reference 0.50–5.00 mIU/L), free T4 at 1.25 ng/dL (reference 0.90–1.70 ng/dL), and serum cortisol at 42 µg/dL (reference >6 µg/dL), all within normal parameters. Serum osmolality was reduced at 267 mOsm/kg (reference <270 mOsm/kg), while urine osmolality was increased at 900 mOsm/kg (reference >300 mOsm/kg), and urinary sodium was elevated at 88 mmol/L (reference >20 mmol/L), indicating impaired excretion of free water. Despite low serum osmolality and hyponatraemia, antidiuretic hormone was found to be elevated at 22.4 pg/mL. Electrocardiogram readings were normal, and imaging studies including chest X-ray and CT scans of the head, chest, and abdomen showed no abnormalities. Collectively, these results supported the diagnosis of SIADH.

By the third day, his headache and vertigo intensified. Despite the absence of neck rigidity or cranial nerve dysfunction, central nervous system inflammation was considered. Brain MRI did not demonstrate notable abnormalities in the hypothalamus or other regions (figure 2). Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) examination showed slightly raised opening pressure (17 cmH₂O), elevated protein (0.97 g/L), and mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL). CSF cultures, including assessments for bacterial and mycobacterial organisms, herpes simplex virus (HSV), varicella zoster virus (VZV), and cryptococcal antigen, all yielded negative results. CSF cytology identified no malignant cells.

Additional evaluations were undertaken to rule out systemic diseases. Sarcoidosis screening displayed normal lysozyme (5.1 µg/mL; reference range 5–10) and angiotensin-converting enzyme (11.4 U/L; reference range 7–25) levels. Hematological malignancy testing found elevated soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) (704 U/mL; reference 122–496 U/mL), and human T-cell leukemia virus type 1 antibody was negative.

By the fifth day, he developed a painful ulcer on the scrotum (figure 1D). Coupled with the presence of oral aphthae and folliculitis-like eruptions, these symptoms indicated a systemic variant of BD. There was no evidence of hypersensitivity to a pinprick. Ophthalmologic assessment found no uveitis. Testing for HLA-B51 was positive.

Differential diagnosis:

Upon admission, the constellation of fever, cephalalgia, profound fatigue, and hyponatremia pointed toward a diagnosis of either a central nervous system infection or a systemic inflammatory process. The initial clinical impression favored viral meningoencephalitis, especially that caused by HSV or VZV. Nevertheless, both viral antigens and antibodies were undetectable in CSF studies, effectively ruling out these etiologies. Bacterial meningitis was contemplated but subsequently discounted owing to the lack of neutrophilic dominance, presence of only mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, and negative CSF cultures. Tuberculous meningitis was also excluded following negative acid-fast bacilli staining and negative results for tuberculosis DNA in the CSF.

A primary CNS neoplasm was another consideration, prompted by the patient’s neurological deficits. However, no abnormal signal was identified on brain MRI, and CSF cytology did not uncover malignant cells. While sIL-2R was found to be modestly increased, this observation was not adequate alone to support a diagnosis of CNS lymphoma.

Autoimmune conditions were also explored, but these were dismissed given the absence of pathognomonic systemic manifestations and negative serologic testing for autoantibodies. Neurosarcoidosis remained part of the differential diagnosis since early MRI might be unrevealing; nonetheless, subsequent laboratory analyses diminished its likelihood. The slightly increased sIL-2R was deemed to be a nonspecific change.

A definitive diagnosis of Behçet’s disease (BD) was made based on the clinical presentation: fever, general malaise, oral aphthae (assigned two points), genital aphthosis (two points), and a folliculitis-like rash (one point). Per the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease, this amounted to five points (with a threshold of ≥4 defining definite disease). Notably, genital aphthosis is highly specific for BD and strongly supported the diagnosis. Having systematically excluded infectious, neoplastic, and autoimmune pathologies, the diagnosis of acute neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) was rendered, given the neurological symptoms and concurrent mucocutaneous signs.

Treatment:

On the first day of hospitalization, clinicians suspected an underlying inflammatory disorder, though the precise cause was indeterminate. Cefepime was commenced empirically, following collection of blood cultures, to address a possible bacterial infection in the absence of an identifiable infectious site.

By the third day, the patient exhibited worsening headache and vertigo, raising concerns for central nervous system inflammation or infection. Empirical treatment with acyclovir was initiated but discontinued once laboratory evaluation excluded HSV and VZV encephalitis.

On the eighth day, colchicine therapy was started after confirming diagnoses of systemic Behçet’s disease (BD) and acute neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD). Oral aphthae were managed with topical corticosteroid ointment, while genital and skin lesions were treated with silver sulfadiazine cream. Syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) was addressed conservatively using fluid restriction (less than 800 mL per day), close daily surveillance of serum sodium concentrations, and avoidance of rapid sodium correction. Hypertonic saline and vasopressin receptor antagonists were not utilized.

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient experienced resolution of fever and an improvement in appetite one day after beginning colchicine therapy. Over the subsequent two weeks, his clinical symptoms—including vertigo, general malaise, hiccups, and mucocutaneous manifestations—gradually subsided. By the sixteenth day, his serum sodium concentration had normalized to 136 mmol/L, and his ADH levels dropped to 1.8 pg/mL. Additionally, myogenic enzyme concentrations returned to reference ranges.

Notably, systemic corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive agents were not administered during his course of treatment. After a hospitalization lasting one month—during which acute-phase recovery was achieved, hyponatraemia was carefully monitored and addressed, and rehabilitation services were provided for physical function impairment—the patient was discharged. Colchicine was continued for nearly 4 years, with no recurrence of Behçet's disease or neurologic symptoms observed throughout this period. Following stabilization of BD, biguanide treatment was initiated, resulting in sustained good glycaemic control.

Discussion:

This report describes a patient who was concurrently diagnosed with systemic Behçet’s disease (BD) and acute neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), an uncommon occurrence. It further illustrates that acute neuro-BD can be associated with the development of the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). The concurrent improvement of mucocutaneous lesions, neurologic symptoms, and hyponatraemia following treatment with colchicine monotherapy implies a direct link between BD, neuro-BD, and SIADH. In BD patients presenting with fever or headache in the setting of hyponatraemia, clinicians should consider acute neuro-BD in the differential diagnosis.

A distinguishing feature of this case was the parallel diagnosis of systemic BD and acute neuro-BD, as evidenced by mucocutaneous involvement and central nervous system (CNS) manifestations. Generally, CNS complications, such as parenchymal lesions, emerge about four years after BD is initially identified, while presenting with neurologic symptoms is relatively infrequent and reported in less than 5% of cases. Only about one in ten individuals experience neuro-BD-associated headache. These factors contributed to the difficulty in promptly diagnosing neuro-BD in this scenario. The acute form of neuro-BD most frequently manifests as a self-limited meningoencephalitis, which may or may not feature focal neurological findings. In the present case, neuroimaging with brain MRI did not identify characteristic changes at common BD sites, including the hypothalamic area. Acute neuro-BD can sometimes appear as aseptic meningitis in the absence of parenchymal brain lesions, and a normal MRI—especially early in the course of illness—does not rule out the diagnosis. The absence of imaging abnormalities made reaching the diagnosis more challenging. HLA-B51 testing was undertaken but did not aid diagnostic determination; this is consistent with international BD guidelines that emphasize clinical criteria over HLA-B51 status. Ultimately, this case underscores the need for clinicians to consider neuro-BD when individuals with mucocutaneous features develop ambiguous neurological complaints.

During the initial assessment of possible causes, we initially suspected infection as the source of CNS inflammation due to the patient’s unexplained fever, neurological manifestations, and hyponatraemia; nonetheless, no specific infectious agent was detected. Other potential etiologies, such as connective tissue disorders and neoplastic conditions—including sarcoidosis and hematologic cancers—were also entertained. Thus, our early broad diagnostic workup included the measurement of autoimmune serological markers, serum ACE, and sIL-2R, employing these tests primarily for exclusionary purposes rather than out of significant clinical suspicion. No supportive clinical, imaging, or CSF findings were observed for these alternative diagnoses, and the screening studies ultimately did not substantially assist in determining the diagnosis. Notably, ACE testing lacks high sensitivity for sarcoidosis, and while sIL-2R can assist in assessing malignant lymphoma, minor elevations are non-specific and may indicate general inflammation. In this patient, the absence of genital ulceration at the early onset of symptoms rendered the diagnosis of BD ambiguous, prompting an expansive differential. This case illustrates the significance of judicious test ordering, reinforcing that such evaluations should be guided by distinct clinical indicators and interpreted prudently.

While SIADH resulting from medications, such as cyclophosphamide, has been documented in patients with BD, the relationship between acute neuro-BD and SIADH remains unclear. Neuro-BD involves inflammation within the central nervous system, which could potentially influence abnormal ADH secretion. Multiple investigations have indicated that interleukin-6 (IL-6), a proinflammatory cytokine often raised in neuroinflammatory conditions, is linked to enhanced ADH production. In BD, abnormal activation of monocytes triggers spontaneous synthesis of IL-6, and high levels of IL-6 have been observed in the cerebrospinal fluid of individuals experiencing acute neuro-BD. These observations imply that IL-6-driven inflammation may play a role in the development of SIADH in this clinical context. Although IL-6 concentrations were not assessed in the serum or CSF for the current case, the significantly increased serum CRP could serve as an indirect marker of IL-6–related inflammatory activity. SIADH can occur in a range of scenarios, including but not limited to CNS inflammation, respiratory disorders, neoplasms, and specific pharmacotherapies, yet the data here do not permit a definitive causal relationship between SIADH and acute neuro-BD. The chronological overlap in this case may simply represent a general reaction to CNS inflammation, rather than being tied to a particular disease process.

This case further demonstrates the potential for complications arising from hyponatraemia due to SIADH in acute neuro-BD. Prior publications have noted that persistent hiccups can represent a significant clinical indicator of severe hyponatraemia stemming from SIADH secondary to central nervous system inflammation, including meningitis. Additionally, as part of the initial assessment for fever, arthralgia, and generalized fatigue—which could be associated with inflammatory or metabolic muscle injury—we measured creatine kinase and discovered rhabdomyolysis, a known but frequently neglected outcome of severe hyponatraemia that is not commonly assessed. Although these clinical features are recognized, their significance in the context of profound hyponatraemia is often underestimated. It is important for healthcare providers to recognize that symptoms such as hiccups or rhabdomyolysis, which may seem unrelated, could signal underlying electrolyte imbalances, especially in patients with neuroinflammatory disorders.

Corticosteroids are widely regarded as the primary therapy for acute neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), with most patients experiencing a favorable response. Nevertheless, there have been accounts of spontaneous improvement in individuals presenting exclusively with meningoencephalitis-like symptoms. In the current report, treatment with colchicine alone led to improvement in both mucocutaneous and neurological manifestations, as well as in hyponatremia. Colchicine acts as an anti-inflammatory agent by preventing neutrophil migration and activation and by downregulating cytokine production, notably IL-1β and IL-6. While colchicine is commonly prescribed for managing mucocutaneous symptoms in BD, systematic reviews indicate it does not effectively treat acute neuro-BD, with immunosuppressive medications typically required for sufficient disease management. Historically, colchicine has been administered in combination with corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants in reported neuro-BD cases, rather than as a standalone therapy. Though it cannot be ruled out that spontaneous remission occurred in this instance, our observations indicate that colchicine may represent a viable therapeutic alternative for acute neuro-BD and related complications, including SIADH, particularly when corticosteroids are unsuitable or contraindicated.

Conclusion:

We reported an uncommon presentation involving simultaneous diagnosis of Behçet’s disease (BD) and acute neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), where the development of SIADH complicated the neurological condition. This report illustrates that SIADH can arise as a sequela of acute neuro-BD, underscoring the need for physicians to be vigilant regarding this possible relationship. Treatment with colchicine alone resulted in prompt resolution of mucocutaneous lesions, neurological deficits, and hyponatraemia, indicating that colchicine may serve as an effective initial treatment for early neuro-BD. In BD patients exhibiting non-specific central nervous system symptoms—including fever and headache—accompanied by hyponatraemia, healthcare providers should consider the possibility of acute neuro-BD complicated by SIADH.
