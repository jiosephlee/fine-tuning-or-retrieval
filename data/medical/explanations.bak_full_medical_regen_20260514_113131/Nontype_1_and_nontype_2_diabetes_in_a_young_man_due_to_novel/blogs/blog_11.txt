Title: Blog about the case report: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

# Turning One Diagnosis Into Many: Family Cascade Testing and Precision Therapy in HNF1A‑MODY

What grabbed my attention in this case was how a seemingly routine discovery of hyperglycaemia in a young man — HbA1c 10.2%, glycosuria on annual check — unmasked a dominantly inherited, treatable form of diabetes. A previously unreported HNF1A variant (p.N270S) explained the early-onset diabetes in multiple generations and directly changed management: insulin stopped, GLP‑1 receptor agonist and metformin achieved HbA1c 6.0% within months. That’s precision medicine in real time — one genetic test did more than label disease; it redirected therapy and triggered family-wide opportunity for diagnosis and prevention.

Why this matters
- HNF1A‑MODY is often mislabelled as type 1 or type 2 diabetes. Misclassification can lead to unnecessary insulin, missed chances for oral agents that work notably well (eg, sulfonylureas, GLP‑1 RAs), and a failure to offer targeted testing to relatives.
- Identification of a pathogenic HNF1A variant has implications beyond the proband: autosomal dominant inheritance means a 50% prior probability for first‑degree relatives, and earlier detection can prevent prolonged hyperglycaemia and overtreatment.
- This case also illustrates that novel variants (absent from ClinVar/VarSome) can be pathogenic based on segregation, in‑silico prediction and domain location — and these findings can and should guide clinical care when interpreted carefully.

From one proband to a family strategy: practical cascade testing steps
- Start with a clear phenotype and documentation: age at onset, BMI, symptoms, autoantibody status, C‑peptide (basal and stimulated), glycosuria, and family pedigree. In this case: early onset, negative GADA/IA‑2, stimulated C‑peptide ~0.83 nmol/L (moderately preserved), multigenerational diabetes — fertile ground for MODY testing.
- Confirm the proband’s genetic diagnosis (NGS panel or targeted sequencing). Once a pathogenic or likely pathogenic HNF1A variant is identified, offer targeted testing to first‑degree relatives for that specific variant — cheaper, faster, and unambiguous compared with broad panels.
- Prioritize testing order: symptomatic relatives and those of reproductive age first, then other first‑degree relatives. Use cascade results to guide screening intervals for mutation‑negative relatives (treat as population risk) versus carriers (early surveillance).
- Pre‑ and post‑test counseling matters: discuss inheritance (50% risk), variable expressivity and penetrance, implications for management (medication choices, pregnancy planning), and psychosocial aspects. Obtain informed consent and document discussions.
- Record and communicate results to primary care and the family: a positive genetic result should trigger medication review, monitoring (retinopathy/microalbuminuria screening), and education about symptoms and lifestyle.

Aligning genotype with therapy — clinical pragmatism
- Use residual beta‑cell function and clinical severity to individualize therapy. HNF1A‑MODY typically causes progressive β‑cell dysfunction but often preserves insulin secretory capacity longer than autoimmune type 1 disease. In the case, stimulated C‑peptide was modestly preserved, supporting a trial off insulin.
- First‑line options historically: low‑dose sulfonylureas are effective in many HNF1A patients because of marked insulin secretory responsiveness. But weigh hypoglycaemia risk, patient preference and lifestyle.
- GLP‑1 receptor agonists (used here as dulaglutide) are an attractive alternative — effective glycaemic lowering, weight neutrality or loss, and lower hypoglycaemia risk than sulfonylureas. This patient achieved rapid and sustained improvement (HbA1c 10.2% → 6.0% at 6 months) after switching to GLP‑1 RA + metformin.
- Insulin can often be withdrawn if C‑peptide shows preserved endogenous insulin and autoantibodies are negative — but do this under close supervision with glucose monitoring and follow‑up HbA1c checks.
- Avoid SGLT‑2 inhibitors without careful consideration: reduced SGLT2 expression in HNF1A‑MODY can produce prominent glycosuria and there are case reports of severe dehydration/DKA risk; exercise caution and individualise decisions.
- DPP‑4 inhibitors were part of the long‑term plan for this patient (saxagliptin with metformin) after stabilization — a reasonable maintenance strategy when glycaemia is controlled and hypoglycaemia risk is a concern.

Counselling and long‑term surveillance
- Emphasize that genetic diagnosis changes screening and prognostic conversations. HNF1A carriers still need standard microvascular risk surveillance (annual retinal and renal screening), and cardiovascular risk should be addressed per guidelines.
- Discuss reproductive implications: each child has a 50% chance of inheriting the variant; consider offering testing to adolescents and young adults with appropriate counselling.
- For relatives who decline genetic testing, consider regular metabolic screening (fasting glucose/HbA1c, urine dipstick for glycosuria) given family history.

Pitfalls and realistic expectations
- Not all “MODY” phenotypes will have an identifiable pathogenic variant; conversely, not all variants flagged by in‑silico tools are disease causing. Variant classification requires integrating family segregation, clinical phenotype and ACMG criteria.
- Absence of autoantibodies and preserved C‑peptide are supportive but not definitive — repeat testing and longitudinal assessment can be informative.
- Be mindful of mixed phenotypes: rising obesity and T2D prevalence can coexist with monogenic disease, complicating interpretation of BMI and metabolic syndrome features.

Clinical pearls
- Always ask about multigenerational early‑onset diabetes: that pedigree (grandmother in 30s, mother late 20s, brother at 20) should trigger MODY consideration.
- Negative islet autoantibodies + preserved stimulated C‑peptide + strong family history = high yield for genetic testing.
- Genetic diagnosis of HNF1A can permit stopping insulin and switching to oral/GLP‑1 therapy if β‑cell function allows — with rapid HbA1c and quality‑of‑life gains.
- Cascade testing is cost‑effective: targeted testing for a known familial variant is far cheaper than de novo panel testing and has immediate clinical utility.

Take‑home messages
- Consider MODY (especially HNF1A) in young adults with non‑insulin autoimmune diabetes, preserved C‑peptide and strong family history; genetic testing changes treatment.
- After identifying a pathogenic HNF1A variant in a proband, perform cascade testing for the specific variant in first‑degree relatives with appropriate counselling.
- Use residual β‑cell function to guide therapy: many HNF1A carriers respond to sulfonylureas or GLP‑1 RAs and can often discontinue insulin under close follow‑up.
- Avoid blanket use of SGLT‑2 inhibitors without assessing risks in HNF1A patients; individualized choice is key.
- A single genetic diagnosis can unlock precision care across a family — don’t miss the opportunity.

This case is a reminder that a simple annual check can lead to a genetic diagnosis that changes medications, reduces hypoglycaemia risk, and identifies at‑risk relatives — the very definition of precision, family‑focused medicine.
