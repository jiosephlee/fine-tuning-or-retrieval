Title: Blog about the case report: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

# When to Order MODY Genetics: A Pragmatic Pathway (and Why Calculators Can Mislead)

What makes this case interesting: a young man with markedly elevated HbA1c (10.2%), glycosuria, a multi-generation history of early-onset diabetes and negative islet autoantibodies was found to have a novel pathogenic HNF1A variant (p.N270S). He avoided lifelong insulin, was transitioned off basal insulin to GLP‑1 agonist + metformin, and achieved HbA1c 6.0% within months. This is a classic reminder that identifying MODY changes treatment, prognosis and family counseling — but only if you think to test for it.

If you’re in the ED or clinic and see “young diabetic,” here’s a concise, pragmatic pathway for when to pull the trigger on genetic testing.

## The pragmatic pathway — think MODY when you see a cluster of features

No single feature is diagnostic. Use a combination approach. The more elements present, the stronger the case for genetic testing.

- Age at onset
  - Strong flag: diagnosis of diabetes before age 25.
  - Consider testing up to age 35 if other features are present (family history, atypical phenotype).
- Family history
  - Autosomal dominant pattern (multiple successive generations with early-onset diabetes) strongly increases pre-test probability.
  - Absence of family history does not exclude MODY (de novo variants, mis- or under-diagnosed relatives).
- Autoantibodies
  - Negative GAD65, IA‑2, ZnT8 is supportive (not definitive). Positive antibodies make type 1 much more likely.
- Endogenous insulin secretion (C‑peptide)
  - Preserved fasting or stimulated C‑peptide (e.g., stimulated >0.6 nmol/L) beyond the period expected for T1DM supports non-autoimmune diabetes.
  - Low but not absent stimulated C‑peptide (as in this case: 0.83 nmol/L) can still be compatible with MODY.
- Pattern of glucose abnormality
  - Marked postprandial hyperglycaemia with relatively preserved fasting glucose early on — HNF1A often shows prominent post‑prandial rises.
  - Glycosuria out of proportion to blood glucose (reduced renal glucose threshold via SGLT2 expression changes) is a classic HNF1A clue.
- Absence of typical type 2 features
  - No acanthosis nigricans, lack of severe obesity, low/normal insulin requirements—these increase suspicion. Rising obesity prevalence weakens this discriminator.
- Treatment response / insulin requirements
  - Low-dose sulfonylurea responsiveness is a classic HNF1A feature; avoidance of insulin or early insulin independence after diagnosis argues against classic T1DM.
- Extra clues
  - Family members with early-onset diabetes who also lack autoantibodies or have preserved C‑peptide.
  - Young adults with diabetes who have autonomic findings (e.g., sudomotor dysfunction was noted in this case) — don’t over-interpret, but consider as part of the phenotype.

If you have several of these features, proceed to genetic testing rather than prolonged empiric insulin.

## How to practically apply thresholds in clinic (quick checklist)

Order genetic testing when:
- Diabetes onset <25 years plus at least one of:
  - First-degree family history of diabetes in multiple generations, OR
  - Negative islet autoantibodies, OR
  - Preserved C‑peptide beyond expected for T1DM, OR
  - Glycosuria disproportionate to glucose level / prominent postprandial hyperglycaemia.

Consider testing up to age 35 when several supporting features are present (e.g., strong family history + negative autoantibodies + atypical body habitus or low insulin requirements).

## The Exeter MODY probability calculator — useful but imperfect

Why it’s popular:
- Combines age of diagnosis, sex, BMI, family history, treatment, time to insulin to give a probabilistic estimate.
- Helps triage who to test, and is validated in many European cohorts.

Limitations you need to know:
- Developed and calibrated primarily in White European populations. Its performance may differ in Asian, African, Hispanic and other populations. It can overestimate MODY probability in some non‑European groups and underperform when obesity prevalence differs.
- BMI and metabolic features are changing globally; rising obesity blunts BMI’s discriminatory power.
- It uses historical treatment variables (e.g., time to insulin) that may be confounded by local practice patterns or by initial misdiagnosis and empiric therapy.
- It’s probabilistic, not diagnostic. A low score doesn’t absolutely exclude MODY if the clinical picture is convincing; conversely, a high score doesn’t replace genetic confirmation.

How to use it sensibly:
- Use the calculator as a supporting tool — not a gatekeeper.
- In a patient with classic red flags (negative antibodies, strong dominant family history, preserved C‑peptide, early onset), proceed to genetic testing even if the calculator is equivocal or was derived from a different population.
- In populations underrepresented in the calculator’s derivation cohort, interpret its score cautiously; consider lowering the threshold for testing if clinical suspicion is high.

The current case: entering the patient into the Exeter tool gave a 62.4% probability — helpful as a corroborating datapoint, but genetic testing was clinically warranted regardless because of negative autoantibodies, strong family history and preserved stimulated C‑peptide.

## Practical testing strategy and downstream actions

- First-line:
  - Negative autoantibodies and strong clinical suspicion → order a MODY panel (NGS panel covering common MODY genes including HNF1A, HNF4A, GCK, HNF1B).
- If panel finds a pathogenic/likely pathogenic variant:
  - Change management according to gene: e.g., HNF1A — consider low‑dose sulfonylurea or GLP‑1 RA; avoid reflexive long‑term insulin if not required.
  - Cascade testing of at-risk family members.
- If variant of uncertain significance (VUS):
  - Interpret in clinical context; consider segregation testing in family, functional data if available, and reclassification over time.
- If negative panel despite strong suspicion:
  - Consider whole-exome/genome sequencing in specialized centers, or repeat review over time as new variants are reported.

## Clinical pearls from this case

- Don’t assume young = type 1 and older = type 2. Multi‑generation early-onset diabetes with negative autoantibodies is a red flag for MODY.
- Glycosuria in HNF1A can be out of proportion to plasma glucose due to SGLT2 downregulation; SGLT2 inhibitors may carry atypical risks in these patients.
- Genetic diagnosis changes therapy: many HNF1A patients can be taken off insulin and do well on sulfonylureas or GLP‑1 receptor agonists; precision medicine matters.
- Databases (ClinVar, VarSome) are useful, but novel variants can be pathogenic — interpret with ACMG criteria and family segregation.
- Stimulated C‑peptide is a helpful supportive test but is not definitive; trend and clinical context matter.

## Take-home messages

- Consider MODY in young-onset diabetes with autosomal dominant family history, negative islet autoantibodies, preserved C‑peptide and disproportionate postprandial hyperglycaemia or glycosuria.
- Use the Exeter MODY calculator as an adjunct, not a substitute for clinical judgment — especially in non‑European populations where calibration may differ.
- Genetic testing (NGS panels) should be pursued when clinical suspicion is moderate-to-high; a positive result alters treatment, prognosis and prompts family testing.
- In suspected HNF1A-MODY, think about sulfonylurea or GLP‑1 RA strategies and be cautious with SGLT2 inhibitors until you know the genotype.

If you see a young patient whose diabetes “doesn’t fit,” take a focused family history, check autoantibodies and C‑peptide, and lower your threshold for genetic testing — because the result may let you stop insulin, simplify therapy, and give the family answers.
