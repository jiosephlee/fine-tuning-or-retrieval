Title: Blog about the case report: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

# Sulfonylurea or GLP-1 RA for HNF1A‑MODY? Balancing physiology, evidence, and patient priorities

What makes this case interesting: a young man with a novel HNF1A pathogenic variant had dramatic glycaemic improvement after precision‑diagnosed HNF1A‑MODY and a switch off insulin to a GLP‑1 receptor agonist (dulaglutide) plus metformin, with HbA1c falling from 10.2% to 6.0% in months. That outcome forces the clinical question we face increasingly in practice: classically HNF1A‑MODY is “sulfonylurea‑sensitive” — does that still mean SU first for everyone? Or is a GLP‑1 RA often a better fit?

Below I unpack the physiology, the limited randomized data, pragmatic pros/cons, and a patient‑centered approach to choosing an agent.

## Why HNF1A‑MODY is sulfonylurea responsive (the physiology)
HNF1A mutations cause progressive β‑cell dysfunction with reduced insulin secretory response to glucose, but preserved KATP channel function. Sulfonylureas close KATP channels downstream of glucose sensing and can therefore restore insulin secretion effectively — often at low doses — making them historically the cornerstone therapy for many HNF1A patients. Expect good initial glucose lowering and the possibility of stopping insulin in many patients after genetic confirmation.

## Why sulfonylureas are not always ideal
- Hypoglycaemia: SUs increase insulin release irrespective of ambient glucose. In young active patients, hypoglycaemia risk can be meaningful.
- Weight gain: SUs are weight‑neutral to weight‑gaining, which may be undesirable, especially in patients with cardiovascular or metabolic risk.
- Durability: As β‑cell function declines over years, SU efficacy may wane and hypoglycaemia risk may rise.
- Patient preference: Injections, weight goals, hypoglycaemia fear, and comorbidities alter the calculus.

## What the randomized data say about GLP‑1 RAs vs sulfonylureas in HNF1A
High‑quality randomized data are sparse. The main randomized, double‑blind, crossover trial in genetically confirmed HNF1A patients compared liraglutide with glimepiride and found:
- Similar reductions in fasting and postprandial glucose between liraglutide and glimepiride.
- GLP‑1 RA had a lower risk of mild hypoglycaemia vs sulfonylurea.
- GLP‑1 RAs bring additional benefits (weight loss or neutrality, favourable CV signals in high‑risk T2D populations, slower decline of β‑cell stress hypothesized).

This is not a large, long‑term cardiovascular or preservation‑of‑β‑cell function trial, but it suggests comparable glycaemic efficacy with lower hypoglycaemia for GLP‑1 RAs in HNF1A‑MODY.

## Practical pros and cons — side‑by‑side

Sulfonylureas
- Pros: Oral, inexpensive (often), potent glucose lowering in HNF1A at low doses, well established.
- Cons: Hypoglycaemia risk, weight gain, may lose efficacy as β‑cell function declines; less attractive for people with erratic schedules.

GLP‑1 receptor agonists
- Pros: Excellent glycaemic efficacy in many patients, weight loss or neutrality, low hypoglycaemia risk, favourable cardiometabolic profile in some high‑risk patients, may be injectable weekly (adherence benefit).
- Cons: Cost, side effects (nausea), limited HNF1A‑specific long‑term data, injections (though oral semaglutide exists), regulatory/coverage issues in some systems.

Other agents
- DPP‑4 inhibitors: modest glucose lowering; used as add‑ons (as in the presented case) but less potent than GLP‑1 RA.
- SGLT‑2 inhibitors: physiologically tempting but be cautious — HNF1A variants lower renal SGLT2 expression and can cause glycosuria; case reports warn of dehydration and DKA risk. Use with caution and close monitoring.
- Insulin: needed in severe hyperglycaemia or ketosis at presentation; may be withdrawable after diagnosis and targeted therapy.

## How I approach agent selection in practice (patient‑centered algorithm)
1. Confirm the diagnosis:
   - Genetic confirmation of HNF1A changes the plan. Negative islet autoantibodies and preserved C‑peptide support the case, but sequencing is decisive.
2. Assess current glycaemia and β‑cell reserve:
   - Severe hyperglycaemia or catabolism may need initial insulin. Reassess after glucotoxicity resolves.
3. Elicit patient priorities:
   - Fear of hypoglycaemia? Desire to avoid weight gain? Cost concerns? Willingness for injections?
4. Shared decision making:
   - If patient prioritizes minimizing hypoglycaemia and weight control and can afford/accept GLP‑1 RA → reasonable to choose GLP‑1 RA (as in the case where dulaglutide worked well).
   - If patient prefers oral therapy, has limited resources, and accepts hypoglycaemia risk → low‑dose sulfonylurea is still a highly evidence‑based first choice.
5. Dose strategy and monitoring:
   - Sulfonylurea: start low (eg, gliclazide or glimepiride low dose), counsel on hypoglycaemia, frequent glucose checks early on.
   - GLP‑1 RA: start per usual titration, expect GI side effects early; monitor weight, glucose, and injection tolerability.
6. Reassess and plan for long term:
   - Monitor HbA1c, hypoglycaemia events, weight, renal function, and microvascular complications.
   - Be prepared to change therapy as β‑cell function declines — combination therapy or insulin may ultimately be needed.

## Applying this to the presented case
That young man had:
- Strong family history, negative autoantibodies, preserved but insufficient C‑peptide, and novel pathogenic HNF1A variant.
- He presented initially on insulin + metformin + DPP‑4 but after genetic confirmation insulin was stopped and dulaglutide initiated. This aligns with a patient‑centered decision to avoid SU‑related hypoglycaemia and to leverage GLP‑1 benefits — and it worked exceptionally well (HbA1c 10.2% → 6.0%).

This illustrates that precision diagnosis enables personalized therapy: sulfonylurea sensitivity is a guideline, not a mandate. GLP‑1 RAs are a defensible first‑line alternative in many patients, especially where hypoglycaemia, weight, or patient preference matter.

## Clinical pearls
- Don’t assume young‑onset, non‑obese diabetes is type 1: family history, negative autoantibodies, preserved C‑peptide should prompt consideration of MODY genetic testing.
- HNF1A‑MODY often responds well to low‑dose sulfonylureas, but consider GLP‑1 RA when hypoglycaemia risk, weight, or cardiometabolic goals are important.
- GLP‑1 RAs have randomized crossover data in HNF1A showing similar glycaemic control to sulfonylureas and less hypoglycaemia — small studies, but clinically meaningful.
- Be cautious with SGLT‑2 inhibitors in HNF1A‑MODY because of baseline glycosuria and case reports of dehydration/DKA.
- Use shared decision‑making: cost, route (oral vs injection), adverse effects, and lifestyle must guide agent choice.

Key take‑home messages
- Genetic confirmation of HNF1A‑MODY should change your therapeutic thinking — precision diagnosis matters.
- Sulfonylureas remain a low‑cost, effective option and are often first‑line, but they carry hypoglycaemia and weight penalties.
- GLP‑1 receptor agonists are a reasonable, evidence‑supported alternative in HNF1A‑MODY when hypoglycaemia avoidance, weight management, or patient preference favor them.
- Individualize therapy using current betacell reserve, severity of hyperglycaemia, and patient priorities; reassess and adapt as disease progresses.



## SGLT2 inhibitors in HNF1A‑MODY: proceed with caution

Here’s the clinical twist that should make you pause when you see “MODY” in a chart: HNF1A mutations don’t just mess with β‑cells — they transcriptionally downregulate SGLT2 in the proximal tubule. That commonly causes baseline glycosuria and a lowered renal threshold for glucose. Add a pharmacologic SGLT2 inhibitor on top of genetically reduced SGLT2 activity and you can get supraphysiologic glycosuria, excessive osmotic diuresis, dehydration — and, in susceptible patients, euglycaemic diabetic ketoacidosis (euDKA). The case above is a perfect illustration: a young adult with HNF1A‑MODY presented with marked glycosuria and, as the literature warns, there are reports of severe dehydration and DKA linked to excessive SGLT2 inhibition in this population.

Let’s walk through the why, the how worried you should be, and practical guidance if you’re considering SGLT2 therapy for cardiorenal indications in a patient with (or suspected of having) HNF1A‑MODY.

Mechanism — why HNF1A matters for SGLT2 drugs
- HNF1A is a transcription factor that upregulates SGLT2 (SLC5A2) expression in the proximal tubule. Loss‑of‑function HNF1A variants reduce SGLT2 expression → lower renal glucose reabsorption → baseline glycosuria and a lower renal threshold.
- Pharmacologic SGLT2 inhibitors block residual transporter activity. In HNF1A‑MODY this can produce exaggerated glycosuria and osmotic diuresis compared with typical T2D patients.
- Excessive fluid losses can lead to hypovolaemia, prerenal AKI, and trigger counter‑regulatory responses (↑glucagon, ↓insulin), predisposing to ketogenesis and euDKA. Because blood glucose may be relatively low (due to glycosuria), euDKA can be missed until advanced.

How worried should you be?
- The absolute risk is not well quantified — HNF1A‑MODY is uncommon and the evidence is mostly case reports and physiologic plausibility. But the signal is strong enough that multiple authors caution against routine SGLT2 use in confirmed HNF1A‑MODY.
- Consider context: cardiorenal benefits of SGLT2 inhibitors are compelling in many patients, but in a young person with preserved renal function and few cardiorenal risks, the marginal benefit is small and the risk (volume depletion, DKA) may outweigh it. In older patients with strong cardiorenal indication, risk/benefit becomes individualized.

Practical guidance — what to do in clinic
- If the patient has known HNF1A‑MODY:
  - Prefer alternatives first: GLP‑1 receptor agonists (for weight/cardio benefit), metformin, or sulfonylureas for glycaemic control in classic HNF1A‑MODY. These align with the pathophysiology and case outcomes (GLP‑1 RA + metformin worked well above).
  - Avoid SGLT2 inhibitors as routine therapy for glucose lowering. If there’s a compelling cardiorenal indication (e.g., HFrEF, albuminuric CKD) and you still consider an SGLT2, proceed only after careful shared decision‑making and specialist input.
  - If you proceed, make a mitigation plan: baseline hydration status and renal function; teach sick‑day rules; check baseline ketones; frequent follow‑up; low threshold to stop the drug during intercurrent illness, reduced oral intake, or dehydration; monitor for orthostatic symptoms and AKI.
- If MODY is suspected but not yet confirmed (young onset, strong autosomal dominant family history, negative GADA/IA‑2/ZnT8, preserved-ish C‑peptide, pronounced postprandial hyperglycaemia and glycosuria):
  - Consider deferring SGLT2 initiation until genetic testing returns, especially in younger patients without strong cardiorenal indications.
  - Use MODY probability tools and consult endocrinology for testing (NGS panels are increasingly accessible).
- If a patient on an SGLT2 inhibitor presents with unexplained nausea, malaise, abdominal pain, or signs of dehydration — check serum ketones (β‑hydroxybutyrate), acid/base, and creatinine early. euDKA can have only mildly elevated glucose.
- Documentation and consent: if you initiate an SGLT2 in any patient with suggestive features of HNF1A‑MODY, document the discussion of potential increased risk of dehydration/euDKA and the plan for monitoring and sick‑day management.

Clinical pearls
- HNF1A mutations frequently produce glycosuria at lower plasma glucose levels — that finding should trigger suspicion for HNF1A‑MODY in young adults with non‑autoimmune diabetes.
- SGLT2 inhibitors can potentiate a genetic reduction in SGLT2 function — “double blocking” the kidney’s glucose reabsorption and increasing osmotic diuresis.
- euDKA risk with SGLT2 inhibitors is mechanistically plausible in HNF1A‑MODY: dehydration + increased glucagon-to‑insulin ratio + ongoing glycosuria that masks hyperglycaemia.
- GLP‑1 receptor agonists are an attractive alternative in HNF1A‑MODY when you want weight loss, cardiovascular benefit and lower hypoglycaemia risk.

Take‑home messages
- HNF1A‑MODY reduces SGLT2 expression and commonly causes baseline glycosuria — adding an SGLT2 inhibitor can amplify osmotic diuresis and raise the risk of dehydration, AKI and euDKA.
- Avoid routine SGLT2 use for glucose lowering in confirmed HNF1A‑MODY; if there is a strong cardiorenal indication, refer to endocrinology/nephrology and use shared decision‑making with strict safety monitoring.
- In young, non‑autoimmune diabetes with family history and glycosuria, consider MODY genetic testing before starting SGLT2 inhibitors.
- If you do start an SGLT2 in a suspected or confirmed HNF1A patient, ensure clear sick‑day rules, ketone education, hydration advice, and low threshold to stop the drug during illness.

Bottom line: SGLT2 inhibitors are powerful tools — but in HNF1A‑MODY the physiology that makes them attractive in T2D may become a liability. Know the diagnosis or at least suspect it before you “block” the kidney’s last remaining glucose reabsorption.
