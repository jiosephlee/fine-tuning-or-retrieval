Title: Blog about the case report: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

# Not All Early-Onset Diabetes Is T1 or T2: Spotting HNF1A‑MODY at the Front Door

What makes this case worth your attention? A young man walks in with marked hyperglycaemia (HbA1c 10.2%), glycosuria, some weight loss, no ketoacidosis, negative islet autoantibodies, and a preserved—but insufficient—stimulated C‑peptide. He has a striking three‑generation history of early‑onset diabetes. Genetic testing ultimately revealed a novel pathogenic HNF1A variant and the management changed from insulin to targeted, non‑insulin therapy with excellent glycaemic response. This is the kind of case that reminds us: early‑onset glucose dysregulation is not automatically type 1 or type 2.

## Why HNF1A‑MODY can be missed
MODY (monogenic diabetes) is uncommon compared with T1D/T2D, but when present it has big implications for treatment, prognosis and family screening. HNF1A mutations (the most common MODY subtype) produce progressive β‑cell dysfunction, reduced expression of glucose‑sensing genes and a lower renal glucose threshold. Clinically this produces:

- Marked postprandial hyperglycaemia out of proportion to BMI or insulin resistance features.
- Glycosuria at relatively modest plasma glucose (because SGLT2 expression is decreased).
- A family history of autosomal dominant early‑onset diabetes (often 2–3 consecutive generations).
- Negative pancreatic autoantibodies and measurable endogenous insulin secretion—at least early on.

Because of overlap with T1D and T2D (weight may be normal/overweight, symptoms non‑specific), MODY often gets misclassified.

## Bedside and clinic clues from this case
This patient illustrates practical, immediately usable signals that should raise your suspicion for HNF1A‑MODY:

- Glycosuria despite plasma glucose levels that might not explain massive osmotic losses. In HNF1A‑MODY, renal threshold for glucose is lower.
- Negative GAD and IA‑2 antibodies in a young person with hyperglycaemia — this makes T1D less likely but doesn’t prove MODY; it should prompt further evaluation.
- Preserved stimulated C‑peptide (here 0.83 nmol/L after glucagon) indicates residual β‑cell function. That argues against complete autoimmune β‑cell destruction.
- Prominent postprandial hyperglycaemia (2‑hour OGTT 19.8 mmol/L) — HNF1A often affects postprandial glucose first.
- Strong multigenerational family history of early‑onset diabetes (mother, grandmother, sibling) — classic autosomal dominant pattern.
- BMI can mislead: a BMI in the overweight range does not exclude MODY in modern populations where obesity is common.

Also useful in this case: the patient had evidence of autonomic dysfunction on sudomotor testing, reminding us that complications/neuropathy can occur and surveillance is still required.

## How this changed management
Initial management for severely elevated glucose often includes insulin for safety. But the genetic diagnosis changed the therapeutic plan:

- Insulin was stopped once genetic HNF1A‑MODY was confirmed and the patient was transitioned to a GLP‑1 receptor agonist (dulaglutide) plus metformin with excellent HbA1c response (10.2% → 6.0% at 6 months).
- Sulfonylureas have traditionally been first‑line for HNF1A‑MODY (they boost insulin secretion), but GLP‑1RAs are attractive alternatives as β‑cell function wanes and to avoid hypoglycaemia.
- Be cautious with SGLT2 inhibitors: given the low renal glucose threshold and glycosuria in HNF1A‑MODY, SGLT2 inhibitors may increase dehydration, risk of DKA or severe genital infections; weigh risks carefully.
- Genetic confirmation allowed for de‑escalation of insulin and informed family counselling and cascade testing.

## Practical diagnostic approach at the front door
If you see early‑onset diabetes, think beyond the T1/T2 binary. Use these pragmatic steps:

- Ask explicitly about a three‑generation history of early‑onset diabetes. If present, MODY probability rises.
- Send islet autoantibodies (GADA, IA‑2, ZnT8 where available). Negative autoantibodies are supportive (but not definitive).
- Measure stimulated C‑peptide (glucagon stimulation or mixed‑meal test) if available—preserved C‑peptide suggests non‑autoimmune disease.
- Consider an OGTT or postprandial glucose testing; HNF1A commonly shows exaggerated postprandial hyperglycaemia.
- Use MODY probability calculators as adjuncts, but interpret cautiously in non‑White populations (they can over/underestimate risk).
- If clinical suspicion is moderate‑high (family history, negative antibodies, preserved C‑peptide, atypical phenotype), arrange genetic testing (preferably an NGS panel for monogenic diabetes).

## Clinical pearls
- Don’t be reassured by an “overweight” BMI — obesity doesn’t exclude MODY in today’s world.
- Glycosuria in someone with only moderate hyperglycaemia should prompt thinking about HNF1A (low renal glucose threshold).
- Negative islet autoantibodies + preserved C‑peptide in a young adult = get genetic testing if family history or atypical features exist.
- A genetic diagnosis changes therapy (often allows stopping insulin) and enables targeted treatment and cascade family screening.
- Avoid reflexive SGLT2 inhibitor use in HNF1A until you’ve considered the risk of exaggerated glycosuria and volume depletion.

## Take‑home messages
- Early‑onset diabetes is not always type 1 or type 2—look for red flags: multigenerational early diabetes, glycosuria out of proportion to glucose level, negative autoantibodies, and preserved C‑peptide.
- Postprandial testing (OGTT) is valuable: HNF1A often causes prominent postprandial hyperglycaemia.
- BMI is a poor discriminator for MODY in modern populations; family history and physiology matter more.
- Genetic testing is the definitive step—when suspicion is high it directs therapy, reduces unnecessary insulin, and enables family counselling.
- In HNF1A‑MODY, sulfonylureas and GLP‑1RAs are reasonable choices; be cautious with SGLT2 inhibitors.

This patient’s case is a tidy example of precision medicine in diabetes: recognizing the phenotype at the bedside led to genetic confirmation and a targeted, safer, and effective treatment strategy. Keep MODY on your differential—your diagnosis can materially change a young person’s life.
