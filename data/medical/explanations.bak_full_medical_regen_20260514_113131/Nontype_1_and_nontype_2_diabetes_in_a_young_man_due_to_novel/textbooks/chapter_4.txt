Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

Chapter 4: ## Precision Medicine, Counseling, and Clinical Pearls

This patient’s course illustrates how a single genetic result can reframe diagnosis, treatment and family care. HNF1A-MODY is not an academic label: it is a molecular diagnosis that changes the expected natural history of disease, opens specific therapeutic options, identifies people at 50% risk in first‑degree relatives, and directs reproductive counselling. Thinking of diabetes as monogenic when onset is young, antibodies are negative and insulin secretion is relatively preserved transforms management from “type 1 versus type 2” heuristics into precision medicine.

Therapeutic implications are among the most immediate clinical consequences of a confirmed HNF1A pathogenic variant. Historically, low‑dose sulfonylureas have been the most effective oral agents for many patients with HNF1A‑MODY because they directly increase insulin release from the remaining β‑cell mass. In practice, sulfonylureas frequently allow successful withdrawal of insulin if endogenous secretion is adequate. More recently, incretin‑based therapies (GLP‑1 receptor agonists and, to a lesser extent, DPP‑4 inhibitors) have emerged as useful alternatives. GLP‑1 receptor agonists combine glucose‑dependent insulin secretion, postprandial glucose control and weight benefits while carrying a lower hypoglycaemia risk than sulfonylureas—features attractive in HNF1A patients who can be very sensitive to insulin secretagogues. The case demonstrates this principle: after genetic confirmation, insulin was de‑escalated and a GLP‑1 receptor agonist plus metformin achieved durable glycaemic control with reduced hypoglycaemia risk. Conversely, agents that further increase glycosuria (SGLT2 inhibitors) should be used cautiously because HNF1A variants reduce SGLT2 expression and baseline glycosuria is common; the theoretical risk of dehydration and euglycaemic ketoacidosis may be higher in some patients.

Once a pathogenic or likely pathogenic HNF1A variant is identified, cascade testing of at‑risk relatives should be offered. Cascade testing is high‑value medicine: a single proband’s result allows targeted, less expensive testing of relatives who can then be spared diagnostic odysseys, unnecessary insulin therapy, or delayed appropriate oral therapy. Pre‑test and post‑test genetic counselling are essential to discuss inheritance (autosomal dominant, ~50% transmission), age‑dependent penetrance and the options for predictive testing in adults. Testing children raises ethical questions: testing asymptomatic minors is generally deferred unless results will change management during childhood, but in some families early diagnosis may allow surveillance and early lifestyle interventions. Reproductive counselling should include the 50% transmission risk, discussion of prenatal and preimplantation genetic testing options, and the potential implications for pregnancy management.

Accurate documentation of the variant is a practical necessity. The clinic note and problem list should record the exact nucleotide change, protein change, exon and affected domain (for example, c.809A>G, p.N270S, exon 4, DNA‑binding domain), the laboratory that performed testing, and the ACMG classification (pathogenic/likely pathogenic/variant of uncertain significance). Include the evidence used to support the classification (segregation, population frequency, in silico predictions, functional data if available). Variants of uncertain significance (VUS) must be managed as indeterminate: do not use a VUS to change therapy or recommend cascade predictive testing. Because public databases and functional data evolve, plan regular re‑evaluation of VUS (for example annually or if new family data emerges) and notify families of reclassification; many clinical laboratories will re‑note reclassifications, but clinicians should also maintain an active follow‑up process.

Expect variable expressivity and incomplete penetrance within affected families. Even with the same pathogenic HNF1A variant, age at onset, severity of hyperglycaemia and coexisting metabolic features differ among relatives. Rising population obesity means that an HNF1A pathogenic variant can coexist with obesity and insulin resistance, which blurs phenotypic distinctions from type 2 diabetes. Do not exclude MODY simply because BMI is above the historical “lean” picture. Conversely, some carriers may have minimal hyperglycaemia for years; surveillance rather than immediate treatment may be appropriate in such cases.

A pragmatic approach to separating type 1 diabetes from MODY in clinic will reduce missed diagnoses. Measure islet autoantibodies (GAD65, IA‑2, ZnT8) at presentation; their presence makes autoimmune type 1 diabetes far more likely. Assess endogenous insulin secretion with either fasting or stimulated C‑peptide testing (glucagon stimulation or mixed‑meal tolerance test). In practice, persistence of detectable C‑peptide and negative autoantibodies beyond the initial presentation—and especially beyond 3–5 years after diagnosis—should prompt reconsideration for MODY testing. (By contrast, most classic type 1 patients become C‑peptide‑negative within several years.) Interpret C‑peptide with the assay units used locally; in many centers a stimulated C‑peptide >0.6 nmol/L (≈1.8 ng/mL) is considered evidence of residual β‑cell function, but local thresholds vary.

There are several high‑yield clinical clues that increase the pre‑test probability of HNF1A‑MODY. Disproportionate glycosuria—glycosuria at moderate plasma glucose levels—reflects a reduced renal glucose threshold related to lower SGLT2 expression under HNF1A control and should raise suspicion. Low high‑sensitivity CRP (hsCRP) relative to typical type 2 diabetes has been reported in HNF1A cases and can be a supportive (not definitive) biomarker. A clear autosomal dominant pedigree with several generations of early‑onset diabetes, prominent postprandial hyperglycaemia on OGTT, and absence of features of severe insulin resistance (acanthosis, extreme dyslipidaemia) are further pointers. Use these features to select patients most likely to benefit from genetic testing rather than testing unselected populations.

From a systems and cost‑effectiveness perspective, targeted testing strategies yield the most value. For patients with classic phenotypes you may choose a single‑gene test (for example, GCK if mild fasting hyperglycaemia is found, HNF1A if prominent postprandial hyperglycaemia with family history), which can be less expensive and faster. When the phenotype is less specific—or when multiple monogenic forms are plausible—multi‑gene NGS panels are efficient because they reduce the need for serial testing and have become affordable. Preauthorization and knowledgeable genetic counselling improve reimbursement success. Cascade testing of relatives is generally inexpensive compared with full panel testing and should be offered whenever a pathogenic variant is confirmed in a proband.

Operational implementation improves case detection. Embed decision support in young‑onset diabetes pathways (for example, automatic flags in the electronic medical record for diabetes diagnosed before age 35, negative autoantibodies and preserved C‑peptide) and establish referral protocols with regional genetic services. Create a standard “MODY letter” for families that includes the molecular result, clinical implications, recommended relatives for testing and contact details for genetic counselling; this facilitates cascade testing and avoids loss to follow‑up. Partnering with clinical genetics teams streamlines informed consent, testing logistics and family counselling, and ensures appropriate interpretation of VUS and reclassification workflows.

In short, the practical clinical pearls are: treat the genetic diagnosis as actionable (it often permits insulin withdrawal or a switch to sulfonylurea or incretin‑based therapy); offer cascade testing to identify at‑risk relatives and direct surveillance; document variant details and classification and re‑assess VUS over time; do not be reassured by obesity alone—phenotypic overlap is common; and use a combination of negative autoantibodies and persistent C‑peptide (especially beyond 3–5 years) to trigger MODY evaluation. Embedding simple screening prompts and close collaboration with genetic services will increase diagnostic yield and convert genetic knowledge into better, safer, and more personalized care for patients and their families.
