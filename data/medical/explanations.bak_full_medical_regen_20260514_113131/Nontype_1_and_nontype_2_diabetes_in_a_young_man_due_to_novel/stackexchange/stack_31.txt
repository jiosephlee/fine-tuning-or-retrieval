Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

### [Differential Diagnosis] How should the occasional presence of islet autoantibodies in patients with MODY be interpreted, and which clinical features retain primacy in distinguishing MODY from type 1 diabetes?

Short answer: an occasional positive islet autoantibody does not reliably exclude MODY. The significance of any antibody result must be judged by its number, titre and persistence, and — most importantly — by the clinical phenotype. High‑titre or multiple positive autoantibodies that appear early and are followed by rapid loss of C‑peptide point to autoimmune (type 1) diabetes; an isolated, low‑titer antibody (especially a single positive GAD) can be incidental/false positive and should not by itself preclude genetic testing for MODY when the clinical picture fits.

Clinical reasoning and practical approach: autoantibodies are biomarkers of autoimmune β‑cell destruction, not direct proof of monogenic disease. In population studies a small fraction of patients with genetically proven MODY have detectable islet antibodies — usually single, low‑titer, non‑persistent results. Assay variability and low positive predictive value for single antibodies mean that isolated positivity can mislead. By contrast, type 1 diabetes typically shows two or more autoantibodies (GAD, IA‑2, ZnT8, IAA), often at higher titres, and a predictable clinical course of progressive insulin deficiency with early requirement for insulin and risk of DKA.

Which clinical features retain primacy when distinguishing MODY from type 1:
- Family history of early‑onset, autosomal‑dominant diabetes across successive generations (strong clue to MODY).
- Pattern and tempo of β‑cell failure: preserved or only slowly declining C‑peptide (stimulated C‑peptide >0.6 nmol/L beyond the honeymoon period) argues for MODY; brisk loss of C‑peptide supports type 1.
- Age at onset and presentation: classic MODY often presents in adolescence/young adulthood without DKA; type 1 presents at any age but with acute symptomatic hyperglycaemia and frequent DKA.
- Insulin requirement: low long‑term insulin needs or stable control on oral agents favors MODY; early, sustained insulin dependence favors type 1.
- Associated features: glycosuria at modest glucose levels (reduced renal threshold) and prominent postprandial hyperglycaemia are typical of HNF1A‑MODY; signs of insulin resistance/obesity point away from classical MODY but do not exclude it in today’s population.
- Autoantibody profile: multiple, high‑titer antibodies carry primacy for type 1; a single, low‑titer or transient antibody should be interpreted cautiously.

Clinical application: do not exclude MODY solely because of a single positive antibody. If the phenotype (strong autosomal family history, preserved C‑peptide, absence of DKA, glycosuria, typical treatment response) suggests MODY, repeat antibody testing, document C‑peptide, and proceed to genetic testing when warranted. Conversely, multiple high‑titer autoantibodies with rapid beta‑cell loss should steer management and counseling toward type 1 diabetes.
