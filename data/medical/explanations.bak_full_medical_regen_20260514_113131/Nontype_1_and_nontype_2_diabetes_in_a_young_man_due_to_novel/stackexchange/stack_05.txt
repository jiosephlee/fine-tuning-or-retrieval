Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

### [Morning Report] Which autoantibodies are most informative when evaluating for autoimmune type 1 diabetes in a young adult, and how do negative results influence the diagnostic workup?

When you’re trying to distinguish autoimmune type 1 diabetes from other causes of early‑onset hyperglycaemia, the most informative autoantibodies to order are glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA), insulinoma‑associated‑2 antibodies (IA‑2A), zinc transporter 8 antibodies (ZnT8), and—if the patient has not yet received exogenous insulin—insulin autoantibodies (IAA). Historically islet cell cytoplasmic antibodies (ICA) were used, but they add little beyond the above assays.

Why these matter: GADA is the most sensitive single marker in adults with new‑onset type 1 diabetes; IA‑2A and ZnT8 increase sensitivity and specificity when combined, and IAA is useful early in disease in children or before insulin exposure. A profile with two or more positive high‑titer antibodies is strongly supportive of autoimmune type 1 disease; a single low‑titer antibody is less definitive and needs clinical correlation.

How negatives change your diagnostic reasoning: negative GADA/IA‑2A/ZnT8/IAA substantially reduces the probability of autoimmune type 1 diabetes but does not rule it out entirely. False‑negative results can occur if testing is done long after disease onset (antibodies can wane), if assays vary in sensitivity, or with atypical immune phenotypes. Therefore negative antibodies should prompt you to integrate other data: measure C‑peptide (fasting and stimulated) to assess endogenous insulin secretion; review tempo of hyperglycaemia, presence/absence of ketoacidosis, BMI and features of insulin resistance, and detailed family history. If C‑peptide is preserved or only modestly reduced, antibodies are repeatedly negative, and there is a strong autosomal dominant family history or atypical course, pursue genetic testing for monogenic diabetes (MODY). Conversely, rapidly falling C‑peptide or development of DKA despite negative antibodies should keep autoimmune type 1 high on your differential and warrant repeat antibody testing and close follow‑up.

Apply this to the case: the patient had negative GADA and IA‑2A, a stimulated C‑peptide indicating residual β‑cell function, and a striking family history of early‑onset diabetes. The negative antibody panel lowered the likelihood of classic autoimmune type 1 disease and, along with preserved C‑peptide and pedigree, correctly led to targeted genetic testing that confirmed HNF1A‑MODY.
