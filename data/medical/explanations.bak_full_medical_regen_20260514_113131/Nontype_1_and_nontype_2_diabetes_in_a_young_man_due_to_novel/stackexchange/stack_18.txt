Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

### [Management] What is the inheritance risk for offspring when a pathogenic HNF1A variant is identified in a proband, and how should this inform genetic counselling?

When a pathogenic HNF1A variant is identified in a proband, the key inheritance message is simple and actionable: HNF1A-MODY is transmitted in an autosomal dominant manner, so each biological child of the proband has an approximately 50% chance of inheriting the variant.

Why that matters for counselling: autosomal dominant means a single heterozygous pathogenic allele is sufficient to cause disease risk, but expression is age-dependent and variable. HNF1A has relatively high but incomplete and age-related penetrance — many carriers develop clinically manifest diabetes by young or middle adulthood, but onset and severity vary between families and even between affected members. Therefore a positive test predicts substantially increased lifetime risk and commonly earlier onset diabetes, whereas a negative test in a child or relative effectively rules out HNF1A-MODY as the explanation for early-onset diabetes in that family and allows usual population screening/care.

Practical counselling points to communicate to the family
- Recurrence risk: each child of an affected parent has a ~50% chance of inheriting the familial HNF1A pathogenic variant. If the proband’s variant is confirmed de novo (no parental carrier), recurrence in siblings is low but each offspring of the proband remains at 50%.
- Testing strategy: offer targeted (cascade) genetic testing to first‑degree relatives (parents, siblings, children). Testing is most useful when it would change management or surveillance; because HNF1A disease typically manifests in adolescence or early adulthood, consider testing minors when results would alter clinical care (some centres offer testing in childhood if glycaemic surveillance or medication strategies would be affected); otherwise many teams defer until adolescence or when the family prefers earlier knowledge.
- Clinical follow-up for carriers: if a relative is found to carry the variant, institute earlier and more frequent glucose surveillance (fasting glucose, HbA1c, or OGTT as indicated), counsel about lifestyle, and tailor pharmacologic choices (sulfonylureas, GLP‑1 agonists often effective; avoid misclassification as type 1). If negative, no special MODY-related follow-up is required beyond routine diabetes screening.
- Reproductive options: explain reproductive choices for carriers who wish to avoid transmission — prenatal diagnosis or preimplantation genetic testing are available when the familial pathogenic variant is known.
- Psychosocial and legal considerations: discuss the emotional impact, potential insurance or employment implications that depend on local law, and refer to genetic counselling for informed consent and detailed discussion.

Finally, coordinate cascade testing through a genetics service (or an experienced endocrine genetics clinic) so family members get pre‑ and post‑test counselling, proper interpretation (including discussion of penetrance and variable expressivity), and a plan linking genetic results to surveillance and treatment decisions.
