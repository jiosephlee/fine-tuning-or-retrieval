Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

### [Management] Once genetic confirmation of HNF1A-MODY is obtained and glycaemic control improves, what medication adjustments are reasonable, and what principles guide de-intensification?

Once HNF1A‑MODY is confirmed and glycaemia has improved, the goal is to simplify therapy to the least intensive regimen that safely maintains target glucose while preserving remaining β‑cell function and minimising hypoglycaemia. The choices and timing of de‑intensification are determined by three clinical measurements—current HbA1c/glucose trends, evidence of endogenous insulin secretion (stimulated C‑peptide), and the patient’s hypoglycaemia risk and preferences.

If stimulated C‑peptide is preserved (many centres use the glucagon‑stimulated threshold >0.6 nmol/L as evidence of meaningful β‑cell reserve) and the patient is not insulin‑dependent, it is reasonable to discontinue exogenous insulin and move to oral/GLP‑1–based therapy with close follow‑up. Historically low‑dose sulfonylureas are the canonical first‑line therapy for HNF1A‑MODY because they directly amplify the defective insulin secretory response; start low, titrate to effect, and counsel about hypoglycaemia. GLP‑1 receptor agonists are a modern, attractive alternative when weight, cardiovascular risk or hypoglycaemia concerns favour them — they improve glycaemia with less hypoglycaemia and may preserve β‑cell function. Metformin can be continued when there is clinical evidence of insulin resistance or metabolic syndrome; it is not disease‑specific but is often helpful adjunctive therapy. DPP‑4 inhibitors have less evidence but may be used if GLP‑1RA are contraindicated or not tolerated. Avoid routine use of SGLT‑2 inhibitors as first‑line in HNF1A‑MODY because HNF1A mutations reduce SGLT2 expression and these agents can increase glycosuria and, rarely, precipitate dehydration or euglycaemic ketoacidosis in patients with limited insulin reserve.

Practical de‑intensification approach: confirm genetic result and reassess C‑peptide; if reserve is adequate and glucose improving, gradually taper insulin rather than abrupt cessation in patients on higher doses—stop basal first if fasting glucose is near target, monitor capillary glucoses closely (daily), and plan review within 1–2 weeks. Start or switch to low‑dose sulfonylurea (or GLP‑1RA) when insulin stopped; continue metformin if indicated. Reassess HbA1c at 3 months and C‑peptide periodically if clinical status changes. Educate the patient about hypoglycaemia recognition, sick‑day rules, and symptoms of ketosis; provide written plan for when to restart insulin if control deteriorates.

Finally, individualise: older patients, pregnancy plans, very low C‑peptide, or histories of severe hyperglycaemia may require continued insulin. Genetic diagnosis allows precision tailoring, but safe de‑intensification hinges on objective β‑cell reserve, careful glucose monitoring, and early follow‑up.
