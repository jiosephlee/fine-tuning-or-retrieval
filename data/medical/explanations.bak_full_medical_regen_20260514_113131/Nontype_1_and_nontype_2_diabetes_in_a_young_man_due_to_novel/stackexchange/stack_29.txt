Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

### [Morning Report] What bedside or clinic test can assess small-fiber autonomic function in diabetes, and what does severely reduced sudomotor conductance signify in a newly diagnosed patient?

The simplest clinic bedside test for small‑fiber autonomic function used in many diabetes clinics is sudomotor assessment by electrochemical skin conductance (eg, Sudoscan). This noninvasive device measures sweat gland (sudomotor) activity by recording electrochemical skin conductance in the palms and soles and provides an index of small unmyelinated C‑fiber sympathetic function. Other ways to test small‑fiber/ autonomic function include the quantitative sudomotor axon reflex test (QSART), thermoregulatory sweat testing, skin biopsy for intraepidermal nerve‑fiber density, and components of a formal autonomic reflex screen (heart‑rate variability, Valsalva, tilt-table), but Sudoscan is the rapid, clinic‑friendly option.

Severely reduced sudomotor conductance in a newly diagnosed diabetic indicates clinically significant small‑fiber autonomic neuropathy involving sympathetic cholinergic fibers. Clinically, this means the patient has early autonomic involvement and is at higher risk for orthostatic intolerance, gastroparesis, bladder dysfunction, impaired thermoregulation, silent myocardial ischemia and impaired wound healing in the feet. In a young patient with apparently recent diagnosis (as in this case of HNF1A‑MODY), severe reduction most likely reflects prolonged or marked hyperglycaemia prior to diagnosis (or an unusually aggressive neuropathic process) rather than measurement artifact; it should prompt a systematic search for other autonomic and small‑fiber complications, optimization of glycemic control, education about foot care and hydration/heat intolerance, and consideration of referral for formal autonomic testing and neurology/endocrine follow‑up.
