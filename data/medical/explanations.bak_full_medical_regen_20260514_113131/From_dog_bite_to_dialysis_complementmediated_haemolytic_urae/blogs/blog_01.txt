Title: Blog about the case report: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

# Sepsis, DIC, and the TMA switch: Recognizing CM‑HUS When the Dust Settles

What makes this case stick in your head? A previously well woman is bitten by her dog, four days later develops fulminant Capnocytophaga canimorsus sepsis with DIC and multi‑organ failure, is treated and starts to improve — and then suddenly develops severe haemolysis, persistent thrombocytopaenia and dialysis‑dependent AKI. The haemolysis only becomes obvious as the inflammatory storm abates. The culprit? A superimposed thrombotic microangiopathy — complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS) — unmasked when routine coagulation tests normalized. This is the clinical trap: once the sepsis is controlled, a second process (TMA) can emerge or persist, and unless you look for it you will miss it — with major prognostic implications.

Below I walk through the diagnostic thinking and practical steps prompted by this case, with specific pointers you can use at the bedside.

## Quick case recap (the parts that matter)
- Late‑40s woman bitten on the lip by her dog; rapidly progressive sepsis 4 days later.
- Initial picture: severe inflammation, pancytopenia, coagulopathy consistent with DIC, AKI progressing to anuria and dialysis. Blood cultures later grew Capnocytophaga canimorsus.
- After initial improvement on antibiotics/supportive care: sudden fall in haemoglobin, undetectable haptoglobin, rising LDH, platelets persistently low, peripheral smear showed 15–20% schistocytes.
- DAT briefly positive (likely false positive from penicillin/transfusion), later negative. ADAMTS13 43.5% (TTP unlikely). C3/C4 normal. Kidney biopsy: fibrin thrombi, ischaemic glomeruli and ATN—consistent with TMA.
- Management: PEX as bridge, then eculizumab started after blood cultures negative; meningococcal vaccines and antimicrobial prophylaxis used. Haematologic remission and partial renal recovery; no pathogenic complement variants identified.

## When to suspect a superimposed TMA in a septic patient
In the heat of fulminant sepsis you reasonably assume DIC is the cause of coagulopathy and microthrombi. But there are warning signs that a separate TMA is present or is emerging as the infection is controlled:

- Falling haemoglobin without overt bleeding while infection markers and coagulation tests (INR, aPTT, fibrinogen, D‑dimer) are improving.
- Rising LDH and undetectable or very low haptoglobin — biochemical evidence of intravascular haemolysis.
- Persistent or recrudescent thrombocytopaenia despite resolving coagulopathy.
- New or increasing schistocytosis on peripheral smear.
- Worsening or dialysis‑dependent AKI that is disproportionate to other organ dysfunction or does not recover as sepsis resolves.
In the case above the haemolysis only became apparent as inflammatory and coagulation markers normalized — a classic “TMA when the dust settles” pattern.

## The peripheral smear is high‑value, low‑cost, and underused
Do a smear early and repeat it. Schistocytes are the hallmark of microangiopathic haemolysis. Automated counters miss schistocytes; look at the slide. Guidelines commonly use ≥1% schistocytes as abnormal; in this patient the smear was dramatic (15–20%), strongly supporting TMA. Also review platelets, polychromasia/reticulocytes and other clues (toxic neutrophils vs haemophagocytosis, blasts).

## Triage: TTP first (PLASMIC, ADAMTS13), then work up TMA causes
- TTP is a medical emergency — if suspected, start plasma exchange emergently. Use PLASMIC score to triage while awaiting ADAMTS13.
- ADAMTS13 <10% essentially confirms TTP; values >10% (our patient 43.5%) argue against it and should pivot your workup away from TTP.
- If TTP is excluded and Shiga toxin is negative, think CM‑HUS, especially with severe AKI and biopsy‑proven TMA.

## Laboratory caveats and timing
- C3 and C4 may be normal in CM‑HUS — normal complement levels do NOT exclude CM‑HUS.
- Send baseline ADAMTS13, C3/C4 and complement functional assays before plasma exchange or complement blockade if possible. PEX removes complement components and eculizumab will confound functional assays (AH50, etc.).
- A positive DAT may be a red herring (penicillin or transfusion‑related). If DAT positive but smear showing schistocytes and low haptoglobin/LDH high, consider concurrent MAHA/TMA.
- Genetic complement testing takes time and can be negative even in infection‑triggered CM‑HUS.

## Role of kidney biopsy
When safe and feasible it can be diagnostic. This case had fibrin thrombi, ischaemic glomeruli and crescents consistent with TMA and helped clinch the diagnosis.

## Therapeutics — practical balance between PEX and eculizumab
- PEX is lifesaving in TTP and can be a reasonable bridge when diagnosis is uncertain; it may transiently improve CM‑HUS but does not correct the underlying complement dysregulation.
- Eculizumab (terminal complement blockade) is the targeted therapy for CM‑HUS and is associated with hematologic remission and improved renal outcomes — earlier initiation usually equals better kidney recovery.
- Infection dilemma: complement is important for defence against encapsulated organisms. In active bacteraemia you should control infection and ideally have negative cultures before starting eculizumab. If urgent eculizumab is given, vaccinate (meningococcal) and give appropriate antimicrobial prophylaxis; remember vaccines require time to induce immunity and PEX/rituals can remove monoclonal antibody or vaccine response.
In the reported patient, PEX was used as a bridge and eculizumab started after cultures became negative; meningococcal vaccines were given and prophylactic Apocillin used because the usual 2‑week vaccine lead time wasn’t available.

## Pathophysiologic takeaway
Infection can be the “second hit” that triggers complement overactivation in susceptible hosts. Capnocytophaga canimorsus has variable interactions with complement and, although rare, has been reported as a trigger for CM‑HUS. Importantly, CM‑HUS can occur without identifiable genetic variants — infection alone can be sufficient.

## Practical bedside algorithm (simplified)
1. Septic patient with coagulopathy/DIC — treat infection, support organ systems.
2. If haemoglobin falls without bleeding, LDH rises, haptoglobin falls, platelets remain low, or schistocytes appear — suspect TMA.
3. Send urgent: peripheral smear, haptoglobin, LDH, reticulocyte count, DAT, ADAMTS13, C3/C4 (and complement functional tests if available) — ideally before PEX/eculizumab.
4. If high suspicion for TTP or PLASMIC score high — start PEX immediately.
5. If TTP excluded and TMA persists with severe AKI — discuss eculizumab with nephrology/hematology; consider PEX as bridge; manage infection aggressively; vaccinate and give prophylaxis if initiating terminal complement blockade.
6. Consider kidney biopsy if diagnosis unclear and it’s safe.

## Clinical pearls
- Don’t wait for dramatic lab derangement — get a smear early and repeat it. Schistocytes are the signal that changes the game.
- Normal INR/PT/fibrinogen as sepsis resolves + ongoing haemolysis = think TMA, not persistent DIC.
- ADAMTS13 <10% = TTP; if >10% look elsewhere. Use PLASMIC to triage while awaiting results.
- C3/C4 can be normal in CM‑HUS — normal complement tests do not exclude it.
- Time your samples: PEX and eculizumab will confound complement testing and vaccine timing.
- Eculizumab is effective for CM‑HUS but takes the immune edge off; ensure cultures are controlled, vaccinate and use antibiotic prophylaxis.
- Infection‑triggered CM‑HUS may occur without identifiable genetic variants — absence of a mutation does not exclude the diagnosis.

Key take‑home points
- Think TMA when haemolysis (↑LDH, ↓haptoglobin), schistocytes and persistent thrombocytopaenia appear despite improving sepsis/coagulation.
- Smear + ADAMTS13 (and PLASMIC for triage) get you on the right pathway quickly: PEX for TTP, eculizumab for CM‑HUS.
- Complement tests and genetics are useful but time‑sensitive and easily confounded by PEX/eculizumab; do baseline sampling before those therapies if possible.
- When treating CM‑HUS in the setting of recent or active infection, balance timing of eculizumab against infection control, ensure vaccinations and prophylaxis, and involve nephrology/haematology early.

This Capnocytophaga canimorsus case is an elegant reminder: once the inflammatory fog lifts, look for schistocytes. If you do, you’ll catch the TMA switch before it costs kidneys.
