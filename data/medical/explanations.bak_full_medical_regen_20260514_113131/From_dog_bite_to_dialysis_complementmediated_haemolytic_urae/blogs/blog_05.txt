Title: Blog about the case report: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

# Don’t Trust the C3: Making Sense of Complement Studies and Genetics in CM‑HUS

What’s interesting: a previously healthy woman developed fulminant sepsis 4 days after a dog bite from Capnocytophaga canimorsus, progressed to DIC and dialysis-dependent acute kidney injury, then — after initial improvement — developed a sudden microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia with schistocytes. C3 and C4 were normal. ADAMTS13 was not consistent with TTP. Kidney biopsy showed thrombotic microangiopathy and the team treated with plasma exchange then eculizumab with clinical improvement. Extended testing showed a very low alternative pathway functional assay (AH50 = 6%) — but that was drawn after plasma exchange (PEX) and one dose of eculizumab. Genetic testing later returned without pathogenic variants.

The punchline: normal C3/C4 does not exclude complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS). Timing of sampling, prior PEX and the use of terminal complement blockade (eculizumab) can profoundly change functional and quantitative complement assays. Misinterpreting those results risks both over‑calling consumption and under‑treating a life‑threatening TMA.

Here’s a practical, case‑grounded guide to what to send, when to send it, how to interpret results in the context of therapies, and how genetics should (and should not) drive treatment decisions.

## Why “normal C3/C4” is misleading
C3 and C4 are crude systemic markers. In CM‑HUS the pathologic complement activation is often local (microvascular endothelium) and can occur with normal systemic C3/C4. Complement activation fragments can be rapidly turned over, redistributed, or replaced by donor plasma during PEX. In sepsis, complement levels can be variably low, normal, or even elevated depending on timing and the pathogen. In this case C3/C4 were within reference range early, yet the patient clearly had a TMA driven by complement dysregulation — shown by biopsy and a dramatic response to complement blockade.

Key applications:
- Don’t use normal C3/C4 to “rule out” CM‑HUS.
- Use C3/C4 as part of the picture, not the deciding factor.

## What to send — and try to get before plasma exchange or eculizumab
If you suspect TMA (MAHA, schistocytes, thrombocytopenia ± AKI), prioritize tests that change immediate management and that are invalidated by PEX or eculizumab if delayed.

Urgent/early tests to obtain before PEX/eculizumab if at all possible:
- ADAMTS13 activity (and inhibitor) — to exclude TTP (activity <10% typical of TTP). Local labs are usually fastest.
- Shiga‑toxin PCR from stool — to exclude STEC‑HUS.
- Routine complement panel: C3, C4 (understand their limits).
- Functional assays: CH50 (classical pathway) and AH50 (alternative pathway) — these are very timing‑sensitive.
- Complement protein levels: Factor H, Factor I, Factor B, and soluble C5b‑9 when available.
- Anti‑factor H autoantibody testing (ELISA).
- Samples for genetic testing: EDTA whole blood for a complement gene panel (CFH, CFI, CFB, C3, MCP/CD46, THBD, etc.). These are not urgent for acute decisionmaking but collect early because once you start PEX or transfusions, DNA testing is still fine but factors/autoantibodies may be altered.
- Send a sample for frozen serum (avoid hemolysis) for any specialized tests your reference lab requests — many require specific collection/transport conditions.

Practical note from the case: ADAMTS13 was available quickly and was 43.5% (not TTP). The team drew extended complement testing but logistical constraints meant some samples were taken after Octaplas PEX and one dose of eculizumab — which explains the very low AH50 result despite normal C3/C4 earlier.

## How PEX and eculizumab change the lab picture
PEX:
- Removes circulating complement proteins, activation fragments and autoantibodies.
- Replaces them with donor plasma (which may transiently normalize or alter levels).
- Can make interpretation of complement kinetics impossible for days; autoantibody testing and functional assays can be falsely low or normalized depending on timing and the replacement product (eg, Octaplas).

Eculizumab:
- Binds C5 and blocks terminal complement activation.
- Causes rapid and profound reduction in functional terminal pathway assays (CH50 and often AH50 as well).
- After eculizumab, CH50 typically falls to near zero; AH50 may also be low because assays measure terminal pathway function.
- Eculizumab itself does not change genetic testing, but it confounds functional assays for the entire course of therapy.

Interpreting low AH50 or CH50 after treatment:
- If the test was drawn after eculizumab → low CH50/AH50 is expected and reflects drug effect, not disease consumption.
- If drawn after PEX → low or normalized results could reflect removal or donor replacement; either way, the result is unreliable for diagnostic attribution.
- Always document timing of PEX/eculizumab on the lab requisition and tell the reference lab — they can often interpret results in that context.

In the case, the AH50 of 6% was most consistent with treatment effects (PEX + eculizumab) rather than intrinsic complement depletion.

## Putting the lab data in clinical context
Diagnosis of CM‑HUS is clinical + pathologic + exclusionary:
- Look for MAHA (schistocytes), thrombocytopenia and AKI ± biopsy evidence of TMA.
- Exclude TTP (ADAMTS13) and STEC‑HUS (stool PCR) promptly.
- Consider timing: infection‑triggered CM‑HUS often becomes apparent as sepsis is improving (as in this patient).
- Complement labs can help but will not always show “consumption” — the biopsy and response to complement blockade are powerful diagnostic clues.
- If biopsy shows fibrin thrombi and TMA, that supports treating for CM‑HUS if other causes are excluded.

## Genetics: what a negative or positive result means for therapy duration
Background yield: a substantial minority of CM‑HUS patients have identifiable pathogenic variants or anti‑factor H antibodies (historically quoted roughly 50–70% may have variants/antibodies depending on cohort and testing depth). However, penetrance is incomplete and many cases are triggered by identifiable secondary events (infection, pregnancy, drugs).

How results affect management:
- Pathogenic variant (especially CFH) or anti‑factor H antibodies: increased risk of relapse; favors longer‑term or indefinite complement blockade and closer monitoring.
- MCP/CD46 mutations often have a milder renal prognosis and sometimes allow treatment withdrawal considerations once clinically stable.
- No pathogenic variant: raises possibility of single‑hit, infection‑triggered CM‑HUS. You can consider finite duration of eculizumab (eg, months) with planned reassessment and rapid withdrawal plan, but decisions should be individualized by severity, renal recovery, and multidisciplinary input.
- A negative result does NOT exclude CM‑HUS or mean that complement blockade was unnecessary. Genetic testing is a tool for risk stratification, not an acute diagnostic gatekeeper.

In the presented case, no pathogenic variants were found. Because the patient had severe disease and partial renal recovery, the treating team continued maintenance eculizumab with plans for reassessment — a pragmatic balance between relapse risk and therapy harms.

## Practical management tips rooted in the case
- Get ADAMTS13 and stool Shiga PCR early — those results change immediate therapy.
- Whenever feasible, draw complement functional assays (CH50/AH50), factor H/I/B levels, anti‑FH antibodies and freeze serum for reference testing before PEX or eculizumab. If you can’t, document timing of interventions and expect assay limitations.
- If you must start PEX or eculizumab urgently, don’t delay life‑saving therapy for specialized testing — but still draw baseline samples first if possible.
- Start eculizumab after reasonable control of the underlying infection (ideally after blood cultures are negative and with meningococcal vaccination/prophylaxis), balancing the risk of unchecked infection and the clear benefit for TMA.
- Kidney biopsy can be invaluable when safe and feasible — it anchored the diagnosis in this case.
- Genetic counselling is important: explain incomplete penetrance, possible negative results, and implications for family members and duration of therapy.

## Key take‑home messages
- Normal C3/C4 does NOT exclude CM‑HUS; complement activation can be local and systemic markers may be normal.
- Draw ADAMTS13 and stool Shiga PCR urgently; these tests most rapidly alter immediate management.
- Obtain CH50/AH50, factor H/I/B, anti‑factor H autoantibodies and freeze serum for specialized testing before PEX or eculizumab whenever possible; once PEX or eculizumab is given, functional assays are difficult to interpret.
- A very low CH50/AH50 after eculizumab or after PEX usually reflects treatment effect, not disease “consumption.”
- Genetic testing is for risk stratification and family counselling: a pathogenic variant supports prolonged therapy, but a negative panel does not exclude CM‑HUS and should not retroactively negate appropriate treatment.
- Clinical context (biopsy, schistocytes, response to therapy) and exclusion of TTP/STEC are central — don’t over‑rely on a single complement number.

Final note: this patient’s journey — from C. canimorsus sepsis to dialysis and then to remission after complement blockade — is a powerful reminder that complement assays are tools whose value depends entirely on timing and context. When in doubt, treat the patient, not the laboratory value.
