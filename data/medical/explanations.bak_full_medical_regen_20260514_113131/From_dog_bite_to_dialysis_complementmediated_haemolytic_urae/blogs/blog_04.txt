Title: Blog about the case report: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

# Needles in a Storm: When and How a Kidney Biopsy Clarifies TMA

This patient’s journey — a previously healthy woman bitten by her dog, fulminant Capnocytophaga canimorsus sepsis with DIC, dialysis-dependent acute kidney injury (AKI), and then a late switch to isolated haemolysis with schistocytes — nicely illustrates an increasingly familiar, frustrating clinical problem: sepsis and DIC can mask a superimposed thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). When the diagnosis is uncertain and the kidneys are on dialysis, a timely renal biopsy can be the difference between empiric, temporizing measures and targeted therapy (in this case, eculizumab for complement-mediated HUS). But doing a biopsy in a patient with prior coagulopathy, recent plasma exchange (PEX), and low platelets requires deliberate planning.

Below I walk through practical questions clinicians face when contemplating a kidney biopsy in suspected TMA with dialysis-dependent AKI: when to biopsy, how to make it as safe as possible, what histology you’re trying to capture, and how the pathology report should change your management and prognostic expectations. I anchor the discussion in the case above — biopsy showed acute tubular necrosis (ATN) plus ischaemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi and crescents, findings that confirmed an active TMA and supported initiation and continuation of complement blockade.

## Why biopsy at all in dialysis-dependent AKI with suspected TMA?

Because the differential for AKI with haemolysis and thrombocytopenia in the context of sepsis is broad and therapeutic implications are huge.

- A biopsy can confirm TMA (glomerular/capillary thrombi, endothelial swelling, mesangiolysis, subendothelial widening) versus isolated ATN (predominantly tubular epithelial necrosis without glomerular thrombi), immune-complex GN, or cortical necrosis.
- In TMA, the severity and chronicity on biopsy (percentage of glomeruli with thrombi, degree of cortical necrosis, interstitial fibrosis/atrophy) informs prognosis and the urgency/likely benefit of expensive, high-risk interventions such as eculizumab.
- Importantly, biopsy may be decisive when laboratory testing is equivocal: e.g., normal C3/C4, ADAMTS13 not profoundly low, negative STEC testing, or when DIC could explain schistocytes yet thrombocytopenia persists after coagulopathy resolves.

In the presented case a biopsy obtained after partial haematologic recovery showed fibrin thrombi and crescents — pathology that shifted the team from supportive care and PEX to full complement blockade with good haematologic remission and partial renal recovery.

## Timing and safety thresholds: when is kidney biopsy reasonable?

You will rarely get perfect coagulation numbers in these patients. The decision is a balance of diagnostic need and bleeding risk.

General safety thresholds for a percutaneous ultrasound-guided biopsy:
- Platelets: ideally ≥50–75 × 10^9/L. Many centres accept ≥50 for straightforward biopsies; some prefer ≥75 when anaesthesia resources or immediate transfusion are limited. In the case above biopsy was performed when platelets were 77 × 10^9/L after PEX sessions — a reasonable threshold.
- INR: <1.5 (if on warfarin, reverse to target). aPTT should be near normal.
- Fibrinogen: >1.0–1.5 g/L is reasonable; severe hypofibrinogenaemia increases bleeding risk.
- Active DIC with consumptive coagulopathy is a relative contra-indication until corrected. If coagulation abnormalities are transient and improving, defer until improved.

When thresholds can’t be met:
- Consider transjugular renal biopsy (TJR) — preferred when platelets <50 × 10^9/L, INR >1.5, or ongoing coagulopathy. TJR has lower perirenal bleed risk and allows central venous haemostasis if bleeding occurs.
- Surgical (open) biopsy is rarely needed but may be considered in select scenarios.

Practical peri-procedural items:
- If platelet transfusion is required, give it immediately prior to the biopsy to raise platelets into acceptable range — remember transfused platelet effect can wane quickly.
- Hold anticoagulants for appropriate windows: stop unfractionated heparin several hours before; LMWH 24 hours for prophylactic doses and 48 hours for therapeutic doses (institutional policies vary); DOACs 48–72 hours based on renal function and agent; warfarin reversal to INR <1.5.
- If the patient is on dialysis with systemic anticoagulation during sessions, plan dialysis timing and anticoagulant hold to minimise residual anticoagulant effect.
- Ensure availability of cross-matched blood and rapid access to interventional radiology if needed.

## PEX, complement sampling, and timing of biopsy — the order matters

PEX and complement-targeted therapies complicate both diagnostics and biopsy timing.

- PEX removes complement proteins and autoantibodies and can transiently change complement assays and ADAMTS13 levels. If possible, obtain key diagnostic blood samples (ADAMTS13, C3/C4, functional complement assays, anti-factor H antibodies, and baseline serum for genetic testing) before PEX or complement blockade.
- PEX can also correct some coagulopathy and raise platelets (via plasma/platelet products), making biopsy safer — in the presented case, PEX sessions preceded biopsy and the platelet count had recovered to 77 × 10^9/L.
- If eculizumab is being considered, recognize that administering it before obtaining baseline complement samples may confound laboratory interpretation. But clinical reality may force therapy first — don’t delay life- or kidney-saving therapy solely for perfect labs.
- Coordinate with transfusion medicine, nephrology, haematology and the lab to get samples drawn and shipped correctly when there’s a plan for PEX or eculizumab.

## What you’re looking for under the microscope — TMA vs isolated ATN

TMA (acute, active) — hallmarks:
- Fibrin thrombi occluding glomerular capillaries and/or small arterioles.
- Endothelial cell swelling and detachment; mesangiolysis.
- Red cell fragmentation in capillaries; often crescents if severe.
- On electron microscopy: subendothelial electron-lucent widening, accumulation of fluffy material, occasional dense deposits from chronic injury.
- Minimal immune complex deposition on immunofluorescence (unless overlap disease).

Isolated ATN — hallmarks:
- Patchy tubular epithelial necrosis, loss of brush border, sloughing of epithelial cells, granular casts in tubules.
- Relatively preserved glomeruli without intraglomerular fibrin thrombi.
- Less glomerular endothelial injury; immunofluorescence usually negative.

Mixed pictures occur. In the case presented, biopsy found severe ATN plus ischaemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi and scattered crescents — a mixed but TMA-dominant picture that supports complement-mediated microvascular injury atop ischemic tubular damage.

Why the distinction matters:
- A biopsy showing pure ATN suggests supportive therapy and time; immunosuppression or complement blockade would be unlikely to help.
- Evidence of active TMA (fibrin thrombi, endothelial injury) makes a stronger case for urgent TMA-directed therapy (PEX while awaiting ADAMTS13, and consideration of eculizumab for CM-HUS when TTP and STEC are unlikely).

## How pathology alters management and prognostication

Management:
- Confirmed TMA in a dialysis-dependent patient — especially with active haemolysis and schistocytosis — raises the threshold to start targeted therapy (e.g., eculizumab for suspected CM-HUS) sooner rather than later. The case patient had haemolysis only after DIC improved; biopsy confirmed ongoing microvascular thrombosis and supported complement blockade initiation.
- If ADAMTS13 <10% → TTP, immediate PEX is lifesaving and immunosuppression indicated; biopsy is rarely necessary to make that decision.
- If biopsy shows predominantly chronic lesions (extensive cortical necrosis, widespread glomerulosclerosis, heavy interstitial fibrosis), the likelihood of renal recovery is low and this informs discussions about dialysis dependence, transplant candidacy, and duration of expensive therapies.
- If biopsy is equivocal, integrate clinical course, labs (ADAMTS13, STEC PCR, complement testing), and response to initial therapies into decision-making.

Prognosis:
- Extent of glomerular thrombosis, crescents, and cortical necrosis correlates with worse renal outcomes.
- Degree of interstitial fibrosis/ tubular atrophy predicts recovery potential.
- Even with severe histologic TMA, early complement blockade is associated with better hematologic remission and some renal recovery — again arguing for timely diagnosis and therapy.

## Practical algorithm (conceptual)

1. Suspect TMA clinically: MAHA, schistocytes, rising LDH, undetectable haptoglobin, thrombocytopenia, AKI.
2. Immediate labs: CBC, peripheral smear, LDH, haptoglobin, DAT, creatinine, coagulation panel, ADAMTS13 (stat send), STEC stool PCR, C3/C4, blood cultures if infection suspected.
3. If ADAMTS13 likely TTP (score high or strong clinical suspicion) → start PEX emergently.
4. If ADAMTS13 not low and clinical picture suggests CM-HUS (infection trigger, renal-predominant, biopsy feasible) → arrange biopsy if safe, and consider PEX as bridging if uncertain.
5. Draw complement and ADAMTS13 samples before PEX or eculizumab when possible.
6. If biopsy demonstrates active TMA and other causes excluded, initiate/continue eculizumab promptly, with appropriate meningococcal vaccination and antimicrobial prophylaxis as per local protocols.

## Clinical pitfalls and pearls

- Don’t let earlier DIC hide a later TMA. Normalising INR and fibrinogen doesn’t rule out ongoing endothelial microthrombosis.
- ADAMTS13 is diagnostic for TTP but often delayed — use PLASMIC score to triage, but don’t rely solely on it.
- PEX can transiently improve clinical parameters (and creatinine/diuresis) but may not treat the underlying complement dysregulation. Pathology will help decide whether PEX was a bridge or sufficient therapy.
- Obtain complement and ADAMTS13 samples before PEX or eculizumab if possible — both interventions will confound testing.
- If platelets are too low for percutaneous biopsy, transjugular biopsy is an excellent alternative and is underused.
- Even with negative genetic testing for complement variants, severe infection can trigger CM-HUS — absence of a pathogenic mutation does not preclude benefit from eculizumab.

## Key take-home messages

- Consider kidney biopsy in dialysis-dependent AKI when TMA is suspected but not proven — the histology can confirm diagnosis, stage injury, and change therapy.
- Aim for platelets ≥50–75 × 10^9/L, INR <1.5, and corrected fibrinogen for percutaneous biopsy; use transjugular biopsy when thresholds can’t be met.
- Draw ADAMTS13 and baseline complement studies before PEX or eculizumab when feasible; PEX and eculizumab will alter test results.
- TMA histology (glomerular/arteriolar fibrin thrombi, endothelial injury, crescents) steers toward targeted therapies like eculizumab and predicts greater urgency; isolated ATN argues for supportive care.
- Don’t delay empiric, life-saving therapy when clinical suspicion is high; use biopsy and labs to refine and guide ongoing management rather than to postpone necessary treatment.

This case — from a dog bite to sepsis to a hidden complement-mediated TMA uncovered by biopsy — is a reminder that needle and microscope still have a central role when the storm of critical illness hides the diagnosis.
