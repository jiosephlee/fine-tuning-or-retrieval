Title: Blog about the case report: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

# When a Positive Coombs Misleads: Drug-Related DAT Positivity vs MAHA

What makes this case interesting
A previously healthy woman developed fulminant Capnocytophaga canimorsus sepsis after a dog bite. After initial improvement, she suddenly developed severe anaemia with a positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT, Coombs). That initial positive DAT sent the team down the autoimmune haemolytic anaemia (AIHA) pathway and steroids were started — but the patient didn’t get better. A peripheral blood film showing 15–20% schistocytes, rising LDH, undetectable haptoglobin and persistent thrombocytopaenia ultimately pointed to a thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) — complement-mediated HUS — not AIHA. Plasma exchange (PEX) was used as a bridge and eculizumab induced sustained remission and partial renal recovery.

This case is a great reminder that a positive DAT in a sick, transfused or drug-exposed patient can be a red herring. If you miss the TMA, the delay costs kidney function — and lives.

Why a DAT can be misleading in septic patients
The DAT detects immunoglobulin or complement bound to red blood cells. It is sensitive but not specific for autoimmune haemolysis. Causes of DAT positivity beyond classic warm AIHA include:

- Drug-related coating of RBCs (penicillins and cephalosporins are classic offenders).
- Recent transfusions or plasma products (passive antibodies or complement).
- Infectious processes that fix complement to RBC surfaces.
- Laboratory artifacts.

In this patient the first DAT was IgG-positive but later became negative. She had exposure to beta‑lactam antibiotics (including penicillin derivatives and blood products), and received transfusions — all plausible contributors to a false- or drug‑related DAT. The key point: a positive DAT does not automatically equal warm AIHA.

Red flags that point away from AIHA and toward MAHA/TMA
When anaemia occurs in a septic patient, ask whether the blood picture looks haemolytic from immune-mediated extravascular destruction or from microangiopathic intravascular shearing. Features that should make you think MAHA/TMA rather than AIHA include:

- Schistocytes on peripheral smear — even modest numbers (>1% by some labs; here 15–20% was dramatic). Schistocytes are the hallmark of mechanical RBC fragmentation.
- Marked LDH rise with haemolysis and undetectable haptoglobin.
- New or persistent thrombocytopaenia concurrent with hemolysis.
- Acute kidney injury out of proportion to hypotension or sepsis.
- Poor or paradoxically low reticulocyte response (can occur early in sepsis or with marrow suppression).
- Lack of response to steroids (in AIHA high-dose steroids usually help).
In the case presented, several of these features were present: schistocytosis, rising LDH, undetectable haptoglobin, AKI requiring dialysis and thrombocytopenia — all arguing strongly for TMA.

Why steroids won’t fix most TMAs
AIHA is mediated by antibody opsonization and splenic clearance of RBCs — dampening the immune response with steroids makes sense. TMA (TTP, STEC-HUS, complement-mediated HUS) is an endothelial and microvascular process: platelets aggregate in the microcirculation, fibrin forms, and red cells fragment across fibrin strands. The underlying pathophysiology differs — immunosuppression alone does not interrupt microthrombi formation. Thus steroids are often ineffective for TMA and can distract from lifesaving therapy (PEX for suspected TTP; complement blockade for CM‑HUS).

A pragmatic algorithm — how to pivot quickly from a positive DAT to a TMA workup
Use the clinical context and the peripheral smear to guide you. Below is a practical, stepwise approach drawn from the case and common practice in acute care:

1) Stabilize first: airway, breathing, circulation, transfuse if life‑threatening anaemia, support renal function (monitor urine output, consider urgent dialysis if anuric or volume overloaded).

2) Immediately get targeted labs while you stabilize: peripheral blood smear, LDH, bilirubin (indirect), haptoglobin, reticulocyte count, CBC with platelets, coagulation profile (PT/INR, aPTT, fibrinogen, D‑dimer), creatinine/urinalysis, blood cultures. Order a DAT but don’t over‑interpret the result in isolation.

3) If you see MAHA features (schistocytes, low haptoglobin, high LDH) with thrombocytopaenia and/or AKI — start thinking TMA. Do not wait for DAT results to drive management.

4) Send ADAMTS13 activity immediately. If clinical suspicion for TTP is high (neurologic signs, very low platelets, minimal renal involvement), start PEX emergently — do not wait for ADAMTS13. If ADAMTS13 returns <10%, TTP is likely and PEX is first‑line.

5) While you’re doing the above, look for infectious triggers: stool PCR for Shiga-toxin, blood cultures, and a careful history for animals, recent procedures, pregnancy, drugs. In this case, blood cultures grew Capnocytophaga canimorsus, an unusual but real trigger of complement-mediated TMA.

6) Consider complement-mediated HUS when ADAMTS13 is not severely reduced and STEC is excluded, especially with prominent renal failure and biopsy evidence of TMA. Complement studies (C3/C4, functional assays) can be sent, but remember normal C3/C4 does not exclude CM‑HUS.

7) Use PEX as a pragmatic bridge if the diagnosis is unclear (it can transiently help in CM‑HUS and is lifesaving in TTP). Definitive therapy for CM‑HUS is complement blockade (eculizumab). Balance the timing of eculizumab in the setting of active infection (vaccinate as possible, give prophylactic antibiotics, and preferentially start eculizumab after bacteremia is controlled).

8) Obtain renal biopsy when the diagnosis is unclear or when renal prognosis will influence therapy; biopsy in this case confirmed TMA and helped clinch the diagnosis.

Pitfalls and practical pearls from this case
- A positive DAT can be induced by penicillins/cephalosporins and by transfused plasma — always review drug and transfusion history when DAT is positive.
- Don’t be reassured by normal complement C3/C4 — complement levels can be normal in CM‑HUS.
- ADAMTS13 <10% = TTP; intermediate values (eg ~40% as in this case) argue against TTP and should prompt searching for other TMAs.
- Schistocytes are a critical bedside test. If you suspect TMA but the smear is not reviewed, request it urgently — automated counters don't report schistocytes reliably.
- Initiate PEX promptly if TTP is in the differential; do not delay while awaiting confirmatory labs.
- Consider infection‑triggered CM‑HUS even in immunocompetent adults when the clinical pattern fits — C. canimorsus is an uncommon but documented trigger.
- When using eculizumab, vaccinate against encapsulated organisms and provide antibiotic prophylaxis if vaccines cannot be given 2 weeks prior.

Key take-home messages
- A positive DAT in a septic, transfused or drug‑exposed patient is not proof of warm AIHA — it can be false positive or drug‑related.
- Look at the smear and haemolysis panel: schistocytes, very high LDH, undetectable haptoglobin, persistent thrombocytopaenia and AKI point to MAHA/TMA, not AIHA.
- Steroids treat AIHA, not TMAs. Don’t let a positive DAT delay PEX or complement-directed therapy when TMA is likely.
- Send ADAMTS13 urgently, start PEX if TTP is suspected, and consider CM‑HUS (and eculizumab) when ADAMTS13 is not severely reduced and STEC is excluded — balance eculizumab timing against active infection risk.
- In the presented case a misleading DAT was likely related to antibiotics/transfusions; recognition of MAHA and timely transition to a TMA pathway (PEX then eculizumab) led to haematologic remission and partial renal recovery.

If you remember one thing: in a sick septic patient, let the smear and the pattern of haemolysis guide you more than a single DAT result.
