Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

### [Clinical Pearls] A patient with new hemolysis has a positive direct antiglobulin (Coombs) test after recent beta-lactam exposure and transfusion. What mechanisms can cause a false-positive DAT in this setting, and which laboratory and smear features help distinguish autoimmune hemolytic anemia from microangiopathic hemolytic anemia?

Direct answer
- False‑positive DAT after recent beta‑lactam exposure and transfusion can be caused by: (1) drug‑dependent antibodies (hapten-type or immune‑complex) that coat RBCs transiently, (2) passive transfer or nonspecific coating of donor immunoglobulins/plasma proteins after transfusion or plasma products, and (3) complement deposition on RBCs from systemic complement activation during sepsis/DIC/TMA.  
- To distinguish autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA) from microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA/TMA) use the peripheral smear and targeted laboratory testing: AIHA → spherocytes, positive DAT (monospecific IgG ± C3), brisk reticulocytosis; MAHA → schistocytes/helmet cells (fragmentation), thrombocytopenia, negative or transient DAT, evidence of end‑organ microthrombosis (renal injury), and supportive tests (ADAMTS13, coagulation profile, kidney biopsy).

Why these mechanisms produce a false‑positive DAT (pathophysiology and examples)
- Hapten (penicillin‑type) mechanism: some beta‑lactams (penicillins, cephalosporins) adsorb to the RBC membrane and elicit anti‑drug IgG that binds only in the presence of drug; routine DAT can be positive for IgG even if clinical hemolysis will stop when the drug is stopped.  
- Immune‑complex / complement fixation: other drugs form immune complexes that fix complement onto RBCs (DAT positive for C3).  
- True drug‑induced autoantibody: certain agents can induce autoantibodies that behave like warm AIHA (persistent DAT).  
- Passive transfer or nonspecific coating after transfusion/plasma products: transfused plasma (or donor antibodies) or Octaplas used during PEX can transiently coat recipient RBCs with IgG or complement, yielding a positive DAT without intrinsic autoimmune hemolysis.  
- Complement deposition from sepsis/TMA: systemic complement activation (as in severe infection or TMA) can deposit C3d on RBCs → DAT positivity even when mechanical fragmentation (MAHA) is the dominant cause of hemolysis.

Practical laboratory and smear distinctions (what to order and what to look for)
- Peripheral smear:
  - AIHA (warm): spherocytes, marked polychromasia, few schistocytes.
  - Cold AIHA: red cell agglutinates, often RBC clumping.
  - MAHA/TMA: increased schistocytes/helmet cells and very few spherocytes; schistocyte proportion >1% (and especially marked percentages like 15–20%) is strongly supportive of fragmentation hemolysis.  
- DAT testing:
  - Use monospecific reagents (anti‑IgG and anti‑C3d) and compare pre‑ and post‑transfusion samples if available.
  - Perform eluate studies if IgG positive to characterise antibody specificity (drug‑dependent vs autoantibody).
  - If recent transfusion/plasma, repeat DAT after washing or after stopping the suspect product; drug‑induced DATs often disappear after stopping the drug.  
- Hemolysis markers (nonspecific):
  - Both AIHA and MAHA: elevated LDH, indirect bilirubin, low haptoglobin.
  - Reticulocyte count: typically brisk in AIHA unless marrow suppressed; may be blunted in sepsis or early MAHA.  
- Platelets and coagulation:
  - Thrombocytopenia out of proportion and evidence of microthrombi (AKI, neurologic signs) point to MAHA/TMA rather than isolated AIHA. Coagulation tests may help distinguish DIC.  
- ADAMTS13 activity:
  - Severely low (<10%) → TTP; normal/moderately reduced argues against TTP and supports other TMAs or secondary MAHA.  
- Tissue/organ evaluation:
  - Organ biopsy (renal biopsy showing fibrin thrombi/capillary injury) confirms TMA when clinical context fits.

Applying this to the presented case (clinical reasoning)
- The patient had recent exposure to beta‑lactams (piperacillin/tazobactam, later ampicillin/phenoxymethylpenicillin) and received blood products/Octaplas. An initial DAT was IgG‑positive, prompting empiric steroids. However:
  - She failed to improve with steroids, the repeat DAT became negative (arguing against a persistent warm AIHA), and the peripheral smear showed 15–20% schistocytes — a pattern diagnostic for MAHA/TMA.  
  - ADAMTS13 activity (43.5%) made TTP unlikely, and kidney biopsy showed fibrin thrombi and ischemic glomeruli consistent with TMA/CM‑HUS.  
  - Together these findings favor a false‑positive DAT (likely drug‑ or transfusion‑related or complement coating during sepsis) superimposed on true microangiopathic hemolysis, rather than primary AIHA.

Bottom line (clinical pearl)
- A positive DAT in a patient with recent beta‑lactam exposure or transfusion can be drug‑ or transfusion‑related or reflect nonspecific complement/Ig coating; it does not by itself prove autoimmune hemolysis. Always integrate smear morphology (spherocytes vs schistocytes), reticulocyte response, platelet count/coagulation tests, ADAMTS13, and organ findings (renal biopsy if indicated) to distinguish AIHA from MAHA/TMA. In the case discussed, the combination of transient DAT positivity, steroid nonresponse, marked schistocytosis, thrombocytopenia and biopsy‑proven fibrin thrombi pointed to MAHA from complement‑mediated TMA rather than true AIHA.
