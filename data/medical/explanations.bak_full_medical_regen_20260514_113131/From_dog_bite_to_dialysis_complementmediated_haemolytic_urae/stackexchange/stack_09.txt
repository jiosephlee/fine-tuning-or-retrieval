Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

### [Clinical Pearls] After a dog bite, which clinical and laboratory features should raise suspicion for Capnocytophaga canimorsus bacteremia, and why is diagnostic confirmation often delayed?

Key clinical clue: any history of a dog or cat bite, scratch, or close contact in a patient who develops a rapid, fulminant systemic infection should immediately raise suspicion for Capnocytophaga canimorsus. In the case presented the patient was bitten on the lip 4 days before presentation and then developed very high fever, refractory hypoglycaemia, lactic acidosis, rapidly progressive sepsis with mottled/cool extremities, multiorgan failure and acute kidney injury—an archetypal severe C. canimorsus syndrome.

Which clinical and laboratory features should make you think specifically of C. canimorsus?
- Recent dog/cat exposure within days. This is the single most important trigger to consider the organism.
- Rapidly progressive sepsis with signs of fulminant disease (high fever, lactic acidosis, shock or marked tissue hypoperfusion, DIC). The patient developed DIC and peripheral ischaemia early.
- Marked cytopenias, especially severe leukopenia or neutropenia and thrombocytopenia; pancytopenia was prominent in this case.
- Early coagulopathy (prolonged PT/aPTT, low fibrinogen) and clinical microthrombotic phenomena (ecchymoses, distal cyanosis) consistent with DIC.
- Refractory hypoglycaemia and high lactate—features reported with fulminant Capnocytophaga sepsis and present in this patient.
- Rapid progression to multiorgan dysfunction including AKI; later development of haemolysis/MAHA, schistocytes and thrombocytopenia may reflect superimposed TMA (as occurred here).
- Gram‑negative bacilli on blood culture or Gram stain can be a clue, though organisms are often scant and fusiform.

Why confirmation is often delayed
- Capnocytophaga canimorsus is fastidious and capnophilic (prefers CO2), encapsulated, and slow‑growing. Standard blood cultures can take several days to become positive (7 days in this report), and growth may be scant.
- Prior or empiric antibiotics can suppress growth and further delay culture recovery.
- Identification may require prolonged incubation, enriched media, or advanced techniques (MALDI‑TOF, 16S PCR or real‑time nanopore sequencing), resources that are not universally or immediately available.
- Early clinical presentation mimics more common causes of sepsis/DIC, so the diagnosis is easily missed unless exposure history is sought and laboratories are instructed to extend incubation/perform specific identification methods.

Clinical bottom line (practical pearl)
Always elicit animal‑exposure history in sepsis. When a dog/cat bite precedes rapid deterioration with cytopenias, coagulopathy, refractory hypoglycaemia or peripheral ischaemia, start empiric therapy that covers Capnocytophaga (eg, beta‑lactam with beta‑lactamase inhibitor or piperacillin‑tazobactam) and notify the microbiology lab to prolong incubation and consider molecular diagnostics—because culture confirmation is characteristically delayed and may not be reliable if empiric antibiotics have already been given.
