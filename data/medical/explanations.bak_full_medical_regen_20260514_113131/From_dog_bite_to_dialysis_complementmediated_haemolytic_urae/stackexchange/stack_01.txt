Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

### [Morning Report] A previously healthy adult presents with sepsis 4 days after a dog bite. What empiric intravenous antibiotic regimen would you start, and which key pathogens associated with dog bites should it cover?

Short answer
For a previously healthy adult presenting with sepsis after a dog bite, I would start empiric broad-spectrum IV beta‑lactam therapy with anaerobic coverage — most commonly piperacillin–tazobactam (or IV ampicillin–sulbactam) — and add anti‑MRSA therapy (vancomycin) if there are risk factors for MRSA or severe purulence. This regimen covers the key dog‑bite pathogens and can be narrowed when culture results return.

Why this choice (clinical reasoning)
- Dog‑bite infections are polymicrobial and often involve oral flora of the animal: you must cover gram‑negative rods (notably Capnocytophaga canimorsus and Pasteurella spp.), aerobic gram‑positives (Streptococcus spp., Staphylococcus aureus), and anaerobes (Prevotella, Porphyromonas, Fusobacterium, etc.).  
- Capnocytophaga canimorsus is a fastidious, encapsulated gram‑negative rod that can cause fulminant sepsis (as in this case). It is generally susceptible to penicillins, third‑generation cephalosporins, carbapenems and beta‑lactam/beta‑lactamase inhibitor combinations.  
- Piperacillin–tazobactam (or ampicillin–sulbactam) provides broad gram‑negative and anaerobic coverage including Pasteurella and Capnocytophaga; it also covers streptococci and many staphylococci (but not MRSA). In the case report the team used piperacillin–tazobactam up front for this reason and later de‑escalated to ampicillin/phenoxymethylpenicillin after C. canimorsus was identified.  
- Add vancomycin if MRSA is a concern (severe soft‑tissue infection, prior MRSA colonization, high local prevalence) or if you need empiric coverage for resistant gram‑positives.

Practical alternatives and special situations
- Immediate penicillin allergy: doxycycline (covers Pasteurella and Capnocytophaga) plus metronidazole or clindamycin for anaerobic coverage; or a fluoroquinolone combined with metronidazole—choose based on allergy severity and local susceptibilities.  
- For outpatient prophylaxis of uncomplicated dog bites, oral amoxicillin–clavulanate is standard.  
- Once blood cultures identify C. canimorsus and susceptibilities are known, de‑escalate to a narrow agent (e.g., IV or oral penicillin/ampicillin or a third‑generation cephalosporin) for an appropriate duration (often at least 10–14 days for bacteraemia; tailor to clinical response).

Key pathogens to cover (dog‑bite empiric coverage)
- Capnocytophaga canimorsus (important, can cause fulminant sepsis)  
- Pasteurella multocida and other Pasteurella spp.  
- Staphylococcus aureus (including consideration of MRSA)  
- Beta‑hemolytic streptococci and viridans streptococci  
- Oral anaerobes of dog oral flora (Prevotella, Porphyromonas, Fusobacterium, Bacteroides spp.)

Operational points
- Obtain blood cultures before antibiotics when feasible, start empiric IV therapy immediately in sepsis.  
- Expect C. canimorsus to be slow and fastidious—molecular diagnostics (if available) can speed identification.  
- Narrow therapy once cultures and susceptibilities are available; monitor clinical response and organ dysfunction (renal failure, DIC, TMA as in the reported case).
