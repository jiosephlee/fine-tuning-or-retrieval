Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

### [Differential Diagnosis] An adult with sepsis develops anemia, thrombocytopenia, elevated LDH, undetectable haptoglobin, schistocytes, and acute kidney injury. How would you differentiate DIC, thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), Shiga toxin–producing E. coli HUS (STEC-HUS), and complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS), and what ADAMTS13 activity threshold supports a diagnosis of TTP?

Bottom line
- In an adult with sepsis who later develops MAHA (schistocytes, high LDH, undetectable haptoglobin), thrombocytopenia and AKI you must distinguish: (1) DIC from sepsis, (2) TTP (severe ADAMTS13 deficiency), (3) STEC‑HUS (Shiga toxin), and (4) complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS). The critical laboratory discriminators are standard coagulation tests, ADAMTS13 activity, stool Shiga‑toxin testing, and targeted complement studies/genetics. An ADAMTS13 activity <10% (≤10%) supports TTP.

How to differentiate them — practical approach and physiologic rationale
1) Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC)
- Pathophysiology: systemic activation of coagulation → consumption of platelets and clotting factors, fibrin deposition and secondary fibrinolysis.
- Typical labs: prolonged PT/PTT, markedly elevated D‑dimer/fibrin degradation products, low fibrinogen, thrombocytopenia. Schistocytes may be present but coagulation abnormalities are prominent.
- Clinical context: sepsis, trauma, obstetric complications. Bleeding and microvascular ischemia coexist.
- In the case: early presentation matched DIC (coagulopathy, pancytopenia, mottled extremities). Coagulation parameters later normalized as infection improved, arguing against ongoing DIC as the cause of the later isolated haemolysis.

2) Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP)
- Pathophysiology: severe deficiency of ADAMTS13 (autoantibody or congenital) → accumulation of ultra‑large vWF multimers and platelet microthrombi, especially affecting brain and heart.
- Key discriminator: ADAMTS13 activity severe deficiency (<10%—some use ≤10% or <5%) is diagnostic. PT/PTT usually normal (coagulation cascade not consumed).
- Clinical pattern: prominent neurologic signs are common; renal injury variable (less severe than HUS classically). Rapid response to plasma exchange is expected.
- Practical note: when TTP is strongly suspected clinically, start plasma exchange immediately while awaiting ADAMTS13.
- In the case: ADAMTS13 = 43.5% — well above the TTP threshold — and the PLASMIC score was low, making TTP unlikely.

3) Shiga toxin–producing E. coli HUS (STEC‑HUS)
- Pathophysiology: endothelial injury from Shiga toxin (often following bloody diarrheal illness) → TMA predominantly affecting kidneys.
- Diagnosis: clinical history of prodromal diarrhoea + stool PCR for Shiga toxin (or culture). Complement tests/ADAMTS13 typically not diagnostic.
- Treatment: mainly supportive (dialysis as needed); eculizumab is not standard except in severe refractory cases.
- In the case: no diarrhoeal prodrome and stool Shiga PCR was negative — STEC‑HUS excluded.

4) Complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS; atypical HUS)
- Pathophysiology: dysregulated alternative complement pathway (genetic variants or autoantibodies) → endothelial injury, platelet activation and TMA; often requires a trigger (infection, pregnancy, drugs).
- Diagnostic clues: severe renal involvement, MAHA with schistocytes, thrombocytopenia, ADAMTS13 activity not severely reduced, negative Shiga test, kidney biopsy consistent with TMA. Standard complement levels (C3/C4) may be normal; extended functional assays and genetic testing are supportive but not required for initial diagnosis.
- Treatment: complement blockade (eculizumab) is the specific therapy; plasma exchange may transiently help but does not correct the underlying complement dysregulation.
- In the case: persistent MAHA with schistocytes, ADAMTS13 43.5% (ruling out TTP), negative Shiga toxin, kidney biopsy showing fibrin thrombi and TMA, and clinical response after eculizumab support CM‑HUS. Early infection and initial DIC were the likely triggering “second hit.”

Putting it together — practical diagnostic sequence in a septic patient who develops MAHA/thrombocytopenia/AKI
1. Rapid labs: CBC with smear, LDH, haptoglobin, bilirubin, reticulocyte count, PT/PTT/fibrinogen/D‑dimer. If PT/PTT or fibrinogen abnormal → consider DIC strongly.
2. Send ADAMTS13 activity and inhibitor immediately. If clinical suspicion for TTP is high, start plasma exchange without waiting.
3. Send stool PCR for Shiga toxin if diarrhoeal prodrome or epidemiologic risk.
4. Send complement studies (C3/C4, functional assays) and arrange genetic testing if CM‑HUS is suspected; recognize normal C3/C4 does not exclude CM‑HUS.
5. Consider kidney biopsy when diagnosis remains uncertain or to document TMA.
6. Therapeutic priorities: empiric PEX for suspected TTP; treat sepsis/DIC aggressively; consider eculizumab for CM‑HUS once active uncontrolled bacteraemia is controlled (vaccination and prophylaxis as appropriate).

Key teaching points illustrated by the case
- Sepsis/DIC can precede or mask a superimposed TMA. Normalization of coagulation tests with new isolated MAHA and persistent thrombocytopenia should prompt reassessment.
- ADAMTS13 activity <10% supports TTP; partial reductions (eg, 40%) occur in sepsis and other TMAs and do not indicate TTP.
- CM‑HUS is a diagnosis of exclusion; complement levels may be normal and genetic testing can be negative — clinical context, biopsy and response to eculizumab are important.
- In infection‑triggered CM‑HUS, delay eculizumab until blood cultures are controlled if possible and provide appropriate vaccination and antibiotic prophylaxis.

Answer to the explicit threshold question
- An ADAMTS13 activity <10% (commonly ≤10%) supports a diagnosis of TTP.
