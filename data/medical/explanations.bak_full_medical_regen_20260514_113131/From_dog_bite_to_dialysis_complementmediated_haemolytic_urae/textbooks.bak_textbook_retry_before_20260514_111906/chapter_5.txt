Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Chapter 5: ## Outcomes, follow-up, and clinical lessons

The patient achieved a clear haematological remission after complement blockade: haemolysis resolved, platelet counts recovered, and transfusion requirements ceased. Her spontaneous diuresis returned after plasma-exchange sessions and was sustained following initiation of eculizumab. Dialysis was therefore discontinued and kidney function partially recovered, stabilising with a serum creatinine of approximately 175 µmol/L at last follow‑up. She remains clinically well and is being followed in the nephrology outpatient clinic.

Ongoing management balances the risk of relapse against the safety and cost of prolonged complement inhibition. After the induction course she continues maintenance eculizumab 1,200 mg intravenously every 2 weeks, planned for 4 months post-induction with reassessment for discontinuation if renal function and hematology remain stable. Extended complement genetic testing did not identify pathogenic variants. This reduces the likelihood of a lifelong predisposition but does not entirely exclude future relapse, so individualized reassessment and close monitoring are warranted before stopping therapy.

Because terminal complement blockade increases susceptibility to invasive infections with encapsulated organisms, completing vaccination and providing antimicrobial prophylaxis were essential parts of care in this case. The patient received conjugate and protein‑based meningococcal vaccines (Menveo and Bexsero) before eculizumab and was given prophylactic phenoxymethylpenicillin while vaccine-induced protection was still developing. Ongoing counselling, prompt evaluation of febrile illnesses and adherence to prophylactic strategies are mandatory while on treatment.

This case offers several practical clinical lessons. First, when thrombocytopenia persists or new haemolysis appears despite apparent improvement in sepsis/DIC, clinicians should promptly consider a superimposed TMA rather than attributing all abnormalities to sepsis alone. Second, normal routine complement tests (C3/C4 and basic pathway assays) do not exclude complement‑mediated HUS; diagnosis is clinicopathological and often requires exclusion of other causes and integration of biopsy, peripheral smear, and functional/extended complement testing. Third, a renal biopsy can be decisive when the diagnosis is unclear: the finding of fibrin thrombi, ischaemic glomeruli and acute tubular injury supported the diagnosis of TMA in this patient.

There are also important disease‑specific points relating to Capnocytophaga canimorsus. It is a rare zoonotic pathogen transmitted by dog or cat bites or close contact; isolates are slow‑growing and fastidious, so culture confirmation can be delayed and empiric antimicrobial coverage after animal bites is critical. Capnocytophaga strains vary in complement susceptibility, and in rare cases infection can act as the “second hit” that precipitates CM‑HUS.

Finally, a pragmatic diagnostic and therapeutic approach to suspected TMA in a septic adult is illustrated by this case: immediately exclude TTP (rapid ADAMTS13 testing or empiric plasma exchange guided by pretest probability), rule out STEC‑HUS (stool PCR), use plasma exchange as a bridge if the diagnosis is uncertain or while awaiting results, and—once ongoing bacteraemia is controlled—initiate eculizumab early for confirmed or highly suspected CM‑HUS to optimise renal and haematologic recovery. Close follow‑up with serial creatinine, urine output, haemolysis markers (LDH, haptoglobin, peripheral smear) and platelet counts is essential after discharge to detect relapse promptly.
