Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

### [Pathophysiology] Explain how Capnocytophaga canimorsus interacts with the complement system, including the role of capsule expression, and why this creates a therapeutic paradox when considering eculizumab.

Capnocytophaga canimorsus sits at an intersection between host defence and host injury because of how it interacts with the complement system. Innate clearance of gram‑negative bacteria depends on three complementary functions of complement: opsonization (C3b) for phagocytosis, generation of anaphylatoxins (C3a/C5a) to recruit and activate inflammatory cells, and assembly of the terminal membrane attack complex (MAC, C5b-9) to lyse bacteria or facilitate killing. Most C. canimorsus isolates are serum‑sensitive and are controlled by these mechanisms; however, isolates that express a polysaccharide capsule gain relative serum resistance by reducing deposition of C3b and limiting MAC formation, effectively evading opsonophagocytosis and killing.

Clinically this has two linked implications. First, when complement functions well it helps clear C. canimorsus; second, in some patients an infection that engages the alternative complement pathway can produce excessive complement activation on the vascular endothelium, especially in those with impaired regulation, leading to endothelial injury, microvascular thrombosis and the clinical syndrome of complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS). Thus complement activation is both necessary to control the organism and, when dysregulated or excessive, the proximate cause of TMA.

That dual role creates the therapeutic paradox with eculizumab. Eculizumab binds C5 and prevents cleavage to C5a and C5b, blocking generation of the potent pro‑inflammatory fragment C5a and preventing MAC assembly. Blocking C5 rapidly halts the downstream effectors that mediate endothelial damage in CM‑HUS and can produce prompt hematologic and renal improvement. But by inhibiting terminal complement it also removes a major defence against encapsulated and certain gram‑negative organisms (notably Neisseria spp. and organisms like C. canimorsus that rely on capsule‑mediated serum resistance). In short, the drug that treats the TMA undermines one of the host’s best tools for eradicating the triggering infection.

Putting that into practice explains the management choices in the case: start eculizumab only after active bacteraemia is controlled and antibiotics are effective, give appropriate vaccinations against encapsulated organisms when feasible, and provide antibiotic prophylaxis until vaccine responses are present. It also explains why routine complement levels can be unhelpful (C3/C4 may be normal while pathogenic activation of the alternative pathway and terminal components causes TMA) and why plasma exchange may transiently help but not definitively treat CM‑HUS. The decision to use complement blockade therefore rests on balancing the immediate risk of ongoing endothelial injury and irreversible renal damage against the increased risk of overwhelming infection from an organism the complement system helps clear.
