Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Chapter 1: ## From bite to bedside: initial presentation, sepsis, and early management

This patient’s course began with a straightforward exposure—a dog bite to the upper lip—followed by a rapid systemic deterioration over 4 days. The temporal relationship is important: she was prescribed oral phenoxymethylpenicillin at the time of the bite but did not start it, and within days developed high fevers (>40 °C), profound malaise with poor intake, refractory hypoglycaemia and later numbness and discolouration of her feet. The rapid progression from a localized bite to overwhelming systemic illness should immediately raise concern for an invasive zoonotic pathogen, and in retrospect fit the classic timeline for fulminant Capnocytophaga canimorsus infection.

On arrival her examination and initial laboratory tests documented multiorgan involvement. She was septic in appearance with mottled, cool lower extremities. Labs showed pancytopenia, marked inflammatory response, metabolic acidosis with elevated lactate (5.7 mmol/L), acute kidney injury with oliguria progressing to anuria, elevated transaminases and coagulopathy. Urinalysis revealed proteinuria (2+) and haematuria (3+), supporting kidney involvement. Clinically she also had refractory hypoglycaemia (nadir 0.6 mmol/L), which in severe sepsis commonly reflects a combination of impaired hepatic gluconeogenesis from liver dysfunction, depleted glycogen reserves, and increased peripheral glucose utilization; glucagon resistance may be seen if hepatic dysfunction is severe, explaining failure to correct hypoglycaemia with usual measures.

The constellation of thrombocytopenia, prolonged coagulation parameters and evidence of end-organ ischaemia (mottling, distal cyanosis) led to the working diagnosis of disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) secondary to bacteraemia. DIC is a dynamic consumptive coagulopathy in which systemic activation of coagulation causes microvascular thrombosis and consumption of platelets and clotting factors, producing a laboratory pattern of thrombocytopenia, prolonged PT/INR and APTT, elevated D-dimer/fibrin degradation products, and low fibrinogen—features that were clinically and biochemically present in this patient. The skin findings (mottling, ecchymoses and later distal ischemic changes of toes and fingers) are compatible with microthrombotic phenomena; importantly, palpable pulses and preserved sensation argued against large-vessel occlusion or compartmental/necrotising soft-tissue infection as the cause. The quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) score was 1/3 in this case; qSOFA is a rapid bedside tool to screen for patients at increased risk of poor outcomes from sepsis but is not a substitute for a comprehensive evaluation—here the overall clinical picture and laboratory severity dictated ICU-level care despite a modest qSOFA.

Early differential diagnoses appropriately included septic complications of the bite (including necrotising soft tissue infection), haematological causes of cytopenias such as acute leukaemia, and hyperinflammatory syndromes like haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH). Necrotising fasciitis was considered because of mottling and rapidly evolving local signs but was effectively excluded by clinical and radiologic assessment. Acute leukaemia and HLH were entertained because profound pancytopenia can reflect primary marrow pathology or immune-mediated marrow suppression; haematology input and flow cytometry were therefore sought and ultimately normal, lowering the likelihood of acute leukaemia and supporting an infectious consumptive or suppressive process as the driver of cytopenias.

Because a dog bite raises a specific microbiological risk profile, empiric antimicrobial therapy was selected to address typical and atypical canine bite pathogens. Broad-spectrum intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam was started on admission to cover Pasteurella species, anaerobes and gram-negative rods frequently associated with animal bites, and importantly to provide activity against Capnocytophaga species. This choice reflects the clinical imperative to start timely, empiric antibiotics that cover likely organisms when systemic infection is suspected; in this case cultures later confirmed C. canimorsus bacteremia, validating the empiric selection. It is worth noting that Capnocytophaga canimorsus is a fastidious, encapsulated gram-negative rod that can cause fulminant sepsis even in previously healthy individuals. Because it grows slowly and requires enriched culture conditions, blood-culture results can be delayed (often several days); modern molecular methods (PCR, sequencing) can speed diagnosis but were not needed here because both aerobic and anaerobic blood cultures became positive approximately 7 days after presentation.

Her ICU course illustrates the typical supportive priorities for severe sepsis with multiorgan failure. She became anuric despite aggressive fluids and diuretics and developed fluid overload in the setting of septic cardiomyopathy—an often reversible myocardial depression seen in sepsis that impairs cardiac output and can limit tolerance of fluid resuscitation. Owing to persistent anuria and volume overload, continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) was initiated. Supportive haematologic care included transfusion of platelets and fibrinogen concentrates given the consumptive coagulopathy and bleeding. Skin ecchymoses and distal ischemic changes evolved, consistent with continuing microvascular thrombosis from DIC. She did not require prolonged vasopressor support beyond stress-dose corticosteroids in the early septic phase, which is notable but does not obviate the need for close hemodynamic monitoring and organ-supportive therapies.

In summary, the early phase of this case illustrates several teaching points: a history of animal bite with rapid systemic symptoms should prompt immediate empiric broad-spectrum antibiotics covering bite pathogens; severe sepsis may present with profound metabolic disturbances such as refractory hypoglycaemia and multiorgan dysfunction including AKI and coagulopathy; DIC is a common complication of fulminant bacteraemia and explains the combination of thrombocytopenia, bleeding and microthrombotic ischemia; and because fastidious organisms such as C. canimorsus can have delayed culture positivity, clinical decisions must be guided by exposure history and the evolving clinical picture while awaiting microbiological confirmation.
