Title: Blog about the case report: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

# Sepsis, DIC, and the TMA switch: Recognizing CM‑HUS When the Dust Settles

What makes this case stick in your head? A previously well woman is bitten by her dog, four days later develops fulminant Capnocytophaga canimorsus sepsis with DIC and multi‑organ failure, is treated and starts to improve — and then suddenly develops severe haemolysis, persistent thrombocytopaenia and dialysis‑dependent AKI. The haemolysis only becomes obvious as the inflammatory storm abates. The culprit? A superimposed thrombotic microangiopathy — complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS) — unmasked when routine coagulation tests normalized. This is the clinical trap: once the sepsis is controlled, a second process (TMA) can emerge or persist, and unless you look for it you will miss it — with major prognostic implications.

Below I walk through the diagnostic thinking and practical steps prompted by this case, with specific pointers you can use at the bedside.

## Quick case recap (the parts that matter)
- Late‑40s woman bitten on the lip by her dog; rapidly progressive sepsis 4 days later.
- Initial picture: severe inflammation, pancytopenia, coagulopathy consistent with DIC, AKI progressing to anuria and dialysis. Blood cultures later grew Capnocytophaga canimorsus.
- After initial improvement on antibiotics/supportive care: sudden fall in haemoglobin, undetectable haptoglobin, rising LDH, platelets persistently low, peripheral smear showed 15–20% schistocytes.
- DAT briefly positive (likely false positive from penicillin/transfusion), later negative. ADAMTS13 43.5% (TTP unlikely). C3/C4 normal. Kidney biopsy: fibrin thrombi, ischaemic glomeruli and ATN—consistent with TMA.
- Management: PEX as bridge, then eculizumab started after blood cultures negative; meningococcal vaccines and antimicrobial prophylaxis used. Haematologic remission and partial renal recovery; no pathogenic complement variants identified.

## When to suspect a superimposed TMA in a septic patient
In the heat of fulminant sepsis you reasonably assume DIC is the cause of coagulopathy and microthrombi. But there are warning signs that a separate TMA is present or is emerging as the infection is controlled:

- Falling haemoglobin without overt bleeding while infection markers and coagulation tests (INR, aPTT, fibrinogen, D‑dimer) are improving.
- Rising LDH and undetectable or very low haptoglobin — biochemical evidence of intravascular haemolysis.
- Persistent or recrudescent thrombocytopaenia despite resolving coagulopathy.
- New or increasing schistocytosis on peripheral smear.
- Worsening or dialysis‑dependent AKI that is disproportionate to other organ dysfunction or does not recover as sepsis resolves.
In the case above the haemolysis only became apparent as inflammatory and coagulation markers normalized — a classic “TMA when the dust settles” pattern.

## The peripheral smear is high‑value, low‑cost, and underused
Do a smear early and repeat it. Schistocytes are the hallmark of microangiopathic haemolysis. Automated counters miss schistocytes; look at the slide. Guidelines commonly use ≥1% schistocytes as abnormal; in this patient the smear was dramatic (15–20%), strongly supporting TMA. Also review platelets, polychromasia/reticulocytes and other clues (toxic neutrophils vs haemophagocytosis, blasts).

## Triage: TTP first (PLASMIC, ADAMTS13), then work up TMA causes
- TTP is a medical emergency — if suspected, start plasma exchange emergently. Use PLASMIC score to triage while awaiting ADAMTS13.
- ADAMTS13 <10% essentially confirms TTP; values >10% (our patient 43.5%) argue against it and should pivot your workup away from TTP.
- If TTP is excluded and Shiga toxin is negative, think CM‑HUS, especially with severe AKI and biopsy‑proven TMA.

## Laboratory caveats and timing
- C3 and C4 may be normal in CM‑HUS — normal complement levels do NOT exclude CM‑HUS.
- Send baseline ADAMTS13, C3/C4 and complement functional assays before plasma exchange or complement blockade if possible. PEX removes complement components and eculizumab will confound functional assays (AH50, etc.).
- A positive DAT may be a red herring (penicillin or transfusion‑related). If DAT positive but smear showing schistocytes and low haptoglobin/LDH high, consider concurrent MAHA/TMA.
- Genetic complement testing takes time and can be negative even in infection‑triggered CM‑HUS.

## Role of kidney biopsy
When safe and feasible it can be diagnostic. This case had fibrin thrombi, ischaemic glomeruli and crescents consistent with TMA and helped clinch the diagnosis.

## Therapeutics — practical balance between PEX and eculizumab
- PEX is lifesaving in TTP and can be a reasonable bridge when diagnosis is uncertain; it may transiently improve CM‑HUS but does not correct the underlying complement dysregulation.
- Eculizumab (terminal complement blockade) is the targeted therapy for CM‑HUS and is associated with hematologic remission and improved renal outcomes — earlier initiation usually equals better kidney recovery.
- Infection dilemma: complement is important for defence against encapsulated organisms. In active bacteraemia you should control infection and ideally have negative cultures before starting eculizumab. If urgent eculizumab is given, vaccinate (meningococcal) and give appropriate antimicrobial prophylaxis; remember vaccines require time to induce immunity and PEX/rituals can remove monoclonal antibody or vaccine response.
In the reported patient, PEX was used as a bridge and eculizumab started after cultures became negative; meningococcal vaccines were given and prophylactic Apocillin used because the usual 2‑week vaccine lead time wasn’t available.

## Pathophysiologic takeaway
Infection can be the “second hit” that triggers complement overactivation in susceptible hosts. Capnocytophaga canimorsus has variable interactions with complement and, although rare, has been reported as a trigger for CM‑HUS. Importantly, CM‑HUS can occur without identifiable genetic variants — infection alone can be sufficient.

## Practical bedside algorithm (simplified)
1. Septic patient with coagulopathy/DIC — treat infection, support organ systems.
2. If haemoglobin falls without bleeding, LDH rises, haptoglobin falls, platelets remain low, or schistocytes appear — suspect TMA.
3. Send urgent: peripheral smear, haptoglobin, LDH, reticulocyte count, DAT, ADAMTS13, C3/C4 (and complement functional tests if available) — ideally before PEX/eculizumab.
4. If high suspicion for TTP or PLASMIC score high — start PEX immediately.
5. If TTP excluded and TMA persists with severe AKI — discuss eculizumab with nephrology/hematology; consider PEX as bridge; manage infection aggressively; vaccinate and give prophylaxis if initiating terminal complement blockade.
6. Consider kidney biopsy if diagnosis unclear and it’s safe.

## Clinical pearls
- Don’t wait for dramatic lab derangement — get a smear early and repeat it. Schistocytes are the signal that changes the game.
- Normal INR/PT/fibrinogen as sepsis resolves + ongoing haemolysis = think TMA, not persistent DIC.
- ADAMTS13 <10% = TTP; if >10% look elsewhere. Use PLASMIC to triage while awaiting results.
- C3/C4 can be normal in CM‑HUS — normal complement tests do not exclude it.
- Time your samples: PEX and eculizumab will confound complement testing and vaccine timing.
- Eculizumab is effective for CM‑HUS but takes the immune edge off; ensure cultures are controlled, vaccinate and use antibiotic prophylaxis.
- Infection‑triggered CM‑HUS may occur without identifiable genetic variants — absence of a mutation does not exclude the diagnosis.

Key take‑home points
- Think TMA when haemolysis (↑LDH, ↓haptoglobin), schistocytes and persistent thrombocytopaenia appear despite improving sepsis/coagulation.
- Smear + ADAMTS13 (and PLASMIC for triage) get you on the right pathway quickly: PEX for TTP, eculizumab for CM‑HUS.
- Complement tests and genetics are useful but time‑sensitive and easily confounded by PEX/eculizumab; do baseline sampling before those therapies if possible.
- When treating CM‑HUS in the setting of recent or active infection, balance timing of eculizumab against infection control, ensure vaccinations and prophylaxis, and involve nephrology/haematology early.

This Capnocytophaga canimorsus case is an elegant reminder: once the inflammatory fog lifts, look for schistocytes. If you do, you’ll catch the TMA switch before it costs kidneys.



# Eculizumab After Bacteremia: A Practical Playbook for Infection‑Triggered CM‑HUS

What makes this case interesting: a previously healthy woman develops fulminant sepsis from a dog bite (Capnocytophaga canimorsus), recovers from the obvious septic phase, then—days later—develops new microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia, thrombocytopaenia and dialysis‑dependent AKI. The diagnosis: complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS) triggered by infection. The hard question faced by the treating team (and by you in real life): when is it safe and appropriate to start terminal complement blockade with eculizumab after a recent bloodstream infection? Start too late and you risk irreversible renal loss; start too early and you risk catastrophic infection on top of immunomodulation. This playbook synthesises the pragmatic steps taken in this case with practical, evidence‑informed guidance you can use at the bedside.

## The clinical dilemma in one sentence
In infection‑triggered TMA you must simultaneously (1) treat/clear infection, (2) rapidly exclude TTP where plasma exchange is lifesaving, and (3) decide whether to start eculizumab promptly to halt complement‑driven endothelial injury and preserve kidney function.

## A stepwise practical approach

1. Rapid recognition and stabilization (hour 0–24)
- Treat sepsis aggressively: source control, appropriate empiric antibiotics covering likely zoonotic pathogens (here, Capnocytophaga—cover with a β‑lactam such as piperacillin/tazobactam or ampicillin for culture‑directed therapy).
- Support organ dysfunction (CRRT if anuric/volume overloaded). Document urine output and trends.
- Send the working labs: CBC with smear, LDH, haptoglobin, bilirubin, creatinine, urinalysis, coagulation panel, blood cultures (multiple sets), and baseline complement tests (C3/C4) and ADAMTS13 sample (see below about timing).

2. Early evaluation for TMA vs DIC vs TTP (first 24–48 hours)
- Distinguish DIC (consumptive coagulopathy, abnormal PT/aPTT, low fibrinogen) from primary TMA. Note both can co‑exist during severe sepsis.
- Use PLASMIC (or clinical reasoning) to estimate pretest probability of severe ADAMTS13 deficiency. If TTP is possible, do not delay therapy.
- Draw ADAMTS13 and send urgently. If clinical suspicion for TTP is moderate/high, start plasma exchange (PEX) immediately—don’t wait for the result.

3. Use plasma exchange as a pragmatic bridge
- PEX removes autoantibodies in TTP and can transiently stabilise some patients with CM‑HUS (it may remove complement activation products and inflammatory mediators). In practice PEX is often started while you confirm ADAMTS13.
- Be aware PEX interferes with complement and serology testing (and removes eculizumab). If possible, obtain baseline complement and genetic samples before PEX.

4. Make the eculizumab timing decision: weigh infection control vs renal prognosis
- Ideal scenario: blood cultures become negative and clinical sepsis is controlled; vaccinate and start eculizumab.
- Real world: waiting for 2 weeks after vaccination is often not feasible if TMA is progressive and dialysis‑dependent. In that case:
  - Give both meningococcal vaccines immediately (MenACWY and MenB) if not previously vaccinated.
  - Start appropriate antibiotic prophylaxis (see below) until 2 weeks after vaccination (or longer based on local advice).
  - If active, uncontrolled bacteraemia with an encapsulated organism persists, strongly prefer to clear blood cultures before eculizumab when possible; if the TMA is fulminant and kidney prognosis poor, multidisciplinary discussion (nephrology/ID/haematology/ICU) may lead to starting eculizumab while continuing targeted antibiotics and close monitoring.
- In the published case, the team waited until blood cultures were negative and then gave vaccines and started eculizumab—an approach that balanced infection control and timely complement blockade.

5. Vaccination and antibiotic prophylaxis — practical tips
- Vaccines: give MenACWY (eg, Menveo) and MenB (eg, Bexsero) ideally ≥2 weeks before starting eculizumab. If eculizumab cannot be deferred, give the vaccines immediately and proceed with prophylactic antibiotics.
- Antibiotic prophylaxis: give oral penicillin/amoxicillin if organism is expected susceptible (as in Capnocytophaga and in many centres). Alternatives: ciprofloxacin or azithromycin for penicillin‑allergy (local resistance patterns and ID input required).
- Duration: continue prophylactic antibiotics until 2 weeks after vaccination (to allow protective antibody response). Some centres continue prophylaxis for the entire period of complement blockade; practices vary—discuss with ID.
- Counseling: explain to patients that vaccination reduces but does not abolish meningococcal risk; any fever, headache, neck stiffness, rash or sepsis‑like symptoms while on eculizumab is an emergency—attend promptly.

6. Practical lab and procedural timing (complement testing, PEX, eculizumab)
- Baseline complement (C3/C4, functional assays) and blood for genetic testing ideally drawn before PEX and before eculizumab. These tests are useful for prognosis and decisions about treatment duration.
- If PEX is performed before sampling, document timing—PEX can reduce complement levels and affect interpretation.
- If eculizumab is given and PEX is needed afterward, remember PEX will remove eculizumab—coordinate dosing (ideally give eculizumab after PEX).
- ADAMTS13 should be sampled before PEX where possible; however, if clinical urgency mandates PEX, do not delay PEX for testing.

7. Renal biopsy: when it helps
- If diagnosis remains unclear and it’s safe to biopsy, kidney histology showing thrombotic microangiopathy supports CM‑HUS and can guide management (was valuable in the case where biopsy showed fibrin thrombi and ischemic changes).
- Balance biopsy risks with bleeding/thrombocytopenia—often done once platelets have recovered sufficiently.

8. Monitoring after starting eculizumab
- Clinical: urine output, blood pressure, fever, signs of infection—low threshold for admission if febrile.
- Labs: CBC, LDH, haptoglobin, creatinine, electrolytes at least twice weekly early, then spacing out as response occurs. Platelets and hemolysis markers are often the earliest indicators of response.
- Microbiology: ensure cultures remained negative before starting; if eculizumab started with recent infection, keep high vigilance and repeat cultures if clinically indicated.
- Document vaccine dates, antibiotic prophylaxis plan and patient education.

9. Duration of complement blockade
- Tailor to the clinical course and underlying risk:
  - If a pathogenic complement variant or anti‑factor H autoantibody is found, prolonged or lifelong therapy may be considered.
  - If no germline variant and the TMA appears to be a single infection‑triggered event with good renal recovery, a limited course (eg, months) with re‑assessment and potential discontinuation can be considered with close follow‑up.
- Always have a written relapse plan for the patient (what to do with fever, hematuria, oliguria, lab triggers to check).

## Special considerations from this Capnocytophaga case
- Capnocytophaga canimorsus is encapsulated and can cause fulminant sepsis in immunocompetent hosts. It is slow‑growing—expect delays in culture confirmation and consider molecular diagnostics if available.
- In the case above the haemolytic picture emerged after the septic phase began to improve—classic for infection‑triggered CM‑HUS where the “second hit” manifests after pathogen control begins.
- The team drew complement/ADAMTS13 early, used PEX as a bridge, waited for culture clearance, vaccinated, gave penicillin prophylaxis and then started eculizumab. This sequence produced haematological remission and partial renal recovery—illustrating the balance between infection control and timely complement blockade.

## Clinical pearls
- Normal C3/C4 does not exclude CM‑HUS. Complement levels can be normal even with active complement‑mediated TMA.
- ADAMTS13 <10% = TTP—start PEX emergently. Intermediate or normal ADAMTS13 points away from TTP and towards other TMAs.
- Plasma exchange can stabilize patients but is unlikely to be definitive therapy for CM‑HUS; eculizumab is disease‑modifying.
- If you must give eculizumab before the 2‑week post‑vaccine window, always combine immediate meningococcal vaccination and antibiotic prophylaxis and consult ID.
- Plan for the logistics: take complement/genetic samples before PEX/eculizumab when possible; if not possible, document timing to aid interpretation.

## Take‑home messages
- Think TMA in any patient with new MAHA, thrombocytopenia and AKI—even during or after sepsis.
- Rapidly exclude TTP (ADAMTS13) and use PEX as an immediate bridge when TTP is possible.
- For infection‑triggered CM‑HUS, aim to clear bloodstream infection before complement blockade when feasible; when not feasible, vaccinate immediately and give antibiotic prophylaxis while proceeding with eculizumab after multidisciplinary risk assessment.
- Draw complement and genetic tests before PEX/eculizumab if possible, but don’t delay life‑saving therapy for lab idealism.
- Educate patients rigorously about increased meningococcal risk despite vaccination and ensure a low threshold to evaluate febrile illness.

# The Dog‑Bite Pathogen You Can’t Miss: Capnocytophaga canimorsus in the ED and ICU

What starts as a tiny puncture to the lip can, rarely, snowball into fulminant sepsis, refractory coagulopathy, anuric kidney failure and even complement‑mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM‑HUS). That’s exactly what happened to a previously healthy woman in her late 40s: four days after a dog bite she developed hypoglycaemia, severe leukopaenia, DIC, multiorgan failure and ultimately dialysis‑dependent AKI. Blood cultures later grew Capnocytophaga canimorsus, and weeks into her admission she developed a new microangiopathic haemolysis that required plasma exchange and eculizumab to achieve haematologic remission and partial renal recovery.

This case teaches several practical and high‑yield lessons for the ED and ICU clinician: suspect C. canimorsus even in immunocompetent patients after dog/cat exposure; choose empiric antibiotics that cover beta‑lactamase–producing strains; expect slow culture turnaround and consider molecular diagnostics; and watch for delayed complications such as ischemic digits, profound hypoglycaemia and secondary TMA/CM‑HUS.

## Why this organism is sneaky (and dangerous)
Capnocytophaga canimorsus is a fastidious, slow‑growing, encapsulated gram‑negative rod in dogs’ and cats’ oral flora. Human infection is uncommon, but when it does occur it can be dramatic — sepsis with leukopaenia, DIC, purpura fulminans, hypoglycaemia and multiorgan failure. Mortality rates in severe disease approach 30% in reported series. Importantly, severe infections can occur in people without classic risk factors (asplenia, alcoholism, immunosuppression).

A paradox worth remembering: the complement system is important for clearing C. canimorsus, yet complement activation (or dysregulation) triggered by infection can precipitate thrombotic microangiopathy such as CM‑HUS. In the case above, haemolysis appeared only after initial sepsis began to improve — a classic pattern for infection‑triggered CM‑HUS.

## Bedside clues that should tip you off
- Recent dog or cat bite, scratch, or close contact — ask specifically.
- Rapidly progressive sepsis with:
  - Profound leukopaenia (can be striking).
  - Refractory hypoglycaemia (this patient had glucose 0.6 mmol/L), which can be a red flag in severe sepsis.
  - Early coagulopathy / DIC with petechiae, ecchymoses or ischemic digits.
  - Rapidly worsening AKI, oliguria/anuria.
- If after initial improvement you see a sudden fall in haemoglobin, rising LDH, undetectable haptoglobin and persistent thrombocytopenia — think microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (look for schistocytes).

In short: dog/cat exposure + rapidly progressive sepsis ± hypoglycaemia ± limb ischemia = think Capnocytophaga.

## Empiric antibiotics — what to start in the ED
Capnocytophaga is usually susceptible to beta‑lactams, but beta‑lactamase–producing strains exist. Bite prophylaxis recommendations and empiric therapy for suspected severe Capnocytophaga should cover beta‑lactamase producers.

Practical options:
- For severe sepsis from an animal bite: start broad IV coverage such as piperacillin–tazobactam (what was used in the case) or a carbapenem if concern for resistant organisms.
- For outpatient bite prophylaxis or less severe infections: amoxicillin–clavulanate provides good coverage for typical bite pathogens including Capnocytophaga.
- Add coverage tailored to the situation (e.g., MRSA or anaerobes) as clinically indicated.

De‑escalate once susceptibilities/culture results are available. In the reported case the patient was initially started on piperacillin–tazobactam, later narrowed to ampicillin and then to oral phenoxymethylpenicillin based on sensitivities and clinical course.

## Diagnosis often lags — use cultures and consider rapid molecular methods
Capnocytophaga is slow and fastidious; blood cultures can take days. Don’t be falsely reassured by negative early cultures if the clinical picture fits. When available, molecular diagnostics (for example, rapid sequencing platforms) can shorten time to identification and may change management earlier than conventional cultures.

Always obtain blood cultures before antibiotics when possible, but do not delay empiric antibiotics in the hemodynamically unstable patient.

## Complications to watch for — early and late
Early complications seen in this case and reported in the literature:
- DIC and purpura fulminans with ischemic digits.
- Profound leukopaenia.
- Refractory hypoglycaemia from severe sepsis.
- Rapid progression to multiorgan failure and AKI requiring dialysis.

Late or secondary complications:
- Thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) presenting as new‑onset MAHA with schistocytes, rising LDH, low/absent haptoglobin and persistent thrombocytopenia. Workup should include peripheral smear, DAT, ADAMTS13, and stool Shiga‑toxin PCR. Renal biopsy (when feasible) can be diagnostic.
- CM‑HUS (infection‑triggered) — consider when TTP and STEC are excluded and there is supporting histopathology or response to complement blockade.

## TMA in the context of sepsis — a pragmatic approach
Sepsis and DIC commonly confuse the picture. Key steps:
- If you see MAHA with thrombocytopenia and AKI, get a smear (schistocytes), DAT (to exclude warm AIHA), LDH, haptoglobin, reticulocyte count and ADAMTS13.
- Use clinical tools (e.g., PLASMIC) to triage for TTP, but treat empirically when suspicion is high: PEX is lifesaving in TTP.
- Rule out STEC‑HUS with stool PCR.
- If ADAMTS13 is not severely reduced and clinical picture points to complement‑mediated TMA, discuss PEX as a bridge and early nephrology/haematology input regarding eculizumab.
- Be aware: routine C3/C4 can be normal in CM‑HUS. Extended complement testing and genetic analysis can be helpful but are not necessary to start therapy if clinically indicated.

In the case described, ADAMTS13 was 43.5% (not TTP), schistocytes were present (15–20%), and renal biopsy confirmed TMA. PEX was used as a bridge and eculizumab was started after blood cultures were negative.

## Eculizumab and the infectious dilemma
Eculizumab (terminal complement inhibitor) is effective for CM‑HUS but blocks a key host defence against encapsulated organisms (classically Neisseria; Capnocytophaga is also encapsulated). Practical considerations from the case:
- Avoid starting complement blockade during ongoing bacteremia if possible — wait for negative cultures.
- When you must start eculizumab, vaccinate against meningococcus (MenACWY and MenB) and give antibiotic prophylaxis until vaccines are effective; the team in this case gave two meningococcal vaccines and continued prophylactic phenoxymethylpenicillin.
- Plasma exchange may transiently improve parameters but does not correct complement dysregulation; eculizumab is often needed for sustained remission.

## The bottom line — clinical pearls and takeaways
- Suspect Capnocytophaga canimorsus after any dog or cat bite/exposure, even in immunocompetent adults, when you see rapidly progressive sepsis, leukopaenia, DIC, hypoglycaemia or ischemic digits.
- Empiric therapy for severe bite‑associated sepsis should cover beta‑lactamase producers: piperacillin–tazobactam (IV) or amoxicillin–clavulanate (oral/PO prophylaxis) are appropriate choices; tailor and de‑escalate to susceptibilities.
- Cultures are slow; consider molecular diagnostics if available to speed identification.
- If new haemolysis, thrombocytopenia and worsening AKI develop after initial sepsis, actively look for TMA: check smear, DAT, ADAMTS13 and STEC PCR; involve nephrology/haematology early.
- PEX may be used as a bridge; eculizumab treats CM‑HUS but timing relative to infection and appropriate vaccination/prophylaxis matters.

Capnocytophaga is uncommon, but its consequences can be catastrophic and unexpected. A careful exposure history, early broad empiric antibiotics that cover beta‑lactamase producers, and vigilance for delayed TMA will save time — and kidneys.



# Needles in a Storm: When and How a Kidney Biopsy Clarifies TMA

This patient’s journey — a previously healthy woman bitten by her dog, fulminant Capnocytophaga canimorsus sepsis with DIC, dialysis-dependent acute kidney injury (AKI), and then a late switch to isolated haemolysis with schistocytes — nicely illustrates an increasingly familiar, frustrating clinical problem: sepsis and DIC can mask a superimposed thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). When the diagnosis is uncertain and the kidneys are on dialysis, a timely renal biopsy can be the difference between empiric, temporizing measures and targeted therapy (in this case, eculizumab for complement-mediated HUS). But doing a biopsy in a patient with prior coagulopathy, recent plasma exchange (PEX), and low platelets requires deliberate planning.

Below I walk through practical questions clinicians face when contemplating a kidney biopsy in suspected TMA with dialysis-dependent AKI: when to biopsy, how to make it as safe as possible, what histology you’re trying to capture, and how the pathology report should change your management and prognostic expectations. I anchor the discussion in the case above — biopsy showed acute tubular necrosis (ATN) plus ischaemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi and crescents, findings that confirmed an active TMA and supported initiation and continuation of complement blockade.

## Why biopsy at all in dialysis-dependent AKI with suspected TMA?

Because the differential for AKI with haemolysis and thrombocytopenia in the context of sepsis is broad and therapeutic implications are huge.

- A biopsy can confirm TMA (glomerular/capillary thrombi, endothelial swelling, mesangiolysis, subendothelial widening) versus isolated ATN (predominantly tubular epithelial necrosis without glomerular thrombi), immune-complex GN, or cortical necrosis.
- In TMA, the severity and chronicity on biopsy (percentage of glomeruli with thrombi, degree of cortical necrosis, interstitial fibrosis/atrophy) informs prognosis and the urgency/likely benefit of expensive, high-risk interventions such as eculizumab.
- Importantly, biopsy may be decisive when laboratory testing is equivocal: e.g., normal C3/C4, ADAMTS13 not profoundly low, negative STEC testing, or when DIC could explain schistocytes yet thrombocytopenia persists after coagulopathy resolves.

In the presented case a biopsy obtained after partial haematologic recovery showed fibrin thrombi and crescents — pathology that shifted the team from supportive care and PEX to full complement blockade with good haematologic remission and partial renal recovery.

## Timing and safety thresholds: when is kidney biopsy reasonable?

You will rarely get perfect coagulation numbers in these patients. The decision is a balance of diagnostic need and bleeding risk.

General safety thresholds for a percutaneous ultrasound-guided biopsy:
- Platelets: ideally ≥50–75 × 10^9/L. Many centres accept ≥50 for straightforward biopsies; some prefer ≥75 when anaesthesia resources or immediate transfusion are limited. In the case above biopsy was performed when platelets were 77 × 10^9/L after PEX sessions — a reasonable threshold.
- INR: <1.5 (if on warfarin, reverse to target). aPTT should be near normal.
- Fibrinogen: >1.0–1.5 g/L is reasonable; severe hypofibrinogenaemia increases bleeding risk.
- Active DIC with consumptive coagulopathy is a relative contra-indication until corrected. If coagulation abnormalities are transient and improving, defer until improved.

When thresholds can’t be met:
- Consider transjugular renal biopsy (TJR) — preferred when platelets <50 × 10^9/L, INR >1.5, or ongoing coagulopathy. TJR has lower perirenal bleed risk and allows central venous haemostasis if bleeding occurs.
- Surgical (open) biopsy is rarely needed but may be considered in select scenarios.

Practical peri-procedural items:
- If platelet transfusion is required, give it immediately prior to the biopsy to raise platelets into acceptable range — remember transfused platelet effect can wane quickly.
- Hold anticoagulants for appropriate windows: stop unfractionated heparin several hours before; LMWH 24 hours for prophylactic doses and 48 hours for therapeutic doses (institutional policies vary); DOACs 48–72 hours based on renal function and agent; warfarin reversal to INR <1.5.
- If the patient is on dialysis with systemic anticoagulation during sessions, plan dialysis timing and anticoagulant hold to minimise residual anticoagulant effect.
- Ensure availability of cross-matched blood and rapid access to interventional radiology if needed.

## PEX, complement sampling, and timing of biopsy — the order matters

PEX and complement-targeted therapies complicate both diagnostics and biopsy timing.

- PEX removes complement proteins and autoantibodies and can transiently change complement assays and ADAMTS13 levels. If possible, obtain key diagnostic blood samples (ADAMTS13, C3/C4, functional complement assays, anti-factor H antibodies, and baseline serum for genetic testing) before PEX or complement blockade.
- PEX can also correct some coagulopathy and raise platelets (via plasma/platelet products), making biopsy safer — in the presented case, PEX sessions preceded biopsy and the platelet count had recovered to 77 × 10^9/L.
- If eculizumab is being considered, recognize that administering it before obtaining baseline complement samples may confound laboratory interpretation. But clinical reality may force therapy first — don’t delay life- or kidney-saving therapy solely for perfect labs.
- Coordinate with transfusion medicine, nephrology, haematology and the lab to get samples drawn and shipped correctly when there’s a plan for PEX or eculizumab.

## What you’re looking for under the microscope — TMA vs isolated ATN

TMA (acute, active) — hallmarks:
- Fibrin thrombi occluding glomerular capillaries and/or small arterioles.
- Endothelial cell swelling and detachment; mesangiolysis.
- Red cell fragmentation in capillaries; often crescents if severe.
- On electron microscopy: subendothelial electron-lucent widening, accumulation of fluffy material, occasional dense deposits from chronic injury.
- Minimal immune complex deposition on immunofluorescence (unless overlap disease).

Isolated ATN — hallmarks:
- Patchy tubular epithelial necrosis, loss of brush border, sloughing of epithelial cells, granular casts in tubules.
- Relatively preserved glomeruli without intraglomerular fibrin thrombi.
- Less glomerular endothelial injury; immunofluorescence usually negative.

Mixed pictures occur. In the case presented, biopsy found severe ATN plus ischaemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi and scattered crescents — a mixed but TMA-dominant picture that supports complement-mediated microvascular injury atop ischemic tubular damage.

Why the distinction matters:
- A biopsy showing pure ATN suggests supportive therapy and time; immunosuppression or complement blockade would be unlikely to help.
- Evidence of active TMA (fibrin thrombi, endothelial injury) makes a stronger case for urgent TMA-directed therapy (PEX while awaiting ADAMTS13, and consideration of eculizumab for CM-HUS when TTP and STEC are unlikely).

## How pathology alters management and prognostication

Management:
- Confirmed TMA in a dialysis-dependent patient — especially with active haemolysis and schistocytosis — raises the threshold to start targeted therapy (e.g., eculizumab for suspected CM-HUS) sooner rather than later. The case patient had haemolysis only after DIC improved; biopsy confirmed ongoing microvascular thrombosis and supported complement blockade initiation.
- If ADAMTS13 <10% → TTP, immediate PEX is lifesaving and immunosuppression indicated; biopsy is rarely necessary to make that decision.
- If biopsy shows predominantly chronic lesions (extensive cortical necrosis, widespread glomerulosclerosis, heavy interstitial fibrosis), the likelihood of renal recovery is low and this informs discussions about dialysis dependence, transplant candidacy, and duration of expensive therapies.
- If biopsy is equivocal, integrate clinical course, labs (ADAMTS13, STEC PCR, complement testing), and response to initial therapies into decision-making.

Prognosis:
- Extent of glomerular thrombosis, crescents, and cortical necrosis correlates with worse renal outcomes.
- Degree of interstitial fibrosis/ tubular atrophy predicts recovery potential.
- Even with severe histologic TMA, early complement blockade is associated with better hematologic remission and some renal recovery — again arguing for timely diagnosis and therapy.

## Practical algorithm (conceptual)

1. Suspect TMA clinically: MAHA, schistocytes, rising LDH, undetectable haptoglobin, thrombocytopenia, AKI.
2. Immediate labs: CBC, peripheral smear, LDH, haptoglobin, DAT, creatinine, coagulation panel, ADAMTS13 (stat send), STEC stool PCR, C3/C4, blood cultures if infection suspected.
3. If ADAMTS13 likely TTP (score high or strong clinical suspicion) → start PEX emergently.
4. If ADAMTS13 not low and clinical picture suggests CM-HUS (infection trigger, renal-predominant, biopsy feasible) → arrange biopsy if safe, and consider PEX as bridging if uncertain.
5. Draw complement and ADAMTS13 samples before PEX or eculizumab when possible.
6. If biopsy demonstrates active TMA and other causes excluded, initiate/continue eculizumab promptly, with appropriate meningococcal vaccination and antimicrobial prophylaxis as per local protocols.

## Clinical pitfalls and pearls

- Don’t let earlier DIC hide a later TMA. Normalising INR and fibrinogen doesn’t rule out ongoing endothelial microthrombosis.
- ADAMTS13 is diagnostic for TTP but often delayed — use PLASMIC score to triage, but don’t rely solely on it.
- PEX can transiently improve clinical parameters (and creatinine/diuresis) but may not treat the underlying complement dysregulation. Pathology will help decide whether PEX was a bridge or sufficient therapy.
- Obtain complement and ADAMTS13 samples before PEX or eculizumab if possible — both interventions will confound testing.
- If platelets are too low for percutaneous biopsy, transjugular biopsy is an excellent alternative and is underused.
- Even with negative genetic testing for complement variants, severe infection can trigger CM-HUS — absence of a pathogenic mutation does not preclude benefit from eculizumab.

## Key take-home messages

- Consider kidney biopsy in dialysis-dependent AKI when TMA is suspected but not proven — the histology can confirm diagnosis, stage injury, and change therapy.
- Aim for platelets ≥50–75 × 10^9/L, INR <1.5, and corrected fibrinogen for percutaneous biopsy; use transjugular biopsy when thresholds can’t be met.
- Draw ADAMTS13 and baseline complement studies before PEX or eculizumab when feasible; PEX and eculizumab will alter test results.
- TMA histology (glomerular/arteriolar fibrin thrombi, endothelial injury, crescents) steers toward targeted therapies like eculizumab and predicts greater urgency; isolated ATN argues for supportive care.
- Don’t delay empiric, life-saving therapy when clinical suspicion is high; use biopsy and labs to refine and guide ongoing management rather than to postpone necessary treatment.

This case — from a dog bite to sepsis to a hidden complement-mediated TMA uncovered by biopsy — is a reminder that needle and microscope still have a central role when the storm of critical illness hides the diagnosis.



# Don’t Trust the C3: Making Sense of Complement Studies and Genetics in CM‑HUS

What’s interesting: a previously healthy woman developed fulminant sepsis 4 days after a dog bite from Capnocytophaga canimorsus, progressed to DIC and dialysis-dependent acute kidney injury, then — after initial improvement — developed a sudden microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia with schistocytes. C3 and C4 were normal. ADAMTS13 was not consistent with TTP. Kidney biopsy showed thrombotic microangiopathy and the team treated with plasma exchange then eculizumab with clinical improvement. Extended testing showed a very low alternative pathway functional assay (AH50 = 6%) — but that was drawn after plasma exchange (PEX) and one dose of eculizumab. Genetic testing later returned without pathogenic variants.

The punchline: normal C3/C4 does not exclude complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS). Timing of sampling, prior PEX and the use of terminal complement blockade (eculizumab) can profoundly change functional and quantitative complement assays. Misinterpreting those results risks both over‑calling consumption and under‑treating a life‑threatening TMA.

Here’s a practical, case‑grounded guide to what to send, when to send it, how to interpret results in the context of therapies, and how genetics should (and should not) drive treatment decisions.

## Why “normal C3/C4” is misleading
C3 and C4 are crude systemic markers. In CM‑HUS the pathologic complement activation is often local (microvascular endothelium) and can occur with normal systemic C3/C4. Complement activation fragments can be rapidly turned over, redistributed, or replaced by donor plasma during PEX. In sepsis, complement levels can be variably low, normal, or even elevated depending on timing and the pathogen. In this case C3/C4 were within reference range early, yet the patient clearly had a TMA driven by complement dysregulation — shown by biopsy and a dramatic response to complement blockade.

Key applications:
- Don’t use normal C3/C4 to “rule out” CM‑HUS.
- Use C3/C4 as part of the picture, not the deciding factor.

## What to send — and try to get before plasma exchange or eculizumab
If you suspect TMA (MAHA, schistocytes, thrombocytopenia ± AKI), prioritize tests that change immediate management and that are invalidated by PEX or eculizumab if delayed.

Urgent/early tests to obtain before PEX/eculizumab if at all possible:
- ADAMTS13 activity (and inhibitor) — to exclude TTP (activity <10% typical of TTP). Local labs are usually fastest.
- Shiga‑toxin PCR from stool — to exclude STEC‑HUS.
- Routine complement panel: C3, C4 (understand their limits).
- Functional assays: CH50 (classical pathway) and AH50 (alternative pathway) — these are very timing‑sensitive.
- Complement protein levels: Factor H, Factor I, Factor B, and soluble C5b‑9 when available.
- Anti‑factor H autoantibody testing (ELISA).
- Samples for genetic testing: EDTA whole blood for a complement gene panel (CFH, CFI, CFB, C3, MCP/CD46, THBD, etc.). These are not urgent for acute decisionmaking but collect early because once you start PEX or transfusions, DNA testing is still fine but factors/autoantibodies may be altered.
- Send a sample for frozen serum (avoid hemolysis) for any specialized tests your reference lab requests — many require specific collection/transport conditions.

Practical note from the case: ADAMTS13 was available quickly and was 43.5% (not TTP). The team drew extended complement testing but logistical constraints meant some samples were taken after Octaplas PEX and one dose of eculizumab — which explains the very low AH50 result despite normal C3/C4 earlier.

## How PEX and eculizumab change the lab picture
PEX:
- Removes circulating complement proteins, activation fragments and autoantibodies.
- Replaces them with donor plasma (which may transiently normalize or alter levels).
- Can make interpretation of complement kinetics impossible for days; autoantibody testing and functional assays can be falsely low or normalized depending on timing and the replacement product (eg, Octaplas).

Eculizumab:
- Binds C5 and blocks terminal complement activation.
- Causes rapid and profound reduction in functional terminal pathway assays (CH50 and often AH50 as well).
- After eculizumab, CH50 typically falls to near zero; AH50 may also be low because assays measure terminal pathway function.
- Eculizumab itself does not change genetic testing, but it confounds functional assays for the entire course of therapy.

Interpreting low AH50 or CH50 after treatment:
- If the test was drawn after eculizumab → low CH50/AH50 is expected and reflects drug effect, not disease consumption.
- If drawn after PEX → low or normalized results could reflect removal or donor replacement; either way, the result is unreliable for diagnostic attribution.
- Always document timing of PEX/eculizumab on the lab requisition and tell the reference lab — they can often interpret results in that context.

In the case, the AH50 of 6% was most consistent with treatment effects (PEX + eculizumab) rather than intrinsic complement depletion.

## Putting the lab data in clinical context
Diagnosis of CM‑HUS is clinical + pathologic + exclusionary:
- Look for MAHA (schistocytes), thrombocytopenia and AKI ± biopsy evidence of TMA.
- Exclude TTP (ADAMTS13) and STEC‑HUS (stool PCR) promptly.
- Consider timing: infection‑triggered CM‑HUS often becomes apparent as sepsis is improving (as in this patient).
- Complement labs can help but will not always show “consumption” — the biopsy and response to complement blockade are powerful diagnostic clues.
- If biopsy shows fibrin thrombi and TMA, that supports treating for CM‑HUS if other causes are excluded.

## Genetics: what a negative or positive result means for therapy duration
Background yield: a substantial minority of CM‑HUS patients have identifiable pathogenic variants or anti‑factor H antibodies (historically quoted roughly 50–70% may have variants/antibodies depending on cohort and testing depth). However, penetrance is incomplete and many cases are triggered by identifiable secondary events (infection, pregnancy, drugs).

How results affect management:
- Pathogenic variant (especially CFH) or anti‑factor H antibodies: increased risk of relapse; favors longer‑term or indefinite complement blockade and closer monitoring.
- MCP/CD46 mutations often have a milder renal prognosis and sometimes allow treatment withdrawal considerations once clinically stable.
- No pathogenic variant: raises possibility of single‑hit, infection‑triggered CM‑HUS. You can consider finite duration of eculizumab (eg, months) with planned reassessment and rapid withdrawal plan, but decisions should be individualized by severity, renal recovery, and multidisciplinary input.
- A negative result does NOT exclude CM‑HUS or mean that complement blockade was unnecessary. Genetic testing is a tool for risk stratification, not an acute diagnostic gatekeeper.

In the presented case, no pathogenic variants were found. Because the patient had severe disease and partial renal recovery, the treating team continued maintenance eculizumab with plans for reassessment — a pragmatic balance between relapse risk and therapy harms.

## Practical management tips rooted in the case
- Get ADAMTS13 and stool Shiga PCR early — those results change immediate therapy.
- Whenever feasible, draw complement functional assays (CH50/AH50), factor H/I/B levels, anti‑FH antibodies and freeze serum for reference testing before PEX or eculizumab. If you can’t, document timing of interventions and expect assay limitations.
- If you must start PEX or eculizumab urgently, don’t delay life‑saving therapy for specialized testing — but still draw baseline samples first if possible.
- Start eculizumab after reasonable control of the underlying infection (ideally after blood cultures are negative and with meningococcal vaccination/prophylaxis), balancing the risk of unchecked infection and the clear benefit for TMA.
- Kidney biopsy can be invaluable when safe and feasible — it anchored the diagnosis in this case.
- Genetic counselling is important: explain incomplete penetrance, possible negative results, and implications for family members and duration of therapy.

## Key take‑home messages
- Normal C3/C4 does NOT exclude CM‑HUS; complement activation can be local and systemic markers may be normal.
- Draw ADAMTS13 and stool Shiga PCR urgently; these tests most rapidly alter immediate management.
- Obtain CH50/AH50, factor H/I/B, anti‑factor H autoantibodies and freeze serum for specialized testing before PEX or eculizumab whenever possible; once PEX or eculizumab is given, functional assays are difficult to interpret.
- A very low CH50/AH50 after eculizumab or after PEX usually reflects treatment effect, not disease “consumption.”
- Genetic testing is for risk stratification and family counselling: a pathogenic variant supports prolonged therapy, but a negative panel does not exclude CM‑HUS and should not retroactively negate appropriate treatment.
- Clinical context (biopsy, schistocytes, response to therapy) and exclusion of TTP/STEC are central — don’t over‑rely on a single complement number.

Final note: this patient’s journey — from C. canimorsus sepsis to dialysis and then to remission after complement blockade — is a powerful reminder that complement assays are tools whose value depends entirely on timing and context. When in doubt, treat the patient, not the laboratory value.



# When a Positive Coombs Misleads: Drug-Related DAT Positivity vs MAHA

What makes this case interesting
A previously healthy woman developed fulminant Capnocytophaga canimorsus sepsis after a dog bite. After initial improvement, she suddenly developed severe anaemia with a positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT, Coombs). That initial positive DAT sent the team down the autoimmune haemolytic anaemia (AIHA) pathway and steroids were started — but the patient didn’t get better. A peripheral blood film showing 15–20% schistocytes, rising LDH, undetectable haptoglobin and persistent thrombocytopaenia ultimately pointed to a thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) — complement-mediated HUS — not AIHA. Plasma exchange (PEX) was used as a bridge and eculizumab induced sustained remission and partial renal recovery.

This case is a great reminder that a positive DAT in a sick, transfused or drug-exposed patient can be a red herring. If you miss the TMA, the delay costs kidney function — and lives.

Why a DAT can be misleading in septic patients
The DAT detects immunoglobulin or complement bound to red blood cells. It is sensitive but not specific for autoimmune haemolysis. Causes of DAT positivity beyond classic warm AIHA include:

- Drug-related coating of RBCs (penicillins and cephalosporins are classic offenders).
- Recent transfusions or plasma products (passive antibodies or complement).
- Infectious processes that fix complement to RBC surfaces.
- Laboratory artifacts.

In this patient the first DAT was IgG-positive but later became negative. She had exposure to beta‑lactam antibiotics (including penicillin derivatives and blood products), and received transfusions — all plausible contributors to a false- or drug‑related DAT. The key point: a positive DAT does not automatically equal warm AIHA.

Red flags that point away from AIHA and toward MAHA/TMA
When anaemia occurs in a septic patient, ask whether the blood picture looks haemolytic from immune-mediated extravascular destruction or from microangiopathic intravascular shearing. Features that should make you think MAHA/TMA rather than AIHA include:

- Schistocytes on peripheral smear — even modest numbers (>1% by some labs; here 15–20% was dramatic). Schistocytes are the hallmark of mechanical RBC fragmentation.
- Marked LDH rise with haemolysis and undetectable haptoglobin.
- New or persistent thrombocytopaenia concurrent with hemolysis.
- Acute kidney injury out of proportion to hypotension or sepsis.
- Poor or paradoxically low reticulocyte response (can occur early in sepsis or with marrow suppression).
- Lack of response to steroids (in AIHA high-dose steroids usually help).
In the case presented, several of these features were present: schistocytosis, rising LDH, undetectable haptoglobin, AKI requiring dialysis and thrombocytopenia — all arguing strongly for TMA.

Why steroids won’t fix most TMAs
AIHA is mediated by antibody opsonization and splenic clearance of RBCs — dampening the immune response with steroids makes sense. TMA (TTP, STEC-HUS, complement-mediated HUS) is an endothelial and microvascular process: platelets aggregate in the microcirculation, fibrin forms, and red cells fragment across fibrin strands. The underlying pathophysiology differs — immunosuppression alone does not interrupt microthrombi formation. Thus steroids are often ineffective for TMA and can distract from lifesaving therapy (PEX for suspected TTP; complement blockade for CM‑HUS).

A pragmatic algorithm — how to pivot quickly from a positive DAT to a TMA workup
Use the clinical context and the peripheral smear to guide you. Below is a practical, stepwise approach drawn from the case and common practice in acute care:

1) Stabilize first: airway, breathing, circulation, transfuse if life‑threatening anaemia, support renal function (monitor urine output, consider urgent dialysis if anuric or volume overloaded).

2) Immediately get targeted labs while you stabilize: peripheral blood smear, LDH, bilirubin (indirect), haptoglobin, reticulocyte count, CBC with platelets, coagulation profile (PT/INR, aPTT, fibrinogen, D‑dimer), creatinine/urinalysis, blood cultures. Order a DAT but don’t over‑interpret the result in isolation.

3) If you see MAHA features (schistocytes, low haptoglobin, high LDH) with thrombocytopaenia and/or AKI — start thinking TMA. Do not wait for DAT results to drive management.

4) Send ADAMTS13 activity immediately. If clinical suspicion for TTP is high (neurologic signs, very low platelets, minimal renal involvement), start PEX emergently — do not wait for ADAMTS13. If ADAMTS13 returns <10%, TTP is likely and PEX is first‑line.

5) While you’re doing the above, look for infectious triggers: stool PCR for Shiga-toxin, blood cultures, and a careful history for animals, recent procedures, pregnancy, drugs. In this case, blood cultures grew Capnocytophaga canimorsus, an unusual but real trigger of complement-mediated TMA.

6) Consider complement-mediated HUS when ADAMTS13 is not severely reduced and STEC is excluded, especially with prominent renal failure and biopsy evidence of TMA. Complement studies (C3/C4, functional assays) can be sent, but remember normal C3/C4 does not exclude CM‑HUS.

7) Use PEX as a pragmatic bridge if the diagnosis is unclear (it can transiently help in CM‑HUS and is lifesaving in TTP). Definitive therapy for CM‑HUS is complement blockade (eculizumab). Balance the timing of eculizumab in the setting of active infection (vaccinate as possible, give prophylactic antibiotics, and preferentially start eculizumab after bacteremia is controlled).

8) Obtain renal biopsy when the diagnosis is unclear or when renal prognosis will influence therapy; biopsy in this case confirmed TMA and helped clinch the diagnosis.

Pitfalls and practical pearls from this case
- A positive DAT can be induced by penicillins/cephalosporins and by transfused plasma — always review drug and transfusion history when DAT is positive.
- Don’t be reassured by normal complement C3/C4 — complement levels can be normal in CM‑HUS.
- ADAMTS13 <10% = TTP; intermediate values (eg ~40% as in this case) argue against TTP and should prompt searching for other TMAs.
- Schistocytes are a critical bedside test. If you suspect TMA but the smear is not reviewed, request it urgently — automated counters don't report schistocytes reliably.
- Initiate PEX promptly if TTP is in the differential; do not delay while awaiting confirmatory labs.
- Consider infection‑triggered CM‑HUS even in immunocompetent adults when the clinical pattern fits — C. canimorsus is an uncommon but documented trigger.
- When using eculizumab, vaccinate against encapsulated organisms and provide antibiotic prophylaxis if vaccines cannot be given 2 weeks prior.

Key take-home messages
- A positive DAT in a septic, transfused or drug‑exposed patient is not proof of warm AIHA — it can be false positive or drug‑related.
- Look at the smear and haemolysis panel: schistocytes, very high LDH, undetectable haptoglobin, persistent thrombocytopaenia and AKI point to MAHA/TMA, not AIHA.
- Steroids treat AIHA, not TMAs. Don’t let a positive DAT delay PEX or complement-directed therapy when TMA is likely.
- Send ADAMTS13 urgently, start PEX if TTP is suspected, and consider CM‑HUS (and eculizumab) when ADAMTS13 is not severely reduced and STEC is excluded — balance eculizumab timing against active infection risk.
- In the presented case a misleading DAT was likely related to antibiotics/transfusions; recognition of MAHA and timely transition to a TMA pathway (PEX then eculizumab) led to haematologic remission and partial renal recovery.

If you remember one thing: in a sick septic patient, let the smear and the pattern of haemolysis guide you more than a single DAT result.
