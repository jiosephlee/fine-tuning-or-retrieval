Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

## From bite to bedside: initial presentation, sepsis, and early management

This patient’s course began with a straightforward exposure—a dog bite to the upper lip—followed by a rapid systemic deterioration over 4 days. The temporal relationship is important: she was prescribed oral phenoxymethylpenicillin at the time of the bite but did not start it, and within days developed high fevers (>40 °C), profound malaise with poor intake, refractory hypoglycaemia and later numbness and discolouration of her feet. The rapid progression from a localized bite to overwhelming systemic illness should immediately raise concern for an invasive zoonotic pathogen, and in retrospect fit the classic timeline for fulminant Capnocytophaga canimorsus infection.

On arrival her examination and initial laboratory tests documented multiorgan involvement. She was septic in appearance with mottled, cool lower extremities. Labs showed pancytopenia, marked inflammatory response, metabolic acidosis with elevated lactate (5.7 mmol/L), acute kidney injury with oliguria progressing to anuria, elevated transaminases and coagulopathy. Urinalysis revealed proteinuria (2+) and haematuria (3+), supporting kidney involvement. Clinically she also had refractory hypoglycaemia (nadir 0.6 mmol/L), which in severe sepsis commonly reflects a combination of impaired hepatic gluconeogenesis from liver dysfunction, depleted glycogen reserves, and increased peripheral glucose utilization; glucagon resistance may be seen if hepatic dysfunction is severe, explaining failure to correct hypoglycaemia with usual measures.

The constellation of thrombocytopenia, prolonged coagulation parameters and evidence of end-organ ischaemia (mottling, distal cyanosis) led to the working diagnosis of disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) secondary to bacteraemia. DIC is a dynamic consumptive coagulopathy in which systemic activation of coagulation causes microvascular thrombosis and consumption of platelets and clotting factors, producing a laboratory pattern of thrombocytopenia, prolonged PT/INR and APTT, elevated D-dimer/fibrin degradation products, and low fibrinogen—features that were clinically and biochemically present in this patient. The skin findings (mottling, ecchymoses and later distal ischemic changes of toes and fingers) are compatible with microthrombotic phenomena; importantly, palpable pulses and preserved sensation argued against large-vessel occlusion or compartmental/necrotising soft-tissue infection as the cause. The quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) score was 1/3 in this case; qSOFA is a rapid bedside tool to screen for patients at increased risk of poor outcomes from sepsis but is not a substitute for a comprehensive evaluation—here the overall clinical picture and laboratory severity dictated ICU-level care despite a modest qSOFA.

Early differential diagnoses appropriately included septic complications of the bite (including necrotising soft tissue infection), haematological causes of cytopenias such as acute leukaemia, and hyperinflammatory syndromes like haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH). Necrotising fasciitis was considered because of mottling and rapidly evolving local signs but was effectively excluded by clinical and radiologic assessment. Acute leukaemia and HLH were entertained because profound pancytopenia can reflect primary marrow pathology or immune-mediated marrow suppression; haematology input and flow cytometry were therefore sought and ultimately normal, lowering the likelihood of acute leukaemia and supporting an infectious consumptive or suppressive process as the driver of cytopenias.

Because a dog bite raises a specific microbiological risk profile, empiric antimicrobial therapy was selected to address typical and atypical canine bite pathogens. Broad-spectrum intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam was started on admission to cover Pasteurella species, anaerobes and gram-negative rods frequently associated with animal bites, and importantly to provide activity against Capnocytophaga species. This choice reflects the clinical imperative to start timely, empiric antibiotics that cover likely organisms when systemic infection is suspected; in this case cultures later confirmed C. canimorsus bacteremia, validating the empiric selection. It is worth noting that Capnocytophaga canimorsus is a fastidious, encapsulated gram-negative rod that can cause fulminant sepsis even in previously healthy individuals. Because it grows slowly and requires enriched culture conditions, blood-culture results can be delayed (often several days); modern molecular methods (PCR, sequencing) can speed diagnosis but were not needed here because both aerobic and anaerobic blood cultures became positive approximately 7 days after presentation.

Her ICU course illustrates the typical supportive priorities for severe sepsis with multiorgan failure. She became anuric despite aggressive fluids and diuretics and developed fluid overload in the setting of septic cardiomyopathy—an often reversible myocardial depression seen in sepsis that impairs cardiac output and can limit tolerance of fluid resuscitation. Owing to persistent anuria and volume overload, continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) was initiated. Supportive haematologic care included transfusion of platelets and fibrinogen concentrates given the consumptive coagulopathy and bleeding. Skin ecchymoses and distal ischemic changes evolved, consistent with continuing microvascular thrombosis from DIC. She did not require prolonged vasopressor support beyond stress-dose corticosteroids in the early septic phase, which is notable but does not obviate the need for close hemodynamic monitoring and organ-supportive therapies.

In summary, the early phase of this case illustrates several teaching points: a history of animal bite with rapid systemic symptoms should prompt immediate empiric broad-spectrum antibiotics covering bite pathogens; severe sepsis may present with profound metabolic disturbances such as refractory hypoglycaemia and multiorgan dysfunction including AKI and coagulopathy; DIC is a common complication of fulminant bacteraemia and explains the combination of thrombocytopenia, bleeding and microthrombotic ischemia; and because fastidious organisms such as C. canimorsus can have delayed culture positivity, clinical decisions must be guided by exposure history and the evolving clinical picture while awaiting microbiological confirmation.

## Recognizing superimposed TMA: diagnostic pivot from resolving sepsis to CM-HUS

The diagnostic turning point in this case was clinical: after several days of treatment the patient’s sepsis and coagulopathy appeared to be improving, yet she developed a sudden, isolated fall in haemoglobin with ongoing thrombocytopenia. That paradox—improvement in infection markers and normalization of PT/aPTT/fibrinogen while anaemia and low platelets persisted—should prompt immediate re-evaluation for a superimposed microangiopathic process rather than attribution to the earlier disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). In this patient the haemoglobin dropped from 111 to 68 g/L over six days without clinical bleeding or haemodynamic compromise, making haemolysis the leading explanation.

Laboratory evidence established the haemolytic picture. Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) rose and haptoglobin became undetectable, classical biochemical markers of intravascular haemolysis. The absence of overt bleeding and the documented fall in haemoglobin despite improving sepsis made transfusion loss or ongoing surgical bleeding unlikely. Early reticulocyte response may be blunted after severe sepsis or in bone‑marrow–suppressed patients, so a non‑compensatory reticulocytosis does not exclude haemolysis in this setting.

An early direct antiglobulin test (DAT) was positive for IgG, which raised the possibility of autoimmune haemolytic anaemia and led to a short trial of high‑dose steroids. However, the clinical course argued against true warm autoimmune haemolysis: the haemolysis and thrombocytopenia failed to respond to steroids, and the DAT subsequently reverted to negative. Infections, some drugs (notably penicillins), and recent transfusions can cause transient DAT positivity. Thus an initial positive DAT in the context of recent antibiotics and transfusions must be interpreted cautiously and does not obviate evaluation for microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA).

The peripheral blood smear proved decisive. A high proportion of schistocytes (fragmented red cells) — reported here at 15–20% — is direct morphologic evidence of MAHA and strongly suggests an intravascular red‑cell shearing process from thrombi in the microcirculation. Once schistocytosis is identified, attention must focus on causes of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), because management differs markedly from that of isolated autoimmune haemolysis or DIC.

Distinguishing residual DIC from a new TMA can be challenging because both can present with thrombocytopenia, haemolysis and organ dysfunction. In this patient the temporal evolution was informative: coagulation tests and fibrinogen normalized while haemolysis and thrombocytopenia persisted, which argues against ongoing consumptive coagulopathy. Clinically localising features also favoured TMA—severe, dialysis‑dependent acute kidney injury and biopsy‑compatible renal microthrombi, and cutaneous ischaemic changes consistent with microvascular thrombosis rather than consumptive bleeding. Thus, a new TMA process superimposed on resolving sepsis/DIC became the working diagnosis.

A focused rule‑out panel was essential. Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) is the immediate emergency to exclude because it requires urgent plasma exchange. In this case a PLASMIC score was low (4/7) and ADAMTS13 activity returned at 43.5%, well above the <10% threshold that defines severe ADAMTS13 deficiency and TTP—making TTP very unlikely. Shiga‑toxin–associated HUS (STEC‑HUS) was also excluded by negative stool PCR and the absence of a diarrhoeal prodrome.

Complement testing and its timing require careful interpretation. Standard complement levels (C3, C4) and pathway screens may be within normal limits in complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS); therefore normal values do not exclude the diagnosis. In this patient, early routine C3/C4 were normal. Later, extended testing showed a markedly reduced AH50 (alternative pathway activity), but those samples were obtained after plasma exchange sessions and after eculizumab dosing—interventions that can suppress functional assays. Plasma exchange can dilute or remove complement components and autoantibodies, and eculizumab blocks terminal complement activity and will reduce AH50. Therefore, interpreting complement function tests requires knowledge of these antecedent therapies; negative or low results obtained after such treatments often reflect therapy effects rather than baseline complement status.

Renal histology provided the clinicopathologic confirmation. Biopsy showed severe acute tubular necrosis plus ischaemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi and scattered crescents—features characteristic of TMA. Immunofluorescence was negative for immune‑complex deposition and electron microscopy did not show features of a primary glomerulopathy, supporting a non‑immune, thrombotic microvascular injury rather than immune‑complex glomerulonephritis or vasculitis.

Putting the pieces together, the diagnosis reached was complement‑mediated HUS triggered by Capnocytophaga canimorsus bacteraemia. This is a diagnosis of exclusion based on (1) convincing MAHA with schistocytes, rising LDH and absent haptoglobin, (2) renal biopsy evidence of TMA, (3) exclusion of TTP (ADAMTS13 not severely reduced) and STEC‑HUS, and (4) the temporal relationship to infection with C. canimorsus and clinical response after complement‑directed therapy. Practically, the team appropriately initiated plasma exchange as a bridging measure while awaiting definitive tests and then proceeded with eculizumab once active bacteraemia was controlled and vaccination/prophylaxis measures were in place. This sequence highlights key diagnostic and therapeutic principles: remain vigilant for TMA when anaemia and thrombocytopenia persist despite resolving sepsis; use morphology and targeted laboratory testing to distinguish causes; remember the limitations of complement assays after intervention; and balance the risks and benefits of complement blockade in an infection‑triggered CM‑HUS.

## Pathophysiology in focus: alternative complement pathway dysregulation and infectious triggers

Understanding complement-mediated HUS requires grasping how the alternative complement pathway is normally restrained at host surfaces, what happens when that restraint fails, and how an infectious stimulus can tip the balance toward a microvascular thrombotic process. In this patient the clinical picture—delayed onset of microangiopathic haemolysis, thrombocytopenia and kidney biopsy evidence of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) after an episode of fulminant Capnocytophaga canimorsus sepsis—is best explained by excessive activation of the alternative pathway at the endothelial surface.

The alternative pathway and its regulators
The alternative pathway serves as a constant amplification loop for complement activation and is kept in check on host cells by both fluid-phase and membrane-bound regulators. Factor B is a component of the alternative C3 convertase (C3bBb): C3b binds factor B, which is then cleaved by factor D to produce the active convertase that generates more C3b and amplifies the response. Factor H is the principal fluid-phase regulator: it discriminates self from non‑self by binding host polyanions (eg, sialic acids, glycosaminoglycans) and accelerates decay of the C3 convertase and acts as a cofactor for factor I–mediated proteolytic inactivation of C3b. Factor I is the serine protease that, with cofactors (factor H in fluid phase and membrane cofactor protein/CD46 on cells), cleaves and inactivates deposited C3b. CD46 (membrane cofactor protein) is a widely expressed membrane cofactor that protects endothelial and other host cells by promoting factor I–mediated C3b cleavage locally. When one or more of these regulators is deficient, dysfunctional, or inhibited (genetically or by autoantibodies), the amplification loop becomes insufficiently controlled and C3b deposition on host endothelium escalates.

Endothelial injury cascade leading to TMA
When regulation fails at the microvascular endothelium, unchecked amplification causes increased generation of C3 and terminal pathway activation. Two biologically important consequences follow. First, higher levels of C5a and other anaphylatoxins produce endothelial activation and leukocyte recruitment, amplifying inflammation. Second, formation of C5b‑9 (the membrane attack complex, MAC) on endothelial surfaces produces sublethal or lytic endothelial injury. Injured and activated endothelial cells expose subendothelial matrix, express procoagulant molecules and release unusually large von Willebrand factor multimers, all of which promote platelet adhesion and aggregation. The resulting platelet‑rich microthrombi in arterioles and glomerular capillaries shear red cells (producing schistocytes and raised LDH), consume platelets (thrombocytopenia) and cause focal ischemia of kidney tissue (acute kidney injury with the histological features seen on this patient’s biopsy—fibrin thrombi, ischemic glomeruli and tubular injury). Thus, the core triad of MAHA, thrombocytopenia and AKI reflects a pathobiological chain from alternative‑pathway dysregulation to microvascular thrombosis.

Genetics, autoantibodies and the need for a “second hit”
Approximately half to two‑thirds of patients labelled as complement‑mediated HUS harbour identifiable pathogenic variants in complement genes (eg, CFH, CFI, CFB, C3, or MCP/CD46) or have autoantibodies (most classically against factor H). Importantly, these abnormalities show incomplete penetrance: many carriers remain well unless exposed to an environmental trigger. This “second‑hit” concept explains why infections, pregnancy, surgery, certain drugs or other inflammatory states frequently precede overt disease. Autoantibodies to factor H act functionally like genetic loss of regulation and are another recognized mechanism producing the same downstream endothelial injury.

Why routine complement levels (C3/C4) can be misleading
Clinicians often order serum C3 and C4 as screening for complement activation, but normal values do not exclude CM‑HUS. Several reasons account for this. First, activation may be highly localised to the endothelial surface (eg, within glomeruli) with little spill‑over into the systemic circulation, so serum C3 can remain within reference range. Second, C4 is part of the classical and lectin pathways and is often normal because the alternative pathway drives CM‑HUS. Third, timing and prior therapies matter: plasma exchange removes complement components and regulators, and eculizumab blocks terminal pathway activity and may alter functional assays. Finally, assays that measure functional pathway activity (eg, AH50) or specific factor levels and genetic testing are more informative for mechanistic understanding than a single snapshot of C3/C4. In this case, the normal routine complement tests early on and subsequent reduction in alternative‑pathway activity (AH50) later are most consistent with sampling timing and effects of plasma exchange and eculizumab rather than proving absence or presence of intrinsic complement activation.

Capnocytophaga canimorsus as a trigger: a clinical paradox
Capnocytophaga canimorsus is an encapsulated Gram‑negative organism commonly found in dog and cat oral flora. Its capsule and other surface features can variably blunt complement deposition and resist serum killing, allowing invasive infection in some hosts. At the same time, infection provokes complement activation as part of host defence. For a patient predisposed to limited regulatory capacity at the endothelial surface (either due to an underlying defect or transient functional susceptibility), the same complement activation that targets the bacterium can spill over to damage the host endothelium. Thus there is a paradox: complement activity is necessary to clear the pathogen but, when inadequately regulated, that amplified response injures the microvasculature and precipitates CM‑HUS. This duality explains why severe infections, including C. canimorsus sepsis, are well‑recognised triggers of CM‑HUS even when the organism itself is cleared.

Applying these principles to the case
In the reported patient the temporal sequence—severe C. canimorsus sepsis with DIC followed by a period of partial recovery and then the onset of isolated MAHA with persistent thrombocytopenia and biopsy‑proven TMA—fits the “second‑hit” model of CM‑HUS. No pathogenic complement gene variant or anti‑factor H antibodies were found, but the absence of an identifiable inherited abnormality does not exclude complement‑mediated disease: some patients have undetected or low‑penetrance variants, transient functional defects, or regulatory disturbances not captured by current testing. Furthermore, initial normal C3/C4 and the later low AH50 are readily explained by compartmentalised activation and by the modifying effects of plasma exchange and eculizumab on circulating measurements. Therapeutically, blocking terminal complement (C5) with eculizumab targets the final effector steps—reducing C5a‑mediated inflammation and preventing MAC formation on endothelium—and thus interrupts the pathological cascade that produced the patient’s TMA.

## Therapeutic strategy and sequencing: balancing infection control with complement blockade

Management in this patient required two simultaneous priorities: definitive control of the inciting infection and prompt treatment of a life‑threatening thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) driven by terminal complement activation. The decisions made illustrate the practical sequencing and trade‑offs that commonly arise when infection and complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS) coexist.

Initial priorities: infection control and organ support
When a patient presents with sepsis and evolving multiorgan failure, immediate priorities are rapid empiric antimicrobial therapy targeted to the likely pathogens, haemodynamic/resuscitation measures, and organ support. In this case, empiric piperacillin–tazobactam was started to cover common dog‑associated pathogens while blood cultures were pending; when cultures grew Capnocytophaga canimorsus, therapy was narrowed to beta‑lactam agents appropriate for that organism (intravenous ampicillin then oral phenoxymethylpenicillin). Concurrently, aggressive supportive care—fluid resuscitation, glucose replacement for refractory hypoglycaemia, and continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) for anuria and fluid overload—stabilised the patient while definitive diagnostic tests were processed. These measures reduce immediate mortality and may temporally improve end‑organ perfusion, which is important before committing to immunomodulatory therapy.

Plasma exchange (PEX) as a bridging measure
PEX remains an important early intervention when a TMA is suspected but TTP has not yet been excluded, because severe ADAMTS13 deficiency (<10%) requires urgent PEX. In practice, PEX is often started empirically while ADAMTS13 results are awaited or when clinical uncertainty persists. In CM‑HUS, PEX does not correct the underlying complement dysregulation but can transiently remove circulating complement fragments, inflammatory mediators, and autoantibodies; therefore it may yield short‑lived clinical improvement. This case exemplifies that pattern: PEX was used as bridging therapy prior to complement blockade and was associated with a temporary return of diuresis, but sustained haematological and renal recovery required eculizumab. Important practical points when using PEX in this setting are (1) obtain baseline ADAMTS13 and complement samples before PEX if feasible, because PEX and complement inhibitors can confound interpretation; and (2) recognise that PEX will remove therapeutic monoclonal antibody (eculizumab) if performed after dosing, necessitating timing adjustments or extra doses.

Eculizumab: mechanism, dosing and timing
Eculizumab is a monoclonal antibody that binds complement component C5 and prevents formation of the membrane attack complex, halting terminal complement–mediated endothelial injury in CM‑HUS. For adults with CM‑HUS the typical induction regimen is 900 mg IV weekly for four weeks, followed by maintenance 1,200 mg IV every two weeks; this is the schedule used in the presented patient. Multiple observational studies and case series show that earlier initiation of eculizumab is associated with better renal outcomes and reduced progression to dialysis‑dependence, so once CM‑HUS is the working diagnosis and active uncontrolled infection is addressed, complement blockade should be started without unnecessary delay.

Balancing eculizumab with infection risk
Blocking terminal complement substantially increases susceptibility to invasive infection with encapsulated organisms—classically Neisseria meningitidis—and likely increases risk from other encapsulated bacteria such as Capnocytophaga. Therefore, strategies to mitigate infectious risk are critical:

- Delay complement blockade until bacteraemia is controlled when clinically feasible. In this case eculizumab was started only after blood cultures were negative and the patient was improving on antibiotics.
- Administer meningococcal vaccination (MenACWY and MenB) before starting eculizumab whenever possible; current practice is to give both vaccine types because together they cover the main disease‑causing serogroups. Ideally vaccination is completed ≥2 weeks before initiating therapy to allow protective immunity to develop.
- If eculizumab cannot be delayed until 2 weeks after vaccination, provide antibiotic prophylaxis (commonly penicillin V or amoxicillin) from the time of first eculizumab dose until at least 2 weeks after vaccination (or longer per local guidance). In this case the team administered meningococcal vaccines immediately before eculizumab and provided penicillin prophylaxis because full vaccine protection had not yet been achieved.
- Educate the patient about the signs of meningococcal disease and ensure easy access to urgent care and empiric antibiotics for febrile illness. Consider longer‑term vigilance for other encapsulated pathogens and tailor prophylaxis if ongoing eculizumab is planned.

Transfusions and adjunctive measures
Supportive blood product therapy is guided by symptoms and procedures rather than by laboratory values alone. Red blood cell transfusions are indicated for symptomatic anaemia or hemodynamic instability, and were used in this patient during the acute haemolytic phase. Platelet transfusion should be avoided unless there is active bleeding, severe thrombocytopenia with high bleeding risk, or need for an invasive procedure—because transfused platelets may fuel microthrombosis in TMA—yet in practice they are given when clinically required, as occurred in this case for haemorrhagic manifestations. Steroids are appropriate when autoimmune haemolytic anaemia is suspected; however, if DAT and clinical features later exclude immune haemolysis (as happened here), steroids should be stopped to limit unnecessary immunosuppression. Note also that plasma products used for PEX (eg, Octaplas) may transiently replace deficient factors and impact laboratory interpretation.

Monitoring response to therapy
Clinical and laboratory markers determine early response and guide ongoing management. Relevant markers include:

- Haematology: rising platelet count is often the earliest indicator of response; normalisation of LDH and haptoglobin and reduction in schistocytes reflect resolving haemolysis.
- Renal function: return of urine output and downtrending creatinine indicate renal recovery; these guide decisions about CRRT discontinuation and dialysis weaning.
- Complement and ADAMTS13 assays: baseline samples are informative, but results obtained after PEX or eculizumab should be interpreted cautiously because both interventions alter assay results (PEX can lower measured complement activity; eculizumab directly blocks functional assays).
- Clinical status: resolution of haemorrhagic manifestations, improvement in oedema and reduced transfusion requirements also reflect recovery.

In this patient, platelets rose after PEX and then improved further with eculizumab; durable haematologic remission and partial renal recovery followed complement blockade.

Planning therapy duration and follow‑up
Duration of complement blockade is individualized. Key factors are the presence of a pathogenic complement gene variant, severity of the initial episode, degree of renal recovery, and patient preference. Identification of a pathogenic variant or autoantibody generally favours long‑term therapy because relapse risk is high. Absence of identifiable genetic predisposition (as in this patient) supports a finite treatment course with careful monitoring and consideration of discontinuation after a period of stability; many centres reassess at 3–6 months and thereafter based on renal function and relapse risk. If stopping therapy, patients require close follow‑up with rapid access to care and education about early signs of relapse. Shared decision‑making with nephrology, haematology and infectious disease specialists is essential.

Practical interactions to remember
- Obtain ADAMTS13 and baseline complement samples before PEX/eculizumab when possible because these therapies interfere with testing.
- PEX removes eculizumab; avoid scheduling PEX soon after eculizumab dosing and be prepared to re‑dose if PEX is performed after drug administration.
- Vaccinate and provide antibiotic prophylaxis per timing constraints; counsel patients about persistent infection risk even after vaccination.

Summary as applied to the case
The team prioritised rapid antimicrobial therapy and organ support, used PEX as a bridge while excluding TTP, and timed eculizumab after blood cultures were negative and initial vaccination was given—while providing antibiotic prophylaxis. PEX produced a transient improvement in diuresis, but sustained haematologic remission and partial renal recovery were achieved only after eculizumab. Genetic testing ultimately showed no pathogenic variant, allowing plans for a limited duration of maintenance therapy with close outpatient surveillance. This sequence—control infection, bridge with PEX if needed, mitigate infectious risk, then initiate complement blockade—illustrates the pragmatic balance between treating CM‑HUS effectively and minimising infectious complications.

## Outcomes, prognosis, and clinical takeaways

Outcome in this case
The patient ultimately achieved haematological remission and came off dialysis, with partial recovery of renal function (serum creatinine stabilised at ≈175 µmol/L). Haemolysis and thrombocytopenia resolved after a combination of plasma exchange (temporary stabilisation) and targeted complement blockade with eculizumab; maintenance eculizumab was continued in the months after discharge while renal recovery was monitored. Extended genetic testing did not identify a pathogenic complement variant, and the team planned reassessment of the need for ongoing complement blockade as renal function and clinical course evolve.

Key prognostic factors
Three factors most strongly influence outcome in CM‑HUS and apply directly to this case: baseline and nadir renal function, the requirement for renal replacement therapy, and time to initiation of effective complement blockade. Patients who remain dialysis-dependent and those with more severe acute kidney injury at presentation have a higher risk of incomplete renal recovery and progression to chronic kidney disease. Early recognition of TMA and rapid initiation of eculizumab are associated with better haematological control and improved renal outcomes; delays increase the risk of irreversible renal injury. Finally, the presence of pathogenic complement gene variants or anti‑factor H antibodies increases relapse risk and often affects the expected duration of therapy — information that guides discussions about long‑term treatment, monitoring and counselling. In this patient, the absence of identified pathogenic variants lowers (but does not eliminate) the predicted relapse risk and supports time‑limited therapy with planned reassessment.

Practical diagnostic and management algorithm (operational approach)
When confronted with microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA) plus thrombocytopenia with or without acute kidney injury, begin an expedited, parallel diagnostic and therapeutic pathway. Urgently send ADAMTS13 activity to exclude TTP; while results are pending, assess clinical probability (eg, PLASMIC score) and start plasma exchange immediately if TTP is a real possibility. Simultaneously evaluate for Shiga toxin–producing E. coli (stool PCR) to rule out STEC‑HUS. Basic haemolysis workup (peripheral smear for schistocytes, LDH, haptoglobin, bilirubin, DAT) and blood cultures should be obtained promptly. If ADAMTS13 activity is >10% and STEC testing is negative, consider CM‑HUS as the leading diagnosis. Perform kidney biopsy when it is safe and will meaningfully alter management; biopsy can confirm TMA when the picture is equivocal. For complement testing and genetics, obtain baseline samples before plasma exchange or eculizumab when feasible, because PEX and complement blockade can distort functional assays and component levels. Once active, life‑threatening infection is controlled and blood cultures are negative or improving, start eculizumab without undue delay in clinically appropriate patients; ensure meningococcal vaccination and provide antimicrobial prophylaxis until vaccine efficacy is established.

Common pitfalls and clinical pearls
TMA can be hidden within or temporally follow sepsis‑associated DIC — an early dominant picture of coagulopathy and leukopaenia does not exclude a later superimposed complement‑mediated TMA. Normal serum C3 and C4 do not exclude CM‑HUS; complement component levels may remain within reference ranges even when the alternative pathway is pathogenic. Interpret complement functional assays with caution if samples are drawn after plasma exchange or after the first dose(s) of eculizumab, since both interventions reduce measurable activity and can be mistaken for intrinsic complement deficiency or activation. A positive direct antiglobulin (Coombs) test may be false positive after certain antibiotics (eg, penicillins) or following recent transfusion — repeat testing and correlation with schistocytes, haptoglobin and LDH help distinguish autoimmune haemolysis from MAHA. Plasma exchange can provide transient improvement in CM‑HUS by removing inflammatory mediators and circulating fragments, but it does not correct uncontrolled complement activation; therefore, PEX is useful as a bridge while awaiting definitive diagnosis and eculizumab availability.

Broader clinical implications
This case reinforces the need to recognise uncommon infectious triggers of CM‑HUS, including Capnocytophaga canimorsus after animal bites. In suspected animal‑bite sepsis, early empiric antimicrobial coverage for zoonotic pathogens can be life‑saving and may reduce the infectious burden that acts as the “second hit” for TMA. Because organisms like C. canimorsus are fastidious, use molecular diagnostics (PCR or sequencing) when cultures are delayed or negative but clinical suspicion remains high. Management of suspected CM‑HUS requires multidisciplinary coordination (nephrology, haematology, infectious diseases and pathology) to balance risks of ongoing infection against the benefits of complement blockade, to time diagnostic sampling appropriately, and to individualise duration of therapy based on genetics and clinical recovery.

Takeaway summary
Maintain a high index of suspicion for CM‑HUS in patients who develop new MAHA, thrombocytopenia and worsening AKI during or after sepsis, even when an initial picture of DIC predominates. Expedite ADAMTS13 testing and STEC evaluation, obtain baseline complement studies and genetics before PEX or eculizumab when possible, and initiate eculizumab promptly once active infection is controlled. Early recognition and timely complement blockade are the strongest modifiable factors to improve haematological remission and preserve renal function.
