Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

### [Morning Report] A previously healthy adult presents with sepsis 4 days after a dog bite. What empiric intravenous antibiotic regimen would you start, and which key pathogens associated with dog bites should it cover?

Short answer
For a previously healthy adult presenting with sepsis after a dog bite, I would start empiric broad-spectrum IV beta‑lactam therapy with anaerobic coverage — most commonly piperacillin–tazobactam (or IV ampicillin–sulbactam) — and add anti‑MRSA therapy (vancomycin) if there are risk factors for MRSA or severe purulence. This regimen covers the key dog‑bite pathogens and can be narrowed when culture results return.

Why this choice (clinical reasoning)
- Dog‑bite infections are polymicrobial and often involve oral flora of the animal: you must cover gram‑negative rods (notably Capnocytophaga canimorsus and Pasteurella spp.), aerobic gram‑positives (Streptococcus spp., Staphylococcus aureus), and anaerobes (Prevotella, Porphyromonas, Fusobacterium, etc.).  
- Capnocytophaga canimorsus is a fastidious, encapsulated gram‑negative rod that can cause fulminant sepsis (as in this case). It is generally susceptible to penicillins, third‑generation cephalosporins, carbapenems and beta‑lactam/beta‑lactamase inhibitor combinations.  
- Piperacillin–tazobactam (or ampicillin–sulbactam) provides broad gram‑negative and anaerobic coverage including Pasteurella and Capnocytophaga; it also covers streptococci and many staphylococci (but not MRSA). In the case report the team used piperacillin–tazobactam up front for this reason and later de‑escalated to ampicillin/phenoxymethylpenicillin after C. canimorsus was identified.  
- Add vancomycin if MRSA is a concern (severe soft‑tissue infection, prior MRSA colonization, high local prevalence) or if you need empiric coverage for resistant gram‑positives.

Practical alternatives and special situations
- Immediate penicillin allergy: doxycycline (covers Pasteurella and Capnocytophaga) plus metronidazole or clindamycin for anaerobic coverage; or a fluoroquinolone combined with metronidazole—choose based on allergy severity and local susceptibilities.  
- For outpatient prophylaxis of uncomplicated dog bites, oral amoxicillin–clavulanate is standard.  
- Once blood cultures identify C. canimorsus and susceptibilities are known, de‑escalate to a narrow agent (e.g., IV or oral penicillin/ampicillin or a third‑generation cephalosporin) for an appropriate duration (often at least 10–14 days for bacteraemia; tailor to clinical response).

Key pathogens to cover (dog‑bite empiric coverage)
- Capnocytophaga canimorsus (important, can cause fulminant sepsis)  
- Pasteurella multocida and other Pasteurella spp.  
- Staphylococcus aureus (including consideration of MRSA)  
- Beta‑hemolytic streptococci and viridans streptococci  
- Oral anaerobes of dog oral flora (Prevotella, Porphyromonas, Fusobacterium, Bacteroides spp.)

Operational points
- Obtain blood cultures before antibiotics when feasible, start empiric IV therapy immediately in sepsis.  
- Expect C. canimorsus to be slow and fastidious—molecular diagnostics (if available) can speed identification.  
- Narrow therapy once cultures and susceptibilities are available; monitor clinical response and organ dysfunction (renal failure, DIC, TMA as in the reported case).

### [Differential Diagnosis] An adult with sepsis develops anemia, thrombocytopenia, elevated LDH, undetectable haptoglobin, schistocytes, and acute kidney injury. How would you differentiate DIC, thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), Shiga toxin–producing E. coli HUS (STEC-HUS), and complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS), and what ADAMTS13 activity threshold supports a diagnosis of TTP?

Bottom line
- In an adult with sepsis who later develops MAHA (schistocytes, high LDH, undetectable haptoglobin), thrombocytopenia and AKI you must distinguish: (1) DIC from sepsis, (2) TTP (severe ADAMTS13 deficiency), (3) STEC‑HUS (Shiga toxin), and (4) complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS). The critical laboratory discriminators are standard coagulation tests, ADAMTS13 activity, stool Shiga‑toxin testing, and targeted complement studies/genetics. An ADAMTS13 activity <10% (≤10%) supports TTP.

How to differentiate them — practical approach and physiologic rationale
1) Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC)
- Pathophysiology: systemic activation of coagulation → consumption of platelets and clotting factors, fibrin deposition and secondary fibrinolysis.
- Typical labs: prolonged PT/PTT, markedly elevated D‑dimer/fibrin degradation products, low fibrinogen, thrombocytopenia. Schistocytes may be present but coagulation abnormalities are prominent.
- Clinical context: sepsis, trauma, obstetric complications. Bleeding and microvascular ischemia coexist.
- In the case: early presentation matched DIC (coagulopathy, pancytopenia, mottled extremities). Coagulation parameters later normalized as infection improved, arguing against ongoing DIC as the cause of the later isolated haemolysis.

2) Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP)
- Pathophysiology: severe deficiency of ADAMTS13 (autoantibody or congenital) → accumulation of ultra‑large vWF multimers and platelet microthrombi, especially affecting brain and heart.
- Key discriminator: ADAMTS13 activity severe deficiency (<10%—some use ≤10% or <5%) is diagnostic. PT/PTT usually normal (coagulation cascade not consumed).
- Clinical pattern: prominent neurologic signs are common; renal injury variable (less severe than HUS classically). Rapid response to plasma exchange is expected.
- Practical note: when TTP is strongly suspected clinically, start plasma exchange immediately while awaiting ADAMTS13.
- In the case: ADAMTS13 = 43.5% — well above the TTP threshold — and the PLASMIC score was low, making TTP unlikely.

3) Shiga toxin–producing E. coli HUS (STEC‑HUS)
- Pathophysiology: endothelial injury from Shiga toxin (often following bloody diarrheal illness) → TMA predominantly affecting kidneys.
- Diagnosis: clinical history of prodromal diarrhoea + stool PCR for Shiga toxin (or culture). Complement tests/ADAMTS13 typically not diagnostic.
- Treatment: mainly supportive (dialysis as needed); eculizumab is not standard except in severe refractory cases.
- In the case: no diarrhoeal prodrome and stool Shiga PCR was negative — STEC‑HUS excluded.

4) Complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS; atypical HUS)
- Pathophysiology: dysregulated alternative complement pathway (genetic variants or autoantibodies) → endothelial injury, platelet activation and TMA; often requires a trigger (infection, pregnancy, drugs).
- Diagnostic clues: severe renal involvement, MAHA with schistocytes, thrombocytopenia, ADAMTS13 activity not severely reduced, negative Shiga test, kidney biopsy consistent with TMA. Standard complement levels (C3/C4) may be normal; extended functional assays and genetic testing are supportive but not required for initial diagnosis.
- Treatment: complement blockade (eculizumab) is the specific therapy; plasma exchange may transiently help but does not correct the underlying complement dysregulation.
- In the case: persistent MAHA with schistocytes, ADAMTS13 43.5% (ruling out TTP), negative Shiga toxin, kidney biopsy showing fibrin thrombi and TMA, and clinical response after eculizumab support CM‑HUS. Early infection and initial DIC were the likely triggering “second hit.”

Putting it together — practical diagnostic sequence in a septic patient who develops MAHA/thrombocytopenia/AKI
1. Rapid labs: CBC with smear, LDH, haptoglobin, bilirubin, reticulocyte count, PT/PTT/fibrinogen/D‑dimer. If PT/PTT or fibrinogen abnormal → consider DIC strongly.
2. Send ADAMTS13 activity and inhibitor immediately. If clinical suspicion for TTP is high, start plasma exchange without waiting.
3. Send stool PCR for Shiga toxin if diarrhoeal prodrome or epidemiologic risk.
4. Send complement studies (C3/C4, functional assays) and arrange genetic testing if CM‑HUS is suspected; recognize normal C3/C4 does not exclude CM‑HUS.
5. Consider kidney biopsy when diagnosis remains uncertain or to document TMA.
6. Therapeutic priorities: empiric PEX for suspected TTP; treat sepsis/DIC aggressively; consider eculizumab for CM‑HUS once active uncontrolled bacteraemia is controlled (vaccination and prophylaxis as appropriate).

Key teaching points illustrated by the case
- Sepsis/DIC can precede or mask a superimposed TMA. Normalization of coagulation tests with new isolated MAHA and persistent thrombocytopenia should prompt reassessment.
- ADAMTS13 activity <10% supports TTP; partial reductions (eg, 40%) occur in sepsis and other TMAs and do not indicate TTP.
- CM‑HUS is a diagnosis of exclusion; complement levels may be normal and genetic testing can be negative — clinical context, biopsy and response to eculizumab are important.
- In infection‑triggered CM‑HUS, delay eculizumab until blood cultures are controlled if possible and provide appropriate vaccination and antibiotic prophylaxis.

Answer to the explicit threshold question
- An ADAMTS13 activity <10% (commonly ≤10%) supports a diagnosis of TTP.

### [Management] In a patient with suspected TMA and microangiopathic hemolytic anemia while awaiting ADAMTS13 testing, what immediate management steps should be taken, and which blood product is generally avoided unless there is life-threatening bleeding?

Immediate steps when you suspect a thrombotic microangiopathy with microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA) while ADAMTS13 is pending

1) Rapid diagnostic triage
- Send urgent tests: ADAMTS13 activity (stat), peripheral blood smear for schistocytes, CBC, reticulocyte count, LDH, haptoglobin, bilirubin, direct antiglobulin (Coombs) test, coagulation panel (PT/aPTT, fibrinogen, D‑dimer) and STEC/shiga toxin testing as indicated. These help distinguish TTP, DIC and STEC‑HUS.  
- In the reported case the peripheral smear (15–20% schistocytes) and rising LDH/haptoglobin nadir pointed to MAHA; ADAMTS13 was sent immediately.

2) Don’t wait for ADAMTS13 to treat suspected TTP
- If clinical suspicion for TTP is moderate–high (eg, neurologic signs, severe thrombocytopenia, MAHA, high PLASMIC score) start plasma exchange (PEX) urgently—ideally within hours—because severe ADAMTS13 deficiency (<10%) is a medical emergency. PEX is lifesaving in TTP.  
- In the case PEX was started empirically as a bridging therapy while ADAMTS13 results were pending.

3) Concurrent empiric therapies
- Give high‑dose corticosteroids when immune TTP is suspected. Consider caplacizumab if available and TTP is likely while awaiting confirmatory testing.  
- Supportive care: transfuse packed red blood cells for symptomatic anemia or hemodynamic instability; treat the underlying trigger (eg, antibiotics for sepsis); provide renal support/dialysis for severe AKI as needed (this patient required continuous renal replacement therapy).

4) Use PEX as a bridge in uncertain cases of TMA
- PEX may transiently help non‑TTP TMAs and is frequently used while the diagnosis is clarified. That is what was done here prior to starting eculizumab for suspected complement‑mediated HUS.

5) If complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS) is diagnosed or strongly suspected
- Arrange for eculizumab early after balancing infectious risk. In active, uncontrolled bacteraemia delay complement blockade if possible until cultures are controlled and meningococcal vaccination / antimicrobial prophylaxis are in place. In this case eculizumab was started after blood cultures became negative and after initial vaccination and prophylaxis planning.

Which blood product is generally avoided
- Platelet transfusion. Platelet transfusions are generally avoided in TMA/TTP because they can exacerbate microvascular thrombosis and worsen ischemic organ injury. Give platelets only for life‑threatening bleeding, before an urgent invasive procedure, or rarely when bleeding cannot be controlled—ideally coordinated with PEX and hematology.  
- In the case report platelets were managed cautiously; earlier thrombocyte/fibrinogen support had been given during overt DIC, but routine platelet transfusion in the setting of evolving TMA was avoided unless bleeding dictated otherwise.

Bottom line: while awaiting ADAMTS13, urgently send diagnostic tests, initiate empiric PEX and steroids if TTP cannot be excluded, provide supportive care (RBC transfusion, dialysis, treat infection), and avoid platelet transfusion except for life‑threatening bleeding.

### [Pathophysiology] Explain how dysregulation of the alternative complement pathway causes endothelial injury and thrombotic microangiopathy in CM-HUS, and why intercurrent infections can trigger disease even when no pathogenic complement gene variant is identified.

Clinical answer — how alternative‑pathway dysregulation produces endothelial injury and TMA, and why infection can trigger disease even when no pathogenic complement gene is found

What the alternative pathway normally does
- The alternative complement pathway is constitutively “on” at a low level by spontaneous hydrolysis of C3 (“tick‑over”). Any deposited C3b can bind factor B, be cleaved by factor D to form the C3 convertase (C3bBb), and thereby amplify generation of more C3b. This amplification loop is powerful and is held in check on host surfaces by regulatory proteins (factor H, factor I, membrane cofactor protein/CD46, thrombomodulin, etc.).

How dysregulation injures endothelium and produces TMA
- Loss of adequate regulation — whether genetic (mutations in factor H, I, B, CD46, thrombomodulin, gain‑of‑function in C3/B) or acquired — allows unchecked amplification of C3b on host endothelial surfaces. Consequences are:
  - Generation of large amounts of C3a/C5a (anaphylatoxins) that recruit and activate neutrophils and monocytes and promote a proinflammatory, procoagulant endothelial phenotype.
  - Formation of the terminal membrane attack complex (C5b‑9) on endothelial cells, causing direct cell injury or sublethal activation.
  - Endothelial activation leads to Weibel‑Palade body release (von Willebrand factor multimers), upregulation of tissue factor and adhesion molecules, and shedding of thromboregulatory surface proteins.
  - The net effect is localized microvascular thrombosis: platelet aggregation on damaged endothelium, formation of fibrin‑rich thrombi in arterioles and glomerular capillaries, mechanical fragmentation of red cells (schistocytes), consumption thrombocytopenia, microangiopathic hemolysis (high LDH, low haptoglobin), and end‑organ ischemia — classically the kidneys in HUS.

How this explains the case
- In the reported patient the sequence fits this pathophysiology: severe C. canimorsus sepsis produced a big complement stimulus and endothelial injury. After the septic parameters and DIC settled, a new picture of MAHA, persistent thrombocytopenia, schistocytes and renal TMA on biopsy emerged — the clinical signature of complement‑mediated endothelial injury and microthrombosis. The patient’s rapid improvement only after complement blockade (eculizumab) further supports complement‑driven endothelial injury as the dominant mechanism.

Why infection can trigger CM‑HUS even when no pathogenic gene variant is found
- “Two‑hit” biology with variable penetrance: many patients who develop CM‑HUS have identifiable complement variants or autoantibodies, but not all. Reasons an infection can precipitate CM‑HUS without a known pathogenic variant:
  - A sufficiently strong complement stimulus (severe sepsis, encapsulated organisms) can overwhelm normal regulators transiently — producing clinical disease without a fixed genetic defect.
  - Patients may carry low‑penetrance risk polymorphisms or noncoding/regulatory variants not detected by routine testing; these increase susceptibility but are not labelled “pathogenic.”
  - Acquired, transient defects occur (consumption of regulators during sepsis, transient anti‑factor H antibodies, or complement‑evading bacterial interactions that dysregulate host control).
  - Timing of testing matters: routine complement levels (C3/C4) often remain normal and functional assays can be altered by prior plasma exchange or eculizumab, so a normal genetic or complement panel does not exclude an infection‑triggered, complement‑mediated process.
- In this case C. canimorsus is known to interact with complement (some strains resist complement), and the patient had fulminant sepsis that could provide the necessary “second hit.” Extended genetic testing found no pathogenic variant, but that does not contradict a complement‑driven TMA precipitated by infection.

Clinical implications (brief)
- Maintain a high index of suspicion for CM‑HUS in patients with new MAHA, thrombocytopenia and renal failure after infection, even if standard complement levels and genetic testing are unrevealing.
- Early recognition and targeted therapy (C5 blockade) can halt endothelial injury and improve outcome; timing and exclusion/management of active infection are critical because terminal complement blockade increases risk for encapsulated organisms.

### [Clinical Pearls] A patient with new hemolysis has a positive direct antiglobulin (Coombs) test after recent beta-lactam exposure and transfusion. What mechanisms can cause a false-positive DAT in this setting, and which laboratory and smear features help distinguish autoimmune hemolytic anemia from microangiopathic hemolytic anemia?

Direct answer
- False‑positive DAT after recent beta‑lactam exposure and transfusion can be caused by: (1) drug‑dependent antibodies (hapten-type or immune‑complex) that coat RBCs transiently, (2) passive transfer or nonspecific coating of donor immunoglobulins/plasma proteins after transfusion or plasma products, and (3) complement deposition on RBCs from systemic complement activation during sepsis/DIC/TMA.  
- To distinguish autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA) from microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA/TMA) use the peripheral smear and targeted laboratory testing: AIHA → spherocytes, positive DAT (monospecific IgG ± C3), brisk reticulocytosis; MAHA → schistocytes/helmet cells (fragmentation), thrombocytopenia, negative or transient DAT, evidence of end‑organ microthrombosis (renal injury), and supportive tests (ADAMTS13, coagulation profile, kidney biopsy).

Why these mechanisms produce a false‑positive DAT (pathophysiology and examples)
- Hapten (penicillin‑type) mechanism: some beta‑lactams (penicillins, cephalosporins) adsorb to the RBC membrane and elicit anti‑drug IgG that binds only in the presence of drug; routine DAT can be positive for IgG even if clinical hemolysis will stop when the drug is stopped.  
- Immune‑complex / complement fixation: other drugs form immune complexes that fix complement onto RBCs (DAT positive for C3).  
- True drug‑induced autoantibody: certain agents can induce autoantibodies that behave like warm AIHA (persistent DAT).  
- Passive transfer or nonspecific coating after transfusion/plasma products: transfused plasma (or donor antibodies) or Octaplas used during PEX can transiently coat recipient RBCs with IgG or complement, yielding a positive DAT without intrinsic autoimmune hemolysis.  
- Complement deposition from sepsis/TMA: systemic complement activation (as in severe infection or TMA) can deposit C3d on RBCs → DAT positivity even when mechanical fragmentation (MAHA) is the dominant cause of hemolysis.

Practical laboratory and smear distinctions (what to order and what to look for)
- Peripheral smear:
  - AIHA (warm): spherocytes, marked polychromasia, few schistocytes.
  - Cold AIHA: red cell agglutinates, often RBC clumping.
  - MAHA/TMA: increased schistocytes/helmet cells and very few spherocytes; schistocyte proportion >1% (and especially marked percentages like 15–20%) is strongly supportive of fragmentation hemolysis.  
- DAT testing:
  - Use monospecific reagents (anti‑IgG and anti‑C3d) and compare pre‑ and post‑transfusion samples if available.
  - Perform eluate studies if IgG positive to characterise antibody specificity (drug‑dependent vs autoantibody).
  - If recent transfusion/plasma, repeat DAT after washing or after stopping the suspect product; drug‑induced DATs often disappear after stopping the drug.  
- Hemolysis markers (nonspecific):
  - Both AIHA and MAHA: elevated LDH, indirect bilirubin, low haptoglobin.
  - Reticulocyte count: typically brisk in AIHA unless marrow suppressed; may be blunted in sepsis or early MAHA.  
- Platelets and coagulation:
  - Thrombocytopenia out of proportion and evidence of microthrombi (AKI, neurologic signs) point to MAHA/TMA rather than isolated AIHA. Coagulation tests may help distinguish DIC.  
- ADAMTS13 activity:
  - Severely low (<10%) → TTP; normal/moderately reduced argues against TTP and supports other TMAs or secondary MAHA.  
- Tissue/organ evaluation:
  - Organ biopsy (renal biopsy showing fibrin thrombi/capillary injury) confirms TMA when clinical context fits.

Applying this to the presented case (clinical reasoning)
- The patient had recent exposure to beta‑lactams (piperacillin/tazobactam, later ampicillin/phenoxymethylpenicillin) and received blood products/Octaplas. An initial DAT was IgG‑positive, prompting empiric steroids. However:
  - She failed to improve with steroids, the repeat DAT became negative (arguing against a persistent warm AIHA), and the peripheral smear showed 15–20% schistocytes — a pattern diagnostic for MAHA/TMA.  
  - ADAMTS13 activity (43.5%) made TTP unlikely, and kidney biopsy showed fibrin thrombi and ischemic glomeruli consistent with TMA/CM‑HUS.  
  - Together these findings favor a false‑positive DAT (likely drug‑ or transfusion‑related or complement coating during sepsis) superimposed on true microangiopathic hemolysis, rather than primary AIHA.

Bottom line (clinical pearl)
- A positive DAT in a patient with recent beta‑lactam exposure or transfusion can be drug‑ or transfusion‑related or reflect nonspecific complement/Ig coating; it does not by itself prove autoimmune hemolysis. Always integrate smear morphology (spherocytes vs schistocytes), reticulocyte response, platelet count/coagulation tests, ADAMTS13, and organ findings (renal biopsy if indicated) to distinguish AIHA from MAHA/TMA. In the case discussed, the combination of transient DAT positivity, steroid nonresponse, marked schistocytosis, thrombocytopenia and biopsy‑proven fibrin thrombi pointed to MAHA from complement‑mediated TMA rather than true AIHA.

### [Management] When initiating eculizumab for CM-HUS in the setting of recent bacteremia, what infection-prevention measures are required or recommended before and during therapy (including vaccines and antimicrobial prophylaxis), and how should therapy be timed relative to ongoing infection?

Short answer
- Before starting eculizumab you must mitigate the markedly increased risk of invasive infection with encapsulated organisms—most importantly Neisseria meningitidis—by giving meningococcal vaccination and providing antimicrobial cover until vaccine-induced protection is achieved. 
- Ideally wait until the triggering bacteremia is controlled (blood cultures negative and patient clinically improving). If eculizumab cannot be safely delayed because of life‑threatening CM‑HUS, give vaccines immediately and start effective antibiotic prophylaxis and full antimicrobial therapy; proceed with extreme caution and close infectious‑disease follow‑up.

What to do (practical steps, with the approach used in the case)
1) Meningococcal vaccination (mandatory in routine practice)
   - Give both a meningococcal conjugate ACWY vaccine (eg, Menveo) and a serogroup B vaccine (eg, Bexsero). In the reported case the team gave Menveo + Bexsero before eculizumab induction and planned the booster/second doses afterwards.
   - Timing: when possible vaccinate ≥2 weeks before the first dose of eculizumab to allow protective immunity. If treatment is urgent you still give the vaccines immediately but treat as “unprotected” for the short term.

2) Antimicrobial prophylaxis until vaccine protection
   - If vaccines were not given ≥2 weeks beforehand (as in this case) give antibiotic prophylaxis and continue until 2 weeks after vaccination (or longer if indicated). The case team used oral phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) because the vaccines had not been given 2 weeks prior.
   - Typical regimens used in practice: oral penicillin (eg, phenoxymethylpenicillin or amoxicillin). For penicillin allergy consider ciprofloxacin (single‑agent) or azithromycin depending on local guidance and susceptibility. Local prophylaxis choices should be informed by allergy history and local epidemiology.

3) Additional vaccines to consider
   - Routine eculizumab guidance focuses on meningococcal vaccines. Many centers also ensure up‑to‑date pneumococcal and Haemophilus influenzae type b (Hib) immunizations (especially in patients with other risk factors such as asplenia). In the presented case the focus was on meningococcal vaccination because of the specific risk of Neisseria and the immediate need for complement blockade.

4) Timing relative to ongoing infection (principle and how it applied in the case)
   - Principle: terminal complement is important for host defence against encapsulated and certain gram‑negative organisms (including Capnocytophaga). Therefore, avoid initiating terminal complement blockade during uncontrolled, ongoing bacteremia if possible.
   - Application in this case: the team waited until blood cultures were negative and the septic process was controlled, administered the meningococcal vaccines, provided antibiotic prophylaxis (phenoxymethylpenicillin because vaccines were given less than 2 weeks prior), and then started eculizumab. This balanced the need to clear infection with the need to rapidly treat CM‑HUS to preserve renal function.

5) Other important measures while on eculizumab
   - Educate the patient about urgent evaluation for fever, headache, neck stiffness or other signs of invasive infection; have a low threshold for empiric antibiotics and hospital assessment.
   - Maintain close infectious‑disease follow‑up and microbiological surveillance. Repeat blood cultures or other targeted testing if concern for recurrent infection.
   - Consider longer term antibiotic prophylaxis in selected high‑risk situations or until vaccination/infection clearance is certain; individualize duration and agent.
   - Document vaccination status clearly and ensure timely booster doses per vaccine schedule (the team planned the second vaccine doses 8 weeks after the first).

Why this matters (brief pathophysiology)
- Eculizumab blocks terminal complement (C5 → MAC formation), preventing complement‑mediated endothelial injury in CM‑HUS but also impairing killing/opsonization of capsule‑bearing bacteria (notably N. meningitidis and other encapsulated organisms). That is why meningococcal immunization and antimicrobial prophylaxis are essential and why initiation during active bacteremia is avoided unless the severity of TMA mandates immediate therapy.

Bottom line
- Give MenACWY + MenB vaccines before eculizumab when possible; if urgent treatment is required give vaccines immediately and continue effective antibiotic prophylaxis until 2 weeks after vaccination (or longer if infection control is uncertain). Prefer to start eculizumab after the causative bacteremia is cleared; if you must start earlier, ensure appropriate antimicrobial therapy and close monitoring. The management in the presented case—vaccination, prophylactic phenoxymethylpenicillin and initiation of eculizumab only after cultures were negative—illustrates this balanced, safety‑focused approach.

### [Clinical Pearls] Why do normal C3 and C4 levels not exclude CM-HUS, and what is the role and limitation of functional complement assays (e.g., AH50) in diagnosis and monitoring, particularly after plasma exchange or eculizumab?

Short answer
Normal C3 and C4 do not exclude complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS) because systemic C3/C4 concentrations are insensitive to the focal, amplification‑loop–driven endothelial complement activation that causes TMA. Functional assays such as AH50 (alternative‑pathway hemolytic activity) can be useful — but their results are strongly influenced by recent plasma exchange (PEX) and by C5 blockade with eculizumab and therefore must be interpreted in the clinical context and timed appropriately.

Why C3/C4 may be normal in CM‑HUS
- Pathophysiology: CM‑HUS is driven by dysregulation of the alternative pathway at the endothelial surface (local C3 convertase formation and amplification). This produces local injury without necessarily causing large‑scale systemic consumption of C3 or C4 measurable in serum.  
- Sensitivity: Only a minority of patients with complement genetic defects show low serum C3 at presentation; C4 is usually normal because the classical and lectin pathways are not the primary drivers. Thus normal C3/C4 has poor negative predictive value and cannot be used to rule out CM‑HUS. The case illustrates this: the patient had normal C3/C4 early despite biopsy‑proven TMA and later clinical response to eculizumab consistent with complement‑mediated disease.

Role and limitations of functional assays (AH50, CH50)
- What AH50 measures: AH50 quantifies the ability of patient serum to mediate alternative‑pathway–dependent lysis (it depends on intact factors of the AP and an intact terminal pathway C5–C9 to produce hemolysis).  
- Diagnostic utility: A reduced AH50 may reflect ongoing consumption of AP components or inherited deficiency of AP proteins; but a normal AH50 does not exclude AP dysregulation at the endothelial surface. Functional assays are more informative when obtained before therapeutic interventions.  
- Effect of eculizumab: Eculizumab binds C5 and prevents formation of the membrane attack complex; because AH50 requires an intact terminal pathway, therapeutic C5 blockade will lower/abolish AH50 regardless of upstream disease activity. Thus a low AH50 after eculizumab usually reflects pharmacodynamic blockade (desired effect), not necessarily complement consumption from disease. In the presented case the markedly reduced AH50 (6%) was measured 4 days after the first eculizumab dose and is most consistent with drug effect.  
- Effect of plasma exchange: PEX alters assay results in several ways — it removes circulating complement proteins and antibodies, and infusion of donor plasma (or Octaplas) can either replenish or transiently change complement activity. Timing matters: assays drawn immediately after PEX may be low (dilution/consumption) or, if donor plasma contains active complement, may be spuriously normal. In this patient, extended testing was performed 8 days after the last PEX but after eculizumab had already been given; therefore the depressed AH50 is best explained by treatment rather than native complement status.

Practical clinical pearls
- If you suspect CM‑HUS, obtain baseline studies before PEX or complement blockade whenever possible: C3/C4, factor H/I/B levels, anti‑factor H antibodies, AH50/CH50 (if available), and specimens for genetic testing. These baseline data are far more interpretable than samples drawn after intervention.  
- Recognize what post‑treatment results tell you: a suppressed AH50 after eculizumab confirms pharmacologic terminal complement blockade (useful to document drug effect) but cannot be used to judge intrinsic disease activity while on therapy. PEX performed while on eculizumab will remove the drug and can restore AH50, so additional eculizumab dosing/re‑loading may be required.  
- Use clinical and routine hematologic/renal markers to diagnose and monitor CM‑HUS: MAHA with schistocytes, rising LDH, falling haptoglobin, thrombocytopenia, worsening creatinine, biopsy if needed — these remain cornerstone data. Complement functional and molecular tests are adjunctive and must be interpreted in light of timing, prior PEX, and prior complement inhibition.  
- When available, complement activation fragments (e.g., plasma sC5b‑9, C3a, C5a) are more direct markers of activation but are also transient and treatment sensitive and are not widely available.

Bottom line
Normal C3/C4 does not rule out CM‑HUS because the disease often reflects localized amplification of the alternative pathway rather than wholesale systemic complement depletion. Functional assays like AH50 are helpful if drawn before PEX or eculizumab; after eculizumab a low AH50 is expected and reflects drug effect rather than proving or excluding ongoing disease. Interpretation of any complement test must always be anchored to timing of therapies (PEX or complement inhibitors) and to the clinical course — as the case clearly demonstrates.

### [Morning Report] On kidney biopsy, what histopathologic features support a diagnosis of thrombotic microangiopathy, and how do immunofluorescence and electron microscopy help exclude immune complex–mediated glomerulonephritides?

Direct answer
- Light microscopy features that support thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA): platelet–fibrin thrombi occluding glomerular capillaries and arterioles, endothelial swelling and detachment, mesangiolysis, ischemic (collapsed) glomeruli, focal necrosis and sometimes crescent formation, and acute tubular necrosis from downstream ischemia. Chronic TMA changes (double contours of the GBM, intimal mucoid change or “onion‑skin” arteriosclerosis) may be present if older injury exists.  
- Immunofluorescence (IF) and electron microscopy (EM) are used to exclude immune‑complex glomerulonephritides: immune complex GN shows granular (or linear, depending on disease) deposits of immunoglobulin and complement on IF and electron‑dense immune complexes in predictable locations on EM (mesangial, subendothelial, subepithelial). In contrast, TMA typically shows absent or only nonspecific trapping of Ig/ C3 on IF and no discrete electron‑dense immune complexes on EM; EM instead shows endothelial cell swelling, subendothelial widening with flocculent or fibrinous material and occasional fibrin tactoids consistent with thrombus.

Clinical reasoning tied to the case
- In this patient the renal biopsy by light microscopy showed severe ATN, ischaemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi and scattered crescents — a pattern characteristic of acute TMA: microvascular endothelial injury with intraluminal platelet–fibrin thrombi producing ischemia and tubular injury.  
- IF revealed no immune‑complex deposition and EM showed no evidence of a primary immune‑complex glomerulopathy. Those negative IF/EM findings effectively exclude immune complex–mediated GN (for example post‑infectious GN, membranoproliferative GN, lupus nephritis), supporting a diagnosis of complement‑mediated TMA rather than an IC‑GN.  
- Practically, the combination of typical light‑microscopic TMA findings plus negative IF/EM directed management away from therapies targeted to immune‑complex disease and toward therapies for TMA (plasma exchange as a bridge and complement blockade with eculizumab), which is what aided the patient’s recovery.

### [Clinical Pearls] After a dog bite, which clinical and laboratory features should raise suspicion for Capnocytophaga canimorsus bacteremia, and why is diagnostic confirmation often delayed?

Key clinical clue: any history of a dog or cat bite, scratch, or close contact in a patient who develops a rapid, fulminant systemic infection should immediately raise suspicion for Capnocytophaga canimorsus. In the case presented the patient was bitten on the lip 4 days before presentation and then developed very high fever, refractory hypoglycaemia, lactic acidosis, rapidly progressive sepsis with mottled/cool extremities, multiorgan failure and acute kidney injury—an archetypal severe C. canimorsus syndrome.

Which clinical and laboratory features should make you think specifically of C. canimorsus?
- Recent dog/cat exposure within days. This is the single most important trigger to consider the organism.
- Rapidly progressive sepsis with signs of fulminant disease (high fever, lactic acidosis, shock or marked tissue hypoperfusion, DIC). The patient developed DIC and peripheral ischaemia early.
- Marked cytopenias, especially severe leukopenia or neutropenia and thrombocytopenia; pancytopenia was prominent in this case.
- Early coagulopathy (prolonged PT/aPTT, low fibrinogen) and clinical microthrombotic phenomena (ecchymoses, distal cyanosis) consistent with DIC.
- Refractory hypoglycaemia and high lactate—features reported with fulminant Capnocytophaga sepsis and present in this patient.
- Rapid progression to multiorgan dysfunction including AKI; later development of haemolysis/MAHA, schistocytes and thrombocytopenia may reflect superimposed TMA (as occurred here).
- Gram‑negative bacilli on blood culture or Gram stain can be a clue, though organisms are often scant and fusiform.

Why confirmation is often delayed
- Capnocytophaga canimorsus is fastidious and capnophilic (prefers CO2), encapsulated, and slow‑growing. Standard blood cultures can take several days to become positive (7 days in this report), and growth may be scant.
- Prior or empiric antibiotics can suppress growth and further delay culture recovery.
- Identification may require prolonged incubation, enriched media, or advanced techniques (MALDI‑TOF, 16S PCR or real‑time nanopore sequencing), resources that are not universally or immediately available.
- Early clinical presentation mimics more common causes of sepsis/DIC, so the diagnosis is easily missed unless exposure history is sought and laboratories are instructed to extend incubation/perform specific identification methods.

Clinical bottom line (practical pearl)
Always elicit animal‑exposure history in sepsis. When a dog/cat bite precedes rapid deterioration with cytopenias, coagulopathy, refractory hypoglycaemia or peripheral ischaemia, start empiric therapy that covers Capnocytophaga (eg, beta‑lactam with beta‑lactamase inhibitor or piperacillin‑tazobactam) and notify the microbiology lab to prolong incubation and consider molecular diagnostics—because culture confirmation is characteristically delayed and may not be reliable if empiric antibiotics have already been given.

### [Management] In infection-triggered CM-HUS with partial renal recovery and negative complement genetic testing, how would you approach the duration of eculizumab therapy and outpatient monitoring for relapse and infectious complications?

Bottom line
- In infection‑triggered CM‑HUS with a clear precipitant, no pathogenic complement variants, and partial but sustained renal recovery, it is reasonable to plan a time‑limited course of eculizumab (commonly 4–6 months) with careful stepwise reassessment and close outpatient surveillance for relapse and infectious complications. The decision must be individualized according to initial disease severity, recovery trajectory and patient preference.

Why
- CM‑HUS is a complement‑mediated endothelial injury syndrome that often requires a “second‑hit” (infection, pregnancy, drugs) in genetically predisposed patients. Identification of a discrete trigger (here Capnocytophaga canimorsus) and negative extended complement genetics both lower—though do not eliminate—the risk of relapse. By contrast, pathogenic variants (or anti‑factor H antibodies) increase relapse risk and usually favour prolonged or indefinite therapy.
- This patient had fulminant, dialysis‑dependent TMA but subsequently achieved haematologic remission and partial renal recovery (creatinine ≈175 µmol/L) after PEX and eculizumab. The treating team elected maintenance eculizumab (1200 mg q2w) for 4 months with planned reassessment—an approach consistent with balancing the benefit of preventing relapse against infectious risks of terminal complement blockade.

Practical approach to duration
- Initial course: continue induction and maintenance until sustained haematologic remission (normalizing hemoglobin, platelets, LDH, absent schistocytes) and a stable or improving creatinine for a period (commonly several weeks).
- Planned trial discontinuation: in patients with a clear infectious trigger and no pathogenic complement variants, many centres treat for 4–6 months and then stop eculizumab if remission is stable. The 4‑month course used in this case is reasonable given the severity of presentation but good recovery.
- Longer therapy: consider longer or indefinite treatment if there is incomplete recovery, recurrent relapses, or identification of pathogenic complement variants or anti‑factor H antibodies.

How to monitor outpatient for relapse
- Educate the patient to seek urgent care for fever, new purpura/petechiae, sudden fatigue/jaundice, dark urine, decreased urine output, or unexplained bruising.
- Active laboratory surveillance after stopping eculizumab (example schedule that can be adapted):
  - First 4 weeks: twice weekly CBC, creatinine, LDH, haptoglobin and urinalysis (or at least 2×/week in the first 2 weeks then weekly).
  - Weeks 5–12: weekly labs.
  - Months 4–12: every 2–4 weeks, then at clinician discretion up to 24 months.
  - At any time: peripheral smear for schistocytes if hemolysis suspected.
- While on maintenance infusions, many centres check CBC, creatinine and LDH immediately before each infusion initially (q2 weeks), then spacing to monthly once stable.
- Trigger thresholds for rapid re‑evaluation/restart of therapy: new thrombocytopenia (fall >25% or absolute <150×10^9/L depending on baseline), new or worsening MAHA (rising LDH, dropping haptoglobin, schistocytes), or new acute kidney injury—these warrant urgent hematology/nephrology review and immediate consideration of re‑initiating eculizumab.

Infectious risk mitigation (specific to this case and general principles)
- Vaccination: ensure meningococcal vaccination (MenACWY and MenB) before starting eculizumab when possible. In this patient the first doses were given before eculizumab and second doses were planned; that is appropriate.
- Antibiotic prophylaxis: give short‑term oral penicillin or alternative until 2 weeks after vaccination if therapy cannot be delayed (as here they used Apocillin). Most centres continue prophylactic antibiotics for the duration of eculizumab; discuss local resistance patterns and patient allergies. Maintain low threshold for empirical antibiotics for febrile illness.
- Patient education and documentation: provide written alert card advising that the patient is complement‑inhibited and at increased risk for invasive encapsulated organisms (meningococcus). Advise immediate medical attention for fever, headache, neck stiffness, rash, or sepsis signs.
- Specific to Capnocytophaga: ongoing vigilance for animal exposures; counsel on prompt wound cleaning and early empirical antibiotics for future bites.

When to restart complement blockade
- Restart promptly if objective evidence of recurrent TMA emerges (MAHA, thrombocytopenia, kidney injury) rather than waiting for genetic data. In centres where eculizumab is not immediately available, urgent PEX can be used as a bridge but is not definitive therapy for CM‑HUS.

Final comment tied to the case
- Given this patient’s severity but clear infectious trigger and negative genetic testing, the team’s decision to continue eculizumab for a finite period (4 months) with planned reassessment was appropriate. The key next steps are structured outpatient lab surveillance as outlined, continued meningococcal vaccination completion and antibiotic prophylaxis while on complement blockade, patient education for early detection of relapse or invasive infection, and a low threshold to reinitiate eculizumab if relapse is suspected.
