Title: Blog about the case report: Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

# Pancytopenia Algorithm 2.0: Add Telomere Biology to Your Adult Workup

What’s new and surprising?
A woman in her late 20s with five years of progressive fatigue and pancytopenia turned out not to have classic aplastic anaemia, autoimmune cytopenia or an occult malignancy — she had a telomere biology disorder (TBD) driven by a monoallelic PARN variant. The case is a useful reminder: in adults with unexplained cytopenia, especially when the picture includes premature ageing features or organ fibrosis, the usual “nutrition → infection → autoimmune → malignancy → classic marrow failure” algorithm can miss heritable telomere disorders that change management, transplant planning and family counselling.

Why this matters at the bedside
TBDs don’t always look like the textbook dyskeratosis congenita triad. Adults can present with isolated cytopenias, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), cryptogenic hepatic fibrosis or combinations of these. Standard labs (bone marrow appearance, normal cytogenetics) can be unhelpful. Mistaking a TBD for acquired aplasia or idiopathic IPF risks inappropriate donor selection for hematopoietic stem cell transplant, missed surveillance for progressive organ fibrosis or cancers, and lost opportunities for family testing.

A real case highlights the pitfalls
- Late-20s woman with pancytopenia, very low reticulocyte count and bone marrow showing erythroid hyperplasia without dysplasia.  
- Severe B12 deficiency was present and treated, but cytopenias and multisystem disease were disproportionate to nutrition alone.  
- Premature greying, osteoporosis, neuropathy, hepatosplenomegaly with cirrhosis on elastography and bilateral pulmonary fibrosis on CT.  
- Clinical exome sequencing found a monoallelic missense PARN variant (p.Cys205Arg), classified as VUS — but the phenotype fit a TBD spectrum.  
This combination — young adult, low retics, marrow not showing classic aplasia/dysplasia, premature ageing signs and fibrotic organs — should raise the telomere flag.

Practical “Algorithm 2.0” — when to escalate to telomere testing
If you’ve worked through the usual causes and you have one or more of the following in an adult with unexplained cytopenia, add telomere evaluation (telomere length ± TBD gene panel):

- Age <50 with cytopenia(s) without an alternative explanation.  
- Low reticulocyte response out of proportion to peripheral findings (ineffective erythropoiesis or marrow exhaustion).  
- Features of premature ageing: premature greying/alopecia, nail dystrophy, skin changes, early osteoporosis.  
- Fibrotic organ disease (idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, cryptogenic hepatic fibrosis) especially when occurring in a young adult or with cytopenia.  
- Multisystem involvement (bone marrow + lung ± liver ± GI ± endocrine/neurology) without clear acquired cause.  
- Family history of early lung disease, unexplained liver disease, bone marrow failure or similar premature ageing signs.

What to order and how to interpret
- Telomere length testing — flow-FISH is the gold standard in clinical testing for leukocyte telomere length. qPCR labs vary and are less reliable for individual clinical decisions. Short telomeres (age-adjusted) support a TBD diagnosis but require context.  
- Genetic testing — use a comprehensive TBD panel (TERT, TERC, RTEL1, PARN, DKC1, TINF2, NAF1, etc.) or clinical exome/genome sequencing. Beware: many variants will be VUS — correlate genetics with phenotype and telomere length.  
- Bone marrow biopsy/cytogenetics — still essential to exclude MDS, leukemia or other infiltrative processes; but a normal cytogenetic result does not exclude TBD.  
- Routine workup to exclude reversible causes (nutrition, infections, autoimmune serologies) should continue in parallel.

Clinical implications — why it changes management
- Transplant planning: reduced-intensity regimens and careful donor selection are critical. Using an affected related donor can be catastrophic.  
- Surveillance: higher risk for progressive pulmonary and hepatic fibrosis, cancers (especially mucocutaneous and hematologic), and bone disease — requires longitudinal follow-up.  
- Family counselling: identification of a pathogenic variant leads to cascade testing and reproductive counselling.  
- Therapies: androgens (danazol, nandrolone) have shown telomere-elongation signals in small studies; hematopoietic stem cell transplant can be curative for marrow failure but carries organ-specific risks in TBD patients. Decisions are individualized and best made in specialized centres.

Clinical pearls
- Don’t be reassured by a finding that “explains” part of the picture (e.g., severe B12 deficiency); ask if the extent of multisystem disease fits the single cause.  
- A very low reticulocyte count with marrow erythroid activity suggests ineffective erythropoiesis or stem cell dysfunction — not just peripheral loss.  
- Premature greying, osteoporosis and cryptogenic cirrhosis in a young adult are red flags for a telomere syndrome when paired with cytopenia.  
- Monoallelic variants (e.g., in PARN) can present with fibrosis and marrow vulnerability — penetrance is variable. A VUS in the right phenotype should still trigger downstream steps (telomere length testing, family assessment, surveillance).  
- Ask transplant teams for input early; conditioning regimens and donor selection must account for TBD physiology.

Take-home messages
- Add telomere biology disorders to your differential for adults with unexplained cytopenia, especially when young age, low retics, premature ageing signs or organ fibrosis are present.  
- Get telomere length testing (flow-FISH preferred) and a targeted TBD gene panel when suspicion is high — genetic results must be interpreted in the clinical context.  
- A diagnosis of TBD changes transplant strategy, prompts family testing and mandates ongoing surveillance for progressive lung, liver and marrow disease.  
- When in doubt, refer early to a tertiary centre with expertise in telomere disorders; these diagnoses have outsized downstream implications.

If you see a young adult with pancytopenia plus weird extras — premature greying, cirrhosis, IPF or unexplained osteoporosis — put “telomere” back on your checklist before you close the case.
