Title: Blog about the case report: Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

# Treating the Untreatable? Androgens, HSCT, and Management Landmines in Telomere Disorders

What makes telomere biology disorders (TBDs) so maddening is their protean, delayed, multisystem presentation and the narrow therapeutic window. The recent case of a woman in her late 20s with pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis carrying a monoallelic PARN variant is a perfect example: a young patient with evidence of organ fibrosis (liver, lung), marrow vulnerability (pancytopenia worsened by severe B12 deficiency), premature ageing stigmata — and a genetic hit in a telomere maintenance gene. Once you suspect a TBD, two thorny therapeutic options rise to the top: androgen therapy (danazol, nandrolone) intended to lengthen telomeres and hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) as a potential cure for marrow failure. Both can help, both can hurt — and in patients with existing hepatic or pulmonary fibrosis, the stakes are high.

Here’s a practical, clinically focused guide for thinking through androgen use and HSCT in TBDs, with emphasis on the real-world landmines illustrated by the case above.

## The androgen story — promise and poison
Why consider androgens?
- Small studies and case series have shown that androgens (oral danazol, injectable nandrolone) can increase telomere length and improve blood counts in patients with telomere-related marrow failure. Mechanism likely includes upregulation of telomerase components and improved hematopoietic proliferation.
- For patients with progressive cytopenias who are poor HSCT candidates or awaiting a donor, androgens can be a bridge.

Why caution is mandatory
- Androgens are hepatotoxic. Danazol in particular has known risks: cholestasis, transaminitis, hepatic adenomas and, rarely, malignant transformation. Nandrolone carries similar hepatic risks.
- In TBDs, hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis may already be present (as in the reported patient), increasing susceptibility to drug-induced liver injury and portal hypertension complications.
- Androgens are also thrombogenic and can worsen lipid profiles, worsening cardiovascular risk.
- Side effects: virilization, acne, mood changes, fluid retention — all relevant to quality of life.

Evidence snapshot (brief)
- The landmark open-label trial of danazol in telomere diseases showed telomere elongation and hematologic improvement in a significant fraction of patients, but follow-up was limited and hepatic adverse events were noted.
- Data are small, non-randomized, and biased toward patients without advanced liver disease.

Clinical approach to androgens
- Candidate selection: consider androgens for patients with symptomatic cytopenias (transfusion dependence, progressive counts) where marrow failure is the main problem and significant organ fibrosis is not advanced. Strongly avoid or proceed with extreme caution if there is clinically significant hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis or active portal hypertension.
- Baseline work-up before starting:
  - Full LFT panel, synthetic function (INR, albumin), platelets, ultrasound/CT elastography to stage fibrosis if not already done.
  - Pregnancy test in people of childbearing potential; counsel on contraception and teratogenicity.
  - Baseline lipid panel, coagulation, and screening for pro-thrombotic states if indicated.
- Monitoring while on therapy:
  - LFTs weekly for the first month, then every 2–4 weeks for 3 months, then monthly if stable.
  - Monitor blood counts, lipids, and signs of virilization.
  - Low threshold to stop therapy for rising bilirubin, transaminases >3x ULN, or any evidence of hepatic decompensation.
- Dosing considerations:
  - Danazol dosing used in trials: commonly 400 mg/day; lower doses might be better tolerated but efficacy data are limited.
  - Nandrolone dosing is less standardized; injectable forms may avoid first-pass hepatic metabolism but still carry hepatic risk.
- Duration and endpoints:
  - Reassess at 3 months for hematologic response; if none and LFTs rise, stop.
  - If hematologic benefit occurs and LFTs stable, consider continuing with careful surveillance and re-evaluation for HSCT candidacy.

Bottom line on androgens: they are a potential bridge or temporizing therapy for marrow failure, not a panacea. In patients with any significant hepatic fibrosis—like the PARN case—using androgens risks worsening an organ that may already limit outcomes. Discuss risk/benefit and have clear stopping rules.

## HSCT — cure with caveats
HSCT can cure the hematologic manifestations of TBDs, but outcomes are highly contingent on conditioning regimens, donor choice, and pre-existing organ disease.

When to consider HSCT
- Progressive, transfusion-dependent marrow failure or frank aplastic anemia not responding to medical therapy.
- High-risk clonal evolution (MDS/AML) related to marrow failure.
- Availability of a clinically suitable donor and a clear plan to minimize conditioning toxicity.

Key transplant-landmines in TBDs
- Conditioning toxicity: traditional myeloablative regimens (high-dose alkylators, total body irradiation) are catastrophically toxic in TBD patients, especially with pulmonary or hepatic fibrosis.
- Organ comorbidity: pre-existing pulmonary fibrosis and hepatic cirrhosis are major predictors of transplant morbidity and mortality. A patient with both liver and lung involvement (again, like the PARN case) faces heightened risk.
- Donor choice: related donors may harbor the same germline telomere mutations (or be phenotypically normal with short telomeres), raising risk of poor graft durability and donor complications.

Transplant strategy — practical recommendations
- Use reduced-intensity or non-myeloablative conditioning whenever possible to reduce DNA-damaging exposures. Examples:
  - Fludarabine-based regimens with reduced doses of melphalan or busulfan (careful: alkylators still risky).
  - Avoid or minimize total body irradiation when feasible.
- Prefer regimens that avoid high-dose cyclophosphamide or busulfan if acceptable alternatives exist; there is no perfect regimen — decisions are individualized by transplant center experience.
- Consider serotherapy (ATG) for engraftment with careful infection monitoring.
- Donor selection:
  - Avoid related donors who are mutation carriers or have short telomeres; test related donors for the familial mutation and consider telomere length assessment.
  - Matched unrelated donors can be preferable if related donors carry risk.
- Pre-transplant optimization:
  - Treat reversible problems (nutritional deficiencies, infections).
  - Consider pulmonary and hepatic panels: PFTs with DLCO, HRCT chest, transient elastography or MRI liver, liver synthetic function assessment.
  - Multidisciplinary discussion with hepatology and pulmonology — sometimes HSCT proceeds only after stabilization, or is deferred if organ disease is prohibitive.
- Post-transplant surveillance:
  - Vigilant monitoring for veno-occlusive disease (VOD), pulmonary complications and graft dysfunction.
  - Long-term surveillance for secondary malignancy and organ decompensation.
- Counseling and alternatives:
  - Explain that HSCT addresses hematopoiesis but not existing fibrosis in lungs or liver. Transplant may remove marrow failure risk but not reverse pulmonary or hepatic disease.
  - Discuss fertility preservation and the likelihood of long-term immunosuppression-related complications.

Where to draw the line
- HSCT makes sense when marrow failure is life-limiting and the patient is an otherwise acceptable candidate (controlled infections, adequate organ reserve). Severe, uncompensated hepatic cirrhosis or advanced pulmonary fibrosis may tip the balance against HSCT or necessitate palliative approaches.
- In complex cases with multiorgan involvement, consider:
  - Sequential organ transplantation (rare and high-risk).
  - Non-transplant supportive strategies and clinical trials.

## Practical pathway for the patient like the PARN case
1. Confirm diagnosis and stage organ involvement: genetics (confirm variant, cascade testing), telomere-length testing if available, HRCT chest, PFTs, transient elastography, liver synthetic function.
2. Correct reversible contributors to cytopenia (e.g., B12) and reassess marrow trajectory.
3. Multidisciplinary team (hematology, transplant, hepatology, pulmonology, genetics) meeting early to define timelines and thresholds.
4. Androgen therapy:
   - If marrow failure is progressive and no immediate HSCT planned, androgens may be considered only if liver disease is mild or absent. In a patient with cirrhosis, avoid androgens unless under exceptional circumstances with hepatology oversight and informed consent.
5. HSCT:
   - If marrow failure becomes progressive/ life-threatening and donor options are clean (non-carrier), evaluate for reduced-intensity conditioning HSCT with institution experienced in TBD.
   - If significant hepatic/pulmonary compromise exists, quantify risk; transplant may still be possible in select centers but with high morbidity/mortality.
6. If neither androgen therapy nor HSCT is safe, focus on supportive care, surveillance for organ complications, and early referral for clinical trials.

## Clinical pearls
- Landmark principle: telomere disorders are diseases of replicative tissues — bone marrow, liver, lung — and therapies that stress DNA repair (radiation, alkylators) amplify toxicity.
- Before offering HSCT, test related donors for the familial mutation and consider telomere length—donor genotype matters.
- Androgens can help blood counts and lengthen telomeres but are distinctly hepatotoxic; in patients with hepatic fibrosis, they can do more harm than good.
- Always involve hepatology and pulmonology early when fibrosis is present — HSCT won’t fix established cirrhosis or fibrotic lung disease.
- Use clear stopping rules for androgen therapy (e.g., rising bilirubin, synthetic dysfunction, ALT/AST thresholds).
- Genetic counseling and family screening are essential: TBDs have variable penetrance and late presentations.

Key take-home messages
- Androgens (danazol, nandrolone) can improve counts and lengthen telomeres in small studies but carry substantial hepatic risk — avoid or use extreme caution in patients with hepatic fibrosis.
- HSCT can cure marrow failure but requires transplant-center expertise in TBDs; conditioning must be minimized and donor selection prioritized to avoid reintroducing genetic risk.
- For patients with multiorgan fibrosis (lungs, liver) — like the PARN case — decisions require a multidisciplinary, individualized plan balancing short-term marrow needs and long-term organ risks.
- Early diagnosis, genetic testing, and proactive multidisciplinary planning are the safest ways to navigate these “treating the untreatable” decisions.

If you’re caring for someone with suspected TBD: get genetics involved early, quantify organ involvement, and don’t reflexively reach for danazol or full-intensity transplant conditioning. These are powerful tools — use them thoughtfully, with clear monitoring and stopping plans.
