Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

Chapter 1: ## Clinical Approach to a Young Adult with Multisystem Premature‑Ageing Features and Cytopenias

This case illustrates a critical diagnostic challenge: a young adult with progressive fatigue, pancytopenia and multisystem findings that mimic acquired nutritional, immune or inflammatory disease but ultimately reflect an underlying telomere biology disorder (TBD). The clinical approach has two parallel and equally important goals. First, identify and promptly treat reversible and common causes of cytopenia and end‑organ dysfunction (nutritional deficiencies, infection, autoimmune disease). Second, maintain a high index of suspicion for an inherited telomere syndrome when the clinical picture is disproportionate, multisystemic, or unexplained after initial therapy — because early recognition changes surveillance, family counselling and definitive management decisions such as hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT).

Recognize red flags that should prompt consideration of TBDs
- Age and phenotype mismatch: significant marrow dysfunction, pulmonary fibrosis, liver fibrosis or premature osteoporosis in a young adult is unlike typical degenerative disease of older age. Premature greying, nail changes, oral leukoplakia or subtle skin hyperpigmentation are helpful when present but are frequently absent in adults.
- Multiorgan fibrosis: idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) or cryptogenic hepatic fibrosis, especially when disproportionate to common exposures, is a cardinal clue. Concurrent involvement of two or more high‑turnover tissues (bone marrow, lung, liver, skin, mucosa, hair) increases the pretest probability.
- Hematologic pattern: unexplained cytopenias with a low reticulocyte index, dimorphic anaemia or marrow erythroid hyperplasia suggest ineffective erythropoiesis rather than peripheral immune destruction. A positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) does not exclude a marrow problem — interpret haemolysis markers (LDH, bilirubin, haptoglobin) and reticulocyte response in context.
- Family history: young relatives with early lung, liver disease or marrow failure may be present, but incomplete penetrance or de novo variants is common, so absence of family history does not rule out genetic disease.

Initial bedside and laboratory evaluation: parallel diagnostic and therapeutic planning
On presentation, pursue a concise but broad workup while simultaneously treating reversible contributors. For example, in this case the severe vitamin B12 and folate deficiency required immediate correction because it accounted for much of the ineffective erythropoiesis and clinical pallor. At the same time, the clinician should continue evaluating for an inherited syndrome if features persist or are disproportionate to the deficiency.

Immediate/initial tests and actions:
- CBC with differential and reticulocyte count, peripheral smear (look for dimorphism, basophilic stippling, hypersegmented neutrophils, Cabot rings).
- Haemolysis panel (LDH, indirect bilirubin, haptoglobin), DAT to assess immune haemolysis — but interpret with marrow data; a low reticulocyte count despite signs of haemolysis argues against a purely peripheral process.
- Nutritional studies (B12, folate, iron studies), thyroid function, vitamin D, calcium and PTH.
- Viral serologies and basic autoimmune testing to exclude treatable causes of fibrosis or cytopenia.
- Prompt replacement of deficiencies (for B12, parenteral therapy when severe) and supportive transfusion as indicated.
- Bone marrow examination early in the course if cytopenias persist or there is concern for marrow failure, MDS, or infiltrative disease. Expect trilineage hypoplasia in classic TBDs, but findings can be non‑specific; erythroid hyperplasia with ineffective erythropoiesis is common when nutritional deficiencies coexist.

Interpreting marrow and haemolysis findings in context
TBDs often present with marrow dysfunction that may be unmasked or worsened by external stresses (nutritional deficiency, infection, cytotoxic drugs). A low reticulocyte count despite anemia and marrow erythroid hyperplasia favors ineffective erythropoiesis — the marrow makes red cell precursors but they fail to mature or survive. This explains why a patient can have marrow erythroid activity and biochemical signs suggesting hemolysis (elevated LDH, indirect bilirubin) while still mounting a poor reticulocyte response. In such situations, immunosuppressive therapy for autoimmune haemolysis is rarely helpful and might be harmful; instead, correcting reversible causes and further assessing marrow function is appropriate.

When to suspect and test for a telomere biology disorder
Suspect TBD when:
- Cytopenias (single-lineage or pancytopenia) occur in young adults without an alternative explanation or are persistent after correction of reversible causes.
- There is idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, cryptogenic liver fibrosis or early-onset osteoporosis, especially when combined with subtle stigmata of premature ageing.
- Family history is suggestive, or there is a constellation of multisystem disease affecting high‑turnover tissues.

Recommended confirmatory testing:
- Telomere length testing by flow‑FISH (preferred, age‑adjusted) or qPCR. Telomere lengths below age‑adjusted percentiles (eg, <1st–10th percentile depending on lab) strongly support TBD.
- Multigene panel or whole‑exome sequencing targeting telomere-related genes (TERT, TERC, RTEL1, PARN, DKC1 and others). Interpret variants in the clinical context; monoallelic variants can be disease‑causing with variable penetrance. Variants of uncertain significance (VUS) are common — additional evidence (telomere length, segregation studies, functional assays) can help clarify their significance.

Concurrent organ evaluation: screen broadly at presentation
Because TBDs are multisystem, initial assessment should extend beyond hematology:
- Pulmonary: high‑resolution CT chest and pulmonary function tests including diffusing capacity (DLCO). Even subclinical fibrosis may be present.
- Hepatic: liver enzymes, synthetic function (albumin, INR), abdominal ultrasound and noninvasive fibrosis assessment (transient elastography). TB-driven hepatic fibrosis can be disproportionate to classic causes.
- Skeletal/endocrine: bone mineral density (DEXA), vitamin D and PTH levels, electrolyte evaluation; osteoporosis is common.
- Neurologic: if neuropathy is suspected, nerve conduction studies and electromyography can delineate axonal polyneuropathy.
- Cancer surveillance: TBDs increase the risk of certain malignancies; maintain vigilance and appropriate age‑appropriate screening.

Management principles while pursuing a genetic diagnosis
- Treat reversible contributors promptly: nutritional repletion, correction of electrolytes, vaccination, and supportive transfusions as needed.
- Avoid unnecessary or potentially harmful therapies until the diagnosis is clear. For example, myeloablative chemotherapy or standard conditioning regimens for HSCT carry increased toxicity in TBD patients; if HSCT is considered, early genetic diagnosis influences donor selection (test related donors) and conditioning choices (reduced‑intensity regimens are preferred).
- Avoid exposures that accelerate telomere attrition and organ injury: tobacco cessation, limit alcohol (especially with hepatic fibrosis), and avoid cytotoxic agents where possible.
- Consider specialist referrals early: coordinated care with hematology, pulmonology, hepatology and clinical genetics optimizes surveillance and timing of definitive therapies.

Genetic counselling, family testing and interpretation of variants
A genetic diagnosis has immediate implications: cascade testing of family members can reveal presymptomatic carriers; this affects donor suitability for HSCT and informs surveillance. When sequencing returns a VUS (as in the presented PARN missense), integrate additional data: telomere length assays, in silico predictions, literature on similar variants, and segregation analysis in family members. Functional assays (where available) and longitudinal clinical follow‑up may be necessary to resolve uncertain results. Counsel patients that monoallelic variants can have variable expressivity and incomplete penetrance; not all carriers will have identical phenotypes.

Long‑term surveillance and therapy options
- Hematologic: periodic CBC and bone marrow evaluation if counts decline; consider early HSCT for progressive marrow failure, but plan conditioning and donor selection carefully in TBDs.
- Pulmonary: regular PFTs and HRCT to detect progressive fibrosis. Antifibrotic therapies (eg, pirfenidone, nintedanib) may be considered in IPF, and transplant assessment is necessary for progressive disease.
- Hepatic: monitor for progression of fibrosis, portal hypertension and hepatic synthetic dysfunction; referral to hepatology for consideration of transplantation when indicated.
- Supportive care: manage osteoporosis, neuropathy, nutritional deficits, and psychosocial issues.
- Experimental and disease‑modifying therapies: androgens (danazol, nandrolone) have shown telomere‑lengthening effects in small studies but long‑term benefit/risks are uncertain; enrolment in clinical trials or specialist centers is advised where available.

Avoid premature diagnostic closure
A pragmatic but cautious approach in young adults with multisystem premature‑ageing signs is essential. Correct clear, reversible causes first, but if the overall clinical burden is disproportionate, persistently abnormal or involves multiple telomere‑sensitive organs, broaden the differential to include TBDs even in the absence of the classic mucocutaneous triad. Early genetic and telomere testing allows proactive surveillance, targeted counselling and better planning for potentially curative but high‑risk therapies such as HSCT.

Practical checklist to apply at the bedside
- Treat emergent/reversible problems immediately (eg, parenteral B12 for severe deficiency).
- Order baseline studies: CBC + reticulocyte, smear, haemolysis panel, marrow exam if unexplained cytopenia; B12/folate/iron; viral and autoimmune tests.
- Screen organs: HRCT chest and PFTs, liver imaging and elastography, DEXA, endocrine panel as indicated.
- If clinical pattern suggests TBD, obtain telomere length testing and refer for genetic testing and counselling.
- Coordinate multidisciplinary follow‑up and discuss implications for family screening and future therapies.

In summary, this patient’s course illustrates the diagnostic interplay: prompt correction of B12 deficiency produced symptomatic improvement, yet the persistent, multisystem features and young age rightly prompted testing for a telomere disorder. Recognizing the red flags and maintaining parallel diagnostic and therapeutic tracks — treat what you can now, while investigating what explains the rest — is the cornerstone of caring for young adults with premature‑ageing features and cytopenias.
