Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

### [Clinical Pearls] Which clinical features in adults should prompt evaluation for a telomere biology disorder: list at least five that span hematologic, hepatic, pulmonary, and premature-ageing phenotypes?

Key teaching point: adult patients with unexplained disease of highly proliferative tissues — bone marrow, lung, liver, skin and hair — should prompt evaluation for a telomere‑biology disorder (TBD). Telomere defects preferentially cripple tissues with high replicative demand, so look for clusters of findings, early onset, or multisystem involvement that are out of proportion to common explanations.

Specific clinical features that should raise suspicion (at least five, spanning the requested domains):

1) Unexplained cytopenias or marrow failure in a young adult — isolated macrocytic anemia, refractory pancytopenia, aplastic anaemia, or transfusion‑dependent marrow failure without an acquired cause. Rationale: haemopoietic stem cells are highly telomere‑dependent; premature telomere attrition causes ineffective haematopoiesis and marrow exhaustion.

2) Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis or early‑onset interstitial lung disease (especially onset <60 years) often with a family history of pulmonary fibrosis. Rationale: alveolar epithelial cells have limited replicative reserve; monallelic telomerase pathway mutations commonly present as familial or early IPF.

3) Non‑viral, non‑alcoholic hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis or unexplained portal hypertension in a young adult. Rationale: hepatocytes must regenerate after injury and show fibrosis when telomere maintenance is defective; cirrhosis can be an under‑recognised TBD manifestation.

4) Premature ageing features — premature greying or hair loss, early osteoporosis with low‑trauma fractures, and characteristic mucocutaneous findings of dyskeratosis congenita (nail dystrophy, reticular hyperpigmentation, oral leukoplakia) when present. Rationale: these reflect stem‑cell exhaustion in skin, hair follicles and bone.

5) Multisystem combination or unusually severe disease out of proportion to a single reversible cause — for example B12 deficiency with persistent cytopenia after correction, cytopenia plus pulmonary fibrosis, or marrow failure plus liver disease. Rationale: the co‑occurrence of organ‑specific fibrosis or failure suggests a unifying defect in telomere biology rather than isolated acquired disease.

Additional red flags that strengthen the case for evaluation: onset at a young age (often <50), family history of marrow failure, pulmonary fibrosis, or early cirrhosis/cancer, unexplained mucocutaneous triad, unusually poor tolerance of cytotoxic therapy, and laboratory evidence of very short telomeres if available.

When to act: in any adult with one of the above features plus another supportive clue (young age, family history, multisystem involvement, or refractory disease), measure telomere length (flow‑FISH if available) and pursue targeted genetic testing for telomere‑related genes. Early recognition guides surveillance (pulmonary, hepatic, haematologic), genetic counselling, choice of therapies (HSCT planning, avoidance of genotoxic exposures), and donor selection.
