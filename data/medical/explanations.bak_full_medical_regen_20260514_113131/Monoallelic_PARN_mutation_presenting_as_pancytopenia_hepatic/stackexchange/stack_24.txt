Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

### [Management] What longitudinal surveillance would you implement for a young adult with a telomere biology disorder affecting marrow, liver, lung, and bone, and which red flags would prompt escalation of care?

Start by remembering the core principle: telomere biology disorders are multisystem, progressive conditions in which surveillance should be proactive, disease‑directed, and multidisciplinary so that organ‑specific decline is detected early and definitive therapies (transplantation, referral to specialty centres, clinical trials) can be timed before irreversible organ failure.

Initial baseline assessments to obtain now (if not already done): formal telomere length testing (flow‑FISH) and confirmation/clarification of the PARN variant with genetic counselling; full haematologic workup including marrow cellularity, cytogenetics and molecular myeloid panel; baseline pulmonary function tests (FVC, FEV1, DLCO) and six‑minute walk with oxygen saturation, and a baseline high‑resolution chest CT; baseline liver staging with LFTs, INR, platelet count, transient elastography (or MRI elastography) and hepatology evaluation including screening for portal hypertension; bone density (DEXA) and endocrine assessment (vitamin D, PTH); infection/immunization review; and a formal discussion with a combined bone‑marrow‑transplant and lung/liver transplant centre about future options and conditioning risks.

Longitudinal surveillance plan (practical, test frequency adjusted to trajectory):

Hematology: complete blood count with differential every 1–3 months when unstable or during active decline; every 3–6 months if stable. Repeat bone marrow evaluation and cytogenetics if new or progressive cytopenias, new blasts, rising transfusion needs, or unexplained marrow changes. Consider FISH/molecular reassessment if concern for clonal evolution. Low threshold for early transplant centre referral when persistent severe cytopenias, transfusion dependence, clonal evolution, or MDS/AML are suspected.

Pulmonary: outpatient PFTs including DLCO and 6‑minute walk every 3–6 months for the first year (more frequently if decline), then at least every 6–12 months if stable. Repeat HRCT if clinical change or every ~12 months if progressive disease is suspected. Home oximetry and prompt evaluation for new dyspnea, exercise desaturation or new cough. Early discussion with lung transplant program if FVC declines ≥10% or DLCO declines ≥15%, or if patient develops resting hypoxemia or increasing oxygen requirement — these are accepted markers of progression in fibrotic lung disease.

Hepatology: LFTs, INR, albumin and platelets every 3–6 months; transient elastography or MRI elastography annually (sooner if changes). Screen for complications of portal hypertension (clinical exam, ultrasound for splenomegaly/ascites); if cirrhosis is present, perform variceal screening (endoscopy per hepatology), and HCC surveillance with abdominal ultrasound ± AFP every 6 months. Refer early to liver transplant program if any sign of hepatic decompensation (ascites, encephalopathy, variceal bleed) or progressive synthetic dysfunction (rising INR, falling albumin, rising MELD).

Bone and endocrine: DEXA every 1–2 years and more often if fractures or rapid change. Monitor calcium, phosphate, vitamin D, PTH every 6–12 months and treat osteoporosis aggressively; avoid long gaps because fragility is common.

Cancer and mucocutaneous surveillance: annual skin inspection and oral cavity exam (dysplastic leukoplakia can progress). Maintain age‑appropriate cancer screening but with heightened suspicion—new weight loss, bleeding, new masses, or lymphadenopathy require urgent workup.

Infection prevention and general care: update pneumococcal and influenza vaccinations; avoid live vaccines if immunosuppression is required. Counsel on tobacco avoidance and minimize exposures that accelerate telomere attrition (smoking, excessive alcohol, cytotoxic agents when possible).

Medication and procedure cautions: avoid myeloablative chemotherapy and radiation when alternatives exist; if transplantation or chemotherapy is being considered, plan reduced‑intensity conditioning and discuss increased toxicity risk with transplant teams experienced in TBDs. Use hematopoietic growth factors and androgens only under specialist guidance and in context of clinical trials or expert opinion.

When to escalate care or refer urgently (red flags)

Hematologic red flags that mandate urgent transplant evaluation: progressive cytopenias with marrow hypocellularity or failure, new transfusion dependence for red cells or platelets, absolute neutrophil count <0.5 ×10^9/L with infections, development of clonal cytogenetic abnormalities or ≥5% blasts (MDS/AML evolution). Any rapid fall in hemoglobin, platelets or neutrophils that is unexplained.

Pulmonary red flags: acute or subacute worsening dyspnea, new hypoxemia at rest or with exertion, a fall in FVC ≥10% or DLCO ≥15% from baseline, increased supplemental oxygen requirement, or hospitalization for respiratory failure—prompt referral to pulmonary specialty and lung transplant centre.

Hepatic red flags: new ascites, hepatic encephalopathy, variceal hemorrhage, jaundice with rising bilirubin or INR, or a rapidly rising MELD score. These require urgent hepatology and liver transplant assessment.

Infectious/bleeding complications: recurrent, severe, or opportunistic infections; severe or uncontrollable bleeding in the setting of thrombocytopenia should lead to inpatient evaluation and escalation.

Other red flags: new or rapidly progressive cytopenia‑related symptoms (severe fatigue, syncope), new focal neurologic deficits, rapid bone loss with pathologic fracture, or any clinical concern for a new malignancy.

Final practical points: coordinate care through a multidisciplinary TBD clinic if available (hematology, pulmonology, hepatology, genetics, transplant teams, infectious disease, endocrinology), document advance care planning and reproductive counseling early, and keep a low threshold to refer to specialized transplant centres because timing of HSCT or organ transplant in TBD is critical and outcomes are improved when done in experienced centres using tailored, reduced‑toxicity regimens. Surveillance intensity should be individualized to the patient’s trajectory, but the default stance is close, proactive monitoring given the risk of rapid, irreversible organ compromise.
