Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook

Chapter 4: ## Diagnostic Workup of Suspected Dermatomyositis with Neck Edema

Neck swelling that resembles angioedema can be a clinical red herring. When it is persistent, non-pruritic, non-fluctuating with antihistamines, and accompanied by even subtle cutaneous or neuromuscular findings, clinicians should broaden the differential to include inflammatory myopathies such as dermatomyositis (DM). The case presented illustrates this point: a patient referred for presumed deep neck infection or idiopathic angioedema was found to have markedly elevated creatine kinase (CK), periungual nailfold changes and proximal weakness, and ultimately a myositis-specific autoantibody (anti‑SAE1) and muscle biopsy features diagnostic of DM. The diagnostic evaluation in this clinical scenario must therefore be systematic: confirm the myopathic process, define its phenotype and severity, exclude mimics that would change management (especially infectious or bradykinin-mediated angioedema), assess organ involvement (particularly lung and pharyngeal musculature), and screen for associated malignancy before committing to prolonged immunosuppression.

Initial laboratory assessment should focus on objective evidence of muscle injury and the complications that may follow. Serum CK is the most sensitive and widely available biomarker of active myositis; very high values (as in this case, >16,000 U/L) strongly support a myopathic process and alert clinicians to the risk of myoglobinuria and acute kidney injury. Complementary tests that are helpful include aldolase, AST/ALT and LDH, which can all be elevated from muscle sources; a urinalysis for myoglobin and routine renal function tests should be obtained to detect or monitor rhabdomyolysis. Basic inflammatory markers (CRP, ESR) are nonspecific but useful adjuncts for the overall clinical picture and to track response to therapy.

Autoantibody testing plays two roles: it helps confirm an autoimmune myopathy and often defines a more precise phenotype with prognostic and therapeutic implications. A screening antinuclear antibody (ANA) is often positive in DM and may display a speckled pattern. More specific testing is with a comprehensive myositis panel that includes myositis-specific and myositis-associated antibodies: antisynthetase antibodies (anti‑Jo‑1 and other tRNA synthetases), anti‑Mi‑2, anti‑TIF1‑γ, anti‑MDA5, anti‑NXP2, anti‑SRP, anti‑HMGCR, and, in this case, anti‑SAE1. The pattern of antibodies guides clinicians because associations exist (for example, antisynthetase antibodies associate with interstitial lung disease and mechanic’s hands; anti‑TIF1‑γ and anti‑NXP2 are linked to higher malignancy risk in adults; anti‑MDA5 often presents with clinically amyopathic disease and rapidly progressive ILD; anti‑SAE1 is associated with prominent cutaneous disease, dysphagia and variable malignancy risk). Importantly, a negative panel does not exclude inflammatory myopathy; seronegative disease occurs.

When the initial presentation mimics angioedema, concurrent testing to exclude true histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema should be performed when clinically appropriate. Measurements of complement C4 and quantitative and functional C1 esterase inhibitor (C1‑INH) levels distinguish hereditary or acquired C1‑INH deficiency (bradykinin-mediated) from other causes. Serum tryptase can be measured acutely if an anaphylactic mechanism is suspected. Normal complement and C1‑INH results, together with absent exposure to common culprits (ACE inhibitors, DPP‑4 inhibitors) and lack of a clear allergic trigger, support the concept of pseudoangioedema from inflammatory soft tissue swelling rather than a primary angioedema disorder.

Imaging has a central role in localizing disease and guiding invasive testing. MRI of affected muscle groups is the preferred modality to identify active muscle edema, fatty replacement, and areas of inflammation that should be targeted for biopsy. In the presented patient, MRI changes in pelvic and thigh muscles helped define active myositis. FDG‑PET can serve a dual purpose: it quantifies systemic inflammatory burden—sometimes highlighting otherwise occult muscle involvement such as prominent head and neck muscle uptake that explains cervical swelling—and simultaneously surveys for occult malignancy. However, clinicians must remember FDG uptake is nonspecific and may reflect inflammation as well as neoplasia. Neck and soft‑tissue CT imaging is useful to exclude alternative local pathology (abscesses, deep neck space infection) and, when combined with high‑resolution CT of the chest, provides essential screening for interstitial lung disease (ILD). Pulmonary function testing with spirometry, lung volumes and DLCO further characterizes respiratory involvement; an extrathoracic or restrictive pattern may point to upper airway or respiratory muscle involvement rather than primary parenchymal ILD, as occurred in this case.

Dysphagia is common in DM and may be clinically occult. Formal assessment with a videofluoroscopic swallow study is the standard to quantify pharyngeal dysfunction and aspiration risk and to guide decisions on nutritional support (e.g., nasogastric feeding) and airway protection. Early recognition of severe pharyngeal weakness is essential because aspiration pneumonitis and respiratory compromise may necessitate urgent interventions, including hospitalization and high‑dose corticosteroids.

Muscle biopsy remains the histopathologic gold standard when the diagnosis is uncertain or when subtype clarification will influence management. Classic dermatomyositis features include perifascicular atrophy and necrosis, perimysial/perivascular inflammation, and complement-mediated capillary damage with membrane attack complex (C5b‑9) deposition in endomysial capillaries. These findings differentiate DM from immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy (which has prominent necrosis with comparatively little inflammation) and inclusion body myositis (which has rimmed vacuoles and distinctive inflammatory patterns). Biopsy interpretation should be integrated with clinical, serologic and imaging data.

Before initiating potent immunosuppression, an infection and immunization screen is mandatory. Test for hepatitis B and C, HIV, and assess latent tuberculosis with interferon‑gamma release assays or chest imaging; check varicella zoster and CMV serostatus if relevant to planned therapy. Where agents such as rituximab or high‑dose steroids are planned, ensure appropriate prophylaxis and management plans (e.g., HBV antiviral prophylaxis if HBsAg or HBcAb positive). Update non‑live immunizations (influenza, pneumococcal, COVID‑19, recombinant zoster) where possible prior to immunosuppression. Consider Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia prophylaxis when prolonged high‑dose steroids or lymphocyte‑depleting agents are used.

Malignancy screening in adult DM should be risk‑stratified: the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group recommends intensive baseline screening for patients with high‑risk clinical features—dermatomyositis phenotype, older age, dysphagia, certain autoantibodies and male sex among others. Practical screening commonly includes CT chest/abdomen/pelvis or PET/CT, age‑appropriate cancer screens (mammography, cervical screening, colonoscopy, prostate evaluation), targeted endoscopy if GI symptoms or high risk, and directed laboratories if indicated. Tumor markers alone lack sensitivity and should not supplant imaging and endoscopic evaluation. Repeat surveillance should be individualized, especially in those with high‑risk antibody profiles or new systemic symptoms.

Finally, establish baseline and serial monitoring to document disease activity and detect treatment toxicity. Quantitative muscle testing (for example, MMT‑8), CK and other muscle enzymes, skin activity scores, periodic pulmonary function tests and repeat imaging when indicated (HRCT for ILD progression, MRI for persistent weakness) form the backbone of follow‑up. Regular laboratory monitoring for cytopenias, liver and renal function is required for most steroid‑sparing agents. In the presented patient, this structured approach allowed early diagnosis, targeted therapy (including IV methylprednisolone, IVIG, mycophenolate and rituximab), close monitoring of swallowing and respiratory function, and a coordinated malignancy screen—measures that together led to clinical remission and recovery of function.

In summary, when neck edema is unexplained by common otolaryngologic or allergic causes, integrate directed muscle enzyme testing and careful skin/musculoskeletal examination, pursue a full myositis panel and muscle imaging, exclude true angioedema with complement studies where appropriate, assess pulmonary and swallowing involvement, and perform histopathologic confirmation and malignancy screening before prolonged immunosuppression. This stepwise, multidisciplinary evaluation optimizes diagnostic accuracy and guides safe, effective treatment.
