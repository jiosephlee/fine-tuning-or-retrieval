Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook

Chapter 3: ## Differential Diagnosis: Cervical Swelling, Dysphagia, and Weakness

Cervical soft‑tissue swelling accompanied by oropharyngeal symptoms and weakness is a diagnostically broad presentation that spans infectious, allergic/angioedema, neuromuscular, inflammatory myopathies, endocrine/metabolic, structural and neoplastic processes. Timely distinction among these entities is critical because management and immediate risks differ markedly — for example, airway compromise from angioedema or myositis‑related pharyngeal weakness, sepsis from deep neck infection, versus the need for urgent immunosuppression and cancer screening in dermatomyositis. The case presented illustrates common pitfalls: an apparently localized “angioedema” that lacked typical allergic features, imaging without a drainable collection, and subtle cutaneous signs and proximal weakness that pointed away from the usual otolaryngology diagnoses and toward an inflammatory myopathy.

Angioedema — histaminergic versus bradykinin‑mediated — is often the first diagnostic consideration for acute neck and facial swelling. Histaminergic angioedema tends to have rapid onset, associated urticaria or pruritus, and responsiveness to antihistamines, corticosteroids or epinephrine. By contrast, bradykinin‑mediated angioedema (eg, ACE inhibitor–related, hereditary or acquired C1‑INH deficiency) is usually non‑pruritic, longer lasting, and refractory to antihistamines and steroids. Laboratory discriminators include low C4 and low or dysfunctional C1 esterase inhibitor for hereditary/acquired C1‑INH deficiency. A careful drug history (ACE inhibitors, neprilysin inhibitors) and family history are essential. In the present case the absence of urticaria or pruritus, lack of culprit medications or family history, and normal C4 and C1‑INH testing argued strongly against bradykinin‑mediated angioedema.

Infectious and inflammatory causes of neck swelling — Ludwig angina, deep neck space infection or abscess, bacterial sialadenitis, cellulitis/erysipelas — typically present with fever, focal pain, elevated white cell count and imaging evidence of an abscess or focal inflammatory collection. Clinical features such as trismus, severe odynophagia, toxic appearance, and CT findings of loculated fluid with rim enhancement point toward a surgically managed deep space infection. Diffuse, non‑tender swelling without a discrete collection and an afebrile, clinically well patient (as in this case) should prompt reconsideration of a noninfectious etiology.

Neuromuscular junction disorders and cranial neuropathies can present predominantly with bulbar symptoms and neck involvement. Myasthenia gravis classically causes fluctuating, fatigable bulbar weakness (dysarthria, dysphagia, ptosis) without a marked rise in serum creatine kinase (CK); electrophysiology and acetylcholine receptor or MuSK antibody testing are diagnostic. Botulism and certain cranial neuropathies produce prominent autonomic signs (eg, dry mouth, dilated pupils) and descending weakness; their time course and exposure history usually provide clues. In contrast, the case patient had fixed proximal limb weakness and markedly elevated CK, findings that disfavor a primary neuromuscular junction disorder.

Inflammatory myopathies form a key differential when weakness and neck or facial swelling coexist. Dermatomyositis (DM) may present with cutaneous signs (heliotrope rash, Gottron’s papules, shawl or V‑sign, periungual capillary changes) and proximal muscle weakness; Importantly, DM can cause prominent subcutaneous edema of the face and neck — a less common but well‑described manifestation — and dysphagia from pharyngeal and upper esophageal muscle involvement. Characteristic laboratory clues are substantially elevated CK, aldolase, transaminases and LDH; myositis‑specific autoantibodies (eg, anti‑SAE1, anti‑TIF1‑γ, anti‑Mi‑2, anti‑MDA‑5, antisynthetase antibodies) can direct phenotype and cancer risk. Imaging with MRI shows muscle edema; PET or FDG uptake may demonstrate diffuse myositis; definitive diagnosis is supported by muscle biopsy, with perifascicular atrophy and complement (C5b‑9) deposition in dermatomyositis. In this patient the constellation of markedly raised CK, nailfold capillary abnormalities, positive ANA and anti‑SAE1 antibody, PET/MRI muscle uptake and classic biopsy findings established DM as the cause of the “angioedema,” attributable to inflammatory myositis and microvascular injury rather than histaminergic or bradykinin processes.

Other inflammatory myopathies have distinguishing features. Immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy (often anti‑HMGCR or anti‑SRP positive) presents with very high CK and severe weakness but typically minimal cutaneous signs; biopsies show necrosis with scarce inflammatory infiltrate. Inclusion body myositis (IBM) affects older patients, has a characteristic distribution (finger flexors, quadriceps), is slowly progressive, and may cause dysphagia; CK is usually only mildly elevated and muscle biopsy reveals rimmed vacuoles and protein aggregates. Antisynthetase syndrome (eg, anti‑Jo‑1) features mechanic’s hands, myositis, interstitial lung disease and arthritis; the presence of ILD and mechanic’s hands helps differentiate it from other entities.

Endocrine, metabolic and toxic causes can mimic myositis or cause rhabdomyolysis with neck swelling and dysphagia. Hypothyroid myopathy typically causes myalgias, slow reflexes and modest CK elevation and will be accompanied by hypothyroid clinical signs and TSH abnormalities. Drug‑related rhabdomyolysis (statins, fibrates, certain antipsychotics) often has an identifiable exposure and markedly raised CK; history is critical. Electrolyte disturbances (severe hypokalemia) may produce acute weakness and should be screened.

Allergic and drug reactions — DRESS, acute contact dermatitis, severe cellulitic dermatitis — can produce facial and neck swelling. These are usually accompanied by pain, pruritus, systemic features such as fever, eosinophilia (DRESS) and an evocative temporal relationship with a new drug or contact exposure. Skin morphology and mucosal involvement can be diagnostic.

Structural and vascular causes are less inflammatory but important to consider. Superior vena cava syndrome produces facial swelling, plethora and venous distension; a history of malignancy, indwelling central venous devices or mediastinal mass suggests this diagnosis and CT venography confirms it. Thyroid enlargement/goiter, lymphatic obstruction from malignancy, benign orofacial granulomatosis, amyloidosis or sarcoidosis may cause chronic, unilateral or bilateral neck swelling; granulomatous diseases often have systemic features and imaging or biopsy establishes diagnosis.

How to discriminate: practical testing and interpretation
- Bedside and early laboratory tests: CBC, CRP/ESR, CK, liver enzymes, creatinine, urinalysis for myoglobin, serum TSH — a very high CK points toward myositis/rhabdomyolysis; leukocytosis and high CRP with focal neck pain favor bacterial infection.
- Angioedema workup: C4 and quantitative/function assay for C1‑INH, drug history for ACE inhibitors; normal complement studies and no response to antihistamines argue against bradykinin‑mediated or histaminergic angioedema, respectively.
- Autoantibodies: ANA and myositis‑specific panels (eg, anti‑SAE1, anti‑TIF1‑γ, anti‑Mi‑2, antisynthetase, anti‑HMGCR, anti‑SRP) guide diagnosis, phenotype and associated cancer risk.
- Imaging: contrast CT neck is essential to exclude abscess or gas‑forming infection and to define the pattern of soft‑tissue swelling. MRI of muscle identifies edema and guides biopsy; FDG‑PET can both highlight active myositis and screen for occult malignancy.
- Functional testing: video fluoroscopic swallow study quantifies pharyngeal dysphagia and guides aspiration risk/feeding decisions; pulmonary function tests and chest imaging evaluate for aspiration pneumonitis or myositis‑related ILD.
- Electrophysiology and biopsy: EMG can differentiate myopathic from neuromuscular junction disorders; muscle biopsy remains diagnostic in many inflammatory myopathies, revealing pattern‑specific findings (perifascicular atrophy and capillary complement deposition in DM, necrotizing features in IMNM, rimmed vacuoles in IBM).

Applying these discriminators to the case: the patient’s afebrile state, normal white cell count, CT neck without an abscess, markedly raised CK, periungual capillary changes, positive anti‑SAE1 antibody and MRI/PET evidence of diffuse muscle inflammation all pointed away from infection or classic angioedema and toward dermatomyositis. Normal C4 and C1‑INH function excluded hereditary/acquired C1‑INH deficiency. The presence of severe pharyngeal dysphagia and FDG uptake in head and neck musculature highlighted the immediate airway and aspiration risks and justified rapid institution of high‑dose corticosteroids and other immunosuppressive therapies.

Practical takeaways for clinicians
- Do not assume all facial or neck swelling is allergic or infectious. Whenever the presentation is atypical for angioedema (no urticaria, non‑pruritic, poor response to antihistamines/steroids) or when CT shows diffuse soft tissue edema without a drainable collection, broaden the differential.
- Early CK measurement, thorough skin and nailfold examination (capillaroscopy where available), and a targeted autoantibody panel can rapidly distinguish dermatomyositis and other inflammatory myopathies from ENT and allergic diagnoses.
- When myositis is suspected, assess swallowing and airway function promptly; dysphagia may require tube feeding and severe pharyngeal weakness may demand urgent immunosuppression.
- Use a combination of serology, imaging, EMG and muscle biopsy to reach a definitive diagnosis, and initiate malignancy screening according to myositis subtype and risk profile.

In summary, cervical swelling with dysphagia and weakness demands a systematic approach that integrates clinical pattern recognition with directed laboratory and imaging studies. Dermatomyositis should be included in the differential diagnosis of unexplained neck or facial edema — particularly when accompanied by proximal weakness, markedly elevated muscle enzymes, nailfold and cutaneous findings — because early recognition alters both acute management and long‑term surveillance, including cancer screening.
