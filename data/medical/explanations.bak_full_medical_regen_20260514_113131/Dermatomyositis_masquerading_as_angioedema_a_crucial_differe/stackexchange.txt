Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook

### [Clinical Pearls] In a patient with subacute, non-tender neck swelling and no clear structural or infectious cause, what clinical and laboratory features should prompt evaluation for an inflammatory myopathy rather than histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema?

Think “pseudo‑angioedema” when the swelling is subacute and indolent rather than the rapid, pruritic, treatment‑responsive picture of histaminergic angioedema or the abrupt, recurrent, bradykinin‑mediated attacks.

Clinically, key red flags that should prompt evaluation for an inflammatory myopathy are a weeks‑long, non‑tender, non‑fluctuant neck or facial swelling that does not follow the typical hourly onset/resolution of allergic angioedema, lacks urticaria or marked pruritus, and fails to respond to antihistamines/epinephrine (or to bradykinin‑directed therapy if that has been tried). Concomitant features that point away from primary angioedema and toward myositis are proximal muscle weakness (even subtle), myalgias, dysphagia or cough/aspiration symptoms, and cutaneous findings of dermatomyositis (heliotrope, Gottron’s signs, shawl/V‑neck/holster erythema, periungual changes/nailfold capillary abnormalities). A subacute systemic picture with mild‑to‑moderate inflammatory markers (CRP), normal C4 and normal C1‑esterase levels/function, and absence of hypogammaglobulinaemia also argues against classic complement‑driven or hereditary angioedema.

Laboratory and imaging clues to seek early are a markedly elevated creatine kinase (CK) and raised muscle enzymes (AST, ALT, LDH), which are strong pointers to muscle inflammation; positive ANA or myositis‑specific/associated antibodies (eg, SAE1 in this case) support the diagnosis; MRI or FDG‑PET showing muscle edema or increased uptake in head/neck or proximal limb muscles confirms active myositis; and muscle biopsy can provide definitive histopathology (perifascicular atrophy, C5b‑9 deposition) when needed.

Putting it together in practice: in any patient with unexplained, subacute neck/face swelling, do a focused skin and neuromuscular exam and get a CK and transaminases promptly while checking C4 and C1‑INH to exclude bradykinin causes. If CK is elevated or examination reveals proximal weakness or dermatomyositis stigmata, escalate to myositis serologies, muscle MRI/biopsy and start immunologic assessment — because the treatment and urgency (immunosuppression for myositis with risk of dysphagia/airway‑muscle involvement) are entirely different from therapies for histaminergic or bradykinin angioedema.

### [Differential Diagnosis] What bedside or historical clues help distinguish pseudoangioedema due to dermatomyositis from true angioedema (histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated)?

Think of angioedema as a clinical syndrome and ask whether the history and exam fit a vascular permeability event (histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated) or something else causing soft‑tissue bulk such as myositis with secondary edema. The key bedside and historical clues that point away from true angioedema and toward “pseudoangioedema” from dermatomyositis are clinical pattern, associated systemic findings, temporal course, response to treatment, and a few simple laboratory checks.

Timing and evolution. True histaminergic angioedema typically evolves over minutes to hours and waxes and wanes; bradykinin‑mediated angioedema classically develops over hours and may progress over 24–72 hours. Subacute, steadily progressive neck or facial swelling over days to weeks is atypical for either and should prompt search for alternative causes such as inflammatory myopathy.

Symptoms and local findings. Angioedema is usually pruritic (if histaminergic) or painful/tight with mucosal involvement and often associated with urticaria or systemic allergic features; bradykinin attacks lack pruritus and hives but commonly produce mucosal and oropharyngeal swelling (tongue, larynx) that threatens airway. By contrast, dermatomyositis–related swelling is often non‑tender, nonpruritic, and may spare mucosal surfaces; flexible nasendoscopy may show increased secretions and impaired swallowing rather than discrete mucosal edema or laryngeal obstruction. Distribution that is regional (eg, predominant neck/face with associated muscle groups) rather than the classic periorbital, lip, or tongue prominence of angioedema is a red flag.

Associated cutaneous and neuromuscular signs. Look carefully for subtle heliotrope/violaceous facial erythema (including over nasolabial folds), shawl/V‑neck or Holster signs, periungual erythema or nailfold capillary changes, and Gottron’s papules. Even when classic findings are absent, any proximal muscle weakness (difficulty rising, hip‑flexor weakness, dysphagia) should point away from isolated angioedema and toward an inflammatory myopathy.

Response to therapy. Lack of prompt response to standard angioedema treatments (antihistamines, corticosteroids for histaminergic attacks, epinephrine, or icatibant/C1‑inhibitor for bradykinin attacks) argues against those mechanisms. Dermatomyositis‑related swelling responds to immunosuppression (high‑dose steroids, IVIG, steroid‑sparing agents).

Simple bedside/lab discriminators. Serum CK and transaminases are inexpensive, rapidly available tests that are often massively elevated in myositis and would not be explained by angioedema. Complement studies and C1‑esterase inhibitor levels and function help exclude hereditary or acquired bradykinin angioedema (normal in the case presented). Absence of offending drugs (ACE inhibitors, DPP‑4 inhibitors) or allergic triggers, and a lack of personal/family history of angioedema, further lowers the pretest probability of true angioedema.

Imaging and scope findings. Cross‑sectional imaging in dermatomyositis may show diffuse soft‑tissue and muscle inflammation (edema), whereas CT in angioedema more often shows subcutaneous/mucosal swelling without the pattern of muscle involvement. Endoscopy/swallow studies may reveal dysphagia from weakened pharyngeal musculature rather than mechanical obstruction.

Putting it together: when neck or facial “angioedema” is subacute, nonpruritic, nonresponsive to standard angioedema therapy, associated with proximal weakness, dysphagia, periungual or violaceous rashes, or markedly raised CK/transaminases, think dermatomyositis. In that circumstance obtain CK, examine nailfolds and proximal strength, check complement/C1INH if angioedema remains a concern, and involve rheumatology/immunology early — because the treatment paradigm and urgency (immunosuppression to prevent muscle, lung, and swallowing complications) differ completely from management of histaminergic or bradykinin angioedema.

### [Morning Report] Which screening laboratory tests are most informative in the initial workup of suspected inflammatory myopathy presenting with neck swelling and dysphagia?

Key point up front: in any patient with unexplained neck “angioedema” plus weakness or dysphagia you should immediately think myositis and order a creatine kinase (CK) — it is the single most informative screening test and will rapidly distinguish skeletal‑muscle inflammation from histaminergic/bradykinin angioedema or isolated soft‑tissue infection.

Why and what to send initially
Start with CK because it directly reflects muscle injury and, when markedly elevated, pivots the differential toward an inflammatory myopathy (as in this case, CK >16,000 U/L). Alongside CK, obtain AST/ALT and LDH (these are often elevated from muscle, not only liver), and consider aldolase if available — these help confirm myopathic rather than purely hepatic pathology and give additional baseline severity markers. A spot urine for myoglobin and serum creatinine are needed to assess for rhabdomyolysis and renal risk.

For inflammatory/autoimmune evaluation, order an ANA and a myositis antibody panel (myositis‑specific and myositis‑associated antibodies) early — a positive panel (eg, SAE1 in this patient) both supports the diagnosis and predicts phenotype (dysphagia, ILD, malignancy risk) and therapy decisions. Basic inflammatory markers (CRP, ESR) and a CBC are useful to gauge systemic inflammation and exclude infectious mimics.

Because neck swelling commonly triggers an angioedema workup, get C4 and C1‑esterase level/function if angioedema remains on the table — normal results steer you away from complement‑mediated causes. Finally, perform baseline infection and immunosuppression screening (HBsAg, anti‑HBc, anti‑HCV, HIV, and TB testing) before initiating high‑dose immunosuppression.

How these tests guide management
A very high CK (with supportive AST/ALT, LDH, aldolase elevations) prompts urgent immunosuppressive therapy and further myositis‑directed testing (MRI, EMG, myositis antibody panel, and muscle biopsy as indicated). A positive myositis antibody helps risk‑stratify for ILD and cancer screening and can influence steroid‑sparing choices. Normal complement/C1 results reduce the likelihood of hereditary/acquired angioedema and refocus evaluation on myositis.

Concise checklist for the initial laboratory screen
Order immediately: CK, AST, ALT, LDH (± aldolase), serum creatinine, urine myoglobin; CBC, CRP/ESR; ANA and myositis antibody panel; C4 and C1‑esterase level/function if angioedema considered; baseline infection screens (HBV, HCV, HIV, TB). These tests will rapidly separate inflammatory myopathy from infectious or angioedema causes and provide the baseline needed before immunosuppression.

### [Clinical Pearls] What characteristic cutaneous findings of dermatomyositis should be sought even when Gottron’s papules and heliotrope rash are absent?

Look beyond the “textbook” Gottron’s papules and heliotrope rash — inspect for the more subtle, but characteristic, cutaneous stigmata of dermatomyositis that often guide diagnosis.

Classically look for the photosensitive poikilodermatous rashes: the shawl sign (erythema/poikiloderma over the posterior neck and upper back) and the V‑neck sign (similar distribution over the anterior chest). Also seek the Holster sign (violaceous erythema over the lateral thighs), periungual changes (periungual erythema, ragged cuticles, capillary dilatation and micro‑hemorrhages on nailfold capillaroscopy), and mechanic’s‑hand changes on the lateral/radial fingers. Other helpful findings are diffuse facial violaceous erythema (especially involving the nasolabial folds), scalp involvement, and generalized poikiloderma.

Clinically this matters because these subtler signs — as in this case, where periungual capillary abnormalities and a Holster sign were present — should prompt measurement of muscle enzymes, myositis serologies, and timely malignancy and pulmonary evaluation. Early recognition of these non‑classic skin findings speeds diagnosis and initiation of immunosuppression, which can prevent serious complications such as severe myositis, dysphagia, aspiration, and interstitial lung disease.

### [Differential Diagnosis] How do normal C4 and normal quantitative/function C1 esterase inhibitor results affect the likelihood of hereditary or acquired bradykinin-mediated angioedema in this presentation?

Normal serum C4 and normal quantitative and functional C1‑esterase inhibitor (C1‑INH) testing in this setting make the common forms of bradykinin‑mediated angioedema — namely hereditary angioedema types I and II and acquired C1‑INH deficiency — very unlikely. The pathophysiology of those disorders produces low C4 (consumption) and either low C1‑INH level (type I) or dysfunctional C1‑INH with abnormal function testing (type II); acquired C1‑INH deficiency usually also has low C1q. Because those complementary abnormalities are absent here, there is no biochemical evidence for C1‑INH‑mediated, bradykinin‑driven swelling.

That said, a few caveats matter for clinical reasoning. C4 and C1‑INH are most reliably abnormal during an acute attack, so if tests were obtained well outside an episode their sensitivity falls; repeating C4 during a recurrence increases diagnostic yield. Second, there is a rare group of hereditary angioedema with normal C1‑INH (HAE‑nC1‑INH) caused by mutations in FXII, plasminogen, angiopoietin‑1, kininogen and others; these patients have normal C4/C1‑INH testing and require genetic testing or specialty referral when clinical suspicion is high (recurrent, family history, laryngeal or abdominal attacks). Finally, acquired bradykinin angioedema from ACE inhibitors or DPP‑4 inhibitors would not change complement/C1‑INH testing but is excluded clinically here by the absence of those drugs.

In this patient the history and exam were atypical for bradykinin angioedema (no medication trigger, no prior recurrent attacks or family history), the complement/C1‑INH studies were normal, and alternative explanations (marked myositis with inflammation and tissue oedema from dermatomyositis) better explain the neck swelling. Therefore bradykinin‑mediated angioedema is effectively ruled out in routine practice; only if there were recurrent unexplained angioedema despite normal testing would workup for HAE‑nC1‑INH or repeat testing during an attack be indicated.

### [Clinical Pearls] What nailfold capillaroscopy findings support a diagnosis of dermatomyositis, and how do they correlate with disease activity or complications?

Key nailfold capillaroscopy abnormalities that point toward dermatomyositis are the same microvascular changes you look for in other microangiopathic connective‑tissue diseases: enlarged (dilated or “giant”) capillary loops, periungual and nailfold microhaemorrhages, loss of capillary density with avascular areas, and abnormal capillary morphology (tortuosity, bushy/ramified capillaries and neoangiogenesis). In dermatomyositis these changes are typically most prominent at the periungual nailfolds and are best seen with videocapillaroscopy.

The clinical relevance is twofold. First, the pattern reflects disease activity: giant capillaries and fresh microhaemorrhages correlate with active endothelial injury and are associated with active cutaneous and myositis flares and with elevated muscle enzymes. Second, capillary loss/avascular areas and compensatory neoangiogenesis tend to signify more severe or chronic microvascular damage and have been linked to greater overall disease severity and complications — in particular interstitial lung disease, more refractory myositis, and a worse prognosis. Serial capillaroscopy can therefore be useful to support a diagnosis when the clinical picture is unclear (as in this case masquerading as angioedema), to corroborate biochemical and biopsy evidence of active disease, and to monitor response to therapy as haemorrhages and giant loops diminish while capillary density may recover with effective treatment.

In the presented case the presence of periungual microhaemorrhages together with avascular areas was concordant with active dermatomyositis with significant muscle and pharyngeal involvement, and supported the clinicians’ decision to escalate immunosuppression urgently.

### [Pathophysiology] Which myositis-specific antibodies are classically associated with dermatomyositis, and what clinical phenotype is most often linked to anti-SAE1 positivity?

The phrase “myositis‑specific antibodies” refers to a set of autoantibodies that help define dermatomyositis (DM) phenotypes and guide prognosis and evaluation. The antibodies classically associated with dermatomyositis include Mi‑2, transcription intermediary factor‑1 gamma (TIF1‑γ, also called p155/140), melanoma differentiation‑associated gene 5 (MDA‑5), nuclear matrix protein NXP2, and small ubiquitin‑like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1). (By contrast, the histidyl‑tRNA synthetase antibody Jo‑1 and other antisynthetase antibodies are more typical of the antisynthetase syndrome and overlap myositis/ILD phenotypes.)

Anti‑SAE1 is most often linked to a “skin‑dominant” phenotype with prominent cutaneous findings (heliotrope, shawl/V‑neck signs, Gottron’s changes) and a clinical pattern in which severe skin disease may precede—or occur with—muscle involvement. Important associated features are frequent dysphagia and a substantial risk of interstitial lung disease; malignancy has been reported at a higher-than‑background rate (roughly one‑in‑five in pooled series). In practice, anti‑SAE1 patients can present with prominent cutaneous inflammation and early severe dysphagia or later progressive myositis, and they warrant careful surveillance for aspiration risk, ILD, and occult cancer. 

In this case the combination of subtle periungual and facial changes, very high CK, severe dysphagia and the positive SAE1 antibody fits that recognized anti‑SAE1 phenotype and explains the need for rapid immunosuppression and targeted screening (swallow evaluation, chest imaging and age‑appropriate malignancy workup).

### [Pathophysiology] What muscle biopsy features are pathognomonic or highly characteristic of dermatomyositis, and how do they differ from immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy and inclusion body myositis?

The key diagnostic histopathology in dermatomyositis reflects a microvascular, complement‑mediated process that preferentially injures the perifascicular region of the muscle. Classically you see perifascicular atrophy or degeneration of myofibers (ischemic‑appearing myofibres clustered at the edges of fascicles), perivascular and perimysial inflammation (usually B cells, plasmacytoid dendritic cells and some CD4+ T cells rather than a heavy endomysial CD8+ infiltrate), loss of endomysial capillaries, and deposition of membrane attack complex (C5b‑9) in endomysial capillaries and perifascicular fibers. Immunohistochemical expression of myxovirus resistance protein A (MxA) in perifascicular fibers is a sensitive and specific marker for type I interferon activation in dermatomyositis and supports the diagnosis when present.

Contrast that with immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM): IMNM is dominated by myofiber necrosis and regeneration with relatively little lymphocytic inflammation. The picture is one of widespread necrosis (not preferentially perifascicular), abundant macrophages clearing necrotic fibers, and often myofiber membrane (sarcolemmal) complement deposition, but without the microvascular capillary destruction and perifascicular atrophy characteristic of dermatomyositis. IMNM is commonly associated with autoantibodies such as anti‑SRP or anti‑HMGCR and with markedly elevated CK — the morphology is necrotic/regenerative rather than ischemic/perifascicular.

Inclusion body myositis (IBM) is again different: it shows prominent endomysial inflammation with CD8+ cytotoxic T cells that invade non‑necrotic muscle fibers, patchy myofiber atrophy, and characteristic degenerative features — rimmed vacuoles and intracellular protein aggregates that contain amyloid precursor protein, phosphorylated tau, TDP‑43 and congophilic/acid‑fast inclusions on special stains. Mitochondrial abnormalities (COX‑negative fibers) are common. Clinically IBM is slowly progressive, often with distal and finger flexor weakness, and the histology combines inflammatory and degenerative changes not seen in dermatomyositis.

Applying this to the case: the biopsy’s perifascicular necrotic and regenerative fibers with perimysial inflammation plus C5b‑9 deposition in perifascicular capillaries is classic for dermatomyositis and distinguishes it from IMNM (which would show diffuse necrosis with scant lymphocytes) and from IBM (which would show endomysial CD8+ invasion, rimmed vacuoles and protein aggregates). In practice, integrating the pattern of fiber injury (perifascicular vs diffuse necrosis vs endomysial invasion), the predominant inflammatory cell types, complement deposition location, and ancillary stains (MxA, congophilic or protein aggregate stains) yields the correct pathophysiologic diagnosis.

### [Pathophysiology] Describe the proposed pathophysiologic mechanism of dermatomyositis involving complement activation and membrane attack complex (C5b-9) deposition, and how this leads to perifascicular injury.

The dominant model for dermatomyositis (DM) is a complement-mediated microangiopathy that injures muscle by targeting the muscle microvasculature rather than the myofiber as the primary event. Clinically and on biopsy this explains the characteristic perifascicular pattern of injury and the associated soft‑tissue oedema.

Immunologically, circulating autoantibodies or immune complexes bind to antigens on endomysial capillary/endothelial surfaces (classically thought to involve the classical complement pathway). This triggers complement activation with formation and deposition of the membrane attack complex (C5b-9) on endomysial capillaries. C5b-9 insertion damages endothelial cells, producing capillary dysfunction, increased permeability and ultimately capillary drop-out.

Loss of capillary perfusion produces focal ischemia in the muscle fascicle regions most vulnerable to reduced microvascular flow — the perifascicular zones at the fascicle periphery — leading to fiber hypoxia, atrophy, necrosis and regeneration that are seen histologically as perifascicular atrophy/necrosis. Simultaneously, endothelial injury increases vascular permeability and releases inflammatory mediators, promoting interstitial and subcutaneous edema and recruiting perivascular/perimysial inflammatory cells (often histiocytes and CD4+ T cells). The combined effect of microvascular loss (ischemic myofiber injury) plus inflammatory edema accounts for both the perifascicular distribution of muscle damage and the clinically apparent swelling (for example the cervical soft‑tissue edema that can mimic angioedema).

In short: antibody‑mediated complement activation → C5b-9 deposition on endomysial capillaries → endothelial injury and capillary loss → focal ischemia of perifascicular fibers → perifascicular atrophy/necrosis, plus increased permeability and inflammation → muscle edema and the soft‑tissue swelling seen in some patients with dermatomyositis.

### [Pathophysiology] Why can AST and ALT be elevated in inflammatory myopathies, and which pattern (AST vs ALT) typically predominates?

Transaminase elevations in inflammatory myopathies come from injured muscle, not from primary hepatocellular injury. Both alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) are present in skeletal muscle, but AST is more abundant (present in both cytosol and mitochondria) and is released in larger amounts with myofibre necrosis. Active myositis therefore commonly causes an isolated transaminase rise in which AST predominates over ALT, often with a raised CK and LDH and without cholestatic markers (GGT, ALP) or synthetic dysfunction to suggest primary liver disease.

Clinically this matters because an AST>ALT (AST:ALT >1) with markedly elevated CK points to muscle as the source. To distinguish muscle vs liver origin, compare transaminases to CK, check GGT/ALP/bilirubin and INR, and review history/medications. If the picture remains unclear, consider viral hepatitis testing and liver imaging, but remember that normalization of CK with treatment of myositis usually brings transaminases down as well.

### [Differential Diagnosis] What is the differential diagnosis for subacute neck swelling with elevated inflammatory markers and no overt airway lesion on nasendoscopy?

When you see subacute neck swelling with raised inflammatory markers but a normal flexible nasendoscopy, think anatomically and mechanistically beyond mucosal or intraluminal airway lesions. The absence of an overt airway lesion pushes the likely pathology to the subcutaneous tissues, muscles, lymphatics, salivary glands, vasculature or mediastinum — and your differential should be broad but prioritised by acuity and clues on exam.

Infectious/inflammatory deep‑tissue causes remain first to consider: deep neck space infections (parapharyngeal, retropharyngeal, submandibular/Ludwig’s) or cellulitis and sialadenitis may present with fever and CRP elevation but often have focal tenderness, systemic signs or CT evidence of fluid collections. Necrotising fasciitis is rare but important to exclude when pain is out of proportion or there is rapid deterioration. Suppurative lymphadenitis and tuberculous lymphadenitis can be subacute and less obvious on endoscopy.

Autoimmune and inflammatory conditions that primarily affect subcutaneous tissues or muscle can mimic angioedema and produce elevated inflammatory markers: inflammatory myopathies (dermatomyositis and other myositis variants) can cause pronounced muscle and soft‑tissue edema of the neck (as in this case); connective‑tissue disease–associated oedema (eg, dermal mucin from hypothyroid myxedema, lupus, or urticarial/vasculitic processes such as hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis) and drug‑related hypersensitivity (DRESS, delayed-type reactions) also present subacutely. Orofacial granulomatosis and sarcoidosis should be considered when swelling is chronic and noninfectious.

Vascular, obstructive and neoplastic causes: superior vena cava or brachiocephalic obstruction and venous thrombosis produce facial/neck fullness and raised inflammatory markers if secondary thrombophlebitis is present. Lymphoma, metastatic lymphadenopathy, salivary gland tumours, thyroid goitre with retrosternal extension or mediastinal masses may cause subacute swelling without mucosal lesions and often have systemic B symptoms or progressive course.

Less common but relevant diagnoses: histaminergic or bradykinin‑mediated angioedema (hereditary or drug‑related) — usually episodic, pruritic or with known triggers and normal inflammatory markers — and neuromuscular disorders causing pooling/secretions (eg, bulbar myasthenia) which may be mistaken for “swelling” by patients.

How to prioritise investigation and bedside reasoning: absence of mucosal lesion → favour subcutaneous/muscular/mediastinal/vascular causes. Look carefully for skin signs (heliotrope, Gottron’s papules, periungual changes), focal tenderness, erythema, crepitus, lymphadenopathy, and systemic signs. Early targeted tests are contrast CT or MRI of neck (to define collections, muscle oedema, masses or vascular obstruction), CK and myositis serologies when myopathy or subtle skin findings are present, blood cultures and inflammatory markers if infection suspected, C1 esterase/function and complement levels if angioedema is considered, and PET/CT or excisional node biopsy when malignancy is a concern. Consultation with ENT, rheumatology/immunology and oncology should be guided by these results.

In short: keep deep neck infection, sialadenitis/lymphadenitis, inflammatory myopathies (dermatomyositis prominently), connective‑tissue/vasculitic causes, vascular obstruction/thrombosis, and neoplasm at the top of the differential when nasendoscopy is unrevealing, and use imaging plus directed labs (CK/myositis panel, complement/C1 tests, cultures, biopsy/PET as needed) to distinguish them.

### [Morning Report] Which MRI and FDG-PET findings support active inflammatory myopathy, and how can these modalities guide biopsy site selection?

In inflammatory myopathies you are looking for imaging evidence of active inflammation (edema, hyperemia, glucose avidity) rather than chronic irreversible change (fatty atrophy, fibrosis). On MRI, active inflammatory myopathy is most reliably signalled by muscle oedema—high signal on fluid‑sensitive sequences (STIR or fat‑suppressed T2) and corresponding gadolinium enhancement of muscle and sometimes fascia. Perifascicular or patchy distributions (eg, perifascicular T2/STIR hyperintensity in dermatomyositis) and signal in muscles that are clinically weak point to active disease; by contrast, T1 hyperintense fatty replacement and volume loss indicate chronic damage and are poor biopsy targets.

FDG‑PET supports active myositis when there is abnormal increased FDG uptake in skeletal muscle that is focal or patchy (or diffuse when disease burden is high), especially when uptake co‑localizes with MRI oedema and with clinical weakness. PET adds whole‑body mapping (including head/neck or trunk muscles that MRI may not cover) and simultaneously screens for occult malignancy.

How this guides biopsy selection: choose a muscle that shows active inflammatory signals (STIR/T2 hyperintensity and/or focal increased FDG uptake) and is accessible and safe to sample. Targeting an area with concordant MRI oedema and FDG uptake maximizes the likelihood of finding diagnostic histopathology (inflammatory infiltrates, necrosis, complement deposition) and avoids sampling chronically fibrotic or fat‑replaced muscle, which yields false‑negative results. Also prioritize muscles with recent clinical weakness and avoid muscles that have been heavily treated with high‑dose steroids (which can suppress imaging and histologic activity). In practice use MRI to localize oedematous, perifascicular or patchy lesions in proximal muscles and use FDG‑PET to confirm activity and identify additional sites (eg, head/neck or forearm muscles) when MRI is limited; then perform biopsy from the concordant, accessible site for optimal diagnostic yield.

### [Pathophysiology] What pulmonary function test pattern suggests extrathoracic (upper airway or pharyngeal) involvement in inflammatory myopathy, and how does dysphagia contribute to pulmonary complications?

Pulmonary function testing in inflammatory myopathies can point to two different mechanisms of respiratory compromise: (1) intrinsic lung disease (eg, interstitial lung disease) and (2) neuromuscular or upper‑airway dysfunction. Extrathoracic (upper airway/pharyngeal) involvement classically produces signs of variable extrathoracic obstruction on the flow–volume loop — most notably flattening of the inspiratory limb with a reduced peak inspiratory flow and a blunted inspiratory curve while the expiratory limb is relatively preserved. This pattern is not reversible with bronchodilator and is distinguished from fixed central obstruction (both limbs flattened) or intrathoracic obstruction (flattened expiratory limb). In addition, neuromuscular involvement of the respiratory pump produces a restrictive physiology: reduced FVC and TLC with preserved or increased FEV1/FVC, low maximal inspiratory and expiratory pressures (MIP/MEP), and sometimes a fall in FVC when supine. Together, an inspiratory‑limb flattening on flow–volume testing plus a restrictive pattern and low respiratory pressures suggests combined upper‑airway/pharyngeal muscle weakness and global respiratory muscle involvement.

Dysphagia contributes to pulmonary complications by permitting oropharyngeal secretions and swallowed material to penetrate and be aspirated into the airways. Impaired pharyngeal contraction, poor laryngeal elevation and defective cough/clearance lead to residue in the pyriform sinuses and transient penetration or frank aspiration during swallowing, causing acute aspiration pneumonitis and recurrent micro‑aspiration. Repeated aspiration produces peribronchial consolidation, chemical pneumonitis, secondary bacterial infection and, over time, chronic airway inflammation that may mimic or worsen interstitial changes and lead to bronchiectasis. In this case the video‑fluoroscopic evidence of severe pharyngeal residue with penetration, the inspiratory‑limb abnormality and the CT peribronchial consolidation all fit that pathophysiology — which is why early recognition of dysphagia, objective swallow testing, protective feeding measures and therapies to improve or protect the airway are as important as immunosuppression for the myositis.

### [Management] What features of this case would prompt urgent high-dose glucocorticoid therapy, and what initial dosing regimens are appropriate for severe bulbar or airway muscle involvement?

The decision to institute urgent, high‑dose glucocorticoid therapy in inflammatory myopathy rests on the presence of organ‑threatening muscle inflammation — not the CK number alone. In this patient several clinical and investigation findings signal imminent or actual threat and therefore justify immediate pulse steroids: progressive bulbar dysfunction with severe pharyngeal dysphagia and documented aspiration on video‑fluoroscopy; prominent head‑and‑neck muscle inflammation producing expanding cervical soft‑tissue swelling that risked airway compromise; objective proximal muscle weakness; radiologic and functional evidence of respiratory and head/neck muscle involvement (FDG‑PET and extrathoracic restrictive pattern); and systemic biochemical evidence of active necrotising myositis (very high CK and transaminases). Together these features imply rapid, destructive inflammation of muscles that maintain airway protection and ventilation — a situation in which delay risks aspiration pneumonitis, respiratory failure and permanent disability.

For such severe bulbar or airway‑muscle involvement the usual initial approach is immediate intravenous high‑dose “pulse” methylprednisolone to achieve rapid immunosuppression, then early transition to high‑dose oral prednisone with a plan for steroid‑sparing agents and adjunctive therapies (IVIG, rituximab, plasmapheresis) as indicated. Reasonable regimens used in practice and supported by case series/guidelines are methylprednisolone 1000 mg IV daily for 3 days (or 500–1000 mg daily for 3–5 days) followed by oral prednisone approximately 1 mg/kg/day (frequently 60–80 mg daily in adults) with gradual taper guided by clinical response and CK. If IV pulse therapy is not available or the presentation is less fulminant, start oral prednisone 1 mg/kg/day (maximum ~80 mg) promptly. Because bulbar disease and dysphagia confer high risk of aspiration and slow steroid response, concurrent early use of IVIG (eg, 2 g/kg total, often given as 0.4 g/kg/day ×5 or split doses) and consideration of rituximab or plasmapheresis should be made rather than waiting for steroid effect alone.

Finally, management must be multidisciplinary: secure the airway as clinically indicated, involve ENT/anaesthetics and speech/swallow therapy, initiate aspiration precautions and nutrition support (NG or feeding tube), and begin infection and PJP prophylaxis when prolonged high‑dose immunosuppression is planned. In the reported case, the team appropriately administered methylprednisolone 1 g daily for 3 days, then prednisone 60 mg daily with IVIG and steroid‑sparing agents — an approach consistent with the need for rapid control of life‑threatening bulbar and airway muscle inflammation.

### [Management] What is the rationale for adding intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) in dermatomyositis with severe dysphagia, and what dosing strategies are commonly used?

Think of IVIG in severe dermatomyositis—particularly when bulbar/airway muscles are involved and there is risk of aspiration or respiratory failure—as a rapid, disease‑modifying adjunct that can bridge patients through the highest‑risk period while slower‑acting steroid‑sparing agents take effect.

Why IVIG? The clinical problem in this case is active immune‑mediated injury to muscle and microvasculature producing severe pharyngeal weakness, aspiration pneumonitis and neck soft‑tissue inflammation. IVIG has multiple immunomodulatory effects (Fc receptor blockade, neutralization of pathogenic autoantibodies, inhibition of complement activation and modulation of cytokine and cellular immune responses) that target the pathogenic mechanisms implicated in dermatomyositis (including complement‑mediated capillary injury). Clinically, IVIG often produces relatively rapid improvement in strength and bulbar function and reduces aspiration risk when added to high‑dose glucocorticoids — important when dysphagia is severe enough to require enteral feeding or when airway muscles are threatened. Because standard steroid‑sparing agents (mycophenolate, azathioprine, methotrexate) and B‑cell therapy (rituximab) take weeks to months to work, IVIG is commonly used as a bridge or in refractory disease.

What does the evidence and guideline practice support? Randomized and open‑label studies and expert guidelines support IVIG as an effective option in refractory or severe dermatomyositis, including for bulbar involvement. High‑quality data are limited, but IVIG is widely accepted in practice for severe dysphagia or respiratory compromise and was used appropriately in this SAE1‑positive patient together with pulse steroids and steroid‑sparing agents.

Common dosing strategies you will see in clinical practice
- Loading regimen: total 2 g/kg (actual or adjusted body weight; use ideal or adjusted body weight in obesity) delivered as 0.4 g/kg/day for 5 days, or 2 g/kg given over 2–5 days. This is the usual acute/induction approach for severe disease (airway/bulbar/respiratory involvement).
- Maintenance regimen: repeated 1–2 g/kg every 3–4 weeks depending on response. Many centres give 2 g/kg every 4 weeks for the first several cycles (as in this case: initial 2 g/kg over 5 days, then 2 g/kg monthly), then taper frequency or dose as clinical control and steroid‑sparing agents permit.
- Alternative split dosing: some providers split the 2 g/kg dose over several days each month (for example, 1 g/kg split as 0.5 g/kg on two consecutive days every 4 weeks) to ease infusion logistics or reduce infusion‑related adverse effects.

Practical points and safety
Continue high‑dose glucocorticoids and start/continue steroid‑sparing therapy (mycophenolate, azathioprine, rituximab as indicated); IVIG is adjunctive. Screen for and mitigate IVIG risks: assess renal function (avoid rapid high‑concentration products in severe renal impairment), assess thrombotic risk and hydration, monitor for infusion reactions, and dose on ideal/adjusted body weight in obesity to limit excessive dosing. Repeat clinical reassessment after 1–3 cycles to decide on ongoing monthly IVIG versus tapering.

In short, IVIG is indicated in dermatomyositis when bulbar/airway involvement threatens nutrition or airway/respiratory function or when rapid immunomodulation is needed. Typical practice is a 2 g/kg induction (0.4 g/kg/day x5 or equivalent) followed by 1–2 g/kg every 3–4 weeks until disease control allows de‑escalation.

### [Management] In a dermatomyositis patient with abnormal baseline pulmonary function, why might mycophenolate mofetil be preferred over methotrexate as a steroid-sparing agent?

Teaching pearl:

In a patient with dermatomyositis and abnormal baseline pulmonary function, mycophenolate mofetil is often chosen over methotrexate because the balance of risks and benefits favors an agent less likely to harm the lungs. methotrexate is well‑recognized to cause or worsen pulmonary toxicity — from hypersensitivity pneumonitis to chronic interstitial lung disease and fibrosis — and can precipitate clinically important respiratory decline in patients with pre‑existing lung disease. By contrast, mycophenolate has a more favourable pulmonary safety profile and is commonly used as a steroid‑sparing agent for inflammatory myopathies, including those with connective‑tissue‑related ILD, where it can both permit glucocorticoid tapering and help control lung inflammation.

Put simply: with an abnormal PFT (restrictive physiology) and prior chest imaging showing parenchymal abnormalities and aspiration changes, exposing the patient to methotrexate carries a nontrivial risk of provoking or accelerating methotrexate‑related pneumonitis or fibrosis. Mycophenolate achieves immunosuppression without that specific pulmonary toxicity signal, making it the safer choice here. This decision should be framed within shared decision‑making and accompanied by appropriate monitoring (clinical respiratory assessment, interval PFTs and imaging, CBC and renal function for mycophenolate), and revisited if the clinical course or new data warrant a different steroid‑sparing strategy.

### [Management] What are indications to consider rituximab in dermatomyositis, and what dosing schedule is commonly used?

Think of rituximab in dermatomyositis as a targeted “rescue” or steroid‑sparing option for patients with disease that is severe, organ‑threatening, or insufficiently controlled by first‑line therapy.

Why and when to consider it: use rituximab for disease that is refractory to glucocorticoids plus a conventional steroid‑sparing agent (eg, methotrexate, azathioprine, mycophenolate), for steroid‑dependent disease when you cannot taper without relapse, or when there are severe extramuscular manifestations that put the patient at risk (rapidly progressive interstitial lung disease, severe oropharyngeal weakness/dysphagia with aspiration risk, severe cutaneous disease or refractory calcinosis). It is also reasonable to consider earlier in patients with myositis associated with specific autoantibody phenotypes or clinical features that predict poor response to conventional agents, or when other agents are contraindicated (eg, significant lung disease limiting methotrexate use) — exactly the situation in this case (marked dysphagia/airway muscle involvement, ILD concerns, and need for rapid control). The pivotal randomized RIM trial did not meet its primary end point but showed clinically meaningful responses in many refractory patients, so rituximab is accepted for refractory or severe myositis in contemporary practice.

Common dosing schedules: two standard regimens are used. The most commonly employed adult regimen is 1000 mg IV on day 0 and day 14 (two infusions 2 weeks apart). An alternative regimen is 375 mg/m2 IV weekly for four weeks. For maintenance some clinicians give 500–1000 mg every 6 months based on clinical response and B‑cell reconstitution, but maintenance is individualized.

Safety and practical points: screen for hepatitis B (HBsAg, anti‑HBc), check baseline immunoglobulins, assess TB risk, update vaccines before therapy if possible, and arrange infection prophylaxis as indicated. Monitor clinical response (strength, CK, pulmonary function, dysphagia) and B‑cell recovery if using maintenance dosing. In the presented patient — severe dysphagia, significant muscle involvement, need to avoid methotrexate because of lung concerns, and steroid‑intolerant course — rituximab given as 1000 mg ×2 two weeks apart was a rational choice and matches common practice.

### [Management] What prophylaxis should be considered for Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia in patients receiving high-dose steroids and additional immunosuppression, and what are reasonable alternatives if sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim is not tolerated?

When you put a patient on prolonged high‑dose glucocorticoids plus another significant immunosuppressant (eg, mycophenolate, rituximab, cyclophosphamide), you must assume materially increased risk for Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP) and provide prophylaxis. The preferred agent is sulfamethoxazole–trimethoprim (SMX–TMP) because it is the most effective and also covers other opportunists and some bacterial urinary infections.

Practical approach and rationale: give SMX–TMP unless there is a clear contraindication (severe allergy, life‑threatening sulfa reaction, severe drug‑induced cytopenia, or hemolysis as in this case). Typical prophylactic regimens are trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole 1 double‑strength tablet (800/160 mg) three times weekly or one double‑strength tablet daily; alternatively one single‑strength tablet (400/80 mg) daily is commonly used. Continue prophylaxis while the patient is receiving ≥20 mg prednisone (or equivalent) daily for more than ~4 weeks, or while on other sustained immunosuppression; stop when the steroid dose is safely tapered below that threshold and additional immunosuppressants have been discontinued or immune function recovered.

Reasonable alternatives when SMX–TMP is not tolerated
If SMX–TMP cannot be used, the accepted alternatives are inhaled (nebulized) pentamidine 300 mg once monthly, oral dapsone (50–100 mg daily or 100 mg nightly), and oral atovaquone (1,500 mg once daily with food). Choice is driven by patient comorbidities and the failure mode that precludes SMX–TMP: dapsone requires G6PD testing because it causes hemolysis in deficiency and can cause methemoglobinemia; atovaquone is well tolerated but more expensive and requires ingestion with fatty food for absorption; inhaled pentamidine provides only pulmonary protection (less reliable for extrapulmonary disease), may be poorly tolerated in patients with airflow limitation, and carries local and systemic side effects. Discuss with pharmacy/infectious diseases if drug–drug interactions or cytopenias are concerns (SMX–TMP can worsen marrow suppression when combined with some agents).

In this patient, hemolytic anemia developed with SMX–TMP despite negative G6PD testing, so switching to monthly nebulized pentamidine (300 mg) was a reasonable alternative and is what was used successfully.

### [Management] How should nutrition and airway protection be managed in dermatomyositis patients with severe oropharyngeal dysphagia, and which tests guide these decisions?

In dermatomyositis the problem is not a structural obstructing lesion but neuromuscular failure of the oropharyngeal and upper‑esophageal musculature. Management therefore has two parallel goals: (1) protect the airway and prevent aspiration while the patient is at risk, and (2) provide reliable nutrition and medication delivery until swallowing recovers with immunotherapy and rehabilitation.

Clinical reasoning and practical approach
Begin by assuming high aspiration risk whenever there is coughing with secretions, wet vocal quality, drooling, or clinical weakness of neck/ swallowing muscles. Early involvement of speech–language pathology (SLP), a swallowing therapist, and ENT is essential. Decisions about feeding route and airway interventions should be driven by objective tests of swallow physiology, not by skin findings or CK alone, because immunotherapy can take days–weeks to restore muscle strength.

What tests guide the decisions
A bedside clinical swallow evaluation by SLP is the first step to screen risk. If there is any concern (coughing, voice change, pooling, inability to manage secretions), obtain an instrumental study: video fluoroscopic swallow study (VFSS, barium swallow) to visualize oral–pharyngeal transit, residue, penetration/aspiration and effectiveness of compensatory maneuvers; or fibreoptic endoscopic evaluation of swallowing (FEES) to assess secretion management, laryngeal function and silent aspiration. Choose VFSS to quantify pharyngeal residue and timing or FEES if bedside access or real‑time secretion assessment is needed. Repeat testing after treatment/rehabilitation guides escalation or de‑escalation of interventions. Chest imaging and clinical surveillance for aspiration pneumonitis/pneumonia and pulmonary function testing are also required when pulmonary involvement or recurrent aspiration is suspected.

Airway protection and nutrition decisions
If the patient cannot manage secretions, has evidence of aspiration (especially silent aspiration), or shows progressive respiratory compromise, institute nil per os (NPO) and provide enteral nutrition.

For short‑term enteral needs (weeks) use a nasogastric (NG) or nasojejunal tube to deliver nutrition and medications while immunotherapy (steroids, IVIG, steroid‑sparing agents) and swallowing therapy are started. NG feeding is appropriate when the expected recovery is weeks to a few months and when nasal route is tolerated.

Consider percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy (PEG) when dysphagia is severe and expected to be prolonged: persistent inability to swallow safely beyond about 4–6 weeks despite optimal medical therapy and rehabilitation, ongoing weight loss, or recurrent aspiration pneumonia. The precise timing is individualized — earlier PEG is reasonable if prolonged immunosuppression is anticipated or if NG tube is not tolerated.

Airway interventions such as elective intubation or tracheostomy are indicated if there is acute respiratory failure from airway muscle involvement, inability to protect the airway despite suctioning, progressive bulbar weakness with threatened compromise of ventilation, or failed extubation due to bulbar weakness. Tracheostomy is preferred over prolonged translaryngeal intubation when long‑term airway access is anticipated.

Adjunctive measures
Implement targeted swallowing rehabilitation (exercises to strengthen suprahyoid and pharyngeal muscles, compensatory postures and maneuvers) guided by SLP and informed by VFSS/FEES findings. Use diet modification (texture/thickness changes) only when instrumental testing shows it reduces aspiration risk. Maintain meticulous oral hygiene to reduce aspiration pneumonia risk. Coordinate immunotherapy aggressively because recovery of dysphagia parallels control of myositis.

Summary teaching point
In dermatomyositis with severe oropharyngeal dysphagia, use bedside SLP assessment followed promptly by VFSS or FEES to quantify aspiration risk and guide therapy; institute NPO and short‑term NG feeding for immediate protection and nutrition, escalate to PEG if dysphagia persists or NG is not feasible, and consider tracheostomy only for persistent inability to protect the airway or ventilatory failure. Simultaneous aggressive immunosuppression and SLP‑directed rehabilitation are essential because treating the underlying myositis is the key to recovery.

### [Clinical Pearls] Which dermatomyositis-associated autoantibodies are most strongly linked with malignancy, and how does the malignancy risk profile of anti-SAE1 compare?

The myositis autoantibodies most consistently linked with an increased risk of an occult malignancy are anti–transcriptional intermediary factor 1‑γ (anti‑TIF1‑γ) and anti‑nuclear matrix protein 2 (anti‑NXP2). In adult dermatomyositis anti‑TIF1‑γ shows the strongest and most reproducible association with cancer (particularly within the first 1–3 years of DM diagnosis); NXP2 is also linked to an elevated cancer risk, especially in older adults. By contrast, antibodies such as anti‑MDA5 are not classically cancer‑associated (they instead predict rapidly progressive interstitial lung disease and cutaneous ulceration).

Anti‑SAE1 occupies an intermediate position. Case series and pooled reviews report malignancy in a minority of SAE1‑positive patients (roughly 15–20% in the largest collated series), so SAE1 is not as strongly cancer‑linked as TIF1‑γ or NXP2 but carries a higher risk than many other myositis specificities. That relative risk is modified importantly by clinical context: older age, male sex, prominent dysphagia, and other paraneoplastic features increase the pretest probability of an occult malignancy irrespective of the antibody. Clinically this means that an SAE1‑positive patient with dysphagia (as in the presented case) should be managed as higher risk—performing comprehensive baseline cancer screening (age‑appropriate imaging, PET/CT or CT chest/abdomen/pelvis, and directed endoscopies where indicated) and close surveillance over the first 1–3 years after diagnosis, when paraneoplastic cancers most commonly present.

Takeaway: anti‑TIF1‑γ (and NXP2) = highest cancer association; anti‑SAE1 = intermediate cancer risk (~15–20%) and should prompt thorough baseline and interval malignancy screening when accompanied by other clinical risk factors.

### [Management] According to IMACS guidance, what clinical and serologic features increase cancer risk in idiopathic inflammatory myopathy, and what malignancy screening strategy is appropriate for a high-risk dermatomyositis patient?

Key teaching point: the idiopathic inflammatory myopathies are paraneoplastic in a subset, and IMACS provides a risk-stratified approach — you look for clinical and serologic features that raise the pretest probability of an occult malignancy, then move from routine age‑appropriate screening to directed, more sensitive investigations (cross‑sectional imaging and endoscopy) in high‑risk patients.

Which features increase cancer risk (clinical reasoning): dermatomyositis phenotype itself confers higher risk than polymyositis because the cutaneous form is more often paraneoplastic; older age at onset, male sex, subacute/rapid onset, prominent constitutional symptoms or weight loss, severe dysphagia or rapidly progressive muscle disease, and lack of response to immunotherapy all raise concern because they increase the prior probability that the myositis is paraneoplastic. Serologically, the strongest antibody associations are anti–transcriptional intermediary factor‑1γ (TIF1‑γ) and anti‑NXP2; these substantially increase cancer risk. Other antibodies (eg, anti‑SAE, anti‑MDA5) have weaker or variable associations (anti‑SAE has been linked to malignancy in some series but is not as specific as TIF1‑γ/NXP2). Use antibody results to modify your index of suspicion, not as a standalone screen.

What to do for a high‑risk dermatomyositis patient (practical IMACS‑based strategy, applied to this case): start with comprehensive baseline evaluation beyond routine age‑appropriate screening. Obtain whole‑body cross‑sectional imaging — either PET‑CT or contrast CT chest/abdomen/pelvis — because these identify many occult solid tumors; add targeted studies guided by symptoms and local cancer epidemiology. In practice this means PET‑CT (or CT CAP if PET unavailable), tumor markers as adjuncts, and directed endoscopy: gastroscopy and colonoscopy in patients with GI risk factors or when high risk overall (the IMACS guidance supports endoscopic evaluation in high‑risk DM). For women include pelvic examination, transvaginal ultrasound and mammography (and consider gynecologic oncology referral); for men consider prostate evaluation as per age/risk. If the baseline screen is negative, repeat intensified surveillance for at least the first 3 years (eg, repeat clinical exam and interval imaging or PET‑CT at 6–12 months and then annually or sooner for new symptoms), because most associated cancers present within the first 3 years. Continue routine age‑appropriate screening thereafter.

Applied to the presented patient: he has two major risk elements (dermatomyositis phenotype and dysphagia) and two intermediate factors (male sex and anti‑SAE positivity), making him high risk by IMACS criteria. The appropriate approach — which the team followed — is baseline PET‑CT or CT chest/abdomen/pelvis, relevant tumor markers, and directed endoscopy (gastroscopy and colonoscopy). If initial studies are negative, maintain close clinical surveillance with repeat exams and consider repeat imaging (typically within 6–12 months and then periodically up to 3 years), and pursue any new symptoms aggressively.

### [Pathophysiology] What is pseudoangioedema in the context of dermatomyositis, and what pathophysiologic process accounts for subcutaneous edema in the face or neck in this disease?

Pseudoangioedema in dermatomyositis is not true angioedema but inflammatory soft‑tissue swelling that mimics angioedema clinically — often of the face, periorbital region or neck — yet is driven by the underlying myositis and microvascular injury rather than by histamine or bradykinin pathways.

The pathophysiology is rooted in dermatomyositis being a microangiopathy: complement activation (classically with membrane attack complex C5b‑9 deposition in endomysial/perifascicular capillaries) and immune‑mediated injury to the muscle capillary endothelium increase capillary permeability and produce a localized capillary leak. That leak, together with inflammatory cell infiltration of peri‑ and intramuscular tissues and occasional lymphatic compromise from perivascular inflammation, causes fluid to accumulate in involved muscles and adjacent subcutaneous tissues. When the head and neck muscles (strap muscles, pharyngeal and cervical muscles) are preferentially inflamed, the result is marked cervical or facial swelling that can look identical to angioedema on inspection but will not have features of histaminergic/bradykinin angioedema (no urticaria/pruritus, poor response to epinephrine/antihistamines/C1 esterase replacement, normal C4/C1 function).

Clinically this distinction matters because the evaluation should look for signs of dermatomyositis (cutaneous findings, elevated CK, myositis autoantibodies, imaging or biopsy evidence of myositis) and the treatment is immunosuppression to control inflammatory capillary leak rather than allergy‑directed therapies.

### [Clinical Pearls] How common is clinically significant edema (including facial or neck swelling) in dermatomyositis, and with which autoantibodies has it been more frequently reported?

Clinically significant subcutaneous oedema — including conspicuous facial or neck swelling that can mimic angioedema — is uncommon in dermatomyositis. Large series and reviews put the frequency in the single digits: one cohort of 121 patients found oedema in 8 patients (≈6.6%), and a systematic review through 2013 identified only 19 published cases of dermatomyositis with overt oedema. Neck‑predominant swelling is even less commonly described than facial swelling.

When oedema does occur it has been reported more often with certain myositis autoantibodies, most notably anti–melanoma differentiation‑associated gene 5 (anti‑MDA5) and anti–transcriptional intermediary factor‑1γ (anti‑TIF1‑γ). Anti‑SAE1 (the antibody in this case) can be associated with oedema but reports are infrequent.

The clinical reasoning is important: the oedema in dermatomyositis is not histaminergic or bradykinin mediated but related to inflammatory microvascular injury and increased capillary permeability — complement deposition (C5b‑9) in endomysial capillaries and cytokine‑driven capillary leak explain the phenomenon. Practically, any unexplained “angioedema” that is atypical (subacute, painless, associated with muscle weakness, rash, nailfold changes, or elevated CK) should prompt consideration of inflammatory myopathy and testing for CK and myositis autoantibodies.

### [Differential Diagnosis] What clinical and laboratory clues help differentiate dermatomyositis from immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy and inclusion body myositis in a middle-aged adult with proximal weakness and markedly elevated CK?

Think of three linked domains: clinical phenotype, autoantibody/serology, and muscle pathology — each provides complementary clues.

Clinically, dermatomyositis typically presents subacutely with proximal muscle weakness often accompanied by characteristic cutaneous stigmata (heliotrope rash, Gottron’s papules/sign, shawl/V‑neck sign, periungual changes) and can have prominent subcutaneous/soft‑tissue oedema and inflammatory dysphagia. Interstitial lung disease and a paraneoplastic association are also well described. Immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) also presents with marked, often very high CK and prominent proximal weakness and can have severe dysphagia, but skin findings are absent and there is usually a history of statin exposure in anti‑HMGCR disease or a more fulminant myonecrotic presentation in anti‑SRP disease. Inclusion body myositis (IBM) is different in course and pattern: it is generally slowly progressive, occurs in older patients, produces asymmetric weakness that preferentially affects finger flexors and quadriceps with early difficulty rising from a chair and relatively modest CK elevations; IBM often responds poorly to immunotherapy.

Serology narrows the differential. Myositis‑specific antibodies associated with dermatomyositis (eg, anti‑SAE1, anti‑Mi‑2, anti‑TIF1‑γ, anti‑MDA5) point strongly to DM and often predict cutaneous, lung, or malignancy risks. IMNM is associated with anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP antibodies. IBM has no reliable disease‑specific circulating autoantibody and muscle biopsy/clinical pattern is key.

Muscle biopsy provides the definitive distinctions. Dermatomyositis shows perifascicular atrophy/necrosis, perivascular and perimysial inflammation, and complement (C5b‑9) deposition in endomysial/perifascicular capillaries reflecting a microangiopathy. IMNM is characterised by scattered necrotic and regenerating fibres with minimal lymphocytic inflammation (a necrotizing myopathy pattern) and diffuse myofibre necrosis without the perifascicular capillary pattern of DM. IBM shows endomysial inflammation with CD8+ T cells invading non‑necrotic fibres, rimmed vacuoles and protein aggregates (p62, TDP‑43) on histology and electron microscopy.

Practical synthesis for a middle‑aged patient with proximal weakness and very high CK: the presence of evocative skin findings or periungual changes, subcutaneous/neck oedema, ILD or dysphagia, a DM‑specific antibody (eg, SAE1 as in this case), MRI/PET showing diffuse muscle inflammation and a biopsy with perifascicular necrosis and C5b‑9 capillary deposition clinch dermatomyositis. A similar picture with anti‑HMGCR or anti‑SRP and a biopsy dominated by necrosis with scant inflammation favors IMNM. A chronic, asymmetric quadriceps/finger‑flexor weakness with modest CK rise, rimmed vacuoles and protein aggregates on biopsy points to IBM and argues against an expectation of steroid responsiveness.

In short: correlate skin and systemic features and serology with the biopsy pattern — perifascicular atrophy + capillary MAC deposition + DM‑specific antibody = dermatomyositis; widespread necrosis with minimal inflammation + anti‑HMGCR/SRP = IMNM; rimmed vacuoles, CD8 invasion and progressive distal/quadriceps predominance = IBM.

### [Management] What are appropriate monitoring parameters to assess treatment response and guide steroid tapering in dermatomyositis with severe muscle and bulbar involvement?

In severe dermatomyositis with prominent limb and bulbar muscle involvement the decision to taper high‑dose glucocorticoids should be driven primarily by objective clinical improvement of muscle function—especially bulbar function—and by corroborating laboratory and instrumental evidence of reduced inflammatory activity rather than by antibody titres alone. Put another way: treat the patient, not the number.

Clinically, follow serial, formal assessments of muscle strength (documented Medical Research Council or manual muscle testing scores for proximal muscle groups) and, most importantly for this patient, repeated objective evaluations of swallowing and airway protection (bedside swallow exams by speech pathology, weekly-to-monthly video fluoroscopic swallow studies or fibreoptic endoscopic evaluation of swallowing as clinically indicated). Recovery of bulbar function (absence of aspiration, ability to protect the airway, sufficient oral intake and weight stability) must precede substantial steroid reduction; persistent dysphagia or airway weakness is a reason to maintain or intensify immunosuppression.

Biochemically, monitor muscle enzymes (creatine kinase foremost, with LDH, AST/ALT and aldolase as adjuncts). A falling and then stable normal CK in parallel with clinical improvement supports steroid tapering; rising or persistently elevated enzymes—especially if clinical weakness persists—suggest ongoing disease activity and argue against taper. CRP and ESR can be followed but are non‑specific; they may help when infection or aspiration complicates the picture but are less reliable for isolated myositis activity.

Instrumental and imaging markers add objective confirmation. Repeat MRI of involved musculature or FDG‑PET (if available and previously positive) can document resolution of active inflammation when the clinical picture is ambiguous. Pulmonary monitoring (pulmonary function tests, DLCO, and chest imaging) is important given aspiration risk and possible ILD; deterioration of respiratory measures should delay steroid reduction and prompt escalation of steroid‑sparing therapy. Electromyography can support diagnosis but is less useful for short‑term monitoring.

Frequency of checks should be frequent early on: strength and swallow assessments daily–weekly in hospital, then every 2–4 weeks outpatient until steady improvement, with CK at least weekly initially and then every 2–4 weeks as it normalises. Imaging and PFTs can be repeated at 3 months or earlier if clinical course dictates.

Safety and broader management considerations must guide the taper strategy. Because severe bulbar disease confers high risk from relapse, taper slowly and in steps tied to objective milestones (for example, after sustained clinical improvement and stable normal CK for several weeks, reduce prednisone by small decrements). Maintain or escalate steroid‑sparing agents (IVIG, mycophenolate, rituximab, etc.) as steroid doses fall to minimize relapse risk. Monitor for steroid adverse effects (glucose, BP, infection surveillance, bone health) and for immunosuppression complications (HBV reactivation, cytopenias, opportunistic infections).

Watch for early signs of relapse—new or worsening proximal weakness, recurrent dysphagia or aspiration, rising CK, new MRI/FDG activity—and treat promptly rather than pursuing an overly rapid taper. In summary: let objective functional recovery (especially of bulbar function), concordant normalization of muscle enzymes, and supportive imaging/PFT data guide a cautious, milestone‑based steroid taper while maintaining appropriate steroid‑sparing therapy and safety surveillance.

### [Morning Report] How can FDG-PET serve a dual role in suspected dermatomyositis by assessing both occult malignancy and the distribution of active myositis?

FDG-PET is uniquely useful in suspected dermatomyositis because the tracer accumulates in any tissue with increased glucose metabolism — both malignant lesions and inflamed muscle — so a single whole‑body study can address the two clinical questions we always face in adult dermatomyositis: is there an occult malignancy driving a paraneoplastic myositis, and where is the myositis most active?

The diagnostic reasoning is straightforward. Malignancies generally show focal, intense FDG uptake distinct from background; inflammatory myositis produces more diffuse, symmetric or regionally concentrated muscle uptake related to active immune cell infiltration and myofibre metabolic stress. In practice, a PET/CT in a newly diagnosed adult with dermatomyositis will detect metabolically active tumors that escaped anatomic imaging and will simultaneously map the distribution and intensity of muscle inflammation. That map can explain atypical presentations (for example, prominent head‑and‑neck uptake corresponding to the patient’s neck swelling and dysphagia), help choose the optimal muscle biopsy site when MRI is equivocal, and provide a baseline to monitor treatment response (decreasing FDG in tandem with falling CK and clinical improvement).

Be aware of limitations so interpretation is clinical: infection, recent exercise, aspiration, or healing tissue can cause false‑positive uptake; small mucosal or low‑FDG–avid tumors may be missed, and high‑dose steroids or IVIG given before imaging can blunt inflammatory uptake. Therefore PET is complementary to targeted anatomic imaging (CT, MRI), endoscopic evaluation where indicated (GI cancers in dermatomyositis), and serial clinical surveillance per IMACS guidance.

In this case PET provided decisive dual information: it showed diffuse, abnormal FDG uptake in multiple muscle groups — most prominent in the head and neck, explaining the pseudo‑angioedema — and it showed no metabolically active lesion to suggest an occult malignancy, allowing the team to focus on immunotherapy while continuing directed cancer surveillance.

### [Differential Diagnosis] Which features of anti-MDA5 dermatomyositis distinguish it from anti-SAE1 disease with respect to cutaneous manifestations and pulmonary risk?

Anti‑MDA5 and anti‑SAE1 dermatomyositis can both present with cutaneous disease, but they have distinct clinics and prognoses that should drive your evaluation and urgency of treatment.

Anti‑MDA5 disease classically produces prominent vasculopathic skin findings — painful palmar/volar papules, cutaneous ulcerations (especially over Gottron’s sites and the digits), digital ischemia/necrosis and often alopecia or panniculitis — frequently with little or no frank muscle weakness or very modest CK elevation (the “amyopathic/hypomyopathic” phenotype). Crucially, anti‑MDA5 is strongly linked to rapidly progressive interstitial lung disease (RP‑ILD) with early, severe hypoxaemia and high short‑term mortality. Because the skin lesions reflect small‑vessel injury and systemic vasculopathy, their presence should raise immediate concern for occult or impending RP‑ILD and prompt early, aggressive pulmonary evaluation (high‑resolution CT, serial PFTs/oximetry) and urgent immunosuppression.

By contrast, anti‑SAE1 disease tends to look more like classic dermatomyositis: the textbook cutaneous features (heliotrope, shawl and V‑neck signs, Gottron’s changes) are common, and significant myositis with high CK and symptomatic weakness (including dysphagia) is more typical. Subcutaneous edema (facial/neck swelling) can occur but is uncommon. Pulmonary involvement is reported with SAE1 but is generally less characteristically rapidly progressive than with MDA5; when present it is often chronic or linked to aspiration from bulbar dysfunction rather than the fulminant RP‑ILD seen with MDA5. SAE1 also carries a meaningful association with malignancy and therefore mandates cancer screening when clinical risk factors are present.

In short: if you see vasculopathic, ulcerative skin lesions with little muscle disease, think anti‑MDA5 and act urgently to exclude RP‑ILD. If you see classic DM rashes with marked myositis, dysphagia and high CK (as in this SAE1 case), prioritize treating the myositis, addressing bulbar dysfunction/aspiration risk and standard malignancy surveillance rather than immediate concern for RP‑ILD.

### [Management] In a patient with CK >10,000 U/L and normal renal function without myoglobinuria, what immediate renal-protective measures are indicated, and how do you monitor for rhabdomyolysis-related complications?

Immediate priority is to prevent acute kidney injury from myoglobin-mediated tubular injury and to identify and treat systemic complications of massive muscle breakdown.

Begin aggressive volume resuscitation with isotonic crystalloid as the first-line renal-protective measure. Give an initial bolus (eg, 1–2 L of normal saline in an adult) and then continue maintenance at a high rate (commonly 200–500 mL/hr, titrated) aiming for a urine output target of ≥1 mL/kg/hr (or at least 200–300 mL/hr in many adults). The rationale is to dilute and flush myoglobin and other nephrotoxins, maintain renal perfusion, and prevent intratubular cast formation.

Stop any nephrotoxins and myotoxic agents (eg, statins, NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors/ARBs) immediately. Avoid contrast imaging if possible until renal risk is reduced.

Consider urine alkalinization (IV sodium bicarbonate infusion to keep urine pH >6.5) when there is visible or suspected myoglobinuria, severe acidaemia, or very high CK with evolving renal risk — evidence is limited but mechanistically may reduce cast formation by limiting myoglobin precipitation. Mannitol or loop diuretics are not routinely recommended as first-line therapy; diuretics may be used only to manage volume overload or to augment urine output when euvolemia has been established, and mannitol is reserved for select cases and specialist input.

Monitoring must be intensive and protocolized. Check serum creatinine and electrolytes (K+, phosphate, corrected Ca2+) frequently — initially every 4–6 hours (or sooner if abnormal) until stable — because hyperkalaemia, hypocalcaemia, hyperphosphataemia and metabolic acidosis are common and life‑threatening. Obtain serial CK levels (eg, every 6–12 hours initially) to follow the magnitude and trend of muscle injury; CK correlates with severity and informs duration of aggressive measures. Monitor urine output hourly and inspect urine for colour/myoglobin; a negative urine dip does not exclude risk, so continue surveillance until CK falls.

Place the patient on continuous cardiac monitoring because hyperkalaemia can cause lethal arrhythmias. Obtain repeated ECGs when potassium is elevated or rising. Check coagulation and consider DIC monitoring if clinical concern (coagulopathy, bleeding).

Assess for local complications of severe muscle swelling: perform serial focused physical exams for compartment syndrome (pain out of proportion, tense swollen compartments, neurovascular compromise) and consult surgery for fasciotomy if suspected.

Escalate early to nephrology if urine output falls despite fluids, if serum potassium is refractory or rapidly rising, if severe acidosis or volume overload develops, or if creatinine worsens — indications for renal replacement therapy (RRT) include refractory hyperkalaemia, refractory acidosis, overt volume overload, or uremic complications.

Remember that absence of myoglobin on a dipstick or an initially normal creatinine does not obviate risk when CK is >10,000 U/L; maintain aggressive hydration and close metabolic and renal monitoring until CK and labs are clearly improving.

### [Morning Report] What ENT or airway red flags in a patient with neck swelling and dysphagia should prompt early involvement of critical care or anesthesia despite a non-obstructive nasendoscopy?

The key teaching point is that a normal or non‑obstructive flexible nasendoscopy does not rule out impending airway compromise when the problem is extraluminal, neuromuscular, or rapidly progressive. In a patient with neck swelling and dysphagia you should call critical care/anesthesia early (and have ENT and surgical airway backup) when any of the following red flags are present — not because the laryngoscope is abnormal, but because they predict loss of airway patency or protective reflexes:

Progressive or rapidly worsening neck/face swelling over hours to days, or documented interval increase on imaging. Rapid progression suggests evolving extrinsic compression or inflammatory edema that can cross the threshold to obstruction.

Bulbar dysfunction: dysphagia with pooling of secretions, frequent coughing or aspiration on bedside exam or swallowing study, weak cough, nasal regurgitation, dysarthria or difficulty managing oral secretions. These indicate loss of airway protection and high aspiration risk even when the glottis looks patent.

Signs of respiratory muscle involvement: new or worsening dyspnea, orthopnea, shallow/rapid breathing, use of accessory muscles, rising or falling respiratory rate, inability to take deep breaths, or unexplained hypercapnia/ hypoxia. Inflammatory myopathies (as in this case) can involve the diaphragm and neck flexors, so early respiratory failure can occur without laryngeal stenosis.

Worrisome nasendoscopy findings even if anatomy is preserved: marked pooling of secretions, inconsistent or weak glottic closure on phonation, paradoxical vocal cord movement, or coughing with scope — all imply impaired protection or dynamic dysfunction.

Imaging–airway discordance: CT/MRI showing retropharyngeal/retro-laryngeal soft‑tissue edema, strap‑muscle swelling, or circumferential neck edema causing luminal narrowing even if flexible scope could be passed; progressive radiologic narrowing merits early airway planning.

Voice change/stridor: new hoarseness, muffled voice, inspiratory stridor or biphasic noisy breathing — these are clinical markers of impending obstruction.

Physiologic instability or failing reserve: hypoxia, hypercapnia, tachypnea or altered mental status, or high oxygen/ventilatory requirements — escalate immediately.

Anatomic or technical risk for rapid loss of airway: limited mouth opening, trismus, neck immobility, prior radiation/surgery, or obesity/Cricothyroid landmarks difficult — these lower rescue options and mandate early controlled airway.

Laboratory/diagnostic clues suggesting high risk: markedly elevated CK or imaging/evidence of myositis involving neck/respiratory muscles; severe dysphagia on videofluoroscopy or evidence of aspiration pneumonitis.

What to do when these red flags are present: notify critical care and anesthesia early, move to a monitored setting, limit sedating drugs, keep the patient nil per os, optimize oxygenation, and prepare a controlled airway with experienced operators (awake fiberoptic or videolaryngoscopy) and immediate surgical airway readiness (ENT/OR notified). Early planned intubation in a controlled environment is far safer than emergency rescue intubation after sudden deterioration.

In short: do not be reassured solely by a “normal” nasendoscopy. Progressive swelling, bulbar signs/pooled secretions, respiratory muscle weakness or discordant imaging are the triggers to escalate to critical care/anesthesia and to make a definitive airway plan.

### [Management] Which infectious disease screenings are recommended before initiating multi-agent immunosuppression for dermatomyositis, and how do results alter management?

Before starting multi‑agent immunosuppression for dermatomyositis you need a focused infectious‑disease screen because several latent or chronic infections can reactivate with high‑intensity therapy (high‑dose corticosteroids, rituximab, mycophenolate, IVIG). The screen should be done promptly so results can change timing and add prophylaxis or treatment.

Essential tests and how results alter management
1) Hepatitis B: HBsAg, anti‑HBc (total) and anti‑HBs (and HBV DNA if any marker positive).
- HBsAg positive (chronic HBV): start nucleos(t)ide antiviral prophylaxis (entecavir or tenofovir) before immunosuppression and continue for at least 6–12 months after B‑cell depletion (longer with rituximab).
- HBsAg negative but anti‑HBc positive (resolved/occult): with agents that carry high reactivation risk (rituximab, intense regimens) give antiviral prophylaxis or at minimum close monitoring with HBV DNA and start antivirals if DNA appears. Do not rely on stopping therapy alone.
- If all negative, no HBV prophylaxis required; baseline serology guides future decisions.

2) Hepatitis C: anti‑HCV with reflex HCV RNA if positive.
- Active HCV viremia: coordinate with hepatology/ID for antiviral therapy if possible before or concurrently with immunosuppression; immunosuppression can worsen hepatic disease. Treated and cleared patients usually proceed with standard precautions.

3) HIV: HIV antigen/antibody screen.
- If positive, coordinate antiretroviral therapy and ID input prior to or concurrently with immunosuppression; consider modified immunosuppression depending on immune status.

4) Tuberculosis: IGRA (QuantiFERON) or TST and chest radiograph (CXR).
- Latent TB (positive IGRA or TST without active disease): treat latent TB (eg, isoniazid +/- rifapentine or alternative regimens) before or soon after starting immunosuppression depending on urgency; for B‑cell depleting therapy and high‑dose steroids, treat promptly and liaise with ID.
- Active TB (symptoms, CXR/ microbiology positive): defer elective immunosuppression until TB therapy established and cleared by ID.

5) Varicella zoster: VZV IgG.
- VZV IgG negative (susceptible adult): if time allows, vaccinate with live varicella vaccine before immunosuppression (ideally weeks before). For herpes zoster prevention in older adults, give recombinant zoster vaccine (RZV) ideally before immunosuppression; live zoster vaccine is contraindicated once immunosuppressed.
- VZV IgG positive: no routine antiviral prophylaxis, but have a low threshold for early therapy if reactivation occurs.

6) CMV: CMV IgG usually checked in transplant/high‑risk settings; routine screening is optional in myositis but consider baseline CMV serology if heavy B‑cell depletion planned (rituximab) or in context of other risk factors — positive serology increases reactivation risk and informs monitoring.

7) Respiratory viruses and other latent infections: screen for SARS‑CoV‑2 per local policy; consider endemic exposures (strongyloides in migrants/immigrants with eosinophilia or exposure history — test and treat before immunosuppression).

Prophylaxis decisions motivated by results and regimen intensity
- Pneumocystis jirovecii prophylaxis: give TMP‑SMX for patients on high‑dose steroids (>20 mg prednisone daily for >4 weeks) plus additional immunosuppression (rituximab, mycophenolate). If TMP‑SMX contraindicated (eg, severe allergy, hemolytic anemia), use alternatives (inhaled pentamidine, atovaquone, dapsone after G6PD check).
- HBV antiviral prophylaxis as above.
- Latent TB therapy before/with immunosuppression when positive.
- Vaccination: update immunizations (influenza, pneumococcal, recombinant zoster) ideally before starting treatment; avoid live vaccines once immunosuppressed.

Practical approach
Obtain HBV panel, HCV, HIV, IGRA/TST + CXR, VZV IgG (and CMV if high‑risk) before rituximab or other potent agents. If screening is positive, involve ID/hepatology and start prophylaxis or treatment per guidelines rather than simply delaying immunosuppression if the patient has urgent disease—balance risks: for severe dermatomyositis with airway or swallowing compromise, begin necessary therapy while instituting antiviral/TB prophylaxis and close monitoring rather than withholding lifesaving immunosuppression.

### [Clinical Pearls] How should patients with anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis be counseled regarding expected time course of recovery of strength and swallowing, relapse monitoring, and the need for ongoing cancer surveillance?

Patients with anti‑SAE1 dermatomyositis should be counseled with a practical, prognosis‑focused plan that sets expectations about recovery, outlines how we will watch for relapse, and explains the need for ongoing malignancy surveillance.

Recovery of strength and swallowing
Tell the patient that muscle weakness and serum CK usually improve much faster than skin disease and that swallowing often takes longer to recover than limb strength. In the case you’ve just seen, proximal strength recovered within weeks (normal CK and full proximal strength by about 3 weeks after discharge), whereas dysphagia required months to resolve (feeding tube for ~2 months, complete resolution by ~7 months). That pattern — rapid improvement in limb strength over weeks with slower resolution of bulbar dysfunction over months — is typical for inflammatory myopathy with prominent pharyngeal involvement. Expect ongoing incremental improvement over 3–6 months, with most patients who respond to therapy regaining near‑baseline strength by 3–6 months; swallowing may continue to improve for several more months and occasionally lags behind. Reassure them that persistent deficits after 6–12 months are possible but uncommon when treatment is timely and adequate.

Relapse monitoring and follow‑up strategy
Explain that relapse is monitored clinically rather than by any single test. We will follow symptoms and physical exam closely and use laboratory and functional tests to corroborate:
- Clinical review and targeted neuromuscular exam at frequent intervals initially (every 2–4 weeks while tapering prednisone, then every 1–3 months during maintenance).
- Serum CK and inflammatory markers at the same intervals (CK typically parallels muscle inflammation but can lag or be normal in isolated dysphagia); rising CK or recurrent proximal weakness should prompt escalation.
- Objective swallowing assessment (video‑fluoroscopic swallow) if any recurrent cough, choking, weight loss, or voice change.
- Pulmonary surveillance with baseline and interval PFTs and chest imaging if there were ILD or aspiration findings (repeat PFTs at 3 months and then as indicated), because SAE1 patients have substantial ILD risk.
- Medication monitoring (drug toxicity and infectious risk) and adjustment guided by clinical activity; immunosuppression tapering should be slow and individualized. Discuss early reporting of red flags — new proximal weakness, new or worsened dysphagia, dyspnea, new skin lesions, fevers — because these often herald relapse.

Cancer surveillance
Be clear that dermatomyositis — and SAE1 positivity in particular — carries an elevated cancer risk (literature estimates vary; roughly 10–20% in cohorts, higher in some antibody subsets). Because malignancy risk is greatest in the first 1–3 years after diagnosis, we will do concentrated surveillance during that period:
- Age‑ and sex‑appropriate baseline cancer screening (mammography/Pap/HPV, colonoscopy, PSA where appropriate), plus CT chest/abdomen/pelvis and PET/CT when indicated by risk factors or symptoms. In this case, given dysphagia and the IMACS risk assessment, proceed with upper and lower endoscopy if not already done.
- Repeat targeted imaging or exams guided by symptoms and interval risk; a practical plan is more intensive screening in year 1 (baseline PET/CT or CT and endoscopy as clinically indicated), then repeat targeted evaluations over the next 2 years if symptoms or examinations warrant. Routine whole‑body PET/CT surveillance beyond the first 1–2 years is not universally recommended; tailor to patient risk factors (age, smoking, antibody profile, new symptoms).
- Maintain a low threshold for expedited cancer workup if constitutional symptoms, unexplained weight loss, new focal pain, or new persistent lab abnormalities develop.

Practical counseling points
Be explicit: we expect limb strength to improve over weeks to a few months and swallowing to improve more slowly over months; we will monitor clinically and with CK, PFTs and swallow studies, and we will taper immunosuppression cautiously to avoid relapse. Because of the increased malignancy risk with dermatomyositis (and the patient’s IMACS risk profile), we will perform comprehensive baseline cancer screening now and remain vigilant for 2–3 years, with additional testing driven by symptoms or abnormal findings.

Finish by giving the patient clear red flags (new or worsening proximal weakness, coughing/choking, new shortness of breath, unexplained fevers or weight loss) and the exact follow‑up plan (timing of clinic visits, labs, and imaging) so they know when to seek urgent review.
