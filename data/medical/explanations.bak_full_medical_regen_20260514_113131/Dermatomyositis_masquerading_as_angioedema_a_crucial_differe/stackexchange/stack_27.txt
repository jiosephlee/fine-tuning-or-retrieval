Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook

### [Morning Report] How can FDG-PET serve a dual role in suspected dermatomyositis by assessing both occult malignancy and the distribution of active myositis?

FDG-PET is uniquely useful in suspected dermatomyositis because the tracer accumulates in any tissue with increased glucose metabolism — both malignant lesions and inflamed muscle — so a single whole‑body study can address the two clinical questions we always face in adult dermatomyositis: is there an occult malignancy driving a paraneoplastic myositis, and where is the myositis most active?

The diagnostic reasoning is straightforward. Malignancies generally show focal, intense FDG uptake distinct from background; inflammatory myositis produces more diffuse, symmetric or regionally concentrated muscle uptake related to active immune cell infiltration and myofibre metabolic stress. In practice, a PET/CT in a newly diagnosed adult with dermatomyositis will detect metabolically active tumors that escaped anatomic imaging and will simultaneously map the distribution and intensity of muscle inflammation. That map can explain atypical presentations (for example, prominent head‑and‑neck uptake corresponding to the patient’s neck swelling and dysphagia), help choose the optimal muscle biopsy site when MRI is equivocal, and provide a baseline to monitor treatment response (decreasing FDG in tandem with falling CK and clinical improvement).

Be aware of limitations so interpretation is clinical: infection, recent exercise, aspiration, or healing tissue can cause false‑positive uptake; small mucosal or low‑FDG–avid tumors may be missed, and high‑dose steroids or IVIG given before imaging can blunt inflammatory uptake. Therefore PET is complementary to targeted anatomic imaging (CT, MRI), endoscopic evaluation where indicated (GI cancers in dermatomyositis), and serial clinical surveillance per IMACS guidance.

In this case PET provided decisive dual information: it showed diffuse, abnormal FDG uptake in multiple muscle groups — most prominent in the head and neck, explaining the pseudo‑angioedema — and it showed no metabolically active lesion to suggest an occult malignancy, allowing the team to focus on immunotherapy while continuing directed cancer surveillance.
