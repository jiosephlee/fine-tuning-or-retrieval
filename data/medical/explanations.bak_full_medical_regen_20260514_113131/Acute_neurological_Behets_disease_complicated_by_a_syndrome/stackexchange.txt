Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

### [Morning Report] A man in his 60s presents with fever, headache, oral aphthae, a new painful genital ulcer, and folliculitis-like rashes. Using the 2014 International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (ICBD), what score does he achieve and what threshold supports a diagnosis of definite Behçet’s disease?

Using the 2014 International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (ICBD), this patient scores 5 points: oral aphthosis = 2 points, genital aphthosis = 2 points, and skin lesions (folliculitis‑like rash) = 1 point. The ICBD threshold for “definite” Behçet’s disease is ≥4 points, so a score of 5 meets the criteria for definite BD.  

Clinically relevant reasoning: the ICBD weights mucocutaneous findings most heavily (oral and genital ulcers each 2 points), so the combination of recurrent oral ulcers plus a painful genital ulcer plus characteristic skin lesions is sufficient for classification without requiring HLA‑B51 or positive pathergy/ocular findings.

### [Clinical Pearls] List the two major neurologic phenotypes of neuro-Behçet’s disease (parenchymal vs non-parenchymal) and give one representative clinical/radiographic example of each.

Neuro‑Behçet’s has two distinct clinicopathologic patterns that determine presentation, imaging and management: the parenchymal form and the non‑parenchymal (vascular/meningeal) form.

The parenchymal phenotype reflects direct inflammatory involvement of brain tissue, most classically the brainstem, thalami and basal ganglia. Clinically this presents with subacute meningoencephalitis or focal brainstem signs — for example progressive brainstem syndrome with dysarthria, vertical gaze palsy, ataxia or hemiparesis. MRI typically shows T2/FLAIR hyperintense, often asymmetric lesions in the brainstem or deep hemispheric nuclei; these lesions are the hallmark of parenchymal neuro‑BD and are managed primarily with high‑dose corticosteroids and often additional immunosuppression.

The non‑parenchymal phenotype includes venous and meningeal complications rather than focal parenchymal inflammation. The classic representative example is cerebral venous sinus thrombosis (CVST): a patient with severe headache, papilledema and raised intracranial pressure, with MR venography demonstrating thrombosis of the transverse or superior sagittal sinus. Management centers on anticoagulation together with treatment of the underlying inflammatory disease. Aseptic meningitis with normal brain parenchymal MRI also falls in the non‑parenchymal spectrum and can explain presentations like the case reported, where CSF pleocytosis and SIADH accompanied normal brain MRI.

Recognizing which phenotype predominates matters because it guides urgent therapy (immunosuppression for parenchymal disease; anticoagulation plus immunomodulation for CVST/non‑parenchymal disease) and has prognostic implications.

### [Differential Diagnosis] In suspected acute neuro-Behçet’s with headache and fever, what cerebrospinal fluid profile supports an aseptic meningitis picture, and which infectious studies should be negative to reasonably exclude common viral and bacterial meningoencephalitides?

An aseptic meningitis CSF profile is one that shows inflammation without evidence of a pyogenic bacterial pathogen. In practice that means a modest pleocytosis with lymphocytic predominance (typically in the range of ~10–200 cells/µL, often low‑double digits as in this case), mildly increased protein (often <1.0 g/L or <100 mg/dL), normal or only mildly reduced glucose (CSF glucose usually >40 mg/dL or CSF:serum ratio ≈0.6), and an opening pressure that may be normal or mildly elevated. That pattern (lymphocytic pleocytosis + mild protein elevation + preserved glucose) points away from acute purulent bacterial meningitis and toward viral, drug‑related, or immune/auto‑inflammatory causes such as neuro‑Behçet’s.

To reasonably exclude the common treatable infectious causes of meningoencephalitis you should document negative directed studies on CSF: a negative Gram stain and routine bacterial cultures, and negative PCR assays for herpesviruses (HSV‑1/2 and VZV) are essential because HSV/VZV are the common causes of treatable sporadic viral encephalitis. Also obtain negative enterovirus PCR in appropriate seasons/ages, negative cryptococcal antigen (when risk factors exist), and negative mycobacterial testing (AFB smear and TB PCR/culture) if tuberculosis is a consideration. Blood cultures and relevant serum serologies (HIV, syphilis when indicated) should also be negative or unrevealing.

Clinical reasoning: pyogenic bacterial meningitis usually produces a neutrophil‑predominant pleocytosis, very high protein and low glucose, and positive Gram stain/culture; HSV/VZV encephalitis often causes lymphocytic CSF but is best excluded by sensitive CSF PCR (early empiric acyclovir is warranted until PCR returns). When those infectious tests are repeatedly negative and the CSF pattern remains lymphocytic with only mild protein elevation and preserved glucose, noninfectious inflammatory causes such as neuro‑Behçet’s become far more likely — especially in the presence of compatible systemic findings.

### [Clinical Pearls] How should a normal early brain MRI influence your diagnostic reasoning in suspected acute neuro-Behçet’s disease presenting as aseptic meningitis?

A normal early brain MRI should not reassure you that neuro‑Behçet’s disease is absent when the clinical picture points toward CNS inflammation. In Behçet’s the neurological phenotype is heterogeneous: the classic “parenchymal” form produces brainstem or hemispheric lesions that are usually visible on MRI, but the acute meningo‑encephalitic/aseptic meningitis presentation often lacks parenchymal abnormalities early in the course. Thus a normal scan is a common and expected finding in early neuro‑BD and must be interpreted in context rather than as a rule‑out test.

Clinically this means you must integrate the history and examination (recent mucocutaneous lesions, genital ulcers, oral aphthae), CSF findings (lymphocytic pleocytosis, elevated protein, sterile studies), and exclusion of infectious, neoplastic and alternative inflammatory causes. Continue an appropriate infection workup and treat empirically as indicated until pathogens are excluded; do not stop diagnostic momentum solely because MRI is normal. Repeat MRI with contrast and focused brainstem/hypothalamic sequences if symptoms progress or fail to improve, because lesions can appear later or be too small to detect initially. Consider adjunctive testing (repeat CSF, targeted serologies, inflammatory markers such as CSF IL‑6 if available) and early neurology/rheumatology consultation to guide immunotherapy.

Therapeutic decisions should be guided by clinical severity rather than radiology alone: significant neurologic dysfunction or progressive disease generally warrants corticosteroids ± additional immunosuppression, whereas mild, self‑limited meningeal presentations may respond to colchicine and supportive care, with close monitoring. Finally, be vigilant for complications of CNS inflammation such as SIADH; normal imaging does not preclude dysregulated ADH and symptomatic hyponatraemia. In short, don’t let a normal early MRI override clinical and CSF evidence of neuro‑Behçet’s — treat the patient, not the scan, and reassess with repeat imaging and tests if the clinical course changes.

### [Morning Report] Define the diagnostic criteria for the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH), and identify the specific serum and urine osmolality and urine sodium thresholds that support the diagnosis.

Think of SIADH as a diagnosis of inappropriate, persistent ADH activity in a patient with hypotonic hyponatraemia after you exclude hypovolaemia, adrenal/thyroid failure and diuretic use. The diagnostic reasoning is: is the plasma hypotonic, is the kidney concentrating urine when it should be dilute, is the patient clinically euvolemic, and are other endocrine causes excluded?

Formal diagnostic criteria (clinical teaching version)
- Hypotonic hyponatraemia: serum sodium <135 mmol/L with low serum osmolality (classically <275 mOsm/kg).  
- Inappropriately concentrated urine for the hypo‑osmolar state: urine osmolality >100 mOsm/kg (and typically substantially elevated — often >300 mOsm/kg) rather than maximally dilute.  
- Urinary sodium consistent with ongoing natriuresis: urine Na generally >30 mmol/L (often >40 mmol/L) when the patient is on a normal salt intake.  
- Clinical euvolaemia on examination (no signs of volume depletion or overload).  
- Normal adrenal and thyroid function (exclude hypothyroidism and adrenal insufficiency).  
- No recent use of diuretics or other drugs that cause hyponatraemia, and no renal failure that would impair free‑water excretion.  
- Supporting finding: measurable or elevated ADH (vasopressin) in the setting of hypo‑osmolarity is “inappropriate,” although ADH assays are not required for routine diagnosis.

Apply this to the case: the patient had serum osmolality 267 mOsm/kg (low), urine osmolality 900 mOsm/kg (markedly inappropriately high), urine sodium 88 mmol/L (high), clinical euvolemia, and normal TSH/cortisol — all satisfying the SIADH criteria. Management follows from the diagnosis (fluid restriction as first line, avoid rapid correction).

### [Differential Diagnosis] In a patient with hypo-osmolar hyponatremia and euvolemia, which tests are essential to rule out endocrine mimics of SIADH (e.g., adrenal insufficiency and hypothyroidism), and why?

When you see hypotonic hyponatraemia in an apparently euvolemic patient and are considering SIADH, the first essential step is to exclude endocrine causes that can produce the same biochemical picture — primarily adrenal insufficiency and hypothyroidism — because treating these conditions (especially giving glucocorticoids) is urgent and because true SIADH should be a diagnosis of exclusion.

Practically, obtain a morning serum cortisol and simultaneous serum ACTH, plus thyroid function tests (TSH and free T4). A random early‑morning cortisol >18 µg/dL (≈500 nmol/L) makes adrenal insufficiency unlikely and supports SIADH; a cortisol <3 µg/dL is strongly suggestive of adrenal failure and mandates prompt glucocorticoid replacement. Values between ~3 and 18 µg/dL are indeterminate and require dynamic testing (standard 250‑µg cosyntropin stimulation test) or measurement of nadir/serial cortisol/ACTH to distinguish primary from secondary adrenal insufficiency. Measuring ACTH is important because secondary (central) adrenal insufficiency can present with hyponatraemia without hyperkalemia and with a low or inappropriately normal ACTH.

For thyroid disease, measure TSH and free T4 together because primary hypothyroidism produces an elevated TSH with low free T4, but central hypothyroidism can have a low/normal TSH with low free T4. Overt hypothyroidism with low free T4 can impair free water clearance and mimic SIADH; therefore a normal free T4 excludes clinically meaningful hypothyroidism as the cause.

Remember to interpret these tests in the clinical context: check for glucocorticoid use that can suppress the HPA axis, severe illness that alters cortisol levels, and pituitary disease if central causes are suspected. Only after excluding adrenal insufficiency and clinically significant hypothyroidism should you confidently label the patient as having SIADH and proceed with SIADH‑directed management.

### [Differential Diagnosis] Differentiate SIADH from cerebral salt wasting in the setting of CNS inflammation: what are the key differences in volume status, urine sodium/excretion patterns, and management implications?

When a patient with CNS inflammation develops hyponatraemia you must distinguish two fundamentally different pathophysiologies because the treatments are opposite.

SIADH is a water‑retention problem caused by inappropriate ADH release from the CNS (or other triggers). Patients are clinically euvolemic (no orthostatic hypotension, no tachycardia or decreased skin turgor, no peripheral edema), serum osmolality is low, urine osmolality is inappropriately high (>100–300–900 mOsm/kg depending on severity), and urine sodium is typically >30–40 mmol/L because the kidney is excreting sodium normally while retaining free water. Biochemical clues include low serum uric acid and low BUN from dilution/renal urate wasting. Importantly, giving isotonic saline usually fails to raise serum sodium (and can worsen it) because the infused sodium is excreted while the retained water remains; the correct initial management is fluid restriction, cautious use of hypertonic saline for severe or symptomatic hyponatraemia, and later consideration of salt tablets, loop diuretics or vasopressin‑receptor antagonists if needed.

Cerebral salt wasting (CSW), by contrast, is a primary renal loss of sodium driven by natriuretic mechanisms in the setting of CNS disease. The key distinguishing feature is true intravascular volume depletion: patients have signs of hypovolaemia (orthostasis, tachycardia, reduced JVP, weight loss, sometimes increased hematocrit), urine sodium is high (>40 mmol/L) and urine output may be large, and serum osmolality is low because sodium and water are both lost with a net negative sodium balance. Biochemically you may see a higher BUN and hemoconcentration reflecting volume loss. In CSW, isotonic (or hypertonic) saline and salt replacement correct both the hypovolaemia and the hyponatraemia; fludrocortisone can be helpful to promote sodium retention.

Because both disorders can present with low serum sodium and high urine sodium in CNS disease, clinical assessment of volume status and the patient’s response to isotonic saline are the decisive tests. If the patient is euvolemic, has low uric acid and the hyponatraemia improves with fluid restriction (as in this Behçet’s neuro‑inflammation case), SIADH is most likely. If the patient is hypovolaemic with orthostatic changes and hyponatraemia improves promptly with volume repletion, CSW is likely and salt/volume repletion is required.

Finally, remember practical pitfalls: clinical volume assessment can be difficult in elderly or edematous patients, and early in illness both processes can coexist or be misread. Use serial weights, orthostatic vitals, urine output trends, serum uric acid/BUN/hematocrit, and an observed trial of isotonic saline when safe to help distinguish them. In the case you read, the patient was clinically euvolemic, had concentrated urine with high urine sodium, low serum osmolality and low uric acid, and his sodium normalized with fluid restriction and treatment of the underlying neuro‑Behçet’s inflammation — a classic pattern for SIADH rather than CSW.

### [Pathophysiology] Explain how neuroinflammation—particularly interleukin-6 (IL-6) signaling—can promote non-osmotic release of antidiuretic hormone and lead to SIADH.

In acute CNS inflammation the usual osmotic control of arginine vasopressin (AVP, or ADH) secretion can be overridden by inflammatory signalling — and interleukin‑6 (IL‑6) is a key mediator of that process. The mechanistic chain is straightforward and explains the findings in this case.

When the meninges or brain parenchyma are inflamed, activated immune cells in blood and CSF release cytokines (notably IL‑6). IL‑6 reaches the hypothalamus and posterior pituitary by several routes: through leaky regions of the blood–brain barrier, across circumventricular organs, and via afferent neural pathways (e.g., vagal and other brainstem inputs triggered by meningeal irritation). IL‑6 acts on hypothalamic neurons in two complementary ways that produce non‑osmotic AVP release. First, it directly stimulates AVP‑producing magnocellular neurons in the supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei, increasing AVP gene expression and exocytosis independent of plasma osmolality. Second, IL‑6 sensitises and/or activates the central integrative pathways that set the AVP secretory “set point” (inflammation‑driven prostaglandin and nitric oxide signalling are intermediary messengers), so that AVP is secreted at lower serum osmolality and in response to non‑osmotic stimuli such as pain, nausea, fever or hypotension. The net effect is persistent, inappropriate AVP release with concentrated urine, natriuresis, and hypo‑osmolar hyponatraemia — the biochemical signature of SIADH.

There are also peripheral and renal effects that amplify hyponatraemia. IL‑6–mediated inflammation increases renal sensitivity to AVP and can up‑regulate aquaporin channels in the collecting duct, reducing free‑water clearance for any given AVP concentration. Finally, systemic inflammation and cytokine‑induced sympathetic activation or baroreceptor reflexes can provide additional non‑osmotic stimuli to AVP secretion.

Clinically this explains the case: aseptic meningeal inflammation produced elevated ADH (measured), very concentrated urine with high urine sodium, and euvolemic hyponatraemia despite a normal-looking MRI. Resolution of inflammation (and likely falling IL‑6 activity) with anti‑inflammatory therapy (colchicine in this patient, which suppresses cytokine production including IL‑6) led to a fall in AVP and correction of sodium without the need for AVP antagonists. Measuring CSF or serum IL‑6 would have strengthened causality, but is not required to apply the pathophysiologic principle.

Takeaway for practice: any CNS inflammatory process can drive non‑osmotic AVP release via cytokine (especially IL‑6) pathways; when you see hyponatraemia with concentrated urine and clinical euvolemia in a patient with meningoencephalitic signs, treat the underlying inflammation and restrict free water while avoiding rapid correction of sodium.

### [Clinical Pearls] Why is direct measurement of plasma ADH not required for the diagnosis of SIADH, and how should an elevated ADH level be interpreted if obtained?

You do not need to directly measure plasma antidiuretic hormone (ADH, vasopressin) to make the diagnosis of SIADH because the diagnosis is a physiologic one based on the clinical and laboratory pattern of water handling, not on a single hormone level. In practice SIADH is diagnosed when there is hypotonic hyponatraemia with clinical euvolemia, concentrated urine (inappropriately high urine osmolality), natriuresis (inappropriately high urine sodium), and exclusion of adrenal, thyroid disease and recent diuretics or other clear causes. Those features demonstrate that free water excretion is impaired despite hypo‑osmolality — which is the essence of “inappropriate” ADH effect — without needing to measure the peptide itself.

There are several practical and biologic reasons ADH measurement is not required and is often unhelpful. ADH is unstable in blood, its assays are technically difficult and not standardized, and the hormone is secreted in pulses and is strongly affected by many non‑osmotic stimuli (nausea, pain, hypotension, stress, certain drugs, inflammation). Because of these issues, a single plasma ADH value has limited sensitivity and specificity for the clinical syndrome. Copeptin (the more stable C‑terminal fragment of the preprohormone) is a better laboratory surrogate in research settings but still adds little to routine, bedside diagnostic judgment.

If an ADH (or copeptin) level is obtained, interpret it only in the context of serum osmolality and the clinical situation. An absolute elevation can support the diagnosis when it is higher than expected for the concurrently measured plasma osmolality (i.e., relative inappropriateness), and a fall in ADH/copeptin with correction of hyponatraemia or treatment of the underlying cause lends causal support. However, an elevated ADH is not specific for SIADH because hypovolaemia, pain, nausea, stress, some malignancies, pulmonary disease, drugs and CNS inflammation (as in this Behçet’s case) also raise ADH. Conversely, a normal or low ADH does not exclude SIADH because of assay and timing issues.

Apply the principle: use the pattern of hypo‑osmolar hyponatraemia plus concentrated urine, natriuresis and exclusion of other causes to make the diagnosis; if ADH/copeptin is measured, use it as supportive, not definitive, evidence and always interpret it against serum osmolality and the clinical context (e.g., CNS inflammation, nausea, medications). In the reported patient, the elevated ADH that fell with clinical improvement is consistent with neuroinflammation‑driven inappropriate ADH release, but it would not have been required to establish SIADH.

### [Management] What is the recommended safe rate of sodium correction in chronic or indeterminate-duration hyponatremia, and what are the clinical indications for initiating hypertonic (3%) saline?

Safe correction of chronic or hyponatremia of indeterminate duration must balance reversing cerebral oedema (when present) against the risk of osmotic demyelination from overly rapid correction. The widely accepted targets are to increase serum sodium by no more than 8 mmol/L in the first 24 hours and no more than 18 mmol/L in the first 48 hours. In patients at particularly high risk for osmotic demyelination (malnourished, chronic alcoholism, advanced liver disease, severe hypokalemia), a more conservative limit of about 4–6 mmol/L in 24 hours is prudent. If overcorrection occurs, the antidote is to promptly re-lower sodium (give free water or D5W and/or desmopressin) to bring the rise back within recommended limits.

Hypertonic (3%) saline is indicated when hyponatremia is accompanied by clinically significant cerebral or cardiorespiratory compromise, or when symptoms are progressive despite conservative measures. Typical indications are severe or moderately severe neurological symptoms such as coma, obtundation, seizure, severe focal deficit, or signs of increased intracranial pressure. It is also appropriate in true acute hyponatremia (<48 hours) with severe symptoms because the brain has had little time to adapt. By contrast, asymptomatic or mildly symptomatic chronic hyponatremia is managed with cause‑directed therapy (fluid restriction, salt tablets, address ADH stimulus) rather than bolus hypertonic saline.

When hypertonic saline is given, the practical approach is to raise serum sodium just enough to relieve dangerous cerebral oedema—typically an initial target rise of 4–6 mmol/L (often achieved by 100 mL of 3% saline over 10 minutes, repeat once or twice as needed) — then slow further correction so the 24‑hour limit is not exceeded. Monitor serum sodium frequently (every 2–4 hours during active correction), track urine output and urine osmolality, and be prepared to give desmopressin and free water if sodium rises too quickly.

In the case presented, the patient was euvolemic with sodium 124 mmol/L from SIADH but without life‑threatening neurologic compromise; conservative management with fluid restriction and close monitoring was appropriate and avoided the risks of unnecessary rapid correction.

### [Clinical Pearls] Name two underrecognized manifestations that can accompany severe hyponatremia due to SIADH in neuroinflammatory states and briefly describe their mechanisms.

Two commonly underrecognized manifestations of profound hyponatremia from SIADH in neuroinflammatory states are persistent hiccups and rhabdomyolysis.

Persistent hiccups often reflect irritation or dysfunction of brainstem centres (the medullary “hiccup reflex” integrating vagal, phrenic and central pathways) and can be triggered by CNS inflammation or by marked hypotonicity causing neuronal swelling and hyperexcitability in those same brainstem circuits. In practice hiccups in a patient with headache/meningitis and hyponatraemia should prompt consideration of central SIADH rather than being dismissed as benign.

Rhabdomyolysis can result directly from severe hyponatraemia: hypotonicity causes myocyte swelling, disruption of membrane ion gradients, intracellular calcium overload and ATP depletion, which lead to myonecrosis and CK release. (Rapid over-rapid correction of sodium is a separate, well-known cause of muscle injury and osmotic demyelination risk, so both the hyponatraemia itself and its mismanagement can produce muscle injury.) Clinically this mandates checking CK and renal function and avoiding too-rapid sodium shifts while treating the underlying cause.

Recognising these associations matters because they broaden the differential for non‑specific symptoms and change monitoring and management — check CK and urine output, monitor sodium frequently, and correct slowly while treating the underlying neuroinflammation.

### [Management] According to contemporary guidelines, what is the recommended first-line therapy for acute parenchymal neuro-Behçet’s disease, and when should an additional immunosuppressive agent be introduced?

The preferred initial treatment for acute parenchymal neuro-Behçet’s disease is high‑dose systemic corticosteroids to rapidly suppress CNS inflammation — typically intravenous methylprednisolone pulses (eg, 1 g/day for 3–5 days) followed by a high‑dose oral prednisone taper (about 0.5–1 mg/kg/day) with gradual reduction guided by clinical and radiographic response.

Because parenchymal neuro‑BD carries a high risk of relapse and accrual of irreversible neurological injury, contemporary guideline practice is to introduce a steroid‑sparing immunosuppressive agent early rather than using steroids alone for prolonged periods. Azathioprine is the common first‑line steroid‑sparing drug used concurrently or started soon after the pulse steroids (ie, at diagnosis or within the first days to weeks) to allow timely steroid tapering and reduce relapses. For severe disease, major neurological deficit, brainstem/deep lesions, treatment failure, or frequent relapses, stronger agents (eg, cyclophosphamide or anti–TNF agents such as infliximab/adalimumab) are recommended.

In short: treat acute parenchymal neuro‑BD with high‑dose corticosteroids immediately and plan a steroid‑sparing immunosuppressant (usually azathioprine) early — essentially at or soon after diagnosis — reserving cyclophosphamide or anti‑TNF therapy for severe or refractory presentations. This approach balances rapid control of inflammation with prevention of relapse and steroid toxicity.

### [Clinical Pearls] Which immunosuppressive agent is generally avoided in patients with neuro-Behçet’s disease due to concerns about neurotoxicity or exacerbation of neurologic involvement, and what is the rationale?

Answer: Cyclosporine (cyclosporin A) is generally avoided in patients with neuro‑Behçet’s disease. Clinically, cyclosporine has well‑described neurotoxic effects (seizures, encephalopathy, posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome) and there are case reports and series in Behçet’s showing new or worsened CNS disease after cyclosporine exposure. The drug can cause endothelial dysfunction, hypertension, and direct neurotoxicity that may mimic or exacerbate parenchymal neuro‑Behçet’s, so it is avoided when CNS involvement is present or suspected. In practice, CNS disease is managed with high‑dose corticosteroids and steroid‑sparing agents with safer CNS profiles (azathioprine, cyclophosphamide or anti‑TNF therapy) rather than cyclosporine.

### [Pathophysiology] Describe colchicine’s mechanism of action relevant to Behçet’s disease and list the BD manifestations for which colchicine has the strongest evidence of benefit.

Colchicine works in Behçet’s disease by targeting the cellular machinery that drives the dominant neutrophil‑mediated inflammation. It binds tubulin and inhibits microtubule polymerization, which impairs neutrophil chemotaxis, adhesion and degranulation, reduces inflammasome activation and downstream release of IL‑1β (and secondarily IL‑6), and interferes with endothelial–leukocyte interactions. Those actions explain why colchicine is particularly effective for manifestations in which acute neutrophilic recruitment and cytokine release are central.

Clinical trial and guideline evidence support colchicine most strongly for the mucocutaneous and articular features of Behçet’s: recurrent oral and genital aphthous ulcers, erythema‑nodosum–type and acneiform skin lesions, and inflammatory arthritis. In these settings colchicine is a standard first‑line, steroid‑sparing agent. By contrast, high‑quality data do not support colchicine as effective for major organ involvement — ocular disease (uveitis), large‑vessel vasculitis, gastrointestinal BD or parenchymal neuro‑Behçet’s — which generally require corticosteroids and additional immunosuppression or biologics. In practice, colchicine may contribute to lowering cytokine levels (eg, IL‑6) and therefore could plausibly ameliorate secondary phenomena such as SIADH related to CNS inflammation, but one should not rely on colchicine alone for established severe neuro‑ or ocular disease.

### [Clinical Pearls] In a newly diagnosed Behçet’s patient with nonspecific CNS symptoms (headache, vertigo) and hyponatremia, which clinical and laboratory features should raise suspicion for concurrent acute neuro-Behçet’s complicated by SIADH?

Think of acute neuro‑Behçet’s complicated by SIADH when a patient with new or newly recognised systemic Behçet’s features develops nonspecific central nervous system symptoms together with euvolaemic hyponatraemia and laboratory evidence of inappropriate antidiuresis.

Clinically, the red flags are the coexistence of characteristic mucocutaneous findings of BD (recurrent oral aphthae, folliculitis‑like rash, genital ulcers) at the time neurological symptoms arise, even if those neurological symptoms are non‑focal — headache, vertigo, persistent hiccups or malaise — because neuro‑BD can present as aseptic meningitis/meningoencephalitis without focal deficits. Note that brain MRI may be normal early, so a normal scan does not exclude neuro‑BD.

Laboratory features that should increase your suspicion for SIADH in this setting are true hypotonic hyponatraemia with clinical euvolaemia, low serum osmolality, inappropriately concentrated urine (elevated urine osmolality >300 mOsm/kg) and elevated urine sodium (>20–40 mmol/L). After excluding hypothyroidism and adrenal insufficiency (normal TSH, free T4 and morning cortisol in this case), an elevated plasma ADH (if available) or the constellation above supports SIADH due to a central cause. Supportive inflammatory signals — high CRP/ESR and mild CSF lymphocytic pleocytosis with raised protein consistent with aseptic meningitis — point toward CNS inflammation as the driver of inappropriate ADH release. Ancillary clues described in this case include hiccups and rhabdomyolysis (elevated CK), both of which can be manifestations or complications of significant hyponatraemia.

Putting it together: when a patient with new BD features presents with headache/vertigo ± hiccups and you find euvolaemic hyponatraemia with low serum osmolality, concentrated urine and elevated urine sodium — and you have excluded thyroid and adrenal causes and common CNS infections — think neuro‑BD causing SIADH. Key practical steps are to confirm the biochemical diagnosis (serum and urine osmolality, urine sodium), obtain CSF to look for aseptic meningitis, obtain MRI (recognising it can be normal early), exclude infection/malignancy, and manage the hyponatraemia cautiously while treating the underlying inflammation.

### [Management] What empiric antimicrobial coverage is appropriate while evaluating a febrile patient with suspected encephalitis/meningitis, and what clinical or laboratory results allow safe de-escalation?

When a patient presents with fever and suspected meningitis/encephalitis you must treat for the most immediately treatable and dangerous causes while you collect diagnostic data — then narrow or stop therapy based on microbiology, CSF pattern and the clinical course.

Empiric regimen to start immediately (after blood cultures, and do not delay lumbar puncture if no contraindication)
Give broad-spectrum bacterial coverage plus empiric antiviral therapy for HSV encephalitis.

- Bacterial: a third‑generation cephalosporin (ceftriaxone 2 g IV q12h or cefotaxime) plus vancomycin to cover resistant Streptococcus pneumoniae. Add ampicillin (2 g IV q4h) if the patient is >50 years, immunocompromised, has listeriosis risk, or there is high clinical suspicion for Listeria. This regimen covers the common community bacterial pathogens (S. pneumoniae, N. meningitidis, H. influenzae) and resistant pneumococci; ampicillin adds Listeria coverage.
- Viral: acyclovir IV (10 mg/kg q8h, adjusted for renal function) for empiric coverage of HSV (and VZV) encephalitis because untreated HSV carries high morbidity and mortality.
- Tailor additional coverage to local epidemiology and risk: consider broader gram‑negative coverage (e.g., cefepime or meropenem) for nosocomial infection, recent neurosurgery, skull fracture, or severe immunosuppression. Consider adjunctive dexamethasone when bacterial meningitis due to S. pneumoniae is suspected and before/with the first antibiotic dose.

How and when to de‑escalate
Use the combination of microbiology results, CSF cell profile/glucose/protein, PCR testing, blood cultures, and the patient’s clinical trajectory.

- Bacterial pathogens identified in CSF or blood: de‑escalate to targeted therapy guided by susceptibility as soon as results are available.
- Negative bacterial Gram stain/cultures at 48–72 hours, a lymphocytic CSF pleocytosis with normal or only mildly reduced CSF glucose and no organism on Gram stain, and negative bacterial PCRs (if available): strongly favour aseptic/viral or inflammatory causes. If bacterial cultures remain negative at 48–72 h and the patient is clinically improving, it is usually safe to stop empiric antibacterial agents (but continue antibacterial therapy if clinical or lab data continue to suggest bacterial disease).
- HSV/VZV PCR negative at 48 hours (and clinical picture inconsistent with HSV encephalitis, e.g., no temporal lobe signs, improving mentation): discontinue acyclovir. If CSF HSV PCR timing is early (<24 h from symptom onset) and clinical suspicion remains high, repeat testing is reasonable before stopping.
- Listeria: because blood/CSF cultures may be slow, maintain ampicillin until Listeria is confidently excluded — typically at least 48–72 h — or until an alternative pathogen is identified and the clinical picture permits stopping.
- Blood cultures: if negative at 48 h and no other source identified, this supports de‑escalation when combined with CSF data.
- Clinical course: marked clinical improvement (defervescence, improving mentation, normalization of inflammatory markers) supports narrowing therapy; ongoing fever, worsening neurologic status, or radiographic progression mandates continuing or broadening coverage and re‑evaluation.

Apply this to the case
In the presented patient the team appropriately started broad antibacterial coverage (they used cefepime) and added acyclovir when encephalitis became a concern. Once CSF showed lymphocytic pleocytosis, CSF cultures and HSV/VZV PCRs were repeatedly negative and the patient clinically improved, it was appropriate to stop antivirals and narrow/stop antibiotics rather than continue prolonged empiric therapy. The safe trigger to de‑escalate was the combination of negative microbiology at 48–72 hours, a CSF profile consistent with aseptic meningitis, and clinical improvement — exactly the sequence that allowed the clinicians to focus treatment on Behçet’s-associated inflammation (colchicine) rather than continued empiric antimicrobials.

### [Clinical Pearls] What is the role of HLA-B51 testing in Behçet’s disease—diagnostic utility and limitations?

HLA-B51 is best thought of as a risk marker, not a diagnostic test. It is strongly associated with Behçet’s disease (BD) in many populations — carriers have an increased odds of developing BD — but the allele is neither necessary nor sufficient for diagnosis. Clinically this means a positive result raises the posterior probability of BD in a patient with compatible features, while a negative result does not exclude the disease.

Why this matters in practice: the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease are entirely clinical and do not require HLA testing because of the allele’s imperfect sensitivity and specificity and marked geographic variation in background prevalence. In populations with high carrier frequency (e.g., parts of Asia, the Middle East), HLA-B51 has lower positive predictive value; in populations with low carrier frequency, a positive test is more informative but still not diagnostic. HLA-B51 may correlate with certain phenotypes (higher risk of ocular and possibly more severe disease, and some reports linking it with more frequent vascular/neuro involvement), so it can have prognostic value in specialist settings, but these associations are probabilistic and do not mandate different management on their own.

Limitations to keep in mind: HLA-B51 testing will not distinguish BD from mimics (infections, inflammatory or neoplastic causes) and should not delay a focused clinical workup or empiric treatment when indicated. A negative test can create false reassurance; a positive test can create anchoring bias if clinicians overweight it relative to the clinical picture. Routine screening with HLA-B51 is not recommended; testing is most useful when the diagnosis is uncertain after a careful clinical assessment or when the result might influence counseling or consideration of prognosis in a specialist clinic.

Applied to this case: the patient met clinical criteria for definite BD based on mucocutaneous findings and neuro involvement, so HLA-B51 added no diagnostic value. Its positivity was supportive but not required, and management decisions (recognising neuro-BD, treating inflammation, and monitoring SIADH) should remain guided by the clinical syndrome rather than the HLA result.

### [Differential Diagnosis] How do you interpret a mildly elevated soluble IL-2 receptor (sIL-2R) in the setting of systemic inflammation when evaluating for hematologic malignancy or sarcoidosis?

A mildly elevated soluble interleukin‑2 receptor (sIL‑2R) should be interpreted as a nonspecific marker of immune activation, not as a diagnostic test for lymphoma or sarcoidosis in isolation. sIL‑2R is shed from activated T‑lymphocytes and rises with any process that drives T‑cell activation — malignant lymphoproliferation, granulomatous disease, and a wide range of infectious and autoimmune inflammatory states. The clinical implication is that the magnitude of elevation, the pretest probability based on history and exam, and concordant objective findings determine how much weight you give the result.

Practically, use three principles when you encounter a mild sIL‑2R rise in a patient with systemic inflammation (as in this Behçet’s case). First, integrate the number into the clinical context: if there are no B symptoms beyond the inflammatory syndrome, no lymphadenopathy or splenomegaly on exam or imaging, normal basic labs aside from inflammatory markers, and no focal lesions on MRI/CT, a modest sIL‑2R elevation more likely reflects non‑malignant immune activation. Second, consider the degree of elevation: modest increases (eg, up to ~1.5–2× upper limit as in this case) are commonly seen with active autoimmune disease and infection; substantially higher values, or progressive rises over time, increase suspicion for lymphoma or active sarcoidosis. Third, seek corroborating data before pursuing invasive testing: targeted imaging (PET/CT if concern for occult lymphoma or sarcoid), organ‑specific tests (ACE/lysozyme for sarcoid are insensitive and should not be used alone), and tissue biopsy remain the gold standards.

Apply this to the case: a sIL‑2R of 704 U/mL (mildly above the reference range) in a patient with marked systemic inflammation (very high CRP), aseptic meningitis, and evolving mucocutaneous findings is most consistent with immune activation from Behçet’s disease rather than occult lymphoma or neurosarcoidosis. The absence of supportive findings — normal imaging, no lymphadenopathy, negative CSF cytology, normal ACE/lysozyme — lowers the posttest probability of malignancy or sarcoidosis and argues against pursuing invasive diagnostic steps solely because of the sIL‑2R. Serial clinical assessment and trend of sIL‑2R and imaging should guide further workup; pursue tissue diagnosis only if new signs (progressive lymphadenopathy, constitutional symptoms, focal masses, or rising sIL‑2R despite control of inflammation) emerge.

### [Management] Outline a monitoring plan for SIADH managed with fluid restriction: specify target intake, frequency of sodium checks, and triggers to escalate therapy to hypertonic saline or vasopressin receptor antagonists.

When you manage SIADH initially with fluid restriction you must do three things simultaneously: set a realistic and physiologically appropriate intake target that will create negative free-water balance, monitor the patient and labs closely so you detect inadequate response or over-rapid correction, and have clear, pre‑specified escalation triggers so that hypertonic fluid or a vasopressin‑receptor antagonist (V2‑antagonist, “vaptan”) are started promptly when indicated.

Aim and rationale for fluid restriction
For most euvolemic SIADH start with a total oral fluid restriction of 800 mL/day (range 500–1000 mL/day individualized to body size and insensible losses). The goal is to make fluid intake less than urine free‑water excretion so serum sodium rises gradually. In the case presented the team used <800 mL/day — a practical starting target in acute, hospitalised patients.

Baseline investigations and continuous monitoring
Before and during restriction obtain a baseline serum sodium, serum osmolality, urine osmolality and urine sodium. Record intake/output and daily weight. While on fluid restriction check serum sodium every 24 hours in clinically stable, mildly symptomatic patients. Also re‑check urine osmolality and urine sodium within 24–48 hours to assess whether restriction is physiologically effective (if urine osmolality remains very high, restriction is unlikely to work). Document mental status and look for new or worsening neurologic symptoms (confusion, lethargy, headache, nausea, vomiting, seizures).

Escalation triggers for hypertonic saline
Use hypertonic (3%) saline when the patient has severe or progressive neurologic symptoms attributable to hyponatraemia (seizure, obtundation, severe confusion, coma) or when serum sodium is very low and there is concern for imminent cerebral oedema. In practice escalate immediately for:
- Any seizure, severe obtundation, or focal neurologic deficit, regardless of the absolute sodium.
- Serum sodium ≤120 mmol/L with moderately severe symptoms (marked nausea, severe headache, agitation, progressive confusion).
When giving hypertonic saline aim for an initial goal increase of 4–6 mmol/L over the first few hours to reverse life‑threatening cerebral oedema, then slow correction to avoid osmotic demyelination. Do not exceed ~8 mmol/L in 24 hours (often safer to target ≤6 mmol/L/24 h in high‑risk patients: chronic hyponatraemia, alcoholism, malnutrition, liver disease).

Escalation triggers for vasopressin receptor antagonists (vaptans)
Consider a vaptan when fluid restriction fails and the patient is stable (no severe neurologic symptoms) but remains hyponatraemic, or when fluid restriction is intolerable/unfeasible. Practical triggers include:
- Failure of serum sodium to rise by ≥2–4 mmol/L after 24–48 hours of strict restriction, or persistent sodium <125–130 mmol/L despite restriction.
- Very high urine osmolality (e.g., >300–500 mOsm/kg) with ongoing urine sodium excretion that makes further restriction unlikely to succeed.
Before starting a vaptan confirm euvolemic SIADH, exclude hypovolaemia and contraindications (concurrent potent CYP3A4 inhibitors, advanced liver disease for some agents). When a vaptan is begun, monitor serum sodium every 6–8 hours for the first 24–48 hours because these agents can produce rapid aquaresis and over‑rapid sodium rise; adjust or stop therapy to avoid >8 mmol/L/24 h correction.

Additional practical safeguards and stopping rules
Weigh daily and maintain strict input/output charting; stop offending medications that cause SIADH. If serum sodium is rising too fast (>0.5 mmol/L/hour or >8 mmol/L/24 h), intervene with free water (D5W) or desmopressin to slow correction. If no response to restriction and you are not yet using a vaptan, consider oral urea (where available) or demeclocycline for chronic outpatient SIADH, but these are not first‑line for the acutely symptomatic patient.

Summary application to this case
Start with total intake of ~800 mL/day, obtain daily serum sodium and clinical neurologic checks, repeat urine osmolality/Na within 24–48 hours to assess response, and escalate to hypertonic saline immediately for seizures/marked neurologic decline or very low Na with symptoms. If the patient is haemodynamically stable, has inadequate sodium rise after 24–48 hours of strict restriction, and no contraindication exists, consider a vaptan with intensive (q6–8 h) sodium monitoring to avoid overcorrection.

### [Clinical Pearls] List the ICBD item weights (oral aphthosis, genital aphthosis, ocular lesions, skin lesions, neurologic signs, vascular signs, pathergy) and the minimum total score that classifies Behçet’s disease.

The International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (ICBD) use weighted clinical items. Oral aphthosis = 2 points, genital aphthosis = 2 points, ocular lesions (uveitis, retinal vasculitis) = 2 points, skin lesions = 1 point, neurologic signs = 1 point, vascular manifestations = 1 point, and a positive pathergy test = 1 point. A total score of ≥4 classifies a patient as having Behçet’s disease.  

Clinically, the higher weights for oral, genital and ocular lesions reflect their greater diagnostic specificity; the lower weights and the optional pathergy test improve sensitivity across different populations.

### [Management] Which organ involvements in Behçet’s disease are associated with worse prognosis, and what long-term immunomodulatory strategies reduce relapse risk in neuro-ocular disease?

Major organ involvement determines prognosis in Behçet’s disease because it reflects both higher inflammatory burden and complications that are irreversible or life‑threatening. In practice, the organ manifestations that portend a worse long‑term outcome are central nervous system disease (parenchymal neuro‑Behçet’s and major venous sinus thrombosis), ocular disease with recurrent sight‑threatening uveitis, large‑vessel disease (arterial aneurysms—classically pulmonary artery—and large‑vessel thrombosis), and severe gastrointestinal involvement (deep ulceration with risk of perforation or bleeding). These sites are associated with increased morbidity and mortality and usually require more aggressive, sustained therapy than isolated mucocutaneous disease.

Prevention of relapse in neuro‑ and ocular Behçet’s rests on long‑term immunomodulation rather than intermittent corticosteroids alone. The evidence and clinical practice points are:

- Azathioprine is a well‑established steroid‑sparing agent for both ocular and parenchymal neuro‑Behçet’s: it reduces attack frequency and preserves function and is commonly used for maintenance after control of an acute episode. Its oral toxicity profile and cumulative data make it a first‑line maintenance choice.

- Anti–TNF agents (infliximab, adalimumab) are highly effective for refractory or severe ocular disease and for disabling neuro‑Behçet’s. They reduce relapse rates and rapidly control inflammation; they are used when azathioprine/other conventional agents fail or when vision/neurological function is threatened.

- Cyclosporine is effective for ocular disease and reduces eye attacks, but it can cause or worsen neurologic disease and therefore should be avoided in patients with known neuro‑Behçet’s.

- Interferon‑alpha has demonstrated benefit in ocular Behçet’s and can reduce relapses; it is an option in some centers for refractory ocular disease.

- Cyclophosphamide or other potent cytotoxic agents are reserved for life‑ or organ‑threatening disease (severe vascular, major CNS involvement) or when biologics are contraindicated; they are not routine long‑term maintenance drugs because of toxicity.

- Colchicine is useful for mucocutaneous and some articular manifestations but does not reliably prevent neuro‑ or severe ocular relapses and should not be relied on as sole maintenance therapy for neuro‑ocular disease.

Clinical reasoning: because neuro‑ and ocular involvement cause irreversible structural damage (brainstem lesions, optic nerve/retinal injury) and relapses commonly produce cumulative deficits, the therapeutic goal is durable suppression of pathogenic inflammation. That requires a glucocorticoid‑sparing maintenance agent started after induction control—azathioprine is a common first‑line choice, anti‑TNF agents for refractory or severe presentations, and avoidance of agents (e.g., cyclosporine) that may worsen other involved organs. Therapy must be individualized to severity, prior response, comorbidities and infection risk, and patients require long‑term monitoring for relapse, drug toxicity, and complications of chronic immunosuppression.

### [Management] If high-dose corticosteroids are necessary for acute neuro-Behçet’s in a patient with poorly controlled diabetes, what strategies can mitigate steroid-induced hyperglycemia during induction and taper?

When high‑dose corticosteroids are required for acute neuro‑Behçet’s in a patient with poor baseline glycaemic control, the management goal is to blunt severe hyperglycaemia while avoiding hypoglycaemia during the steroid taper. Think ahead: steroids will raise insulin resistance quickly (within hours), and doses often need to be escalated with pulse IV or high‑dose oral therapy and then de‑escalated — so plan for a proactive, dynamic insulin strategy and close monitoring.

Start with these practical, evidence‑based steps and the reasoning behind them.

1) Anticipate and monitor aggressively. Begin frequent capillary glucose monitoring at baseline and once steroids start: pre‑meal and bedtime (or q4–6‑hour checks if inpatient on IV methylprednisolone). Early detection lets you titrate insulin before very high glucose levels or ketosis develop.

2) Use insulin as the primary tool. Oral agents are unreliable for the rapid, large glycaemic swings caused by high‑dose steroids. In hospital use a structured insulin regimen:
   - For IV pulse methylprednisolone or very high doses: use an intravenous insulin infusion with hourly or q1–2‑hour glucose checks until hyperglycaemia is controlled and the steroid pulse is finished.
   - For high‑dose oral steroids or when off the infusion: use basal‑bolus subcutaneous insulin. Give a long‑acting basal insulin to cover background insulin resistance and rapid‑acting insulin before meals with correction doses. Empiric starting doses are higher than usual (for insulin‑naïve patients often 0.4–0.6 U/kg/day, titrated to glucose). Increase total daily insulin based on glucose trends, paying attention that morning steroid dosing commonly causes large post‑prandial and afternoon hyperglycaemia.

3) Match insulin type to the steroid pharmacodynamics. Daytime hyperglycaemia from morning prednisone is often post‑prandial and late‑afternoon; consider using intermediate‑acting insulin (eg, NPH) or increasing daytime rapid‑acting prandial doses. For prednisone given in the morning, adding a mid‑day NPH can blunt the afternoon glucose peak. For once‑daily long‑acting glucocorticoids or multiple doses, lean toward basal‑bolus with corrections.

4) Plan the taper and de‑escalation up front. As steroids are reduced, insulin requirements will fall — often abruptly. Reduce insulin doses progressively (eg, 10–20% reductions with each steroid dose decrease) guided by glucose readings to avoid hypoglycaemia. Educate the team and patient that insulin will likely be needed transiently and will be tapered as steroids are tapered.

5) Avoid or cautiously use non‑insulin agents while acutely ill. Metformin can be resumed when renal function is stable and the acute illness resolves; it is not useful for rapid control of steroid‑induced hyperglycaemia. Sulfonylureas may be insufficient and can cause hypoglycaemia when steroid dose falls. SGLT2 inhibitors have dehydration and infection risks and are not appropriate for the inpatient acute steroid phase. DPP‑4 inhibitors have modest effect and may be adjunctive outpatient but are not substitutes for insulin during high‑dose steroid therapy.

6) Address comorbid issues and safety. Monitor for dehydration, electrolyte changes (especially with SIADH history), infection risk, and ketoacidosis if very high glucose or if insulin is withheld. Involve endocrinology for complex cases or if insulin requirements are unpredictable. Provide diabetes education and arrange close outpatient follow‑up after discharge for rapid insulin tapering and transition to chronic therapy.

7) Use steroid‑sparing strategies when appropriate. If the neurologic disease can be controlled with alternatives or early steroid‑sparing agents (eg, colchicine in selected cases, azathioprine, cyclosporine, or biologics depending on severity and specialist guidance), that reduces cumulative steroid exposure and the metabolic burden. This decision balances acute neurologic risk and long‑term control and should involve rheumatology/neurology.

In summary: expect markedly increased insulin requirements with high‑dose corticosteroids, treat promptly and primarily with insulin (IV infusion for pulses, basal‑bolus for oral regimens), monitor frequently, and plan a proactive taper of insulin as steroids are tapered. Coordinate with endocrinology and consider steroid‑sparing options once the acute neurologic threat is addressed. These steps minimize hyperglycaemic complications while allowing necessary immunosuppression.

### [Clinical Pearls] What are the limitations of serum ACE and lysozyme as screening tests for neurosarcoidosis, and how should normal values be interpreted in a patient with CNS inflammation?

Serum ACE and lysozyme are commonly ordered as “screening” blood tests when sarcoidosis is on the differential, but their performance characteristics and biology impose important limitations — especially when the question is isolated CNS inflammation.

First, neither test is sensitive enough to rule out neurosarcoidosis. ACE is produced by epithelioid cells in granulomas, so serum ACE rises when there is a substantial systemic granulomatous burden. In patients with disease confined to the CNS or with an early/limited inflammatory focus, serum ACE is often normal. Lysozyme behaves similarly: it can be elevated with granulomatous inflammation but is neither sensitive nor specific for intracranial disease. In practice a normal serum ACE or lysozyme is common in neurosarcoidosis and therefore does not exclude it.

Second, both tests lack specificity. Elevations occur with many other inflammatory states, hepatic disease, some infections and certain malignancies; laboratory assays vary between centers; and medications (for example systemic corticosteroids) and patient factors (age, ACE polymorphisms) can alter results. Conversely, ACE inhibitors and some lab methods can suppress measured ACE, producing false negatives. CSF ACE or lysozyme measurements have been proposed, but they also have limited sensitivity and are not definitive.

What this means for clinical interpretation: treat serum ACE and lysozyme as adjuncts that can increase or decrease your suspicion when the pre-test probability is moderate to high, but do not use a normal value to exclude neurosarcoidosis in a patient with CNS inflammation. In a patient like the one in this case — meningitic CSF, normal brain MRI, strong alternative clinical clues (mucocutaneous Behçet’s findings) and no systemic sarcoid features — normal serum ACE and lysozyme simply lower the likelihood of systemic sarcoidosis but do not reliably rule out isolated neurosarcoid. The correct approach is to integrate these labs with imaging, CSF studies, targeted searches for systemic sarcoidosis (chest imaging, PET if indicated), and, when necessary and feasible, tissue diagnosis from the most accessible site. In short: use ACE/lysozyme to support a diagnosis when positive, but never to exclude neurosarcoidosis when they are normal.

### [Morning Report] In a patient with suspected neuro-Behçet’s but a normal initial brain MRI, what additional studies (e.g., MR venography, spinal MRI, repeat MRI, CSF IL-6) would you consider and why?

When you suspect neuro‑Behçet’s but the first brain MRI is normal, treat the imaging result as a snapshot rather than a definitive exclusion. Neuro‑BD commonly produces brainstem/hypothalamic/parenchymal lesions that can be subtle or absent early, and Behçet’s also confers a high risk of venous thrombosis — so plan investigations that (1) look for occult parenchymal disease in locations MRI may miss initially, (2) look for venous causes that mimic meningitis/headache and cause SIADH, and (3) document inflammatory activity in the CSF.

Specifically, obtain a contrast‑enhanced brain MRI with thin slices focused on the brainstem and hypothalamic region (and include diffusion and FLAIR sequences), and repeat it if clinical suspicion persists — commonly within 7–14 days or sooner if new focal signs develop — because lesions can evolve after presentation. Add MR venography at the same time to exclude cerebral venous thrombosis/dural sinus thrombosis, which is relatively common in BD, can present with headache and hyponatraemia and may be missed on routine non‑venous sequences.

If there are spinal symptoms (sensory level, radicular pain, bladder dysfunction) or if parenchymal brain imaging remains uninformative, obtain spinal MRI with contrast to look for myelitis — neuro‑BD classically involves brainstem and spinal cord as well as meninges. If the clinical picture is dominated by aseptic meningitis (as in this case), repeat lumbar puncture and broaden CSF testing: cell count, glucose/protein, microbiologic PCRs already done, cytology if malignancy remains a concern, and consider measuring CSF inflammatory markers such as IL‑6. CSF IL‑6 (or other cytokines) is not diagnostic but can demonstrate CNS inflammatory activity, may support a neuroinflammatory mechanism for SIADH, and can be useful for monitoring response to therapy in select cases — keeping in mind limited availability and lack of disease‑specific cutoffs.

If imaging and CSF remain indeterminate but suspicion is high, consider whole‑body FDG‑PET or targeted CT to exclude systemic malignancy or sarcoidosis (which can have MRI‑negative neurosarcoid early) and, when available and relevant, dedicated pituitary MRI if central endocrine causes for SIADH are suspected. Always integrate serial clinical findings — mucocutaneous lesions, HLA‑B51, CSF pleocytosis and response to therapy — because neuro‑BD is a clinical diagnosis and early imaging can be falsely normal.

### [Differential Diagnosis] What clinical features help distinguish Behçet’s mucocutaneous lesions (oral aphthae, genital ulcers, folliculitis-like rash) from mimics such as HSV infection, aphthous stomatitis alone, or bacterial folliculitis?

Key teaching point: distinguish mucocutaneous Behçet’s lesions by their clinical pattern — recurrent, multisite, intensely painful ulcers (oral and genital) with a propensity to be deep and to scar, plus sterile “folliculitis‑like” papulopustules and systemic inflammatory features — rather than by any single lesion’s appearance. The diagnosis is clinical and rests on combinations of findings and course that differ from HSV, simple aphthous stomatitis, or bacterial folliculitis.

Why this matters (clinical reasoning)
Behçet’s is a systemic neutrophilic inflammatory disorder. Its mucocutaneous lesions reflect that systemic process and therefore tend to be recurrent, occur at multiple mucocutaneous sites simultaneously, be more painful/deeper, and be associated with other inflammatory manifestations (fever, elevated CRP, uveitis, pathergy, HLA‑B51 association, or — as in this case — neurologic symptoms). In contrast the mimics are usually more limited in distribution, have characteristic lesion morphology or microbiologic findings, and follow different natural histories and treatment responses.

Practical discriminators you can use at the bedside

- Pattern and multiplicity: Behçet’s usually produces recurrent oral aphthae plus at least one other mucocutaneous feature (genital ulcers or typical skin lesions). The concurrent occurrence of oral ulcers, genital ulceration (classically painful scrotal/labial ulcers) and sterile papulopustular/folliculitis‑like lesions strongly favors Behçet’s and fulfils diagnostic criteria when recurrent.

- Ulcer characteristics and healing: Oral ulcers in simple aphthous stomatitis are usually small (minor aphthae), shallow, heal without scarring. In Behçet’s ulcers are often larger/deeper (major aphthae), more painful, and genital ulcers particularly tend to be deep and heal with scarring — scarring of genital ulcers is a useful distinguishing feature that points away from idiopathic aphthae or HSV.

- Temporal course / recurrence: Behçet’s lesions recur repeatedly over months–years and often in different sites; HSV recurrences are episodic but classically involve the same anatomical site and are often preceded by grouped vesicles. Ask about prior similar episodes and sites.

- Morphology and prodrome of genital lesions: HSV usually begins with grouped vesicles that rupture to form shallow painful ulcers; lesions are superficial and often clustered. Behçet’s genital ulcers more commonly present as solitary or multiple deep punched‑out ulcers without an antecedent vesicular phase.

- Skin lesions: The “folliculitis‑like” BD lesions are erythematous papules/pustules that are sterile on culture and may appear at sites of trauma (pathergy). True bacterial folliculitis has pustules centered on hair follicles, often with surrounding erythema, purulence, positive bacterial cultures, and clinical response to antibiotics. A positive pathergy test or sterile pustules on culture supports Behçet’s.

- Systemic context: Fever, markedly elevated inflammatory markers, ocular inflammation (uveitis), arthralgia, or neurologic findings point to systemic disease (Behçet’s), not isolated HSV or aphthous stomatitis. In this case the combination of fever, high CRP, aseptic meningitis and hyponatraemia with mucocutaneous lesions strongly favored systemic Behçet’s disease.

- Microbiology and labs: HSV diagnosis is supported by PCR or culture from lesion swab or by Tzanck/cytology; bacterial folliculitis yields positive bacterial cultures. Behçet’s lesions are culture‑negative. HLA‑B51 and a positive pathergy test are supportive but neither is required for diagnosis.

- Response to therapy: HSV responds to antivirals; bacterial folliculitis improves with appropriate antibiotics; Behçet’s mucocutaneous disease typically responds to colchicine, topical steroids, or other anti‑inflammatory/immunomodulatory agents and does not remit with antibiotics or antivirals.

Apply these principles to the case
In this patient the simultaneous painful oral aphthae, the appearance of a painful scrotal ulcer that is typical for Behçet’s (deep and likely to scar), sterile folliculitis‑like eruptions, systemic inflammation (high CRP), and later neurologic involvement all argued against isolated HSV, simple aphthous stomatitis, or bacterial folliculitis and supported the diagnosis of systemic Behçet’s disease.

### [Clinical Pearls] When present, what are the characteristic MRI findings in parenchymal neuro-Behçet’s disease with respect to lesion location and signal characteristics?

In parenchymal neuro‑Behçet’s disease the MRI pattern is distinctive because it reflects a deep, inflammatory rather than a vascular process. Lesions classically involve the brainstem (most often the mesodiencephalic junction and pontine tegmentum), thalami and basal ganglia, with frequent extension into adjacent deep white matter and internal capsule; supratentorial deep white‑matter lesions may also occur. On conventional sequences these lesions are hyperintense on T2 and FLAIR and hypointense on T1, reflecting edema and inflammatory infiltrate. Contrast enhancement is variable and often patchy or nodular rather than homogenous; acute lesions can show restricted diffusion, whereas subacute/chronic lesions may become gliotic and atrophic over time. Importantly, the distribution is typically non‑territorial (not confined to an arterial vascular territory), multifocal, and centered in deep gray‑white structures — a pattern that helps distinguish neuro‑Behçet’s from ischemia, demyelination, or typical infectious abscesses. Finally, normal MRI early in the disease (as in this case) does not exclude parenchymal neuro‑Behçet’s; repeat imaging or correlation with CSF and clinical findings is often necessary.

### [Management] When, if ever, should vasopressin receptor antagonists (vaptans) be used in SIADH secondary to neuroinflammation, and what are the main risks and monitoring requirements?

Short answer: Vaptans are rarely first‑line for SIADH from neuroinflammation. They should be reserved as a short‑term rescue therapy when (a) the patient has clinically significant or worsening neurologic symptoms from hyponatraemia despite conservative measures, or (b) fluid restriction and treatment of the underlying CNS inflammation have failed or are impractical and the patient remains at imminent risk of complications (seizure, profound encephalopathy, severe rhabdomyolysis). In most cases of neuro‑inflammatory SIADH — including the reported Behçet’s case — treating the underlying inflammation and careful fluid restriction with close monitoring is preferred.

Why: In SIADH the pathophysiology is inappropriate vasopressin activity and vaptans (oral tolvaptan, IV conivaptan) directly antagonize the V2 receptor to produce aquaresis and raise serum sodium. That makes them mechanistically attractive, but clinical experience and guidelines caution their routine use because the risks (particularly overly rapid correction) are substantial and because correcting the underlying CNS cause often normalizes ADH secretion.

Main risks and how to monitor
1) Over‑rapid correction → osmotic demyelination syndrome (ODS). This is the principal danger. Target correction rates of serum Na should be conservative (aim ≤8 mmol/L in 24 hours, certainly avoid >10–12 mmol/L in 24 h; many experts prefer ≤8). If overcorrection occurs, promptly re‑lower sodium with hypotonic fluids and/or desmopressin. Monitor serum sodium every 2–4 hours while initiating vaptan therapy until stable, then at least daily.

2) Excessive aquaresis, hypovolaemia and hypotension. Monitor urine output hourly initially, daily weights, orthostatic signs and intravascular volume. Adjust fluids if volume depletion develops.

3) Hypernatremia. Check sodium frequently and stop the drug if sodium approaches upper safe limit or rises too quickly.

4) Hepatotoxicity (tolvaptan). Tolvaptan has been associated with idiosyncratic liver injury with prolonged use. Avoid long courses; obtain baseline liver tests and monitor LFTs if therapy continues beyond a few days. Do not use prolonged (>30 days) therapy unless specialist oversight and monitoring are in place.

5) Drug interactions and contraindications. Tolvaptan is a CYP3A4 substrate; avoid strong inhibitors. Vaptans are contraindicated in hypovolaemic hyponatraemia and in end‑stage liver disease (conivaptan IV has additional vascular effects).

Practical approach in neuroinflammatory SIADH
Start with diagnosis confirmation (euvolemic hypo‑osmolar hyponatraemia, high urine osmolality and urine Na), exclude hypovolaemia/other causes, and immediately institute targeted management of the neurologic disorder plus fluid restriction and frequent monitoring. Reserve vaptans for hospitalized patients when: severe symptoms persist despite initial measures, fluid restriction is ineffective/unfeasible, or ongoing hyponatraemia is causing complications and more rapid but controlled correction is needed. If used, give vaptan in the hospital, monitor serum Na q2–4 h for the first 24–48 h, watch urine output and volume status, check baseline LFTs and avoid prolonged outpatient use. Have a plan to treat overcorrection (desmopressin + hypotonic fluid).

Applied to the presented case: the patient improved rapidly with anti‑inflammatory therapy (colchicine) and fluid restriction; that is the preferred course. A vaptan would not have been indicated unless he had worsening neurologic compromise or failed conservative therapy — and if one had been started, it should have been short, inpatient, and closely monitored to avoid rapid correction and hepatic toxicity.
