Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

## Clinical Approach to Concurrent Behçet’s Disease, Neuro-Behçet’s, and Hyponatremia

Patients who present with fever, headache or other nonspecific CNS symptoms together with mucocutaneous lesions (oral and genital aphthae, acneiform or folliculitis‑like rash) and hyponatraemia should prompt early consideration of three linked problems: a systemic inflammatory disorder such as Behçet’s disease (BD), neurologic involvement of BD (neuro‑BD), and an electrolyte disturbance most commonly due to the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). The case just presented is a useful model for a bedside‑first, safety‑prioritizing approach: (1) stabilise the patient and identify life‑threatening hyponatraemia or CNS infection; (2) rapidly characterise the hyponatraemia and volemic status; (3) pursue a focused diagnostic workup for CNS infection and neuro‑inflammation; and (4) treat the underlying inflammatory disorder while supporting and monitoring sodium correction.

Initial stabilisation and urgency. The first priority is to assess the acuity of neurologic compromise and the severity of hyponatraemia. Severe or life‑threatening manifestations of hyponatraemia—seizures, obtundation or deep coma, severe respiratory compromise, or rapid neurologic deterioration—mandate prompt treatment with hypertonic saline (eg, bolus 3% NaCl) even before a definitive diagnosis is established. For patients who are symptomatic but not critically ill, cease hypotonic fluids, institute close neurologic monitoring and proceed with investigations. Be mindful that the clinical picture may be mixed: fever, neck stiffness or altered behaviour may reflect infection; persistent hiccups, as in this case, can be an early clue to profound hyponatraemia or brainstem irritation.

Rapid characterisation of hyponatraemia at the bedside. A systematic biochemical assessment allows differentiation of pseudohyponatraemia and the principal pathophysiologies:

- Measure plasma glucose, serum osmolality, renal function, TSH and cortisol. Hyperglycaemia can produce a translocational hyponatraemia and should be corrected for when interpreting sodium. Hypothyroidism and adrenal insufficiency must be excluded because their treatments differ.
- If hypotonic hyponatraemia is confirmed (low serum osmolality), obtain urine osmolality and urine sodium. SIADH typically shows inappropriately concentrated urine (urine osmolality >100–300 mOsm/kg, often >300 mOsm/kg) with elevated urine sodium (>30 mmol/L) in a euvolemic patient.
- Assess volume status carefully—most patients with SIADH are clinically euvolemic. Use orthostatic vital signs, mucous membranes, skin turgor, presence/absence of peripheral oedema or raised JVP; these clinical findings help distinguish hypovolaemic, euvolaemic and hypervolaemic hyponatraemia and guide therapy. Remember that the physical exam is imperfect; integrate biochemical data and clinical context.

Diagnosing SIADH in the setting of suspected neuro‑inflammation. When you have hypotonic hyponatraemia with concentrated urine and high urine sodium in a euvolemic patient, normal thyroid and adrenal function, and no recent diuretic use, the diagnosis of SIADH is most likely. Measurement of plasma ADH is rarely necessary for routine clinical care and results are often not timely; the biochemical pattern plus clinical context is usually sufficient. In patients with CNS inflammation (meningoencephalitis, meningitis, brainstem disease) SIADH is a well‑recognised mechanism — inflammatory cytokines (eg, interleukin‑6) can stimulate ADH secretion — so the presence of aseptic meningitis or neuro‑BD should raise suspicion that SIADH is secondary to CNS disease.

Empiric management while workup proceeds. Because fever and focal neurologic symptoms can signal treatable CNS infections, empiric antimicrobial coverage is often appropriate until infection is excluded. Obtain blood cultures and perform a lumbar puncture promptly if there are no contraindications; if increased intracranial pressure or focal mass lesion is suspected, obtain a head CT or MRI first. Empiric regimens should cover likely pathogens and include acyclovir when viral encephalitis is a consideration. Empiric antibiotics/antivirals can be discontinued when cultures and PCRs are negative and clinical picture supports inflammatory disease rather than infection.

Recognising neuro‑Behçet’s disease even with normal imaging. Neuro‑BD commonly presents with meningoencephalitis, headache and behavioural change, and less commonly with focal deficits or brainstem signs. Crucially, early in the disease MRI can be normal or nonspecific; therefore a normal MRI does not exclude acute neuro‑BD. Cerebrospinal fluid in neuro‑BD often shows mild lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein without an identified pathogen, as in this case. The diagnosis of neuro‑BD in a patient with mucocutaneous signs should be clinical: use the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease to establish systemic BD, then interpret CNS findings in that context after excluding infection, neoplasm and other inflammatory disorders (eg, sarcoid). HLA‑B51, when present, supports the diagnosis but is not diagnostic.

Therapeutic priorities for SIADH in this setting. For SIADH due to CNS inflammation, two principles apply: treat the hyponatraemia to prevent acute neurologic complications, and treat the underlying inflammatory disease to remove the stimulus for inappropriate ADH release.

- Initial, conservative measures for mild to moderate, asymptomatic SIADH include free water restriction (often to <800–1000 mL/day), correction of precipitants (eg, discontinue offending drugs), and close monitoring of serum sodium and neurologic status. In many patients with reversible CNS inflammation, these measures and treatment of the CNS disease lead to gradual correction.
- For severe symptoms (seizures, coma, severe obtundation) or very low sodium, administer hypertonic saline promptly. Use modest boluses of 3% NaCl and aim for a small, rapid rise (eg, 4–6 mmol/L) to control life‑threatening symptoms, then slow the correction. Avoid overcorrection: plan to limit the rise to ≤8 mmol/L in 24 hours (some guidelines suggest ≤6–8 mmol/L in high‑risk patients for osmotic demyelination), and ≤18 mmol/L in 48 hours. If overcorrection occurs, re‑lower sodium with hypotonic fluid or desmopressin as indicated.
- Pharmacologic agents (demeclocycline, urea, vasopressin receptor antagonists) have a role in chronic or refractory SIADH but are generally not first‑line in the acute neuroinflammatory setting. Vaptans can correct sodium but carry risks and do not treat the underlying disease.

Treating the underlying neuro‑inflammatory disorder. Corticosteroids are the mainstay of therapy for acute neuro‑BD and most patients respond to high‑dose steroids, often supplemented by other immunosuppressants for more severe or relapsing disease. However, as demonstrated by the present case, some patients with meningoencephalitis‑type neuro‑BD may improve with less intensive therapy — colchicine is effective for mucocutaneous BD and has anti‑inflammatory effects (inhibition of neutrophil chemotaxis and suppression of cytokine production) that may, in selected cases, ameliorate systemic and neurologic inflammation. This response is not the norm for neuro‑BD, and the decision to withhold systemic corticosteroids should be individualized after careful evaluation and close monitoring; empiric steroids are appropriate when neuro‑BD is suspected and infection has been reasonably excluded. Multidisciplinary involvement (neurology, rheumatology, infectious disease) is important for tailoring immunosuppression.

Avoiding common diagnostic and management pitfalls. Do not be reassured by a normal MRI early in the disease course; rely on CSF analysis and clinical pattern recognition. Avoid indiscriminate testing — for example, ACE and sIL‑2R have limited sensitivity and specificity and should be directed by clinical suspicion (eg, features of sarcoid or lymphoma). Always check for drug‑induced SIADH (eg, cyclophosphamide, antipsychotics, SSRIs) and correct for serum sodium in marked hyperglycaemia. Watch for less obvious manifestations of hyponatraemia such as persistent hiccups, nausea, or unexplained rhabdomyolysis; measure CK when symptoms or severe electrolyte disturbance raise concern.

Monitoring and follow‑up. In the inpatient setting, monitor serum sodium every 4–6 hours during active correction and daily once stable. Reassess neurologic status frequently. If SIADH resolves with treatment of neuro‑inflammation, the patient can often transition from fluid restriction to ad lib intake as appropriate; persistent or recurrent SIADH may require longer‑term therapy. Arrange ophthalmologic evaluation for uveitis, outpatient rheumatology/neurology follow‑up for ongoing management of BD, and patient education about recognising flares and medication‑related side effects.

Takeaway clinical checklist for the triad presentation
- Recognise the syndrome: new mucocutaneous lesions + nonspecific CNS symptoms + hyponatraemia should raise immediate suspicion for neuro‑BD complicated by SIADH.
- Stabilise first: rapidly identify life‑threatening hyponatraemia or CNS infection and treat empirically as needed.
- Characterise hyponatraemia: measure serum osmolality, urine osmolality and urine sodium; exclude hypothyroidism and adrenal insufficiency; assess volume status.
- Cover infection while you investigate: obtain blood cultures and CSF studies; start empiric antimicrobials/acyclovir when clinically warranted.
- Look for neuro‑BD even with normal imaging: CSF lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein in the context of BD mucocutaneous features support the diagnosis.
- Treat the hyponatraemia safely (fluid restriction, limited use of hypertonic saline for severe symptoms, strict limits on correction rate) while addressing CNS inflammation (usually corticosteroids; colchicine may help mucocutaneous disease and, in select cases, systemic inflammation).

Applying this framework to the presented case explains the successful course: early recognition of SIADH and careful sodium management while empirically covering infection, combined with focused clinical diagnosis of BD and targeted anti‑inflammatory therapy (colchicine with close observation), led to resolution of CNS symptoms and normalization of sodium without the need for aggressive immunosuppression.

## Pathophysiology: From Behçet’s Inflammation to Neuroinvolvement and SIADH

Behçet’s disease (BD) is best understood as an autoinflammatory vasculitis in which a genetically susceptible host mounts an excessive innate and adaptive immune response to poorly defined triggers. At the cellular level this state is characterised by neutrophil hyperactivity and an adaptive T‑cell skewing towards Th1 and Th17 phenotypes with increased production of interferon‑γ, tumor necrosis factor (TNF), IL‑1 and IL‑6. HLA‑B51 is the strongest genetic risk marker identified to date and increases the probability of developing BD, but it is neither necessary nor sufficient for diagnosis; clinical criteria remain paramount. The downstream effect of this immune milieu is a propensity for neutrophil‑driven endothelial injury of small and medium vessels that manifests clinically as recurrent mucosal ulceration, skin pustules, ocular inflammation and, in a subset of patients, involvement of major organs including the central nervous system.

Neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD) arises when the inflammatory process involves the brain, meninges, venous sinuses or cerebral vessels. Two broad phenotypes are recognised. The parenchymal form typically presents as an acute or subacute meningoencephalitis with a predilection for brainstem and diencephalic structures; patients may have headache, fever, behavioural change, cranial nerve dysfunction or focal deficits. The non‑parenchymal phenotype comprises cerebral venous sinus thrombosis, intracranial hypertension and, less commonly, isolated meningeal disease. Importantly, early parenchymal neuro‑BD may produce only inflammatory changes in the cerebrospinal fluid or microscopic perivascular infiltration that are not immediately visible on conventional MRI. Thus a normal MRI does not exclude active CNS inflammation; the case patient illustrates this point—he had CSF lymphocytic pleocytosis and raised protein consistent with aseptic meningitis despite an unremarkable brain MRI early in the course.

How does CNS inflammation in BD promote inappropriate antidiuretic hormone (ADH) secretion? The connection lies in cytokine‑mediated modulation of hypothalamic and circumventricular structures that regulate arginine vasopressin (AVP, often referred to clinically as ADH) release. Proinflammatory cytokines produced in the periphery or intrathecally—most notably IL‑6—can stimulate hypothalamic neurons directly or indirectly via glial activation to increase AVP secretion. In several CNS inflammatory states (meningitis, encephalitis, subarachnoid haemorrhage) elevated CSF and serum IL‑6 levels correlate with increased ADH/AVP activity and the development of the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). In BD there is evidence of elevated IL‑6 in CSF during acute neuro‑BD, and the disease’s intrinsic ability to provoke robust IL‑6 production through activated monocytes and neutrophils provides a plausible mechanistic link to ADH dysregulation. In the present patient the temporal association—CNS symptoms and CSF inflammation concurrent with elevated ADH (22.4 pg/mL), low serum osmolality and concentrated urine—supports this pathophysiologic sequence, although direct measurement of CSF cytokines would be required to prove causality.

The pathobiology of SIADH is straightforward in physiological terms but has important clinical consequences. Excess circulating ADH acts at V2 receptors in the renal collecting duct to insert aquaporin‑2 channels into the apical membrane, increasing free‑water reabsorption independent of sodium transport. The result is retention of electrolyte‑free water, expansion of total body water that is small enough to maintain an apparently euvolemic state on clinical exam, and a fall in serum sodium concentration (dilutional hyponatraemia). Laboratory hallmarks are hypotonic hyponatraemia with inappropriately concentrated urine (urine osmolality often >300 mOsm/kg) and elevated urine sodium (>30 mmol/L) in the absence of adrenal, thyroid or renal failure and in the absence of diuretic use. Clinically, the spectrum of manifestations ranges from subtle neurocognitive symptoms (fatigue, headache, mild confusion, gait disturbance) to severe cerebral oedema with seizures, obtundation and coma when hyponatraemia develops rapidly or becomes profound. Less commonly appreciated are downstream complications such as rhabdomyolysis, which can occur in the setting of severe hyponatraemia and was manifest in the patient described by an elevated CK; persistent hiccups are another sometimes‑reported and under‑recognized manifestation that can reflect diaphragmatic irritability in the setting of electrolyte disturbance.

Several features of BD and its treatment can contribute to SIADH. Central nervous system inflammation—even when radiologically occult—can directly stimulate ADH release via cytokine signalling as above. Additionally, certain medications used in BD (eg, cyclophosphamide) have been implicated in SIADH through direct effects on renal concentrating mechanisms or by enhancing ADH secretion. In the present case no offending medication was identified and the timing favoured SIADH as a consequence of the inflammatory CNS process.

Therapeutic implications follow from understanding these mechanisms. Acute neuro‑BD is typically treated with high‑dose corticosteroids and often additional immunosuppression; these agents reduce meningeal and parenchymal inflammation and would be expected to lower cytokine levels and hence ADH drive. Colchicine, which was used successfully in this patient as monotherapy, has a mechanistic rationale: it inhibits microtubule polymerisation in neutrophils, reduces neutrophil chemotaxis and degranulation, and interferes with inflammasome activation and downstream production of IL‑1β and IL‑6. By attenuating the upstream inflammatory cascade, colchicine could plausibly reduce intrathecal cytokine concentrations and secondarily lower ADH stimulation. However, evidence that colchicine alone can consistently control acute parenchymal neuro‑BD is limited; most series support corticosteroids and steroid‑sparing immunosuppressants for moderate or severe CNS disease. The favorable response in this patient may reflect a relatively mild, self‑limited inflammatory episode, a prompt anti‑inflammatory effect of colchicine, or both. It is also worth noting practical limitations in validating the inflammation→IL‑6→ADH pathway in individual patients: AVP/ADH assays are technically challenging and pulse‑dependent, and copeptin (the stable C‑terminal portion of the vasopressin precursor) is now frequently used as a surrogate; similarly, CSF cytokine assays are not routinely available and lack standardisation.

In summary, BD sets the stage for neuro‑involvement by creating a neutrophil‑ and cytokine‑rich inflammatory environment that preferentially targets small vessels and perivascular regions in the CNS. When this inflammatory activity reaches hypothalamic or periventricular regulatory centres—or when inflammatory mediators diffuse into the CSF—it can provoke inappropriate ADH release, leading to the laboratory and clinical picture of SIADH. Recognising this pathophysiologic link is important because hyponatraemia may be a presenting or complicating feature of neuro‑BD and requires targeted management (fluid restriction, cautious use of hypertonic saline when indicated, and treatment of the underlying inflammation) to prevent neurologic deterioration and other complications.

## Differential Diagnosis

When a patient presents with fever, headache and CSF pleocytosis accompanied by hyponatraemia, the immediate clinical challenge is twofold: first, to distinguish infectious from non-infectious causes of meningo‑encephalitis; and second, to determine the mechanism of hyponatraemia and whether it reflects true hypotonic hyponatraemia (for which SIADH is a leading cause) or an endocrine/metabolic mimic. Acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD) can present as aseptic meningitis and precipitate SIADH through CNS inflammation, but many other conditions produce a very similar clinical picture. The following discussion organizes the major alternatives and highlights the features and tests that discriminate them from neuro‑BD and from an inflammation‑driven SIADH phenotype.

Infectious meningo‑encephalitis
Infectious causes must be excluded early because they require specific antimicrobial therapy. Viral agents (HSV-1/2, VZV, enteroviruses) are common causes of febrile meningo‑encephalitis with lymphocytic CSF pleocytosis. CSF PCR for HSV and VZV is highly sensitive and specific and should be obtained immediately if encephalitis is suspected; empiric acyclovir is often started pending results. Enteroviral PCR and serology may be informative during outbreaks or in immunocompromised hosts. Bacterial meningitis classically produces neutrophilic CSF pleocytosis, low glucose and markedly elevated protein, but partially treated or indolent bacterial infections (for example Listeria monocytogenes in older adults or neonates) can present atypically; blood cultures and CSF Gram stain and cultures remain essential. Tuberculous meningitis frequently causes lymphocytic pleocytosis with very high protein, low CSF glucose and elevated opening pressure; tests include acid‑fast staining, nucleic acid amplification tests and mycobacterial culture (which are slow). Cryptococcal meningitis often causes elevated opening pressure and may be diagnosed rapidly with CSF cryptococcal antigen (or India ink in resource‑limited settings), particularly in immunocompromised patients. Distinguishing features: infectious causes are supported by positive CSF PCR/culture, a neutrophilic predominance (bacterial), very low CSF glucose (TB, fungal), markedly raised opening pressure (cryptococcus), and focal parenchymal lesions on MRI in some viral infections. Early exclusion of these causes was pivotal in the presented case (negative CSF PCRs and cultures), making neuro‑inflammatory etiologies more likely.

Autoimmune and inflammatory mimics
A number of non‑infectious inflammatory disorders can produce subacute meningitis or meningo‑encephalitis with hyponatraemia and constitutional symptoms.

- Neurosarcoidosis often manifests with cranial neuropathies, chronic meningitis and leptomeningeal or pachymeningeal enhancement on contrast MRI; systemic clues include pulmonary hilar adenopathy, pulmonary infiltrates, erythema nodosum and elevated serum ACE or lysozyme (although these tests lack sensitivity). CSF may show lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein. Chest imaging and tissue biopsy of accessible lesions (if present) are critical to establish the diagnosis.

- Primary CNS vasculitides or systemic vasculitis with CNS involvement can present with multifocal neurologic deficits, headaches and scattered infarcts on MRI. Angiography (MR/CT angiography or catheter angiography) and vessel wall imaging may demonstrate vessel irregularities. Laboratory tests (ANCA, complement, inflammatory markers) and targeted systemic evaluation help discriminate vasculitis from neuro‑BD.

- Demyelinating antibody‑mediated disorders such as neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (AQP4‑IgG) or MOG antibody disease have characteristic clinical phenotypes (optic neuritis, longitudinally extensive transverse myelitis, relapsing demyelination) and MRI patterns that usually differ from BD; testing for AQP4 and MOG antibodies is indicated when the clinical presentation suggests demyelination.

Distinguishing neuro‑BD: neuro‑BD is suggested by the concurrence of typical mucocutaneous features (recurrent oral and genital ulcers, folliculitis or erythema nodosum‑like lesions), a compatible CSF picture (lymphocytic pleocytosis, raised protein), and characteristic parenchymal MRI lesions when present (brainstem, basal ganglia, thalamus). However, the meningitic (aseptic) form of neuro‑BD may have a normal early MRI, so the absence of MRI abnormalities does not exclude it—systemic mucocutaneous findings and clinical criteria remain central. Ancillary tests such as ACE or sIL‑2R are more helpful for sarcoid; their absence argues against neurosarcoidosis but does not rule it out.

Neoplastic causes: primary CNS lymphoma and leptomeningeal carcinomatosis
Neoplastic involvement of the CNS can mimic subacute meningitis and can also cause SIADH. Primary CNS lymphoma often presents with deep periventricular mass lesions that strongly enhance with contrast and demonstrate restricted diffusion on MRI; in immunocompromised patients the presentation can be fulminant. Leptomeningeal carcinomatosis produces diffuse leptomeningeal enhancement, high opening pressures and may present with multiple cranial neuropathies. CSF cytology and flow cytometry are the definitive investigations for leptomeningeal disease or primary CNS lymphoma with CSF involvement; however, multiple CSF samples may be required to detect malignant cells. Systemic imaging (CT chest/abdomen/pelvis or PET) is also necessary to search for an occult primary malignancy. In the present case, the lack of focal MRI lesions, negative CSF cytology and the prompt clinical resolution with anti‑inflammatory therapy make a neoplastic cause unlikely.

SIADH etiologies beyond CNS inflammation
SIADH is a common mechanism of euvolemic hypotonic hyponatraemia and may be triggered by many causes outside the CNS. Important alternatives to consider are:

- Pulmonary disease: pneumonia (including atypical and TB), empyema and small cell lung cancer (SCLC) can all provoke ectopic ADH secretion or stimulate ADH release via pulmonary afferents. A chest radiograph and, when indicated, CT chest are important parts of the evaluation.

- Medications: a wide range of drugs cause SIADH (or potentiate ADH action) — notably selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, tricyclic antidepressants, carbamazepine and oxcarbazepine, cyclophosphamide, vincristine, opioids and some antipsychotics. A careful medication history is essential. In BD, immunosuppressive agents such as cyclophosphamide have been reported to provoke SIADH; in our patient no offending medication preceded presentation.

- Paraneoplastic ectopic ADH production (SCLC being the classic example) requires chest imaging and tumor markers as clinically indicated.

Distinguishing features: pulmonary causes often have respiratory symptoms or pulmonary imaging abnormalities; drug‑induced SIADH follows exposure to a known culprit. In the case described, normal chest imaging and absence of relevant medications argued against these causes.

Endocrine and metabolic mimics of SIADH and hyponatraemia
Before labeling hyponatraemia as SIADH, endocrine disorders that alter free water handling must be excluded:

- Hypothyroidism can reduce free water clearance and mimic SIADH; measure TSH and free T4.

- Adrenal insufficiency (secondary or primary) causes hyponatraemia through cortisol deficiency and increased ADH secretion—measure serum cortisol and consider ACTH stimulation testing when cortisol is low or the clinical index of suspicion is high.

- Translocational (hyperosmolar) hyponatraemia: severe hyperglycaemia causes water to shift from the intracellular to extracellular compartment, lowering measured serum sodium. Always correct the sodium for hyperglycaemia when present (approximate correction: add ~1.6 mmol/L to measured sodium for each 100 mg/dL glucose above 100 mg/dL; this is an approximation and depends on the formula used). In the present patient the plasma glucose was high but correction minimally altered the sodium and did not fully explain the degree of hypotonic hyponatraemia.

- Pseudohyponatraemia: artifactually low sodium can occur when indirect ion‑selective electrode methods are used in the setting of severe hyperlipidaemia or hyperproteinaemia; direct ISE methods or measurement of plasma osmolality will clarify true tonicity.

Hyponatraemia classification and pragmatic diagnostic steps
A structured approach prevents misdiagnosis:

1. Confirm true hypotonic hyponatraemia by measuring serum osmolality. If measured osmolality is normal or high, consider pseudohyponatraemia or translocational hyponatraemia (e.g., hyperglycaemia).

2. Assess volume status clinically (euvolemic, hypovolemic, hypervolemic). This guides the differential: hypovolaemic hyponatraemia is commonly due to renal or extrarenal salt loss (diuretics, vomiting, diarrhoea); hypervolemic hyponatraemia suggests heart failure, cirrhosis or nephrotic syndrome.

3. In hypotonic hyponatraemia with apparent euvolaemia, obtain urine osmolality and urine sodium. SIADH is likely when urine osmolality is inappropriately elevated (>100–300 mOsm/kg, commonly >300) and urine sodium is >30 mmol/L in the setting of normal renal, adrenal and thyroid function.

4. Exclude hypothyroidism and adrenal insufficiency by measuring TSH and morning cortisol (or performing dynamic testing when indicated).

5. Seek a trigger for SIADH: CNS disease (infection, hemorrhage, tumor), pulmonary disease, medications, or malignancy.

Applying this framework to the case helps explain the diagnostic reasoning: the patient had hypotonic hyponatraemia with low serum osmolality, inappropriately concentrated urine (urine osmolality 900 mOsm/kg) and elevated urinary sodium (88 mmol/L), consistent with SIADH; thyroid and cortisol were normal, chest imaging was unrevealing, and there was no culprit medication. Thus central (CNS) inflammation was the most plausible trigger.

Clinical pearls to distinguish neuro‑BD from mimics
- Systemic mucocutaneous features are the hallmarks that point toward BD in the setting of aseptic meningitis. Recurrent painful genital ulcers, typical oral aphthae and pathergy (when present) are highly suggestive and should prompt consideration of BD even if MRI is normal.

- Timing and MRI pattern: parenchymal neuro‑BD classically involves the brainstem and deep grey nuclei; leptomeningeal or isolated aseptic meningitis forms may lack MRI parenchymal lesions initially. In contrast, neurosarcoidosis often shows leptomeningeal enhancement and systemic chest findings, while CNS lymphoma produces enhancing mass lesions with diffusion restriction.

- CSF profile: neuro‑BD typically causes a lymphocytic pleocytosis with moderately elevated protein; very low CSF glucose suggests TB or fungal infection rather than BD. Repeated CSF sampling for cultures, PCRs and cytology is appropriate when infection or malignancy remains in the differential.

- Response to therapy: rapid improvement with anti‑inflammatory therapy (colchicine, corticosteroids) supports an inflammatory etiology; however, definitive diagnosis of BD is clinical and requires demonstration of characteristic systemic features and exclusion of other causes.

Summary
The constellation of fever, aseptic meningitis and hyponatraemia demands a broad but focused evaluation. Infectious etiologies must be excluded urgently with CSF PCRs and cultures and appropriate imaging. Autoimmune/inflammatory diseases (neurosarcoidosis, vasculitis, demyelinating disorders) and neoplastic processes (primary CNS lymphoma, leptomeningeal carcinomatosis) should be considered and pursued with MRI (with contrast), targeted serologies, chest imaging and CSF cytology/flow cytometry. For hyponatraemia, a systematic workup—confirm tonicity, assess volume status, measure urine osmolality and sodium, and exclude hypothyroidism and adrenal insufficiency—will distinguish true SIADH from endocrine or artifactual mimics. In a patient with typical mucocutaneous BD manifestations plus CSF lymphocytosis and no other identifiable cause, acute neuro‑BD complicated by inflammation‑driven SIADH is a defensible diagnosis, but only after infection, malignancy and endocrine causes have been reasonably excluded.

## Diagnostic Workup

When a patient presents with mucocutaneous lesions and new central nervous system (CNS) symptoms, a structured, stepwise diagnostic approach is essential to (1) establish whether the patient meets criteria for Behçet’s disease (BD), (2) determine whether the CNS process represents neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD) or an alternative cause, and (3) identify and classify the cause of hyponatraemia if present (for example, syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion, SIADH). The case above illustrates how these three threads must be woven together: the mucocutaneous findings led to classification as definite BD, aseptic meningitis in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) pointed toward acute neuro‑BD despite a normal early MRI, and laboratory testing confirmed SIADH. Below is a practical diagnostic framework, with attention to common pitfalls.

1) Establishing the clinical diagnosis of Behçet’s disease
- Use the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (ICBD) as the initial classificatory tool. Points are allocated as follows: oral aphthae (2), genital aphthae (2), ocular involvement (2), skin lesions (1), vascular lesions (1), neurologic involvement (1), and a positive pathergy test (1). A total score of ≥4 supports a diagnosis of BD. In practice, careful bedside documentation of oral and genital ulcers, the timing and appearance of skin lesions, and a targeted ophthalmologic examination (to detect current or prior uveitis) are high‑yield.
- Ancillary tests such as HLA‑B51 can support but not establish the diagnosis; its presence increases disease probability in the appropriate clinical context but is neither necessary nor sufficient. Pathergy testing has variable sensitivity by region/ethnicity and should be interpreted accordingly.
- Avoid over-reliance on non‑specific serologic biomarkers (eg, ACE, soluble IL‑2 receptor) unless there is a specific clinical suspicion for an alternate diagnosis such as sarcoidosis or lymphoma; these tests can produce false leads if applied indiscriminately.

2) Evaluating suspected neuro‑Behçet’s disease
- Neuro‑BD is a clinical diagnosis that rests on compatible neurological features in the setting of BD and exclusion of mimics. Typical presentations include meningoencephalitis, brainstem syndromes, and parenchymal lesions with subacute progression. Headache is common but non‑specific.
- Brain MRI with and without gadolinium is the cornerstone of evaluation. Sequences should include T1, T2, FLAIR, diffusion‑weighted imaging, and post‑contrast images with attention to the brainstem and diencephalic/hypothalamic regions. However, MRI can be normal in early or purely meningeal/aseptic presentations — a normal early MRI does not exclude neuro‑BD. If clinical suspicion remains high, repeat MRI (often within 1–2 weeks) or follow‑up imaging is appropriate.
- Lumbar puncture is mandatory when CNS infection is in the differential or when neuro‑BD is suspected. CSF studies should include opening pressure, cell count with differential, protein, glucose, Gram stain, bacterial cultures, and targeted PCR testing for HSV, VZV, enteroviruses and, where indicated, mycobacterial studies and cryptococcal antigen. CSF cytology is useful if malignancy is being considered.
- Typical CSF findings in acute neuro‑BD are a lymphocytic (or mixed) pleocytosis and moderately elevated protein; glucose is usually normal. Oligoclonal IgG bands are typically absent (which can help distinguish neuro‑BD from multiple sclerosis), although this is not absolute. CSF interleukin‑6 (IL‑6) can be elevated and may be informative as an adjunct marker of CNS inflammation in specialized centers, but it is not required for diagnosis.
- Interpretation requires integration: CSF pleocytosis and elevated protein with negative infectious studies in a patient who meets ICBD criteria increases the likelihood of acute neuro‑BD.

3) Workup and confirmation of SIADH as the cause of hyponatraemia
- Confirm true hypotonic hyponatraemia before labeling SIADH: measure serum sodium and serum osmolality. SIADH requires a low serum osmolality (typically <275 mOsm/kg; some laboratories use <270) in the setting of hyponatraemia.
- Demonstrate inappropriate concentration of urine: urine osmolality that is inappropriately high for the plasma hypo‑osmolal state (generally >100 mOsm/kg and commonly >300 mOsm/kg) indicates impaired water excretion.
- Urine sodium is usually >30 mmol/L when the patient is euvolemic and dietary sodium intake is not severely restricted. Low urine sodium suggests volume depletion or recent diuretic use.
- Confirm euvolemia clinically (no signs of hypovolemia such as orthostasis or tachycardia, and no signs of hypervolemia such as pulmonary edema or peripheral edema). Be aware that clinical assessment of volume status can be challenging and requires integration of history, exam, and laboratory data.
- Exclude other causes of hyponatraemia: normal thyroid function tests (TSH and free T4), normal adrenal function (random cortisol may suffice in many settings, with stimulation testing if there is concern for insufficiency), and normal renal function. A careful medication review is essential because thiazide diuretics, some antidepressants, anticonvulsants and chemotherapeutic agents are frequent culprits. Pulmonary disease and malignancy must also be considered as potential causes of SIADH.
- Adjust measured serum sodium for severe hyperglycaemia before making the diagnosis. Hyperglycaemia causes osmotic translocation of water from intracellular to extracellular compartments and lowers measured sodium. A practical correction is: add approximately 1.6 mmol/L to the measured sodium for every 5.6 mmol/L (100 mg/dL) increase of glucose above ~5.6 mmol/L. Example from the case: plasma glucose 14.2 mmol/L (≈256 mg/dL) represents ≈8.6 mmol/L above 5.6 mmol/L; corrected Na would increase modestly (~+2–3 mmol/L). Correcting for glucose avoids misclassification of hyponatraemia and prevents inappropriate treatment.
- Additional laboratory clues: low serum uric acid and low BUN are common in SIADH but are non‑specific.

4) Excluding infections, malignancy and other mimics
- Because CNS infection can mimic neuro‑BD and cause SIADH, it must be actively excluded. Empiric antimicrobial or antiviral therapy is reasonable while results are pending if the pretest probability of infection is significant.
- Evaluate for malignancy (particularly small cell lung cancer) as a cause of SIADH when indicated by clinical features or imaging; chest radiography or CT chest is appropriate when pulmonary symptoms or risk factors exist.
- If neurosarcoidosis, CNS lymphoma, or other inflammatory disorders are part of the differential, target tests should be ordered only when clinical or imaging clues exist (eg, suspicious systemic findings, lymphadenopathy, focal MRI lesions). Non‑specific biomarkers such as ACE and sIL‑2R have limited sensitivity and specificity and commonly give false leads if used in an undifferentiated workup; interpret them in the clinical context.

5) Practical integration and common pitfalls
- Do not exclude neuro‑BD solely because early MRI is normal. A normal MRI early in the disease course can occur with aseptic meningitis or limited parenchymal involvement; rely on clinical findings and CSF studies and consider repeat imaging if symptoms persist or worsen.
- Avoid over‑testing with low‑yield assays that are unlikely to change management. Targeted testing guided by the history, exam, and basic imaging/CSF findings is more productive than broad panels that generate incidental abnormalities.
- Always correct sodium for severe hyperglycaemia and review medications before diagnosing SIADH. Diuretics, recent intravenous fluids, and osmotic agents can confound urine sodium and osmolality measurements.
- Measure and monitor electrolytes frequently when treating hyponatraemia. Rapid over‑correction can cause osmotic demyelination; while this is a management concern, it underscores the importance of an accurate initial diagnosis and careful monitoring.
- Recognize that laboratory measurements of ADH are rarely required for routine diagnosis of SIADH; elevated ADH, when measured, may support the diagnosis but is not necessary. Similarly, CSF IL‑6 can be informative in academic centers but is not required for routine diagnosis of neuro‑BD.

In summary, the diagnostic workup of a patient with suspected BD, new neurologic symptoms and hyponatraemia is an exercise in synthesis: apply the ICBD to classify BD, obtain MRI and lumbar puncture to define CNS inflammation and to exclude infection, and use standard biochemical criteria (with correction for hyperglycaemia and exclusion of endocrine and renal causes) to diagnose SIADH. The case presented exemplifies this approach: detailed mucocutaneous inspection and application of the ICBD established BD; CSF showed aseptic meningitis consistent with acute neuro‑BD despite a normal early MRI; and a focused endocrine and laboratory assessment met criteria for SIADH after correcting for hyperglycaemia and excluding thyroid/adrenal disease and diuretics.

## Management

Management of a patient with acute neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) complicated by SIADH requires two parallel and coordinated strategies: control the CNS inflammation that is driving the disease, and safely correct and monitor the hyponatraemia. Each problem influences the other—successful suppression of neuroinflammation is often the definitive treatment for SIADH in this context, while inappropriate or overly rapid correction of sodium can cause catastrophic neurologic injury. Treatment choices should therefore balance the urgency of controlling inflammation against the risks of sodium correction, drug toxicity, and infectious complications from immunosuppression.

Controlling neuroinflammation: first-line and escalation
- First-line therapy for acute neuro-BD is high‑dose corticosteroids. For patients with suspected or proven parenchymal CNS involvement or meningoencephalitis causing significant symptoms, the usual initial regimen is intravenous methylprednisolone 1 g daily for 3–5 days, followed by an oral prednisone taper (typical starting dose ~1 mg/kg/day, maximum ~60–80 mg/day) with gradual tapering over weeks to months guided by clinical response and relapse risk. Rapid initiation of steroids is frequently necessary to prevent permanent neurologic deficit.
- For most patients, a steroid‑sparing immunosuppressant is added when there is relapsing disease, high risk of recurrence, or need for prolonged steroid exposure. Common options with evidence in BD include azathioprine (generally 2–3 mg/kg/day, after TPMT testing when available) and methotrexate (typically 15–25 mg weekly, subcutaneously or intramuscularly, with folic acid supplementation). Mycophenolate mofetil is an alternative in some practices (up to 2–3 g/day), although comparative trial data are limited.
- For fulminant, refractory or progressive parenchymal neuro-BD despite steroids and conventional immunosuppressants, escalation to biologic therapy is recommended. Anti‑TNF agents have the strongest evidence in refractory neuro‑BD: infliximab (5 mg/kg IV at weeks 0, 2, 6, then every 6–8 weeks) and adalimumab (often an 80 mg loading dose followed by 40 mg every other week) are commonly used and can induce durable control. Prior to TNF blockade, screen for latent tuberculosis, hepatitis B and other infections, and counsel patients about infection risk.
- IL‑6 blockade (e.g., tocilizumab) has been used in selected refractory cases and when TNF inhibitors fail or are contraindicated; evidence is limited to case series and reports. Cyclophosphamide may be used for life‑ or function‑threatening disease in some centres, but cyclosporine A should be avoided in patients with neuro‑BD or who are at risk for CNS involvement because of a well‑described association with neurotoxicity and precipitating neurologic symptoms.
- Role of colchicine: colchicine is effective for mucocutaneous and some joint manifestations of BD (typical dosing for mucocutaneous disease is 0.5 mg to 1 mg total daily, often given as 0.5 mg twice daily), but randomized data and reviews do not support colchicine as a reliable monotherapy for established parenchymal neuro‑BD. Nevertheless, in milder phenotypes—patients with aseptic meningitis, minimal deficits, or when corticosteroids are contraindicated—it may be reasonable to trial colchicine while maintaining a low threshold for escalation. When used, monitor for gastrointestinal intolerance, cytopenias and drug interactions (especially renal impairment and concomitant P‑glycoprotein or CYP3A4 inhibitors).

Treating the SIADH: immediate and staged measures
- The initial steps in SIADH focus on assessing severity and symptoms, instituting simple measures, and preventing overly rapid sodium shifts. For asymptomatic or mildly symptomatic patients with chronic or subacute SIADH, conservative therapy—fluid restriction to approximately 500–1,000 mL/day (individualized by insensible losses and ongoing outputs)—is first‑line, together with removal of offending drugs and treatment of the underlying cause (here, suppression of CNS inflammation).
- In more severe or symptomatic hyponatraemia (e.g., seizures, severe confusion, obtundation) prompt hypertonic saline is indicated. The commonly used initial approach for severe symptoms is a 100 mL bolus of 3% saline over 10 minutes, repeated up to two additional times as needed to produce a modest and rapid rise in serum sodium (typically 4–6 mmol/L) or clear clinical improvement. After acute stabilization, slow controlled correction is essential: target an increase no greater than 6–8 mmol/L in the first 24 hours (some guidelines allow up to 8 mmol/L), and no more than ~18 mmol/L over 48 hours. Patients with risk factors for osmotic demyelination (malnutrition, alcoholism, advanced liver disease, hypokalemia) should be treated more conservatively.
- For euvolemic SIADH that is fluid‑restrictive‑refractory or where restriction is impractical, several options exist: oral salt tablets combined with a loop diuretic (to reduce medullary concentrating ability), oral urea (an effective osmotic agent widely used in Europe and increasingly used elsewhere; typical doses 15–30 g/day), and demeclocyline (rarely used because of nephrotoxicity and slow onset). Vasopressin receptor antagonists (vaptans such as tolvaptan) produce aquaresis and rapid sodium correction and can be effective in selected hospitalized patients; however, they are costly, require careful inpatient monitoring to avoid overcorrection, and have been associated with hepatotoxicity with longer‑term use. In practice, vaptans are reserved for short‑term use under specialist guidance when other measures fail or are contraindicated.
- Close monitoring is mandatory: serum sodium should be checked every 4–6 hours during active correction or hypertonic therapy, with concomitant tracking of urine output, urine osmolality and urine sodium to assess ongoing ADH activity. Stop or slow hypertonic infusion once sodium has risen sufficiently (typically by 4–6 mmol/L) or symptoms improve. Be vigilant for “autocorrection” when the inciting cause (neuroinflammation) begins to resolve—urine osmolality may fall and prompt rapid sodium rises; frequent monitoring allows early detection and measures (free water or desmopressin) to blunt overcorrection if necessary.

Practical sequencing and integration in the case of neuro‑BD with SIADH
- Start with a simultaneous but prioritized plan: stabilize serum sodium and neurologic status, and treat the presumed inflammatory driver. In patients with severe neurologic compromise from neuro‑BD, immediate high‑dose IV steroids should not be delayed while investigating hyponatraemia; at the same time, institute appropriate measures to prevent dangerous sodium shifts (e.g., controlled hypertonic saline for severe symptoms, fluid restriction for milder cases).
- Because CNS inflammation commonly drives SIADH, effective anti‑inflammatory treatment often corrects the inappropriate ADH secretion. Expect improvement in serum sodium as disease activity falls; this temporal relationship should guide expectations and monitoring frequency.
- Avoid medications that may aggravate SIADH (certain antidepressants, carbamazepine, selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, some antipsychotics) unless they are essential; review the medication list carefully before starting immunosuppressive therapy.

Safety considerations and monitoring on immunosuppression
- Screen for latent infections (tuberculosis with interferon‑gamma release assay and chest X‑ray, hepatitis B surface antigen/core antibody and hepatitis C testing) prior to starting biologics or other long‑term immunosuppressants. Vaccinate appropriately (influenza, pneumococcal, others) before immunosuppression where feasible.
- Monitor laboratory parameters routinely: complete blood counts and liver enzymes for azathioprine (and TPMT testing where available), methotrexate (LFTs, renal function, and provide folic acid), and for colchicine when used chronically (renal function, particularly in the elderly).
- Be aware that cyclosporine A carries a risk of neurotoxicity and is best avoided in patients with known or suspected neuro‑BD; if cyclosporine is used for other BD manifestations, monitor closely and reconsider alternatives if neurologic symptoms arise.
- In patients with hyponatraemia, correct co‑morbid metabolic contributors: correct hypokalemia (which will increase serum sodium when replaced), and account for hyperglycaemia—serum sodium should be “corrected” for hyperglycaemia when interpreting values and planning therapy. In the index case, poor glycaemic control likely affected sodium measurements and should be optimized.

Topical and supportive measures
- While systemic therapy is instituted, treat mucocutaneous lesions symptomatically: topical corticosteroids or analgesic mouth rinses for oral aphthae, topical antiseptics or silver sulfadiazine for genital ulcers as needed, and supportive measures for pain and nutrition. Address complications such as rhabdomyolysis (as in the presented patient) with careful fluid management balancing the need for renal protection and the risk of worsening hyponatraemia.
- Provide rehabilitation and assess for cognitive or focal deficits before discharge; arrange close outpatient follow‑up with neurology, rheumatology or a multidisciplinary BD clinic and regular sodium monitoring until stabilization is confirmed.

When colchicine alone may be considered
- The reported case responded to colchicine monotherapy, which is informative but not generalizable. Colchicine is an appropriate first‑line agent for mucocutaneous BD and may be considered in patients with very mild, self‑limited neuro‑inflammatory presentations (aseptic meningitis without focal deficits and normal or stable imaging) when corticosteroids are undesirable or contraindicated. However, clinicians should maintain a low threshold to escalate to corticosteroids and immunosuppressants if neurologic symptoms progress or do not improve rapidly, because delaying effective therapy risks permanent neurologic injury.

Summary
- Manage neuro‑BD aggressively when parenchymal involvement or significant neurologic symptoms are present, using high‑dose IV corticosteroids followed by an appropriate steroid‑sparing agent; escalate to anti‑TNF biologics or other agents for refractory disease. Avoid cyclosporine in patients at risk for CNS disease.
- Treat SIADH with a staged approach: fluid restriction and removal of reversible contributors for mild cases; hypertonic saline for severe or symptomatic hyponatraemia with strict limits on the rate of correction (≤6–8 mmol/L in 24 h, ≤18 mmol/L in 48 h); consider salt + loop diuretic, urea, or vaptans in refractory situations under specialist guidance.
- Coordinate care so that therapy for the underlying neuroinflammation and sodium management proceed in parallel, with frequent monitoring (serum sodium every 4–6 hours during active correction) and attention to drug safety, infection screening and comorbidity optimisation.

## Prognosis, Follow-up, and Clinical Pearls

Prognosis after an episode of acute neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) is heterogeneous and depends largely on the pattern and severity of CNS involvement and the rapidity with which inflammation is controlled. Neuro-BD may present as a monophasic aseptic meningitis-like illness that resolves completely, as in the current case, but more commonly it follows a relapsing–remitting course. When parenchymal lesions occur—particularly in the brainstem, thalamus or hemispheres—patients are at increased risk for permanent neurological deficits, cumulative disability and cognitive decline with recurrent attacks. Early recognition and suppression of CNS inflammation reduce the likelihood of irreversible damage; therefore timeliness of immunomodulatory therapy is a major determinant of long-term outcome.

Clinical vigilance is required even when early imaging is normal. Brain MRI can be normal in the first days of an inflammatory episode of neuro-BD, particularly when the phenotype is predominantly meningeal or when lesions are small. A normal early MRI does not exclude neuro-BD; if clinical suspicion remains, repeat imaging (with and without contrast, using thin slices and dedicated brainstem sequences) should be obtained within 1–4 weeks or sooner if the clinical picture worsens. The development of new focal deficits, prolonged or progressive symptoms, or new cognitive impairment should prompt re-imaging and reconsideration of escalation of immunotherapy. In practice, patients with an initial self-limited presentation warrant close outpatient follow-up at short intervals (weeks) to detect relapse early; those with parenchymal disease typically require more intensive follow-up and longer-term immunosuppression.

Hyponatraemia and SIADH in the context of neuro-BD deserve particular attention because they both affect management and can produce misleading clinical features. When a patient with suspected or established BD presents with headache, fever or other CNS symptoms plus hyponatraemia, maintain a high index of suspicion for SIADH related to CNS inflammation. The diagnosis of SIADH should be based on the standard biochemical and clinical criteria (hypotonic hyponatraemia, inappropriately concentrated urine, elevated urine sodium in the setting of euvolemia, and exclusion of hypothyroidism, adrenal insufficiency and recent diuretic use), rather than reliance on plasma ADH measurements. ADH assays are technically difficult, variably reproducible, and often unhelpful for clinical decision-making; they can be misleading, as illustrated by the elevated ADH in this case. In the acute setting, manage SIADH according to severity: mild or asymptomatic cases respond to fluid restriction and addressing the underlying inflammatory driver; severe symptomatic hyponatraemia (seizures, obtundation) requires controlled administration of hypertonic saline to raise serum sodium promptly enough to relieve cerebral edema while avoiding excessive correction. Aim for a modest acute rise sufficient to reverse severe symptoms (for example, several mmol/L), then limit the total correction to a safe rate—generally no more than about 8–10 mmol/L in 24 hours—to minimize the risk of osmotic demyelination. Monitor serum sodium frequently (every 2–4 hours while actively correcting), reassess volume status, and treat the inflammatory disorder that is driving inappropriate ADH release. Recognize that non-neurological clues—persistent hiccups, anorexia, nausea, or an unexpected rise in creatine kinase/rhabdomyolysis—can be manifestations of significant hyponatraemia and should prompt measurement of serum sodium.

Follow-up after an episode of neuro-BD should be structured and multidisciplinary. Early post‑discharge contact is important to confirm clinical improvement and sodium stability. Neurology follow-up with periodic examinations and cognitive screening is indicated; repeat MRI is reasonable if symptoms persist or recur. Ophthalmology evaluation is essential because ocular involvement can progress silently and strongly influences morbidity; schedule regular eye exams even if none were present at the index episode. Because BD is a multisystem vasculitis, screen and monitor for vascular involvement (venous thrombosis and arterial aneurysms) guided by symptoms and physical findings—utilise vascular imaging selectively rather than as routine indiscriminate testing. Rehabilitation services should be engaged for any residual motor, vestibular or cognitive deficits.

Decisions about long-term immunosuppression should be individualized according to phenotype, severity and relapse risk. Colchicine is appropriate and often effective for mucocutaneous and arthritic manifestations and—as in this patient—can be associated with resolution of a self-limited neuro-meningitic presentation. However, the standard approach to acute parenchymal neuro-BD commonly includes high-dose corticosteroids to rapidly control inflammation, usually followed by a steroid‑sparing immunosuppressant (azathioprine, mycophenolate, cyclophosphamide for severe cases) or a biologic agent (anti-TNF agents such as infliximab or adalimumab) for refractory or relapsing disease. Avoid medications known to worsen neurological disease: cyclosporine is associated with neurotoxicity and has been linked to new or worsening neuro-BD and should be avoided in patients with CNS involvement. Prior to initiation of long-term immunosuppression, perform appropriate baseline screening (TB testing, hepatitis serologies, vaccinations where appropriate) and institute monitoring protocols for drug toxicity (periodic CBCs, liver and renal function tests, and vigilance for infectious complications).

Preventing iatrogenic harm is a consistent theme in follow-up care. Avoid indiscriminate testing that is unlikely to alter management; use targeted investigations driven by the clinical picture. Correct hyponatraemia cautiously and under close monitoring to prevent osmotic demyelination. When instituting immunosuppression, use agents appropriate to the neuro-BD phenotype and avoid cyclosporine when CNS disease is present. Educate patients about relapse symptoms—new focal neurological signs, persistent severe headache, altered cognition, visual changes or recurrence of mucocutaneous lesions—and ensure rapid access to care because early treatment of relapse preserves neurologic function.

Clinical pearls
- Acute neuro-BD may be monophasic but has a meaningful risk of relapse and cumulative disability; early and appropriate control of CNS inflammation improves outcomes.  
- A normal brain MRI early in the course does not exclude neuro-BD; repeat imaging and close clinical follow-up are essential.  
- In BD patients with headache or fever plus hyponatraemia, think about SIADH from CNS inflammation; use standard biochemical criteria and exclude mimics rather than relying on ADH assays.  
- Persistent hiccups, unexplained anorexia or an unexpected rise in CK can be clues to clinically important hyponatraemia.  
- Correct hyponatraemia slowly and under frequent monitoring; treat severe neurologic symptoms with controlled hypertonic saline but limit total correction rates to reduce risk of osmotic demyelination.  
- Tailor immunosuppression to the neuro-BD phenotype: use steroids and steroid‑sparing agents for parenchymal disease; avoid cyclosporine if CNS involvement is present; consider biologics for refractory or recurrent cases.  
- Long‑term follow-up should include neurologic and ophthalmologic surveillance, assessment for vascular disease, monitoring for treatment toxicity and rehabilitation for residual deficits.

Taken together, these principles will help clinicians recognise and manage the short‑term complications (such as SIADH), reduce the risk of long‑term neurological morbidity, and limit iatrogenic harm through judicious investigation and appropriately targeted therapy.
