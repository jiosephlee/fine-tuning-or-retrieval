Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

### [Differential Diagnosis] What clinical features help distinguish Behçet’s mucocutaneous lesions (oral aphthae, genital ulcers, folliculitis-like rash) from mimics such as HSV infection, aphthous stomatitis alone, or bacterial folliculitis?

Key teaching point: distinguish mucocutaneous Behçet’s lesions by their clinical pattern — recurrent, multisite, intensely painful ulcers (oral and genital) with a propensity to be deep and to scar, plus sterile “folliculitis‑like” papulopustules and systemic inflammatory features — rather than by any single lesion’s appearance. The diagnosis is clinical and rests on combinations of findings and course that differ from HSV, simple aphthous stomatitis, or bacterial folliculitis.

Why this matters (clinical reasoning)
Behçet’s is a systemic neutrophilic inflammatory disorder. Its mucocutaneous lesions reflect that systemic process and therefore tend to be recurrent, occur at multiple mucocutaneous sites simultaneously, be more painful/deeper, and be associated with other inflammatory manifestations (fever, elevated CRP, uveitis, pathergy, HLA‑B51 association, or — as in this case — neurologic symptoms). In contrast the mimics are usually more limited in distribution, have characteristic lesion morphology or microbiologic findings, and follow different natural histories and treatment responses.

Practical discriminators you can use at the bedside

- Pattern and multiplicity: Behçet’s usually produces recurrent oral aphthae plus at least one other mucocutaneous feature (genital ulcers or typical skin lesions). The concurrent occurrence of oral ulcers, genital ulceration (classically painful scrotal/labial ulcers) and sterile papulopustular/folliculitis‑like lesions strongly favors Behçet’s and fulfils diagnostic criteria when recurrent.

- Ulcer characteristics and healing: Oral ulcers in simple aphthous stomatitis are usually small (minor aphthae), shallow, heal without scarring. In Behçet’s ulcers are often larger/deeper (major aphthae), more painful, and genital ulcers particularly tend to be deep and heal with scarring — scarring of genital ulcers is a useful distinguishing feature that points away from idiopathic aphthae or HSV.

- Temporal course / recurrence: Behçet’s lesions recur repeatedly over months–years and often in different sites; HSV recurrences are episodic but classically involve the same anatomical site and are often preceded by grouped vesicles. Ask about prior similar episodes and sites.

- Morphology and prodrome of genital lesions: HSV usually begins with grouped vesicles that rupture to form shallow painful ulcers; lesions are superficial and often clustered. Behçet’s genital ulcers more commonly present as solitary or multiple deep punched‑out ulcers without an antecedent vesicular phase.

- Skin lesions: The “folliculitis‑like” BD lesions are erythematous papules/pustules that are sterile on culture and may appear at sites of trauma (pathergy). True bacterial folliculitis has pustules centered on hair follicles, often with surrounding erythema, purulence, positive bacterial cultures, and clinical response to antibiotics. A positive pathergy test or sterile pustules on culture supports Behçet’s.

- Systemic context: Fever, markedly elevated inflammatory markers, ocular inflammation (uveitis), arthralgia, or neurologic findings point to systemic disease (Behçet’s), not isolated HSV or aphthous stomatitis. In this case the combination of fever, high CRP, aseptic meningitis and hyponatraemia with mucocutaneous lesions strongly favored systemic Behçet’s disease.

- Microbiology and labs: HSV diagnosis is supported by PCR or culture from lesion swab or by Tzanck/cytology; bacterial folliculitis yields positive bacterial cultures. Behçet’s lesions are culture‑negative. HLA‑B51 and a positive pathergy test are supportive but neither is required for diagnosis.

- Response to therapy: HSV responds to antivirals; bacterial folliculitis improves with appropriate antibiotics; Behçet’s mucocutaneous disease typically responds to colchicine, topical steroids, or other anti‑inflammatory/immunomodulatory agents and does not remit with antibiotics or antivirals.

Apply these principles to the case
In this patient the simultaneous painful oral aphthae, the appearance of a painful scrotal ulcer that is typical for Behçet’s (deep and likely to scar), sterile folliculitis‑like eruptions, systemic inflammation (high CRP), and later neurologic involvement all argued against isolated HSV, simple aphthous stomatitis, or bacterial folliculitis and supported the diagnosis of systemic Behçet’s disease.
