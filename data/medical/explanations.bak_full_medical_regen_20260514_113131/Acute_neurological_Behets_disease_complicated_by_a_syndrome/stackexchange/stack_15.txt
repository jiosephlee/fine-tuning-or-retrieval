Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

### [Clinical Pearls] In a newly diagnosed Behçet’s patient with nonspecific CNS symptoms (headache, vertigo) and hyponatremia, which clinical and laboratory features should raise suspicion for concurrent acute neuro-Behçet’s complicated by SIADH?

Think of acute neuro‑Behçet’s complicated by SIADH when a patient with new or newly recognised systemic Behçet’s features develops nonspecific central nervous system symptoms together with euvolaemic hyponatraemia and laboratory evidence of inappropriate antidiuresis.

Clinically, the red flags are the coexistence of characteristic mucocutaneous findings of BD (recurrent oral aphthae, folliculitis‑like rash, genital ulcers) at the time neurological symptoms arise, even if those neurological symptoms are non‑focal — headache, vertigo, persistent hiccups or malaise — because neuro‑BD can present as aseptic meningitis/meningoencephalitis without focal deficits. Note that brain MRI may be normal early, so a normal scan does not exclude neuro‑BD.

Laboratory features that should increase your suspicion for SIADH in this setting are true hypotonic hyponatraemia with clinical euvolaemia, low serum osmolality, inappropriately concentrated urine (elevated urine osmolality >300 mOsm/kg) and elevated urine sodium (>20–40 mmol/L). After excluding hypothyroidism and adrenal insufficiency (normal TSH, free T4 and morning cortisol in this case), an elevated plasma ADH (if available) or the constellation above supports SIADH due to a central cause. Supportive inflammatory signals — high CRP/ESR and mild CSF lymphocytic pleocytosis with raised protein consistent with aseptic meningitis — point toward CNS inflammation as the driver of inappropriate ADH release. Ancillary clues described in this case include hiccups and rhabdomyolysis (elevated CK), both of which can be manifestations or complications of significant hyponatraemia.

Putting it together: when a patient with new BD features presents with headache/vertigo ± hiccups and you find euvolaemic hyponatraemia with low serum osmolality, concentrated urine and elevated urine sodium — and you have excluded thyroid and adrenal causes and common CNS infections — think neuro‑BD causing SIADH. Key practical steps are to confirm the biochemical diagnosis (serum and urine osmolality, urine sodium), obtain CSF to look for aseptic meningitis, obtain MRI (recognising it can be normal early), exclude infection/malignancy, and manage the hyponatraemia cautiously while treating the underlying inflammation.
