Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

### [Management] What empiric antimicrobial coverage is appropriate while evaluating a febrile patient with suspected encephalitis/meningitis, and what clinical or laboratory results allow safe de-escalation?

When a patient presents with fever and suspected meningitis/encephalitis you must treat for the most immediately treatable and dangerous causes while you collect diagnostic data — then narrow or stop therapy based on microbiology, CSF pattern and the clinical course.

Empiric regimen to start immediately (after blood cultures, and do not delay lumbar puncture if no contraindication)
Give broad-spectrum bacterial coverage plus empiric antiviral therapy for HSV encephalitis.

- Bacterial: a third‑generation cephalosporin (ceftriaxone 2 g IV q12h or cefotaxime) plus vancomycin to cover resistant Streptococcus pneumoniae. Add ampicillin (2 g IV q4h) if the patient is >50 years, immunocompromised, has listeriosis risk, or there is high clinical suspicion for Listeria. This regimen covers the common community bacterial pathogens (S. pneumoniae, N. meningitidis, H. influenzae) and resistant pneumococci; ampicillin adds Listeria coverage.
- Viral: acyclovir IV (10 mg/kg q8h, adjusted for renal function) for empiric coverage of HSV (and VZV) encephalitis because untreated HSV carries high morbidity and mortality.
- Tailor additional coverage to local epidemiology and risk: consider broader gram‑negative coverage (e.g., cefepime or meropenem) for nosocomial infection, recent neurosurgery, skull fracture, or severe immunosuppression. Consider adjunctive dexamethasone when bacterial meningitis due to S. pneumoniae is suspected and before/with the first antibiotic dose.

How and when to de‑escalate
Use the combination of microbiology results, CSF cell profile/glucose/protein, PCR testing, blood cultures, and the patient’s clinical trajectory.

- Bacterial pathogens identified in CSF or blood: de‑escalate to targeted therapy guided by susceptibility as soon as results are available.
- Negative bacterial Gram stain/cultures at 48–72 hours, a lymphocytic CSF pleocytosis with normal or only mildly reduced CSF glucose and no organism on Gram stain, and negative bacterial PCRs (if available): strongly favour aseptic/viral or inflammatory causes. If bacterial cultures remain negative at 48–72 h and the patient is clinically improving, it is usually safe to stop empiric antibacterial agents (but continue antibacterial therapy if clinical or lab data continue to suggest bacterial disease).
- HSV/VZV PCR negative at 48 hours (and clinical picture inconsistent with HSV encephalitis, e.g., no temporal lobe signs, improving mentation): discontinue acyclovir. If CSF HSV PCR timing is early (<24 h from symptom onset) and clinical suspicion remains high, repeat testing is reasonable before stopping.
- Listeria: because blood/CSF cultures may be slow, maintain ampicillin until Listeria is confidently excluded — typically at least 48–72 h — or until an alternative pathogen is identified and the clinical picture permits stopping.
- Blood cultures: if negative at 48 h and no other source identified, this supports de‑escalation when combined with CSF data.
- Clinical course: marked clinical improvement (defervescence, improving mentation, normalization of inflammatory markers) supports narrowing therapy; ongoing fever, worsening neurologic status, or radiographic progression mandates continuing or broadening coverage and re‑evaluation.

Apply this to the case
In the presented patient the team appropriately started broad antibacterial coverage (they used cefepime) and added acyclovir when encephalitis became a concern. Once CSF showed lymphocytic pleocytosis, CSF cultures and HSV/VZV PCRs were repeatedly negative and the patient clinically improved, it was appropriate to stop antivirals and narrow/stop antibiotics rather than continue prolonged empiric therapy. The safe trigger to de‑escalate was the combination of negative microbiology at 48–72 hours, a CSF profile consistent with aseptic meningitis, and clinical improvement — exactly the sequence that allowed the clinicians to focus treatment on Behçet’s-associated inflammation (colchicine) rather than continued empiric antimicrobials.
