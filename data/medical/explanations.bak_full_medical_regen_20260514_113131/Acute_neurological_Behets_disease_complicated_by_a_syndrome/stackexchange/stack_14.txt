Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

### [Pathophysiology] Describe colchicine’s mechanism of action relevant to Behçet’s disease and list the BD manifestations for which colchicine has the strongest evidence of benefit.

Colchicine works in Behçet’s disease by targeting the cellular machinery that drives the dominant neutrophil‑mediated inflammation. It binds tubulin and inhibits microtubule polymerization, which impairs neutrophil chemotaxis, adhesion and degranulation, reduces inflammasome activation and downstream release of IL‑1β (and secondarily IL‑6), and interferes with endothelial–leukocyte interactions. Those actions explain why colchicine is particularly effective for manifestations in which acute neutrophilic recruitment and cytokine release are central.

Clinical trial and guideline evidence support colchicine most strongly for the mucocutaneous and articular features of Behçet’s: recurrent oral and genital aphthous ulcers, erythema‑nodosum–type and acneiform skin lesions, and inflammatory arthritis. In these settings colchicine is a standard first‑line, steroid‑sparing agent. By contrast, high‑quality data do not support colchicine as effective for major organ involvement — ocular disease (uveitis), large‑vessel vasculitis, gastrointestinal BD or parenchymal neuro‑Behçet’s — which generally require corticosteroids and additional immunosuppression or biologics. In practice, colchicine may contribute to lowering cytokine levels (eg, IL‑6) and therefore could plausibly ameliorate secondary phenomena such as SIADH related to CNS inflammation, but one should not rely on colchicine alone for established severe neuro‑ or ocular disease.
