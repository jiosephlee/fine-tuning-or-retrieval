Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

### [Clinical Pearls] How should a normal early brain MRI influence your diagnostic reasoning in suspected acute neuro-Behçet’s disease presenting as aseptic meningitis?

A normal early brain MRI should not reassure you that neuro‑Behçet’s disease is absent when the clinical picture points toward CNS inflammation. In Behçet’s the neurological phenotype is heterogeneous: the classic “parenchymal” form produces brainstem or hemispheric lesions that are usually visible on MRI, but the acute meningo‑encephalitic/aseptic meningitis presentation often lacks parenchymal abnormalities early in the course. Thus a normal scan is a common and expected finding in early neuro‑BD and must be interpreted in context rather than as a rule‑out test.

Clinically this means you must integrate the history and examination (recent mucocutaneous lesions, genital ulcers, oral aphthae), CSF findings (lymphocytic pleocytosis, elevated protein, sterile studies), and exclusion of infectious, neoplastic and alternative inflammatory causes. Continue an appropriate infection workup and treat empirically as indicated until pathogens are excluded; do not stop diagnostic momentum solely because MRI is normal. Repeat MRI with contrast and focused brainstem/hypothalamic sequences if symptoms progress or fail to improve, because lesions can appear later or be too small to detect initially. Consider adjunctive testing (repeat CSF, targeted serologies, inflammatory markers such as CSF IL‑6 if available) and early neurology/rheumatology consultation to guide immunotherapy.

Therapeutic decisions should be guided by clinical severity rather than radiology alone: significant neurologic dysfunction or progressive disease generally warrants corticosteroids ± additional immunosuppression, whereas mild, self‑limited meningeal presentations may respond to colchicine and supportive care, with close monitoring. Finally, be vigilant for complications of CNS inflammation such as SIADH; normal imaging does not preclude dysregulated ADH and symptomatic hyponatraemia. In short, don’t let a normal early MRI override clinical and CSF evidence of neuro‑Behçet’s — treat the patient, not the scan, and reassess with repeat imaging and tests if the clinical course changes.
