Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

### [Differential Diagnosis] How do you interpret a mildly elevated soluble IL-2 receptor (sIL-2R) in the setting of systemic inflammation when evaluating for hematologic malignancy or sarcoidosis?

A mildly elevated soluble interleukin‑2 receptor (sIL‑2R) should be interpreted as a nonspecific marker of immune activation, not as a diagnostic test for lymphoma or sarcoidosis in isolation. sIL‑2R is shed from activated T‑lymphocytes and rises with any process that drives T‑cell activation — malignant lymphoproliferation, granulomatous disease, and a wide range of infectious and autoimmune inflammatory states. The clinical implication is that the magnitude of elevation, the pretest probability based on history and exam, and concordant objective findings determine how much weight you give the result.

Practically, use three principles when you encounter a mild sIL‑2R rise in a patient with systemic inflammation (as in this Behçet’s case). First, integrate the number into the clinical context: if there are no B symptoms beyond the inflammatory syndrome, no lymphadenopathy or splenomegaly on exam or imaging, normal basic labs aside from inflammatory markers, and no focal lesions on MRI/CT, a modest sIL‑2R elevation more likely reflects non‑malignant immune activation. Second, consider the degree of elevation: modest increases (eg, up to ~1.5–2× upper limit as in this case) are commonly seen with active autoimmune disease and infection; substantially higher values, or progressive rises over time, increase suspicion for lymphoma or active sarcoidosis. Third, seek corroborating data before pursuing invasive testing: targeted imaging (PET/CT if concern for occult lymphoma or sarcoid), organ‑specific tests (ACE/lysozyme for sarcoid are insensitive and should not be used alone), and tissue biopsy remain the gold standards.

Apply this to the case: a sIL‑2R of 704 U/mL (mildly above the reference range) in a patient with marked systemic inflammation (very high CRP), aseptic meningitis, and evolving mucocutaneous findings is most consistent with immune activation from Behçet’s disease rather than occult lymphoma or neurosarcoidosis. The absence of supportive findings — normal imaging, no lymphadenopathy, negative CSF cytology, normal ACE/lysozyme — lowers the posttest probability of malignancy or sarcoidosis and argues against pursuing invasive diagnostic steps solely because of the sIL‑2R. Serial clinical assessment and trend of sIL‑2R and imaging should guide further workup; pursue tissue diagnosis only if new signs (progressive lymphadenopathy, constitutional symptoms, focal masses, or rising sIL‑2R despite control of inflammation) emerge.
