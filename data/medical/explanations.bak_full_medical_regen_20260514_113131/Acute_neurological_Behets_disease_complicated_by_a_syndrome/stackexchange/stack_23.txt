Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone

### [Management] If high-dose corticosteroids are necessary for acute neuro-Behçet’s in a patient with poorly controlled diabetes, what strategies can mitigate steroid-induced hyperglycemia during induction and taper?

When high‑dose corticosteroids are required for acute neuro‑Behçet’s in a patient with poor baseline glycaemic control, the management goal is to blunt severe hyperglycaemia while avoiding hypoglycaemia during the steroid taper. Think ahead: steroids will raise insulin resistance quickly (within hours), and doses often need to be escalated with pulse IV or high‑dose oral therapy and then de‑escalated — so plan for a proactive, dynamic insulin strategy and close monitoring.

Start with these practical, evidence‑based steps and the reasoning behind them.

1) Anticipate and monitor aggressively. Begin frequent capillary glucose monitoring at baseline and once steroids start: pre‑meal and bedtime (or q4–6‑hour checks if inpatient on IV methylprednisolone). Early detection lets you titrate insulin before very high glucose levels or ketosis develop.

2) Use insulin as the primary tool. Oral agents are unreliable for the rapid, large glycaemic swings caused by high‑dose steroids. In hospital use a structured insulin regimen:
   - For IV pulse methylprednisolone or very high doses: use an intravenous insulin infusion with hourly or q1–2‑hour glucose checks until hyperglycaemia is controlled and the steroid pulse is finished.
   - For high‑dose oral steroids or when off the infusion: use basal‑bolus subcutaneous insulin. Give a long‑acting basal insulin to cover background insulin resistance and rapid‑acting insulin before meals with correction doses. Empiric starting doses are higher than usual (for insulin‑naïve patients often 0.4–0.6 U/kg/day, titrated to glucose). Increase total daily insulin based on glucose trends, paying attention that morning steroid dosing commonly causes large post‑prandial and afternoon hyperglycaemia.

3) Match insulin type to the steroid pharmacodynamics. Daytime hyperglycaemia from morning prednisone is often post‑prandial and late‑afternoon; consider using intermediate‑acting insulin (eg, NPH) or increasing daytime rapid‑acting prandial doses. For prednisone given in the morning, adding a mid‑day NPH can blunt the afternoon glucose peak. For once‑daily long‑acting glucocorticoids or multiple doses, lean toward basal‑bolus with corrections.

4) Plan the taper and de‑escalation up front. As steroids are reduced, insulin requirements will fall — often abruptly. Reduce insulin doses progressively (eg, 10–20% reductions with each steroid dose decrease) guided by glucose readings to avoid hypoglycaemia. Educate the team and patient that insulin will likely be needed transiently and will be tapered as steroids are tapered.

5) Avoid or cautiously use non‑insulin agents while acutely ill. Metformin can be resumed when renal function is stable and the acute illness resolves; it is not useful for rapid control of steroid‑induced hyperglycaemia. Sulfonylureas may be insufficient and can cause hypoglycaemia when steroid dose falls. SGLT2 inhibitors have dehydration and infection risks and are not appropriate for the inpatient acute steroid phase. DPP‑4 inhibitors have modest effect and may be adjunctive outpatient but are not substitutes for insulin during high‑dose steroid therapy.

6) Address comorbid issues and safety. Monitor for dehydration, electrolyte changes (especially with SIADH history), infection risk, and ketoacidosis if very high glucose or if insulin is withheld. Involve endocrinology for complex cases or if insulin requirements are unpredictable. Provide diabetes education and arrange close outpatient follow‑up after discharge for rapid insulin tapering and transition to chronic therapy.

7) Use steroid‑sparing strategies when appropriate. If the neurologic disease can be controlled with alternatives or early steroid‑sparing agents (eg, colchicine in selected cases, azathioprine, cyclosporine, or biologics depending on severity and specialist guidance), that reduces cumulative steroid exposure and the metabolic burden. This decision balances acute neurologic risk and long‑term control and should involve rheumatology/neurology.

In summary: expect markedly increased insulin requirements with high‑dose corticosteroids, treat promptly and primarily with insulin (IV infusion for pulses, basal‑bolus for oral regimens), monitor frequently, and plan a proactive taper of insulin as steroids are tapered. Coordinate with endocrinology and consider steroid‑sparing options once the acute neurologic threat is addressed. These steps minimize hyperglycaemic complications while allowing necessary immunosuppression.
