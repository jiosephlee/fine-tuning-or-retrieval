Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting

### [Differential Diagnosis] How do you distinguish hereditary alpha-tryptasemia from systemic mastocytosis in a patient with elevated baseline tryptase, and what specific testing confirms hereditary alpha-tryptasemia?

Elevated baseline tryptase narrows the differential but does not tell you the mechanism. The two common explanations you’re asking about — hereditary alpha‑tryptasemia (HαT) and systemic mastocytosis (SM) — have very different pathophysiology and therefore different downstream testing.

Think first in terms of mechanism and associated findings. HαT is a germline copy‑number gain of the alpha‑tryptase–encoding TPSAB1 allele that produces chronically higher total serum tryptase without clonal mast cell expansion. Patients often have lifelong mildly-to-moderately elevated baseline tryptase, a family history of elevated tryptase or allergic/functional symptoms (urticaria, flushing, dysautonomia, GI symptoms, connective‑tissue complaints), but they lack the organ infiltration, cytopenias, bone lesions or skin lesions typical of SM. SM is a clonal mast cell disorder (usually KIT D816V) with multifocal dense mast cell aggregates in bone marrow or extracutaneous tissue and systemic features from mast cell infiltration and mediator release; baseline tryptase is frequently >20 ng/mL and other WHO criteria or end‑organ signs are often present.

Clinically, distinguish them by the overall syndrome and by directed testing rather than by baseline tryptase alone. If the history shows lifelong elevated tryptase or a family pattern and no evidence of mast cell organ infiltration, HαT becomes likely. If there are skin lesions (urticaria pigmentosa), hepatosplenomegaly, unexplained cytopenias, osteoporosis/lytic lesions, or very high tryptase with clonal features, pursue SM.

Specific testing to confirm HαT: germline TPSAB1 copy‑number analysis demonstrating extra alpha‑tryptase copies. This is done by specialized molecular assays — digital droplet PCR (ddPCR) or other quantitative copy‑number assays (some labs use MLPA or targeted NGS with copy‑number calling). A positive result is increased TPSAB1 alpha‑tryptase copy number consistent with HαT. Important point: this is germline testing (usually from blood); family testing can corroborate inheritance.

Workup algorithm in practice: measure a true baseline tryptase (≥24 hours after symptoms have resolved). If elevated, obtain KIT D816V testing (sensitive PCR/ddPCR) on peripheral blood and, if suspicion persists or KIT is positive, refer for bone marrow biopsy with histology, mast cell immunophenotyping (CD25/CD2), and KIT testing to apply WHO criteria for SM. If bone marrow and KIT do not support SM and family history or phenotype suggests HαT, order TPSAB1 copy‑number testing (ddPCR) to confirm hereditary alpha‑tryptasemia.

In short: elevated baseline tryptase is a clue only. Confirm HαT by TPSAB1 copy‑number testing (ddPCR/quantitative assay). Exclude or diagnose SM by combining KIT D816V testing and bone marrow evaluation against WHO criteria — because SM (clonal expansion) and HαT (germline copy gain) can coexist, both sets of tests are often necessary.
