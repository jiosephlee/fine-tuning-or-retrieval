Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting

### [Management] In patients with suspected or confirmed systemic mastocytosis and recurrent anaphylaxis, what long-term strategies reduce event frequency and severity (for example, H1 antihistamines, leukotriene antagonists, omalizumab, trigger avoidance), and when are KIT inhibitors (such as avapritinib or midostaurin) considered?

When you see recurrent anaphylaxis in a patient with suspected or proven systemic mastocytosis the first principle is to treat the disorder of excessive mast cell mediator release with rigorous, tiered symptomatic prevention and to reserve disease‑directed cytoreduction for patients with high mast‑cell burden or organ‑threatening disease.

Begin with baseline preventive measures that are low‑risk and evidence‑supported: lifelong access to and training with epinephrine auto‑injectors and an individualized action plan; daily non‑sedating H1‑antihistamine (often at standard or sometimes up‑titrated doses) to blunt histamine‑mediated symptoms; add an H2 blocker for upper GI/vascular mediator effects if symptoms suggest benefit; and consider a leukotriene receptor antagonist for persistent respiratory or cutaneous symptoms. These measures reduce frequency and severity of mediator‑driven flares in many patients and are the first-line long‑term strategy.

Omalizumab (anti‑IgE) has become an important next step for patients with recurrent severe anaphylaxis despite optimal antihistamine/H2/ leukotriene therapy. Although evidence is largely from case series and cohort data rather than randomized trials, omalizumab reproducibly reduces anaphylactic episodes and mediator symptoms in many patients with mast cell disorders and is used off‑label for this indication. Trial treatment for several months is reasonable in referral centers when allergy/immunology and hematology agree.

Trigger avoidance is essential and must be individualized. Avoidance includes obvious precipitants (known drugs, foods, stings), peri‑procedural precautions (premedication and controlled environment), and careful review of medicines that may potentiate mediator release. For patients with a clear Hymenoptera venom allergy, venom immunotherapy is generally recommended even in mastocytosis patients because the baseline risk of fatal sting reactions is high; such therapy should be delivered in specialty centers.

Long‑term systemic corticosteroids are not a routine strategy for prevention and are avoided unless there is a separate indication; they do not reliably prevent biphasic relapses and carry substantial harm.

When to consider KIT inhibitors (midostaurin, avapritinib): these are disease‑directed therapies best managed by a hematologist with expertise in mast cell neoplasms and are indicated primarily for advanced systemic mastocytosis with high mast‑cell burden or organ dysfunction (so‑called C‑findings) or for severe, refractory mediator symptoms when optimal symptomatic therapy (maximized antihistamines/H2 blockers/leukotriene antagonists, omalizumab) has failed. Before embarking on kinase inhibitor therapy you should document systemic disease (bone marrow evaluation, KIT D816V testing, baseline tryptase and organ assessment) and weigh benefits against toxicity. Midostaurin and more recently avapritinib have demonstrated activity against KIT D816V‑positive disease and can reduce mast‑cell burden and mediator release; they are not first‑line for routine prevention of anaphylaxis in patients with indolent disease without organ damage.

In short: maximize proven, low‑risk symptomatic prevention (H1 ± H2 antihistamines, leukotriene antagonist, epinephrine availability, trigger avoidance, consider venom immunotherapy when appropriate), escalate to omalizumab for refractory recurrent anaphylaxis, and reserve KIT inhibitor therapy for patients with documented systemic disease with organ‑threatening involvement or refractory mediator symptoms after optimal symptomatic therapies and specialist review. Always manage these patients in concert with an allergy/immunology and hematology mast‑cell disease center.
