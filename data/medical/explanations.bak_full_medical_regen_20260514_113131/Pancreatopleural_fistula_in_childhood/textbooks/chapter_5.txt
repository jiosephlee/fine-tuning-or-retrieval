Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood

Chapter 5: ## Management: Stepwise Medical, Endoscopic, and Surgical Care

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is managed in a staged fashion: stabilize the patient, control the pleural collection, attempt non‑operative closure of the fistula while resting the pancreas, then proceed to endoscopic or surgical repair when conservative care fails or is not feasible. The childhood case above illustrates these principles: initial respiratory compromise from a large, blood‑stained effusion required urgent drainage and respiratory support, while definitive diagnosis (MRCP, pleural fluid amylase/lipase) directed pancreatic‑directed therapy. Below is a practical, evidence‑informed approach tailored to pediatric patients.

Initial stabilization and pleural management
The first priority is cardiopulmonary stabilization. Provide oxygen and analgesia, and treat shock or hypoxemia if present. If infection is suspected (fever, purulent fluid, systemic inflammatory signs), start empiric antibiotics and tailor to cultures; routine antibiotics are not indicated for sterile pancreatic fluid collections. In children in whom alternate causes such as tuberculosis had been suspected empirically (as in the case), empiric anti‑tuberculous therapy should be discontinued once PPF is established to avoid unnecessary therapy.

Therapeutic thoracentesis is usually the first pleural procedure and provides rapid symptom relief and diagnostic fluid for chemistry (total protein, LDH), cell count, cytology, culture and pleural amylase/lipase. A very high pleural amylase (commonly >1,000 U/L) strongly supports a pancreatic source. Place an intercostal drain (ICD/chest tube) in children who have large, recurrent, loculated effusions or respiratory compromise not relieved by single taps. With chest tube drainage, monitor daily volume and character of output and obtain periodic radiographs and/or ultrasound to document lung re‑expansion and resolution of fluid. There is no absolute pediatric volume cutoff universally accepted; clinical escalation is guided by persistent high output, failure of radiographic improvement, or ongoing respiratory compromise—practically, persistent significant drainage beyond 7–14 days or lack of reduction in output should prompt re‑evaluation and consideration of endoscopic therapy. Remove the tube once output is minimal, there is radiographic resolution of the collection, and the clinical condition is improving.

Conservative pancreatic “rest” measures
Once PPF is suspected or confirmed and the child is hemodynamically stable, conservative (non‑operative) therapy is the usual initial strategy unless there are clear indications for immediate intervention. The goals are to suppress pancreatic exocrine secretion, promote fistula closure, and maintain nutrition.

- NPO and pancreatic rest: Keep the child nil per os (NPO) initially to limit pancreatic stimulation. For prolonged periods, provide parenteral nutrition. Total parenteral nutrition (TPN) or jejunal feeding can be used; jejunal (post‑pyloric) enteral feeds may be preferential when available because they maintain gut integrity and carry less risk of catheter‑related infection than prolonged TPN. TPN has risks (catheter infection, metabolic derangements, cholestasis) and should be monitored closely.

- Somatostatin analogues: Octreotide is commonly used to reduce pancreatic secretions and encourage fistula closure. Pediatric dosing reported in the literature is 10–40 µg/kg/day, typically given as a continuous infusion. For practical conversion, this corresponds to approximately 0.4–1.7 µg/kg/hr. Some centers use intermittent subcutaneous dosing, but continuous infusion is preferred in critically ill or NPO children. Start at a moderate dose (for example, 20–30 µg/kg/day) and adjust clinically. Monitor glucose (risk of hypo‑ or hyperglycemia), gallbladder stasis (with prolonged use predisposition to sludge/stones), bradycardia, and gastrointestinal side effects. There are no absolute pediatric contraindications, but therapy should be supervised by pediatric gastroenterology.

- Pancreatic enzyme replacement (PERT): PERT is generally introduced when the child is refeedings orally or enterally to improve fat digestion, limit steatorrhea, and may reduce endogenous pancreatic stimulation. The exact dosing of pancrelipase should be individualized (consult pediatric gastroenterology); dosing is generally weight‑based and titrated to symptoms and growth. Monitor for fat‑soluble vitamin deficiency if oral intake is limited or malabsorption develops; supplement vitamins A, D, E and K as needed.

Duration and expectations of conservative therapy
A trial of conservative care is reasonable for most children with PPF if there is no evidence of a large disconnected ductal segment, uncontrolled sepsis, or other urgent indications for intervention. Published pediatric reports suggest conservative success is modest—around 10–15% in small series—but selected patients will close with bowel rest, drainage, and somatostatin analogues. Timeframe: allow 2–3 weeks of conservative therapy while closely watching clinical course and drain output. In the illustrated case, octreotide, TPN and ICD drainage resulted in cessation of output within 10 days and successful discharge.

When to progress to endoscopic therapy (ERCP)
If a patient has ongoing significant pleural drainage, persistent high amylase‑rich output, continued radiographic collections, or symptomatic recurrence despite 7–14 days of well‑executed conservative care, pursue endoscopic therapy. Indications for ERCP in children include:

- Demonstrable ductal disruption or leak that is anatomically amenable to transpapillary stenting.
- Persistent high‑output fistula despite an adequate conservative trial.
- Ductal stricture causing upstream pressure leading to leakage.
- Failure or contraindication to percutaneous/endoscopic drainage of collections.

ERCP objectives are diagnostic and therapeutic: define duct anatomy, identify the level of disruption, perform pancreatic sphincterotomy if indicated, and place a transpapillary stent across the site of disruption to lower ductal pressure and allow internal drainage of secretions into the duodenum. In pediatric practice ERCP requires experienced pediatric therapeutic endoscopists because duct size and instrument selection differ from adults. Stents are sized to duct caliber and are typically left in place for a few weeks to months; many fistulae close within 2–3 weeks of stenting. Reported pediatric series show high technical success (many small series report very favorable outcomes) and mixed‑age cohorts demonstrate long‑term success >90%; patient selection and timing are important. Be aware of ERCP risks—post‑ERCP pancreatitis, bleeding, infection, and perforation—and balance these against the morbidity of prolonged leakage.

Surgical therapy: indications and options
Surgery is reserved for children in whom endoscopic therapy is not feasible (for example, very distal or inaccessible leaks, disconnected pancreatic duct syndrome), when there is persistent fistula after adequate endoscopic attempts, or when there are large symptomatic pseudocysts or pancreatic necrosis requiring debridement. Surgical options are chosen based on ductal anatomy and location of disease:

- Distal pancreatectomy (with or without splenic preservation) is indicated for irreparable distal ductal disruption.
- Lateral pancreaticojejunostomy (Puestow procedure) is appropriate for a dilated main pancreatic duct with multiple side‑branch disruption or chronic ductal hypertension.
- Cystojejunostomy (internal drainage of pseudocyst) for large pseudocysts that communicate poorly with the duct or cause mass effect.

Pediatric surgical series describe high success rates (around 90–95% in reported cohorts), low perioperative mortality (<5%), and recurrence rates in the order of 10–15%. As always, surgical planning should be multidisciplinary and individualized.

Nutrition, rehabilitation and long‑term follow‑up
After the acute episode resolves, gradually reintroduce oral intake starting with a low‑fat diet and advance as tolerated. Re‑start or adjust PERT with meals to reduce malabsorption. Monitor growth, weight, and development in children and assess for exocrine insufficiency (steatorrhea, poor weight gain) and endocrine dysfunction (new‑onset diabetes mellitus). If splenectomy was performed, ensure appropriate immunizations.

Children with PPF should be followed in a multidisciplinary clinic that includes pediatric gastroenterology, nutrition, radiology, and surgery. Surveillance imaging should favor MRCP over repeated CT to limit radiation exposure while defining ductal anatomy and recurrent collections. Consider genetic testing for hereditary pancreatitis (PRSS1, SPINK1, CFTR, CTRC) in children with recurrent pancreatitis or family history; counseling of the family and screening of at‑risk relatives are appropriate.

Practical pediatric care pearls and escalation thresholds
- Early consider PPF in any child with large, recurrent, amylase‑rich pleural effusion—ask explicitly about abdominal trauma and prior pancreatitis.  
- Thoracentesis is diagnostic and therapeutic; place an ICD for recurrent or large effusions or if lung re‑expansion is incomplete.  
- Trial conservative therapy for 7–14 days with NPO/TPN or jejunal feeds and octreotide (typical 10–40 µg/kg/day as continuous infusion), along with ICD drainage. Document a clear plan and objective stopping/step‑up criteria (for example, no fall in drain output or persistent collections on imaging by day 10–14).  
- Escalate to ERCP when the ductal disruption is endoscopically accessible or when conservative management fails; involve experienced pediatric endoscopists.  
- Reserve surgery for failed endoscopic management, disconnected duct syndrome, or other anatomic indications.  
- Always coordinate care across PICU, pediatric GI, thoracic/interventional radiology, nutrition, and surgery; minimize radiation exposure and screen for genetic causes in recurrent disease.

Applying these steps to the case: urgent thoracentesis and subsequent ICD addressed the respiratory compromise; MRCP defined the fistula and ductal anatomy; a focused conservative regimen (NPO, TPN, octreotide at ~30 µg/kg/day and PERT on refeeding) led to progressive reduction of drain output and clinical recovery within 10 days, avoiding endoscopic or surgical intervention. This sequence—stabilize, drain, rest the pancreas, then escalate if needed—is the pragmatic algorithm for pediatric PPF.
