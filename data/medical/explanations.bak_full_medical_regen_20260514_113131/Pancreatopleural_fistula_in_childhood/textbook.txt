## Clinical Approach to a Child with Recurrent Pleural Effusion: Recognizing the Pulmonary Mask of Pancreatic Disease

Recurrent or large pleural effusions in a child are a diagnostic and management challenge. The case above — an otherwise well-appearing boy with recurrent, massive, dark red right pleural effusions and only a later-revealed history of blunt abdominal trauma — illustrates two important lessons. First, stabilization and timely drainage of a large effusion take precedence. Second, the origin of recurrent pleural fluid may lie outside the thorax: pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is an uncommon but important diagnostic consideration because it is treatable but easily missed if the clinician anchors on primary pulmonary disease.

Initial stabilization and bedside assessment
The first priorities are airway, breathing and circulation. Assess work of breathing, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation and hemodynamics. Provide supplemental oxygen, analgesia and monitor for progressive respiratory compromise. Bedside lung ultrasound is valuable to confirm the presence, size and character (free versus loculated) of an effusion and guide safe thoracentesis. If the child has respiratory distress, tension physiology, hypoxemia not responsive to oxygen, or a very large effusion occupying the hemithorax, urgent drainage (thoracentesis or chest tube) is indicated both for symptom relief and to obtain diagnostic fluid.

History that should raise suspicion for a pancreatic source
Ask about antecedent abdominal pain or trauma, even if the child minimizes or hides it. PPF in children is most often associated with blunt abdominal injury, recurrent or chronic pancreatitis, or genetic predisposition (PRSS1, SPINK1, CFTR, chymotrypsin C). Other risk clues include a history of prior pancreatitis, family history of pancreatitis, medications that can cause pancreatitis, gallstone disease, cystic fibrosis, and obesity/high-calorie diets that may predispose to pancreatitis. Importantly, children with PPF frequently present with prominent respiratory complaints and few abdominal symptoms — the “pulmonary mask” — so an abdominal etiology should be entertained even when abdominal exam is benign.

Pleural exam and general exam findings
Physical findings are those of a large pleural effusion: reduced chest expansion, stony dullness to percussion, decreased tactile fremitus and diminished breath sounds over the affected hemithorax. The abdominal exam may be normal. Look for signs of chronic pancreatitis or systemic disease (growth failure or malnutrition, steatorrhea suggestive of exocrine insufficiency, or signs of cystic fibrosis). Note whether effusions are unilateral or bilateral and whether they recur after drainage — recurrent large-volume effusions are more likely to have an ongoing source such as PPF.

What to do with the first diagnostic tap
Any unexplained pleural effusion merits diagnostic thoracentesis unless there is a strong contraindication. In addition to routine studies (cell count and differential, protein and LDH to apply Light’s criteria, Gram stain and aerobic bacterial culture), send tests that will broaden the differential and increase the chance of catching an abdominal source on the first tap:
- pH and glucose (low pH/glucose suggest empyema or oesophageal rupture),
- cytology if malignancy is possible,
- triglycerides if chylothorax is considered,
- adenosine deaminase (ADA) and TB tests if TB is in the differential (interpret in clinical context),
- and crucially, pleural fluid amylase and lipase. Pleural amylase ≥ 1,000 IU/L (and markedly elevated lipase) strongly favors a pancreatic origin and should prompt further pancreatic imaging and directed management. If available, isoenzyme typing (pancreatic versus salivary amylase) helps discriminate PPF from other amylase-rich causes such as oesophageal rupture or salivary gland tumors.

Interpretation and differential diagnoses of amylase-rich effusions
High pleural fluid amylase is not specific to PPF: oesophageal perforation, certain malignancies (especially adenocarcinomas and leukemic infiltration), and pancreatic ascites communicating through diaphragmatic defects can also elevate pleural amylase. Clinical context is essential: a history of vomiting or sudden severe chest pain favors Boerhaave’s syndrome; chronic wasting or suggestive imaging favors malignancy. In PPF, fluid is typically exudative by Light’s criteria, often hemorrhagic or dark red, and contains very high pancreatic enzymes. Mildly elevated ADA, as in the case, can mislead toward tuberculosis; pleural amylase measurement helps avoid such anchoring errors.

Imaging to define the source and anatomy
After initial drainage and pleural fluid analysis, cross-sectional imaging should be used to evaluate for pancreatic causes when pleural amylase/lipase are elevated or the clinical picture suggests pancreatitis or trauma. CT of the chest and abdomen will detect pleural collections, hydropneumothorax, pulmonary consolidation and retroperitoneal or peripancreatic fluid collections; it may show a tract but often does not demonstrate the fistula itself. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) has better sensitivity (≈70–80%) to visualize the fistulous tract and ductal anatomy noninvasively and is the preferred next test in stable children. Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) is the diagnostic and therapeutic gold standard for detailed ductal imaging and for transpapillary stent placement, but in children ERCP requires pediatric expertise and sedation/anesthesia.

Management principles and when to escalate
Management proceeds in stages based on severity, ductal anatomy and response:

1. Immediate measures
- Drain large symptomatic effusions promptly (therapeutic thoracentesis or chest tube). Drainage is essential for respiratory support and to quantify ongoing leak.
- Provide supportive care: NPO (bowel rest) and nutritional support. In many centers this begins as NPO with initiation of parenteral nutrition when prolonged fasting is anticipated.

2. Conservative medical therapy
- Somatostatin analogues (e.g., octreotide) reduce pancreatic exocrine secretion and may help fistula closure. Pediatric dosing is variable; the case used approximately 30 µg/kg/day (range 10–40 µg/kg/day) as an infusion. Monitor for side effects.
- Total parenteral nutrition (TPN) or aggressive enteral management to decrease pancreatic stimulation.
- Pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy (PERT) may be used adjunctively once feeding is resumed.
Conservative therapy should be continued for an adequate trial (commonly 2–3 weeks) while monitoring drainage output. Success rates are lower in children than adults; published pediatric series report modest success with conservative measures alone.

3. Endoscopic therapy (ERCP with transpapillary stenting)
If there is radiographic evidence of a persistent ductal leak, ductal disruption amenable to endoscopic therapy, or failure of conservative therapy, ERCP with stent placement across the site of ductal disruption reduces intraductal pressure and diverts pancreatic secretions into the duodenum, promoting fistula closure. In pediatric series ERCP has high technical success and favorable outcomes when performed by experienced endoscopists.

4. Surgical management
Indications for surgery include failure of conservative and endoscopic therapy, complex ductal obstruction not amenable to stenting, or recurrent disease. Surgical options depend on ductal anatomy and the location of the leak and include distal pancreatectomy with or without splenectomy, pancreatojejunostomy (Puestow), or cyst-enteric drainage procedures. Outcomes after appropriately selected surgery are generally good, but surgery carries operative risk and long-term sequelae.

Practical pitfalls and red flags
- Do not anchor on TB or primary lung disease when pleural fluid is hemorrhagic and recurs after drainage; always measure pleural amylase on the first diagnostic tap in unexplained large effusions.
- Mildly elevated ADA does not rule in TB if other data conflict; rely on microbiology and imaging.
- Hemorrhagic pleural fluid has a broad differential (trauma, malignancy, pulmonary embolism, PPF). Correlate with history (abdominal trauma), imaging and pleural enzyme studies.
- Remember that abdominal signs may be minimal or absent. Repeated, focused history-taking (including private questioning of adolescents about trauma) can reveal crucial clues.

Putting it together — an algorithmic bedside approach
1. Stabilize: assess and treat airway/oxygenation, analgesia; perform ultrasound to quantify effusion.
2. If large/symptomatic: perform therapeutic and diagnostic thoracentesis or place chest tube if needed for ongoing drainage or loculations.
3. Send broad pleural fluid tests at first tap: cell count, protein, LDH, pH, glucose, Gram stain and culture, cytology, triglycerides, ADA (if indicated), and pleural amylase/lipase (and isoenzyme if available).
4. If pleural amylase/lipase are markedly elevated (especially amylase ≥1,000 IU/L): obtain pancreatic imaging (MRCP preferred) and consult pediatric gastroenterology/interventional endoscopy.
5. Initiate conservative therapy (NPO/TPN, octreotide, PERT, continue chest drainage) while planning ERCP if needed. If conservative therapy fails after 2–3 weeks or if ductal anatomy indicates, proceed to ERCP with possible stenting; reserve surgery for refractory cases.

Conclusion
A high index of suspicion for pancreatic causes is essential when a child presents with recurrent, massive or hemorrhagic pleural effusions, particularly with a history of abdominal trauma or pancreatitis risk factors. Early pleural fluid amylase measurement and appropriate cross-sectional pancreatic imaging can shorten the diagnostic delay, avoid misdirected treatments, and guide timely conservative, endoscopic or surgical therapy. The “pulmonary mask” of PPF reminds clinicians to keep a broad differential and to look beyond the chest when the effusion recurs or behaves unusually.

## Pathophysiology and Epidemiology of Pancreatopleural Fistula

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is not a primary thoracic disease but an uncommon complication of disruption of the pancreatic ductal system or leakage from a pancreatic pseudocyst. The essential pathophysiologic event is escape of enzyme-rich pancreatic secretions from the pancreatic duct into adjacent spaces. When the duct disruption is anterior, pancreatic juice tends to spill into the peritoneal cavity producing pancreatic ascites. When the disruption is posterior — either from ductal rupture, erosion of a pseudocyst into the retroperitoneum, or from traumatic transection — the leaked fluid follows paths of least resistance within the retroperitoneum and may ascend through natural diaphragmatic openings (most commonly the aortic or esophageal hiatus) into the posterior mediastinum. From there the collection can dissect through mediastinal tissues and eventually breach the pleura, establishing a continuous tract between the pancreatic ductal system and the pleural space. That tract is the pancreatopleural fistula.

An important mechanical driver of this process is increased ductal pressure. Obstruction of outflow — from chronic ductal strictures, stones, inflammatory edema, or an expanding pseudocyst — raises intraductal pressure and promotes leakage at a weak point. In trauma, a blunt force may directly tear the pancreatic parenchyma or duct, creating an immediate communication. Once pancreatic juice communicates with the chest, large volumes of fluid can accumulate because pancreatic secretion rates are high and the fistulous communication may act as a persistent conduit. The proteolytic and lipolytic enzymes in that fluid (amylase, lipase and proteases) not only produce a high-volume exudate but also injure local tissues: enzymes digest pleural surfaces and small vessels, provoking inflammation, hemorrhage and the characteristic often blood-stained (hemorrhagic) pleural effusion. Lipase-mediated fat necrosis and protease-mediated tissue destruction account for the frank blood and the chemically irritating nature of the effusion.

Laterality of the pleural effusion reflects the anatomic course of the fistula. Because the pancreas lies predominantly in the left upper abdomen, left-sided pleural effusions are reported most commonly; however, the fistulous tract can cross the midline in the retroperitoneum and ascend to either hemithorax. A fistula that tracks up and over the esophageal hiatus to the right paravertebral/mediastinal region, as in the case presented, produces a right-sided or even bilateral effusion. Thus, although a left predominance is typical, right-sided and bilateral PPFs are well described and should not dissuade clinicians from considering a pancreatic source when other features suggest it.

Epidemiologically, PPF is rare. It complicates a small fraction of all pancreatitis cases — commonly quoted around 0.4% — and the condition is far more frequently reported in adults than in children. In adults the dominant predisposing condition is chronic alcoholic pancreatitis; long-standing ductal disease, strictures and pseudocysts create the anatomic and physiologic conditions for a fistula to develop. In contrast, pediatric PPF is very uncommon: only a few dozen cases have been documented in the literature to date. Children who develop PPF fall into a different etiologic mix than adults. In pediatric patients chronic pancreatitis is often associated with heritable or genetic conditions — mutations in PRSS1, SPINK1, CFTR and CTRC are well recognized contributors to early or recurrent pancreatitis, and genetic predisposition is identified in a substantial minority of cases (for example, SPINK1 and PRSS1 variants account for a sizeable proportion of pediatric chronic pancreatitis cohorts). Blunt abdominal trauma is another important pediatric cause and may account for a large fraction of reported childhood cases in some series. Idiopathic cases also occur and may reflect unrecognized genetic susceptibility or subtle ductal anomalies.

Clinically this epidemiology matters because the presenting picture is frequently misleading. Many patients present to chest-focused services with massive, recurrent unilateral pleural effusions and respiratory symptoms; abdominal pain or other typical pancreatic complaints may be absent or minimal. This “pulmonary mask” delays consideration of a pancreatic origin and leads to misdirected investigations. Laboratory and pleural-fluid testing offers a crucial clue: pleural fluid in PPF is exudative and characteristically contains very high amylase and lipase concentrations — amylase levels often exceed 1,000 IU/L and can be orders of magnitude higher. Importantly, the isoenzyme pattern may distinguish pancreatic-type amylase (true pancreatic secretion) from salivary-type elevations seen in other conditions (for example, esophageal rupture or some malignancies). Elevation of pleural fluid amylase should prompt evaluation of the pancreas (serum amylase/lipase, cross-sectional imaging and MRCP) and consideration of ERCP for ductal assessment and potential therapy.

In summary, PPF arises when increased ductal pressure or direct ductal injury allows pancreatic secretions to escape posteriorly into the retroperitoneum and then ascend into the chest through diaphragmatic hiatuses, with subsequent pleural erosion and accumulation of enzyme-rich, often hemorrhagic fluid. It is a rare complication of pancreatitis overall and is particularly uncommon in children, where genetic disease and trauma are relatively prominent causes. Because the dominant clinical manifestations may be thoracic, a high index of suspicion and early measurement of pleural amylase are essential to avoid diagnostic delay. The case presented — a child with blunt pancreatic injury producing a posterior ductal disruption that tracked to the right pleural space and yielded a dark, amylase-rich effusion — exemplifies these pathophysiologic and epidemiologic principles.

## Differential Diagnosis of Amylase-Rich or Hemorrhagic Pleural Effusions

When confronted with a child who has recurrent, large-volume, dark red (hemorrhagic) pleural effusions, the clinician must maintain a broad differential and use pleural fluid analysis plus targeted imaging and history to triage between thoracic and extrathoracic sources. The presented case illustrates how a pancreatic source can masquerade as a primary pulmonary disease and how routine assumptions (for example, empiric therapy for tuberculosis) can delay the correct diagnosis. Below I review the major competing diagnoses for amylase-rich or hemorrhagic pleural effusions, describe the characteristic pleural fluid and clinical features that distinguish them, and give practical diagnostic “decision cues” to prioritize further testing.

Tuberculous pleuritis
- Typical presentation: subacute to chronic symptoms—fever, night sweats, weight loss, pleuritic chest pain; often unilateral, small-to-moderate effusion. In children contact history can be present and clinicians are right to consider TB.
- Pleural fluid: lymphocyte-predominant exudate, elevated protein and LDH, and often raised ADA (adenosine deaminase). ADA is sensitive but not specific; moderate elevations can occur with other exudative processes.
- Diagnostic confirmation: microbiology from pleural fluid (AFB smear and culture, GeneXpert MTB/RIF) has limited sensitivity. If suspicion remains, pleural biopsy for histology and culture increases yield.
- Pitfall illustrated by the case: a mildly raised ADA plus exposure history led to empiric anti‑TB treatment despite a hemorrhagic, recurrent effusion. In a recurrent large hemorrhagic effusion, consider alternative causes; do not rely on ADA alone.

Parapneumonic effusion and empyema
- Typical presentation: acute febrile illness with productive cough and radiographic consolidation; patient is often toxic.
- Pleural fluid: neutrophil-predominant exudate, low pH (<7.2) and low glucose (<60 mg/dL) in complicated parapneumonic effusions/empyema, positive Gram stain or culture in many cases.
- Hemorrhagic pleural effusions can occur with necrotising infection, but markedly elevated pleural amylase is uncommon.
- Management: requires antibiotics and drainage. In the absence of systemic infection, look elsewhere.

Malignancy (primary pleural malignancy, lymphoma, leukemia, metastatic disease)
- Typical presentation: systemic “B” symptoms (fever, night sweats, weight loss) may be present; persistent or recurrent unilateral effusions; in children, lymphoma/leukemia are more relevant than primary lung carcinoma.
- Pleural fluid: exudate; cytology may reveal malignant cells (sensitivity varies). Some tumors produce salivary-type amylase, causing elevated pleural amylase.
- Diagnostic steps: pleural fluid cytology, peripheral blood smear, bone marrow studies if hematologic malignancy suspected, and cross-sectional imaging. Persistent bloody effusions raise concern for malignancy—investigate aggressively.

Esophageal perforation (Boerhaave syndrome)
- Typical presentation: acute severe chest pain after forceful vomiting, subcutaneous emphysema, rapid deterioration with mediastinitis.
- Pleural fluid: can be bloody or serosanguineous; typically low pH, low glucose, presence of food particles or bile, and markedly elevated amylase that is salivary-type rather than pancreatic.
- Imaging and diagnosis: contrast esophagram with water-soluble contrast (followed by barium if negative) or CT showing mediastinal air/fluid and pleural collections.
- Distinguishing features: history of violent emesis and rapid systemic toxicity distinguish Boerhaave’s from pancreatic fistulas.

Hepatic hydrothorax
- Typical presentation: right-sided effusion in the setting of portal hypertension and cirrhosis, with ascites.
- Pleural fluid: usually a transudate (low protein, low LDH) unless secondary infection or malignancy is present; amylase is not elevated.
- Clues: liver disease on exam/labs and imaging, and resolution with management of portal hypertension.

Chylothorax
- Typical presentation: milky or opalescent pleural fluid after thoracic surgery/trauma, or in lymphatic obstruction.
- Pleural fluid: high triglycerides (>110 mg/dL), chylomicrons on lipoprotein electrophoresis.
- Distinguishing features: appearance and triglyceride/chylomicron testing; amylase not typically elevated.

Diaphragmatic hernia
- Typical presentation: breathlessness and bowel sounds in the chest, sometimes gastrointestinal symptoms.
- Pleural fluid: depends on associated visceral injury; imaging usually shows abdominal viscera in the thorax.
- Diagnosis: plain radiograph, chest/abdomen CT.

Pancreatitis-related pleural effusion vs pancreatopleural fistula (PPF)
- Pancreatitis-associated pleural effusion: small-to-moderate, usually left-sided, often transient and occurring during or shortly after an episode of pancreatitis.
- Pancreatopleural fistula: tends to produce recurrent, large-volume (often massive), unilateral or bilateral pleural effusions that can be hemorrhagic and may predominantly produce thoracic symptoms with minimal abdominal complaints—particularly in children. A history of pancreatitis, blunt abdominal trauma, or genetic predisposition (e.g., PRSS1, SPINK1 mutations) increases the pretest probability.
- Pleural fluid: hallmark is very high amylase (commonly >1,000 IU/L). Pleural fluid lipase is often elevated and can be more specific for a pancreatic source. Amylase isoenzyme analysis (pancreatic vs salivary type) helps distinguish pancreatic leak from salivary-type amylase produced by some tumors or from esophageal leaks.
- Imaging: CT may show pseudocysts, retroperitoneal collections, or tracking fluid but often misses the fistula itself. MRCP has better sensitivity (approximately 70–80%) for visualizing fistulous tracts and duct anatomy. ERCP is both diagnostic and therapeutic—visualizes the duct and allows transpapillary stenting.
- Clinical cue from the case: recurrent, large right-sided hemorrhagic effusions with pleural amylase of 6,118 U/L and elevated pleural lipase pointed to a pancreatic origin confirmed on MRCP.

Practical diagnostic approach and “red flags”
- Always send a full pleural fluid panel on the first thoracentesis: cell count and differential, protein, LDH, pH, glucose, Gram stain and culture, AFB/GeneXpert when TB suspected, cytology, triglycerides, amylase and lipase, and ADA if TB in differential.
- Consider the following features as decision cues favoring a pancreatic source/PPF:
  - Very high pleural fluid amylase (commonly >1,000 IU/L), especially when pleural fluid lipase is also elevated.
  - Recurrent large-volume or “massive” pleural effusion that rapidly reaccumulates despite drainage.
  - Poor clinical response to conventional pulmonary therapies (antibiotics, anti‑TB drugs) and lack of systemic features of infection.
  - History of pancreatitis, alcohol abuse (in adults), blunt abdominal trauma, or family history of hereditary pancreatitis.
  - Imaging demonstrating retroperitoneal collections tracking through the diaphragmatic hiatus, or a pseudocyst adjacent to the diaphragm.
- Use amylase isoenzyme testing when the source is unclear (pancreatic vs salivary). A high pleural lipase strengthens the pancreatic diagnosis.
- If PPF is suspected, obtain cross-sectional imaging (contrast-enhanced CT chest/abdomen and MRCP) early; defer empiric, prolonged anti-TB therapy until pancreatic and malignant causes are excluded when the clinical picture is atypical for TB.

Summary
Hemorrhagic or amylase-rich pleural effusions have a focused differential—infectious (parapneumonic/empyema, TB), traumatic, malignant, esophageal perforation, chylothorax, hepatic hydrothorax, and pancreatic sources (including PPF). Distinguishing features include the pleural fluid cell type, pH and glucose, ADA, triglycerides, and especially pleural amylase and lipase with isoenzyme typing. Recurrent, massive pleural effusions with very high pleural amylase (and elevated lipase), particularly after abdominal trauma or in the setting of pancreatitis, should trigger early abdominal imaging (MRCP/CT) and consideration of ERCP—this approach could have avoided prolonged empiric anti-TB therapy in the presented child and led to earlier definitive management.

## Diagnostic Workup and Imaging Strategy

When a child presents with a large, recurrent, or otherwise unexplained pleural effusion, the diagnostic workup must be broadened beyond common pulmonary causes to include intra‑abdominal sources such as pancreatitis with an internal pancreatic fistula. The clinical course of the case presented — repeated large right‑sided, dark‑red pleural effusions that improved transiently after thoracentesis, a delayed disclosure of prior blunt abdominal trauma, and ultimately markedly elevated pleural amylase with MRCP demonstration of a tract from the pancreatic tail through the esophageal hiatus — illustrates how a pancreatic source can masquerade as primary thoracic disease. A pragmatic, pediatric‑focused algorithm balances rapid assessment and drainage for respiratory compromise with early laboratory and imaging steps that can identify pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) before unnecessary or prolonged pulmonary treatments are pursued.

Initial laboratory testing should be broad but targeted. In any child with a large or recurrent pleural effusion obtain serum amylase and lipase early; elevations support a pancreatic source but may be absent in some cases. Routine baseline studies — CBC with differential, CRP or ESR, liver and renal function tests, and fasting triglycerides — are useful to identify alternative causes (for example, hypertriglyceridemia and chylothorax) and to guide general management. If a child has recurrent or chronic pancreatitis or onset at a young age, arrange genetic evaluation (PRSS1, SPINK1, CFTR, CTRC) and sweat chloride testing to assess for cystic fibrosis; these investigations are particularly important when there is no clear traumatic or alcoholic etiology.

Pleural fluid analysis is pivotal and should be performed at the first diagnostic thoracentesis. Fluid must be characterized by Light’s criteria (protein and LDH) to distinguish transudate from exudate, and cell count with differential, Gram stain and aerobic/anaerobic cultures should be sent. Pancreatic disease should be suspected when pleural fluid is an exudate and either grossly hemorrhagic or serosanguinous, and when pleural fluid amylase is markedly elevated. A pleural fluid amylase concentration in excess of 1,000 IU/L is highly suggestive of a pancreatic source; when available, amylase isoenzyme typing (to distinguish pancreatic from salivary amylase) can help differentiate pancreatic fistula from other causes of amylase‑rich effusions (eg, esophageal rupture or some malignancies). Also measure pleural fluid pH and glucose: low pH and very low glucose point to complicated parapneumonic effusion or empyema but may also be seen in long‑standing pancreatic effusions; triglycerides should be checked if chylothorax is in the differential. Adenosine deaminase (ADA) can be elevated with tuberculous pleuritis but must be interpreted cautiously: mild elevations do not confirm TB and can mislead the clinician, as in this case where empiric antitubercular therapy was initiated before pancreatic testing ruled it out. Cytology should be sent when malignancy is a concern.

Imaging should follow a stepwise approach that reflects urgency, diagnostic yield, and pediatric considerations about radiation and sedation. A frontal chest radiograph and bedside ultrasound are the initial imaging modalities of choice. They rapidly confirm the presence, laterality and approximate volume of effusion and help to identify loculations; ultrasound is the best bedside tool to guide safe diagnostic thoracentesis or chest‑tube placement and avoids radiation exposure. In the presented case, bedside ultrasound quantified an approximately one‑litre effusion and facilitated safe aspiration, which temporarily improved the child’s respiratory status.

Cross‑sectional imaging is the next step when pleural fluid analysis suggests a pancreatic origin (eg, high pleural amylase), when there is a history of abdominal trauma, or when recurrent effusions prompt a search for an extrathoracic cause. Contrast‑enhanced CT of the chest and abdomen is widely available and excellent at detecting pancreatitis complications — pseudocysts, pancreatic calcifications, ductal dilation, and retroperitoneal or pleural collections — and is particularly helpful for operative planning or when MRCP is not immediately available. However, CT detects the actual pancreatic‑to‑pleural fistulous tract in only a minority of cases (reported around one‑third), so its inability to show a tract does not exclude a PPF. In the case described, CT raised suspicion by demonstrating a multiloculated hypodense collection tracking from the paraesophageal region to the pancreatic tail and a hydropneumothorax, which prompted dedicated pancreatobiliary imaging.

Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) is the preferred noninvasive ductal imaging modality in children when technical resources and pediatric anesthesia support permit. MRCP has substantially greater sensitivity than CT for delineating pancreatic duct anatomy and visualizing a fistulous trajectory through diaphragmatic hiatuses (esophageal or aortic), with reported sensitivities of roughly 70–80%. When available, secretin‑enhanced MRCP further increases sensitivity by transiently stimulating pancreatic exocrine secretions and mapping ductal leaks. MRCP in our patient confirmed a tubular cystic collection in the distal body and tail communicating with the distal pancreatic duct and tracking superiorly into the right pleural cavity, providing the anatomical diagnosis that redirected therapy away from pulmonary etiologies.

Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) remains the diagnostic and therapeutic gold standard for ductal disruption: it can directly demonstrate leak sites and allows transpapillary interventions such as stent placement or dilatation that lower intraductal pressure and divert pancreatic secretions to promote fistula closure. In pediatrics ERCP has been used successfully, but it requires careful weighing of benefits and risks (notably post‑ERCP pancreatitis) and should be performed by experienced pediatric interventional endoscopists. Indications for early ERCP include persistent high volume pleural drainage despite conservative measures, demonstrable ductal obstruction or stricturing on MRCP/CT, or when MRCP suggests a stentable site. If endoscopic therapy is not feasible or fails, surgical options (eg, distal pancreatectomy with or without pancreaticojejunostomy, cystogastrostomy/cystojejunostomy) are the definitive alternatives.

A practical diagnostic algorithm based on the preceding considerations is as follows and can be applied in most pediatric settings. For any child with a large, unilateral, hemorrhagic or recurrent pleural effusion: obtain chest radiograph and bedside ultrasound, perform diagnostic thoracentesis and send a comprehensive pleural fluid panel that explicitly includes amylase (and triglycerides, pH, glucose, LDH, protein, cell count, Gram stain/culture, cytology and ADA as indicated). If pleural amylase is markedly elevated (eg, >1,000 IU/L) or serum amylase/lipase are raised, obtain targeted pancreatic imaging rather than repeating blind thoracenteses: MRCP is preferred when available and feasible for children; contrast CT chest/abdomen is useful to detect collections, calcifications and guide percutaneous interventions. If MRCP or CT demonstrates ductal disruption or a pseudocyst communicating with the chest, early multidisciplinary discussion (pediatric gastroenterology/interventional endoscopy, thoracic surgery, interventional radiology, and intensive care) should occur to plan conservative therapy (NPO, bowel rest, total parenteral nutrition, octreotide) versus earlier ERCP with transpapillary stenting. Conservative measures may be attempted for a limited period (generally 2–3 weeks) when the fistula is small and drainage is controlled; failure to improve or persistent high output favors endoscopic or surgical intervention.

Pediatric‑specific considerations must be woven through the diagnostic and imaging strategy. Minimize ionizing radiation in children: preferentially use ultrasound and MRCP when clinically appropriate; when CT is necessary, limit the field of view and use pediatric dose reduction protocols. Plan for sedation or general anesthesia for MRCP and ERCP in young children, including airway management and post‑procedure monitoring. Coordinate early with pediatric specialists — pediatric gastroenterology/hepatology, pulmonology, radiology, surgery, anesthesia and genetics — so imaging, endoscopy and possible operative interventions proceed efficiently. Ensure that laboratory requests specify pancreatic amylase isoenzyme typing if available, and that pleural fluid is sent promptly and processed to avoid delays that can lead to repeated, non‑diagnostic thoracenteses. Finally, interpret tests like ADA in clinical context; mild ADA elevations or an apparent “tuberculosis contact” history (as in our patient) should not delay consideration of a pancreatic source when the pleural fluid is hemorrhagic and amylase‑rich.

In summary, the diagnostic approach to suspected pancreatopleural fistula in children is based on early pleural fluid analysis including amylase, judicious use of bedside ultrasound and non‑ionizing ductal imaging (MRCP when possible), and early multidisciplinary planning for endoscopic or surgical therapy when indicated. Applying this strategy to the presented case would have appropriately prioritized pancreatic testing after the first hemorrhagic effusion and might have shortened the interval to definitive diagnosis and therapy, sparing repeated empirical pulmonary treatments.

## Management: Stepwise Medical, Endoscopic, and Surgical Care

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is managed in a staged fashion: stabilize the patient, control the pleural collection, attempt non‑operative closure of the fistula while resting the pancreas, then proceed to endoscopic or surgical repair when conservative care fails or is not feasible. The childhood case above illustrates these principles: initial respiratory compromise from a large, blood‑stained effusion required urgent drainage and respiratory support, while definitive diagnosis (MRCP, pleural fluid amylase/lipase) directed pancreatic‑directed therapy. Below is a practical, evidence‑informed approach tailored to pediatric patients.

Initial stabilization and pleural management
The first priority is cardiopulmonary stabilization. Provide oxygen and analgesia, and treat shock or hypoxemia if present. If infection is suspected (fever, purulent fluid, systemic inflammatory signs), start empiric antibiotics and tailor to cultures; routine antibiotics are not indicated for sterile pancreatic fluid collections. In children in whom alternate causes such as tuberculosis had been suspected empirically (as in the case), empiric anti‑tuberculous therapy should be discontinued once PPF is established to avoid unnecessary therapy.

Therapeutic thoracentesis is usually the first pleural procedure and provides rapid symptom relief and diagnostic fluid for chemistry (total protein, LDH), cell count, cytology, culture and pleural amylase/lipase. A very high pleural amylase (commonly >1,000 U/L) strongly supports a pancreatic source. Place an intercostal drain (ICD/chest tube) in children who have large, recurrent, loculated effusions or respiratory compromise not relieved by single taps. With chest tube drainage, monitor daily volume and character of output and obtain periodic radiographs and/or ultrasound to document lung re‑expansion and resolution of fluid. There is no absolute pediatric volume cutoff universally accepted; clinical escalation is guided by persistent high output, failure of radiographic improvement, or ongoing respiratory compromise—practically, persistent significant drainage beyond 7–14 days or lack of reduction in output should prompt re‑evaluation and consideration of endoscopic therapy. Remove the tube once output is minimal, there is radiographic resolution of the collection, and the clinical condition is improving.

Conservative pancreatic “rest” measures
Once PPF is suspected or confirmed and the child is hemodynamically stable, conservative (non‑operative) therapy is the usual initial strategy unless there are clear indications for immediate intervention. The goals are to suppress pancreatic exocrine secretion, promote fistula closure, and maintain nutrition.

- NPO and pancreatic rest: Keep the child nil per os (NPO) initially to limit pancreatic stimulation. For prolonged periods, provide parenteral nutrition. Total parenteral nutrition (TPN) or jejunal feeding can be used; jejunal (post‑pyloric) enteral feeds may be preferential when available because they maintain gut integrity and carry less risk of catheter‑related infection than prolonged TPN. TPN has risks (catheter infection, metabolic derangements, cholestasis) and should be monitored closely.

- Somatostatin analogues: Octreotide is commonly used to reduce pancreatic secretions and encourage fistula closure. Pediatric dosing reported in the literature is 10–40 µg/kg/day, typically given as a continuous infusion. For practical conversion, this corresponds to approximately 0.4–1.7 µg/kg/hr. Some centers use intermittent subcutaneous dosing, but continuous infusion is preferred in critically ill or NPO children. Start at a moderate dose (for example, 20–30 µg/kg/day) and adjust clinically. Monitor glucose (risk of hypo‑ or hyperglycemia), gallbladder stasis (with prolonged use predisposition to sludge/stones), bradycardia, and gastrointestinal side effects. There are no absolute pediatric contraindications, but therapy should be supervised by pediatric gastroenterology.

- Pancreatic enzyme replacement (PERT): PERT is generally introduced when the child is refeedings orally or enterally to improve fat digestion, limit steatorrhea, and may reduce endogenous pancreatic stimulation. The exact dosing of pancrelipase should be individualized (consult pediatric gastroenterology); dosing is generally weight‑based and titrated to symptoms and growth. Monitor for fat‑soluble vitamin deficiency if oral intake is limited or malabsorption develops; supplement vitamins A, D, E and K as needed.

Duration and expectations of conservative therapy
A trial of conservative care is reasonable for most children with PPF if there is no evidence of a large disconnected ductal segment, uncontrolled sepsis, or other urgent indications for intervention. Published pediatric reports suggest conservative success is modest—around 10–15% in small series—but selected patients will close with bowel rest, drainage, and somatostatin analogues. Timeframe: allow 2–3 weeks of conservative therapy while closely watching clinical course and drain output. In the illustrated case, octreotide, TPN and ICD drainage resulted in cessation of output within 10 days and successful discharge.

When to progress to endoscopic therapy (ERCP)
If a patient has ongoing significant pleural drainage, persistent high amylase‑rich output, continued radiographic collections, or symptomatic recurrence despite 7–14 days of well‑executed conservative care, pursue endoscopic therapy. Indications for ERCP in children include:

- Demonstrable ductal disruption or leak that is anatomically amenable to transpapillary stenting.
- Persistent high‑output fistula despite an adequate conservative trial.
- Ductal stricture causing upstream pressure leading to leakage.
- Failure or contraindication to percutaneous/endoscopic drainage of collections.

ERCP objectives are diagnostic and therapeutic: define duct anatomy, identify the level of disruption, perform pancreatic sphincterotomy if indicated, and place a transpapillary stent across the site of disruption to lower ductal pressure and allow internal drainage of secretions into the duodenum. In pediatric practice ERCP requires experienced pediatric therapeutic endoscopists because duct size and instrument selection differ from adults. Stents are sized to duct caliber and are typically left in place for a few weeks to months; many fistulae close within 2–3 weeks of stenting. Reported pediatric series show high technical success (many small series report very favorable outcomes) and mixed‑age cohorts demonstrate long‑term success >90%; patient selection and timing are important. Be aware of ERCP risks—post‑ERCP pancreatitis, bleeding, infection, and perforation—and balance these against the morbidity of prolonged leakage.

Surgical therapy: indications and options
Surgery is reserved for children in whom endoscopic therapy is not feasible (for example, very distal or inaccessible leaks, disconnected pancreatic duct syndrome), when there is persistent fistula after adequate endoscopic attempts, or when there are large symptomatic pseudocysts or pancreatic necrosis requiring debridement. Surgical options are chosen based on ductal anatomy and location of disease:

- Distal pancreatectomy (with or without splenic preservation) is indicated for irreparable distal ductal disruption.
- Lateral pancreaticojejunostomy (Puestow procedure) is appropriate for a dilated main pancreatic duct with multiple side‑branch disruption or chronic ductal hypertension.
- Cystojejunostomy (internal drainage of pseudocyst) for large pseudocysts that communicate poorly with the duct or cause mass effect.

Pediatric surgical series describe high success rates (around 90–95% in reported cohorts), low perioperative mortality (<5%), and recurrence rates in the order of 10–15%. As always, surgical planning should be multidisciplinary and individualized.

Nutrition, rehabilitation and long‑term follow‑up
After the acute episode resolves, gradually reintroduce oral intake starting with a low‑fat diet and advance as tolerated. Re‑start or adjust PERT with meals to reduce malabsorption. Monitor growth, weight, and development in children and assess for exocrine insufficiency (steatorrhea, poor weight gain) and endocrine dysfunction (new‑onset diabetes mellitus). If splenectomy was performed, ensure appropriate immunizations.

Children with PPF should be followed in a multidisciplinary clinic that includes pediatric gastroenterology, nutrition, radiology, and surgery. Surveillance imaging should favor MRCP over repeated CT to limit radiation exposure while defining ductal anatomy and recurrent collections. Consider genetic testing for hereditary pancreatitis (PRSS1, SPINK1, CFTR, CTRC) in children with recurrent pancreatitis or family history; counseling of the family and screening of at‑risk relatives are appropriate.

Practical pediatric care pearls and escalation thresholds
- Early consider PPF in any child with large, recurrent, amylase‑rich pleural effusion—ask explicitly about abdominal trauma and prior pancreatitis.  
- Thoracentesis is diagnostic and therapeutic; place an ICD for recurrent or large effusions or if lung re‑expansion is incomplete.  
- Trial conservative therapy for 7–14 days with NPO/TPN or jejunal feeds and octreotide (typical 10–40 µg/kg/day as continuous infusion), along with ICD drainage. Document a clear plan and objective stopping/step‑up criteria (for example, no fall in drain output or persistent collections on imaging by day 10–14).  
- Escalate to ERCP when the ductal disruption is endoscopically accessible or when conservative management fails; involve experienced pediatric endoscopists.  
- Reserve surgery for failed endoscopic management, disconnected duct syndrome, or other anatomic indications.  
- Always coordinate care across PICU, pediatric GI, thoracic/interventional radiology, nutrition, and surgery; minimize radiation exposure and screen for genetic causes in recurrent disease.

Applying these steps to the case: urgent thoracentesis and subsequent ICD addressed the respiratory compromise; MRCP defined the fistula and ductal anatomy; a focused conservative regimen (NPO, TPN, octreotide at ~30 µg/kg/day and PERT on refeeding) led to progressive reduction of drain output and clinical recovery within 10 days, avoiding endoscopic or surgical intervention. This sequence—stabilize, drain, rest the pancreas, then escalate if needed—is the pragmatic algorithm for pediatric PPF.

## Clinical Pearls and Pitfalls

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is uncommon in children but important to recognize because its presentation is often dominated by thoracic signs and because early, targeted therapy prevents recurrent morbidity. The cardinal clinical clue is a massive, recurrent, or hemorrhagic pleural effusion—particularly when there is a history of recent abdominal trauma, pancreatitis, or risk factors for pediatric pancreatitis (family history or known genetic mutations). In children, respiratory symptoms frequently predominate and abdominal complaints may be absent or subtle, so maintain a low threshold to consider a pancreatic source whenever pleural effusions recur rapidly after thoracentesis or recur despite apparent improvement.

Always send pleural fluid amylase (and lipase) early in the workup of an unexplained exudative pleural effusion. Extremely high pleural amylase (classically ≥1,000 IU/L) strongly suggests a pancreatic etiology; measurement of amylase isoenzymes or “pancreatic-type” amylase can distinguish pancreatic from salivary sources (the latter points to oesophageal rupture or salivary contamination). In addition to fluid studies, obtain serum amylase/lipase. Remember that the pleural fluid is typically an exudate and may be blood-stained, as in this case, and that other causes of amylase-rich effusions (oesophageal rupture, malignancy, tuberculosis) must be actively excluded.

Interpret common pleural biomarkers carefully. A mildly elevated ADA can occur in non-tuberculous pleural disease, and a borderline elevation should not automatically lead to empirical antitubercular therapy—especially when clinical features and other tests (smear, GeneXpert, imaging, cytology) point elsewhere. Avoid anchoring on a single abnormal test; persistently hemorrhagic, high-amylase pleural fluid should shift the diagnostic focus to the pancreas.

Choose imaging to answer specific questions. Cross-sectional abdominal imaging is essential when PPF is suspected. MRCP is the preferred noninvasive test for delineating ductal anatomy and for visualizing the fistulous tract (sensitivity ~70–80%) and should be obtained early if available. Contrast-enhanced CT of the chest/abdomen is useful to identify pseudocysts, collections, pulmonary complications, and to plan drainage, but it visualizes the actual fistula less reliably (reported detection in one-third to one-half of cases). ERCP remains the gold standard for ductal visualization and, importantly, permits therapeutic transpapillary stent placement when appropriate; in children, ERCP visualisation rates range and therapeutic success is substantial when ductal anatomy allows.

Management is staged and should be individualized. Initial conservative management—aimed at reducing pancreatic exocrine flow and allowing the fistula to close—includes bowel rest (NPO), total parenteral nutrition, octreotide (somatostatin analogue), pancreatic enzyme replacement as indicated, and adequate pleural drainage. In pediatric series conservative closure rates are modest (successful in a minority—roughly 10–15% in some reports), so set clear clinical endpoints: decreasing chest tube output toward minimal levels, radiographic resolution of collections, improving amylase trend, and clinical stability. If high-output drainage persists, or if imaging/ERCP demonstrates ductal obstruction, disruption not amenable to conservative therapy, or a distal pancreatic duct stricture, escalate to endoscopic therapy (ERCP with transpapillary stenting) rather than persisting indefinitely with repeat thoracenteses.

Avoid the pitfall of repeated blind thoracenteses without addressing the pancreatic source. Recurrent large-volume effusions that compromise respiration warrant chest tube drainage rather than serial taps; this provides continuous drainage, better monitoring of daily output (an important metric to judge response) and avoids repeated procedures and delays in definitive therapy. However, chest tube drainage alone without pancreas-directed therapy usually does not stop the leak.

Recognize the role and limitations of octreotide. Somatostatin analogues reduce pancreatic secretions and can assist fistula closure; pediatric dosing varies (10–40 µg/kg/day in reported series; the case used 30 µg/kg/day). Monitor for adverse effects including hyperglycaemia or hypoglycaemia, gallbladder stasis or sludge, abdominal discomfort, and potential effects on thyroid function or bradycardia in rare instances. Total parenteral nutrition and central venous access introduce risks (catheter-related infection, metabolic derangements), so institute careful monitoring and line care.

Know when to call in endoscopy and surgery. If conservative measures fail after a reasonable trial (commonly 2–3 weeks in clinical practice) or if ERCP shows a ductal leak amenable to stenting, proceed to endoscopic therapy—stent placement lowers intraductal pressure and promotes healing, often within weeks. If endoscopic access is not feasible (distal fistula, severe obstruction, anatomic limitations) or closure fails, surgical options (distal pancreatectomy, pancreaticojejunostomy, or cystogastro/jejunostomy depending on anatomy) are effective; pediatric surgical series have shown high success rates though with operative risks and some recurrence potential.

Don’t overlook history-taking nuances and psychosocial barriers. Children may not volunteer trauma or may minimize symptoms because of embarrassment, bullying, or fear of consequences. Repeated, gentle inquiry about recent abdominal trauma—combined with collateral history from caregivers—often yields information pivotal to diagnosis. Consider genetic testing and family counselling in children with recurrent or chronic pancreatitis, since PRSS1, SPINK1, CFTR and other mutations are recognised contributors.

Finally, emphasize multidisciplinary care and family counselling. Coordinate care among pediatric gastroenterology, interventional endoscopy, thoracic surgery/pulmonology, radiology, nutrition, and critical care. With timely source control (endoscopic or surgical when needed) and appropriate supportive measures, long-term outcomes in children are excellent. Counsel families on recurrence risk, the potential need for further interventions, and modifiable contributors (nutrition, avoidance of abdominal trauma where possible, and surveillance for chronic pancreatic disease). Document clear plans for follow-up imaging and symptom review so that recurrent collections are detected early rather than mistaken for primary pulmonary disease.
