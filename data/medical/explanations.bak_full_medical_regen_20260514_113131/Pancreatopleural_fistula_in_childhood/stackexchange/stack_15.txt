Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood

### [Management] Under what circumstances should endoscopic transpapillary pancreatic duct stenting be pursued for PPF, and what are its goals and expected time to fistula closure?

Endoscopic transpapillary pancreatic duct stenting is indicated when conservative therapy (bowel rest/TPN, somatostatin analogue, thoracic drainage) is unsuccessful or unlikely to succeed, and when the ductal anatomy on MRCP/ERCP is favorable for an endoscopic repair. Practically, pursue ERCP with transpapillary stent when the patient has ongoing high-output pleural drainage or respiratory compromise after about 2–3 weeks of optimized conservative care, when imaging shows a main-duct disruption or pseudocyst communicating with the duct that can be “bridged” by a stent, or when there is a ductal stricture (or stone) producing elevated intraductal pressure that is driving external leakage.

The clinical reasoning is twofold. First, a transpapillary stent lowers intraductal pressure and diverts pancreatic juice preferentially into the duodenum, which removes the pressure gradient that maintains the fistula and permits the disrupted duct or communicating pseudocyst to heal. Second, ERCP defines the ductal anatomy (location and extent of disruption, strictures, side-branch leaks) and allows simultaneous therapy (stent placement, dilation, sphincterotomy, stone extraction) tailored to the lesion. If the duct disruption is distal and cannot be bridged, or if there is severe obstruction that cannot be relieved endoscopically, surgical drainage/resection is preferable.

Expect fistula closure fairly rapidly after successful stent placement: many series report clinical cessation of external drainage within about 2–3 weeks. The stent itself is typically left in place for weeks to months (commonly 4 weeks to 6 months depending on the underlying duct pathology and response) to allow durable healing. In mixed-age cohorts endoscopic therapy achieves high technical and long‑term success (most series >80–90% when anatomy is amenable); pediatric reports likewise show high ERCP success when performed by experienced teams. In the presented child, conservative measures led to closure within 10 days, so endoscopic stenting was not required.
