Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood

### [Differential Diagnosis] Which genetic mutations should be considered in a child with recurrent or chronic pancreatitis who develops pancreatopleural fistula, and how does this influence counseling and long-term management?

Think of this child as having “early chronic pancreatitis” until proven otherwise — in children recurrent pancreatitis is often genetic, and identifying a pathogenic variant changes counseling, follow‑up intensity, and sometimes management.

Which genes to test
Start with a hereditary pancreatitis/chronic pancreatitis panel that includes PRSS1 (cationic trypsinogen), SPINK1 (serine protease inhibitor, Kazal type 1), CFTR (cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator), and CTRC (chymotrypsin C). Include CPA1 and other less common susceptibility genes if the panel is available. PRSS1 gain‑of‑function variants (eg, R122H, N29I) and deleterious SPINK1 variants (eg, N34S) are classically associated with hereditary pancreatitis in children; CFTR mutations can present as CF‑related pancreatitis even without classic pulmonary disease, and CTRC/CPA1 variants confer increased susceptibility.

Why this matters (clinical reasoning)
A documented pathogenic mutation explains a predisposition to recurrent ductal injury and pseudocyst formation that can lead to complications such as pancreatopleural fistula. PRSS1 mutations are usually autosomal dominant with variable penetrance and are associated with earlier onset, more frequent relapses, and a substantially increased lifetime risk of pancreatic adenocarcinoma. SPINK1, CFTR and CTRC are generally susceptibility alleles (often autosomal recessive or complex inheritance) that modify risk and clinical course. Knowing the genotype therefore informs prognosis (risk of recurrence and complications), anticipatory surveillance, and family counseling (inheritance pattern and cascade testing).

How this changes counseling and long‑term management
Counseling: refer for formal genetic counseling early. Explain inheritance, variable penetrance, and what a positive, negative, or indeterminate result means for parents and siblings. Offer targeted (cascade) testing to first‑degree relatives if a pathogenic variant is found.

Surveillance and prevention: emphasize strict avoidance of pancreatic stressors (no alcohol, no smoking as the child ages), optimize nutrition and weight management, and treat exocrine insufficiency and pain proactively. Because hereditary pancreatitis increases lifetime pancreatic cancer risk, plan long‑term surveillance: for patients with confirmed hereditary pancreatitis, consider pancreatic cancer surveillance in adulthood (typically beginning at ~age 40 or 10 years earlier than the youngest affected relative) using MRI/MRCP and/or endoscopic ultrasound per specialist guidance. Monitor growth, nutritional status, fat‑soluble vitamins, and screen periodically for endocrine insufficiency (annual glucose/HbA1c) because diabetes risk rises.

Clinical management implications: a genetic diagnosis lowers threshold for earlier definitive or endoscopic ductal interventions when ductal strictures, obstruction, or pseudocysts recur, since recurrent leaks/fistulae are more likely. It also supports long‑term involvement of a multidisciplinary pancreatitis team (pediatric gastroenterology, surgery, nutrition, pain management, genetics). If CFTR is implicated, evaluate for cystic fibrosis with sweat testing and consider CF care cascade; certain CFTR modulators may be relevant for classic CF phenotypes.

Psychosocial and reproductive planning: discuss implications for future fertility and reproductive options (prenatal testing, preimplantation genetic diagnosis) when appropriate, and provide psychosocial support for chronic disease.

Practical steps now
Order a pediatric pancreatitis genetic panel and arrange genetic counseling. Meanwhile continue standard care: treat current complication, ensure pancreatic enzyme replacement as needed, optimize nutrition, and schedule follow‑up with pediatric gastroenterology for a long‑term plan that incorporates the genetic results.

In short: test PRSS1, SPINK1, CFTR and CTRC (±CPA1/others). A positive result alters prognosis, prompts family cascade testing and targeted surveillance (including later pancreatic‑cancer surveillance), and lowers the threshold for earlier definitive/endoscopic interventions and multidisciplinary long‑term care.
