Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I

### [Clinical Pearls] A 40-year-old woman has nocturnal oxygen desaturations to 70%–80% without dyspnea, with a type III sleep study showing AHI 6.0 and 72% central events. What methodological limitation of a type III study could underestimate the true severity of sleep-disordered breathing, and why?

The key limitation of a type III (home, four‑channel) study is that it lacks EEG — so it cannot distinguish sleep from wake. Because the AHI is calculated against total recording time rather than true sleep time, periods of wake are included in the denominator and respiratory events that occur only during sleep are diluted, producing a spuriously low AHI. In this patient that methodological feature explains the marked mismatch between severe nocturnal desaturations and an AHI of 6.0.

Beyond underestimating AHI, the absence of EEG also prevents detection of sleep stage and arousal‑related events and makes it impossible to document sleep‑related hypoventilation reliably (no CO2 monitoring is typically included in type III studies). For suspected central hypoventilation or brainstem disease, a full type I polysomnogram with EEG, arousal scoring, and end‑tidal or transcutaneous CO2 is required to measure true sleep time, quantify central apneas, identify arousal‑linked breathing abnormalities, and detect nocturnal hypoventilation.
