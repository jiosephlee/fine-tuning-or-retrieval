Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

### [Morning Report] A 21-week pregnant patient presents with acute left upper quadrant and pelvic pain plus dyspnoea. Ultrasound shows two large unilocular cystic masses with peripheral vascularity, one subphrenic (18 cm) and one in the pouch of Douglas (9–10 cm). What is your first-pass differential for the pain and dyspnoea, and which life-threatening obstetric and non-obstetric causes must be excluded early?

Think first about what is causing the acute pain and the new dyspnoea, then prioritise exclusion of immediate threats that would require urgent intervention.

Clinical reasoning and first‑pass differential
The combination of acute severe left upper‑quadrant and pelvic pain in the second trimester with two large abdominal cystic masses points to mass‑related mechanical and ischemic processes first: torsion (pedunculated myoma or adnexal torsion), acute haemorrhage into a mass, and red (ischaemic) degeneration or infarction of a leiomyoma. These explain focal pain, local peritoneal irritation and fever. The pelvic mass adherent to a posterior myoma makes red degeneration or cystic degeneration and torsion of a pedunculated component especially likely. Infective complication (infected cyst or myoma with sepsis) is also possible — the low‑grade fever and positive urine culture support infection as a cofactor.

Dyspnoea in this setting is most often mechanical: massive upward displacement of the diaphragm by large intra‑abdominal masses and uterine enlargement limiting tidal volume. Other causes that must be considered are anaemia from acute or chronic blood loss (noted Hb 9.9 → 7.5 g/dL postop in this case), septic physiology, and true cardiopulmonary disease (pulmonary embolism, pneumonia, pleural effusion, cardiogenic failure). In pregnancy, PE remains a high‑risk diagnosis even when mechanical causes seem likely.

Life‑threatening obstetric and non‑obstetric causes to exclude early
You should rapidly exclude conditions that necessitate immediate intervention: massive intra‑abdominal haemorrhage or rupture of a mass (myoma or ovarian cyst), ovarian/adnexal torsion with infarction, severe infection progressing to sepsis, and pulmonary embolism. Less likely but important to think about are strangulating bowel obstruction, splenic rupture (if left subphrenic pain is prominent), and acute cardiopulmonary collapse (peripartum cardiomyopathy is rare at 21 weeks but consider cardiogenic causes if exam suggests it).

Practical early work‑up and decision points
Begin with rapid bedside assessment: continuous maternal vitals and fetal heart rate, focused abdominal exam, oxygen saturation, ECG. Obtain STAT labs: CBC with differential, type & screen, coagulation profile, blood gas/lactate if hypotension or severe dyspnoea, CRP, blood cultures if febrile. Send urine culture and treat urinary sepsis promptly if suspected. Perform targeted imaging: expedite transabdominal and transvaginal ultrasound with colour Doppler to look for ovarian/adnexal torsion, active bleeding, free fluid, and mass vascularity; if ultrasound is inconclusive and diagnosis will change management, non‑contrast MRI is appropriate in the second trimester to clarify origin and complications. For dyspnoea with concern for PE, do not rely on D‑dimer alone — proceed to imaging (ventilation‑perfusion scan or CTPA), balancing radiation but prioritising maternal life.

Management priorities
If haemodynamic instability or peritoneal signs develop, proceed to surgical exploration. If torsion, rupture, or infected/necrotic mass is likely and symptoms are progressive, timely surgical management (ideally after multidisciplinary planning) is indicated even in the second trimester. Treat sepsis aggressively with pregnancy‑appropriate antibiotics and supportive care. If PE is confirmed, start anticoagulation while balancing risk of recent or planned surgery.

Takeaway
Acute pain with large pelvic/abdominal masses in pregnancy is most commonly due to torsion, haemorrhage into a mass or ischaemic (red) degeneration of a myoma; infection can coexist and large masses can cause restrictive dyspnoea. Rapidly exclude life‑threatening haemorrhage/rupture, ovarian torsion, sepsis, and pulmonary embolism with focused clinical assessment, urgent ultrasound (±MRI), appropriate labs and, where indicated, thoracic imaging. Management should prioritise maternal stabilisation and definitive treatment of the complication while preserving the pregnancy when safe to do so.
