Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

### [Morning Report] Why might a second, fixed posterior intramural–subserosal fibroid be left in situ during mid-pregnancy myomectomy, and what risks does its removal pose at that gestation?

Because the second lesion was an intramural–subserosal fibroid that was broad‑based, fixed to the posterior uterine wall and not acutely compromised, the team chose to leave it in place to avoid the predictable maternal and fetal hazards of trying to excise it in mid‑pregnancy.

The clinical reasoning is straightforward: removing a fixed intramural/subserosal myoma requires a deep uterine incision and extensive dissection into the myometrium. In the mid‑second trimester that almost always produces brisk bleeding from a highly vascular uterus, risks entry into or disruption of the uterine cavity, and creates a large myometrial defect that is technically difficult to repair under time pressure. Those intraoperative problems translate into serious obstetric risks — hemorrhage requiring transfusion or hysterectomy, premature rupture of membranes, uterine irritability or preterm labor, direct fetal compromise from maternal instability or anesthesia, and an increased chance of pregnancy loss. In addition, making a substantial myometrial scar in the mid‑trimester raises the theoretical risk of later uterine rupture, and it may complicate placentation or fetal growth. 

By contrast, pedunculated or torsed myomas that are the source of acute pain, infection, leaking or necrosis can be safely removed because they can often be excised with minimal myometrial disruption and controlled haemostasis. A fixed posterior intramural mass without those complications is better managed expectantly: shorter operative time, less blood loss, continuous fetal monitoring, perioperative tocolaxis/progesterone as used here, and the option to defer definitive treatment until after delivery (or at the time of planned cesarean). This balances maternal benefit against fetal risk and is why the caudal mass was left in situ.
