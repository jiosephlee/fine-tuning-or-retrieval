Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

### [Management] When is laparoscopy versus laparotomy appropriate for management of adnexal or uterine masses in pregnancy, and what patient- and mass-specific factors determine the choice?

The choice between laparoscopy and laparotomy for adnexal or uterine masses in pregnancy is not arbitrary; it follows the same clinical logic as in non‑pregnant patients but must be adapted to gestational physiology, fetal safety and obstetric consequences. Laparoscopy is preferable when it can safely achieve the diagnostic or therapeutic goal with less maternal morbidity, but laparotomy remains the appropriate option when the mass, the patient or the intraoperative risks make minimally invasive access unsafe or unlikely to succeed.

Practically, favor laparoscopy for presumed benign, mobile adnexal lesions or pedunculated subserosal myomas that are relatively small (generally up to ~8–12 cm depending on surgeon experience and trocar placement), are not densely adherent to the uterus, and when the gestation is in the first or early second trimester. The benefits—reduced postoperative pain, shorter hospital stay and fewer adhesions—are real and supported by series in pregnancy. To minimize fetal risk use an open (Hasson) entry, place trocars high and lateral to the uterus, limit insufflation pressures (10–12 mmHg), tilt the patient left, and monitor the fetal heart rate before and after the procedure. Experienced laparoscopic teams are essential; conversion should be anticipated.

Choose laparotomy when mass‑specific or patient‑specific factors increase the risk of incomplete resection, major bleeding, prolonged operative time, or uterine injury with consequent pregnancy loss. Those factors include large size that limits working space (masses filling the abdomen), multiple lesions, intramural or posterior isthmic myomas adherent to uterine wall, highly vascular or necrotic/degenerating masses, unclear anatomy with concern for malignancy, evidence of torsion with haemodynamic compromise, infection, or need for extensive reconstruction. Later gestational age (mid‑late second and third trimesters) also favors laparotomy because the enlarged fundal height impedes safe trocar placement and instrument manipulation. Patient factors—obesity, prior extensive abdominal surgery with adhesions, cardiopulmonary disease that makes pneumoperitoneum high risk, and limited surgeon experience—also push toward open surgery.

Decision making must balance three goals: preserve the pregnancy, address maternal risk from the mass (pain, torsion, bleeding, infection, malignancy), and avoid overtreatment. That balance explains why a multidisciplinary team elected laparotomy in this case: two very large, vascularized, atypical masses occupying the abdomen and pelvis with worsening pain, dyspnoea from mass effect, and MRI features that did not clearly separate adnexal from uterine origin. The intraoperative finding of a twisted, highly vascular pedunculated leiomyoma justified removal; leaving the adherent pelvic myoma in situ minimized obstetric risk. Had the lesion been smaller, mobile and clearly pedunculated earlier in pregnancy, a laparoscopic approach (open entry, low pressure, careful uterine handling) could reasonably have been attempted.

Finally, whatever approach is chosen, prepare for obstetric contingencies: document fetal heart tones before, during (as feasible) and after surgery, have blood products available, use uterine‑protective measures (avoid unnecessary uterotonics; consider perioperative progesterone or tocolysis based on local practice), and plan delivery mode guided by residual myomas and obstetric anatomy rather than by the incision alone. The surgical approach should be individualized, prioritizing fetal–maternal safety and the likelihood of definitive, timely management.
