Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

### [Differential Diagnosis] How would an elevated LDH in the setting of a rapidly enlarging uterine mass influence suspicion for leiomyosarcoma, and how is oncologic concern balanced with fetal considerations in mid-pregnancy?

An isolated laboratory abnormality never makes the diagnosis, but an elevated lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) in the context of a rapidly enlarging uterine mass should raise the index of suspicion for a malignant smooth‑muscle neoplasm and prompt more urgent, multidisciplinary assessment.

Why LDH matters here: LDH is a nonspecific marker of cell turnover and tissue necrosis. Rapid tumor growth with central ischemic necrosis—features that can produce a high LDH—are characteristic of sarcomas (including leiomyosarcoma) but can also be seen with degenerating benign fibroids, torsion of a pedunculated myoma, infarction, infection, or even pregnancy‑related changes. Thus an elevated LDH increases pretest probability for malignancy when seen together with other worrying features: marked and persistent rapid growth despite pregnancy, new focal solid components or irregular enhancement on MRI, heterogeneous signal with hemorrhage/necrosis, new severe pain or systemic symptoms, and atypical vascular patterns on Doppler. By itself LDH has poor specificity and modest sensitivity — it is a clue, not a confirmation.

How to act in mid‑pregnancy: balance oncologic urgency against fetal maturity and the maternal/fetal risks of surgery or delayed diagnosis. The clinical approach is therefore individualized and governed by three principles: confirm the level of suspicion, minimize harm to the pregnancy when reasonable, and expedite definitive care when concern for malignancy is substantial.

If suspicion is low–moderate (e.g., mildly elevated LDH but imaging consistent with degenerating fibroid, stable fetal status, and symptoms controlled): obtain targeted, non‑contrast MRI if not already done; repeat imaging and tumour markers in short interval; increase surveillance frequency; treat complications conservatively; involve gynecologic oncology in planning. Avoid percutaneous biopsy of presumed uterine mass during pregnancy because sampling is often nondiagnostic and may risk bleeding or dissemination.

If suspicion is high (rapid, unabated growth; markedly elevated LDH; MRI showing large necrotic solid components; systemic signs): counsel the patient that the benefit of prompt surgical resection—both to establish diagnosis and to achieve tumor‑free margins—may outweigh the fetal risks. In the second trimester (as in this case) surgical myomectomy or excisional biopsy via laparotomy can be performed with acceptable fetal and maternal outcomes when indicated, because the uterus is less irritable than in the third trimester and teratogenic risks are lower after organogenesis. Intraoperative continuous fetal monitoring, perioperative steroids if preterm delivery is a possibility, and a plan for fetal resuscitation are essential. If malignancy is confirmed, further management (completion hysterectomy, staging, adjuvant therapy) must be timed according to gestational age, the extent of disease, and maternal wishes; chemotherapy in pregnancy is feasible in some situations but requires oncologic expertise.

In all scenarios the discussion must be shared and documented: explain the rarity of uterine sarcoma, the imperfect predictive value of LDH and CA‑125, the risks of waiting (progression, rupture, hemorrhage, missed window for cure) versus the risks of surgery to the fetus (preterm labor, fetal loss), and the alternatives, including deferred surgery after fetal viability. The multidisciplinary team should include obstetrics, gynecologic oncology, maternal–fetal medicine, anesthesia, and neonatology to tailor timing and the surgical plan to both oncologic safety and fetal maturity.

Applied to the presented case: had LDH been elevated in addition to the clinical picture of pain, fever, mixed solid–cystic mass and rapid enlargement, the threshold to operate at 21 weeks would have been lower—both to relieve symptoms and to obtain tissue diagnosis—while preserving the pregnancy when possible, as the team ultimately did.
