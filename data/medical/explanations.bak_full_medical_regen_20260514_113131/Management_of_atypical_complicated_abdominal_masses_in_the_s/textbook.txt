Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Assessment

When a pregnant patient in the second trimester presents with acute abdominal pain and dyspnea, the immediate priority is the same as in any acute presentation: assess and stabilize airway, breathing and circulation while simultaneously considering pregnancy-specific physiologic changes and fetal wellbeing. In this clinical context, however, the evaluation must also be directed at distinguishing obstetrical and gynecologic causes (torsion, degeneration, rupture, infection of an adnexal lesion or myoma, placental problems) from primary cardiopulmonary disease. A succinct but systematic approach reduces delay in diagnosis and allows early multidisciplinary planning.

Begin with a focused yet rapid physiologic assessment. Check pulse oximetry, heart rate, blood pressure, respiratory rate and temperature; in pregnancy, maintain a high index of concern for hypoxaemia because maternal oxygenation directly affects fetal oxygen delivery (aim for an SpO2 that preserves adequate fetal oxygenation — many teams use a target ≥95% when possible). Document any chest symptoms, and obtain an ECG if there is chest pain or concern for cardiac pathology. Routine chest radiography (with abdominal shielding) or CT should not be withheld if clinically indicated to evaluate life‑threatening pulmonary processes (for example, suspected pulmonary embolus or pneumonia), but consultation with radiology and obstetrics is appropriate to weigh risks and optimize technique. In the absence of cardiopulmonary findings, dyspnea in a woman with very large abdominopelvic masses is commonly mechanical: diaphragmatic elevation, reduced functional residual capacity and limited chest wall excursion can produce subjective shortness of breath without primary lung or heart disease, as occurred in the case patient whose SpO2 and vitals were initially normal.

A careful abdominal and pelvic examination provides high-yield information and should be performed early. Note overall abdominal size and compare the measured or palpated fundal height with the expected gestational age; an abdomen that is “too large” for dates suggests an additional intra‑abdominal process such as a large cystic mass, ascites, multiple leiomyomas, or polyhydramnios. Palpate the abdomen for localized tenderness, masses, peritoneal signs (guarding, rigidity, rebound), and for signs of free fluid. Characterize any palpable lesion for location (quadrant, midline), size, contour, consistency, mobility and tenderness. Perform a bimanual pelvic examination when feasible and acceptable: a pelvic mass that is fixed in the pelvis and bulges into the posterior fornix (as in this case) suggests a pelvic origin or uterine-adherent lesion, whereas a mobile, high, upper-abdominal mass may be pedunculated from the uterine fundus or ovarian in origin. Documentation of the relationship of masses to the uterine fundus and cervix (fundal height, whether the mass moves separately from the uterus) helps guide the next diagnostic steps. Throughout the examination remain mindful of the comfort and positioning needs of a pregnant patient—left lateral tilt and semi‑upright positioning can reduce aortocaval compression and diaphragmatic compromise.

Initial laboratory testing should be broad and directed at the clinical possibilities: a complete blood count, blood group and antibody screen, ferritin and iron indices, basic metabolic panel, liver enzymes, coagulation studies if bleeding is suspected, C‑reactive protein, and urinalysis with culture. Interpret results in the context of pregnancy. Pregnancy commonly produces a mild physiologic leukocytosis and neutrophilia (white-cell counts are often higher than the non‑pregnant range, particularly in the second and third trimesters), and CRP can be modestly elevated; therefore small deviations from non‑pregnant reference ranges should not be overinterpreted as infection without corroborating clinical or microbiologic evidence. Nevertheless, rising leukocytes, a high CRP, fever, or a positive urine culture — as in this patient with a Klebsiella urinary tract infection — point to an infectious process that requires treatment. Early urine culture is essential since urinary infection is a frequent and treatable contributor to fever and systemic inflammation in pregnancy.

Assessment for anemia is essential. Many pregnant women have iron deficiency; ferritin <30 mcg/L and low hemoglobin support that diagnosis. Acute or subacute blood loss from a ruptured or degenerating mass, haemorrhagic degeneration of a leiomyoma, or chronic inflammatory anaemia can coexist. Clinical severity and gestational considerations determine management: symptomatic anemia or very low hemoglobin (institutional thresholds vary, but many teams use Hb ≤7–8 g/dL as a transfusion trigger in hemodynamically unstable or symptomatic patients) often prompts transfusion and iron repletion (intravenous iron is commonly used in pregnancy for moderate–severe iron deficiency). In this case the patient’s Hb fell postoperatively to 7.5 g/dL and required IV iron and transfusion, illustrating the need to anticipate and plan for possible blood loss when masses are large or surgery is undertaken.

Early, pregnancy‑safe symptomatic treatment eases discomfort and avoids precipitating iatrogenic problems. Paracetamol (acetaminophen) is first‑line for acute pain in pregnancy and was effective in the presented case. Opioids may be used for short‑term, severe pain under supervision; nonsteroidal anti‑inflammatory drugs should generally be avoided in late pregnancy because of risks to the fetus (ductus arteriosus constriction, oligohydramnios), although earlier use may be considered cautiously. Non-pharmacologic measures can be helpful: semi‑orthostatic or semi‑Fowler positioning and left lateral tilt reduce diaphragmatic elevation and aortocaval compression by the gravid uterus and large masses, often improving dyspnea and venous return. Supplemental oxygen should be given only when indicated by hypoxaemia.

Concurrent evaluation for the source and nature of the mass(es) should be rapid and multidisciplinary. Transabdominal and transvaginal ultrasonography with color Doppler is the first‑line imaging modality in pregnancy: it defines cystic versus solid features, vascularity, relationship to the uterus, and the presence of free fluid. If sonographic findings are inconclusive and further anatomic detail is required, non‑contrast MRI (preferably ≤1.5 T) provides valuable soft‑tissue characterization and can delineate the distance between lesions and the uterine cavity without ionizing radiation; gadolinium contrast should be avoided unless the potential benefit clearly outweighs the fetal risk. Consult radiology early to tailor imaging. In most centers, CT is reserved for scenarios in which MRI is not available and CT findings would materially alter acute management; fetal shielding and radiology consultation are essential.

Multidisciplinary coordination should begin at presentation. Obstetricians and gynecologists should direct maternal‑fetal assessment and manage obstetrical interventions. Radiology interprets imaging strategy and findings. Anesthesiology should be involved early when there is any potential for operative intervention so that airway and haemodynamic planning and blood‑product availability are anticipated. If malignancy is a concern, gynecologic oncology should be included. Neonatology should be aware of any possibility of preterm delivery so that steroid and tocolysis considerations, as well as neonatal preparedness, are addressed. In the presented case, such coordination led to timely imaging, operative planning, intraoperative fetal monitoring and appropriate postoperative obstetrical follow‑up.

Finally, triage decisions must balance maternal risks (hemodynamic instability, peritonitis, sepsis, ischemia from torsion or degeneration) and fetal risks (preterm birth, fetal compromise during maternal hypotension or anesthesia). Indications for urgent surgical exploration in pregnancy include haemodynamic instability, peritonitis, evidence of ovarian torsion or ruptured hemorrhagic mass, sepsis not controlled by medical therapy, or progressive respiratory compromise from space‑occupying intra‑abdominal lesions. When surgery is elected, steps to protect the pregnancy include continuous fetal monitoring when feasible, minimizing operative time and blood loss, and involvement of an experienced obstetric anesthesia team — measures that were followed in the reported patient.

In summary, the initial assessment of a second‑trimester pregnant woman with acute abdominal pain and dyspnea focuses first on stabilization, then on a targeted history and physical examination (noting discrepancy between abdominal size and gestational age and bimanual findings), appropriate laboratory and imaging investigations tuned for pregnancy, early detection and management of infection and anemia, symptom control with pregnancy‑safe measures, and early multidisciplinary coordination to guide imaging and determine the urgency and extent of intervention. The case illustrates these principles: careful clinical assessment identified large, symptomatic masses producing mechanical respiratory compromise and localized signs of infection, targeted imaging with ultrasound and non‑contrast MRI refined the differential, and a coordinated operative plan prioritized maternal stabilization while preserving the ongoing pregnancy.

## Pathophysiology and Epidemiology of Leiomyomas and Adnexal Masses in Pregnancy

Uterine leiomyomas (fibroids) and adnexal masses are encountered relatively commonly in pregnancy and can present diagnostic and management challenges because their behaviour is shaped both by tumour biology and by the altered hormonal and hemodynamic milieu of pregnancy. Leiomyomas are benign monoclonal smooth muscle tumours of the myometrium whose growth is influenced by oestrogen and progesterone signalling, growth factors and extracellular matrix production. In pregnancy, the circulating levels of oestrogen and progesterone rise markedly and uterine blood flow increases; together these factors can stimulate both cellular hypertrophy and expansion of the extracellular matrix and promote increased perfusion of fibroids. Epidemiologically, reported prevalences vary with the population studied and detection method, but leiomyomas are found in roughly 0.3–10% of pregnancies and adnexal masses (including benign ovarian cysts and endometriomas) in approximately 1–6%.

Fibroid growth during pregnancy is characteristically non‑linear. Many lesions enlarge most rapidly in early gestation: studies describe the steepest size increase between the first and second trimesters, with slower growth thereafter and a tendency for regression in late pregnancy and the puerperium. Clinically, this produces an important implication: a small, asymptomatic fibroid found early in pregnancy may increase in size and symptom burden before eventual partial involution postpartum. The case illustrates this dynamic—large leiomyomas that likely grew under the influence of pregnancy hormones and increased perfusion ultimately produced mass effect and symptoms.

Degenerative changes are common explanations for acute symptoms arising from fibroids in pregnancy. As a myoma outgrows its blood supply or its vascular pedicle becomes compromised, areas of hyaline, cystic or red (“carneous”) degeneration and even focal infarction may develop. Red degeneration is particularly associated with pregnancy: venous thrombosis within the periphery of a fibroid leads to haemorrhagic infarction and a typical clinical picture of localized acute pain, low‑grade fever and an inflammatory laboratory pattern (mild leukocytosis and elevation of acute phase reactants). Cystic degeneration can produce large cystic cavities within a fibroid and, on imaging, produce an appearance that may mimic an adnexal cyst. Pedunculated subserosal leiomyomas are at special risk for torsion of their stalk; torsion produces acute abdominal pain, may lead to leakage or rupture, and can be complicated by infection or necrosis. In the presented patient the upper lesion proved at operation to be a highly vascular, pedunculated cystic leiomyoma that had twisted and leaked—explaining the fever and the inflammatory laboratory signals and accounting for why preoperative imaging suggested an ovarian cyst.

Adnexal masses in pregnancy include functional ovarian cysts, benign neoplasms and endometriomas. Endometriomas undergo an important transformation in pregnancy known as decidualization: under the influence of progesterone, ectopic endometrial stroma becomes hypertrophic and vascular, producing mural nodules and increased Doppler flow that can mimic malignant mural nodules on ultrasound or MRI. Endometriomas classically have “ground glass” echogenicity on ultrasound and characteristic T1 hyperintensity on MRI from blood products, but decidualized nodules blur these distinctions and complicate the imaging differential. This diagnostic overlap—decidualized endometriomas appearing nodular and vascular, and cystic or degenerating fibroids appearing as large adnexal‑appearing cysts—explains why large uterine and adnexal lesions are often misclassified preoperatively.

Imaging features that favour fibroid versus ovarian origin are important to recognize in pregnancy because management differs. Fibroids typically arise from the uterus and may demonstrate a bridging vessel or a stalk connecting to uterine tissue; on ultrasound they are commonly hypoechoic with well‑defined margins, although cystic degeneration produces anechoic spaces and can obscure the myometrial origin. On MRI, typical leiomyomas are low signal on T2, but degenerating fibroids show heterogeneous high T2 signal and, if hemorrhagic, high T1 signal. Endometriomas usually show T1 hyperintensity and “shading” on T2, but decidualized areas can be T2 hyperintense and vascular. The need to avoid gadolinium in pregnancy limits contrast‑enhanced discrimination, so careful attention to morphology, the relationship to the uterus, and serial ultrasound follow‑up are often decisive.

Large abdominal or pelvic masses exert mechanical effects that are physiologically relevant in pregnancy. A mass that occupies substantial abdominal volume displaces the gravid uterus and elevates the diaphragm, reducing functional residual capacity and causing positional or exertional dyspnoea even in the absence of intrinsic cardiopulmonary disease. Venous return and cardiac preload may also be variably affected by mass position. In the reported patient, dyspnoea was attributable to diaphragmatic compression from the enlarged uterus plus the bulky leiomyomas rather than primary pulmonary pathology—an important distinction that supported conservative respiratory management while addressing the surgical indication.

Serum tumour markers must be interpreted cautiously in pregnancy. CA‑125, the marker most often used in adnexal mass evaluation, undergoes physiologic variation: it is frequently elevated in the first trimester and may rise again near term, while values in the second trimester are often lower. Moreover, CA‑125 is non‑specific and can be modestly increased by benign gynecologic conditions (endometriosis, adenomyosis, large fibroids), pelvic inflammation, or decidualized endometriomas. Thus, an isolated mild elevation of CA‑125 in pregnancy should not be taken as evidence of malignancy; trends over time and correlation with imaging are more informative. In the case, a mildly rising CA‑125 contributed to concern but was not definitive; the intraoperative finding of a benign, infarcted pedunculated fibroid confirmed the non‑malignant origin.

Clinically, these pathophysiologic and epidemiologic realities shape management. Most fibroids and small adnexal masses are managed expectantly during pregnancy, with intervention reserved for complications—severe pain from degeneration, torsion of a pedunculated lesion, infection, or significant mass effect causing cardiopulmonary compromise or obstructed labour. When intervention is necessary, multidisciplinary planning is required to balance maternal benefit and fetal risk, to use the safest imaging modalities (ultrasound and non‑contrast MRI), and to choose the timing and surgical approach that minimize obstetric complications. The present case exemplifies this approach: the combination of acute symptoms, imaging uncertainty, and mass effect prompted laparotomy, but intraoperative assessment allowed targeted excision of the twisted pedunculated myoma while leaving a fixed pelvic myoma in situ, thereby preserving the pregnancy and avoiding unnecessary resection.

In summary, leiomyomas and adnexal lesions interact with pregnancy physiology in ways that can produce rapid size changes, degenerative complications and diagnostic confusion. Understanding the hormonal drivers of growth, the types of degeneration that produce pain and fever, the mechanisms by which fibroids can mimic ovarian pathology, and the limits of tumour markers in pregnancy is essential for safe, patient‑centred decision making.

## Differential Diagnosis of Large Cystic Abdominopelvic Masses in the Second Trimester

Large cystic or mixed abdominopelvic masses encountered in the second trimester present a diagnostic challenge because the gravid uterus alters anatomy, imaging windows are restricted, and serum markers have limited specificity in pregnancy. In this situation the clinician must integrate the clinical presentation (pain, fever, mass effect), focused ultrasound with colour Doppler, and—when ultrasound is non-diagnostic—non‑contrast MRI to separate lesions of uterine origin from adnexal or peritoneal pathology. The case presented (two large cystic masses in a 21‑week pregnancy, one ultimately shown at surgery to be a twisted pedunculated cystic leiomyoma) illustrates the overlapping appearances and the importance of searching specifically for features that discriminate uterine leiomyomas (including degenerating or pedunculated subserosal types) from ovarian cysts, decidualized endometriomas, adnexal torsion, and other rarer entities.

Approach and priorities
- First, establish clinical urgency. Acute severe pain, peritoneal signs, fever suggest torsion, infarction/necrosis, superinfection or rupture and prioritise urgent intervention even if imaging is imperfect. Mass effect causing respiratory compromise or compression of urinary tract or the uterus also pushes toward intervention. In the presented patient, worsening pain, dyspnoea from mass effect and signs consistent with infection/necrosis were the drivers to operate.
- Second, perform systematic imaging: transabdominal and transvaginal ultrasound with Doppler is the first-line test in pregnancy. If ultrasound cannot confidently determine origin or exclude malignancy, proceed to non‑contrast MRI (preferably 1.5 T) which better delineates relationships between mass and myometrium, evaluates internal contents and defines vascular/pedicle anatomy without gadolinium exposure.
- Third, use tumor markers judiciously. CA‑125 and other markers can be altered by pregnancy, endometriosis and benign conditions; an isolated mild CA‑125 elevation is not diagnostic. Serial trends may add information but should not trump convincing imaging or clinical findings.

Common diagnostic considerations and distinguishing features

Degenerating leiomyoma (cystic degeneration)
- Context: Uterine fibroids are common and may undergo degeneration during pregnancy as blood supply becomes insufficient to rapidly enlarging fibroid tissue. Cystic degeneration produces large fluid-filled spaces within a fibroid.
- Imaging: Ultrasound shows a generally uterine‑contiguous mass with cystic areas and well‑defined margins; Doppler may show peripheral vascularity with relatively avascular cystic core. On MRI the degenerated areas are variable: acute haemorrhagic degeneration may be T1 hyperintense, whereas chronic cystic degeneration is T2 hyperintense; fibroid capsule and surrounding myometrium often identifiable. The “beak” or “claw” sign (myometrium curving around the lesion) and direct continuity with the uterine wall support leiomyomatous origin.
- Clinical clues: Localised pain (especially with red degeneration), low‑grade fever and raised inflammatory markers may occur. Large lesions can cause mass effect (dyspnoea, urinary frequency).

Pedunculated subserosal leiomyoma with torsion
- Context: A pedunculated fibroid may twist on its stalk, compromising vascular supply and producing acute, often severe, focal abdominal pain, fever and necrosis.
- Imaging: If visible, a pedicle connecting the mass to the uterus clinches the diagnosis. Doppler may show absence or reduced flow in the pedicle or in the mass if torsion has led to infarction. MRI can show a twisted vascular pedicle (whirlpool) and areas of haemorrhagic infarction (T1 hyperintensity). In pregnancy, a pedicle may be displaced or obscured by the enlarged uterus and bowel.
- Clinical clues: Acute, focal severe pain, systemic inflammatory response or fever, and a previously mobile mass that becomes fixed favor torsion; surgical management is often necessary.

Decidualized endometrioma
- Context: Endometriomas (ovarian “chocolate” cysts) may undergo decidualization in pregnancy, producing vascularised mural nodules that can mimic malignancy.
- Imaging: Classic non‑pregnant endometrioma shows T1 hyperintensity and T2 shading (progressive signal loss on T2), reflecting blood products. In pregnancy, decidualized mural nodules are typically vascular on Doppler and may enhance on imaging (contrast not used in pregnancy), making distinction from neoplasm difficult. Identification of ovarian stroma adjacent to the cyst, and a plane separating the cyst from the uterus, supports ovarian origin.
- Clinical clues: History of known endometriosis, cyclical pain prior to pregnancy, and typical MRI signal characteristics favour endometrioma; however, decidualized nodules may require close follow‑up or surgical evaluation if suspicious.

Benign ovarian cysts (serous/mucinous)
- Imaging: Serous cystadenomas are usually unilocular, thin‑walled, anechoic on ultrasound. Mucinous cystadenomas may be multilocular with septations. An ovary seen separately from the cyst or visualisation of ovarian tissue adjacent to the lesion supports ovarian origin. Absence of solid papillary projections or suspicious mural nodules and lack of ascites favour benign cysts.
- Clinical clues: Typically asymptomatic unless large; torsion is a complication if pedunculated.

Paraovarian cyst and hydrosalpinx
- Paraovarian cyst: Grows in the mesosalpinx/mesovarium; ultrasound shows a simple cyst located adjacent to but separate from the ovary with a plane of cleavage. The ipsilateral ovary can generally be identified next to the cyst.
- Hydrosalpinx: Tubular, elongated cystic structure with incomplete septations and the “beads on a string” sign on ultrasound. Often bilateral or associated with pelvic inflammatory disease history.

Peritoneal inclusion cyst
- Imaging: Multiloculated fluid collection that molds around ovary and pelvic organs; the ovary is typically seen within or at the periphery of the cyst (“ovary within a cyst” sign). Often associated with prior abdominal surgery, endometriosis or pelvic inflammatory disease.
- Clinical clues: Longstanding pelvic pain and prior surgery/adhesions.

Ovarian torsion
- Imaging and clinical features: Enlarged, oedematous ovary with peripheral displaced follicles on ultrasound and absent or reduced Doppler flow. The whirlpool sign (twisted vascular pedicle) is diagnostic when seen. Torsion is a surgical emergency.

Neoplastic processes (primary ovarian cystadenomas, borderline tumors, cystic carcinomas)
- Imaging: Complex cystic masses with papillary projections, solid enhancing components, nodularity, thick irregular septations, or ascites raise concern for neoplasia. In pregnancy the absence of gadolinium complicates assessment of enhancement, but morphology and vascularity of mural nodules on Doppler help risk‑stratify.
- Clinical clues: Advanced maternal age, rapidly growing complex cyst, or markedly elevated tumor markers raise suspicion though markers are imperfect in pregnancy.

Imaging signs that help determine uterine versus adnexal origin
- Continuity with myometrium: Direct visualization of the mass arising from or indenting the myometrium is the most reliable sign of uterine origin.
- Beak/claw sign: Myometrium forming a beak or claw around the mass suggests uterine origin.
- Bridging vessel sign: Vessels seen on colour Doppler or MRI running between the uterus and the mass favour a fibroid or subserosal myoma with pedicle.
- Identification of ovarian stroma: Visualization of normal ovarian tissue adjacent to the lesion (follicles or stromal echotexture) supports adnexal origin.
- Absence of ipsilateral ovary: If the ipsilateral ovary is not identified separately, an adnexal origin is less likely—though gravid anatomy can obscure the ovary.

Interpretation of serum tumor markers in pregnancy
- CA‑125 and other tumor markers are of limited utility because pregnancy, endometriosis and benign pelvic pathology alter concentrations. CA‑125 typically rises in the first and third trimesters and may be mildly elevated in benign conditions. An isolated mild elevation (for example low‑forties as in the case) is non‑diagnostic; serial measurements showing rising trend can prompt closer surveillance, but imaging and clinical context carry more weight in decision‑making.

Putting it together in practice
- Use a stepwise algorithm: (1) Assess clinical stability and need for urgent surgery. (2) Perform targeted ultrasound (transvaginal when feasible) with Doppler looking for pedicle, bridging vessels, ovarian stroma, papillary projections and haemorrhagic signal. (3) If origin remains uncertain or the lesion is large/complex, obtain non‑contrast MRI to define relation to myometrium, detect haemorrhagic infarction and assess mural nodules. (4) Interpret tumor markers cautiously and in serial context. (5) Convene a multidisciplinary discussion (obstetrics, gynaecology, radiology, anaesthesia) to weigh conservative management against surgery—indications for operation include torsion, rupture, uncontrolled pain, infection/sepsis, significant mass effect or reasonable concern for neoplasm.
- Recognise limitations: The gravid uterus may displace, compress or hide pedicles and planes of cleavage; decidualized endometriomas can look malignant; and non‑contrast MRI cannot provide contrast enhancement patterns that sometimes help differentiate benign from malignant mural nodules.

Clinical application to the case
In the reported patient, ultrasound showed two large unilocular cystic masses with peripheral vascularity and the caudal cyst appearing adherent to a posterior myoma; MRI revealed thick walls and inhomogeneous T1/T2 contents but did not clearly demonstrate a myometrial stalk for the cranial lesion. The mild CA‑125 elevation supported but did not prove an endometriotic origin. Because acute worsening pain, dyspnoea from mass effect, evidence of infection and imaging uncertainty were present, surgical exploration was appropriate. Intraoperative inspection identified a pedunculated, twisted cystic leiomyoma for the upper lesion—explaining fever, vascular appearance on imaging and the operative necessity—and a separate large cystic intramural-subserosal leiomyoma in the pelvis that was left in situ to preserve the pregnancy. This outcome highlights a few practical lessons: (1) large cystic leiomyomas can mimic adnexal cysts on imaging, particularly when gravid anatomy obscures bridging signs; (2) clinical deterioration should prompt surgical management even with imperfect preoperative characterization; and (3) decision making must balance maternal–fetal risks, weighing the risks of intervention against those of expectant management.

Summary
A methodical combination of clinical assessment, targeted ultrasound with Doppler, and non‑contrast MRI when needed will usually differentiate the common causes of large cystic abdominopelvic masses in pregnancy. Look specifically for continuity with the myometrium, bridging vessels, the beak/claw sign and the presence or absence of ovarian stroma. Interpret tumor markers cautiously and prioritise urgent surgery for torsion, rupture, infection or significant mass effect rather than pursuing exhaustive preoperative tissue diagnosis when the maternal–fetal balance mandates timely intervention.

## Diagnostic Workup and Imaging Algorithm in Pregnancy

When a pregnant patient presents with an atypical or symptomatic abdominal or pelvic mass, the diagnostic workup must balance the need for an accurate diagnosis and timely management against fetal safety and avoidance of unnecessary interventions. The guiding principle is to use the least invasive, lowest‑risk studies that will answer the clinical question. For most patients this pathway begins with a careful history and physical examination followed immediately by high‑quality ultrasound with color Doppler; additional imaging with non‑contrast magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is reserved for indeterminate cases, large or posterior lesions, multiplicity, or when precise anatomical delineation will influence management. Laboratory testing and fetal assessment are complementary, not substitutive, and must be integrated into a coordinated, multidisciplinary plan with obstetrics, radiology, and, when appropriate, gynecologic oncology and anesthesia.

Ultrasound is the first‑line imaging modality in pregnancy because it is widely available, portable, free of ionizing radiation, and excellent for real‑time evaluation of pelvic structures. Both transabdominal and transvaginal approaches should be employed when feasible. The sonographic exam should document the number and maximum dimensions of masses, wall thickness and regularity, internal architecture (unilocular vs multilocular cystic, solid components, papillary projections), echogenicity, septations, and presence of free fluid. Color and power Doppler assessment of vascularity is essential: hypervascular solid nodules, prominent peripheral vessels, or neovascularization raise concern for neoplasm or decidualized tissue in pregnancy, whereas a hypovascular, well‑circumscribed lesion favors a degenerating leiomyoma or benign simple cyst. Importantly, the relationship of a mass to the uterus and placenta should be sought: continuity with myometrium, an identifiable stalk, or a “bridging vessel” connecting a mass to the uterine wall strongly supports a uterine origin (pedunculated or subserosal leiomyoma), whereas an adnexal origin is suggested when the ovary is separately visualized and the mass is distinct from uterine tissue. In obese patients or when bowel gas and gravid uterus distort anatomy, sonographic windows can be limited; meticulous multiplanar scanning and repeat examinations improve accuracy.

When ultrasound is indeterminate or when masses are large, posteriorly located, multiple, or complex, non‑contrast MRI is the next step. MRI provides superior soft tissue contrast and multiplanar assessment that can define myometrial continuity, intracavitary extension, the presence of hemorrhagic contents, and relationships to adjacent viscera without ionizing radiation. A 1.5‑T magnet is commonly recommended in pregnancy because of its broad safety data; many centers also perform 3‑T exams but with careful consideration of specific absorption rate and acoustic exposure. Sequences useful in the evaluation include T1‑weighted (to detect blood products and fat), T2‑weighted (to evaluate cystic versus solid components and myometrial texture), and fat‑suppressed sequences (to highlight hemorrhage or fatty lesions). Hemorrhagic content appears hyperintense on T1‑weighted sequences, whereas the classic “T2 shading” of endometriomas—progressive loss of signal on T2—can help differentiate endometrioma from other cystic lesions. MRI can detect a pedicle and myometrial continuity that sonography sometimes cannot, and it can characterize degeneration of leiomyomas (hyaline, red/hemorrhagic, cystic) which commonly produce cystic or heterogeneous signal and can mimic adnexal pathology. The MRI report should explicitly address whether the mass is contiguous with the uterus, whether there are solid mural nodules, wall thickness, internal septations, and any adenopathy or signs of local invasion. Importantly, gadolinium contrast should be avoided in pregnancy except for compelling, life‑altering indications: gadolinium crosses the placenta and has been associated in observational data with rare adverse outcomes and potential neonatal risks. In nearly all cases of pelvic mass evaluation in pregnancy, diagnostic non‑contrast MRI provides sufficient information.

Laboratory studies are adjuncts that help refine the differential diagnosis and identify treatable contributors to the clinical picture. A complete blood count with iron indices should be obtained to quantify anemia—iron deficiency and anemia from degenerating fibroids are common—and to guide perioperative planning. Inflammatory markers (CRP) and white blood cell count are useful when infection or sepsis is suspected; a urine culture should be performed for any fever or urinary symptoms because urinary infection both complicates pregnancy and may explain fever and leukocytosis. Tumor markers (for example CA‑125, CA 19‑9, CEA, and beta‑hCG when appropriate) should be interpreted with extreme caution: CA‑125 commonly rises in the first and third trimesters and may be elevated with benign conditions such as endometriosis, adenomyosis, and degenerating fibroids. Thus, isolated tumor marker elevations are rarely diagnostic in pregnancy; serial measurements and interpretation in the context of imaging findings can be informative, but markers should never supplant tissue diagnosis when malignancy is strongly suspected. LDH and beta‑2 microglobulin have niche roles (for suspected germ cell or hematologic processes) but are not routine.

Clinical urgency and fetal considerations determine whether and how promptly imaging is obtained and whether intervention is pursued. When imaging or clinical signs suggest acute complications—torsion, rupture with hemoperitoneum, strangulation, infection of a mass, or obstruction—expedited surgical consultation and treatment are indicated regardless of gestational age. For nonurgent, indeterminate masses with benign‑appearing imaging, conservative management with repeat ultrasound surveillance is reasonable, since many leiomyomas change little or regress postpartum and some cystic masses may stabilize. If surgery is being considered, fetal assessment must be integrated into the plan. Documented fetal heart rate before and after any procedure is essential, and intraoperative fetal monitoring should be arranged when gestational age and logistics permit. Multidisciplinary discussion including maternal–fetal medicine, anesthesia, neonatology, and surgery is important to plan perioperative fetal monitoring, anesthesia, tocolytic/uterotonic strategies, and administration of corticosteroids for fetal lung maturity if preterm delivery is possible.

Although computed tomography and other ionizing radiation studies are generally avoided during pregnancy, there are scenarios in which CT or angiographic studies are justified after careful maternal–fetal risk–benefit assessment—trauma, life‑threatening hemorrhage, or when CT will change management immediately and alternatives are inadequate. When CT is performed, dose‑minimizing protocols and abdominal shielding should be used. Plain radiographs (for example chest X‑ray) pose very low fetal risk when properly shielded and should not be withheld when indicated.

Putting these elements together yields a practical algorithm. Begin with a focused history and examination, including assessment for red flags (fever, peritonitis, severe pain, hemodynamic instability, urinary symptoms). Proceed immediately to comprehensive transabdominal and transvaginal ultrasound with color Doppler to classify the lesion and attempt to determine uterine versus adnexal origin. If ultrasound is diagnostic and the patient is stable with a benign‑appearing lesion, schedule interval imaging and routine obstetric follow‑up. If ultrasound is indeterminate, if the mass is large, posterior, multiple, or suspicious for neoplasm, obtain a non‑contrast MRI (preferably 1.5 T) to clarify anatomy and tissue characteristics. Use laboratory studies to assess anemia, infection, and other systemic contributors; treat urinary infections and iron deficiency promptly. Reserve gadolinium, CT, or invasive diagnostics for life‑threatening or strongly suspected malignant processes after multidisciplinary discussion. If imaging and clinical status indicate complication (torsion, rupture, infection, or progressive respiratory compromise from mass effect as in the presented case), involve surgical teams early and plan operative management with pre‑ and post‑procedure fetal monitoring and appropriate perioperative support.

Finally, remember the limitations inherent to imaging in pregnancy. A pedunculated, cystic, degenerating leiomyoma can closely mimic an adnexal cyst or decidualized endometrioma on both ultrasound and MRI; conversely, decidualized tissue in an endometrioma can mimic malignant nodules because of increased vascularity. Correlation of imaging findings with clinical features, laboratory trends (for example serial CA‑125), and, when necessary, intraoperative inspection with conservative, pregnancy‑preserving techniques will minimize unnecessary interventions while appropriately addressing complications and malignancy risk. In the case described, systematic use of ultrasound and non‑contrast MRI guided a measured decision to proceed to laparotomy because of symptoms and imaging concerns; intraoperative findings proved the mass to be a pedunculated, torsed, cystic leiomyoma, illustrating both the diagnostic challenges and the value of the stepwise, pregnancy‑safe algorithm.

## Management Strategies During Pregnancy: Expectant Versus Surgical

The evaluation and management of newly discovered abdominal or pelvic masses in pregnancy requires a careful balance between maternal indications for intervention and the obligation to preserve fetal well‑being. The case above — a woman in her early 40s at 21 weeks gestation with two very large cystic uterine masses producing pain, dyspnoea and a presumed episode of torsion with secondary inflammation — illustrates the central principles that guide clinicians when deciding whether to observe or operate.

When to observe versus when to intervene
The default strategy for most masses discovered in pregnancy is expectant management. Small, asymptomatic lesions without suspicious imaging features (regular margins, unilocular/simple cystic appearance, no rapid growth, no solid enhancing nodules on MRI) can be safely followed with serial ultrasound and clinical review. Indications for surgical intervention are clinical and practical rather than purely radiologic: torsion of a pedunculated lesion, rupture, haemorrhage, infection, intractable pain not controlled with conservative measures, progressive respiratory compromise or other mass effects, obstructive anatomy (for example when a myoma would obstruct the birth canal), rapid growth with genuine oncologic concern, or diagnostic uncertainty that would change management. Emergency surgery is warranted for life‑ or pregnancy‑threatening complications; semi‑elective surgery is reasonable when symptoms are progressive and the interval risk of an acute event (for example torsion or necrosis) is high.

Timing of non‑obstetric surgery in pregnancy
When surgery is indicated, timing matters. The second trimester (commonly approximated as 14–22 or up to 24 weeks) is generally the safest window because the risks of miscarriage are lower than in the first trimester and the risk of preterm labour is lower than in the third trimester. In the case presented, the operation at 21 + 6 weeks placed the intervention squarely in this optimal window and reduced the likelihood of both early pregnancy loss and late preterm delivery. Urgent maternal indications, however, override timing considerations — maternal stabilization and urgent intervention should not be delayed when necessary.

Choosing the surgical approach
Selection of laparoscopic versus open (laparotomy) approaches depends on lesion size, number, location, gestational age and surgical expertise. Laparoscopy can be performed safely in pregnancy in selected patients and offers reduced postoperative pain and faster recovery. Best candidates are those with smaller, pedunculated or superficially located lesions in early to mid‑pregnancy, with experienced teams comfortable adapting trocar placement and insufflation pressures. In pregnant patients, an open (Hasson) entry is often preferred to minimise the risk of uterine injury. Insufflation pressures are generally kept as low as possible (commonly ≤12 mm Hg), and trocar sites are adjusted cephalad as the uterus enlarges.

Very large masses, multiple lesions, intramural or posteriorly situated myomas adhered to the uterine wall, or complex anatomy (as in this patient, with two huge masses and one appearing continuous with the posterior uterine wall) often mandate laparotomy to provide exposure and rapid haemorrhage control. A midline vertical incision from xiphoid to pubis provides the best access for very large lesions or when conversion to more extensive surgery is a concern; it was appropriate in the index case to permit safe excision of the twisted, highly vascular pedunculated myoma.

Intraoperative principles
Regardless of approach, several intraoperative principles apply. Minimise uterine manipulation; gentle handling reduces uterine irritability and the risk of contractions. Prioritise meticulous haemostasis — the pregnant uterus is highly vascular and bleeding can be brisk. For fibroids, pedunculated subserosal myomectomy is the least risky: removing a torsed pedunculated myoma is often indicated and can be accomplished with ligation of the stalk and minimal myometrial disruption. Intramural myomectomy during pregnancy is more hazardous: it carries higher risks of hemorrhage, uterine contractility, pregnancy loss and potential weakening of the uterine wall. Therefore, reserve intramural myomectomy for situations where the maternal benefit clearly outweighs obstetric risk; otherwise, leave the lesion in situ and plan definitive treatment postpartum. Sampling of any peritoneal fluid for culture and cytology is reasonable when tumour cannot be excluded or when there is intra‑abdominal contamination or suspected infection; this was done appropriately in the case described.

Perioperative fetal and maternal monitoring
Fetal monitoring should be planned according to gestational age and institutional resources. Documentation of fetal heart activity before and after the procedure is mandatory. Continuous intraoperative fetal monitoring is technically feasible and advised when fetal viability has been reached (institutional definitions often ≥24 weeks), although intermittent auscultation or ultrasound may be the practical choice when continuous monitoring is not possible — as was done with intermittent transvaginal ultrasound in this case before, during and after the operation. An experienced multidisciplinary team (surgery, obstetrics, anaesthesia, neonatology) should be present or immediately available when operating at or beyond viability.

Anaesthetic considerations and uterine relaxants/progestogens
Modern anaesthetic agents are considered generally safe in pregnancy when used judiciously; the priorities are to avoid maternal hypoxia, hypotension, and acidosis and to maintain uterine perfusion. Agents commonly used for induction and maintenance (propofol, opioids, volatile agents) are acceptable under standard guidance. Routine use of tocolytics as prophylaxis to prevent preterm labour is not universally recommended and should be individualized; evidence does not support routine tocolysis for all pregnant patients undergoing non‑obstetric surgery. Similarly, the prophylactic use of progesterone for prevention of miscarriage after non‑obstetric surgery has limited evidence and is not standard; however, clinicians sometimes use it empirically in high‑risk contexts — as in the present case — and this should be part of an informed discussion with the patient.

Perioperative medical management
Preoperative preparation should include baseline laboratories (CBC, type & screen/crossmatch if risk of bleeding), correction of iron‑deficiency anaemia where feasible, and treatment of active infections. In the index patient, iron deficiency and anaemia were identified and addressed postoperatively with intravenous iron and transfusion as needed. Antibiotic prophylaxis should follow usual surgical protocols, and therapeutic antibiotics should be guided by cultures (for example, the Klebsiella urinary infection in this patient determined postoperative antibiotic choices). Venous thromboembolism risk is increased in pregnancy and in postoperative states; perform a VTE risk assessment and use appropriate prophylaxis (mechanical and/or pharmacologic LMWH) when indicated. Postoperative monitoring should include assessment for uterine activity, pain control with pregnancy‑compatible analgesics, and vigilance for bleeding or infection.

Decision making, consent and multidisciplinary care
Shared decision‑making is central. The patient must be counselled about maternal indications, the likelihood of symptom relief, potential maternal complications (bleeding, need for transfusion, re‑operation) and fetal risks (miscarriage, preterm labour, fetal distress). Documented informed consent that outlines the rationale for surgery, alternatives (continue observation), and the contingency plans (e.g., need for emergent delivery if fetal compromise occurs) is essential. Management should be undertaken in a multidisciplinary setting with obstetricians, surgeons, anaesthesiologists, radiologists and neonatology, taking into account local expertise.

Postoperative course and planning delivery
After surgery, close obstetric follow‑up is required. Monitor fetal growth and amniotic fluid, reassess residual masses, and plan the mode and timing of delivery based on remaining anatomy, obstetric factors and patient preference. A residual large pelvic myoma that might obstruct the birth canal often leads to planned caesarean delivery; in the case described, the remaining caudal myoma posed a likely obstacle to vaginal birth and an elective caesarean at term was therefore chosen, with excellent maternal and neonatal outcomes.

Summary and practical algorithm
In practice, begin with careful clinical and imaging assessment (ultrasound ± non‑contrast MRI), correct treatable comorbidities (anaemia, infection), and monitor asymptomatic masses. Reserve surgery for clear maternal indications or when diagnostic uncertainty would change management. If surgery is necessary, aim for the second trimester when possible; select laparoscopy for small, accessible lesions in experienced hands and laparotomy for very large, multiple or anatomically complex masses. Intraoperatively, minimise uterine trauma, secure haemostasis and obtain diagnostic samples as indicated. Provide perioperative antibiotics, VTE prophylaxis when indicated, and individualized use of tocolytics or progestogens after discussing limited evidence. Throughout, engage in shared decision‑making and document informed consent, with a multidisciplinary plan for postoperative obstetric care and delivery planning. The management of the patient described followed these principles: the decision to remove the twisted, highly vascular pedunculated myoma while leaving the adherent intramural‑subserosal lesion in situ minimized obstetric risk, addressed an acute maternal problem and preserved a healthy pregnancy.

## Delivery Planning, Prognosis, and Follow-Up

Once a pregnant patient with large uterine fibroids has been stabilized and the immediate indication for surgery resolved, delivery planning becomes the central element of ongoing care. The primary determinants of mode and timing of delivery are the number, size and precise anatomic relationship of fibroids to the cervix and lower uterine segment, together with the obstetric course (fetal growth, fetal presentation, presence of preterm labour, bleeding, or placental complications). A fibroid that physically obstructs the birth canal — for example, a large isthmic or lower-segment lesion, or a bulky posterior fibroid that prevents fetal descent — is a clear indication to favour an elective cesarean delivery. In contrast, pedunculated subserosal myomas that were removed without deep myometrial entry (as in our case for the pedunculated superior mass) do not by themselves mandate a cesarean delivery; if the remainder of the pelvis and lower uterine segment are free of obstructing disease and there are no other obstetric indications, attempted vaginal delivery may be reasonable.

Aim to achieve delivery at term when maternal and fetal status allow. Expectant management is appropriate for most women; however, earlier delivery should be arranged if obstetric complications develop (progressive pain, infection, rapid enlargement of lesions, red degeneration, preterm labour, or suspected placental compromise). In patients who have undergone non‑obstetric abdominal surgery during pregnancy, document fetal heart rate before, during and after any subsequent operative interventions and keep obstetric monitoring available during labour. In our patient, the residual large posterior myoma was judged likely to impede second-stage descent; therefore, after careful multidisciplinary discussion, an elective cesarean at 37 weeks through the prior midline scar was performed, balancing the risks of earlier delivery against the anticipated obstructed labour.

Because substantial myometrial dissection and bleeding are the principal intraoperative concerns in deliveries complicated by fibroids, preparation for haemorrhage is essential. If a cesarean delivery is planned, the surgical team should anticipate and prepare for increased blood loss: ensure blood is typed and cross‑matched, arrange ready access to packed red blood cells and cell‑saver capability where available, and have uterotonic agents, uterine compression sutures, internal iliac or uterine artery ligation equipment, and interventional radiology back‑up (postpartum uterine artery embolization) considered in advance. Reusing an existing midline incision, as was done here, can shorten operative time and reduce additional fascial trauma; the type and location of uterine incision should be individualized to avoid the fibroid(s) when possible and to optimize uterine closure.

Anticipate intrapartum challenges beyond haemorrhage. Large fibroids increase the risk of malpresentation, obstructed labour, and need for operative delivery. They are associated with higher rates of placental abruption and preterm birth, and they may cause abnormal labour mechanics even if not directly obstructive. Continuous intrapartum fetal monitoring is prudent when fibroids are large or numerous. Pedunculated fibroids are at risk for torsion and degeneration during pregnancy and labour; if torsion or infection arise intrapartum, prompt surgical management is indicated.

Postpartum behaviour of fibroids and timing of interval interventions should inform follow-up and counselling. The vast majority of leiomyomas show partial regression after delivery because of uterine involution and the abrupt fall in circulating progesterone and oestrogen; studies report substantial reductions in size by six weeks postpartum, with mean decreases around 40–50% in some series. Lactation may be associated with further regression. Because of this expected involution, routine immediate postpartum myomectomy is generally avoided unless complications mandate it. In our case the residual posterior myoma decreased modestly to about 7.5 cm and the patient remained asymptomatic while breastfeeding; conservative management was therefore appropriate.

Reassessment should include clinical examination and targeted imaging. A transvaginal and/or transabdominal ultrasound performed at approximately 6–12 weeks postpartum is a reasonable first step to document residual size, location and relationship to the endometrial cavity. If the lesion has complex features, or if surgical planning is contemplated, non‑contrast pelvic MRI provides detailed mapping of intramural versus submucosal components and their relation to the uterine cavity and adnexa. MRI should be reserved for cases where management will change based on additional information.

Decisions about interval surgical therapy (myomectomy or other interventions) depend on symptoms, desire for further fertility, residual fibroid size and location, and the technical feasibility of a minimally invasive approach. For women who desire future pregnancy, myomectomy is the primary fertility‑preserving option; the approach (hysteroscopic, laparoscopic, or open abdominal) is chosen according to location and size. Uterine artery embolization should generally be avoided in women seeking pregnancy because of concerns about subsequent fertility and placental function. If an interval myomectomy is performed with significant myometrial entry and full‑thickness repair, counselling regarding the potential need for cesarean delivery in subsequent pregnancies and an appropriate interpregnancy interval is important. Although specific recommendations vary, many clinicians advise allowing several months for uterine scar healing (commonly 3–6 months after minimally invasive myomectomy and often 6 months or longer after extensive open repair); if a classical uterine incision or very deep myometrial reconstruction occurred, waiting up to 6–12 months and planning delivery by cesarean may be prudent. These timelines should be individualized and discussed in the context of the patient’s fertility goals.

Counselling should also address recurrence risk and contraception. Women with fibroids should be counselled that new lesions can arise and that remaining fibroids may enlarge in future pregnancies. For contraception, levonorgestrel intrauterine devices are effective at reducing bleeding and are contraceptive choices for many women who do not desire near‑term pregnancy; combined hormonal methods are generally avoided in the immediate postpartum period if the patient is breastfeeding, but progestin‑only methods (oral, injectable, or implant) are compatible with lactation. If future pregnancy is planned relatively soon and residual fibroids are asymptomatic, expectant management is reasonable; if symptomatic or obstructive fibroids remain, interval myomectomy prior to conception improves obstetric options.

Finally, evidence on maternal and neonatal outcomes in pregnancies complicated by fibroids shows that with individualized, multidisciplinary care outcomes are generally excellent. The most important elements are appropriate surveillance, preparedness for obstetric haemorrhage, and timely decision‑making about mode and timing of delivery. Clinical pearls from this case that have general application: large fibroids can mimic adnexal masses — search for bridging vessels and continuity with the uterus on ultrasound and confirm with non‑contrast MRI when the diagnosis is unclear; avoid gadolinium during pregnancy; document fetal heart activity before, during and after non‑obstetric surgery; intervene surgically in pregnancy only for clear maternal or fetal indications (torsion, uncontrolled pain, infection, severe mass effect), and when you do operate limit resection to what is necessary to preserve the pregnancy and maternal safety. With these principles, both maternal and neonatal outcomes can be optimized, and interval management can be tailored to the patient’s symptoms and reproductive plans.
