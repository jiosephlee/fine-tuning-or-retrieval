Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery

Chapter 2: ## Pathophysiology: From Classic HIT to Autoimmune HIT

Heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) is fundamentally an immune‑mediated, prothrombotic disorder that arises when antibodies of the IgG class recognise complexes of platelet factor 4 (PF4) bound to polyanions such as unfractionated heparin (UFH). PF4 is a small, highly cationic tetramer released from platelet alpha granules that avidly binds to anionic molecules on bacterial surfaces, endothelial glycocalyx, and therapeutic polyanions such as heparin. When PF4 and heparin associate they form multimolecular complexes that expose neoepitopes not present on PF4 alone. B cells recognising these neoepitopes, with T‑cell help, differentiate and class‑switch to make anti‑PF4/polyanion IgG. Pathogenic IgG antibodies form immune complexes with PF4–heparin on platelet surfaces; the Fc portion of these IgG molecules engages and clusters platelet FcγRIIa receptors, triggering platelet activation, release of procoagulant microparticles, and further PF4 release. The net effect is a powerful amplification loop in which antibody‑mediated platelet activation generates thrombin and fibrin — producing thrombosis even in the face of thrombocytopenia.

The classical laboratory and mechanistic hallmark of heparin‑dependent HIT is that platelet activation occurs at low to therapeutic heparin concentrations (for example 0.1–0.3 U/mL in functional assays such as the serotonin‑release assay, SRA) and is suppressed at very high heparin concentrations (e.g., 100 U/mL). High heparin levels disrupt the lattice of PF4–heparin multimers and thereby prevent formation of the immune complexes required for FcγRIIa clustering; this property is exploited diagnostically. Clinically, classic HIT typically appears within 5–10 days after heparin exposure in patients without recent heparin exposure, although an anamnestic, rapid‑onset HIT can occur within hours in patients previously sensitised in the preceding few weeks.

Autoimmune HIT (aHIT) denotes a distinct and clinically important subgroup in which the antibodies display qualitatively different serologic and functional behaviour: they can activate platelets even in the absence of added heparin. Mechanistically, aHIT antibodies either recognise PF4 alone, PF4 in complexes with endogenous polyanions that decorate platelet, monocyte, or endothelial surfaces, or induce PF4 to adopt conformations that mimic the heparin‑bound state. These antibodies are often higher affinity and/or recognise broader or more exposed neoepitopes on PF4, permitting immune complex formation on cell surfaces without exogenous heparin. In functional assays this manifests as strong platelet activation at 0 U/mL heparin. Clinically, aHIT presents with more severe and protracted thrombocytopenia, frequent and sometimes atypical thrombotic events (including arterial thromboses such as stroke), delayed‑onset or persisting HIT after heparin cessation, and in extreme cases a consumptive coagulopathy with falling fibrinogen, rising D‑dimer, and schistocytes due to widespread thrombin generation and microvascular fibrin formation.

Cellular amplification beyond platelets explains much of the hypercoagulability seen in aHIT. PF4–antibody immune complexes deposit on monocytes and endothelial cells, inducing tissue factor expression and release of proinflammatory cytokines. Complement activation can occur via classical pathway engagement of C1q by IgG within immune complexes, further opsonising cell surfaces and recruiting neutrophils. Neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation, platelet–neutrophil aggregates, and endothelial perturbation all contribute to a milieu that amplifies coagulation. The downstream effect is excessive thrombin generation that consumes fibrinogen and platelets and can produce microangiopathic haemolysis (schistocytes) — a consumptive picture that can be misread as disseminated intravascular coagulation or thrombotic microangiopathy unless HIT is considered.

Laboratory correlates reflect this biology. Strong positive PF4/polyanion ELISA readings (high optical density) correlate with likelihood of clinically relevant, platelet‑activating antibodies, and functional assays such as the SRA help define dependence on heparin. In aHIT, the SRA typically shows marked serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin and preserved inhibition at very high heparin concentrations (confirming PF4 involvement). One caveat illustrated by the present case is that some highly potent aHIT antibodies may show only partial inhibition by FcγRIIa‑blocking agents in vitro (for example monoclonal IV.3). Partial resistance to FcγRIIa blockade does not exclude HIT: it most likely reflects extremely avid immune complexes that either overwhelm the blocking reagent, interact with FcγRIIa in a way not fully prevented by a particular blocker, or recruit additional receptors/co‑factors (complement receptors, other Fc receptors) contributing to activation. Thus, incomplete IV.3 inhibition should prompt consideration of aHIT rather than premature exclusion of the diagnosis.

Temporal dynamics of the antibody response are also important. In many patients with aHIT the initial dominant serologic phenotype is heparin‑independent activation; over weeks to months, as antibody titres fall and affinity maturation changes, the reactivity often evolves toward a more classic heparin‑dependent pattern or eventually becomes undetectable. This evolution explains clinical scenarios in which early, fulminant disease with heparin‑independent SRA positivity later gives way to more conventional SRA profiles. It also underlies the clinical observation that recent preoperative heparin exposure (for example, during angiography) can prime an anamnestic response, causing unusually early postoperative HIT manifestations.

Understanding these mechanistic distinctions has practical implications. aHIT is highly prothrombotic and often requires prompt non‑heparin anticoagulation and consideration of adjunctive therapies aimed at interrupting Fc receptor‑mediated activation — high‑dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) is commonly used because it can saturate Fc receptors and neutralise pathogenic antibodies, but exceptionally potent aHIT antibodies may respond incompletely, as in the reported case. Alternative options in refractory or life‑threatening presentations include plasmapheresis to remove pathogenic IgG and targeted strategies to blunt downstream coagulation (carefully chosen non‑heparin anticoagulants). Finally, awareness that a negative or “indeterminate” functional assay does not rule out aHIT — and that serologic evolution over time occurs — is essential for interpreting serial tests and guiding ongoing management.

In summary, classic HIT results from heparin‑dependent PF4 immune complexes that activate platelets via FcγRIIa clustering, with high heparin concentrations being inhibitory. Autoimmune HIT represents a spectrum of more aggressive anti‑PF4 responses in which antibodies can activate platelets and other blood and vascular cells without heparin, provoke marked thrombin generation and consumptive coagulopathy, and in some cases partially resist FcγRIIa blockade in vitro. Recognition of these mechanistic nuances is critical to diagnosis, risk stratification, interpretation of laboratory testing, and selection of appropriate therapeutic interventions.
