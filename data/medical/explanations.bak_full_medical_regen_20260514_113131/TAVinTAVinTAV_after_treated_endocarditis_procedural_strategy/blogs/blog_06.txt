Title: Blog about the case report: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

## When the Access Bites Back: Preventing and Managing Vascular Complications in Redo TAVR

What grabs your attention in this case is the contrast: a patient with two prior TAVIs and a significant access complication after the second (a pulsatile femoral haematoma that evolved into a pseudoaneurysm requiring open vascular repair) went on to have a technically complex third TAVR (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV) with an uneventful vascular course. That drift from “access disaster” to “clean access” is not luck — it’s planning, technique, and bailout preparedness. For the growing number of patients needing redo TAVR, the access strategy can make or break the procedure.

Why redo TAVR access is different
Redo TAVR patients bring a higher baseline risk for vascular complications: previous large-bore access sites with scar tissue, altered iliofemoral anatomy from prior punctures or devices, higher likelihood of peripheral vascular disease, and comorbidities (this patient had diabetes, prior PCI, COPD and prior GI bleeding). The second TAVR in this patient was complicated by a femoral pseudoaneurysm requiring open repair — a reminder that even a previously “used” access can harbor hidden hazards. When planning the third TAVR, the team deliberately used CT-guided planning, left radial for ancillary catheters and optimized femoral strategy to avoid repeating history.

Preprocedural checklist: CT, access history, and multidisciplinary planning
Before you bring the patient to the lab, do the homework.

- CT iliofemoral assessment is mandatory for redo cases. Don’t just report minimal luminal diameter — review calcification pattern (eccentric, circumferential), tortuosity, previous stent/closure device remnants and the true minimal lumen along the intended track. In this case, MSCT was used to map valve anatomy and guided the procedural plan; apply the same rigor to iliofemoral mapping.
- Review prior access sites and complications. Prior pseudoaneurysm, surgical repair or patching changes your approach — consider contralateral, alternative access, or surgical cut-down if anatomy or scar makes percutaneous closure unreliable.
- Include vascular surgery or endovascular specialists in the heart team for high-risk redo access planning. Have them scrubbed or on standby if recent access complications or severe disease exist.

Puncture technique: ultrasound and microsite planning
The old “feel-based” technique doesn’t cut it for redo TAVR.

- Ultrasound-guided puncture is standard. Visualize the common femoral artery (CFA), identify bifurcation, avoid puncturing near previous scars or above a heavily calcified segment. Mark the optimal skin entry point to minimize sheath kink and facilitate device/closure alignment.
- Aim for a mid-CFA puncture (between the inferior epigastric takeoff and the bifurcation). If prior access is scarred or repaired, shift puncture cranially or caudally as needed, but only after reassessing vessel quality on CT and ultrasound.
- Consider micropuncture first and upsizing under wire protection. This reduces the risk of creating a large hematoma from an initial missed arterial stick.

Sheath selection and sheath management
Minimize vessel trauma and preserve options for bailout.

- Use the smallest feasible large-bore sheath compatible with your valve and delivery system. Consider sheathless techniques where appropriate, and plan for sheath orientation to align with closure devices.
- If previous puncture site is close to your intended entry, choose the side with the better vessel on CT and ultrasound even if it means changing operator ergonomics.
- Maintain meticulous hemostasis during upsizing. If resistance or bleeding is noted during dilation, pause and reassess rather than force passage.

Closure strategy: choose wisely and prepare backups
Closure is where prior complications often recur. Have a plan A and plan B.

- Prophylactic percutaneous suture-based closure (e.g., Perclose ProGlide) in a pre-close fashion is common for planned large-bore access. In redo settings, assess whether tissue quality and scar will allow reliable suture capture. If doubtful, have surgical exposure or hybrid options ready.
- Consider combination strategies: pre-close plus adjunctive collagen plug (if compatible), or planned deployment of vascular covered stents for bailout.
- If the CFA is heavily calcified at the puncture site, suture-based devices may not seal; plan for immediate manual compression, prolonged compression devices, or surgical repair.
- In this case the team used percutaneous femoral access with CT guidance and had a clean outcome — likely from careful site selection and closure planning.

Leverage radial access: ancillary catheters and safety lines
Adding radial access can reduce femoral manipulations and provide quick diagnostic/aortic control options.

- Use radial or contralateral femoral access for pigtail catheters, contralateral safety wires, or angiography. In this report the team used left radial and left femoral arterial access — radial for angiography and femoral for the large-bore system — reducing cross-fire and simplifying bailout angiography.
- Radial access gives immediate alternative arterial access for angiography if femoral problems are suspected intra-procedurally.

Intra-procedural vigilance and early recognition of bleeding
Early recognition of hematoma or bleeding changes outcomes.

- Routinely check the groin under sterile drapes after sheath insertion and during manipulation. If swelling is detected, remove drapes to inspect — small expanding hematomas progress fast.
- Monitor hemodynamics closely; unexplained hypotension or tachycardia in the setting of recent large-bore access should trigger rapid femoral evaluation.
- Use completion angiography through the sheath to confirm no perforation or active extravasation before sheath removal in high-risk cases.

Immediate bailouts for hematoma/pseudoaneurysm
Have tools and expertise ready.

- Manual compression and prolonged pressure: still effective for many small hematomas/pseudoaneurysms detected early.
- Ultrasound-guided thrombin injection: first-line percutaneous therapy for femoral pseudoaneurysms with narrow necks and accessible anatomy. Know contraindications (infection, wide-neck lesions communicating freely with the artery).
- Covered stent deployment: excellent for active extravasation or short neck pseudoaneurysms not amenable to thrombin. Needs appropriate device sizing and wire access across the lesion.
- Surgical repair: required when percutaneous options fail or when pseudoaneurysm anatomy is not amenable (infected pseudoaneurysm, open wound, or complex vessel disruption). This patient had open vascular surgery after the prior complication — a reminder that early surgical backup matters.
- Anticoagulation/antiplatelet management: tailor periprocedural anticoagulation to bleeding risk and closure strategy. The case notes recurrent GI bleeding — integrate that history into your plan and discuss with the team.

Post-procedural surveillance and delayed complications
Don’t pack up and go home too fast.

- Inspect groins before discharge and educate nursing staff on signs of expanding hematoma, limb ischemia, or groin swelling.
- Consider routine 24–48 hour duplex in high-risk redo patients or when there was a difficult closure.
- Early outpatient follow-up within a week can catch evolving pseudoaneurysms before they require surgery.

Takeaways — keep bleeding and limb ischemia off your complication list
- Do CT iliofemoral sizing for every redo TAVR and integrate calcification, tortuosity and prior access history into access selection.  
- Use ultrasound-guided micropuncture entry and plan the puncture site to avoid scar, bifurcation and heavy calcification.  
- Pre-close with suture-based devices when appropriate, but have an alternative plan (covered stent, thrombin, surgical repair) if tissue quality is poor.  
- Leverage radial access for ancillary catheters and angiography to reduce femoral manipulation and provide quick diagnostic capability.  
- Maintain low threshold for completing completion angiography and early duplex if anything feels off; early intervention beats delayed surgery.  

This patient’s story illustrates that prior access complications don’t condemn future procedures — they demand better planning. Invest time in CT review, choose your puncture wisely, close with a backup, and have the vascular toolbox ready. Do that and your redo TAVR will be defined by the valve result, not the groin.
