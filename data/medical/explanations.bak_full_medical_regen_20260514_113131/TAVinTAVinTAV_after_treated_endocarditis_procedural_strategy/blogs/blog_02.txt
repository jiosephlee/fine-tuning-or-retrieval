Title: Blog about the case report: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

# Reading the Neo‑Skirt: CT Planning to Dodge Coronary Obstruction in Triple TAVR

What makes this case interesting? A man in his late 70s with two prior transcatheter aortic valves (Lotus → Evolut) developed Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic valve endocarditis, was treated successfully, then presented with severe prosthetic stenosis/regurgitation and underwent a third transcatheter valve (Acurate neo2) — a true TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV. The team used MSCT‑guided planning to intentionally implant high and “pin” the prior leaflets, preserve coronary flow and access without prophylactic coronary protection, and achieved a durable 1‑year clinical and echocardiographic result. Triple‑stented anatomy plus a history of PVE is exactly the scenario that forces you to stop relying on single‑measure heuristics and start thinking in 3D: where will the neo‑skirt sit relative to the coronaries, what happens when frames deform each other, and when is coronary protection mandatory?

This post focuses on the concrete CT planning pearls and procedural logic used in layered‑frame TAVR — what to measure, what to anticipate, and the common misreads that can put coronary flow (and future access) at risk.

Why CT matters more than ever in reinterventions
- Every additional valve stacks metal and tissue into a new 3D construct. The “neo‑skirt” (the effective obstruction created by prior leaflets and skirt material when they’re pinned or folded) is a function of prior valve geometry, leaflet mobility, and how a new valve will sit. Plain echo or fluoroscopy can’t reliably predict the spatial relationship between that neo‑skirt and the coronary ostia.
- MSCT lets you model valve‑to‑coronary distances, sinus dimensions, and the landing zone available for a high or low implant strategy. In this case CT showed a minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm through the Evolut frame and adequate valve‑to‑coronary clearance to attempt a high implant with a supra‑annular device (Acurate neo2).

CT pearls for layered frames — what to measure (and why)
- Coronary ostial height (relative to the native annulus plane): still essential, but interpret in the context of the stacked frames. A “safe” ostial height with a single valve may be inadequate once prior leaflets are pushed upward by a new device.
- Sinus of Valsalva diameters and height: small sinuses increase the risk that pinned leaflets/neo‑skirt will occlude coronaries. Measure maximal sinus width at the level of the sinotubular junction and mid‑sinus.
- Valve‑to‑coronary distance (VTC): measure from the anticipated final leaflet/neo‑skirt plane (not necessarily the annulus) to the coronary ostia. In TAV‑in‑TAV scenarios this means modelling where the pinned prior leaflets will sit after the new valve is deployed.
- Internal lumen and residual orifice diameter through existing frames: gives you the landing zone sizing and whether the new frame can expand enough to provide acceptable EOA or whether you’ll get severe PPM. In the case, a 13.7 mm minimum lumen implied a restrictive environment that dictated choice of a supra‑annular, low‑frame height valve.
- Frame interaction and commissural alignment potential: map the commissural posts of prior valves relative to coronaries and to fluoroscopic landmarks so you can attempt commissural alignment with the new valve to preserve future access.
- Consider dynamic modelling: if available, 3D reconstruction or valve‑in‑valve planning software (or manual 3D CT reconstructions) can simulate where the neo‑skirt will be after the new valve pins the old leaflets.

How to anticipate the neo‑skirt
- The neo‑skirt is formed by prior leaflets and the skirt material when they’re pushed into a new position by the new prosthesis. Think of it as a curtain that can drape over a coronary ostium.
- High implantation that pins old leaflets at or above their leaflet tips can prevent inward folding of the leaflets into the flow lumen — this helps preserve EOA and avoids creating a skirt that extends below the coronaries.
- Conversely, a low implant can trap leaflet tissue below the coronary ostium and create a neo‑skirt that projects into the sinuses and occludes a coronary.
- Balloon post‑dilatation can radially expand prior frames (partial mechanical expansion) and change the neo‑skirt shape — that can be beneficial (improving EOA) but also unpredictable for coronary clearance if not modelled.

When you can reasonably forgo coronary protection
- After careful CT planning demonstrating sufficient VTC from the final leaflet plane to each coronary ostium, preserved sinus dimensions, and a deploy strategy that minimizes neo‑skirt projection, you may proceed without routine coronary protection.
- In the reported case, team review of CT modelling showed that high implantation of a low‑frame, supra‑annular device would place the neoleaflet line below the coronary ostia, preserving flow and access — so they proceeded without wire‑protection or chimney stents.
- Key caveat: for marginal VTC or small sinuses, or when CT modelling is uncertain because of heavy calcification, eccentric frames, or poor image quality, have coronary protection planned (wires, undeployed stent) — it’s a small step that can avert catastrophe.

Technical strategy points to reduce coronary risk and preserve access
- Choose a valve with design features that match your constraints: in narrow layered anatomy, a supra‑annular valve with relatively low outer frame height can deliver better effective orifice area and easier commissural alignment.
- Aim for commissural alignment of the new valve relative to prior frames and coronaries. Use CT landmarks and known fluoroscopic orientation markers to orient the valve during deployment.
- Consider high implantation to pin prior leaflets at the leaflet tips (not deep) — this helps avoid formation of a skirt below the coronary ostia and reduces PPM caused by leaflet infolding.
- Pre‑dilate or post‑dilate judiciously: post‑dilation can help expand prior frames and improve gradients but can change the neo‑skirt geometry; have coronary wires or bailout plans ready if VTC is borderline.
- Plan for future coronary access: even when immediate obstruction is avoided, multiple frames can impede catheter passage later. Strive for commissural alignment and avoid leaflet/skirt positioning that will block sinus windows.

Common CT misreads and pitfalls in layered‑frame (TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV) planning
- Measuring VTC to the annulus rather than to the anticipated final leaflet plane — this underestimates obstruction risk.
- Ignoring prior leaflet length and where those leaflets will be pinned relative to the coronaries.
- Overreliance on ostial height alone — small sinuses or bulky skirts can obstruct despite reasonable ostial height.
- Failing to appreciate that self‑expanding frames (eg, Evolut) can be further expanded by post‑dilatation, changing the final geometry.
- Skipping commissural mapping — postoperative reaccess for PCI can be impossible if commissures are misaligned across multiple frames.

Case‑driven takeaways
This patient shows that with meticulous CT planning and a deliberate implant strategy you can:
- Perform a third TAVR after treated PVE if infection is resolved and anatomy is favourable.
- Use high implantation to pin prior leaflets and both preserve coronary flow and improve EOA compared with leaving leaflet tissue to fold inward.
- Forego coronary protection when CT modelling and sinus anatomy convincingly demonstrate adequate VTC and clearance — but always have a bailout plan.

Key take‑home messages
- Measure valve‑to‑coronary distance to the anticipated final neo‑skirt/leaflet plane, not merely to the annulus.
- Assess sinus dimensions and prior leaflet length; small sinuses + long leaflets = high risk of neo‑skirt coronary obstruction.
- Plan commissural alignment and choose a valve whose frame/leaflet design helps preserve EOA and coronary access in layered anatomy.
- High implantation to pin prior leaflets can prevent inward leaflet folding, reduce PPM, and preserve coronaries when CT supports the strategy.
- If VTC or sinus anatomy is borderline, proceed with coronary protection ready — it’s a cheap insurance policy against an otherwise life‑threatening complication.

This case doesn’t make the impossible routine, but it does show what disciplined CT planning and strategy‑driven deployment can achieve in even the most complex layered TAVR anatomies.
