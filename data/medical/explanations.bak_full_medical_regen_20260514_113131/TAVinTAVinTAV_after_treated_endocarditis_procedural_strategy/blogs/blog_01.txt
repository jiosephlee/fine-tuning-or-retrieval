Title: Blog about the case report: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

# Third Time’s a Charm: TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV after Treated Endocarditis—When Is It Reasonable?

What makes this case interesting? A patient in his late 70s with two prior transcatheter aortic valves, a documented episode of Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) treated with a full antibiotic course, and then severe prosthetic degeneration—who ultimately received a third TAVI (Acurate neo2) into an Evolut-in-Lotus stack. One year later he’s asymptomatic with stable gradients. That sequence (TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV) after treated PVE is rare, technically challenging and forces you to balance infection control, structural feasibility and prohibitive surgical risk.

If you care for patients with failing bioprostheses and a history of PVE, this case is a great launchpad to discuss a practical decision framework: what “cured” endocarditis looks like, when prior PVE should or shouldn’t block a transcatheter redo, timing after antibiotics, and the imaging/technical considerations that make a successful redo more likely.

## The big-picture rule: PVE ≠ automatic veto of TAVI, but cure must be demonstrable
- The 2025 ESC guidance reiterates that surgical replacement is generally preferred for active PVE (especially with heart failure, uncontrolled infection or complications). But guidelines also emphasise individualised decision-making: in patients with prohibitive surgical risk, and where infection is controlled, a transcatheter approach can be considered.
- Key principle: you must be confident infection is eradicated before implanting another prosthesis. If not, you risk seeding a new device or missing occult complications (abscess, fistula).

## What “infection control / cured” really looks like in practice
No single test makes the call. Treat the whole clinical picture:

Required (minimum):
- Completed a full, targeted antibiotic course appropriate for the organism (this patient completed 6 weeks of IV ampicillin-based therapy).
- Repeated blood cultures negative off antibiotics (or at least not persistently positive). Absence of persistent bacteremia is critical.
- Imaging without evidence of active infection: transoesophageal echo (TEE) with no vegetations, no new paravalvular leak suspicious for abscess/fistula, and MSCT showing no periannular collections or dehiscence.

Helpful adjuncts (when available):
- 18F-FDG PET/CT can help detect residual metabolic activity in prosthetic material—useful if TEE/MSCT are equivocal.
- Serial inflammatory markers and clinical stability (no fever, improving heart failure symptoms).

In the case: the team had negative repeat cultures, TEE and MSCT without active findings, no suppressive antibiotics, and clinical stability—meeting pragmatic criteria for considering redo TAVI.

## Timing: how long after antibiotics is “safe”?
There’s no fixed interval, but practical considerations:
- Wait until you have documented microbiologic and imaging evidence of resolution. Many teams wait several weeks to months after completing therapy to ensure clearance and to reassess valve function.
- Avoid a hasty implant while still on suppressive antibiotics or with unresolved bacteremia.
- Be cautious in early reintervention if the initial infection was complicated (abscess, persistent bacteremia, difficult organism).

In this case, the patient had recurrent symptoms and valve dysfunction shortly after the 2022 TAV-in-TAV and developed PVE that same year; after completing therapy and confirming resolution, reintervention proceeded in 2024—so there was a clinically reasonable interval with objective negative testing.

## When prior PVE should push you away from TAVI
- Ongoing bacteremia, positive cultures, persistent fevers, or imaging evidence of abscess/fistula — these favour surgery if feasible.
- Organisms known for biofilm/persistent infection (eg, some staphylococci, fungi) may require more caution.
- Situations where structural infection has destroyed the annulus/root such that a transcatheter frame cannot provide a durable solution.

If the patient is truly prohibitive risk for surgery and surgical options are unrealistic (as in this case—EuroSCORE II 27% and hostile aortic root after two integrated stent frames), a carefully staged transcatheter strategy may still be the best risk-balanced choice.

## Practical pre-procedure checklist before considering redo TAVI after treated PVE
1. Multidisciplinary heart team discussion (imaging, ID, cardiac surgery, interventional cardiology, anesthesiology).
2. Confirm microbiologic cure: no persistent bacteremia; documented completion of therapy; consider ID input on need for suppressive therapy (ideally none).
3. TEE to exclude vegetations, PVL, abscesses, root disruption.
4. High-resolution MSCT for:
   - Assessing valve-to-coronary distances and sinus anatomy.
   - Measuring residual lumen inside prior frames (important for valve sizing and PPM risk).
   - Planning implantation depth, commissural alignment and need for coronary protection/BASILICA planning.
5. Consider PET-CT if TEE/MSCT equivocal.
6. Shared decision-making with the patient/family about durability limits and worst-case surgical bailout scenarios.
7. Prepare for coronary protection or bailout strategies in lab (wires, stents, possible BASILICA tools), even if not used.

## Technical pearls from this case (and generalizable tips)
- Use CT-guided modelling for valve selection and implantation depth. In triple-stent anatomies, high implantation to pin prior leaflets against the frame can preserve an effective orifice and avoid creating a neo-skirt that blocks coronaries.
- Valve choice matters: supra-annular valves with favorable hemodynamics and lower frame heights (and the ability to achieve commissural alignment) can be preferable in narrow residual lumens.
- Post-dilatation can partially expand prior self-expanding frames (as likely happened here) and improve gradients; plan balloon sizes carefully to avoid annular injury.
- Always evaluate coronary access risk pre-procedure. Commissural alignment attempts and anticipation of mesh burden are essential—lay out a plan for coronary protection even if CT suggests a low risk.
- Remember prosthesis–patient mismatch risk will be high with layered frames. Set realistic hemodynamic expectations and counsel the patient about symptom vs survival goals.

## Pitfalls and red flags to watch for
- Smoldering infection: low-grade fevers, elevated CRP/ESR without clear source — consider PET-CT and repeat cultures before reintervention.
- Positive cultures after stopping antibiotics or need for indefinite suppressive therapy—these generally argue against implanting prosthetic material.
- Underestimating coronary obstruction risk in multi-frame anatomy; angiography during balloon predilatation can be revealing (this team confirmed coronaries patent during pre-dilation).
- Overconfidence in CT alone—combine TEE, MSCT and microbiology for a robust assessment.

## Shared decision-making and follow-up
- Document the multidisciplinary rationale, alternative options (surgery vs conservative), and the risks of reinfection and limited future options if the new valve fails.
- Plan close post-procedure surveillance: early serial echocardiography, clinical monitoring for infection recurrence, and low threshold for imaging if symptoms recur.

## Take‑home messages
- Prior treated PVE is not an absolute contraindication to redo TAVI, but it raises the bar: you must document microbiologic cure and obtain concordant negative imaging (TEE/MSCT ± PET-CT).
- Multidisciplinary assessment and CT-guided procedural planning are essential—especially when dealing with layered frames and coronary access concerns.
- Timing matters: wait for documented infection resolution; avoid implanting while bacteremia or active inflammation is suspected.
- Expect high risk of prosthesis–patient mismatch, need for careful valve selection, commissural alignment and readiness for coronary protection.
- Shared decision-making, clear documentation and close follow-up are mandatory—because options after a failed third transcatheter valve are extremely limited.

This case shows it can be done: a carefully selected, CT-planned TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV after treated Enterococcus PVE produced a durable 1-year result. But success hinges on rigorous infection control assessment, meticulous imaging-based planning, and honest risk–benefit conversations with patients and families.
