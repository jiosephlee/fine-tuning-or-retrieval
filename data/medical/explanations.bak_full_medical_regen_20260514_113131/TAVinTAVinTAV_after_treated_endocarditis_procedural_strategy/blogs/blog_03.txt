Title: Blog about the case report: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

# The Gradient That Vanished: Making Sense of Early Severe PPM after Redo TAVR

This case grabbed my attention because of the jaw‑dropping immediate post‑implant numbers: mean gradient 67 mm Hg and a Doppler velocity index (DVI) of 0.24 right after a third TAVR (Acurate neo2) implant into two prior transcatheter frames — and then a drop to mean gradient ~27 mm Hg by 1 month with durable function at 1 year. That’s not a trick of the echo machine — it’s a pattern we need to recognise so we don’t reflexively reintervene.

Why it matters: as TAVI gets used earlier and patients live longer, multilayered valve anatomy (TAV‑in‑TAV, and sometimes TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV) will be seen more often. Immediate haemodynamics after implant can be misleading. Understanding the physiology and mechanics that convert an alarming early gradient into an acceptable one over days–weeks helps avoid unnecessary procedures and guides optimal intra‑procedural strategy.

What likely happened in this case
- Initial under‑expansion / leaflet restriction: The previously degenerated Lotus→Evolut inner anatomy meant the new valve had to sit within a narrow lumen (MSCT minimum of 13.7 mm). Even a well‑deployed self‑expander can be partially constrained by rigid prior frames and folded leaflets immediately after implantation, producing very high Doppler gradients.
- Delayed radial expansion / self‑expander remodeling: Self‑expanding frames (and even pre‑expanded frames after post‑dilation) can continue to expand and conform over hours to weeks as tissue and stent stresses redistribute. This can increase effective orifice area progressively.
- Effect of post‑dilation: The team performed post‑dilation (20/40 mm) after implantation. High‑pressure ballooning can (a) improve valve frame expansion, (b) push previously immobile leaflets aside, and (c) partially expand an underlying self‑expander (the Evolut frame) — all of which reduce gradient over time.
- Left ventricular (LV) afterload regression and reverse remodelling: Relief of severe obstruction and improvement in LV function after valve therapy reduce transvalvular flow dynamics and gradients over weeks. The patient’s EF improved and chamber volumes decreased — lowering the driving pressures contributing to Doppler gradients.
- Measurement nuance: Immediate post‑implant gradients can be confounded by tachycardia, low stroke volume states, or pressure recovery phenomena. DVI and indexed AVA are less preload/flow dependent and should be incorporated into early assessment.

How to think about immediate high gradients after valve implant
- Don’t panic at a single post‑implant Doppler gradient unless the patient is unstable or there’s clear evidence of obstruction (e.g., haemodynamic collapse, severe LV dysfunction not explained otherwise). Immediate high gradients are not always permanent.
- Combine metrics: use DVI and indexed AVA (AVA indexed to BSA) along with mean gradient. DVI <0.25 and indexed AVA <0.6 cm²/m² suggest severe prosthesis‑patient mismatch (PPM), but context matters early after implant.
- Correlate with valve leaflets on fluoroscopy/TEE, aortography, and with preprocedural CT anatomy. Look for frame underexpansion, leaflet immobility, or malposition that would mandate immediate correction.
- If residual gradient is due to underexpansion and anatomy allows, consider prompt balloon post‑dilation during the same procedure rather than immediate re‑TAVR. But in layered anatomies, caution is needed to avoid annular injury or coronary compromise.

Practical procedural implications (especially for redo and layered TAVR)
- Preprocedural CT planning is essential: quantify lumen inside prior frame, valve‑to‑coronary distances, and predict neo‑skirt. Plan valve choice and implantation depth to maximise effective orifice area.
- Prefer supra‑annular platforms when you anticipate a small inner lumen. Supra‑annular leaflets often give better effective orifice area in constrained anatomies.
- Implantation depth matters: a high implant that seats the new valve at the level of the inner valve leaflets can fix prior leaflets outward and minimise neo‑skirt below the coronaries — this preserves EOA and coronary access.
- Commissural alignment: attempt to align to preserve coronary access and future PCI options; in layered frames this becomes harder but remains important.
- Be ready to post‑dilate: plan the balloon size and have bail‑out equipment ready. Post‑dilation can both expand the new frame and exert radial force on previous frames to improve EOA.
- Measure and monitor: document DVI, absolute and indexed AVA, gradients, and LV function immediately and at short interval follow‑up (days–weeks). Trends matter.

When to consider reintervention vs observation
- Immediate reintervention (repeat valve‑in‑valve or surgical explant) is rarely required based solely on a single high immediate gradient if the patient is haemodynamically stable and imaging suggests underexpansion or reversible causes.
- Indications for urgent reintervention include haemodynamic instability, cardiogenic shock, severe LV failure attributable to obstruction, or clear structural problem (malposition, significant paravalvular leak with haemodynamic consequence).
- If stable, observe and reassess with TTE at 24–72 hours and at 1 month — many gradients will improve substantially as in this case.

Clinical pearls
- An immediate post‑implant mean gradient of 60–70 mm Hg in a layered TAVR patient is alarming but can fall into the 20s within weeks — mechanisms: underexpansion, delayed self‑expander remodeling, effects of post‑dilation, and LV afterload regression.
- Use DVI and indexed AVA alongside mean gradient to judge true stenosis severity early after implant.
- In redo TAVR, choose supra‑annular devices and aim for high implant + commissural alignment to maximise effective orifice area and future coronary access.
- Avoid knee‑jerk reintervention: stabilise the patient, review imaging, consider immediate post‑dilation if feasible, and reassess gradients over time before committing to another invasive procedure.

Take‑home messages
- Immediate high gradients after TAVR in multilayered anatomy can be transient — understand the mechanics before reintervening.
- Combine haemodynamic measures (mean gradient), flow‑independent indices (DVI), and indexed AVA for decision making.
- Preprocedural CT, valve choice, implant depth, commissural alignment, and planned post‑dilation are your tools to prevent severe PPM in redo settings.
- When in doubt and the patient is stable, reassess over days–weeks — the gradient that looks catastrophic in the cath lab can be the gradient that vanishes.
