## Clinical Presentation, Differential, and Initial Evaluation of Failed Transcatheter Aortic Valves After Treated PVE

Patients with failing transcatheter aortic valves most commonly present with a gradual recrudescence of the symptoms that prompted their index procedure: exertional dyspnoea, reduced exercise tolerance, chest discomfort or presyncope/dizziness. Heart‑failure symptoms (orthopnoea, peripheral oedema, exertional limitation) and signs of low cardiac output may predominate when stenosis is severe or regurgitation is marked. In the index case, the patient’s progressive exertional dyspnoea, dizziness and fatigue were the clinical triggers for reinvestigation — typical for structural valve deterioration (SVD) or haemodynamically important prosthetic dysfunction. A succinct but thorough review of comorbidities (coronary artery disease and prior PCI, COPD, diabetes, conduction disease with pacemaker, bleeding history) and of the precise valve history (device types, sizes, implantation dates, and any prior reinterventions or complications such as vascular injury or PVE) is essential because these factors alter both the differential diagnosis and the feasibility/safety of reintervention.

The differential diagnosis for late symptomatic dysfunction after TAVI is broad but can be pragmatically grouped into a few mechanistic categories that guide early evaluation and therapy. Structural valve deterioration refers to progressive leaflet fibrosis, calcification and tearing leading to stenosis, regurgitation, or both. Leaflet thrombosis — presenting as reduced leaflet motion often associated with increased gradients — must be considered because it is potentially reversible with anticoagulation. Underexpansion or pannus formation (fibrous tissue ingrowth) around the stent can cause fixed obstruction. Paravalvular leak from incomplete sealing or late dehiscence causes regurgitation and heart failure. Patient–prosthesis mismatch (PPM) occurs when the effective orifice area (EOA) is too small for the patient’s body size and can present as persistent or progressive gradients. Finally, recurrent or ongoing prosthetic valve endocarditis must always be considered in anyone with a prior episode of PVE; infection can directly damage leaflets causing regurgitation, lead to leaflet immobilization, or produce paravalvular complications (abscess, pseudoaneurysm) that mimic or worsen prosthetic dysfunction.

Because the management of these etiologies diverges sharply, early attempts should be made to distinguish active infection from non‑infective causes. Active PVE is suggested clinically by fever, chills, elevated inflammatory markers (CRP, ESR), leukocytosis and, most importantly, positive blood cultures. In the presented case the patient had documented Enterococcus faecalis bacteremia and appropriate 6‑week intravenous therapy; the decision to pursue further intervention was contingent on demonstration of infection control. Practical steps when PVE is in the differential include obtaining multiple sets of blood cultures (ideally three sets from separate venipuncture sites, spaced over time) before starting or changing antibiotics, and measuring inflammatory markers serially. Persistence of bacteremia, increasing inflammatory markers, new conduction abnormalities, or evidence of systemic embolization should raise concern for uncontrolled PVE and prompt urgent multidisciplinary review.

Echocardiography is the first‑line imaging modality for both haemodynamic quantification and infection assessment. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) provides mean transvalvular gradient, Doppler velocity index (DVI), and continuity‑equation–derived aortic valve area (AVA); it is useful for serial comparisons. For prosthetic valves, transoesophageal echocardiography (TEE) is more sensitive for vegetations, leaflet morphology and paravalvular complications such as abscess or fistula. Key haemodynamic thresholds are the same concepts applied to native valves but must be interpreted in the prosthetic context: an AVA ≤1.0 cm² and elevated mean gradients signal severe stenosis; a DVI ≤0.25 is a practical cut‑off suggesting severe prosthetic stenosis/PPM. Because prosthetic valves have smaller orifice areas by design, indexing AVA to body surface area is important: an indexed AVA ≤0.6–0.65 cm²/m² is commonly used to define severe PPM, though different sources use slightly different thresholds and clinical context matters. In our patient the calculated AVA was 0.5 cm² with a mean gradient of 49 mm Hg before the third procedure — unequivocal evidence of severe prosthetic obstruction and clinically significant dysfunction.

Multislice CT (MSCT) provides complementary, often decisive information in the failing TAVI. CT is superior for visualising valve frame geometry, degrees of stent underexpansion, leaflet calcification or thickening, and for assessing valve‑to‑coronary distances and sinus dimensions that determine the risk of coronary obstruction with repeat valve implantation. For suspected leaflet thrombosis, contrast‑enhanced 4D CT can demonstrate hypoattenuated leaflet thickening (HALT) and reduced leaflet motion; these findings have therapeutic implications because anticoagulation frequently restores motion and lowers gradients. In this case MSCT revealed a minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm within the Evolut frame and was central to selecting valve type, size and implant depth for the third procedure while assessing coronary risk.

When TTE/TEE and CT do not resolve the question of active infection — for example, when echocardiography is equivocal or there are persistent inflammatory markers despite negative blood cultures — FDG‑PET/CT can add diagnostic value by localising prosthetic valve inflammation and extracardiac septic foci. PET/CT has particular utility in prosthetic valve endocarditis because acoustic shadowing and prosthetic material can limit echocardiographic sensitivity; however, interpretation requires awareness of false positives (early post‑implant inflammation) and the influence of recent antibiotic therapy. In the reported patient, serial clinical assessment, repeat blood cultures, TEE and MSCT collectively supported eradication of infection and absence of paravalvular complications, enabling the heart team to plan reintervention.

Initial haemodynamic assessment should always consider loading conditions and left ventricular function. Low‑flow states (low stroke volume) can depress transvalvular velocities and mask stenosis; conversely, high gradients immediately after valve implantation sometimes reflect underexpansion or PPM and can partially improve as the new valve fully expands or as left ventricular function and stroke volume recover. The case illustrates this dynamic: an immediate post‑implant mean gradient of 67 mm Hg fell to 27 mm Hg at one month, consistent with mechanical improvement after post‑dilation and interval hemodynamic adaptation. This underlines the importance of correlating symptoms, serial echocardiography, and careful timing when judging procedural success or need for further therapy.

Risk stratification for reintervention in the context of prior PVE integrates surgical risk scores (EuroSCORE II, STS), the technical feasibility of surgical explantation given frame integration and calcification, comorbid conditions that increase perioperative morbidity (advanced COPD, prior bleeding, frailty), coronary anatomy and the likelihood of preserving future coronary access. When active infection is present or complications such as abscess, fistula, or prosthetic dehiscence exist, surgery is generally preferred when the patient is a reasonable surgical candidate; current guidelines prioritise eradication of infection and correction of structural complications by surgical means when feasible. However, when infection has been treated, reintervention strategy should be individualised by a multidisciplinary heart team. For patients with prohibitive surgical risk, and when CT imaging confirms acceptable anatomy (adequate valve‑to‑coronary distance, feasible landing zone and acceptable residual EOA), a carefully planned transcatheter reintervention (TAV‑in‑TAV or alternative) may be appropriate, as in this patient.

In practice, the initial evaluation of a symptomatic patient with a prior TAVI and history of PVE follows a staged approach: rapid clinical assessment with vitals and symptom review; obtain multiple blood cultures and baseline inflammatory markers; perform TTE to quantify haemodynamics; proceed to TEE if PVE is suspected or TTE is inadequate; obtain MSCT to characterise valve anatomy, frame interactions and coronary relationships; reserve PET/CT for equivocal cases or to search for occult infection. Management decisions should hinge on whether active infection is present (antibiotics ± surgery) versus a noninfective mechanical problem (anticoagulation for thrombosis vs reintervention for SVD/underexpansion/PPM), and they should always be made in a multidisciplinary heart team setting with input from imaging specialists, cardiac surgery, infectious disease, and interventional cardiology.

This case encapsulates these principles: the patient presented with symptoms suggestive of prosthetic dysfunction, had a clear episode of Enterococcus faecalis PVE that was treated with an appropriate antibiotic course, and underwent stepwise reassessment (blood cultures, TEE, MSCT) to exclude ongoing infection before CT‑guided planning of a third transcatheter implantation. The careful differentiation between resolved PVE and SVD, and the use of multimodality imaging to assess anatomy and haemodynamics, were pivotal to a successful, individualized treatment strategy.

## Pathophysiology: SVD, Sequelae of Endocarditis, and PPM Across Serial TAVI

Structural valve deterioration (SVD) after bioprosthetic valve implantation is a progressive, multifactorial process that ultimately determines the need for repeat intervention. In surgically implanted and transcatheter aortic valves (TAVs) the final common pathways—leaflet thickening, fibrosis, calcification, tearing and stiffening—are similar, but the mechanical environment imposed by the stent frame, the initial lesion treated and any intervening complications (such as infective endocarditis) shape how SVD manifests. Understanding these mechanisms is essential when planning and troubleshooting valve-in-valve (VIV) and serial TAV-in-TAV procedures, because each additional frame changes leaflet motion, the effective orifice geometry and the forces acting on both new and old leaflets.

Mechanisms of SVD in transcatheter prostheses
- Leaflet degeneration: native bioprosthetic leaflets tend to undergo a spectrum of changes—fibrosis, collagen cross-linking, calcific deposition and eventual leaflet retraction or tearing. These changes reduce leaflet mobility and coaptation, leading to stenosis, regurgitation or a mixed lesion.
- Mechanical constraint and stent underexpansion: the stent frame defines the working orifice. Underexpansion of the implanted frame—whether from anatomic factors (small annulus, heavy calcification), interaction with surgical sewing rings or previous transcatheter frames—produces a smaller true lumen and higher transvalvular gradients. Self-expanding and balloon-expandable frames behave differently, but all are subject to radial constraint when placed inside a previously implanted rigid structure.
- Leaflet pinwheeling and inward folding after VIV: in a valve-in-valve scenario the prior valve’s leaflets are displaced by the new valve. Rather than being everted outward, they can fold or "pinwheel" inward toward the central flow stream, effectively creating an additional obstruction inside the new frame. This is particularly important when the initial prosthesis has bulky leaflets or when the new valve is seated deep relative to the leaflet sewing ring.
- Cumulative frame constraint in serial implants: with each additional transcatheter valve a layered anatomy develops—metal frame within metal frame, multiple skirts and stacked leaflets. The cumulative mesh reduces radial compliance of the entire construct, increases the density of material along the flow axis and can progressively narrow the functional lumen. This is not only a geometric problem but a dynamic one: the superimposed frames change the leaflet opening angles, coaptation heights and central orifice shape under physiologic flow.

How prior endocarditis accelerates and alters SVD
Infective endocarditis causes active destruction of valve tissue—vegetations, perforations and leaflet loss are the acute consequences. After successful antimicrobial therapy, the infection may be clinically cured but leave a substrate for accelerated SVD:
- Residual scarring and leaflet retraction: inflammatory injury heals with fibrosis, producing stiff, shortened leaflets that do not open or close normally. Even without persistent infection, these changes predispose to earlier stenosis or regurgitation.
- Altered leaflet geometry and pannus formation: healed infection frequently results in irregular leaflet contours and fibrotic pannus at commissures or on the ventricular side, further reducing valve opening.
- Integration with stent frame and fixation: when endocarditis involves a transcatheter prosthesis, the inflammatory process may promote adhesions between leaflet tissue and the stent frame or aortic wall. This mechanical fixation increases the likelihood of incomplete leaflet opening and can complicate surgical explantation if it becomes necessary.
Clinically, a history of treated prosthetic valve endocarditis should raise the pre-test probability that a failing valve has a structural component related to prior infection, even if cultures are negative and imaging shows no active vegetations. In the case presented, Enterococcus faecalis endocarditis likely produced leaflet injury that contributed to the rapid recurrence of prosthetic dysfunction despite successful antibiotic therapy.

Prosthesis-patient mismatch (PPM) in VIV and serial TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV
PPM occurs when the effective orifice area (EOA) of a prosthetic valve is too small relative to the patient’s body size, resulting in higher transvalvular gradients and incomplete symptomatic or remodelling benefit. PPM is common after VIV and is amplified with each additional valve:
- Geometric causes: stacking of prior leaflets and the new valve’s sewing skirt reduces the luminal cross-sectional area; the previously implanted frame can prevent full expansion of the new valve. Small original prostheses or those with intra-annular leaflet position are at particular risk.
- Functional indicators: Doppler and echocardiographic indices that suggest PPM include elevated mean gradients (generally >40 mm Hg suggest severe obstruction in a prosthesis, though thresholds vary with device and flow), a small indexed EOA (indexed AVA ≤0.65 cm^2/m^2 is commonly used to define severe PPM), and a low Doppler velocity index (DVI). A DVI <0.35 suggests prosthetic stenosis; in severe cases it can be <0.25 (the presented patient had a DVI of 0.24 immediately post-implant, consistent with severe PPM).
- Consequences: PPM reduces the symptomatic benefit of valve replacement, limits regression of left ventricular hypertrophy, and is associated with worse long-term outcomes. In serial TAVI patients the expected EOA after reintervention should be realistically estimated pre-procedure; often a supra-annular valve or a device with superior haemodynamics is preferred to maximise EOA.

Immediate post-implant high gradients and subsequent evolution
It is common to encounter elevated transvalvular gradients immediately after implanting a valve-in-valve or a serial valve. Multiple interacting reasons explain this early picture and why gradients may fall over hours to months:
- Underexpansion and constrained frames: initial deployment may leave the new frame partially constrained by the rigid, previously implanted stent. Post-dilatation or gradual remodeling of the self-expanding frame under physiologic forces can increase radial expansion over time, improving leaflet excursion and reducing gradients.
- Hemodynamic state: immediate post-procedural cardiac output, heart rate, and left ventricular function influence measured gradients. For example, low flow states or suboptimal loading conditions can elevate gradients initially and then normalize as hemodynamics stabilize.
- Leaflet adaptation and mechanical "settling": newly placed leaflets can become more mobile after several beats to days as they assume their functional conformation and as any trapped debris or edema resolves.
- Role of post-dilatation: deliberate high-pressure balloon post-dilatation can increase the diameter of the new and sometimes even the prior self-expanding frames (partial radial expansion), lowering gradients. In the case presented, aggressive post-dilatation likely contributed to the fall of the mean gradient from 67 mm Hg immediately after implantation to 27 mm Hg at 1 month.
Because of these dynamics, classification of procedural success and the decision to re-intervene immediately should account for the potential for early improvement. However, immediate very high gradients should prompt a search for mechanical explanations (malposition, valve entrapment by prior leaflets, acute thrombosis) before conservative observation.

Balancing coronary protection, future coronary access and maximising EOA in serial TAVI
Each procedural choice—valve type, implantation depth, commissural alignment strategy—carries trade-offs that are magnified in the serial TAVI population:
- Implantation height: high implantation places the new valve more supra-annularly. This can push prior leaflets against the stent frame and reduce the formation of a neo-skirt that might obstruct the coronary ostia, preserving coronary patency and facilitating future coronary access. Conversely, too high a position can leave insufficient contact with the annulus or increase the risk of valve embolisation; a lower implant can maximise effective orifice area but place more material in front of the coronaries.
- Commissural alignment: deliberately orienting the new valve so that its commissures align with native or prior prosthesis commissures reduces the chance that commissural posts will obstruct coronary ostia and makes future coronary cannulation easier. However, the ideal orientation for coronary access may not be the same as the orientation that maximises valve leaflet opening or minimises leaflet-to-frame interaction.
- Choice of valve design: supra-annular valves typically confer better haemodynamics in small annuli or constrained VIV scenarios because their leaflets sit above the stent frame and can generate a larger EOA. In the presented case, an Acurate neo2 (a supra-annular design) was chosen to optimise EOA in a triple-stented anatomy while keeping frame height and mesh burden acceptable.
- Coronary protection strategies: when preprocedural imaging shows a high risk of coronary obstruction, adjunctive techniques (wiring and balloon/stent standby, or leaflet laceration techniques such as BASILICA in selected anatomies) may be required. In serial TAV scenarios the cumulative risk must be calculated from the relationship of the anticipated neo-skirt(s) to coronary ostia, the sinus of Valsalva size and the valve-to-coronary distance on CT.

Clinical implications and lifetime planning
Serial TAVI cases illustrate that the first valve choice and implantation technique can influence the ease and haemodynamics of all subsequent procedures. Teaching points that flow directly from pathophysiology are:
- Anticipate the future: for younger or lower-risk patients, choose valves and implant techniques with future coronary access and reintervention in mind (consider commissural alignment and devices with favourable haemodynamics).
- Use CT-based modelling: precise assessment of valve-to-coronary distance, sinus dimensions and the expected interaction of frames is essential when planning reintervention after VIV or in serial TAVI. This imaging guides both the procedural risk assessment and the selection of valve type and implantation depth.
- Expect and differentiate early high gradients: identify mechanical causes amenable to correction (malposition, underexpansion amenable to post-dilation) versus those likely to persist (small initial annulus and stacked leaflets producing true PPM).
- Recognise the long-term risk of prior endocarditis: even after cure, scarring and leaflet retraction can precipitate earlier SVD. Documented prior PVE should prompt careful scrutiny for residual structural damage that may limit the benefit of further transcatheter therapy.

In the case described, these pathophysiologic principles explain the progressive deterioration after the initial Lotus valve, the abrupt restenosis following infective endocarditis, and both the severe immediate postoperative gradients and their subsequent improvement after implantation of a supra-annular Acurate neo2 with targeted post-dilatation. The procedural choices—high implantation, commissural alignment and selection of a valve with favorable supra-annular leaflet position—were dictated by anatomic constraints, the need to preserve coronary access and the imperative to maximise effective orifice area within a triple-stented construct.

## Multimodality Imaging and Preprocedural Planning for TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV

When a patient presents with a failed transcatheter valve and one or more prior transcatheter frames in situ, careful preprocedural planning using multimodality imaging becomes the central determinant of safety and success. The case above—three prior transcatheter devices (Lotus → Evolut → Acurate neo2) with a history of treated prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE)—illustrates how planning is driven by a sequence of specific anatomic questions that can be answered only by targeted CT and echocardiographic assessment, supplemented by procedural testing when indicated. In complex triple‑stented anatomy the planning goals are to (1) determine whether a further transcatheter implant is anatomically feasible, (2) size and select a device that maximises effective orifice area (EOA) while minimising the risk of coronary compromise and severe patient–prosthesis mismatch (PPM), (3) define implantation depth and commissural orientation to preserve coronary access, and (4) decide whether upfront coronary protection or leaflet‑modification (eg, BASILICA) is required.

Core CT measurements and what they mean
Multislice CT (MSCT) is the workhorse for planning TAV-in-TAV procedures and is indispensable in layered‑frame anatomy. The most important CT measurements are:
- The internal or residual lumen diameters of the in‑situ frame(s) at the level of the leaflet tips and at the annular plane: the narrowest internal diameter (often at the plane of the prior valve leaflets) governs the maximal external diameter of a new transcatheter valve and the likely EOA after implantation. In the presented case the minimum lumen diameter in the Evolut frame was 13.7 mm — a critical constraint that determined valve size selection and predicted a high likelihood of residual gradients.
- Aortic annulus dimensions and shape: to confirm that further device expansion will be seated correctly and to detect eccentricity that might predispose to malposition or PVL.
- Sinus of Valsalva dimensions (width and height) and the sinus‑to‑coronary ostium geometry: narrow or shallow sinuses increase the risk that displaced leaflets and frame material will obstruct coronary flow or lead to sinus sequestration.
- Coronary ostial heights from the annular plane and valve‑to‑coronary (VTC) distance: VTC is conceptually the predicted distance between the new valve’s skirt/leaflet edge (after displacement of existing leaflets) and the coronary ostium. A VTC <3–4 mm is widely regarded as high risk for coronary obstruction in valve‑in‑valve scenarios and mandates protective planning.
- Valve‑to‑sinotubular junction (VTS) distance and the height of sinotubular junction relative to the valve: a low sinotubular junction or one that is close to the anticipated neo‑skirt height increases the risk of sinus sequestration (a scenario in which the sinus is functionally excluded from coronary perfusion).
These measurements are obtained from multiplanar reconstructions aligned to the annular plane and to the leaflet/commissural plane. When available, virtual valve implantation software can project the expected location of the new valve frame and leaflet skirt to calculate VTC and predict the neo‑skirt height; if not available, measurements should be made carefully on multiple cross‑sections to approximate these distances.

Echocardiography and infection assessment
Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) remains essential for two purposes before reintervention. First, it documents prosthetic valve haemodynamics, regurgitation mechanism and presence of paravalvular complications. Second, and especially in the setting of prior PVE, TEE is used to exclude active infection: residual vegetations, leaflet destruction, abscesses, dehiscence or prosthetic fistulae are contraindications to elective TAVI until controlled or surgically managed. In the presented case, both TEE and MSCT confirmed absence of vegetations or active paravalvular complications after completion of antibiotic therapy—permitting the team to proceed with a transcatheter strategy. Good practice is also to document sterile blood cultures and clinical stability; timing of reintervention should follow documented infection resolution and multidisciplinary consultation.

Assessing the risk of coronary obstruction and sinus sequestration
The single most feared complication of valve‑in‑valve (and particularly valve‑in‑valve‑in‑valve) procedures is coronary obstruction. The risk is increased by:
- Short VTC distance (<3–4 mm) between the expected neo‑skirt/leaflet plane and the coronary ostium.
- Narrow or shallow sinuses of Valsalva and a low sinotubular junction that limit the space for displaced leaflets.
- Tall native leaflets (or long bioprosthetic leaflets) that when pushed outward create a taller neo‑skirt.
- Multiple overlapping frames that raise the effective height and bulk of the neo‑skirt and further reduce clearance.
CT allows explicit visualization and measurement of these features and, often, virtual simulation of how the new valve will interact with existing leaflets and frames. If the predicted neo‑skirt covers a coronary ostium or the VTC is short, the operator must plan coronary protection (see below) or consider leaflet modification (BASILICA) where anatomically feasible. In layered frames the cumulative frame height and the likelihood of incomplete expansion increase uncertainty, and a conservative approach to coronary protection is warranted when CT margins are borderline.

Commissural alignment and preservation of coronary windows
Commissural alignment of the new valve such that its commissural posts fall between prior posts/leaflets can preserve coronary windows and maintain access for future coronary interventions. Planning commences on CT: identify fluoroscopic markers of the in situ valve commissures, map the coronary ostia relative to the prior commissures and determine an optimal orientation for the new valve. For many contemporary devices there are recommended orientation markers or “alignment techniques” (eg, orientation of the delivery system flush with the C‑marker) that can be rehearsed on table and executed during deployment. In our case, preprocedural CT modelling guided an implantation orientation intended to align the Acurate neo2 commissures with the Evolut leaflet positions to maximise coronary clearance and the available EOA. Commissural alignment becomes even more important when successive frames may otherwise produce an impenetrable mesh overlying the ostia.

Device selection and sizing in restrictive, multi‑frame anatomy
When several stent frames are present, the internal diameter of the most restrictive frame (not the native annulus) should guide valve sizing. Sizing must anticipate that the new valve will be constrained by the preexisting frame and that the effective orifice will be smaller than the external diameter of the implanted THV. Several practical considerations follow:
- Prefer supra‑annular leaflet devices when EOA is paramount. Supra‑annular valves typically give better effective orifice area for a given internal diameter and therefore reduce the severity of PPM risk.
- Prefer devices with lower skirt heights or shorter overall frame height when coronary obstruction or sinus sequestration is of concern—provided the device still offers good supra‑annular EOA.
- Choose valves with delivery systems and frames that support planned commissural orientation.
- In very tight lumens, consider smaller size options but balance the need to avoid severe PPM (indexed EOA thresholds: severe PPM generally considered when indexed EOA ≤0.65 cm^2/m^2) against the risk of coronary obstruction.
In the presented patient, the minimal lumen of 13.7 mm constrained the choice; the team selected an Acurate neo2 (supra‑annular, relatively low frame) and planned high implantation to maximise the residual EOA and reduce the neo‑skirt obstructing the coronaries.

Deciding on coronary protection and leaflet modification
Coronary protection strategies fall broadly into two categories: prophylactic ostial protection and leaflet modification.
- Prophylactic protection: placement of coronary guidewires (with or without undeployed stents) in the at‑risk coronary arteries before valve deployment allows immediate stent deployment (“chimney” stenting) if obstruction occurs. This strategy is relatively straightforward but creates another metallic layer protruding into the aorta and can complicate future interventions.
- Leaflet modification (BASILICA): intentional transcatheter laceration of the at‑risk leaflet(s) to create an open leaflet that will not produce a continuous neo‑skirt is an attractive approach when anatomy allows. However, BASILICA in the setting of multiple prior stent frames is technically more complex and may be limited by lack of catheter space, the presence of metallic frames obstructing laceration, or an inability to position and electrify the guidewire safely. Data on BASILICA in triple‑stented anatomy are scarce.
CT‑based prediction of VTC and neo‑skirt height is the dominant determinant of need for protection. If VTC is comfortably >4 mm and sinuses are roomy, operators may elect to proceed without protection, with a strategy of immediate angiographic assessment during balloon valvuloplasty (the “balloon test”) and ready bailout equipment. In borderline cases (VTC 2–4 mm or other risk features), most teams will perform prepositioned coronary wires and have stents ready to deploy. In the reported case CT predicted sufficient clearance, and the team proceeded without upfront protection but performed balloon predilation under rapid pacing with simultaneous aortography to confirm coronary flow before final valve release—an acceptable strategy when CT margins are reassuring.

Intraoperative testing and procedural tactics
Balloon predilation or a balloon‑occlusion test can provide a real‑time check of coronary patency: simultaneous selective coronary angiography during balloon inflation or aortic root angiography after balloon inflation will reveal dynamic obstruction. Rapid ventricular pacing is used to stabilise the balloon and reduce transvalvular flow. If coronary compromise is observed, an already‑positioned coronary wire and undeployed stent can be deployed immediately. High implantation of the new valve can be planned to keep skirt height below the coronary ostia and to anchor the prosthesis at the level of prior leaflets, as was done in this patient; high implantation can also press the prior leaflets outward and reduce leaflet billowing into the orifice, improving flow. Post‑dilatation with high‑pressure balloons may be required to expand constrained prior frames and reduce gradients, but carries a risk of annular injury and should be balanced against potential benefit. Intentional fracturing of surgical rings (bioprosthetic valve fracture) is possible in surgical valves but is generally not applicable to transcatheter frames.

Access planning and ancillary lines
CT angiography of the iliofemoral arteries is mandatory for access planning. Prior vascular complications (this patient had a femoral pseudoaneurysm and open vascular repair after the second TAVI) affect access choice and mandate careful planning. When femoral access is possible, maintain a contralateral arterial and radial access for angiographic control and coronary monitoring. Radial access for coronary angiography is useful for real‑time evaluation of coronary flow during balloon inflation and valve deployment. Additionally, plan for left ventricular pacing (temporary wire or existing pacemaker) and have peripheral vascular surgical backup if prior repairs or fragility increase risk.

Commissural mapping for long‑term coronary reaccess
Given the patient’s extensive coronary disease, preserving future coronary access was a major objective. Preprocedural CT should be used to map prior commissures and coronaries and to choose a valve and orientation that avoid placing new commissures in front of ostia. Where possible, use devices and deployment techniques that allow predictable alignment of commissural posts (manufacturer guidance and fluoroscopic marks are critical). In layered frames, although perfect alignment may not be obtainable, planned orientation reduces the chance that multiple posts will aggregate over a single coronary ostium.

When prior endocarditis has occurred
A history of PVE adds two additional planning requirements. First, ensure the infection has been definitively treated: negative blood cultures, completed appropriate antibiotic course, and absence of vegetations or prosthetic complications on TEE/CT. Reintervention during unresolved infection increases the risk of recurrent infection and prosthetic seeding. Second, recognize that prior infection may have caused leaflet destruction or fibrosis that changes how leaflets will behave when displaced by a new valve—another reason to use CT and intraoperative balloon testing to predict behaviour.

Advanced planning tools and the heart team
Three‑dimensional printed models and virtual implantation software can be invaluable in extreme cases. These tools allow bench testing of different valve sizes and positions, give visualisation of frame interference and neo‑skirt formation, and can simulate coronary patency. Not all centers have these resources; when available, they should be used for triple‑stented anatomies. Regardless, all decisions—device choice, implantation depth, coronary protection strategy—should be reviewed by a multidisciplinary heart team including interventional cardiology, cardiac imaging, structural heart surgery, and infectious diseases (when prior PVE is present).

A practical stepwise preprocedural checklist
1. Confirm clinical stability and infection control: negative blood cultures, completed therapy per ID, no vegetations/abscess on TEE.
2. MSCT with annular plane alignment and virtual valve projection: measure internal lumen diameters, annulus, sinuses, coronary heights, VTC and VTS.
3. Evaluate PPM risk: use indexed EOA predictions and choose supra‑annular valves where possible.
4. Decide device and size based on smallest internal diameter and desire for supra‑annular leaflet position; plan commissural orientation.
5. Determine need for coronary protection or BASILICA based on VTC (<3–4 mm borderline), sinus size, leaflet length, and layered frame height.
6. Plan vascular access with CT angiography; prepare alternative access and have vascular surgical backup if prior complications exist.
7. Prepare intraoperative strategy: arterial and radial lines, prophylactic coronary wiring if indicated, equipment for chimney stenting, rapid‑pacing wire, and ready‑to‑perform balloon test.
8. Obtain heart team signoff and document shared decision‑making with the patient/family.
9. Post‑procedure: early TTE and clinical surveillance; plan serial follow‑up imaging to detect gradients, PPM and recurrent infection.

Applying these principles to the case
In the patient presented, systematic CT measurement identified a critically small internal lumen of the Evolut frame (13.7 mm) but also demonstrated adequate VTC and sufficiently large sinuses to justify proceeding without upfront coronary protection. The team chose a supra‑annular, alignment‑friendly Acurate neo2 and planned a high implant to fix the prior leaflets against the stent frame, reduce neo‑skirt height and maximise EOA. Balloon predilation with simultaneous angiography served as an intraoperative safety test of coronary patency. Post‑deployment, immediate gradients were high but improved over the first month as the frames partially expanded and optimisation (post‑dilatation) took effect—an expected phenomenon in constrained, layered frames. The thorough preprocedural exclusion of active endocarditis by TEE and CT, and the multidisciplinary planning that followed, were critical prerequisites for proceeding with a third transcatheter implant in this high‑risk patient.

In summary, successful TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV requires meticulous CT‑ and echo‑guided planning that addresses internal lumen sizing, coronary clearance (VTC), sinus anatomy, commissural alignment, device selection and access strategy. Where measurements suggest small margins for error, prophylactic coronary protection or leaflet‑modification strategies should be employed. Multidisciplinary review, CT‑based simulation when available, and careful intraoperative testing are the cornerstones of a safe, reproducible approach to serial transcatheter aortic valve interventions.

## Interventional Strategy: Device Deployment, Gradient Optimization, and Periprocedural Care

Performing a third transcatheter aortic valve implantation inside two existing frames requires disciplined, stepwise execution that integrates pre-procedural CT planning with intra‑procedural decision making. The procedure in this case illustrates core principles that are generally applicable when addressing TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV scenarios: secure and monitored access, crossing and stabilising the left ventricle, anatomic confirmation of coronary safety, controlled deployment to pin prior leaflets outward and minimise neo‑skirt formation, physiologic and imaging surveillance during the procedure, and targeted post‑dilation to optimise gradients while avoiding catastrophic complications.

Access, wire strategy and initial haemodynamic assessment
Begin by establishing large‑bore femoral arterial access for device delivery and an upper extremity arterial access (usually radial) for aortic root angiography and haemodynamic monitoring. The radial route provides a stable catheter position to perform contrast injections that document coronary flow during key steps. Cross the prosthesis with a soft catheter and place a supportive LV guidewire (eg, a Safari or Safari‑2) to stabilise the system for valve delivery and to permit rapid pacing when needed. In this case, a left femoral delivery approach with left radial monitoring access and a Safari wire in the LV provided the intended support and monitoring capability.

Before committing to device deployment, obtain baseline invasive haemodynamics and perform targeted balloon predilation under rapid ventricular pacing. Predilation serves two purposes in a triple‑stented anatomy: (1) it confirms the degree of leaflet immobility and residual orifice area and (2) it is an opportunity to assess coronary patency dynamically. Simultaneous aortography while the balloon is inflated demonstrates whether the coronaries fill freely or are jeopardised by existing frames or by leaflet displacement. In the reported procedure a 22/40 mm predilation balloon under 180 bpm pacing was used to confirm coronary patency and to test the mechanical behaviour of the stacked leaflets.

High implantation and leaflet pinning: rationale and execution
One of the principle strategies to limit patient–prosthesis mismatch (PPM) when implanting another valve inside small or obstructed prior frames is high implantation—positioning the new valve at, or slightly above, the level of the prior leaflet tips so that the new frame pins the immobile or flail leaflets outward rather than letting them fold inward to form a bulky neo‑skirt. High implantation reduces the height of any neo‑skirt below the coronary ostia and tends to preserve the largest possible effective orifice because the working leaflet orifice is carried to the supra‑annular portion of the new device. In triple‑layered constructs this approach also reduces the amount of leaflet tissue protruding into the central lumen where it can create severe obstruction.

Achieving a reproducible high implant relies on precise fluoroscopic landmarks derived from pre‑procedural CT: the relative positions of the prior valve frames, the leaflet tips, and the native annular plane should be mapped and projected into the fluoroscopic working view (often using cusp‑overlap or dedicated orthogonal views). During deployment advance or retract the introducer to align the radiopaque markers on the new valve with the planned landing zone; controlled, gradual release allows final adjustment. In the present case the Acurate neo2 was positioned to align with the Evolut leaflet tips based on CT modelling, thereby pinning the leaflets outward and keeping the neo‑skirt low.

Commissural alignment and preserving coronary access
Commissural alignment is more than theoretical nicety in multi‑stent anatomies—it materially affects future coronary access and the distribution of open cells in front of the coronary ostia. Use device‑specific radiopaque markers and pre‑procedural CT to determine the desired rotational orientation of the replacement valve such that its commissural posts avoid overlapping the coronary ostia or the largest inflow cells of previous frames. For supra‑annular self‑expanding devices that allow rotation in the sheath, the delivery system can be rotated to the planned orientation before and during deployment; for balloon‑expandable devices, rotational cues are set before sheath removal. Aim to align the new commissures with those of the prior valve when possible so that the stacked frames produce predictable windows for future coronary cannulation.

Despite best efforts, stacked frames will still create a mesh through which accessing a coronary may be difficult. When CT predicts marginal valve‑to‑coronary distances, plan a coronary protection strategy (eg, a guidewire and undeployed stent ready in the coronary artery, or readily deployable extracorporeal support). In the described patient, CT showed adequate coronary clearance and a no‑protection approach was chosen; simultaneous angiography during predilation corroborated coronary patency.

Gradient optimisation: post‑dilation and frame interaction
Residual transvalvular gradients after TAV‑in‑TAV are common because the prior stents confine the new valve and limit expansion. The principal means to reduce gradients acutely are accurate positioning (maximising effective orifice area by high, supra‑annular seating), mechanical optimisation of the new device (full expansion through proper deployment technique), and adjunctive post‑dilation to expand both the newly implanted frame and—where possible—the underlying stent(s).

Post‑dilation should be performed with appropriately sized non‑compliant balloons at high pressure, chosen to expand the composite construct to the largest safe diameter without risking annular or aortic root injury. Gradual serial dilations with careful haemodynamic monitoring are preferable to aggressive one‑shot dilations. High‑pressure post‑dilation can impart radial force to a previously underexpanded frame (for example, further expanding an Evolut frame), improving leaflet coaptation and central flow; this effect likely contributed to the haemodynamic improvement observed in the case. Intentional bioprosthetic valve fracture (BVF) is a technique used in some native SAVR redo settings to enlarge the rigid sewing ring and allow greater expansion, but BVF in multi‑layered transcatheter frames is unpredictable, potentially hazardous (annular injury, root rupture, or embolisation) and generally requires explicit planning and device compatibility; it should not be attempted unless specifically indicated and planned.

Intra‑procedural imaging and haemodynamic surveillance
Continuous intra‑procedural surveillance is mandatory. Fluoroscopic control documents positioning and expansion; aortography performed before and after implantation assesses aortic regurgitation and coronary perfusion; and invasive pressure tracings quantify immediate gradient changes across the valve (peak-to-peak and mean gradients). Transoesophageal or intraprocedural transthoracic echocardiography is valuable when paravalvular leak, malposition, or other structural complications are suspected and can confirm leaflet motion.

Pay attention to the possibility that transvalvular gradients may be high immediately post‑implant and improve over hours to weeks as the frames settle and as after‑load and cardiac output change. Still, an unacceptably high residual mean gradient (often quoted >20–25 mm Hg depending on body size and valve label) or a very low Doppler velocity index should prompt consideration of further balloon optimisation during the index procedure.

Periprocedural medical management: infection prevention, antithrombotic strategy, and conduction considerations
Prior prosthetic valve endocarditis raises specific periprocedural management questions. If active infection has been excluded by clinical assessment, serial blood cultures and imaging (as in this case), proceed with the same prophylactic perioperative antibiotics recommended for prosthetic valve procedures—typically a peri‑procedural single dose tailored to local microbiology and prior pathogens; prolonged suppressive therapy is only indicated when eradication is uncertain. Document clear multidisciplinary consensus regarding infection control before reintervention.

Antithrombotic therapy after valve implantation must be individualised. In patients with recent coronary stents or an indication for oral anticoagulation (eg, atrial fibrillation), combination strategies may be necessary. However, in patients with high bleeding risk (recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding in this patient) and without a separate indication for anticoagulation, many centres favour single antiplatelet therapy (SAPT) after TAVI rather than dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT), balancing thrombotic risk against bleeding. Decisions should involve cardiology, haemostasis and the referring team, and be guided by contemporary guideline recommendations.

Conduction disturbances are common after TAVI and are more likely with deeper implants and with valve types that exert pressure on the membranous septum. Pre‑existing pacemakers (as in this patient) simplify immediate management but do not eliminate the need for intra‑procedural pacing preparedness and post‑procedure rhythm monitoring. Program temporary pacing options, interrogate existing devices pre‑ and post‑procedure, and plan for extended telemetry post‑TAVI to detect delayed high‑degree AV block.

Success criteria and anticipated complications
Define procedural success using standardised criteria such as VARC‑3: correct device positioning, absence of procedural mortality, acceptable haemodynamic performance (mean gradient and regurgitation thresholds) and freedom from major complications. In high‑complexity reinterventions one must also accept realistic functional goals—reduction of symptoms and preservation of LV function may be as important as achieving completely normal valve gradients.

Anticipate and plan for complications unique to the setting: coronary obstruction (particularly when valve‑to‑coronary distance is small or when leaflet material is mobile), annular or aortic root injury from overzealous dilation, embolic stroke during instrumentation, vascular access complications, acute kidney injury from contrast, conduction system injury requiring pacing, persistent severe PPM, and leaflet thrombosis in the postoperative period. Have bail‑out strategies available—coronary protection wires and stents, covered stents for annular/root rupture, cerebral embolic protection (institutional choice), and surgical backup—recognising that surgical salvage in a triple‑stented root is challenging.

Follow-up and surveillance
After discharge, early echocardiographic assessment (before hospital discharge and at 30 days) documents immediate haemodynamic behaviour; subsequent follow‑up at 3, 6 and 12 months (including TTE and clinical assessment) confirms stability, LV remodelling, and absence of recurrent infection. In this patient, an initially high immediate post‑procedure mean gradient (67 mm Hg) improved to 27 mm Hg at 1 month and remained stable at ~26 mm Hg at 1 year—an important reminder that acute gradients can improve after optimisation and time. Continued vigilance for leaflet thrombosis, progressive PPM, and the difficulty of future coronary access must guide long‑term management and inform earlier decisions during the lifetime management of structural valve disease.

Summary
A third TAVI in the setting of two prior transcatheter valves is feasible when CT confirms adequate coronary clearance and when the procedural plan emphasises high implantation to pin prior leaflets outward, commissural alignment to preserve coronary windows, and targeted post‑dilation to maximise the effective orifice while avoiding annular injury. Comprehensive intra‑procedural surveillance, antibiotic prophylaxis when indicated, tailored antithrombotic therapy, and careful management of conduction issues are essential to translate technical success into durable clinical benefit.

## Outcomes, Surveillance, and Lifetime Management

Triple transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV) represents a new frontier of structural heart intervention that shifts the emphasis from a single procedure to lifetime management. The immediate haemodynamic picture after a “third” valve is often counterintuitive: transvalvular gradients can be markedly elevated immediately after deployment in a constrained, multi‑frame anatomy, yet may fall substantially over the following days to months as the frames settle, stents partially expand with ballooning and the left ventricle remodels. The patient in this case illustrates that trajectory clearly — an immediate post‑implant mean gradient of 67 mm Hg with an indexed AVA consistent with severe prosthesis‑patient mismatch improved to a mean gradient of 27 mm Hg at 1 month and was sustained (26 mm Hg) at 1 year, accompanied by favourable left ventricular remodelling and symptom relief. These dynamic changes remind clinicians that a single early Doppler measurement may overstate the long‑term haemodynamic penalty of a carefully planned implant, provided valve position and leaflet motion are appropriate and no procedural complication (malposition, severe paravalvular leak, coronary obstruction) has occurred.

Surveillance after a third TAVI should be structured and vigorous, because multiple overlapping frames increase the likelihood of late complications that may be treatable if detected early. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) should be obtained prior to hospital discharge to document baseline post‑procedural valve function and to screen for complications such as paravalvular leak or new regional wall motion abnormalities. Routine follow‑up TTE at 1 month and 3 months helps distinguish immediate post‑implant transience from persistent obstruction; thereafter, annual imaging is reasonable for stable patients, with earlier reassessment prompted by symptoms. Each study should report peak velocity and mean gradient, aortic valve area (using the continuity equation), Doppler velocity index, presence and grade of transvalvular or paravalvular regurgitation, left ventricular ejection fraction and dimensions, and estimated pulmonary artery pressure. Given the high risk of prosthesis‑patient mismatch in layered valves, indexed AVA (AVA/BSA) should be calculated and documented. If gradients rise, leaflet motion is impaired or clinical deterioration occurs, further imaging with transoesophageal echocardiography and gated cardiac CT is indicated — CT provides superior anatomic detail to assess frame geometry, leaflet thickening, neo‑skirt configuration and valve‑to‑coronary relationships, and it is particularly useful to plan any reintervention.

Because this patient’s prosthesis failure followed an episode of Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE), lifelong vigilance for recurrent infection is mandatory. Patients with prosthetic valves should be counselled about basic preventive measures — meticulous oral hygiene, timely treatment of dental sepsis, and awareness that fevers or unexplained systemic symptoms warrant immediate medical assessment. Periprocedural antibiotic prophylaxis for dental procedures is recommended selectively for patients at highest risk of adverse outcomes from bacteremia (for example, prosthetic heart valves) according to contemporary guidance; local practice and infectious disease consultation should guide individualized recommendations. In any febrile episode, a low threshold for obtaining blood cultures and repeat echocardiography is appropriate. Advanced imaging such as 18F‑FDG PET/CT has an established role when endocarditis is suspected but echocardiography is inconclusive, particularly in prosthetic valves; infectious disease involvement remains essential in decisions about prolonged therapy or suppressive antibiotics, which are not routinely indicated after a resolved episode.

Long‑term antithrombotic strategy after multiple valve implants must balance coronary thrombotic risk and the patient’s bleeding propensity. There is no one‑size‑fits‑all regimen. In patients with coexisting coronary artery disease and recent percutaneous coronary intervention, transient dual antiplatelet therapy may be required according to standard indications for stenting, but for most stable TAVI recipients single antiplatelet therapy (aspirin) is reasonable. If atrial fibrillation or valve thrombosis is present, oral anticoagulation is indicated and should supersede antiplatelet therapy unless there is a compelling reason otherwise. This patient’s history of recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding necessitates prioritising the lowest‑effective antithrombotic exposure; decisions should be individualized with cardiology, gastroenterology and, when indicated, haematology input. Additionally, clinicians should remain alert to the possibility of valve thrombosis as a cause of increased gradients; gated CT is the most sensitive test for hypoattenuated leaflet thickening and restricted leaflet motion, and anticoagulation often reverses thrombotic obstruction.

Preservation of coronary access is one of the most consequential lifetime‑management issues after serial valve implantation. Overlapping nitinol and cobalt‑chromium frames create an increasingly dense meshwork that can impede ostial engagement. It is essential to document commissural alignment and fluoroscopic landmarks at the time of the procedure and to store CT datasets that delineate the coronary windows relative to the valve frames. For future percutaneous coronary interventions, a preprocedural CT roadmap combined with fluoroscopic projections can facilitate selective catheterization; techniques such as using high‑backup support catheters, mother‑and‑child systems, or guide‑extension catheters may be required. In anatomies at high risk for coronary obstruction at the time of valve implantation, pre‑emptive strategies (for example, coronary protection with a parked wire and a readily deployable stent, or leaflet modification techniques such as BASILICA) should be considered — though the safety and efficacy of leaflet laceration or coronary protection strategies in triple‑stented anatomies are not well established and must be weighed case by case.

If the newly implanted valve fails over time, therapeutic options become progressively limited and more complex. Surgical explant remains a definitive option, but the presence of multiple, well‑incorporated transcatheter stent frames and adhesions frequently makes explantation equivalent to aortic root replacement with high morbidity and mortality. Percutaneous salvage options include aggressive high‑pressure post‑dilatation and, in certain bioprosthetic configurations, bioprosthetic valve fracture to expand the constrained orifice; however, the ability to fracture or safely expand transcatheter frames is device‑dependent and may be ineffective in some THV designs. Leaflet modification techniques (BASILICA) can prevent coronary obstruction in valve‑in‑valve contexts but their applicability and durability in a triple‑stented anatomy remain uncertain. Repeating a valve‑in‑valve strategy (a fourth valve) is sometimes technically possible but typically compounds prosthesis‑patient mismatch and may offer only transient benefit. Given these limitations, proactive strategies at the time of each intervention that maximise valve area, preserve coronary access (through commissural alignment and appropriate valve choice and depth), and minimise future procedural complexity are paramount.

Finally, lifetime management of patients undergoing complex sequential TAVI must be patient‑centred and documented for future providers. Every transcatheter intervention should be accompanied by a detailed implant summary given to the patient and included in the medical record: prosthesis types, sizes, orientation of commissures (when recorded), implantation depth, post‑dilation pressures, any coronary protection maneuvers, and CT images or reconstructions that define coronary windows. Regular, multidisciplinary heart‑team follow‑up that includes imaging specialists, interventionalists, cardiac surgeons and infectious disease experts is essential to revisit goals of care as the clinical context evolves. In shared decision‑making conversations, clinicians should be explicit about the trade‑offs of transcatheter reintervention versus surgery, the realistic durability of a triple valve construct, the possible need for complex future therapies, and the importance of prompt evaluation for febrile illness. With meticulous planning, close surveillance and clear communication, many patients — like the one presented here — can derive meaningful symptomatic and functional benefit from complex repeat transcatheter strategies, but the work of management extends well beyond the cath lab into careful lifetime stewardship.
