Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

### [Clinical Pearls] What features of the sinus of Valsalva and coronary ostial height increase the risk of coronary obstruction during TAV-in-TAV, and what numeric cutoffs are commonly used to define higher risk?

Think of coronary obstruction in TAV‑in‑TAV as a mechanical problem: previously implanted leaflets and the new valve’s skirt are displaced outward and can form a “neo‑skirt” that sits across the coronary ostia. Two anatomic features govern whether that displaced tissue will be accommodated or will block flow — the height of the ostium above the annulus and the room available in the sinus of Valsalva (and downstream sinotubular junction) to accept the displaced leaflets.

Clinically important high‑risk findings on CT and the practical cutoffs most groups use are these. First, low coronary ostial height from the annulus increases risk — an ostial height <10 mm is generally considered high risk and 10–12 mm is borderline. The lower the ostium, the more likely an outward‑moved leaflet or skirt will sit across it. Second, small sinus of Valsalva dimensions increase risk because the sinuses can’t accommodate the displaced leaflet tissue; a mean sinus diameter <30 mm (often <28–30 mm used) is associated with higher risk. Third, the valve‑to‑coronary (VTC) distance — the predicted gap between the anticipated final transcatheter valve frame/neo‑skirt and the coronary ostium — is a more direct CT‑based predictor for TAV‑in‑TAV: a VTC <4 mm (many use <3–4 mm as high risk) is considered unsafe; 4–6 mm is intermediate and >6 mm low risk. Related measurements — valve‑to‑sinotubular junction (VTSTJ) and sinotubular junction diameter — matter because an already narrow STJ can trap leaflets against the ostia even if the sinus is borderline.

Put another way: the highest risk scenario is low coronary ostia (<10 mm), small SOV/STJ (<28–30 mm), and a predicted VTC <3–4 mm. These are not absolute thresholds — they are used to stratify risk and to plan mitigation (coronary protection wiring, prophylactic BASILICA, surgical referral). Always interpret them in the context of valve type and leaflet height (native or prosthetic), CT virtual valve implantation modeling, and the patient’s clinical risk.
