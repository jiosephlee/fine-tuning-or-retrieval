Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

### [Management] Given a minimal internal lumen diameter of approximately 14 mm within a prior TAVR frame, which transcatheter valve design features (e.g., supra-annular leaflet position, frame height and cell geometry, radial force) and implantation strategies (e.g., high implantation, commissural alignment) help maximise effective orifice area and preserve future coronary access for a third valve?

Think of the problem as two linked goals: (1) restore the largest possible effective orifice area (EOA) inside a very small, double‑stented landing zone (~14 mm) and (2) avoid creating a neo‑skirt or cell overlap that blocks coronary ostia or makes future reaccess impossible. The device choice and how you implant it are inseparable — each decision trades haemodynamics versus coronary access.

Which valve design features help EOA
Choose a valve that places its leaflets above the annulus (supra‑annular leaflets). Supra‑annular valve leaflets function in a larger flow stream and reliably give a larger EOA than intra‑annular leaflets for the same geometric orifice — a critical advantage in a confined 13–14 mm lumen. A valve with a low internal skirt/short sealing cuff that avoids adding a bulky sub‑coronary neo‑skirt also helps preserve cross‑sectional area and coronary clearance. Repositionability/recapturability is valuable so you can optimise final depth and commissural orientation before release.

Which frame characteristics preserve coronary access
A frame with large, widely spaced open cells in the supra‑coronary region preserves the ability to catheterise the coronaries through the frame in the future. A relatively low frame height in the supra‑coronary zone (or at least minimal skirt height) limits the amount of metal and fabric that can cover the sinus and ostia. Clear fluoroscopic commissural markers that allow rotational alignment reduce the chance that coronary ostia will sit behind a narrow cell or behind a leaflet. Thus large-cell geometry + known commissural orientation is preferable for future coronary access.

Radial force — the tradeoff
Higher radial force can be advantageous because it can expand a previously under‑expanded self‑expanding frame (improving leaflet mobility and EOA) and can resist recoil. However, very high permanent radial force may compress or deform previously implanted frames in ways that alter cell geometry and potentially make coronary access harder. Practically, choose a device with sufficient radial force to obtain good expansion (and plan for post‑dilatation/ballooning if needed), but accept that radial force is a tool to be used intentionally (with planned ballooning) rather than an unselected virtue.

Implantation strategies that matter
High implantation: deliberately implant the new valve at or slightly above the level of the prior leaflets so the old leaflets are pinned outward against the frame and do not fold inward to create an even smaller orifice. High implantation also keeps any neo‑skirt material away from the coronary ostia.

Commissural alignment: use preprocedural CT to determine native and prior‑valve commissural orientation and align the new valve’s commissures to avoid placing posts or narrow cells in front of coronaries. This preserves direct catheter access and reduces risk of coronary obstruction.

Pre‑procedural CT and modelling: quantify valve‑to‑coronary distances and simulate how a neo‑skirt will relate to the ostia. This planning determines whether coronary protection or BASILICA is needed.

Coronary protection backup: even if CT looks favourable, have guidewires/wiring or undeployed stents available; perform simultaneous aortography during ballooning or deployment when risk is finite.

Planned post‑dilatation / BV fracture: plan for controlled post‑dilatation to gain additional frame expansion and improve EOA. If prior frames and valve type permit safe bioprosthetic valve fracture (and coronary anatomy allows), consider it as part of planned strategy — but only after CT/heart‑team assessment because fracture can change cell geometry and coronary relations.

Practical synthesis for a 14 mm minimal lumen (what I would pick and do)
Prefer a supra‑annular, low‑skirt, repositionable valve with large open cells in the supra‑coronary portion and adequate radial force (the Acurate neo2 is an example used successfully here). Use pre‑procedural CT planning to define commissural targets, plan a high implant that pins prior leaflets outward, and align commissures fluoroscopically. Be prepared to post‑dilate to optimise expansion; have coronary protection ready and document commissural orientation fluoroscopically for future reference. Accept the trade‑off that some radial expansion may be required to improve EOA, but always balance that against the risk of creating inaccessible coronary ostia.

Broader principle
Every initial TAVI should be done with a “lifetime management” mindset: choose devices and align implants in ways that preserve coronary access and permit future valve expansion because each reintervention progressively narrows options.
