Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

### [Differential Diagnosis] In an older patient with prior transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) who now has dyspnoea, a rising mean transvalvular gradient, and moderate-to-severe intraprosthetic regurgitation, what are the leading causes of transcatheter prosthetic valve dysfunction (e.g., structural valve deterioration, leaflet thrombosis, prosthetic valve endocarditis, malposition/underexpansion, prosthesis–patient mismatch), and which clinical or echocardiographic features help distinguish among them?

Think about five broad mechanisms when a transcatheter aortic prosthesis in an older patient presents with dyspnea, rising mean gradient and new moderate–severe intraprosthetic regurgitation: structural valve deterioration (SVD), leaflet thrombosis, prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE), malposition/under‑expansion (including frame–frame interaction after TAV‑in‑TAV), and prosthesis–patient mismatch (PPM). Distinguishing among them requires integrating timing, clinical context, blood tests/cultures, echocardiography, and CT (and sometimes PET-CT) — this determines therapy (anticoagulation, antibiotics, reintervention).

Structural valve deterioration (degenerative leaflet fibrosis, tearing, calcification)
- When it occurs: typically months–years after implantation but can be accelerated by prior infection or mechanical stress (as in layered frames).
- Clinical clues: progressive dyspnea and gradually rising gradients, often with mixed stenosis and regurgitation.
- Imaging: TTE/TEE shows restricted, thickened or torn leaflets with increased transvalvular velocities, reduced AVA and usually no systemic inflammatory signs. MSCT confirms leaflet thickening/fibrosis and absence of hypo‑attenuated leaflet thickening caused by thrombus; there are no vegetations.
- Response: degeneration does not improve with anticoagulation; definitive treatment is valve reintervention (TAV‑in‑TAV or surgery) guided by anatomy.

Leaflet thrombosis (bioprosthetic valve thrombosis; HALT/RLM)
- When: often subacute, can occur early after implantation, but at any time; more common with low flow states, small valve area/underexpansion, or inadequate anticoagulation.
- Clinical clues: sudden or subacute rise in gradient, sometimes with dyspnea; usually absence of fever or positive blood cultures.
- Imaging: on TTE you see restricted leaflet motion and increased transvalvular gradient; MSCT is most sensitive — hypo‑attenuated leaflet thickening (HALT) and reduced leaflet motion (RLM). DVI and Doppler patterns show stenosis. No vegetations on TEE.
- Response: often reversible with systemic anticoagulation (gradients and leaflet motion improve within days–weeks); thus a therapeutic trial of anticoagulation (if no contraindication) is both diagnostic and therapeutic.

Prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE)
- When: any time after implantation but typically presents with systemic signs; prior bacteremia or prosthetic infection history increases risk (this patient had prior Enterococcus PVE).
- Clinical clues: fever, positive blood cultures, sepsis, new conduction abnormalities, embolic phenomena, or worsening heart failure. Fever and leukocytosis point to infection.
- Imaging: TEE is the cornerstone — mobile vegetations, abscess, fistula, or prosthetic dehiscence/paravalvular leak. MSCT and 18F‑FDG PET‑CT can demonstrate periprosthetic infection when TEE is equivocal. Gradients can rise from leaflet destruction or obstruction from vegetations; regurgitation is common with leaflet damage or paravalvular involvement.
- Response: antibiotics ± surgery; anticoagulation is contraindicated when active infection is present until infection controlled.

Malposition / underexpansion / frame interaction (mechanical obstruction from stent geometry)
- When: immediate post‑implant or late if frame embeds or becomes constrained by prior frames (classic after TAV‑in‑TAV).
- Clinical clues: abrupt gradient increase after a procedure or progressive rise in a patient with multiple nested frames. May coexist with PPM.
- Imaging: fluoroscopy and MSCT show underexpanded frame, asymmetric expansion, small internal lumen, or leaflet impingement by adjacent frame. TTE shows elevated gradients with otherwise normal leaflets if the problem is geometric; CT directly measures true internal diameter.
- Response: balloon post‑dilation, intentional fracture of surgical ring in some surgical valves, or reintervention (another valve) depending on anatomy; anticoagulation is not helpful.

Prosthesis–patient mismatch (PPM)
- When: effectively present immediately after implantation if effective orifice area is too small for body surface area, or becomes clinically relevant with progressive LV dysfunction.
- Clinical clues: persistent high gradient after technically successful implantation, low indexed AVA and low DVI but with mobile leaflets and no evidence of thrombus or infection. Symptoms of heart failure out of proportion to valve appearance.
- Imaging: TTE shows high mean gradient with normal leaflet mobility; indexed AVA ≤0.85 cm²/m² for moderate and ≤0.65 cm²/m² for severe PPM (values modified in valve type). MSCT will show normal frame expansion.
- Response: depends on severity — redo valve (if feasible) or medical therapy; PPM is not improved by anticoagulation or antibiotics.

Putting it together in practice
- Step 1: clinical assessment — fever, sepsis, embolic phenomena, timing relative to procedures, and anticoagulation status. Positive blood cultures and systemic signs point to PVE.
- Step 2: TTE/TEE — assess gradients, leaflet mobility, presence of vegetations, paravalvular leak, and quantify AVA, DVI. Normal leaflets with high gradient → suspect PPM or underexpansion; thickened/restricted leaflets → consider thrombosis or SVD; mobile masses → PVE.
- Step 3: MSCT (gated) — definitive for HALT/RLM (thrombosis) and for measuring frame expansion/true lumen (malposition/underexpansion, frame–frame crowding). PET‑CT is helpful if TEE/MSCT are inconclusive for PVE.
- Step 4: short therapeutic trials when appropriate — anticoagulation if CT shows HALT or if thrombosis is suspected (and infection excluded); antibiotics immediately if PVE is likely based on cultures/TEE. Reassess gradients and leaflet motion after therapy to confirm diagnosis.

Apply these principles to the presented case: the patient had prior PVE that was treated and then developed combined severe stenosis and intraprosthetic regurgitation. Absence of fever, negative cultures and TEE/MSCT without vegetations argue against active PVE. MSCT showed severe luminal narrowing of the Evolut frame (minimum diameter ~13.7 mm) consistent with mechanical underexpansion/frame crowding and progressive structural degeneration; the poor AVA and low DVI before the third procedure suggested a combination of SVD and frame‑related obstruction with a component of PPM. Leaflet thrombosis was less likely given the timeline, absence of HALT on CT, and lack of response to anticoagulation trial (not described). This reasoning justified CT‑guided TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV with attention to commissural alignment and post‑dilation to restore lumen and relieve gradients.

In short: use timing and systemic signs to separate infection; use TEE for vegetations/abscess and MSCT for leaflet morphology and frame geometry; use the pattern of gradients and leaflet mobility plus response to anticoagulation to distinguish thrombosis from SVD or mechanical underexpansion; and remember PPM is a hemodynamic diagnosis when leaflets are otherwise normal.

### [Morning Report] When prosthetic valve endocarditis is suspected after TAVI, what is the recommended diagnostic workup, and how do the modified Duke criteria incorporate blood cultures, transoesophageal echocardiography, and adjunct imaging (cardiac CT and FDG-PET/CT) to establish the diagnosis?

When you suspect prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) after TAVI, treat it as a high‑risk, multidisciplinary problem: obtain microbiology, high‑quality echocardiography and targeted adjunct imaging early, because prosthetic material blunts the sensitivity of any single test and management hinges on demonstrating active infection or its complications.

Start with blood cultures before antibiotics: obtain at least three sets from separate venipunctures spaced over the first 24 hours (more rapidly if the patient is septic). Persistent or reproducible isolation of typical organisms for endocarditis (Staphylococcus aureus, enterococci, HACEK organisms, or streptococci) is a major microbiologic signal and guides urgent therapy.

Simultaneously perform transthoracic echocardiography as an initial screen, but proceed promptly to transoesophageal echocardiography (TEE). TEE is the cornerstone imaging test for prosthetic valves because it is more sensitive for vegetations, leaflet motion abnormalities, dehiscence and paravalvular extension (abscess, pseudoaneurysm). Bear in mind that TEE sensitivity is reduced with stented transcatheter valves and layered valve frames; a negative TEE does not exclude PVE.

Recognize how the modified Duke criteria are applied in the prosthetic valve setting. The major criteria remain: (1) positive blood cultures with typical organisms from multiple sets, and (2) evidence of endocardial involvement on echocardiography (vegetation, abscess, new partial dehiscence) or new prosthetic regurgitation. Because prosthetic valves produce more false negatives on echo, modern practice incorporates adjunct imaging into the diagnostic algorithm: abnormal focal FDG uptake around the prosthesis on FDG‑PET/CT or paravalvular lesions (abscess, pseudoaneurysm, new dehiscence) identified on cardiac CT are now accepted as additional major criteria in many guidelines. Adding FDG‑PET/CT and cardiac CT raises diagnostic sensitivity for PVE and for detecting complications that mandate surgery.

Put this into practice with a pragmatic sequence: obtain blood cultures, start empirical therapy when indicated, perform TEE promptly. If TEE is indeterminate or clinical suspicion remains high despite negative echo, proceed to FDG‑PET/CT and high–resolution multislice cardiac CT. Use FDG‑PET/CT to detect abnormal periprosthetic metabolic activity (keeping in mind false positives from recent implantation or recent surgery/inflammation—interpret cautiously in the first 3 months post‑implant) and use cardiac CT to define anatomy, paravalvular extension, and to plan interventions. Coordinate timing with the infectious diseases and imaging teams; when possible do imaging before prolonged antibiotic exposure because therapy can reduce sensitivity of PET.

In short: diagnosis of PVE after TAVI rests on the modified Duke major criteria (repeat positive blood cultures and echocardiographic evidence of endocardial involvement), but in prosthetic valves you must routinely integrate FDG‑PET/CT and cardiac CT into the workup. That combined approach increases sensitivity, detects surgical indications (abscess, fistula, dehiscence, prosthesis dysfunction), and informs the multidisciplinary decision whether to pursue surgery or, as in this case, a carefully planned transcatheter reintervention after proven eradication of infection.

### [Clinical Pearls] Before planning a TAV-in-TAV (or TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV) procedure, which CT-based measurements stratify the risk of coronary obstruction and sinus sequestration, and what threshold values would prompt coronary protection or leaflet modification strategies (e.g., valve-to-coronary distance, coronary ostial height, sinus of Valsalva dimensions, sinotubular junction height/virtual valve-to-STJ distance, and virtual transcatheter valve-to-coronary distance)?

When you plan a TAV‑in‑TAV (or multiple repeat TAV) you must move beyond simple annular sizing and explicitly quantify the anatomic relationship between the predicted neo‑skirt (stacked leaflets/frames) and the coronaries and the sinuses. CT lets you do that reliably; the key measurements are the ones that predict two related hazards — coronary obstruction and sinus (and sinotubular) sequestration — and each has practical threshold values that change your procedural strategy.

Measure and report on a gated contrast CT with virtual THV simulation (or software modeling) the following:

1) Coronary ostial height (from annular plane to coronary ostium). Low ostial height increases obstruction risk because a low origin requires less leaflet displacement to occlude the ostium. A height <10–12 mm is high risk (especially <10 mm); 12–14 mm is intermediate and needs close attention.

2) Virtual transcatheter valve‑to‑coronary distance (VTC, also called valve‑to‑coronary). This is the perpendicular distance from the expected neoskirt/leaflet plane of the planned THV (or layered THV complex) to the coronary ostium on CT simulation. VTC <4 mm indicates high risk of coronary obstruction and should prompt coronary protection (guidewire + undeployed stent/“chimney”) and strong consideration of leaflet‑modification (BASILICA) or choosing a different strategy. VTC 4–6 mm is intermediate risk — consider BASILICA if anatomy (leaflet length/attachment) is favorable, or at minimum plan for coronary protection; VTC >6 mm is generally low risk.

3) Sinus of Valsalva (SOV) dimensions. Small sinuses cannot accommodate displaced leaflets and increase both coronary obstruction and sinus sequestration risk. Sinus diameters <30 mm (particularly <28 mm) are associated with higher risk when combined with low coronaries or small VTC. Large sinuses (>30–32 mm) are protective.

4) Sinotubular junction (STJ) height and virtual valve‑to‑STJ distance (VTSJ). A low STJ or a small VTSJ means displaced leaflets can be trapped above the sinuses and create a sealed “neo‑sinus” that sequesters flow. A VTSJ (distance from predicted THV skirt/leaflet top to STJ) <2–4 mm (or STJ diameter that is narrow relative to the SOV and THV frame) raises concern for sinus sequestration. If VTSJ is very small or the STJ is tubular/narrow, plan for leaflet modification (BASILICA) or avoid strategies that create a sealed space.

5) Leaflet length/location and prior prosthesis characteristics. In TAV‑in‑TAV scenarios the native/prosthetic leaflet length, the height of prior valve leaflets and the outward displacement by prior frames are critical. Long leaflets that will be pushed above the coronary ostium greatly increase obstruction risk even when ostial height is borderline. Also note frame height, frame cell size (affects future coronary access), and whether prior valve leaflets are supra‑annular.

How thresholds translate to action:
- High risk (any of: VTC <4 mm; coronary height <10 mm; SOV diameter <28–30 mm with small VTC; VTSJ <2–4 mm): plan coronary protection at a minimum (guidewire + undeployed stent), strongly consider BASILICA (leaflet laceration) if feasible, and re‑consider valve choice/implantation depth. In multi‑stent anatomy, BASILICA technical feasibility must be carefully assessed.
- Intermediate risk (VTC 4–6 mm; coronary height 10–12 mm; SOV 28–32 mm; VTSJ borderline): individualized decision — often perform pre‑emptive coronary protection, strongly consider BASILICA if leaflet anatomy suitable, use detailed CT simulation, and involve heart team.
- Low risk (VTC >6 mm; coronary height >12–14 mm; SOV >32 mm; adequate VTSJ): routine TAV‑in‑TAV may proceed without prophylactic coronary protection, but plan for rapid access if needed.

Clinical caveats and reasoning: the essence is the distance between the anticipated neoskirt and the coronary ostium (VTC) and the ability of the sinuses/STJ to accept displaced leaflets. Coronary height alone is insufficient in TAV‑in‑TAV because stacked leaflets and frames raise the effective skirt above the annulus; thus virtual THV simulation and VTC/VTSJ from CT are the most discriminating measures. Always integrate leaflet length, prior prosthesis type and frame geometry into risk assessment, and if in doubt err toward coronary protection and heart‑team discussion about BASILICA or alternative strategies.

### [Management] Given a minimal internal lumen diameter of approximately 14 mm within a prior TAVR frame, which transcatheter valve design features (e.g., supra-annular leaflet position, frame height and cell geometry, radial force) and implantation strategies (e.g., high implantation, commissural alignment) help maximise effective orifice area and preserve future coronary access for a third valve?

Think of the problem as two linked goals: (1) restore the largest possible effective orifice area (EOA) inside a very small, double‑stented landing zone (~14 mm) and (2) avoid creating a neo‑skirt or cell overlap that blocks coronary ostia or makes future reaccess impossible. The device choice and how you implant it are inseparable — each decision trades haemodynamics versus coronary access.

Which valve design features help EOA
Choose a valve that places its leaflets above the annulus (supra‑annular leaflets). Supra‑annular valve leaflets function in a larger flow stream and reliably give a larger EOA than intra‑annular leaflets for the same geometric orifice — a critical advantage in a confined 13–14 mm lumen. A valve with a low internal skirt/short sealing cuff that avoids adding a bulky sub‑coronary neo‑skirt also helps preserve cross‑sectional area and coronary clearance. Repositionability/recapturability is valuable so you can optimise final depth and commissural orientation before release.

Which frame characteristics preserve coronary access
A frame with large, widely spaced open cells in the supra‑coronary region preserves the ability to catheterise the coronaries through the frame in the future. A relatively low frame height in the supra‑coronary zone (or at least minimal skirt height) limits the amount of metal and fabric that can cover the sinus and ostia. Clear fluoroscopic commissural markers that allow rotational alignment reduce the chance that coronary ostia will sit behind a narrow cell or behind a leaflet. Thus large-cell geometry + known commissural orientation is preferable for future coronary access.

Radial force — the tradeoff
Higher radial force can be advantageous because it can expand a previously under‑expanded self‑expanding frame (improving leaflet mobility and EOA) and can resist recoil. However, very high permanent radial force may compress or deform previously implanted frames in ways that alter cell geometry and potentially make coronary access harder. Practically, choose a device with sufficient radial force to obtain good expansion (and plan for post‑dilatation/ballooning if needed), but accept that radial force is a tool to be used intentionally (with planned ballooning) rather than an unselected virtue.

Implantation strategies that matter
High implantation: deliberately implant the new valve at or slightly above the level of the prior leaflets so the old leaflets are pinned outward against the frame and do not fold inward to create an even smaller orifice. High implantation also keeps any neo‑skirt material away from the coronary ostia.

Commissural alignment: use preprocedural CT to determine native and prior‑valve commissural orientation and align the new valve’s commissures to avoid placing posts or narrow cells in front of coronaries. This preserves direct catheter access and reduces risk of coronary obstruction.

Pre‑procedural CT and modelling: quantify valve‑to‑coronary distances and simulate how a neo‑skirt will relate to the ostia. This planning determines whether coronary protection or BASILICA is needed.

Coronary protection backup: even if CT looks favourable, have guidewires/wiring or undeployed stents available; perform simultaneous aortography during ballooning or deployment when risk is finite.

Planned post‑dilatation / BV fracture: plan for controlled post‑dilatation to gain additional frame expansion and improve EOA. If prior frames and valve type permit safe bioprosthetic valve fracture (and coronary anatomy allows), consider it as part of planned strategy — but only after CT/heart‑team assessment because fracture can change cell geometry and coronary relations.

Practical synthesis for a 14 mm minimal lumen (what I would pick and do)
Prefer a supra‑annular, low‑skirt, repositionable valve with large open cells in the supra‑coronary portion and adequate radial force (the Acurate neo2 is an example used successfully here). Use pre‑procedural CT planning to define commissural targets, plan a high implant that pins prior leaflets outward, and align commissures fluoroscopically. Be prepared to post‑dilate to optimise expansion; have coronary protection ready and document commissural orientation fluoroscopically for future reference. Accept the trade‑off that some radial expansion may be required to improve EOA, but always balance that against the risk of creating inaccessible coronary ostia.

Broader principle
Every initial TAVI should be done with a “lifetime management” mindset: choose devices and align implants in ways that preserve coronary access and permit future valve expansion because each reintervention progressively narrows options.

### [Morning Report] How do you interpret an immediate post-implant mean gradient of 67 mm Hg with a calculated aortic valve area of 0.9 cm², indexed area 0.4 cm²/m², and a Doppler velocity index (DVI) of 0.24 after a third TAVI, and what intra- and early post-procedural steps help differentiate severe prosthesis–patient mismatch from valve underexpansion or high-flow states?

Immediate post‑implant numbers — mean gradient 67 mm Hg, AVA 0.9 cm2 (indexed 0.4 cm2/m2) and DVI 0.24 — by Doppler alone fulfil numerical criteria for severe obstruction and, on first glance, severe prosthesis–patient mismatch (PPM). But in the immediate post‑TAVI setting these Doppler measures can reflect three different mechanistic problems: true severe PPM (anatomic effective orifice area too small for the patient), mechanical underexpansion or leaflet restriction of the newly placed valve (or prior frame), or a transient high‑flow/high‑velocity state. Management and prognosis differ, so the critical task is to distinguish them before committing to further invasive therapy.

How to think about the numbers and physiology
The DVI (velocity ratio) is conceptually flow‑independent compared with continuity‑equation AVA, and a DVI ≤0.25 strongly suggests significant obstruction of the prosthetic orifice rather than simple measurement artifact. Indexed EOA ≤0.65 cm2/m2 (many groups use ≤0.6) defines severe PPM; an index of 0.4 is well below that threshold and therefore raises high suspicion for severe mismatch. However, immediately after valve deployment gradients may be spuriously high if stroke volume is large (tachycardia, hyperdynamic LV after relief of obstruction) or if the new valve and the underlying frames are not yet fully expanded — both can give the same Doppler phenotype.

Practical intra‑ and early post‑procedural steps to differentiate the causes
First, use haemodynamics and imaging together rather than relying on Doppler alone. Measure an invasive transvalvular gradient across the prosthesis with catheter pullback to confirm the Doppler gradient. Simultaneously record blood pressure, heart rate and rhythm.

Second, quantify flow. Calculate stroke volume and stroke‑volume index (SVI) from LVOT diameter and LVOT VTI (or invasively). A very high SVI/heart rate suggests a high‑flow state that can raise gradients; conversely a low SVI argues against flow‑related elevation. If flow is high, treat reversible contributors (tachycardia, anemia, vasodilation, inotropes) and remeasure gradients after optimization.

Third, look at valve motion and frame geometry. Immediate TEE or high‑frame‑rate transthoracic imaging and cine fluoroscopy will show leaflet mobility and the frame expansion. Underexpansion or residual constraint of the newly implanted valve (or partial expansion of the underlying Evolut frame) will be evident as reduced frame diameter or immobile leaflets. Aortography can confirm central jet vs significant paravalvular leak (which can complicate interpretation).

Fourth, consider CT or fluoroscopic measurements if uncertainty remains. On‑table fluoroscopic cine can compare frame diameters with expected nominal values; early post‑procedure CT gives definitive assessment of frame expansion and residual lumen if the patient’s condition allows.

Fifth, integrate the time course. If the immediate high gradient is accompanied by preserved leaflet motion and evidence of high flow, and the patient is hemodynamically compensated, short‑interval reassessment (hours to days) often shows fall in gradients as heart rate and flow normalize. If underexpansion is suspected (constricted frame, immobile leaflets), targeted intervention is indicated.

Therapeutic implications
If the problem is a high‑flow state or hemodynamic artifact, treat and reassess; avoid unnecessary re‑intervention. If imaging and invasive gradients confirm mechanical underexpansion/constraint of the new valve (or of the prior Evolut frame), percutaneous remedies include aggressive balloon post‑dilatation (as was performed in this case) or consideration of bioprosthetic valve fracture/techniques to increase expansion when anatomically feasible. If true severe PPM is the cause (anatomic indexed EOA too small despite full expansion), options are limited and must be weighed against risk — medical management and careful follow‑up or, rarely, another intervention if symptoms and gradients remain prohibitive.

Why this patient improved
This case illustrates the diagnostic sequence: immediate high gradient and DVI 0.24 suggested severe obstruction, but post‑dilatation and partial mechanical expansion of the prior Evolut frame plus hemodynamic settling produced a substantial fall in gradient to 27 mm Hg and AVA ~1.1 cm2 by 1 month. That pattern supports a mix of residual mechanical constraint that was at least partly relieved by ballooning and early dynamic factors rather than fixed, irreversible severe PPM alone.

Clinical pearl
An immediate high post‑TAVI Doppler gradient with low DVI and a low indexed EOA is a red flag but not a definitive call for re‑intervention. Confirm with invasive gradients, assess flow (SVI), and inspect valve frame and leaflet motion with TEE/fluoroscopy. Treat reversible hemodynamic factors, consider balloon optimization if underexpansion is demonstrated, and re‑assess before concluding that severe, fixed PPM is present.

### [Pathophysiology] What pathophysiological mechanisms link a prior episode of prosthetic valve endocarditis—after microbiologic cure—to subsequent leaflet dysfunction and restenosis (consider leaflet fibrosis/scar, commissural restriction, pannus, and interaction with sequential valve-in-valve frames)?

Short answer: Even after microbiologic cure, prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) can set in motion inflammation-driven structural changes and mechanical interactions that culminate in leaflet immobilization and restenosis. The pathophysiology is a mix of post‑infectious tissue injury and healing (fibrosis/pannus), altered biomechanics from overlapping stent frames, and changes in local flow and endothelialization that promote progressive leaflet restriction.

Clinical reasoning and mechanisms
When bacteria seed a bioprosthetic valve they injure the leaflet and sewing‑ring interface by direct tissue destruction, by eliciting a local inflammatory response and by depositing adherent material (vegetation, fibrin, microthrombus, and sometimes residual biofilm). Even when antibiotics sterilize the valve, the healing response does not simply restore native pliability. Instead, the injured leaflet and adjacent tissue undergo fibroproliferation and neo‑intimal overgrowth (pannus), with collagen deposition and scarring that stiffen leaflet tissue and narrow the orifice. Commissural areas are particularly vulnerable: inflammation and ensuing scar can tether commissures, producing commissural restriction that prevents full leaflet coaptation and opening.

Layering additional transcatheter frames compounds these effects. A prior infected valve that has healed with scar and pannus is more likely to have leaflets adherent to or incorporated into the metallic frame. When a second (or third) valve is implanted inside this construct, the new frame can mechanically pin already stiffened leaflets in a partially closed position, reduce leaflet excursion by compressing commissures, and create a "neo‑skirt" that further limits the effective orifice area. Repetitive ballooning or post‑dilation can partially expand older frames (improving area) but can also traumatize tissue, promoting more fibrosis. Altered hemodynamics within a multiple‑stent conduit—low shear zones, turbulence, and stasis—favour pannus growth and thrombus formation that may mimic or contribute to restenosis.

Other contributory processes include accelerated calcification of bioprosthetic tissue after inflammation, impaired endothelialization with persistent irregular surfaces that attract thrombus, and persistent low‑grade inflammation (sterile) that perpetuates remodeling even without viable organisms. Finally, overlapping frames increase mesh density and can interfere with commissural alignment and coronary flow, which in turn influence procedural choices (eg, high implantation to pin dysfunctional leaflets open and preserve coronaries).

Practical implications
Recognizing this pathophysiology explains why PVE—even when cured microbiologically—predicts higher rates of later structural dysfunction and why redo TAV procedures need detailed imaging and strategy. Distinguishing pannus/fibrosis (chronic, often fixed restriction on CT/TEE) from thrombus (possibly reversible with anticoagulation) is essential because management differs. Preprocedural MSCT and TEE should evaluate leaflet mobility, frame‑leaflet relationships, valve‑to‑coronary distances, and potential for frame expansion. Procedural strategies such as high implantation, commissural alignment, and post‑dilation are aimed at mechanical solutions to an essentially scar‑mediated problem; they may improve orifice area but cannot reverse established leaflet fibrosis.

Takeaway
PVE leaves a legacy of scar, pannus and altered biomechanics that predispose to prosthetic leaflet restriction and restenosis even after microbiologic cure. In patients with sequential valve layers, mechanical pinning of scarred leaflets and adverse flow patterns are additive drivers of dysfunction. This pathophysiologic understanding should guide imaging, timing, and technical planning for reintervention and temper expectations about durability and the feasibility of future therapies.

### [Management] In a patient with treated Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic valve endocarditis, two prior TAVR frames integrated into the aortic root, prohibitive surgical risk (e.g., EuroSCORE II ~27%), and multiple comorbidities, what multidisciplinary considerations and guideline principles support redo TAVI over surgical explantation/aortic root replacement?

Teaching answer:

The decision to pursue a third transcatheter valve rather than surgical explantation/aortic root replacement rests on three interlocking principles: infection control, individualized guideline application with formal risk stratification, and anatomic/technical feasibility as determined by multidisciplinary heart‑team assessment.

First, guideline and infection‑control principles. Contemporary infective endocarditis guidance emphasises that active, uncontrolled infection or prosthetic complications (abscess, fistula, ongoing bacteremia) mandate surgery when feasible. Conversely, once infection is proven eradicated—documented by completion of an appropriate antibiotic course, persistently negative blood cultures, and no residual vegetations or paravalvular complications on high‑resolution imaging—management of prosthetic structural deterioration may follow the same decision framework used for noninfective valve failure. In this case the team documented microbiologic cure and negative TEE/MSCT for active PVE, satisfying the prerequisite that active infection is excluded before considering repeat transcatheter therapy.

Second, individualized application of guideline recommendations and risk‑benefit analysis. Surgical explantation and root replacement remain definitive for many PVE cases, but guidelines explicitly require individualized decisions that weigh operative risk. This patient’s EuroSCORE II ≈27%, severe comorbidities (COPD, diabetes, recurrent GI bleeding, prior coronary disease and pacemaker), prior femoral vascular complication and two deeply embedded TAVI frames predict prohibitive operative mortality and morbidity and a high likelihood of complex root/ascending aorta reconstruction. The heart‑team must therefore balance the putative incremental durability advantage of surgery against a very high immediate surgical risk and the real possibility that surgery would be technically extremely hazardous or non‑restorative. In such situations, guidelines and consensus statements permit alternate, less invasive strategies when anatomy and infection status permit.

Third, anatomic and procedural feasibility proven by advanced imaging and multidisciplinary planning. MSCT and TEE in this case quantified valve‑to‑coronary distances, sinus dimensions, residual lumen and frame relationships and demonstrated anatomic conditions amenable to a TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV approach with acceptable coronary risk. CT‑guided procedural planning enabled valve selection (supra‑annular, low frame Acurate neo2), strategy for high implantation and commissural alignment to preserve coronary access, and contingency planning (coronary protection considered but not required). The heart team explicitly considered and documented the risks unique to serial frame layering (PPM, coronary access, future options) and judged that a transcatheter redo offered acceptable immediate haemodynamics and far lower peri‑procedural mortality than surgical explant in this patient.

Putting it together: multidisciplinary considerations that supported redo TAVI were documented eradication of Enterococcus faecalis PVE, prohibitive surgical risk with expectation of complex root replacement and high perioperative mortality, CT‑confirmed anatomic feasibility with strategies to mitigate coronary obstruction and optimise effective orifice area, and shared decision‑making with the patient/family. These points align with guideline principles that surgery is preferred for active PVE but that management must be individualized when infection is cleared and surgical risk is prohibitive. The heart team’s role is central: to integrate infection control, objective surgical risk, advanced imaging, procedural expertise, and patient values to choose the least‑harmful effective option.

Clinical pearl: in PVE patients, the single most important determinant that can move the team from “surgery required” to “transcatheter reasonable” is confirmed eradication of infection plus objective demonstration that the anatomy can be treated percutaneously with acceptable coronary and haemodynamic risk; when surgical risk is prohibitive, such a carefully planned TAV‑in‑TAV strategy is an appropriate, guideline‑consistent alternative.

### [Management] During redo TAVI in stacked frames, which intra-procedural strategies mitigate coronary obstruction—choice of valve type (supra-annular vs intra-annular), commissural alignment, high vs low implantation depth, pre-emptive coronary protection (wires/‘chimney’ stents), and leaflet laceration (BASILICA)—and when would you choose each approach?

When you plan a redo TAVI into stacked frames the central problem is not the new valve’s gradient but the risk that layered leaflets and skirts will create a “neo‑skirt” that covers the coronary ostia. The choice of intra‑procedural strategy should therefore be driven by anatomy (MSCT virtual valve‑to‑coronary distance, sinus size, coronary height, and frame geometry), the feasibility of preserving leaflet motion or lacerating leaflets, and your bail‑out options.

Valve choice: pick the device that best balances haemodynamics and coronary preservation for that anatomy. Supra‑annular valves (eg, Acurate neo2) give superior effective orifice area in small, layered landing zones and often have lower skirt material below the annulus; that was the rationale in this case. Balloon‑expandable valves have shorter final frame profiles but can create a bulky skirt or push pre‑existing leaflets outward toward the sinus; self‑expanding valves may allow gentler expansion and commissural alignment. In short: favour a valve with proven haemodynamics in small or constrained orifices and a frame/skirt geometry that minimizes neo‑skirt projection toward the coronaries.

Commissural alignment: always plan it when coronary preservation or future access matters. Good alignment spreads stent posts away from ostia, reduces the mesh obstructing coronary catheters, and may lessen leaflet crowding over a particular sinus. Use CT simulation and fluoroscopic markers to orient the new valve during deployment as part of a lifetime‑management strategy.

Implantation depth: high implantation (more aortic) is often preferred to reduce displacement of prior leaflets into the sinuses and to keep the neo‑skirt from sitting level with the coronary ostia. High implants were deliberately used in this patient to fix older leaflets against the frame and avoid creating a new skirt under the coronaries. Low implantation may be chosen when anchoring concerns or risk of migration outweigh coronary risk, but in stacked frames low implants increase the chance of coronary obstruction.

Pre‑emptive coronary protection (wires/chimney stents): reserve routine pre‑positioning of coronary guidewires and the chimney strategy for cases with borderline CT metrics (small sinus, short valve‑to‑coronary distance, low coronary height) or when BASILICA is not available/feasible. A parked wire in the coronary (with equipment ready to deliver a stent) is a low‑cost, rapid bail‑out and is reasonable when obstruction risk is intermediate. A planned chimney stent is used if obstruction is judged likely and urgent revascularization is anticipated; it effectively preserves flow but at the cost of a persistent stent through layered frames and long‑term issues with stent durability and re‑access.

Leaflet laceration (BASILICA): BASILICA is the preferred prophylactic strategy when CT predicts high risk of coronary obstruction due to leaflet displacement (eg, single‑leaflet length that will reach the ostium, shallow sinuses, narrow valve‑to‑coronary distance) and when the anatomy and operator experience permit effective laceration. In multi‑stented anatomies, BASILICA becomes more complex: multiple leaflet layers and overlying frames can make leaflet traversal and effective laceration technically difficult or impossible, and the success rate is less well defined. Use BASILICA when you can cross the target leaflet(s) and are confident the laceration will create a persistent flow channel; otherwise, plan protection with wires/balloons or be prepared for chimney stenting.

How to decide in practice: use MSCT virtual valve implantation (VTTC) to stratify risk. If VTTC and anatomy show adequate valve‑to‑coronary distance and roomy sinuses, you can proceed without routine protection, choose a valve with good haemodynamics and perform high implantation plus commissural alignment (as in this case). If VTTC is borderline, park coronary wires and have a stent ready. If VTTC shows high risk of obstruction and BASILICA is feasible, do BASILICA plus valve implantation. If BASILICA is not feasible but obstruction is likely, plan upfront coronary protection with guidewires and the option to deploy a chimney stent immediately. Always have rapid bailout plans and experienced operators available.

In summary: tailor the approach. Use CT‑based risk stratification to choose a valve that maximizes EOA while minimizing neo‑skirt projection, align commissures to protect ostia, implant high when safe, pre‑position coronary wires for intermediate risk and use BASILICA for high‑risk anatomies when technically feasible; resort to planned chimney stenting when BASILICA cannot be done and obstruction is likely. The reported case exemplifies CT‑guided selection of a supra‑annular valve, commissural alignment and high implantation to avoid coronary protection because the anatomy permitted it.

### [Management] What antithrombotic regimen is recommended after TAVI for a patient without atrial fibrillation but with a history of recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding, and what evidence or guideline rationale supports that choice over alternatives?

After TAVI in a patient who does not have an indication for long‑term oral anticoagulation (no atrial fibrillation) but who has recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding, the preferred antithrombotic approach is single antiplatelet therapy (SAPT) — typically low‑dose aspirin — rather than dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) or empiric anticoagulation.

Why: randomized TAVI trials and guideline summaries show that in patients without another indication for anticoagulation, adding clopidogrel to aspirin increases bleeding without reducing ischemic events. The POPular TAVI trial (cohort A) demonstrated that aspirin alone produced less bleeding than aspirin plus clopidogrel with no excess in thromboembolic events. Conversely, trials that tested routine anticoagulation after TAVI (eg, rivaroxaban arms in GALILEO/ATLANTIS programs) found increased bleeding and in some analyses worse outcomes when anticoagulation was used without a separate indication. Contemporary ESC/STS guidance therefore favors SAPT over DAPT after TAVI in patients who do not require oral anticoagulation.

How to apply this to a patient with recurrent GI bleeding: choose the strategy with the lowest bleeding risk while still protecting the valve and vascular beds. Start/continue low‑dose aspirin alone as the default. Intensification to DAPT or to a DOAC should be avoided unless there is a compelling, time‑limited ischemic indication (recent coronary stent with high thrombotic risk), and even then the shortest feasible duration should be used with GI protective measures. In a patient with active or very high risk GI bleeding, it is reasonable after multidisciplinary discussion to stop antiplatelet therapy once the immediate periprocedural period has passed — recognizing this is a balance of thrombotic vs bleeding risks and evidence is limited. Always co‑prescribe a proton‑pump inhibitor for gastroprotection, address reversible bleeding sources (GI referral), and involve cardiology, gastroenterology and hematology in shared decision‑making.

Bottom line: for this patient without AF and with recurrent GI bleeding, low‑dose aspirin alone is recommended over DAPT or empiric anticoagulation (supported by POPular TAVI and guideline recommendations); if bleeding risk is extreme, consider individualized shorter or no antiplatelet therapy after heart‑team discussion and GI optimization.

### [Clinical Pearls] For long-term surveillance after a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV, which clinical and echocardiographic parameters and time points should be monitored to detect structural valve deterioration and prosthesis–patient mismatch, and how would you interpret a stable mean gradient of ~26–27 mm Hg with an aortic valve area of ~1.1 cm² at 1 year in an asymptomatic patient?

After a third transcatheter valve you must treat surveillance as both routine prosthetic follow‑up and targeted monitoring for the two specific problems most likely to threaten the result: structural valve deterioration (SVD) and prosthesis–patient mismatch (PPM). The surveillance strategy is determined by clinical stability but should be more intensive in the first year and whenever symptoms or haemodynamic change occur.

What to monitor and why
1) Clinical status: interval history focused on exertional dyspnoea, angina, syncope, fever or constitutional symptoms (for recurrent endocarditis). Physical exam for new murmurs and signs of heart failure. New fever or bacteremia mandates immediate evaluation for prosthetic valve endocarditis.

2) Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) — the cornerstone. At each study obtain peak velocity and mean gradient, Doppler‑derived aortic valve area (continuity equation), Doppler velocity index (DVI), indexed effective orifice area (EOAi = AVA/BSA), presence and grade of transvalvular or paravalvular regurgitation, left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), stroke volume index and any changes in LV size or hypertrophy. DVI and EOAi are less load‑dependent than calculated AVA and help distinguish true obstruction from low‑flow states.

3) Timing: baseline immediate post‑procedure (inpatient), 30 days, 6 months, 12 months, then yearly if stable. Sooner and more frequently if symptomatic or if gradients rise. Also re‑image at any unexplained change in clinical status or new murmur.

4) Trigger thresholds prompting advanced evaluation or intervention: an increase in mean gradient of ≥10 mm Hg from nadir, a new mean gradient ≥40 mm Hg, a fall in AVA >0.3 cm², a DVI <0.35 (suggesting obstruction), progressive LV dysfunction or new moderate–severe regurgitation. Rising gradient or new regurgitation → transoesophageal echo (for leaflet motion, pannus, vegetations) and multislice CT (to assess leaflet thickening, frame geometry, coronary obstruction risk and neo‑skirt). Consider invasive hemodynamics when noninvasive data discordant.

5) Laboratory and other testing: blood cultures for febrile patients, hemolysis labs if suspected paravalvular leak, and routine anticoagulation/antiplatelet review. In a patient with prior PVE, maintain a low threshold for infection workup.

How to interpret a stable mean gradient ~26–27 mm Hg with AVA ≈1.1 cm² at 1 year in an asymptomatic patient
A mean gradient in the mid‑20s with an AVA ≈1.1 cm² most consistently represents a moderate residual stenosis or moderate PPM rather than ongoing aggressive SVD, particularly when the values are unchanged between 1 month and 1 year and the patient remains asymptomatic with preserved LVEF. The key points of interpretation are that (1) the haemodynamics have improved substantially from the immediate post‑procedure severe gradient (67 mm Hg) and stabilized, (2) the lack of progressive gradient rise over time argues against active leaflet degeneration causing rapid SVD, and (3) absence of regurgitation, normal LV function and clinical well‑being support conservative follow‑up rather than reintervention.

You must, however, quantify PPM by indexing AVA to body surface area: severe PPM is typically EOAi ≤0.65 cm²/m² (moderate 0.65–0.85 cm²/m²; cutoffs adjusted in obesity), so in many older men with BSA ≈1.7–2.0 m² an AVA of 1.1 cm² may correspond to moderate (borderline severe if small BSA) PPM. DVI and EOAi should be reported at each study to clarify this. Persistent moderate PPM can be clinically acceptable if the patient is asymptomatic and LV parameters are stable, but it raises long‑term risk of earlier SVD and should inform counseling and future planning.

Practical next steps
Continue routine follow‑up with TTE at yearly intervals (and sooner if symptoms), document EOAi and DVI each time, watch for any gradient rise ≥10 mm Hg or mean ≥40 mm Hg, and lower threshold for CT/TEE if changes appear. Maintain vigilance for endocarditis and optimize medical therapy and risk factor control. In short, the current 1‑year findings are compatible with a stable, moderate residual stenosis/PPM rather than progressive SVD; conservative surveillance with the trigger thresholds above is appropriate.

### [Differential Diagnosis] Three months after a TAV-in-TAV, a patient presents with fever and a new mean gradient rise to 35 mm Hg. Which bedside findings, laboratory results, and imaging features help discriminate leaflet thrombosis from prosthetic valve endocarditis, and how would initial management differ between these two entities?

This is a common and critical bedside dilemma: new fever plus a rising transvalvular gradient after a recent TAV-in-TAV can be either prosthetic leaflet thrombosis causing obstructive dysfunction, or recurrent/ongoing prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). Distinguishing the two quickly directs opposite initial therapies (anticoagulation for thrombosis versus prompt antibiotics ± surgery for endocarditis), so focus your assessment on clinical context, microbiology/inflammation, and targeted imaging.

Clinical reasoning and bedside features
Ask whether the clinical picture is dominated by systemic infection (high fevers, chills, rigors, sepsis, new systemic embolic phenomena) or by progressive dyspnea/heart failure from obstruction. PVE usually presents with persistent high fever, constitutional symptoms, new or changing regurgitant murmur, peripheral stigmata or embolic events; leaflet thrombosis is classically subacute worsening of gradient and dyspnea and often lacks prominent systemic inflammatory features. A history of subtherapeutic or absent anticoagulation, low‑flow states, atrial fibrillation, or recent cessation of anticoagulant increases the pretest probability of thrombosis. Prior Enterococcus PVE (as in this case) raises the baseline risk of relapse and raises your suspicion for infection.

Laboratory tests
Draw multiple sets of blood cultures before giving antibiotics if endocarditis is suspected. Persistent positive cultures, especially with the same organism, and a marked leukocytosis or very elevated CRP/ESR favour PVE. By contrast, leaflet thrombosis typically has normal or only mildly elevated inflammatory markers and repeatedly negative blood cultures. Procalcitonin may help: higher in bacterial infection. Note that low‑grade fever alone is not specific.

Imaging strategy
Start with transthoracic echo (TTE) to document gradient and gross regurgitation. Proceed urgently to transesophageal echo (TEE) and to gated 4‑D cardiac CT because each detects different features: TEE is best for vegetations, paravalvular abscess, dehiscence, and new regurgitation; 4‑D CT is most sensitive for hypoattenuated leaflet thickening (HALT) and reduced leaflet motion (RELM) — the hallmarks of prosthetic leaflet thrombosis. On CT, central focal hypoattenuated masses along leaflets with restricted motion without perivalvular extension indicate thrombosis. In contrast, mobile irregular masses with associated perivalvular extension, abscess cavities, or prosthesis dehiscence on TEE (and increased uptake on 18F‑FDG PET/CT) point to PVE. PET/CT is particularly useful when TEE and CT are inconclusive for prosthetic infections. Serial imaging and correlation with microbiology are often required.

Putting it together in this patient
Three months after TAV-in-TAV, both entities are possible. His prior Enterococcus faecalis PVE makes relapse a real concern — so persistent fever and positive cultures would strongly favour recurrent PVE. If he has fever but normal inflammatory markers and negative cultures and CT demonstrates HALT/RELM without vegetations or abscess, thrombosis is more likely.

Initial management differences
If you suspect leaflet thrombosis (hemodynamically stable, no signs of systemic infection): obtain CT/TEE to confirm and start therapeutic anticoagulation (typically unfractionated heparin or warfarin to target INR 2.5–3.5; some centers use DOACs but evidence is limited for prosthetic transcatheter valves). Monitor clinical response and gradients; consider urgent intervention (thrombolysis or repeat transcatheter/surgical therapy) only if refractory, hemodynamic compromise, or contraindication to anticoagulation.

If you suspect PVE (fever, positive blood cultures, elevated inflammatory markers, vegetations, abscess, dehiscence, emboli): obtain at least three sets of blood cultures, start empiric broad‑spectrum IV antibiotics after cultures (tailored to prosthetic organisms and local guidelines — include coverage for enterococci in this patient), involve infectious diseases and cardiac surgery early, and avoid therapeutic anticoagulation until the diagnosis is clarified because anticoagulation increases risk of hemorrhagic conversion of embolic stroke and bleeding from surgery. Indications for urgent surgery include uncontrolled infection, abscess, prosthetic dehiscence with heart failure, or persistent bacteremia despite appropriate antibiotics.

Practical approach at the bedside
Obtain blood cultures immediately, check CBC/CRP/ESR and procalcitonin, perform urgent TTE then TEE and gated 4‑D cardiac CT (and PET/CT if equivocal). While awaiting imaging and cultures, if clinical features and labs strongly suggest infection, begin empiric IV antibiotics after cultures; if clinical picture and imaging point to thrombosis and infection is unlikely, start therapeutic anticoagulation. In ambiguous cases with high stakes (prior Enterococcus PVE, prosthesis dysfunction), treat as possible endocarditis until proven otherwise and expedite multimodality imaging and ID/cardiac surgery consultation.

In sum: use clinical phenotype and inflammatory/microbiologic data to triage, use TEE and 4‑D CT (± PET) to discriminate HALT/RELM from vegetations/abscess, and remember the therapies are nearly opposite — anticoagulate for thrombosis, but prioritize blood cultures and early antibiotics (and surgical evaluation) for suspected PVE.
