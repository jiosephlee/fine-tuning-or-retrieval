# Third Time’s a Charm: TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV after Treated Endocarditis—When Is It Reasonable?

What makes this case interesting? A patient in his late 70s with two prior transcatheter aortic valves, a documented episode of Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) treated with a full antibiotic course, and then severe prosthetic degeneration—who ultimately received a third TAVI (Acurate neo2) into an Evolut-in-Lotus stack. One year later he’s asymptomatic with stable gradients. That sequence (TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV) after treated PVE is rare, technically challenging and forces you to balance infection control, structural feasibility and prohibitive surgical risk.

If you care for patients with failing bioprostheses and a history of PVE, this case is a great launchpad to discuss a practical decision framework: what “cured” endocarditis looks like, when prior PVE should or shouldn’t block a transcatheter redo, timing after antibiotics, and the imaging/technical considerations that make a successful redo more likely.

## The big-picture rule: PVE ≠ automatic veto of TAVI, but cure must be demonstrable
- The 2025 ESC guidance reiterates that surgical replacement is generally preferred for active PVE (especially with heart failure, uncontrolled infection or complications). But guidelines also emphasise individualised decision-making: in patients with prohibitive surgical risk, and where infection is controlled, a transcatheter approach can be considered.
- Key principle: you must be confident infection is eradicated before implanting another prosthesis. If not, you risk seeding a new device or missing occult complications (abscess, fistula).

## What “infection control / cured” really looks like in practice
No single test makes the call. Treat the whole clinical picture:

Required (minimum):
- Completed a full, targeted antibiotic course appropriate for the organism (this patient completed 6 weeks of IV ampicillin-based therapy).
- Repeated blood cultures negative off antibiotics (or at least not persistently positive). Absence of persistent bacteremia is critical.
- Imaging without evidence of active infection: transoesophageal echo (TEE) with no vegetations, no new paravalvular leak suspicious for abscess/fistula, and MSCT showing no periannular collections or dehiscence.

Helpful adjuncts (when available):
- 18F-FDG PET/CT can help detect residual metabolic activity in prosthetic material—useful if TEE/MSCT are equivocal.
- Serial inflammatory markers and clinical stability (no fever, improving heart failure symptoms).

In the case: the team had negative repeat cultures, TEE and MSCT without active findings, no suppressive antibiotics, and clinical stability—meeting pragmatic criteria for considering redo TAVI.

## Timing: how long after antibiotics is “safe”?
There’s no fixed interval, but practical considerations:
- Wait until you have documented microbiologic and imaging evidence of resolution. Many teams wait several weeks to months after completing therapy to ensure clearance and to reassess valve function.
- Avoid a hasty implant while still on suppressive antibiotics or with unresolved bacteremia.
- Be cautious in early reintervention if the initial infection was complicated (abscess, persistent bacteremia, difficult organism).

In this case, the patient had recurrent symptoms and valve dysfunction shortly after the 2022 TAV-in-TAV and developed PVE that same year; after completing therapy and confirming resolution, reintervention proceeded in 2024—so there was a clinically reasonable interval with objective negative testing.

## When prior PVE should push you away from TAVI
- Ongoing bacteremia, positive cultures, persistent fevers, or imaging evidence of abscess/fistula — these favour surgery if feasible.
- Organisms known for biofilm/persistent infection (eg, some staphylococci, fungi) may require more caution.
- Situations where structural infection has destroyed the annulus/root such that a transcatheter frame cannot provide a durable solution.

If the patient is truly prohibitive risk for surgery and surgical options are unrealistic (as in this case—EuroSCORE II 27% and hostile aortic root after two integrated stent frames), a carefully staged transcatheter strategy may still be the best risk-balanced choice.

## Practical pre-procedure checklist before considering redo TAVI after treated PVE
1. Multidisciplinary heart team discussion (imaging, ID, cardiac surgery, interventional cardiology, anesthesiology).
2. Confirm microbiologic cure: no persistent bacteremia; documented completion of therapy; consider ID input on need for suppressive therapy (ideally none).
3. TEE to exclude vegetations, PVL, abscesses, root disruption.
4. High-resolution MSCT for:
   - Assessing valve-to-coronary distances and sinus anatomy.
   - Measuring residual lumen inside prior frames (important for valve sizing and PPM risk).
   - Planning implantation depth, commissural alignment and need for coronary protection/BASILICA planning.
5. Consider PET-CT if TEE/MSCT equivocal.
6. Shared decision-making with the patient/family about durability limits and worst-case surgical bailout scenarios.
7. Prepare for coronary protection or bailout strategies in lab (wires, stents, possible BASILICA tools), even if not used.

## Technical pearls from this case (and generalizable tips)
- Use CT-guided modelling for valve selection and implantation depth. In triple-stent anatomies, high implantation to pin prior leaflets against the frame can preserve an effective orifice and avoid creating a neo-skirt that blocks coronaries.
- Valve choice matters: supra-annular valves with favorable hemodynamics and lower frame heights (and the ability to achieve commissural alignment) can be preferable in narrow residual lumens.
- Post-dilatation can partially expand prior self-expanding frames (as likely happened here) and improve gradients; plan balloon sizes carefully to avoid annular injury.
- Always evaluate coronary access risk pre-procedure. Commissural alignment attempts and anticipation of mesh burden are essential—lay out a plan for coronary protection even if CT suggests a low risk.
- Remember prosthesis–patient mismatch risk will be high with layered frames. Set realistic hemodynamic expectations and counsel the patient about symptom vs survival goals.

## Pitfalls and red flags to watch for
- Smoldering infection: low-grade fevers, elevated CRP/ESR without clear source — consider PET-CT and repeat cultures before reintervention.
- Positive cultures after stopping antibiotics or need for indefinite suppressive therapy—these generally argue against implanting prosthetic material.
- Underestimating coronary obstruction risk in multi-frame anatomy; angiography during balloon predilatation can be revealing (this team confirmed coronaries patent during pre-dilation).
- Overconfidence in CT alone—combine TEE, MSCT and microbiology for a robust assessment.

## Shared decision-making and follow-up
- Document the multidisciplinary rationale, alternative options (surgery vs conservative), and the risks of reinfection and limited future options if the new valve fails.
- Plan close post-procedure surveillance: early serial echocardiography, clinical monitoring for infection recurrence, and low threshold for imaging if symptoms recur.

## Take‑home messages
- Prior treated PVE is not an absolute contraindication to redo TAVI, but it raises the bar: you must document microbiologic cure and obtain concordant negative imaging (TEE/MSCT ± PET-CT).
- Multidisciplinary assessment and CT-guided procedural planning are essential—especially when dealing with layered frames and coronary access concerns.
- Timing matters: wait for documented infection resolution; avoid implanting while bacteremia or active inflammation is suspected.
- Expect high risk of prosthesis–patient mismatch, need for careful valve selection, commissural alignment and readiness for coronary protection.
- Shared decision-making, clear documentation and close follow-up are mandatory—because options after a failed third transcatheter valve are extremely limited.

This case shows it can be done: a carefully selected, CT-planned TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV after treated Enterococcus PVE produced a durable 1-year result. But success hinges on rigorous infection control assessment, meticulous imaging-based planning, and honest risk–benefit conversations with patients and families.



# Reading the Neo‑Skirt: CT Planning to Dodge Coronary Obstruction in Triple TAVR

What makes this case interesting? A man in his late 70s with two prior transcatheter aortic valves (Lotus → Evolut) developed Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic valve endocarditis, was treated successfully, then presented with severe prosthetic stenosis/regurgitation and underwent a third transcatheter valve (Acurate neo2) — a true TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV. The team used MSCT‑guided planning to intentionally implant high and “pin” the prior leaflets, preserve coronary flow and access without prophylactic coronary protection, and achieved a durable 1‑year clinical and echocardiographic result. Triple‑stented anatomy plus a history of PVE is exactly the scenario that forces you to stop relying on single‑measure heuristics and start thinking in 3D: where will the neo‑skirt sit relative to the coronaries, what happens when frames deform each other, and when is coronary protection mandatory?

This post focuses on the concrete CT planning pearls and procedural logic used in layered‑frame TAVR — what to measure, what to anticipate, and the common misreads that can put coronary flow (and future access) at risk.

Why CT matters more than ever in reinterventions
- Every additional valve stacks metal and tissue into a new 3D construct. The “neo‑skirt” (the effective obstruction created by prior leaflets and skirt material when they’re pinned or folded) is a function of prior valve geometry, leaflet mobility, and how a new valve will sit. Plain echo or fluoroscopy can’t reliably predict the spatial relationship between that neo‑skirt and the coronary ostia.
- MSCT lets you model valve‑to‑coronary distances, sinus dimensions, and the landing zone available for a high or low implant strategy. In this case CT showed a minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm through the Evolut frame and adequate valve‑to‑coronary clearance to attempt a high implant with a supra‑annular device (Acurate neo2).

CT pearls for layered frames — what to measure (and why)
- Coronary ostial height (relative to the native annulus plane): still essential, but interpret in the context of the stacked frames. A “safe” ostial height with a single valve may be inadequate once prior leaflets are pushed upward by a new device.
- Sinus of Valsalva diameters and height: small sinuses increase the risk that pinned leaflets/neo‑skirt will occlude coronaries. Measure maximal sinus width at the level of the sinotubular junction and mid‑sinus.
- Valve‑to‑coronary distance (VTC): measure from the anticipated final leaflet/neo‑skirt plane (not necessarily the annulus) to the coronary ostia. In TAV‑in‑TAV scenarios this means modelling where the pinned prior leaflets will sit after the new valve is deployed.
- Internal lumen and residual orifice diameter through existing frames: gives you the landing zone sizing and whether the new frame can expand enough to provide acceptable EOA or whether you’ll get severe PPM. In the case, a 13.7 mm minimum lumen implied a restrictive environment that dictated choice of a supra‑annular, low‑frame height valve.
- Frame interaction and commissural alignment potential: map the commissural posts of prior valves relative to coronaries and to fluoroscopic landmarks so you can attempt commissural alignment with the new valve to preserve future access.
- Consider dynamic modelling: if available, 3D reconstruction or valve‑in‑valve planning software (or manual 3D CT reconstructions) can simulate where the neo‑skirt will be after the new valve pins the old leaflets.

How to anticipate the neo‑skirt
- The neo‑skirt is formed by prior leaflets and the skirt material when they’re pushed into a new position by the new prosthesis. Think of it as a curtain that can drape over a coronary ostium.
- High implantation that pins old leaflets at or above their leaflet tips can prevent inward folding of the leaflets into the flow lumen — this helps preserve EOA and avoids creating a skirt that extends below the coronaries.
- Conversely, a low implant can trap leaflet tissue below the coronary ostium and create a neo‑skirt that projects into the sinuses and occludes a coronary.
- Balloon post‑dilatation can radially expand prior frames (partial mechanical expansion) and change the neo‑skirt shape — that can be beneficial (improving EOA) but also unpredictable for coronary clearance if not modelled.

When you can reasonably forgo coronary protection
- After careful CT planning demonstrating sufficient VTC from the final leaflet plane to each coronary ostium, preserved sinus dimensions, and a deploy strategy that minimizes neo‑skirt projection, you may proceed without routine coronary protection.
- In the reported case, team review of CT modelling showed that high implantation of a low‑frame, supra‑annular device would place the neoleaflet line below the coronary ostia, preserving flow and access — so they proceeded without wire‑protection or chimney stents.
- Key caveat: for marginal VTC or small sinuses, or when CT modelling is uncertain because of heavy calcification, eccentric frames, or poor image quality, have coronary protection planned (wires, undeployed stent) — it’s a small step that can avert catastrophe.

Technical strategy points to reduce coronary risk and preserve access
- Choose a valve with design features that match your constraints: in narrow layered anatomy, a supra‑annular valve with relatively low outer frame height can deliver better effective orifice area and easier commissural alignment.
- Aim for commissural alignment of the new valve relative to prior frames and coronaries. Use CT landmarks and known fluoroscopic orientation markers to orient the valve during deployment.
- Consider high implantation to pin prior leaflets at the leaflet tips (not deep) — this helps avoid formation of a skirt below the coronary ostia and reduces PPM caused by leaflet infolding.
- Pre‑dilate or post‑dilate judiciously: post‑dilation can help expand prior frames and improve gradients but can change the neo‑skirt geometry; have coronary wires or bailout plans ready if VTC is borderline.
- Plan for future coronary access: even when immediate obstruction is avoided, multiple frames can impede catheter passage later. Strive for commissural alignment and avoid leaflet/skirt positioning that will block sinus windows.

Common CT misreads and pitfalls in layered‑frame (TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV) planning
- Measuring VTC to the annulus rather than to the anticipated final leaflet plane — this underestimates obstruction risk.
- Ignoring prior leaflet length and where those leaflets will be pinned relative to the coronaries.
- Overreliance on ostial height alone — small sinuses or bulky skirts can obstruct despite reasonable ostial height.
- Failing to appreciate that self‑expanding frames (eg, Evolut) can be further expanded by post‑dilatation, changing the final geometry.
- Skipping commissural mapping — postoperative reaccess for PCI can be impossible if commissures are misaligned across multiple frames.

Case‑driven takeaways
This patient shows that with meticulous CT planning and a deliberate implant strategy you can:
- Perform a third TAVR after treated PVE if infection is resolved and anatomy is favourable.
- Use high implantation to pin prior leaflets and both preserve coronary flow and improve EOA compared with leaving leaflet tissue to fold inward.
- Forego coronary protection when CT modelling and sinus anatomy convincingly demonstrate adequate VTC and clearance — but always have a bailout plan.

Key take‑home messages
- Measure valve‑to‑coronary distance to the anticipated final neo‑skirt/leaflet plane, not merely to the annulus.
- Assess sinus dimensions and prior leaflet length; small sinuses + long leaflets = high risk of neo‑skirt coronary obstruction.
- Plan commissural alignment and choose a valve whose frame/leaflet design helps preserve EOA and coronary access in layered anatomy.
- High implantation to pin prior leaflets can prevent inward leaflet folding, reduce PPM, and preserve coronaries when CT supports the strategy.
- If VTC or sinus anatomy is borderline, proceed with coronary protection ready — it’s a cheap insurance policy against an otherwise life‑threatening complication.

This case doesn’t make the impossible routine, but it does show what disciplined CT planning and strategy‑driven deployment can achieve in even the most complex layered TAVR anatomies.



# The Gradient That Vanished: Making Sense of Early Severe PPM after Redo TAVR

This case grabbed my attention because of the jaw‑dropping immediate post‑implant numbers: mean gradient 67 mm Hg and a Doppler velocity index (DVI) of 0.24 right after a third TAVR (Acurate neo2) implant into two prior transcatheter frames — and then a drop to mean gradient ~27 mm Hg by 1 month with durable function at 1 year. That’s not a trick of the echo machine — it’s a pattern we need to recognise so we don’t reflexively reintervene.

Why it matters: as TAVI gets used earlier and patients live longer, multilayered valve anatomy (TAV‑in‑TAV, and sometimes TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV) will be seen more often. Immediate haemodynamics after implant can be misleading. Understanding the physiology and mechanics that convert an alarming early gradient into an acceptable one over days–weeks helps avoid unnecessary procedures and guides optimal intra‑procedural strategy.

What likely happened in this case
- Initial under‑expansion / leaflet restriction: The previously degenerated Lotus→Evolut inner anatomy meant the new valve had to sit within a narrow lumen (MSCT minimum of 13.7 mm). Even a well‑deployed self‑expander can be partially constrained by rigid prior frames and folded leaflets immediately after implantation, producing very high Doppler gradients.
- Delayed radial expansion / self‑expander remodeling: Self‑expanding frames (and even pre‑expanded frames after post‑dilation) can continue to expand and conform over hours to weeks as tissue and stent stresses redistribute. This can increase effective orifice area progressively.
- Effect of post‑dilation: The team performed post‑dilation (20/40 mm) after implantation. High‑pressure ballooning can (a) improve valve frame expansion, (b) push previously immobile leaflets aside, and (c) partially expand an underlying self‑expander (the Evolut frame) — all of which reduce gradient over time.
- Left ventricular (LV) afterload regression and reverse remodelling: Relief of severe obstruction and improvement in LV function after valve therapy reduce transvalvular flow dynamics and gradients over weeks. The patient’s EF improved and chamber volumes decreased — lowering the driving pressures contributing to Doppler gradients.
- Measurement nuance: Immediate post‑implant gradients can be confounded by tachycardia, low stroke volume states, or pressure recovery phenomena. DVI and indexed AVA are less preload/flow dependent and should be incorporated into early assessment.

How to think about immediate high gradients after valve implant
- Don’t panic at a single post‑implant Doppler gradient unless the patient is unstable or there’s clear evidence of obstruction (e.g., haemodynamic collapse, severe LV dysfunction not explained otherwise). Immediate high gradients are not always permanent.
- Combine metrics: use DVI and indexed AVA (AVA indexed to BSA) along with mean gradient. DVI <0.25 and indexed AVA <0.6 cm²/m² suggest severe prosthesis‑patient mismatch (PPM), but context matters early after implant.
- Correlate with valve leaflets on fluoroscopy/TEE, aortography, and with preprocedural CT anatomy. Look for frame underexpansion, leaflet immobility, or malposition that would mandate immediate correction.
- If residual gradient is due to underexpansion and anatomy allows, consider prompt balloon post‑dilation during the same procedure rather than immediate re‑TAVR. But in layered anatomies, caution is needed to avoid annular injury or coronary compromise.

Practical procedural implications (especially for redo and layered TAVR)
- Preprocedural CT planning is essential: quantify lumen inside prior frame, valve‑to‑coronary distances, and predict neo‑skirt. Plan valve choice and implantation depth to maximise effective orifice area.
- Prefer supra‑annular platforms when you anticipate a small inner lumen. Supra‑annular leaflets often give better effective orifice area in constrained anatomies.
- Implantation depth matters: a high implant that seats the new valve at the level of the inner valve leaflets can fix prior leaflets outward and minimise neo‑skirt below the coronaries — this preserves EOA and coronary access.
- Commissural alignment: attempt to align to preserve coronary access and future PCI options; in layered frames this becomes harder but remains important.
- Be ready to post‑dilate: plan the balloon size and have bail‑out equipment ready. Post‑dilation can both expand the new frame and exert radial force on previous frames to improve EOA.
- Measure and monitor: document DVI, absolute and indexed AVA, gradients, and LV function immediately and at short interval follow‑up (days–weeks). Trends matter.

When to consider reintervention vs observation
- Immediate reintervention (repeat valve‑in‑valve or surgical explant) is rarely required based solely on a single high immediate gradient if the patient is haemodynamically stable and imaging suggests underexpansion or reversible causes.
- Indications for urgent reintervention include haemodynamic instability, cardiogenic shock, severe LV failure attributable to obstruction, or clear structural problem (malposition, significant paravalvular leak with haemodynamic consequence).
- If stable, observe and reassess with TTE at 24–72 hours and at 1 month — many gradients will improve substantially as in this case.

Clinical pearls
- An immediate post‑implant mean gradient of 60–70 mm Hg in a layered TAVR patient is alarming but can fall into the 20s within weeks — mechanisms: underexpansion, delayed self‑expander remodeling, effects of post‑dilation, and LV afterload regression.
- Use DVI and indexed AVA alongside mean gradient to judge true stenosis severity early after implant.
- In redo TAVR, choose supra‑annular devices and aim for high implant + commissural alignment to maximise effective orifice area and future coronary access.
- Avoid knee‑jerk reintervention: stabilise the patient, review imaging, consider immediate post‑dilation if feasible, and reassess gradients over time before committing to another invasive procedure.

Take‑home messages
- Immediate high gradients after TAVR in multilayered anatomy can be transient — understand the mechanics before reintervening.
- Combine haemodynamic measures (mean gradient), flow‑independent indices (DVI), and indexed AVA for decision making.
- Preprocedural CT, valve choice, implant depth, commissural alignment, and planned post‑dilation are your tools to prevent severe PPM in redo settings.
- When in doubt and the patient is stable, reassess over days–weeks — the gradient that looks catastrophic in the cath lab can be the gradient that vanishes.



# Frame‑on‑Frame Strategy: Cross‑Platform Valve Choice and Commissural Alignment That Matter

What makes this case worth reading? A man in his late 70s—Lotus in 2014, Evolut TAV‑in‑TAV in 2022, then Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic valve endocarditis treated and cleared—and in 2024 underwent a third transcatheter implant: Acurate neo2 inside an Evolut inside a Lotus. Triple‑stented anatomy, prior infection, prohibitive surgical risk, and yet a CT‑guided, strategy‑led TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV delivered a durable 1‑year result with preserved coronary flow and improved hemodynamics. This is not showmanship; it’s a pragmatic lesson in valve selection, imaging-led planning, and the nuts‑and‑bolts of commissural alignment when there is no margin for error.

Why the Acurate neo2 after Lotus→Evolut?
- Supra‑annular leaflets: In an already narrowed lumen (MSCT showed a 13.7 mm minimal lumen in the Evolut frame), supra‑annular leaflet position is a major plus for effective orifice area. The neo2 aims to place leaflets above the annulus, which helps maximize flow when multiple frames compete for space.
- Lower frame height and design: A lower external frame profile and a design that facilitates commissural alignment were chosen deliberately to reduce neo‑skirt formation beneath the coronaries and to ease future coronary access. In this patient the team intentionally targeted a higher implant—anchoring at the Evolut leaflet level—to fix prior leaflets outward and avoid inward leaflet folding that would worsen PPM.
- Cross‑platform complementarity: The neo2’s features were chosen to complement—not fight—the mechanical constraints imposed by the Lotus (first) and Evolut (second) frames. That planning matters more than brand loyalty.

CT is not optional — it’s the procedure
- Preprocedural multislice CT (MSCT) guided everything: valve‑to‑coronary distances, sinus dimensions, frame interaction, and whether a high implant could preserve coronary flow. In this case CT confirmed feasibility so the team proceeded without prophylactic coronary protection.
- Use CT to plan: intended implantation depth, which valve size, expected neo‑skirt location, and whether you can achieve commissural alignment that will leave fenestrations toward coronary ostia.
- Don’t skip 3‑D modelling if available—when frames are layered, subtle angulations and eccentric expansions change everything.

Commissural alignment across different platforms — practical approach
- Aim: preserve coronary access, minimise neo‑skirt obstruction, improve leaflet coaptation and hemodynamics.
- From CT → fluoroscopy: Identify CT landmarks (native commissures, coronary ostia relation to existing frame posts) and translate them to fluoroscopic markers (cusp overlap view, stent posts, radiopaque commissural markers).
- For Evolut and Acurate neo2 specifically: use distinct radiopaque features of the existing Evolut frame as reference points and orient the neo2 during rotation so its commissural posts line up with the Evolut leaflets rather than sit in front of a coronary.
- Practical steps used in this case: CT modelling indicated the neo2 could be implanted high so that its commissures would align with Evolut leaflets; fluoroscopic alignment at deployment used pre‑identified stent markers and cusp overlap views to orient the device.
- Commissural alignment in redo cases is not cosmetic — it affects residual EOA, risk of coronary obstruction, and whether future PCI will be feasible.

High implant strategy — why and how
- Goal: seat the new valve at the level of the prior valve leaflets so the prior leaflets are pinned outward, preventing inward folding and reducing effective leaflet redundancy that would otherwise narrow the new valve or create a neo‑skirt over the coronaries.
- Technique used here: balloon pre‑dilation, careful positioning guided by CT/fluoro, deployment targeting Evolut leaflet plane, then aggressive post‑dilatation (20/40 mm balloon) to optimize expansion. No intentional valve fracture was done, but post‑dilation likely partially expanded the Evolut frame and helped gradients.
- Risk balance: higher implant can increase conduction disturbance risk or paravalvular leak in native annuli; when you’re inside prior frames, the calculus is different—your main enemies are PPM and coronary obstruction.

Coronary protection: plan, but don’t overuse it
- In this case CT showed acceptable valve‑to‑coronary distances and predicted that high implantation would keep the neo‑skirt below the coronaries; the team proceeded without prophylactic protection.
- General rule: if CT predicts uncertain clearance, use coronary protection (guidewire ± undeployed stent) or consider BASILICA if appropriate and available. In triple frames, BASILICA feasibility is uncertain; prevention by planning is preferable.

Expect rough immediate hemodynamics — follow the trend
- Immediate post‑implant TTE in this case showed a high mean gradient (67 mm Hg) and severe PPM metrics, but by 1 month gradients dropped to ~27 mm Hg and stayed ~26 mm Hg at 1 year with AVA ~1.1 cm² and EF 65%.
- Why? Post‑dilation, partial mechanical expansion of the Evolut frame, improved leaflet motion as layered frames settle, and optimized commissural alignment can all improve effective orifice area over days to weeks.
- Don’t panic at an immediately elevated gradient if anatomy and valve seating look appropriate; monitor and correlate clinically.

Infective endocarditis history — key considerations
- Active infection should be excluded by repeat imaging and negative blood cultures before reintervention. This patient completed antibiotics and had no evidence of active PVE on TEE and CT.
- Prior PVE raises concerns about leaflet integrity and healing; nonetheless, once cured and with prohibitive surgical risk, a percutaneous redo can be reasonable with multidisciplinary agreement.
- Shared decision‑making and heart team approval are essential given the off‑label complexity.

Clinical pearls and takeaways
- Preprocedural CT planning is mandatory for layered‑frame interventions — size, angulation, valve‑to‑coronary distance and expected neo‑skirt location drive strategy.
- Valve selection in redo cases should be strategic: favor supra‑annular leaflets and designs that facilitate commissural alignment and lower external frame height when the landing zone is tight.
- Commissural alignment is functional, not cosmetic — it preserves coronary access, reduces neo‑skirt obstruction and can improve effective orifice area.
- High implantation to pin prior leaflets outward is a useful tactic to reduce PPM in frame‑on‑frame cases, but requires CT confirmation and careful execution.
- Immediate high gradients post‑implant can improve over weeks after ballooning and frame settling; integrate echo trends with clinical status before reintervening.
- Prior treated PVE is not an absolute contraindication to TAV‑in‑TAV procedures—if infection is resolved, surgical risk is prohibitive, and CT shows feasibility, a carefully planned transcatheter approach can be durable.

This case shows that with deliberate valve choice (Acurate neo2), CT‑driven commissural alignment, high implantation strategy and targeted balloon optimization, even a triple‑stented aortic root can be treated percutaneously with a sustained good outcome at 1 year. As we treat more patients earlier in life, thinking in "lifetime strategy" terms—what we implant today and how it will affect tomorrow’s options—matter more than ever.



## Coronary Access in a Steel Cage: Future PCI after Multiple TAVRs

What grabbed my attention about the case from Merkulov et al. was not just that a patient survived a TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV after treated endocarditis — it’s that the team planned the third implant with coronary access and lifetime management explicitly in mind. When you stack stent frames and leaflets inside the aortic root, you’re not just solving today’s valve problem; you’re building a steel cage around the coronary ostia. That cage can make tomorrow’s PCI extremely challenging or impossible unless you plan for it up front.

This patient had a 2014 Lotus, a 2022 Evolut inside it, then an Acurate neo2 in 2024. Pre‑procedural MSCT drove valve choice, implantation depth and commissural alignment so the Acurate’s supra‑annular leaflets could minimise neo‑skirt formation and preserve the valve‑to‑coronary relationship. The team avoided coronary protection based on CT modelling — and at 1 year the valve performs well with preserved coronary flow. That outcome highlights the power of deliberate imaging and strategy when face‑painting a future access problem.

Why coronary access matters after multiple TAVRs
- Multiple overlapping frames raise two central problems: physical obstruction of catheter engagement by frame struts and obstruction of coronary flow by a new neo‑skirt created when leaflets/frames are pushed outwards.
- Even if immediate coronary flow is preserved, the metal mesh and displaced leaflets can prevent selective engagement, catheter support, or delivery of devices (balloons, stents, thrombectomy catheters).
- Patients with prior coronary disease — like this case — are the ones most likely to need PCI later, so preserving reaccess is not academic.

Pre‑procedure work: imaging and planning that actually change the implant
This case shows what excellent planning looks like and what you should be ordering and reviewing before a redo TAVR:
- High‑resolution gated MSCT with 3D reconstructions to assess sinus of Valsalva dimensions, coronary ostial height, valve‑to‑coronary distance, and the spatial relationship of prior frames & leaflets.
- Virtual valve implantation and 3D modelling when possible to predict the neo‑skirt and whether commissural posts will land in front of ostia.
- Ask explicitly: will a new valve create a sealed neo‑skirt above the coronary ostium? Is there enough sinus clearance to permit a coronary catheter to reach the ostium through the frame?
- Use CT to decide valve type and target implantation depth: choose devices and positions that minimise frame height across the ostia and favour supra‑annular leaflets when haemodynamics in narrow landing zones matter.

Techniques to preserve coronary access at implant
- Commissural alignment: rotate and orient the transcatheter valve so posts/struts don’t align with coronary ostia. Not perfect, but it reduces the chance of direct obstruction and helps future catheter navigation.
- High implantation can limit neo‑skirt encroachment on the sinotubular junction — but balance this against risk of valve embolisation or paravalvular leak.
- Valve choice matters: low‑frame, supra‑annular designs (used here: Acurate neo2) may offer better flow and less metal across the ostia compared with tall stents.
- Consider coronary protection (wires or balloons) during redo TAVR when CT indicates marginal valve‑to‑coronary distance or questionable sinus clearance — placement of a guidewire in the coronary can save a life if acute obstruction occurs.

If PCI is needed later: practical strategies for catheterists facing a jumbled root
When you’re called to the lab years after multiple TAVRs and you find a “steel cage” around the coronaries, here are pragmatic tactics:
- Start with CT review before the case. Know which commissure/post is in front of the ostium, and plan fluoroscopic landmarks.
- Catheter selection: don’t be dogmatic. Standard JR/JL curves can sometimes engage through large fenestrations; if not, try Amplatz left, extra‑back‑up shapes, or more eccentric curves to reach the ostium through struts. Long sheaths and guiding catheters with more support may be needed.
- Use smaller, more maneuverable catheters initially (e.g., 6Fr or 7Fr hockey‑stick/curve hybrids); escalate to 7–8Fr guide with extension (Guideliner or Guidezilla) for device delivery.
- Consider the “through‑strut” technique: cross the appropriate cell (identified fluoroscopically/CT) then pass a small workhorse wire and deliver devices through that cell. This requires patience and sometimes microcatheters.
- Benchmarks for wiring: aim for selective wire access with minimal manipulation; avoid forceful cathetering that could damage frames or displace leaflets.
- If access is impossible via standard femoral approach, don’t forget alternative access: radial (often better torque control) or even direct aortic access in selected centres.

Leaflet modification and protection — when to think BASILICA, snorkel, or chimney
- BASILICA (intentional leaflet laceration) is effective to prevent acute coronary obstruction by splitting leaflet tissue that would otherwise be displaced over an ostium. Its role in redo and triple‑stent situations is less well defined but conceptually useful when native or bioprosthetic leaflets — not just frame mesh — threaten the coronary.
- In serial TAVR where a neo‑skirt is expected and coronaries are at risk, BASILICA or leaflet laceration may be considered preemptively. But caution: leaflet laceration through multiple layers of frames is technically challenging and of unclear durability/risk.
- Snorkel/chimney stenting (pre‑positioning stents either jailed or deployed to preserve flow) is a bailout but comes with long‑term tradeoffs (stent thrombosis, difficult redo).
- If CT suggests unacceptably small valve‑to‑coronary distance despite commissural alignment, consider upfront protection strategies or converting to surgery if feasible.

The evidence gap — what we do and don’t know
- There is sparse data on PCI techniques and outcomes after TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV. Most recommendations are extrapolated from single TAVR or TAV‑in‑TAV literature and case reports (like this one).
- Long‑term consequences of snorkel/chimney stents traversing multiple frames are not well studied.
- Efficacy and safety of BASILICA in triple‑frame anatomies are unproven; procedural planning software and bench testing may help but are not yet standard.
- Trials or registries focused on lifetime valve management, reaccess strategies and outcomes after serial transcatheter interventions are needed.

Take‑home pearls
- Plan for future PCI at the time of each TAVR — especially in patients with known CAD. CT modelling and commissural alignment aren’t optional; they’re mission‑critical.
- Valve selection and implantation depth should be driven not only by immediate haemodynamics but by preserving sinus clearance and limiting neo‑skirt formation.
- If CT suggests compromised valve‑to‑coronary distance, consider protection (wire/balloon), BASILICA, or alternative strategies rather than assuming PCI will be easy later.
- When performing PCI through multiple frames, start with CT‑guided strategy, try multiple catheter shapes, use guide extensions for support, and be ready to use alternative access routes.
- Recognise the evidence gaps: many of these maneuvers are based on case series and expert consensus; document these cases and contribute to registries.

This case is a useful reminder that TAVR is not a one‑time procedure in many patients — it’s a lifetime pathway. If you’re implanting the first valve, think like a future interventionalist: where will my catheter need to go in 5–10 years? Plan the implant to keep that door open.



## When the Access Bites Back: Preventing and Managing Vascular Complications in Redo TAVR

What grabs your attention in this case is the contrast: a patient with two prior TAVIs and a significant access complication after the second (a pulsatile femoral haematoma that evolved into a pseudoaneurysm requiring open vascular repair) went on to have a technically complex third TAVR (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV) with an uneventful vascular course. That drift from “access disaster” to “clean access” is not luck — it’s planning, technique, and bailout preparedness. For the growing number of patients needing redo TAVR, the access strategy can make or break the procedure.

Why redo TAVR access is different
Redo TAVR patients bring a higher baseline risk for vascular complications: previous large-bore access sites with scar tissue, altered iliofemoral anatomy from prior punctures or devices, higher likelihood of peripheral vascular disease, and comorbidities (this patient had diabetes, prior PCI, COPD and prior GI bleeding). The second TAVR in this patient was complicated by a femoral pseudoaneurysm requiring open repair — a reminder that even a previously “used” access can harbor hidden hazards. When planning the third TAVR, the team deliberately used CT-guided planning, left radial for ancillary catheters and optimized femoral strategy to avoid repeating history.

Preprocedural checklist: CT, access history, and multidisciplinary planning
Before you bring the patient to the lab, do the homework.

- CT iliofemoral assessment is mandatory for redo cases. Don’t just report minimal luminal diameter — review calcification pattern (eccentric, circumferential), tortuosity, previous stent/closure device remnants and the true minimal lumen along the intended track. In this case, MSCT was used to map valve anatomy and guided the procedural plan; apply the same rigor to iliofemoral mapping.
- Review prior access sites and complications. Prior pseudoaneurysm, surgical repair or patching changes your approach — consider contralateral, alternative access, or surgical cut-down if anatomy or scar makes percutaneous closure unreliable.
- Include vascular surgery or endovascular specialists in the heart team for high-risk redo access planning. Have them scrubbed or on standby if recent access complications or severe disease exist.

Puncture technique: ultrasound and microsite planning
The old “feel-based” technique doesn’t cut it for redo TAVR.

- Ultrasound-guided puncture is standard. Visualize the common femoral artery (CFA), identify bifurcation, avoid puncturing near previous scars or above a heavily calcified segment. Mark the optimal skin entry point to minimize sheath kink and facilitate device/closure alignment.
- Aim for a mid-CFA puncture (between the inferior epigastric takeoff and the bifurcation). If prior access is scarred or repaired, shift puncture cranially or caudally as needed, but only after reassessing vessel quality on CT and ultrasound.
- Consider micropuncture first and upsizing under wire protection. This reduces the risk of creating a large hematoma from an initial missed arterial stick.

Sheath selection and sheath management
Minimize vessel trauma and preserve options for bailout.

- Use the smallest feasible large-bore sheath compatible with your valve and delivery system. Consider sheathless techniques where appropriate, and plan for sheath orientation to align with closure devices.
- If previous puncture site is close to your intended entry, choose the side with the better vessel on CT and ultrasound even if it means changing operator ergonomics.
- Maintain meticulous hemostasis during upsizing. If resistance or bleeding is noted during dilation, pause and reassess rather than force passage.

Closure strategy: choose wisely and prepare backups
Closure is where prior complications often recur. Have a plan A and plan B.

- Prophylactic percutaneous suture-based closure (e.g., Perclose ProGlide) in a pre-close fashion is common for planned large-bore access. In redo settings, assess whether tissue quality and scar will allow reliable suture capture. If doubtful, have surgical exposure or hybrid options ready.
- Consider combination strategies: pre-close plus adjunctive collagen plug (if compatible), or planned deployment of vascular covered stents for bailout.
- If the CFA is heavily calcified at the puncture site, suture-based devices may not seal; plan for immediate manual compression, prolonged compression devices, or surgical repair.
- In this case the team used percutaneous femoral access with CT guidance and had a clean outcome — likely from careful site selection and closure planning.

Leverage radial access: ancillary catheters and safety lines
Adding radial access can reduce femoral manipulations and provide quick diagnostic/aortic control options.

- Use radial or contralateral femoral access for pigtail catheters, contralateral safety wires, or angiography. In this report the team used left radial and left femoral arterial access — radial for angiography and femoral for the large-bore system — reducing cross-fire and simplifying bailout angiography.
- Radial access gives immediate alternative arterial access for angiography if femoral problems are suspected intra-procedurally.

Intra-procedural vigilance and early recognition of bleeding
Early recognition of hematoma or bleeding changes outcomes.

- Routinely check the groin under sterile drapes after sheath insertion and during manipulation. If swelling is detected, remove drapes to inspect — small expanding hematomas progress fast.
- Monitor hemodynamics closely; unexplained hypotension or tachycardia in the setting of recent large-bore access should trigger rapid femoral evaluation.
- Use completion angiography through the sheath to confirm no perforation or active extravasation before sheath removal in high-risk cases.

Immediate bailouts for hematoma/pseudoaneurysm
Have tools and expertise ready.

- Manual compression and prolonged pressure: still effective for many small hematomas/pseudoaneurysms detected early.
- Ultrasound-guided thrombin injection: first-line percutaneous therapy for femoral pseudoaneurysms with narrow necks and accessible anatomy. Know contraindications (infection, wide-neck lesions communicating freely with the artery).
- Covered stent deployment: excellent for active extravasation or short neck pseudoaneurysms not amenable to thrombin. Needs appropriate device sizing and wire access across the lesion.
- Surgical repair: required when percutaneous options fail or when pseudoaneurysm anatomy is not amenable (infected pseudoaneurysm, open wound, or complex vessel disruption). This patient had open vascular surgery after the prior complication — a reminder that early surgical backup matters.
- Anticoagulation/antiplatelet management: tailor periprocedural anticoagulation to bleeding risk and closure strategy. The case notes recurrent GI bleeding — integrate that history into your plan and discuss with the team.

Post-procedural surveillance and delayed complications
Don’t pack up and go home too fast.

- Inspect groins before discharge and educate nursing staff on signs of expanding hematoma, limb ischemia, or groin swelling.
- Consider routine 24–48 hour duplex in high-risk redo patients or when there was a difficult closure.
- Early outpatient follow-up within a week can catch evolving pseudoaneurysms before they require surgery.

Takeaways — keep bleeding and limb ischemia off your complication list
- Do CT iliofemoral sizing for every redo TAVR and integrate calcification, tortuosity and prior access history into access selection.  
- Use ultrasound-guided micropuncture entry and plan the puncture site to avoid scar, bifurcation and heavy calcification.  
- Pre-close with suture-based devices when appropriate, but have an alternative plan (covered stent, thrombin, surgical repair) if tissue quality is poor.  
- Leverage radial access for ancillary catheters and angiography to reduce femoral manipulation and provide quick diagnostic capability.  
- Maintain low threshold for completing completion angiography and early duplex if anything feels off; early intervention beats delayed surgery.  

This patient’s story illustrates that prior access complications don’t condemn future procedures — they demand better planning. Invest time in CT review, choose your puncture wisely, close with a backup, and have the vascular toolbox ready. Do that and your redo TAVR will be defined by the valve result, not the groin.
