Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

Chapter 3: ## Multimodality Imaging and Preprocedural Planning for TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV

When a patient presents with a failed transcatheter valve and one or more prior transcatheter frames in situ, careful preprocedural planning using multimodality imaging becomes the central determinant of safety and success. The case above—three prior transcatheter devices (Lotus → Evolut → Acurate neo2) with a history of treated prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE)—illustrates how planning is driven by a sequence of specific anatomic questions that can be answered only by targeted CT and echocardiographic assessment, supplemented by procedural testing when indicated. In complex triple‑stented anatomy the planning goals are to (1) determine whether a further transcatheter implant is anatomically feasible, (2) size and select a device that maximises effective orifice area (EOA) while minimising the risk of coronary compromise and severe patient–prosthesis mismatch (PPM), (3) define implantation depth and commissural orientation to preserve coronary access, and (4) decide whether upfront coronary protection or leaflet‑modification (eg, BASILICA) is required.

Core CT measurements and what they mean
Multislice CT (MSCT) is the workhorse for planning TAV-in-TAV procedures and is indispensable in layered‑frame anatomy. The most important CT measurements are:
- The internal or residual lumen diameters of the in‑situ frame(s) at the level of the leaflet tips and at the annular plane: the narrowest internal diameter (often at the plane of the prior valve leaflets) governs the maximal external diameter of a new transcatheter valve and the likely EOA after implantation. In the presented case the minimum lumen diameter in the Evolut frame was 13.7 mm — a critical constraint that determined valve size selection and predicted a high likelihood of residual gradients.
- Aortic annulus dimensions and shape: to confirm that further device expansion will be seated correctly and to detect eccentricity that might predispose to malposition or PVL.
- Sinus of Valsalva dimensions (width and height) and the sinus‑to‑coronary ostium geometry: narrow or shallow sinuses increase the risk that displaced leaflets and frame material will obstruct coronary flow or lead to sinus sequestration.
- Coronary ostial heights from the annular plane and valve‑to‑coronary (VTC) distance: VTC is conceptually the predicted distance between the new valve’s skirt/leaflet edge (after displacement of existing leaflets) and the coronary ostium. A VTC <3–4 mm is widely regarded as high risk for coronary obstruction in valve‑in‑valve scenarios and mandates protective planning.
- Valve‑to‑sinotubular junction (VTS) distance and the height of sinotubular junction relative to the valve: a low sinotubular junction or one that is close to the anticipated neo‑skirt height increases the risk of sinus sequestration (a scenario in which the sinus is functionally excluded from coronary perfusion).
These measurements are obtained from multiplanar reconstructions aligned to the annular plane and to the leaflet/commissural plane. When available, virtual valve implantation software can project the expected location of the new valve frame and leaflet skirt to calculate VTC and predict the neo‑skirt height; if not available, measurements should be made carefully on multiple cross‑sections to approximate these distances.

Echocardiography and infection assessment
Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) remains essential for two purposes before reintervention. First, it documents prosthetic valve haemodynamics, regurgitation mechanism and presence of paravalvular complications. Second, and especially in the setting of prior PVE, TEE is used to exclude active infection: residual vegetations, leaflet destruction, abscesses, dehiscence or prosthetic fistulae are contraindications to elective TAVI until controlled or surgically managed. In the presented case, both TEE and MSCT confirmed absence of vegetations or active paravalvular complications after completion of antibiotic therapy—permitting the team to proceed with a transcatheter strategy. Good practice is also to document sterile blood cultures and clinical stability; timing of reintervention should follow documented infection resolution and multidisciplinary consultation.

Assessing the risk of coronary obstruction and sinus sequestration
The single most feared complication of valve‑in‑valve (and particularly valve‑in‑valve‑in‑valve) procedures is coronary obstruction. The risk is increased by:
- Short VTC distance (<3–4 mm) between the expected neo‑skirt/leaflet plane and the coronary ostium.
- Narrow or shallow sinuses of Valsalva and a low sinotubular junction that limit the space for displaced leaflets.
- Tall native leaflets (or long bioprosthetic leaflets) that when pushed outward create a taller neo‑skirt.
- Multiple overlapping frames that raise the effective height and bulk of the neo‑skirt and further reduce clearance.
CT allows explicit visualization and measurement of these features and, often, virtual simulation of how the new valve will interact with existing leaflets and frames. If the predicted neo‑skirt covers a coronary ostium or the VTC is short, the operator must plan coronary protection (see below) or consider leaflet modification (BASILICA) where anatomically feasible. In layered frames the cumulative frame height and the likelihood of incomplete expansion increase uncertainty, and a conservative approach to coronary protection is warranted when CT margins are borderline.

Commissural alignment and preservation of coronary windows
Commissural alignment of the new valve such that its commissural posts fall between prior posts/leaflets can preserve coronary windows and maintain access for future coronary interventions. Planning commences on CT: identify fluoroscopic markers of the in situ valve commissures, map the coronary ostia relative to the prior commissures and determine an optimal orientation for the new valve. For many contemporary devices there are recommended orientation markers or “alignment techniques” (eg, orientation of the delivery system flush with the C‑marker) that can be rehearsed on table and executed during deployment. In our case, preprocedural CT modelling guided an implantation orientation intended to align the Acurate neo2 commissures with the Evolut leaflet positions to maximise coronary clearance and the available EOA. Commissural alignment becomes even more important when successive frames may otherwise produce an impenetrable mesh overlying the ostia.

Device selection and sizing in restrictive, multi‑frame anatomy
When several stent frames are present, the internal diameter of the most restrictive frame (not the native annulus) should guide valve sizing. Sizing must anticipate that the new valve will be constrained by the preexisting frame and that the effective orifice will be smaller than the external diameter of the implanted THV. Several practical considerations follow:
- Prefer supra‑annular leaflet devices when EOA is paramount. Supra‑annular valves typically give better effective orifice area for a given internal diameter and therefore reduce the severity of PPM risk.
- Prefer devices with lower skirt heights or shorter overall frame height when coronary obstruction or sinus sequestration is of concern—provided the device still offers good supra‑annular EOA.
- Choose valves with delivery systems and frames that support planned commissural orientation.
- In very tight lumens, consider smaller size options but balance the need to avoid severe PPM (indexed EOA thresholds: severe PPM generally considered when indexed EOA ≤0.65 cm^2/m^2) against the risk of coronary obstruction.
In the presented patient, the minimal lumen of 13.7 mm constrained the choice; the team selected an Acurate neo2 (supra‑annular, relatively low frame) and planned high implantation to maximise the residual EOA and reduce the neo‑skirt obstructing the coronaries.

Deciding on coronary protection and leaflet modification
Coronary protection strategies fall broadly into two categories: prophylactic ostial protection and leaflet modification.
- Prophylactic protection: placement of coronary guidewires (with or without undeployed stents) in the at‑risk coronary arteries before valve deployment allows immediate stent deployment (“chimney” stenting) if obstruction occurs. This strategy is relatively straightforward but creates another metallic layer protruding into the aorta and can complicate future interventions.
- Leaflet modification (BASILICA): intentional transcatheter laceration of the at‑risk leaflet(s) to create an open leaflet that will not produce a continuous neo‑skirt is an attractive approach when anatomy allows. However, BASILICA in the setting of multiple prior stent frames is technically more complex and may be limited by lack of catheter space, the presence of metallic frames obstructing laceration, or an inability to position and electrify the guidewire safely. Data on BASILICA in triple‑stented anatomy are scarce.
CT‑based prediction of VTC and neo‑skirt height is the dominant determinant of need for protection. If VTC is comfortably >4 mm and sinuses are roomy, operators may elect to proceed without protection, with a strategy of immediate angiographic assessment during balloon valvuloplasty (the “balloon test”) and ready bailout equipment. In borderline cases (VTC 2–4 mm or other risk features), most teams will perform prepositioned coronary wires and have stents ready to deploy. In the reported case CT predicted sufficient clearance, and the team proceeded without upfront protection but performed balloon predilation under rapid pacing with simultaneous aortography to confirm coronary flow before final valve release—an acceptable strategy when CT margins are reassuring.

Intraoperative testing and procedural tactics
Balloon predilation or a balloon‑occlusion test can provide a real‑time check of coronary patency: simultaneous selective coronary angiography during balloon inflation or aortic root angiography after balloon inflation will reveal dynamic obstruction. Rapid ventricular pacing is used to stabilise the balloon and reduce transvalvular flow. If coronary compromise is observed, an already‑positioned coronary wire and undeployed stent can be deployed immediately. High implantation of the new valve can be planned to keep skirt height below the coronary ostia and to anchor the prosthesis at the level of prior leaflets, as was done in this patient; high implantation can also press the prior leaflets outward and reduce leaflet billowing into the orifice, improving flow. Post‑dilatation with high‑pressure balloons may be required to expand constrained prior frames and reduce gradients, but carries a risk of annular injury and should be balanced against potential benefit. Intentional fracturing of surgical rings (bioprosthetic valve fracture) is possible in surgical valves but is generally not applicable to transcatheter frames.

Access planning and ancillary lines
CT angiography of the iliofemoral arteries is mandatory for access planning. Prior vascular complications (this patient had a femoral pseudoaneurysm and open vascular repair after the second TAVI) affect access choice and mandate careful planning. When femoral access is possible, maintain a contralateral arterial and radial access for angiographic control and coronary monitoring. Radial access for coronary angiography is useful for real‑time evaluation of coronary flow during balloon inflation and valve deployment. Additionally, plan for left ventricular pacing (temporary wire or existing pacemaker) and have peripheral vascular surgical backup if prior repairs or fragility increase risk.

Commissural mapping for long‑term coronary reaccess
Given the patient’s extensive coronary disease, preserving future coronary access was a major objective. Preprocedural CT should be used to map prior commissures and coronaries and to choose a valve and orientation that avoid placing new commissures in front of ostia. Where possible, use devices and deployment techniques that allow predictable alignment of commissural posts (manufacturer guidance and fluoroscopic marks are critical). In layered frames, although perfect alignment may not be obtainable, planned orientation reduces the chance that multiple posts will aggregate over a single coronary ostium.

When prior endocarditis has occurred
A history of PVE adds two additional planning requirements. First, ensure the infection has been definitively treated: negative blood cultures, completed appropriate antibiotic course, and absence of vegetations or prosthetic complications on TEE/CT. Reintervention during unresolved infection increases the risk of recurrent infection and prosthetic seeding. Second, recognize that prior infection may have caused leaflet destruction or fibrosis that changes how leaflets will behave when displaced by a new valve—another reason to use CT and intraoperative balloon testing to predict behaviour.

Advanced planning tools and the heart team
Three‑dimensional printed models and virtual implantation software can be invaluable in extreme cases. These tools allow bench testing of different valve sizes and positions, give visualisation of frame interference and neo‑skirt formation, and can simulate coronary patency. Not all centers have these resources; when available, they should be used for triple‑stented anatomies. Regardless, all decisions—device choice, implantation depth, coronary protection strategy—should be reviewed by a multidisciplinary heart team including interventional cardiology, cardiac imaging, structural heart surgery, and infectious diseases (when prior PVE is present).

A practical stepwise preprocedural checklist
1. Confirm clinical stability and infection control: negative blood cultures, completed therapy per ID, no vegetations/abscess on TEE.
2. MSCT with annular plane alignment and virtual valve projection: measure internal lumen diameters, annulus, sinuses, coronary heights, VTC and VTS.
3. Evaluate PPM risk: use indexed EOA predictions and choose supra‑annular valves where possible.
4. Decide device and size based on smallest internal diameter and desire for supra‑annular leaflet position; plan commissural orientation.
5. Determine need for coronary protection or BASILICA based on VTC (<3–4 mm borderline), sinus size, leaflet length, and layered frame height.
6. Plan vascular access with CT angiography; prepare alternative access and have vascular surgical backup if prior complications exist.
7. Prepare intraoperative strategy: arterial and radial lines, prophylactic coronary wiring if indicated, equipment for chimney stenting, rapid‑pacing wire, and ready‑to‑perform balloon test.
8. Obtain heart team signoff and document shared decision‑making with the patient/family.
9. Post‑procedure: early TTE and clinical surveillance; plan serial follow‑up imaging to detect gradients, PPM and recurrent infection.

Applying these principles to the case
In the patient presented, systematic CT measurement identified a critically small internal lumen of the Evolut frame (13.7 mm) but also demonstrated adequate VTC and sufficiently large sinuses to justify proceeding without upfront coronary protection. The team chose a supra‑annular, alignment‑friendly Acurate neo2 and planned a high implant to fix the prior leaflets against the stent frame, reduce neo‑skirt height and maximise EOA. Balloon predilation with simultaneous angiography served as an intraoperative safety test of coronary patency. Post‑deployment, immediate gradients were high but improved over the first month as the frames partially expanded and optimisation (post‑dilatation) took effect—an expected phenomenon in constrained, layered frames. The thorough preprocedural exclusion of active endocarditis by TEE and CT, and the multidisciplinary planning that followed, were critical prerequisites for proceeding with a third transcatheter implant in this high‑risk patient.

In summary, successful TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV requires meticulous CT‑ and echo‑guided planning that addresses internal lumen sizing, coronary clearance (VTC), sinus anatomy, commissural alignment, device selection and access strategy. Where measurements suggest small margins for error, prophylactic coronary protection or leaflet‑modification strategies should be employed. Multidisciplinary review, CT‑based simulation when available, and careful intraoperative testing are the cornerstones of a safe, reproducible approach to serial transcatheter aortic valve interventions.
