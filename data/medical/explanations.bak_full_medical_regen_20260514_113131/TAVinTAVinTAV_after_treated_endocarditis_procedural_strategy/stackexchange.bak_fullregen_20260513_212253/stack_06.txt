Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

### [Management] This team selected a self-expanding, supra-annular Acurate neo2 valve and aimed for high implantation with commissural alignment at the level of the prior Evolut leaflets. What device and deployment features make this strategy advantageous in a triple-stented anatomy, and what are the intended haemodynamic and coronary-access benefits?

Choose a valve and a deployment technique to solve two linked problems created by layered prostheses: (1) a narrowed inflow caused by inward‑folded or constrained prior leaflets that reduces effective orifice area and raises gradients, and (2) the risk that a new neo‑skirt or additional frame will cover the coronary ostia and make future coronary access impossible.

The team’s choice of a self‑expanding, supra‑annular Acurate neo2 and a high, commissure‑aligned implant addresses both problems by design and by deployment mechanics. Device features that matter here are the supra‑annular leaflet position (leaflets sit above the frame), a relatively low and open frame with radiopaque commissural markers, and a top‑down controlled deployment that facilitates precise axial and rotational positioning. As a self‑expanding valve it exerts continuous radial force that can partially expand and re‑shape the existing Evolut frame without the shock of a rigid balloon fracture; controlled post‑dilation can then augment expansion. The low frame/short skirt and the ability to orient commissures relative to the prior valve leaflets permit a deliberate “high” landing at the level of the Evolut leaflets so that the old leaflets are pressed outward and become part of the flow lumen rather than forming a new obstructing skirt.

The intended haemodynamic benefits follow directly: supra‑annular leaflets and fixation at the prior‑leaflet level maximize the residual effective orifice area, reduce leaflet infolding, and improve leaflet excursion, all of which lower transvalvular gradients and reduce the risk of severe prosthesis‑patient mismatch. The intended coronary‑access benefits derive from minimizing a neo‑skirt below the coronary ostia, keeping the sinuses and valve‑to‑coronary distances more favourable, and aligning commissures so catheter engagement of the coronaries is less impeded by overlapping struts. In short, this strategy trades precise axial/rotational control and favorable leaflet geometry for improved immediate haemodynamics and higher likelihood of safe, repeat coronary interventions in a triple‑stented anatomy.

A final practical caveat: even with these features and meticulous CT‑guided alignment, cumulative mesh burden still poses residual risk to coronary access and may complicate future interventions, so the strategy is mitigation, not elimination, of those long‑term risks.
