Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

### [Clinical Pearls] For TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV planning, which preprocedural CT measurements and anatomic relationships most strongly predict risk of coronary obstruction, and what threshold values would prompt you to consider coronary protection or leaflet modification (e.g., BASILICA)?

The critical question in any repeat TAV procedure — and especially in a TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV situation — is not simply “how tall are the coronaries?” but “what will the new valve and the displaced old leaflets/frames do to the ostia in three dimensions?” Pre‑procedural CT must therefore move beyond single numbers to a small set of measurements and relationships that best predict displacement of leaflet tissue or formation of a neo‑skirt that will obstruct coronary flow.

First, measure and simulate a virtual valve implantation on contrast gated CT (ideally with dedicated valve‑in‑valve software) to generate the predicted neo‑skirt and its relation to the coronaries. From that model the two most predictive metrics are the valve‑to‑coronary (VTC) distance and the expected neo‑skirt height relative to the coronary ostium. A VTC (distance from the anticipated outer leaflet/neo‑skirt to the coronary ostium) <4 mm is high risk for obstruction; 4–6 mm is intermediate risk and should prompt strong consideration of protection or leaflet modification; >6 mm is generally low risk, assuming other features are favorable.

Second, contextualize VTC with coronary ostial height and sinus geometry. Coronary ostial height <10 mm is associated with higher obstruction risk; heights 10–12 mm are borderline and require attention to VTC and sinus size. Small sinuses of Valsalva (SOV) or narrow sinotubular junction (STJ) dramatically increase risk because there is no room for displaced leaflets: SOV diameter <30–32 mm (depending on prosthesis size) and a short or narrow STJ raise concern. If SOV diameter is small and VTC is borderline, treat as high risk.

Third, account for the prior valve(s) anatomy and frame/leaflet configuration. Supra‑annular bioprostheses (e.g., self‑expanding Evolut) with tall leaflets and high stent frames, prior commissural misalignment, and cumulative stacked frames increase the chance that leaflet tissue will be held up against the coronary ostia and form a persistent neo‑skirt. Measure the leaflet length and predicted maximum extent of displaced leaflet tissue; if displaced leaflet reaches at or above the coronary ostium on the simulation, obstruction risk is high even if ostial height alone seems acceptable.

Practical thresholds I use in planning (and that should trigger coronary protection or BASILICA discussion)
- VTC <4 mm: high risk — plan BASILICA (if anatomy and operator experience permit) or mandatory upfront coronary protection with a guidewire/balloon and ready covered stent.  
- VTC 4–6 mm or coronary height 10–12 mm with small SOV (≤32 mm) or narrow STJ: intermediate risk — strongly favor coronary protection and consider BASILICA if simulation predicts leaflet contact; discuss chimney/stent bailout strategy if BASILICA not feasible.  
- Coronary height <10 mm or predicted neo‑skirt at or above ostium regardless of VTC: treat as high risk and favour leaflet laceration (BASILICA) if feasible; if not feasible, plan for protection/backup chimney stenting or reconsider strategy (surgical explant if possible).  
- SOV diameter <30–32 mm or STJ that will trap leaflets: escalate to BASILICA or protection even when VTC borderline.

Other practical considerations that modify decision‑making include commissural alignment (poor alignment increases obstruction and makes future access harder), ability to deliver BASILICA safely across multiple frames, and coronary artery disease status (if coronaries are critical territory, lower threshold for protection). Always perform full CT simulation, include 3‑D printed or virtual fluoroscopic projections when available, and review in the heart team. If BASILICA is planned, ensure the leaflet to be lacerated can be snared/crossed through the stacked frames — when crossing is unlikely, an upfront coronary protection strategy with a prepared covered stent is prudent.

In short: use virtual implantation to get a VTC and neo‑skirt estimate; treat VTC <4 mm (or predicted neo‑skirt at/above ostium, coronary height <10 mm, or small SOV/STJ) as high risk mandating BASILICA or routine coronary protection; treat VTC 4–6 mm or borderline anatomy as intermediate risk and lean toward protection or BASILICA after heart‑team deliberation.
