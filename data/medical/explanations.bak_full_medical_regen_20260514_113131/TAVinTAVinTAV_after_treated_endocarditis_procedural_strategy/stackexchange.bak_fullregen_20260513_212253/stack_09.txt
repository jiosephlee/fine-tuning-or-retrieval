Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

### [Pathophysiology] Describe how balloon pre- and post-dilatation under rapid pacing can improve valve haemodynamics in TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV without intentional frame fracture. What are the mechanical effects on underexpanded frames and constrained leaflets that lower gradients?

Think of the layered TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV as a concentric set of tubes and constrained leaflets: the inner Evolut frame in this case was markedly under‑expanded (minimum lumen ~13–14 mm) and the older leaflets were folded or tethered inward so the effective orifice was tiny. Strategic balloon dilatation before and after the new valve works by simple mechanical principles rather than by “fracturing” metal.

Under rapid ventricular pacing you nearly eliminate forward flow and cardiac motion, which lets you deliver high balloon pressure safely and transiently without violent displacement of wires or devices and without generating large transvalvular pressure gradients. Pre‑dilatation does two things: it mechanically pries open the most stenotic, fibrotic leaflet folds and it exerts radial force on the under‑expanded nitinol frame producing plastic (and elastic) deformation — increasing the internal diameter enough to permit passage and better seating of the new prosthesis. That initial expansion also pushes adherent leaflet tissue and pannus aside, reducing leaflet crowding that would otherwise constrain the new valve.

Once the new supra‑annular valve is deployed, post‑dilatation under pacing accomplishes further, more controlled radial expansion of the composite construct. The balloon transmits force through the new stent into the old frame(s), producing incremental widening of the lumen, flattening of inward‑folded leaflets and improved leaflet separation from the central flow stream. The net mechanical effects are increased effective orifice area, improved leaflet mobility (less restriction/tethering), and a more circular, fuller stent geometry — all of which lower transvalvular velocity and gradient.

Clinically important corollaries are: ballooning can compress fibrous tissue and break adhesive attachments without needing to intentionally fracture the frame; supra‑annular valve selection and high implantation help keep the new leaflets out of the most constrained zone; and pacing is essential to permit high‑pressure, controlled dilation with less embolic or hemodynamic risk. The tradeoffs are the usual ones — risk of coronary compromise, annular injury, valve migration, or conduction disturbance — so careful CT planning, sizing and coronary protection considerations remain mandatory.
