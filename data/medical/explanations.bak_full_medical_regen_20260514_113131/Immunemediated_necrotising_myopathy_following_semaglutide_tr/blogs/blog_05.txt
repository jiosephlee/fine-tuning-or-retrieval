Title: Blog about the case report: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

# Hit Early, Hit Hard: Steroids and IVIG for Severe or Bulbar IMNM

What grabs your attention in the case above is how a single dose of semaglutide was followed—over weeks to months—by a rapidly progressive, biopsy-proven immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) with prominent bulbar features and CK in the thousands, and how the patient recovered after a short, aggressive immunomodulatory course (pulse methylprednisolone then oral prednisone + a single course of IVIG). Whether semaglutide was the trigger or an unlucky temporal association, the take-home clinical lesson is clear: when IMNM presents with severe weakness, dysphagia/dysphonia, respiratory compromise or very high CK, early aggressive therapy — “hit early, hit hard” — can be limb- and life‑saving and may even allow a shorter immunosuppressive course when the offending agent is removed.

Below is a pragmatic, bedside-focused approach to initiating pulse steroids plus IVIG for severe or bulbar IMNM, using the case as an anchoring example.

## When to consider pulse steroids + IVIG up front
Consider early combined therapy when IMNM presents with any of the following:
- Rapidly progressive proximal weakness over days–weeks or new inability to sit/stand
- Bulbar dysfunction (dysphagia, dysphonia) or aspiration risk
- Respiratory compromise or falling vital capacity
- CK markedly elevated (classically >1000 U/L and especially several‑thousand)
- Severe functional decline or evidence of ongoing muscle necrosis on imaging/biopsy
- Clinical suspicion for drug‑triggered IMNM where withdrawal alone may be insufficient

The case illustrates classic indications: progressive proximal weakness, bulbar involvement and CK up to ~4000 U/L prompting pulse steroids and IVIG.

## Practical regimen: dosing and timing
There’s no one proven protocol for IMNM, but reasonable, widely used aggressive regimens are:

- Pulse steroid induction:
  - Methylprednisolone 1000 mg IV daily for 3–5 days (many centers use 5 days for severe disease). This was the regimen used in the case.
- Transition to oral steroids:
  - Prednisone 1 mg/kg/day (or equivalent), started after pulse course.
  - Adjust for age/comorbidity; consider 0.75 mg/kg in frail elderly.
- IVIG:
  - 2 g/kg total dose, delivered as 0.4 g/kg/day over 5 days or 1 g/kg/day for 2 consecutive days (or split as clinically indicated).
  - Give IVIG early—ideally concurrent with or immediately after pulse steroids in severe/bulbar disease.
- Duration:
  - Evaluate response clinically and by CK. If rapid, marked improvement occurs (as in the case), you may be able to wean steroids faster than traditional prolonged courses.
  - Many idiopathic IMNM patients need prolonged immunosuppression; however, suspected drug‑triggered IMNM that responds promptly to withdrawal and initial immunotherapy may permit a shorter steroid course.

## Monitoring during and after treatment
- Strength and function:
  - Frequent bedside strength checks (daily in hospital), timed get‑up tests, and swallow assessments for bulbar patients.
- CK:
  - Baseline, then at least twice weekly inpatient until trending down; outpatient frequency depends on trajectory (e.g., weekly then spacing).
  - Expect CK fall over days–weeks; discordance between CK and strength can occur.
- Respiratory:
  - Baseline and serial forced vital capacity (FVC) if bulbar/respiratory involvement; consider ICU monitoring if FVC declines or bulbar dysfunction is severe.
- Cardiac:
  - Baseline ECG and troponin; consider echo and cardiac MRI if troponin elevated or suspicion for myocarditis (this case had cardiac MRI changes that normalized).
- Labs for therapy safety:
  - CMP (renal function, LFTs), CBC, glucose, electrolytes.
  - With IVIG: watch creatinine, urine output, and signs of volume overload or thrombosis.
- Infection surveillance:
  - Fever workup, cultures as indicated—note that aggressive immunosuppression increases infection risk (this patient had pseudomonal urosepsis during recovery).

## Infection mitigation and general supportive care
- PJP prophylaxis (trimethoprim‑sulfamethoxazole) is indicated if prolonged high‑dose steroids (>20 mg/day prednisone equivalent for >4 weeks) or combined immunosuppression is anticipated.
- Vaccinations:
  - Update vaccines pre‑immunosuppression when possible; avoid live vaccines while immunosuppressed.
- Glycemic control:
  - Monitor capillary glucose closely; steroids commonly cause hyperglycaemia. Treat promptly.
- Thrombosis prophylaxis:
  - Consider pharmacologic DVT prophylaxis while immobile, and remember IVIG can increase thrombotic risk.
- Renal protection with IVIG:
  - Avoid sucrose‑stabilized IVIG in CK/renal impairment; ensure adequate hydration and use slower infusion rates in at‑risk patients.
- Swallow care:
  - Early speech‑language pathology assessment, consider nasogastric feeding if severe dysphagia/aspiration risk.
- Physical therapy and progressive mobilization once safe.

## Tapering steroids — how fast?
Tapering should be individualized:
- If dramatic improvement clinically and CK normalizes quickly (days–weeks), a more rapid taper over 6–8 weeks may be feasible with close follow‑up. The case patient was weaned off prednisone over ~2 months.
- If improvement is slower, relapse occurs, or autoantibody‑positive IMNM is suspected, taper more slowly and consider maintenance immunosuppression.
- Watch for relapse with decreasing steroid dose; rising CK and recurrent weakness indicate need for escalation.

## When to add steroid‑sparing or escalation agents
Add or switch to additional immunotherapy if:
- Incomplete response after initial steroids + IVIG (persistent severe weakness or rising CK)
- Relapse during steroid taper
- Autoantibody‑positive IMNM (eg, anti‑HMGCR or anti‑SRP) often needs combination therapy
Options to consider:
- Rituximab — increasingly used for refractory IMNM, particularly antibody‑mediated disease
- Methotrexate, mycophenolate mofetil, azathioprine — steroid‑sparing agents for maintenance
- Cyclophosphamide — for life‑threatening or organ‑threatening disease (rare)
Choice depends on severity, comorbidities, infection risk, and whether the IMNM appears drug‑triggered (drug‑withdrawal + short aggressive course may obviate prolonged therapy).

## Other diagnostic and safety considerations
- Muscle biopsy and myositis panel remain valuable: biopsy confirms necrotising myofibres and macrophage‑predominant inflammation (as in the case). Note 20–30% of IMNM are seronegative.
- Screen for malignancy appropriately in new‑onset inflammatory myopathy per age and risk factors.
- If a medication trigger is suspected (statins, GLP‑1RA, others), stop the drug and record reaction. Rechallenge is generally avoided.
- Document and counsel patient about future exposure risk to suspected triggers (eg, avoid GLP‑1 receptor agonists in this case).

## When to involve ICU or escalate care
- Respiratory compromise (declining FVC, hypoventilation) or severe bulbar dysfunction with aspiration risk → early ICU involvement.
- Rapid deterioration despite therapy or need for airway protection → intubation and ventilatory support as needed.

## Key clinical takeaways
- IMNM can present rapidly and severely; bulbar involvement and very elevated CK justify early aggressive therapy.
- Combined pulse methylprednisolone (1 g IV daily x3–5 days) plus IVIG (2 g/kg total) is a pragmatic first‑line strategy for severe/bulbar IMNM.
- Start oral prednisone (~1 mg/kg) after pulses and tailor taper to clinical and CK response; some drug‑triggered cases may permit a shorter course.
- Monitor strength, CK, respiratory function, cardiac involvement and complications of immunosuppression closely.
- Mitigate infection risk (PJP prophylaxis if prolonged therapy), monitor glucose and renal function, and provide supportive care (swallowing, thrombosis prophylaxis, PT).
- If inadequate response or relapse, escalate with steroid‑sparing or B‑cell targeted agents (rituximab, methotrexate, mycophenolate, cyclophosphamide as indicated).

This case is a useful reminder: when IMNM presents with severe weakness or bulbar signs, don’t sit and wait. Early combination therapy can change outcomes.
