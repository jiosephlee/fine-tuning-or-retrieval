Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

### [Differential Diagnosis] In a patient with progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia, and a creatine kinase (CK) that rises over months after exposure to a new medication, what clinical and laboratory features should prompt suspicion for immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) rather than toxic rhabdomyolysis?

Think first about the tempo and the tissue response. Toxic rhabdomyolysis is usually an acute toxic or metabolic injury that produces a very rapid rise in CK with myoglobinuria/AKI and then improves promptly after the offending exposure is removed and supportive care is given. Immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) is an autoimmune, subacute to progressive disease that often continues or worsens despite stopping a putative trigger and requires immunotherapy.

Clinically, IMNM should be suspected when weakness is subacute–progressive over weeks to months (rather than abrupt), is symmetric and predominantly proximal (often with neck‑flexor weakness and bulbar symptoms such as dysphagia or dysphonia), and when the patient deteriorates or fails to recover after withdrawal of the new medication. Dysphagia or early respiratory or cardiac involvement (troponin/proBNP abnormalities, myocarditis features) points toward an inflammatory myopathy rather than simple toxic rhabdomyolysis.

Laboratory and ancillary features that favour IMNM include a persistently elevated CK that remains high or rises over weeks–months rather than a single dramatic spike that rapidly falls with hydration; modest to marked elevations in ESR/CRP; myopathic changes on EMG with irritability; muscle MRI showing symmetric proximal muscle oedema; and, most definitively, muscle biopsy showing widespread myofibre necrosis with macrophage/myophagocytosis, membrane‑attack‑complex deposition and relative paucity of lymphocytic infiltrates. The presence of myositis‑specific autoantibodies (anti‑HMGCR, anti‑SRP) supports IMNM when positive, but up to one‑third of IMNM patients are seronegative, so a negative panel does not exclude the diagnosis. By contrast, toxic rhabdomyolysis typically shows necrosis without the immune activation pattern (complement deposition, macrophage‑predominant inflammatory response) and resolves after stopping the toxin and supportive care.

Finally, the therapeutic response is informative: improvement with immunosuppression and IVIG after drug cessation supports an immune‑mediated process, whereas uncomplicated toxic rhabdomyolysis improves with withdrawal and supportive measures alone. In practice, when you see progressive proximal weakness, bulbar involvement, persistent or rising CK over months after exposure to a new drug, inflammatory markers or cardiac involvement, and biopsy/MRI/EMG findings as above, treat and investigate for IMNM rather than attributing the syndrome to simple toxic rhabdomyolysis.
