Introduction

Hepatic fibrosis is a wound-healing response to chronic liver injury characterized by excessive deposition of extracellular matrix (ECM) and architectural distortion that, when progressive, culminates in cirrhosis. Understanding fibrosis requires integrating molecular and cellular mechanisms of matrix production and remodeling with the spectrum of etiologies that chronically injure the liver and drive fibrogenesis. Clinical practice demands accurate, ideally noninvasive, assessment of fibrosis stage because stage, more than the underlying etiology, predicts complications (portal hypertension, hepatocellular carcinoma), prognosis, and need for surveillance. This chapter reviews the cellular drivers of fibrosis, major and less common causes of chronic liver disease, telomere-related liver disease, noninvasive fibrosis and steatosis tests (with strengths and limitations), imaging features of cirrhosis and portal hypertension, and the principal complications of cirrhosis together with contemporary surveillance recommendations.

# Cellular drivers of fibrosis (stellate cells, TGF-β signaling, ECM deposition)

Fibrosis reflects a net increase in hepatic ECM synthesis that overwhelms physiologic matrix degradation. The dominant cellular mediator is the hepatic stellate cell (HSC), a perisinusoidal, vitamin A–storing cell in the space of Disse. In the healthy liver, HSCs are quiescent, store retinoids, and contribute to ECM homeostasis. Following repeated or persistent hepatocellular injury, sinusoidal endothelial cell dysfunction, parenchymal necrosis/apoptosis, and inflammatory cell recruitment generate a profibrogenic milieu that activates HSCs. Activation is a transdifferentiation to myofibroblast-like cells: HSCs lose retinoid droplets, upregulate alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), increase proliferative and contractile capacity, and markedly increase production of fibrillar collagens (type I and type III), fibronectin, and other ECM components.

Transforming growth factor–β (TGF-β), particularly TGF-β1, is the central profibrogenic cytokine. It is produced by injured hepatocytes, Kupffer cells (resident macrophages), platelets, and activated HSCs themselves, establishing autocrine and paracrine loops. TGF-β signals predominantly through the canonical SMAD pathway: ligand binding to TGF-β receptors type I/II leads to phosphorylation of SMAD2/3, association with SMAD4, and nuclear translocation to regulate transcription of pro-fibrotic genes (COL1A1, COL3A1, TIMP1). Noncanonical pathways (MAPK, PI3K/AKT, Rho-like GTPases) modulate TGF-β effects and contribute to HSC survival, proliferation, and contractility. Other cytokines and growth factors — platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), connective tissue growth factor (CTGF), interleukin-13, and endothelin-1 — further stimulate HSC proliferation and matrix production.

ECM accumulation in fibrosis is not merely increased synthesis but a shift in composition and impaired degradation. Healthy liver ECM is a delicate basement membrane–like network (glycoproteins, laminin, collagen IV). Fibrotic liver ECM contains abundant cross-linked fibrillar collagens (type I and III), elastin, and proteoglycans, which stiffen the liver and alter cell-matrix signaling. Matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) normally degrade ECM; tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases (TIMPs), particularly TIMP-1 and TIMP-2, are upregulated during chronic injury, tipping the balance toward net matrix accumulation. Cross-linking enzymes such as lysyl oxidase (LOX) and transglutaminases stabilize collagen fibrils, making fibrosis less reversible.

Persistent fibrosis leads to architectural distortion: fibrous septa subdivide the hepatic parenchyma into regenerative nodules — the defining lesion of cirrhosis. Cirrhosis is associated with sinusoidal capillarization, altered hepatocyte polarity and bile flow, and increased intrahepatic vascular resistance due to both structural (fibrous septa, nodule compression) and dynamic (HSC contraction, endothelial dysfunction with reduced nitric oxide) components. These changes underlie portal hypertension and its clinical sequelae.

# Etiologies: viral hepatitis, alcohol, NAFLD/NASH, autoimmune, metabolic, genetic/inherited causes

Hepatic fibrosis arises from a wide range of chronic injurious stimuli. Distinct etiologies have characteristic natural histories, patterns of injury, and potential for reversal, but fibrosis pathway activation converges on shared profibrogenic mechanisms.

Viral hepatitis: Chronic infection with hepatitis B virus (HBV) or hepatitis C virus (HCV) is a common global cause of fibrosis and cirrhosis. Chronic HCV typically causes progressive parenchymal inflammation and fibrosis over decades, with genotype, coexisting metabolic disease, alcohol use, and age at infection influencing progression. Chronic HBV has a variable course; high viral replication and active inflammation increase fibrogenesis, while effective antiviral therapy reduces progression and can lead to regression of fibrosis and reduced hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) risk (but does not eliminate it in those with advanced fibrosis). Hepatitis D virus (HDV) superinfection accelerates progression to cirrhosis.

Alcohol: Alcoholic liver disease results from chronic excessive alcohol intake with a spectrum from steatosis to steatohepatitis to fibrosis and cirrhosis. Mechanisms include hepatocyte oxidative stress, acetaldehyde-mediated adduct formation, mitochondrial dysfunction, endotoxin-mediated Kupffer cell activation, and induction of stellate cells. The risk of fibrosis increases with both quantity and duration of alcohol consumption; patterns of drinking, female sex, and coexisting metabolic or viral liver disease amplify risk. Abstinence can halt progression and may permit regression of fibrosis.

NAFLD/NASH: Nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) is strongly linked to obesity, insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, and metabolic syndrome. NAFLD ranges from simple steatosis (low short-term risk of progression) to nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), characterized by hepatocyte ballooning, lobular inflammation, and necrosis, which predisposes to progressive fibrosis. Fibrosis stage is the principal determinant of outcomes in NAFLD. Pathophysiology involves lipotoxicity (free fatty acids, toxic lipid species), mitochondrial dysfunction, endoplasmic reticulum stress, inflammatory cytokines, and gut-liver axis alterations. Approximately 20%–30% of individuals with NAFLD develop NASH, and among those with NASH a significant proportion progress to advanced fibrosis.

Autoimmune: Autoimmune hepatitis (AIH) causes chronic immune-mediated hepatocellular inflammation with interface hepatitis and plasma cell–rich infiltrates. If untreated or undertreated, ongoing inflammation leads to progressive fibrosis and cirrhosis. Primary biliary cholangitis (PBC) and primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC) produce cholestatic injury: chronic bile duct loss (PBC) or large-duct fibrosis/strictures (PSC) leads to periportal fibrosis and eventually cirrhosis. Biliary fibrosis tends to be portal-based initially, progressing to bridging fibrosis and cirrhosis; cholestasis itself is pro-fibrogenic via toxic bile acids, ductular reaction, and portal inflammation.

Metabolic: Heritable or acquired metabolic disorders cause fibrosis through specific biochemical insults. Hemochromatosis (HFE-related and non-HFE) produces iron overload with hepatocellular iron deposition, oxidative injury, and risk of cirrhosis and HCC. Wilson disease (ATP7B mutation) causes copper accumulation and hepatocellular injury, including fulminant hepatitis and chronic progressive liver disease. Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency (SERPINA1 mutant Z allele) causes misfolded A1AT accumulation within hepatocytes, ER stress, and potential early-onset cirrhosis. Glycogen storage disorders and a variety of less common metabolic defects can also lead to fibrosis.

Genetic/inherited causes: In addition to the metabolic disorders above, other inherited diseases cause progressive fibrosis. Progressive familial intrahepatic cholestasis (PFIC) subtypes produce early cholestasis and biliary fibrosis. Congenital hepatic fibrosis is associated with ductal plate malformation and conditions such as autosomal recessive polycystic kidney disease (ARPKD). These conditions usually present in childhood or young adulthood and have distinct histologic and clinical patterns.

When evaluating a patient with chronic liver disease, a systematic approach to etiology is essential: history for alcohol and exposures; serologic testing for HBV, HCV, autoimmune markers (ANA, SMA, IgG, AMA), metabolic screens (iron studies, ceruloplasmin, alpha-1 antitrypsin), and imaging for biliary disease. Identifying and treating the underlying cause can arrest or sometimes reverse fibrosis.

# Telomere-related liver disease: associations with TBDs

Telomeres protect chromosome ends, and telomerase maintains telomere length in replicating cells. Telomere biology disorders (TBDs), caused by germline mutations in genes such as TERT, TERC, DKC1, RTEL1, PARN, and others, lead to accelerated telomere shortening and impaired regenerative capacity in high-turnover tissues. Classically recognized for mucocutaneous features (dyskeratosis congenita), bone marrow failure, and pulmonary fibrosis, TBDs are increasingly recognized as a cause of liver disease.

In the liver, telomere dysfunction impairs hepatocyte proliferative reserve and predisposes to liver injury and fibrosis. Clinical manifestations include cryptogenic cirrhosis, noncirrhotic portal hypertension (nodular regenerative hyperplasia), recurrent hepatic steatosis with rapid progression, and an increased risk of vascular complications post–liver resection or transplant due to poor regenerative response. Telomere-related liver disease may present in middle age or later, often in association with other telomere syndrome features (pancytopenia, pulmonary fibrosis) or with an autosomal dominant family history of early stringency diseases. Liver disease in TBDs may progress more rapidly and may be associated with treatment-related complications — for example, enhanced bone marrow toxicity from azathioprine, or poor recovery after hepatic surgery.

Recognition of a potential telomere syndrome is important clinically because it affects management and counseling: genetic testing for telomerase-related mutations can be considered when cryptogenic cirrhosis occurs with cytopenias, pulmonary fibrosis, premature graying, or family history of similar findings. In candidates for liver transplantation, the presence of a TBD influences perioperative risk assessment and decisions about combined organ transplantation (e.g., lung), immunosuppressive regimens, and long-term follow-up for multiorgan involvement.

# Noninvasive tests: transient elastography (liver stiffness), controlled attenuation parameter for steatosis, serum fibrosis markers

Liver biopsy is the classical gold standard for staging fibrosis, but it is invasive, subject to sampling error and interobserver variability, and impractical for serial monitoring in many patients. Noninvasive assessment has therefore become central to clinical care. These methods include elastography-based imaging to measure liver stiffness, ultrasound-based controlled attenuation parameter (CAP) for steatosis, and serum biomarker panels.

Transient elastography (TE), commonly performed with the FibroScan device, measures liver stiffness in kilopascals (kPa) as a surrogate for fibrosis. A small shear wave is generated and its velocity through the liver is measured; faster velocity corresponds to greater stiffness. Liver stiffness measurement (LSM) thresholds vary by etiology, inflammation, and comorbidities and must be interpreted in clinical context. General guideline values often used (with caveats) include: for chronic hepatitis C, F2 (significant fibrosis) approximately ≥7.1 kPa, F3 ≥9.5 kPa, and F4 (cirrhosis) ≥12.5 kPa. For chronic hepatitis B and NAFLD, cutoffs differ and are sometimes slightly higher because steatosis and elevated transaminases increase stiffness independent of fibrosis. In NAFLD, thresholds for advanced fibrosis (≥F3) are commonly around 9–10 kPa and for cirrhosis around 12–14 kPa. Factors that can falsely elevate LSM include acute inflammation (ALT markedly elevated), cholestasis, hepatic congestion (heart failure), postprandial state, and biliary obstruction; obesity may reduce measurement reliability unless the XL probe is used. Low kPa values reliably exclude advanced fibrosis, and serial increases/decreases can track progression or regression.

Controlled attenuation parameter (CAP) is measured simultaneously with TE and quantifies ultrasound attenuation; attenuation increases with hepatic fat. CAP is reported in decibels per meter (dB/m). Suggested CAP thresholds for steatosis degree are approximate because device, population, and probe affect cutoffs; commonly cited values are: S1 (≥5% steatosis) ≈ 238 dB/m, S2 ≈ 260 dB/m, and S3 ≈ 290 dB/m. CAP is useful for detecting and quantifying steatosis noninvasively but is influenced by BMI, diabetes, and probe selection.

Serum fibrosis markers include simple, readily available indices and proprietary panels. Commonly used simple indices are APRI and FIB-4.

- APRI (aspartate aminotransferase to platelet ratio index) is calculated as APRI = (AST / AST upper limit of normal) / platelet count (10^9/L) × 100. An APRI >1 suggests significant fibrosis and >2 suggests cirrhosis in some contexts, though cutoffs and accuracy vary by etiology.

- FIB-4 uses age, AST, ALT, and platelet count: FIB-4 = (Age [years] × AST [U/L]) / (platelets [10^9/L] × sqrt(ALT [U/L])). For patients <65 years, FIB-4 <1.3 has high negative predictive value to exclude advanced fibrosis, whereas FIB-4 >2.67 is suggestive of advanced fibrosis. In older patients thresholds need adjustment (higher cutoff to retain specificity).

Proprietary and research-grade panels include FibroTest/FibroSure (apolipoprotein A1, haptoglobin, GGT, total bilirubin, α2-macroglobulin with age and sex adjustments), ELF (enhanced liver fibrosis panel — hyaluronic acid, procollagen III N-terminal peptide, TIMP-1), and other multiplex biomarker models. These have varying accuracy and availability, and many combine well with elastography in a sequential testing strategy: use inexpensive serum tests to rule out advanced disease, perform elastography for indeterminate cases, and reserve liver biopsy for discrepant or high-stakes situations.

Clinical decision points: Many guidelines advocate a stepwise approach — for example, use FIB-4 or NFS (NAFLD fibrosis score) in primary care to stratify risk; patients with low scores can be reassured and monitored, while those with indeterminate or high scores proceed to elastography and/or specialty referral. Cutoffs should be interpreted by etiology, age, and context. Serial noninvasive testing is useful to monitor therapy response (e.g., viral suppression in HBV, cure of HCV, weight loss or pharmacotherapy in NASH) since fibrosis can regress.

Limitations of noninvasive tests include reduced accuracy in the intermediate fibrosis stages, confounding by inflammation or congestion, and variable performance across etiologies. Combining modalities improves diagnostic yield.

# Imaging features of cirrhosis and portal hypertension (ultrasound, CT, MRI)

Imaging assesses morphological changes of cirrhosis, signs of portal hypertension, the biliary tree, and focal lesions (including HCC). Ultrasound is usually the first-line modality because it is widely available, inexpensive, and free of ionizing radiation. CT and MRI provide higher resolution, better characterization of focal lesions, and vascular assessment.

On ultrasound, the cirrhotic liver may appear nodular with irregular surface contour, heterogeneous echotexture, and segmental volume redistribution. Classic morphologic changes include caudate lobe hypertrophy and relative left lobe hypertrophy, with right lobe atrophy in advanced disease. Sonographic evidence of portal hypertension includes splenomegaly (commonly >12 cm; clinical significance increases with larger spleen size), dilated portal vein (diameter typically >13 mm suggests portal hypertension), diminished respiratory variation of portal vein flow, and portosystemic collaterals (recanalized paraumbilical vein, splenorenal shunt). Ascites—anechoic free fluid in dependent peritoneal spaces—is readily detected. Doppler ultrasound assesses portal vein velocity (normal ~20–40 cm/s; markedly reduced or reversed flow indicates advanced portal hypertension), hepatic artery resistive indices, and patency of hepatic and portal veins.

CT imaging shows surface nodularity, mosaic or heterogeneous parenchymal enhancement, lobe size changes (caudate hypertrophy), and signs of portal hypertension such as splenomegaly, portosystemic collaterals, and varices. Multiphase contrast-enhanced CT is essential for detecting and characterizing focal liver lesions; HCC typically shows arterial-phase hyperenhancement with washout on portal venous/delayed phases. CT can detect portal vein thrombosis, collaterals, and ascites, and quantify liver/spleen volumes for surgical planning.

MRI provides superior soft-tissue contrast and multiphasic dynamic imaging without radiation. MRI with hepatobiliary-specific contrast agents (e.g., gadoxetate disodium) enhances detection and characterization of focal lesions, including early HCC. MR elastography (MRE) is an MRI-based stiffness measurement with higher technical success and greater diagnostic accuracy than TE, especially in obese patients and those with ascites; thresholds vary by etiology but MRE is highly accurate for detecting advanced fibrosis and cirrhosis. MRI can also assess biliary pathology (MRCP) and hepatic iron or fat quantification (R2*, proton density fat fraction) for metabolic or iron-overload disorders.

Imaging also reveals complications: HCC detection relies on size and classic vascular patterns; portal vein thrombosis or cavernous transformation may be seen; hepatic hydrothorax or spontaneous bacterial peritonitis are suggested by pleural effusion or complex ascites. Recognition of these features guides surveillance and management decisions.

# Complications of cirrhosis and surveillance recommendations

Cirrhosis is a systemic disease with multisystem complications driven by portal hypertension, hepatic synthetic and metabolic dysfunction, and immune dysregulation. Important complications include portal hypertension with variceal bleeding and ascites, hepatic encephalopathy, hepatorenal and hepatopulmonary syndromes, coagulopathy and infection susceptibility, and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). Surveillance strategies aim to detect treatable complications early.

Portal hypertension: Progressive fibrosis and architectural distortion increase intrahepatic vascular resistance. Clinically significant portal hypertension (CSPH) is often defined by hepatic venous pressure gradient (HVPG) ≥10 mmHg; variceal bleeding risk notably increases with HVPG ≥12 mmHg. Manifestations include varices, splenomegaly, ascites, and portosystemic shunts. Screening endoscopy (esophagogastroduodenoscopy, EGD) is recommended for patients with established cirrhosis to detect esophageal or gastric varices. If noninvasive markers reliably indicate low risk (e.g., platelet count >150 × 10^9/L and liver stiffness below validated thresholds), some guidelines allow deferral of endoscopy. For medium/large varices, primary prophylaxis with nonselective beta-blockers (propranolol, nadolol, carvedilol) or endoscopic variceal ligation reduces bleeding risk. Acute variceal hemorrhage is a medical emergency with resuscitation, vasoactive drugs (terlipressin, somatostatin analogs), endoscopic therapy, and consideration of early TIPS in high-risk patients.

Ascites: Portal hypertension and sodium retention lead to ascitic fluid accumulation. Cirrhotic ascites is managed with sodium restriction and diuretics (spironolactone with loop diuretics), serial paracenteses for tense ascites, and consideration of TIPS in refractory cases. Diagnostic paracentesis is indicated for new or worsening ascites to assess for spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP); ascitic fluid polymorphonuclear leukocyte count ≥250 cells/mm^3 or positive culture diagnoses SBP and requires prompt antibiotics.

Hepatic encephalopathy (HE): HE results from impaired hepatic clearance of neurotoxins (ammonia and others), portosystemic shunting, and neuroinflammation. Clinical spectrum ranges from minimal HE to coma. Management emphasizes identifying precipitating factors (infection, gastrointestinal bleeding, electrolyte disturbances), reducing ammonia production (lactulose, rifaximin), and supportive care.

Hepatorenal syndrome and hepatopulmonary syndrome: These are severe systemic complications. Hepatorenal syndrome is a functional renal failure due to splanchnic vasodilation and renal vasoconstriction; treatment includes vasoconstrictor therapy (terlipressin plus albumin) and consideration for liver transplantation. Hepatopulmonary syndrome is characterized by intrapulmonary vascular dilatations leading to hypoxemia; liver transplantation is the definitive therapy.

Coagulopathy and infections: Cirrhosis entails complex hemostatic alterations (reduced clotting factors, thrombocytopenia, and compensatory changes). Patients are susceptible to bacterial infections because of immune dysfunction, and infections precipitate decompensation. Vaccination (hepatitis A and B if susceptible, pneumococcus, influenza) is advised.

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC): Cirrhosis is the major risk factor for HCC across most etiologies. Surveillance aims to detect HCC at an early stage when curative therapies are possible. Standard surveillance for patients with cirrhosis is abdominal ultrasound every 6 months, with or without serum alpha-fetoprotein (AFP). This interval balances tumor doubling time and test performance. In patients with higher HCC risk (e.g., cirrhosis due to HBV, metabolic risk factors), many centers include AFP as an adjunct; MRI or CT may be used when ultrasound is suboptimal (obesity, nodular parenchyma). Lesions detected on surveillance should be characterized with multiphase CT or MRI with liver-specific contrast per established imaging criteria (e.g., arterial phase hyperenhancement and washout). Surveillance is not recommended for noncirrhotic NAFLD except in those with advanced fibrosis but without cirrhosis a tailored approach may be considered.

Surveillance and preventive measures: Key recommendations for patients with cirrhosis include routine HCC surveillance (ultrasound ± AFP every 6 months), periodic endoscopic screening for varices at diagnosis with repeat intervals guided by baseline findings and ongoing risk (platelet trends, stiffness measures), annual vaccination status review and vaccination where appropriate, and monitoring for decompensation signs (routine clinical assessment, labs). Laboratory monitoring includes complete blood count (look for thrombocytopenia), liver biochemistry (AST, ALT, alkaline phosphatase, bilirubin), synthetic function (albumin, INR), and renal function/electrolytes when clinically indicated. Transition from compensated to decompensated cirrhosis marks a major prognostic turning point, and patients with decompensation should be evaluated for transplantation candidacy.

Prognostic tools and decision points: Child-Pugh score and Model for End-Stage Liver Disease (MELD) score remain widely used to quantify severity and prioritize transplantation. Child-Pugh incorporates bilirubin, albumin, INR, ascites, and encephalopathy; MELD (and MELD-Na) uses objective labs (bilirubin, creatinine, INR, sodium) and better predicts short-term mortality and transplantation need. In clinical practice, rising MELD or repeated decompensation prompts consideration of transplant referral.

Conclusion

Hepatic fibrosis is the common pathway of chronic liver injury from diverse etiologies. Molecular and cellular mechanisms—principally HSC activation and TGF-β–driven ECM deposition—explain the structural and hemodynamic consequences of fibrosis and cirrhosis. Identifying the underlying cause is essential because etiology-specific therapy can arrest or reverse fibrosis. Noninvasive tests including transient elastography, CAP, serum biomarkers, and advanced imaging have transformed fibrosis assessment, enabling stratified care and surveillance without routine biopsy. Recognizing telomere biology disorders as an underappreciated contributor to cryptogenic liver disease is important for comprehensive evaluation. Finally, appreciating the complications of cirrhosis and applying guideline-based surveillance (HCC every 6 months, endoscopic variceal assessment and prophylaxis decisions based on variceal size and noninvasive markers, vaccination, and transplant evaluation for decompensation) are central to optimizing outcomes in patients with advanced liver disease.

Appendix: commonly used normal reference ranges and clinical cutoffs

- AST: approximately 10–40 U/L; ALT: approximately 7–56 U/L (lab-dependent).
- Alkaline phosphatase: approximately 44–147 U/L.
- Total bilirubin: approximately 0.1–1.2 mg/dL.
- Platelet count: 150–450 × 10^9/L.
- INR: approximately 0.8–1.2 (variance with lab).
- APRI = (AST / AST ULN) / platelet (10^9/L) × 100; APRI >1 suggests significant fibrosis; >2 suggests cirrhosis in some settings.
- FIB-4 = (Age × AST) / (platelets × sqrt(ALT)); FIB-4 <1.3 (in patients <65) has high negative predictive value for advanced fibrosis; >2.67 suggests advanced fibrosis.
- TE (FibroScan) approximate thresholds (etiology-dependent): F2 ≈ 7–9.5 kPa; F3 ≈ 9.5–12 kPa; F4 (cirrhosis) ≈ 12–14 kPa. Interpret in context.
- CAP approximate steatosis thresholds: S1 ≈ 238 dB/m, S2 ≈ 260 dB/m, S3 ≈ 290 dB/m.
- Imaging signs of portal hypertension: splenomegaly >12 cm, portal vein diameter >13 mm, collaterals/varices, ascites.
- HCC surveillance: ultrasound ± AFP every 6 months for patients with cirrhosis.
