Introduction

Telomeres are tandem hexameric DNA repeats (TTAGGG in vertebrates) that cap chromosome ends and protect genomic integrity. Telomere shortening occurs with each cell division and through oxidative and genotoxic stress; critically short telomeres trigger cellular senescence or apoptosis. Measurement of telomere length (TL) has both research and clinical applications, most notably in the evaluation of suspected telomere biology disorders (TBDs) such as dyskeratosis congenita, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis with short telomeres, and familial bone marrow failure syndromes. Several laboratory techniques are used to quantify telomere length: flow-FISH, qPCR-based relative assays, and Southern blot analysis of terminal restriction fragments (TRF). Each method has distinct principles, strengths, limitations, and implications for clinical interpretation. This chapter reviews the methods in depth, discusses sources of analytical variability and standardization issues, and outlines practical guidelines for clinical use and interpretation of telomere length results in the context of suspected TBDs.

# Flow-FISH methodology and cell-type–specific telomere length measurement

Flow-FISH (flow cytometry with fluorescence in situ hybridization) combines fluorescence in situ hybridization using a labeled peptide nucleic acid (PNA) probe complementary to telomeric repeats with flow cytometric analysis of single cells. The PNA probe is typically labeled with a fluorophore (e.g., fluorescein or Cy3) and binds with high affinity to telomeric TTAGGG repeats under stringent conditions that minimize non-specific hybridization. After hybridization, cells are analyzed by flow cytometry; fluorescence intensity from the telomeric probe is quantified for each cell or gated cell population. Because flow cytometry can concurrently measure forward/side scatter and lineage-specific surface markers, flow-FISH permits telomere length assessment in defined cell types (for example, total lymphocytes, CD4+ and CD8+ T-cells, B-cells, natural killer cells, and granulocytes), which is a major advantage for clinical evaluation.

Key technical steps and controls: Whole blood or isolated cells are permeabilized and hybridized with the PNA probe under controlled temperature and formamide concentration to ensure specificity. A parallel sample without probe (no-probe control) is used to adjust for autofluorescence. An internal standard is required to convert fluorescence intensity to relative or absolute telomere length units; commonly used standards include bovine thymocytes or cell lines with a known telomere length. Many clinical laboratories use a single-copy DNA control dye (e.g., an antibody to a nuclear antigen) or beads to control for instrument variation, but the most robust approach is to run reference cells with established telomere length on every assay. Data are typically reported as telomere fluorescence units converted to kilobases (kb) using calibration curves derived from reference samples measured by an orthogonal method (often Southern blot TRF). Because flow-FISH measures telomeric signal in intact nuclei, it effectively excludes subtelomeric sequences that can artifactually inflate length estimates in DNA-based methods.

Advantages: Flow-FISH provides single-cell resolution and allows cell-type–specific measurement, which is clinically important because telomere attrition can differ across leukocyte subsets. It has good reproducibility when performed in experienced laboratories and is the preferred clinical method for evaluating patients for TBDs in several centers. It is also less affected by DNA extraction artifacts and gives a direct signal per cell.

Limitations and pitfalls: Flow-FISH requires fresh or properly cryopreserved viable cells; delayed processing, poor viability, or inappropriate freezing can degrade performance. The assay is labor-intensive and requires flow cytometry expertise and strict standardization of hybridization conditions. Conversion of fluorescence to absolute kilobase values depends on valid reference samples and calibration against TRF or other standards; inter-laboratory differences in calibration can cause systematic shifts. Certain leukocyte populations are rarer or fragile and may be underrepresented or lost during processing. Additionally, flow-FISH measures telomeric repeat abundance, which can be influenced by genomic variations in telomeric or subtelomeric sequences in rare cases.

Clinical performance characteristics: Large clinical series report that flow-FISH yields age-adjusted telomere lengths that correlate strongly with clinical phenotypes of TBDs. Common interpretive thresholds used in practice are percentiles relative to age-matched controls (discussed further in the analytic variability section): telomere lengths below the 10th percentile raise suspicion for a TBD, and values below the 1st percentile are considered highly predictive in the appropriate clinical context. However, these thresholds vary by lab and must be applied using the laboratory’s validated age-adjusted reference ranges.

# qPCR-based relative telomere length assays: principles and caveats

qPCR-based telomere length measurement is a DNA-based, high-throughput method that estimates average telomere length across a bulk cell population by quantifying the abundance of telomeric sequence relative to a single-copy reference gene. The classic method, introduced by Cawthon, computes a telomere to single-copy gene (T/S) ratio using real-time quantitative PCR. In separate reactions, one amplifies telomeric repeats (using primers that hybridize to the repetitive sequence and amplify a ladder of products) and a single-copy control gene (commonly 36B4, albumin, or beta-globin). The relative quantity of telomeric PCR product normalized to the control gene yields the T/S ratio, which is proportional to mean telomere length in the sample.

Advantages: qPCR requires small amounts of DNA (typically 5–50 ng per reaction), can be run on archived DNA, and is scalable for large cohort studies. It is faster and less labor-intensive than TRF and does not require viable cells as flow-FISH does. For population-level comparisons, qPCR can be useful to detect relative differences in telomere length across groups.

Major caveats and technical limitations: qPCR provides a relative rather than absolute measurement. The T/S ratio depends on PCR efficiency, primer design, DNA quality, and the choice of single-copy gene. Inter-assay and inter-laboratory variability can be substantial; reported coefficients of variation are often in the range of 5–15% or higher for absolute T/S values, and batch effects can dominate. Unlike flow-FISH, qPCR cannot resolve cell-type–specific telomere length and will reflect the composite of cells present in the DNA sample (e.g., leukocyte differential). Hematologic conditions that alter leukocyte subset proportions (infection, inflammation, granulocytosis, neutropenia) can change measured TL independent of actual telomere attrition.

Normalization strategies and standard curves: To improve comparability, many labs include a reference DNA sample run on every plate to generate a relative T/S ratio normalized to that calibrator. Some groups generate a standard curve from serial dilutions of reference DNA to correct for plate-to-plate variation. Despite these measures, absolute conversion of T/S to kilobases requires calibration against TRF or another absolute method, and such conversions can differ across labs.

Pre-analytic and analytic sources of error: DNA extraction method and quality are critical; sheared DNA or contamination affects amplification. The telomeric PCR amplifies many templates and is highly sensitive to primer-dimer artifacts; stringent reaction conditions and melt-curve analysis help identify problems. The single-copy gene must be stable and not subject to copy-number variation in the tested population. PCR inhibitors, pipetting errors, and small changes in reaction efficiency produce misleading T/S ratios. For clinical decision-making in individual patients, these limitations mean that qPCR is generally less reliable than flow-FISH or TRF unless the assay has been rigorously validated in a clinical laboratory with documented performance characteristics and age-adjusted reference ranges.

Interpretive context: qPCR is best used for group comparisons and epidemiologic studies, or as an initial screen in settings where validated, standardized protocols and controls are used. For diagnosing suspected TBDs or guiding clinical care, qPCR alone is insufficient; confirmatory testing using flow-FISH or TRF and integration with clinical and genetic data is recommended.

# Southern blot (TRF analysis) as historical gold standard

Southern blot analysis of terminal restriction fragments (TRF) has historically been regarded as the gold standard for telomere length measurement. The method measures the length of DNA fragments that include telomeric repeats plus a variable adjacent subtelomeric region after digestion with restriction enzymes that cleave genomic DNA but do not cut within telomeric repeats. The DNA fragments are separated by size using agarose gel electrophoresis, transferred to a membrane, and hybridized with a labeled telomeric probe. The resulting smear is visualized and quantified to determine the mean TRF length, usually reported in kilobases (kb).

Principles and strengths: TRF provides an absolute measure of the distribution of terminal fragment sizes for the bulk cell population and yields a mean length and often a range or median. Because the probe binds telomeric repeats in denatured DNA, TRF directly quantifies telomeric DNA content in a sample. When high-quality DNA and rigorous protocols are used, TRF is accurate and reproducible for measuring average telomere length and has been the standard for establishing calibration curves for flow-FISH and qPCR.

Limitations and biases: A fundamental limitation of TRF is that the measured fragments include subtelomeric sequences that are contiguous with telomeric repeats but are not telomeric repeats themselves. Consequently, TRF measurements overestimate the true telomeric repeat length by an amount that varies with restriction enzyme choice and the distance from the restriction site to the telomeric tract. This overestimation is typically on the order of several hundred base pairs to a kilobase and is variable between samples and subtelomeres. TRF also requires relatively large amounts of high-quality, high molecular weight genomic DNA (commonly micrograms), making it unsuitable for small or degraded samples. The technique is labor-intensive, time-consuming (several days), and low-throughput compared with qPCR.

Interpretation and utility: TRF remains useful for calibration and method comparison, and some reference laboratories continue to use TRF in clinical settings. TRF results are commonly reported as mean TRF length in kb with a distribution smear. Clinical interpretation requires comparison to age-adjusted reference data generated using the same method and restriction enzymes. Like qPCR, TRF measures the bulk average from a mixed cell population and cannot provide cell-type specificity unless cells are sorted prior to DNA extraction.

# Analytical variability, age-adjusted reference ranges and lab standardization

Analytical variability in telomere length measurement arises from pre-analytical, analytical, and post-analytical sources. Pre-analytical factors include specimen type (whole blood, isolated leukocytes, bone marrow), collection and processing timing, storage conditions, and DNA extraction methods. For flow-FISH, cell viability and freezing protocols affect results. Analytical variability stems from reagent quality, probe batches, instrument settings, PCR efficiencies, gel conditions, and calibration standards. Post-analytical differences include data processing, curve fitting, and the use of different reference datasets for age adjustment.

Quantifying variability: Laboratories should document intra-assay and inter-assay coefficients of variation (CV). For flow-FISH performed in experienced clinical labs, reported intra-assay CVs for lymphocyte telomere length often fall under 5–10%; inter-assay CVs are typically somewhat higher. qPCR assays commonly report CVs ranging from 5% to over 15% depending on rigor of controls. TRF reproducibility can be good with high-quality DNA but typically requires consistent lab technique; inter-lab variation can be several hundred base pairs.

Age-adjusted reference ranges: Telomere length declines with age in an approximately nonlinear fashion, with more rapid shortening early in life and a more gradual decline in adulthood. Any clinically meaningful interpretation requires comparison to age-matched reference data. Laboratories should generate or adopt normative data spanning the age range and ideally stratified by sex and ethnicity if significant differences are observed. Reference ranges are commonly expressed as percentiles (e.g., 1st, 10th, 50th, 90th) or as mean ± 2 standard deviations for each age band. Representative values (approximate and method-dependent) illustrate the concept: newborn mean telomere lengths by TRF may be roughly 9–13 kb, young adult means around 7–10 kb, and older adults decreasing further; however, absolute values depend on method and calibration. For clinical decision-making, using the laboratory’s validated age-adjusted percentile curves is essential.

Lab standardization and quality control: Given method-dependent differences, inter-lab standardization is crucial for clinical reliability. Best practices include: using well-characterized reference samples (plasma or cell controls) run on every assay, participation in external quality assessment programs when available, documenting analytic performance metrics (limits of detection, CVs, linearity), and reporting method-specific reference ranges. Where possible, converting relative measurements to absolute units should be done using calibration against an orthogonal method validated in the same laboratory. For serial testing in a patient (monitoring), the same laboratory and method should be used to avoid spurious changes due to inter-method variability.

Reporting recommendations: Laboratory reports should state the method used, specimen type, time from collection to processing, age-adjusted reference percentiles, and assay performance metrics. For example: “Telomere length measured by flow-FISH in lymphocytes is X kb, corresponding to the Yth percentile for age; laboratory inter-assay CV = Z%.” Such transparency aids clinicians in interpreting results and integrating them with clinical and genetic information.

# Clinical utility of telomere length testing in suspected TBDs

Telomere length testing plays a diagnostic and prognostic role in evaluating suspected telomere biology disorders. TBDs encompass a spectrum of disorders caused by germline pathogenic variants in telomere maintenance genes (e.g., TERT, TERC, DKC1, RTEL1, PARN, TINF2) and manifest variably with marrow failure, pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic disease, early graying, mucocutaneous features, and cancer predisposition. The clinical utility of TL testing depends on the pre-test probability, the chosen assay, and integration with genetic testing and clinical findings.

When to test: Consider TL testing in patients with unexplained cytopenias (especially aplastic anemia), idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis or interstitial lung disease with family history, early-onset emphysema, unexplained hepatic cirrhosis in a young patient, cutaneous/mucosal features suggestive of dyskeratosis congenita, or a family history of TBDs. Testing may guide decisions about donor selection for hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, therapeutic choices (e.g., avoidance of alkylators or high-dose radiation in patients with short telomeres), and genetic counseling.

Choice of assay: Flow-FISH is preferred in clinical settings assessing for TBDs because of its cell-type specificity and better-established clinical thresholds. TRF can be used in specialized labs where calibration is well-defined. qPCR is generally not recommended as a standalone clinical diagnostic tool for individual patients suspected of having a TBD due to its higher analytic variability and lack of cell-type resolution; it may be acceptable as an initial screen if the lab provides validated age-adjusted percentiles and follows up abnormal results with flow-FISH or genetic testing.

Diagnostic thresholds and clinical decision points: Interpretation is age-dependent. In adults, lymphocyte telomere lengths below the 10th percentile for age suggest shortened telomeres and warrant further evaluation; values below the 1st percentile are highly suggestive of a TBD in the appropriate clinical context. In children and young adults, the thresholds are similar but absolute kilobase values are higher owing to age-related decline. For example, a lymphocyte telomere length below the 10th percentile on flow-FISH should prompt genetic testing for telomere maintenance gene variants, family history assessment, and specialist referral. However, TL alone is not diagnostic of a monogenic TBD; pathogenic variants may be absent in a proportion of clinically affected individuals, and conversely, short telomeres can result from non-genetic factors.

Prognostic and management implications: Short telomeres correlate with disease severity and age of onset for some manifestations (e.g., bone marrow failure and pulmonary fibrosis). Knowledge of short telomeres can influence therapeutic risk assessment—patients with TBDs have increased sensitivity to cytotoxic therapies and may experience worse transplant-related toxicities. Telomere length can also inform family screening and reproductive counseling. Nonetheless, TL measurement should complement—not replace—comprehensive genetic testing and clinical assessment. Multidisciplinary care including hematology, pulmonology, hepatology, and genetic counseling is often required.

Limitations in clinical utility: Not all individuals with pathogenic variants show extremely short leukocyte telomeres at the time of testing, and not all individuals with short telomeres have identifiable pathogenic germline variants; environmental exposures (smoking, chronic inflammation), somatic clonal hematopoiesis, and certain acquired disorders can affect leukocyte telomere lengths. Therefore, TL testing must be interpreted with caution and in the context of clinical phenotype and family history.

# Interpreting short telomeres in context of clinical and genetic data

Interpreting an isolated laboratory result of short telomeres requires synthesis of clinical context, family history, and genetic testing results. The clinical significance of short telomeres varies with age, cell type measured, assay used, and potential confounding factors.

Age and cell-type considerations: A telomere length that is short relative to age-matched controls is more concerning in children and younger adults because baseline telomere lengths are higher at younger ages. Flow-FISH lymphocyte measurements are commonly used for clinical interpretation; granulocyte lengths are often measured as a comparison because granulocyte telomere lengths may reflect hematopoietic stem cell telomere status more directly in some contexts. Discrepancies between subsets (e.g., very short lymphocyte telomeres but relatively preserved granulocyte telomeres) may reflect lineage-specific dynamics, recent marrow stress, or technical issues and warrant careful reevaluation.

Integration with genetic testing: Findings of telomere shortening should prompt targeted genetic testing for genes implicated in TBDs, ideally using next-generation sequencing panels or exome sequencing coupled with copy-number analysis. A documented pathogenic or likely pathogenic variant in a telomere maintenance gene together with very short telomeres supports a molecular diagnosis. However, absence of a pathogenic variant does not exclude a TBD: some variants remain undiscovered or are noncoding/regulatory. Conversely, detection of a variant of uncertain significance requires correlation with TL, family segregation, and functional data where available.

Clinical phenotype correlation: Short telomeres acquire clinical significance when aligned with pertinent features: early-onset marrow failure, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, unexplained liver disease, mucocutaneous findings (nail dystrophy, leukoplakia, reticular pigmentation), premature greying, or a striking family history. For example, a patient with aplastic anemia and lymphocyte telomere length below the 1st percentile has a high likelihood of having an underlying telomeropathy; such a finding should inform management and urgency of genetic evaluation.

Confounders and alternate causes: Before attributing short telomeres to a heritable TBD, consider acquired causes that shorten leukocyte TL: chronic inflammatory states, active infection, recent chemotherapy or radiotherapy, smoking, obesity, and severe psychosocial stress have all been associated with accelerated telomere shortening. Hematologic conditions that alter leukocyte subset composition (e.g., leukocytosis dominated by granulocytes with different telomere lengths) can alter bulk DNA-based measures like qPCR or TRF. Clonal hematopoiesis or large clonal expansions of a subset with short telomeres can skew results. Repeated measurements, assessment of different cell types by flow-FISH, and review of recent exposures and medical history help distinguish acquired from inherited shortening.

Decision thresholds and next steps: In practice, clinicians often use age-adjusted percentile thresholds to guide next steps. A lymphocyte telomere length below the laboratory’s 10th percentile should prompt consideration of further evaluation: confirmatory testing with flow-FISH if not already used, targeted genetic testing, family history assessment, and specialist referral. Telomere lengths below the 1st percentile strongly favor a telomere biology disorder and generally justify expedited genetic testing and counseling. For values near the 10th percentile, interpretation should be cautious: integrate phenotype and consider repeat testing or alternate assays. If clinical suspicion is high despite borderline or normal telomere length, proceed with genetic testing because TL can be normal in some mutation carriers early in life or in certain tissues.

Communicating uncertainty: Because interpretive challenges can have major implications (e.g., transplant decisions, family planning), clinicians must communicate the limitations and uncertainty of TL testing clearly to patients. Genetic counseling before and after testing is advised, and multidisciplinary discussion is beneficial when TL results would affect major management decisions.

Conclusion

Accurate measurement and interpretation of telomere length require an understanding of assay principles, technical limitations, and clinical context. Flow-FISH offers cell-type specificity and is the preferred clinical assay in many centers assessing suspected telomere biology disorders. qPCR is useful for high-throughput epidemiologic studies but has substantial analytic variability that limits its standalone clinical utility. TRF by Southern blot provides an absolute measure and historically served as the gold standard, but its technical demands and inclusion of subtelomeric DNA limit its routine clinical use. Laboratories and clinicians must pay careful attention to age-adjusted reference ranges, lab-specific performance characteristics, and standardization practices. Critically short telomeres—particularly lymphocyte TL below the 1st percentile for age on a validated clinical assay—are highly suggestive of a TBD when coupled with compatible clinical and family history and should prompt genetic investigation and specialist referral. Integration of TL results with clinical phenotype and genetic data remains essential to guide diagnosis, prognosis, and management.
