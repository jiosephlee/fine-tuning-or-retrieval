Chapter 6: Laboratory Evaluation of Cytopenias — Interpretation of Peripheral Blood and Bone Marrow Findings

Introduction

Cytopenias—deficiencies of one or more blood cell lines—are common findings in clinical practice and can reflect processes ranging from simple nutrient deficiency to life-threatening bone marrow failure or hematologic malignancy. Accurate interpretation of hematologic laboratory data requires integration of the automated complete blood count (CBC) and indices, manual review of the peripheral smear, assessment of marrow production by reticulocyte measures, biochemical tests of iron and inflammation, and, when indicated, careful analysis of bone marrow aspiration/biopsy including cytogenetic and molecular studies. This chapter reviews the systematic approach necessary to interpret these tests in patients with anemia, leukopenia, neutropenia, thrombocytopenia, bicytopenia, and pancytopenia, emphasizing pathophysiology and clinical reasoning that connects basic science findings to diagnostic decision-making.

# Complete blood count parameters and patterns in different cytopenias

The CBC provides quantitative information on hemoglobin (Hb), hematocrit (Hct), red blood cell (RBC) count, red cell indices (mean corpuscular volume [MCV], mean corpuscular hemoglobin [MCH], mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration [MCHC]), red cell distribution width (RDW), white blood cell (WBC) count with differential, absolute neutrophil count (ANC), and platelet count. Understanding reference ranges, patterns, and how they change with disease is the foundation for initial interpretation.

Reference ranges commonly used (adult; may vary by laboratory)
- Hemoglobin: men 13.0–17.0 g/dL; women 12.0–16.0 g/dL.
- Hematocrit: men 39–51%; women 36–47%.
- MCV: 80–100 fL.
- RDW (coefficient of variation): 11–15%.
- WBC: 4.0–11.0 × 10^9/L.
- ANC: normally >1500/µL; neutropenia defined as ANC <1500/µL, severe <500/µL.
- Platelets: 150–450 × 10^9/L.

Patterns and clinical implications
- Isolated anemia: often due to nutritional deficiency (iron, B12, folate), chronic blood loss, hemolysis, or primary marrow disorder. MCV guides differential: microcytic (MCV <80 fL) favors iron deficiency, thalassemia, chronic disease with iron-restricted erythropoiesis; macrocytic (MCV >100 fL) suggests megaloblastic anemia (B12/folate), liver disease, alcohol, or bone marrow disorders; normocytic anemia may reflect acute blood loss, early iron deficiency, hemolysis, or anemia of chronic disease.
- Isolated thrombocytopenia: consider immune-mediated destruction (ITP), drug-induced thrombocytopenia, sequestration (splenomegaly), or decreased production from marrow disorders.
- Isolated leukopenia or neutropenia: infections, autoimmune disease, medications, and marrow failure; severity of neutropenia correlates with infection risk.
- Bicytopenia: involvement of two cell lineages (e.g., anemia + thrombocytopenia) focuses differential on processes affecting marrow broadly such as hypersplenism, marrow infiltration, aplastic processes, or severe nutritional deficiencies.
- Pancytopenia: simultaneous reduction of all three formed elements suggests marrow hypoplasia/failure (aplastic anemia), extensive marrow infiltration (metastatic cancer, leukemia), megaloblastic anemia, or severe systemic illness.

Quantitative indices that refine interpretation
- MCV: distinguishes microcytic vs macrocytic etiologies, but mixed populations (dimorphic picture) can average to normocytic MCV; always confirm with smear.
- RDW: increased RDW suggests anisocytosis and supports a mixed population or evolving deficiency (e.g., iron deficiency causes high RDW), whereas normal RDW with microcytosis favors thalassemia trait.
- MCHC: elevated MCHC is seen in spherocytosis; low MCHC is nonspecific but can accompany hypochromia.
- Platelet morphology can raise suspicion for pseudothrombocytopenia if clumping is seen.

Clinical decision points
- When cytopenia is detected, confirm on peripheral smear and repeat CBC if automated flags or potential preanalytical issues (clotting, platelet clumps) are present.
- Calculate ANC and reticulocyte indices early to distinguish hypoproliferative vs destructive causes.
- Use MCV and RDW to direct initial biochemical testing (iron studies, B12/folate) and to determine urgency of bone marrow evaluation.

# Reticulocyte count and reticulocyte production index interpretation

Reticulocytes are immature red blood cells containing residual ribosomal RNA; their proportion in blood provides a measure of marrow erythropoietic activity. The raw reticulocyte percentage (% retic) must be corrected for the degree of anemia and for prolonged maturation time in the blood. The most commonly used corrected indexes are the corrected reticulocyte percentage and the reticulocyte production index (RPI).

Key reference values
- Absolute reticulocyte percentage (uncorrected): normally approximately 0.5–2.5% in adults.
- Absolute reticulocyte count: typically 25–75 × 10^9/L (derived from retic% × RBC count).
- Reticulocyte Production Index (RPI): values ≥2 generally indicate an adequate marrow response to anemia; RPI <2 indicates inadequate marrow response.

Calculations
- Corrected reticulocyte percentage = Reticulocyte% × (Patient Hct / Normal Hct).
- RPI adjusts for prolonged reticulocyte transit time in anemia. Several formulae exist; a commonly used method:
  RPI = Reticulocyte% × (Hct / 45) / maturation correction
  where the maturation correction factor is based on severity of anemia: Hct ≥35% -> 1; Hct 25–34% -> 1.5; Hct 15–24% -> 2; Hct <15% -> 2.5. (Some labs use Hct or Hb thresholds; use the laboratory's preferred correction.)

Interpretation and clinical significance
- Elevated RPI (≥2): indicates an appropriate marrow response—hemolysis or blood loss are likely causes of anemia. Additional tests should look for hemolytic markers (LDH, bilirubin indirect, haptoglobin), reticulocyte morphology (polychromasia), and sources of bleeding.
- Low RPI (<2): indicates hypoproliferative anemia due to decreased marrow production—possible causes include iron deficiency (early), anemia of chronic disease, aplastic anemia, marrow infiltration, endocrine disorders, and medication effects. In suspected nutritional deficiency (iron, B12, folate), the RPI helps determine whether marrow is responding to therapy.
- Caveats: Reticulocyte counts can be affected by recent transfusion, erythropoietin therapy, and hemoglobinopathies. Automated reticulocyte measurements may include immature reticulocyte fraction (IRF) which can show earlier marrow recovery before RPI rises.

Clinical decision points
- Use reticulocyte response to decide whether to pursue evaluation for hemolysis/bleeding vs marrow failure.
- In anemia with low RPI and low MCV, proceed to iron studies; if RPI low with high MCV, check B12/folate and consider bone marrow evaluation if severe.
- If RPI is high but anemia persists, evaluate hemolytic indices and look for occult bleeding.

# Peripheral smear findings: dimorphic anemia, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, hypersegmented neutrophils — mechanisms and differential

The peripheral blood smear remains an indispensable tool, revealing cell morphology that often identifies or narrows the differential diagnosis.

Dimorphic anemia
Dimorphic anemia refers to the simultaneous presence of two distinct populations of red cells—typically one population normocytic/normochromic and another microcytic/hypochromic or macrocytic. Mechanisms include mixed nutritional deficiencies (e.g., iron deficiency plus B12 deficiency), recent blood transfusion producing newer normal donor cells alongside patient's abnormal cells, or treatment effect (iron therapy or vitamin B12 therapy resulting in reticulocytosis and larger cells alongside older microcytic cells). Hemoglobin electrophoresis, iron studies, and nutritional assays can distinguish causes. On smear, dimorphic pictures are often accompanied by a high RDW. Clinically, recognition of dimorphic anemia avoids misclassification based on averaged indices (e.g., a seemingly normocytic MCV concealing a mixed picture).

Basophilic stippling
Basophilic stippling consists of punctate, coarse basophilic granules in red cells representing aggregates of ribosomal RNA. It is classically associated with lead poisoning, where inhibition of pyrimidine-5'-nucleotidase and other enzymes leads to defective RNA degradation. Other causes include sideroblastic anemia, thalassemia, severe alcohol use, and some hemolytic anemias. When basophilic stippling is present, correlate with exposure history and check lead level and a complete hemolytic workup. In sideroblastic processes, basophilic stippling may coexist with ring sideroblasts in marrow.

Cabot rings
Cabot rings are threadlike or looped inclusions in erythrocytes, usually described as remnant mitotic spindle microtubules or nuclear membrane remnants and are relatively nonspecific markers of dyserythropoiesis. They are seen in megaloblastic anemia (B12/folate deficiency), myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS), severe lead poisoning, and after splenectomy. They are rare but when present suggest defective nuclear maturation or severe marrow stress; their presence should prompt evaluation for megaloblastic states and marrow dysplasia.

Hypersegmented neutrophils
Hypersegmented neutrophils, defined by the presence of neutrophils with ≥5 nuclear lobes or an increased mean lobulation count, are an early and sensitive indicator of megaloblastic anemia due to impaired DNA synthesis from vitamin B12 or folate deficiency. They are often present before macrocytosis becomes obvious on the CBC. Mechanistically, inadequate thymidine pools cause discordant nuclear maturation (delayed nuclear division) relative to cytoplasmic development. Hypersegmentation can also be seen after administration of granulocyte-colony stimulating factor (rarely), in myelodysplasia, and in severe infections, but their positive predictive value for megaloblastic anemia is high and should prompt immediate B12/folate testing.

Other useful smear features
- Schistocytes (fragmented RBCs) suggest microangiopathic hemolysis (DIC, TTP/HUS) or mechanical hemolysis (prosthetic valves).
- Spherocytes suggest extravascular hemolysis such as autoimmune hemolytic anemia.
- Target cells are seen in thalassemia and liver disease.
- Teardrop cells suggest marrow infiltration or myelofibrosis.
- Atypical lymphocytes may indicate viral infection (e.g., EBV) or certain lymphoproliferative disorders.

Clinical reasoning
- Always integrate smear findings with quantitative CBC and clinical history (drugs, exposures, transfusions, alcohol use).
- Specific inclusions direct targeted testing: basophilic stippling -> lead level; hypersegmented neutrophils -> B12/folate; Cabot rings or dysplastic forms -> consider bone marrow and cytogenetics.

# Iron studies in ineffective erythropoiesis vs iron deficiency vs anaemia of chronic disease

Iron laboratory tests—serum iron, total iron-binding capacity (TIBC), transferrin saturation (TSAT), and ferritin—distinguish between iron deficiency anemia (IDA), anemia of chronic disease/inflammation (ACD), and states of iron overload or ineffective erythropoiesis (e.g., sideroblastic anemia, thalassemia).

Reference ranges (typical, lab-dependent)
- Serum iron: 60–170 µg/dL.
- TIBC: 250–450 µg/dL.
- Transferrin saturation (TSAT): 20–50% (TSAT = serum iron / TIBC × 100%).
- Ferritin: men 30–400 ng/mL; women 15–150 ng/mL. Ferritin is an acute-phase reactant and increases with inflammation.

Iron deficiency anemia (IDA)
Pathophysiology: depletion of iron stores leads to inadequate hemoglobin synthesis, microcytic hypochromic red cells, increased RDW, and low reticulocyte response until repletion occurs.

Typical iron study pattern
- Serum iron: low.
- TIBC: high (transferrin upregulated).
- TSAT: low (<15% often).
- Ferritin: low (<30 ng/mL generally diagnostic; in the setting of inflammation, ferritin may be falsely elevated and thresholds of <100 ng/mL may be used to rule out iron deficiency).

Clinical considerations
- Ferritin <15–30 ng/mL is highly specific for IDA; ferritin >100 ng/mL usually excludes IDA unless inflammation is present.
- If ferritin is indeterminate (30–100 ng/mL) and inflammation suspected, consider CRP/ESR, soluble transferrin receptor (sTfR increased in IDA but not in ACD), or bone marrow iron stain as the gold standard.

Anemia of chronic disease/inflammation (ACD)
Pathophysiology: inflammatory cytokines (notably interleukin-6) induce hepatic hepcidin synthesis, which decreases ferroportin-mediated iron export from enterocytes and macrophages. Iron is sequestered in storage cells and unavailable for erythropoiesis despite normal or increased body iron stores.

Typical iron study pattern
- Serum iron: low.
- TIBC: normal or low.
- TSAT: low to normal.
- Ferritin: normal or high (reflecting adequate stores and acute-phase response).

Distinguishing ACD from IDA
- sTfR is usually normal in ACD and elevated in IDA.
- Ferritin adjusted for CRP or using sTfR/log ferritin index may be helpful.
- Bone marrow iron staining will show adequate or increased iron in ACD, absent iron in IDA.

Ineffective erythropoiesis and iron overload (e.g., sideroblastic anemia, thalassemia)
Pathophysiology: defective erythroid maturation leads to increased iron absorption (low hepcidin in some subtypes) and iron accumulation despite anemia. Sideroblastic anemia shows iron-laden mitochondria forming ring sideroblasts; thalassemia shows ineffective erythropoiesis with increased intestinal iron absorption.

Iron study pattern
- Serum iron: normal to high.
- TIBC: normal or low.
- TSAT: normal to high.
- Ferritin: normal to high, often markedly elevated when chronic transfusions or iron loading have occurred.

Clinical implications
- In sideroblastic anemia and thalassemia, transfusion burden and ineffective erythropoiesis lead to iron overload—monitor ferritin and consider cardiac/liver iron by MRI when ferritin is elevated or transfusion history is significant.
- Ring sideroblasts (≥15% of erythroblasts by traditional criteria; peripheral thresholds vary with new WHO definitions that emphasize SF3B1 mutations and sideroblastic features) on bone marrow Prussian blue stain confirm diagnosis.

Clinical decision points
- Evaluate CRP/ESR to interpret ferritin.
- Use sTfR and sTfR/log ferritin index when the distinction between IDA and ACD is critical.
- If clinical uncertainty persists, bone marrow iron stain is definitive for marrow iron stores.

# Bone marrow cellularity, lineage assessment, erythroid hyperplasia, dysplasia, and iron stores

Bone marrow aspiration and trephine biopsy integrate histologic architecture, cellularity, cytologic morphology, and iron distribution. Interpretation demands correlation with clinical and laboratory data. Key components include cellularity (overall marrow cellularity and its relation to age), lineage-specific assessment, M:E ratio, signs of dysplasia, presence of infiltrative processes, fibrosis, and iron stores assessed by Prussian blue stain.

Cellularity
Marrow cellularity varies with age. A commonly used estimate of expected cellularity is roughly (100 − age) ±10% (e.g., a 30-year-old would have approximately 70% cellular marrow); this is a guide rather than an absolute rule. Hypocellular marrow in an adult suggests aplastic anemia, marrow suppression by drugs/radiation, or late-stage marrow replacement by fat, while hypercellular marrow indicates marrow stimulation (e.g., hemolysis, recovery from aplasia), infiltration by malignant cells (leukemia), or myeloproliferative processes.

Lineage assessment and M:E ratio
The myeloid-to-erythroid (M:E) ratio describes the relative proportions of granulocytic precursors to erythroid precursors in the marrow. A typical M:E ratio is approximately 3:1 (myeloid predominance). Alterations provide diagnostic clues:
- Erythroid hyperplasia (decreased M:E ratio) occurs with increased peripheral RBC destruction (hemolysis), ineffective erythropoiesis, or recovery from anemia. For example, hemolysis produces marked erythroid hyperplasia with increased polychromatic erythroblasts and elevated reticulocyte counts.
- Myeloid hyperplasia (increased M:E ratio) may be seen with infection, myeloproliferative neoplasms, or marrow response to granulocyte colony-stimulating factors.
- Markedly abnormal M:E ratios with morphologic dysplasia across lineages suggest myelodysplastic syndromes.

Erythroid hyperplasia and ineffective erythropoiesis
Erythroid hyperplasia is characterized by an increased number of erythroid precursors. Ineffective erythropoiesis occurs when expanded erythroid elements fail to mature and are destroyed intramedullary, a feature of conditions such as megaloblastic anemia, sideroblastic anemia, and thalassemia. Markers of ineffective erythropoiesis include erythroid hyperplasia with frequent apoptosis on marrow examination, ring sideroblasts on Prussian blue stain, and a paradox of increased marrow production with low reticulocyte output.

Dysplasia
Dysplastic changes are abnormalities in the maturation and morphology of hematopoietic precursors and are key to diagnosing MDS. Dysplastic features may be subtle or lineage-specific:
- Erythroid dysplasia: nuclear irregularities, multinucleation, nuclear-cytoplasmic asynchrony, megaloblastoid changes.
- Granulocytic dysplasia: hypogranulation, pseudo-Pelger-Huët anomaly (bilobed nuclei), abnormal nuclear segmentation, and decreased primary granules.
- Megakaryocytic dysplasia: micromegakaryocytes, mononuclear megakaryocytes, or hypersegmented forms.
Quantifying dysplasia (percentage of dysplastic cells in a lineage) forms part of WHO criteria for MDS classification. Additionally, presence of increased blasts (≥5%–20% depending on classification) alters diagnostic category toward myelodysplastic or overt leukemic processes.

Iron stores and Prussian blue stain
Prussian blue (Perls) staining of marrow provides a semi-quantitative assessment of iron stores and the presence of ring sideroblasts. A normal marrow shows iron in macrophages and some erythroid precursors; absence of iron supports iron deficiency. Ring sideroblasts—erythroblasts with perinuclear ring deposition of iron—indicate mitochondrial iron accumulation and occur in sideroblastic anemias and certain MDS subtypes (e.g., MDS with ring sideroblasts). Quantification and clinical correlation guide diagnosis and therapy (e.g., pyridoxine trial in hereditary or acquired sideroblastic anemia).

Special marrow features
- Marrow fibrosis is assessed by reticulin and trichrome stains; marked fibrosis (e.g., myelofibrosis) explains cytopenias and teardrop red cells.
- Marrow infiltration by metastatic carcinoma, granulomas, or storage diseases is identified by architecture disruption and special stains/immunohistochemistry.
- Cytochemical stains (myeloperoxidase, nonspecific esterase) help distinguish myeloid from monocytic lineage in blasts.

Clinical decision points
- Consider marrow biopsy in unexplained cytopenias that are progressive, associated with abnormal smear findings (blasts, dysplasia), or when marrow infiltration or fibrosis is suspected.
- Use marrow iron stain when iron studies are equivocal or when sideroblastic anemia is suspected.
- Correlate marrow cellularity and lineage distribution to reticulocyte index and peripheral counts to determine whether cytopenias are due to production failure vs peripheral destruction.

# Cytogenetics and molecular testing in marrow disorders

Cytogenetic and molecular analyses have transformed the evaluation and classification of marrow disorders. Karyotyping, fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH), and next-generation sequencing (NGS) detect chromosomal abnormalities and gene mutations that have diagnostic, prognostic, and therapeutic implications.

Indications and approach
- Indications for cytogenetic/molecular testing include new diagnoses of suspected hematologic malignancy (MDS, AML, ALL), unexplained persistent cytopenias with marrow dysplasia, evaluation of marrow failure syndromes, and risk stratification in established hematologic disorders.
- A combined approach is standard: conventional karyotype for large-scale chromosomal rearrangements; FISH for targeted translocations or deletions (e.g., del(5q), del(7q), t(9;22)); NGS panels for recurrent somatic mutations across myeloid neoplasms.

Key cytogenetic findings and their implications
- Recurrent chromosomal abnormalities help define disease categories and prognosis. For example, in MDS and AML, abnormalities such as del(5q), monosomy 7, complex karyotype, and certain translocations carry prognostic weight and influence therapy.
- In chronic myeloid leukemia (CML), the BCR-ABL1 fusion (t(9;22)) is diagnostic and directs tyrosine kinase inhibitor therapy.
- Loss of chromosome 7 or complex karyotype in MDS/AML is associated with poor prognosis and may affect transplant decision-making.

Key molecular mutations
- JAK2 V617F, CALR, and MPL mutations are central to the diagnosis of myeloproliferative neoplasms (PV, ET, MF).
- SF3B1 mutations are strongly associated with ring sideroblasts and are included in WHO diagnostic criteria for certain MDS subtypes; identification of SF3B1 has both diagnostic and prognostic implications.
- TP53 mutations and complex cytogenetics predict poor outcomes in MDS and AML and may identify patients for alternative therapeutic approaches.
- DNMT3A, TET2, ASXL1 mutations are common age-related clonal hematopoiesis mutations and are also found in myeloid neoplasms; interpretation requires clinical context because such mutations can be present in asymptomatic clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP).
- FLT3 internal tandem duplications or tyrosine kinase domain mutations, NPM1, CEBPA are relevant in AML classification, prognosis, and targeted therapy considerations.

Integration and clinical utility
- Cytogenetics and molecular testing refine diagnosis when morphologic features are equivocal (e.g., distinguishing clonal MDS from nonclonal marrow dysplasia).
- Molecular results impact prognosis and guide treatment: for example, del(5q) MDS responds to lenalidomide; BCR-ABL1 positive disease responds to tyrosine kinase inhibitors; IDH1/2 mutations in AML may be targeted by specific inhibitors.
- Serial molecular testing is useful to monitor minimal residual disease (MRD) in leukemia and to detect clonal evolution.

Interpretive caveats
- The presence of somatic mutations in older adults without overt marrow disease (CHIP) complicates interpretation; mutations should be interpreted within the clinical and morphologic context.
- Low-level clones detected by highly sensitive NGS may reflect early disease or benign clonal hematopoiesis; thresholds for clinical action vary and should be discussed in multidisciplinary settings.
- Cytogenetic and molecular abnormalities can be present in peripheral blood and do not always require marrow sampling for detection; however, marrow assessment often provides essential morphologic correlation.

Clinical decision points
- Perform cytogenetic/molecular panels for patients with unexplained cytopenias with marrow dysplasia, for all new MDS/AML diagnoses, and when therapeutic decisions depend on mutation status.
- Use the combination of conventional karyotype, FISH for specific abnormalities, and targeted NGS panels to maximize diagnostic yield.

Conclusion

A systematic laboratory evaluation of cytopenias synthesizes information from the CBC and indices, reticulocyte response, peripheral smear morphology, iron studies, and bone marrow examination with cytogenetic and molecular testing. Thoughtful interpretation—grounded in mechanisms such as hepcidin-mediated iron sequestration, ineffective erythropoiesis, and marrow dysplasia—and correlation with clinical context is essential to prioritize diagnoses, choose targeted tests, and determine urgency of interventions, including bone marrow biopsy and specialized genetic testing. Mastery of the patterns and decision points presented in this chapter equips clinicians to interpret complex hematologic data and to manage patients with cytopenias appropriately.
