Introduction

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) are a group of inherited conditions caused by defects in telomere maintenance. Telomeres are repetitive nucleotide sequences and associated proteins that protect chromosomal ends from degradation and inappropriate DNA repair. Pathogenic defects in telomere maintenance lead to accelerated telomere shortening, genomic instability, and premature cellular senescence or apoptosis in high-turnover tissues. Because different tissues vary in their proliferative demands, the clinical manifestations of TBDs span multiple organ systems and range from classic pediatric syndromes with early-onset, multisystem failure to isolated, organ-predominant disease in adults such as idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) or cryptogenic cirrhosis. This chapter reviews the clinical spectrum of TBDs, emphasizing the classic mucocutaneous features of dyskeratosis congenita, severe pediatric phenotypes, adult presentations, multisystem involvement, genotype–phenotype correlations, and natural history including complications and cancer risk.

# Classic dyskeratosis congenita features

Dyskeratosis congenita (DC) is the canonical TBD in which the historic diagnostic triad comprises nail dystrophy, oral leukoplakia, and reticular hyperpigmentation of the skin. These mucocutaneous findings typically appear in childhood or adolescence, but onset can be variable. Nail dystrophy often begins as longitudinal ridging that progresses to thinning, splitting, pterygium formation, or nail loss. Oral leukoplakia appears as white, well-demarcated patches on the buccal mucosa, tongue, or oropharynx and may precede other features. The cutaneous hyperpigmentation is often reticular or lace-like, classically on the chest, neck, and upper extremities, though generalized pigmentation changes can occur.

Beyond the triad, DC is often accompanied by other cutaneous and mucosal signs: premature graying of scalp hair, sparse or brittle hair, early balding, and mucosal atrophy. Dental abnormalities such as microdontia, hypodontia, or periodontal disease can be present. Ocular findings include epiphora from lacrimal duct stenosis and conjunctival abnormalities. Importantly, mucocutaneous features reflect the vulnerability of squamous and ectodermal tissues to shortened telomeres, but their presence is neither necessary nor sufficient for diagnosis; many patients, particularly adults, may lack the classic triad.

Diagnosis of DC rests on a combination of clinical features, family history, laboratory evidence of telomere shortening, and genetic testing. Telomere length measurement in leukocyte subsets is commonly used; telomere length below the 1st percentile for age in lymphocytes or granulocytes strongly supports a TBD. In clinical practice, thresholds vary by assay and laboratory, but generalized criteria consider telomere length <1st percentile for age as abnormal and <10th percentile as potentially significant, particularly when supported by compatible features. Genetic testing targeting known telomere-related genes (e.g., DKC1, TERT, TERC, TINF2, RTEL1, PARN, NAF1, NHP2, NOP10) can confirm the diagnosis and help predict inheritance patterns. However, up to one-third of clinically diagnosed DC cases historically lacked identifiable pathogenic variants in known genes, reflecting genetic heterogeneity and evolving discovery.

# Severe pediatric phenotypes (Hoyeraal–Hreidarsson, Revesz, Coats plus)

A subset of TBDs manifests in infancy or early childhood with severe, multisystem pathology. Hoyeraal–Hreidarsson syndrome (HHS), Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus represent severe variants on the telomere disease continuum. These phenotypes often reflect biallelic or severe dominant-negative mutations resulting in profoundly short telomeres and widespread organ involvement.

Hoyeraal–Hreidarsson syndrome is characterized by intrauterine growth retardation or failure to thrive, cerebellar hypoplasia with neurological dysfunction (ataxia, developmental delay, microcephaly), immunodeficiency with combined cellular and humoral defects, and bone marrow failure. Cerebellar hypoplasia on neuroimaging is a hallmark. HHS patients commonly present with severe cytopenias early in life with transfusion dependence and increased susceptibility to opportunistic infections. The neurological phenotype is believed to arise from impaired proliferation and viability of neuronal progenitors due to short telomeres during neurodevelopment.

Revesz syndrome shares features of DC and HHS but has distinctive bilateral exudative retinopathy leading to vision loss. Revesz syndrome includes IUGR, skin and nail abnormalities, bone marrow failure, and central nervous system calcifications. The proliferative demands of retinal vascular endothelial cells and retinal support tissues explain susceptibility in the context of telomere dysfunction.

Coats plus (also called cerebroretinal microangiopathy with calcifications and cysts) overlaps clinically with Revesz syndrome but is usually caused by biallelic mutations in CTC1 and is typified by retinal vascular telangiectasia with exudation (Coats disease-like appearance), intracranial calcifications, leukoencephalopathy, and multisystem involvement including gastrointestinal bleeding and portal hypertension. Although CTC1 primarily affects the CST complex and telomere maintenance, Coats plus may involve additional DNA repair or microvascular pathobiology leading to a vascular-leukoencephalopathic phenotype.

These severe pediatric phenotypes underscore that telomere dysfunction during development can impair organogenesis and result in irreversible structural anomalies (e.g., cerebellar hypoplasia) and profound immune and hematologic failure. Early recognition is critical because management differs from isolated acquired marrow failure—hematopoietic stem cell transplantation is often considered but requires modified conditioning because of increased toxicity risks, and transplant outcomes are influenced by preexisting organ dysfunction from telomere disease.

# Adult presentations: idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, cryptogenic cirrhosis, bone marrow failure, premature greying

In adults, TBDs frequently present as apparently organ-limited conditions, often without the classic DC triad. The most common adult presentations are idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), cryptogenic liver disease progressing to cirrhosis, acquired aplastic anemia or other bone marrow failure, and premature greying of hair.

Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis is a fibrotic interstitial lung disease characterized by progressive dyspnea, restrictive physiology, and a usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP) pattern on high-resolution computed tomography. A significant fraction of familial pulmonary fibrosis and sporadic IPF cases harbor pathogenic variants in telomerase components (TERT, TERC, RTEL1, PARN) and have shortened leukocyte telomeres. Key clinical clues suggesting an underlying TBD in patients with IPF include early age at onset (often younger than typical IPF), a family history of pulmonary fibrosis or bone marrow failure, extrapulmonary findings such as premature greying, and increased toxicity from immunosuppressive or cytotoxic therapies. Knowledge of telomere-related disease is important for lung transplant candidacy and conditioning choices, as affected patients have increased risks of post-transplant complications.

Cryptogenic cirrhosis attributable to TBD often presents as progressive liver dysfunction, portal hypertension, and splenomegaly. Telomere-related liver disease may manifest as nodular regenerative hyperplasia, nonalcoholic steatohepatitis-like pathology, or frank cirrhosis with portal hypertension. Liver enzyme abnormalities may be mild and nonspecific; thrombocytopenia due to splenic sequestration can be an early sign. Clinically, unexplained cirrhosis in a relatively young person, particularly with other features such as pulmonary fibrosis or cytopenias, should prompt consideration of a telomere disorder.

Bone marrow failure in adults can present as aplastic anemia or as cytopenias progressing to myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) or acute myeloid leukemia (AML). Telomere-mediated marrow failure tends to be refractory to standard immunosuppression in some cases and is associated with increased sensitivity to cytotoxic agents. Hematologic presentation can range from isolated thrombocytopenia or macrocytic anemia to pancytopenia. Standard evaluation includes complete blood count with differential, reticulocyte count, bone marrow biopsy, and cytogenetics; telomere length testing and genetic analysis should be considered when unexplained marrow failure is present, especially with family history or other organ involvement.

Premature greying of hair—often defined clinically as significant hair graying before age 30—is a common but nonspecific sign of telomere shortening. In the context of other features (e.g., family history, IPF, cytopenias), early graying can be an important systemic clue to a TBD. Isolated premature greying without other features is insufficient to diagnose a TBD but may raise pretest suspicion.

Clinical decision points for adult presentations include the threshold to pursue telomere length testing and genetic testing. Consider evaluation for TBD in adults with: familial pulmonary fibrosis, unexplained cirrhosis at a young age, marrow failure without clear autoimmune or toxic cause, or combinations of these phenotypes especially when accompanied by premature greying, mucocutaneous findings, or a suggestive family history. A working diagnostic approach is to measure leukocyte telomere length (by flow-FISH or quantitative PCR depending on availability) and proceed to targeted gene panel sequencing if telomere shortening is detected or clinical suspicion remains high.

# Multisystem involvement: skin, hair, mucosa, liver, lung, bone marrow, endocrine and skeletal manifestations

TBDs are inherently multisystemic because telomere shortening affects tissues with high replicative demand. Skin and adnexal structures: in addition to reticular hyperpigmentation and nail dystrophy, TBD patients may have atrophy, telangiectasia, poikiloderma, photosensitivity, and abnormal wound healing. Hair involvement includes early greying, hair thinning, and alopecia; abnormal hair growth patterns may be subtle but support systemic telomere biology dysfunction.

Mucosa: oral leukoplakia is the classic mucosal lesion. Other mucosal manifestations include esophageal strictures, atrophic gastritis, and chronic mucosal ulcerations, reflecting impaired epithelial regeneration.

Liver: hepatic manifestations range from asymptomatic transaminase elevations to fibrosis and cirrhosis with portal hypertension. Nodular regenerative hyperplasia without overt fibrosis is seen and can produce portal hypertension with preserved synthetic function until late stages. Hepatopulmonary syndrome and portopulmonary hypertension are potential complications following progressive liver disease.

Lung: interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis are common adult manifestations. The histopathology is often usual interstitial pneumonia but can include nonspecific interstitial pneumonia or organizing pneumonia patterns. Accelerated progression of fibrosis is common, and susceptibility to acute exacerbations is increased.

Bone marrow: marrow hypoplasia leading to cytopenias is a core feature. Pancytopenia, macrocytosis, and dysplastic changes are frequently observed. There is also an increased risk of MDS and AML. Bone marrow biopsy commonly shows hypocellularity in classic aplastic presentations, but hypercellularity with dysplasia may occur as clonal hematopoiesis develops.

Endocrine: endocrinopathies reported include hypothyroidism and gonadal dysfunction, including premature ovarian insufficiency and testicular failure. Short stature and delayed puberty can occur in pediatric cases. These failures stem from inadequate stem/progenitor cell maintenance in endocrine tissues.

Skeletal: skeletal abnormalities include osteopenia/osteoporosis, metaphyseal dysplasia in severe pediatric forms, and short stature. Craniofacial abnormalities and dental anomalies are more prominent in syndromic pediatric presentations.

Across organ systems, there is an interplay between cell-intrinsic telomere-driven replicative limits and extrinsic stresses (inflammation, toxins, infections) that influence clinical expression. For clinicians, this means careful attention to subclinical organ dysfunction: serial pulmonary function testing, liver imaging and function tests including synthetic markers, and regular hematologic monitoring are reasonable in at-risk individuals.

# Spectrum, age of onset and organ predominance associated with specific genes

There is important genotype–phenotype correlation in TBDs, though variability exists even within families carrying the same mutation. Genes encoding core telomerase components (TERT, TERC) commonly produce dominant inheritance with variable penetrance and age-dependent expression. Heterozygous mutations in TERT or TERC often present in adults with pulmonary fibrosis, liver disease, or marrow failure, with median age of onset typically in the 40s–60s but with wide range. In contrast, X-linked DKC1 mutations classically produce earlier onset DC in males, frequently with the mucocutaneous triad by childhood. Recessive or biallelic mutations in genes like RTEL1, PARN, and CTC1 can cause more severe, early-onset disease including HHS, Coats plus, or childhood-onset marrow failure.

Specific associations include:
- DKC1: X-linked DC; early mucocutaneous features, bone marrow failure in childhood; high penetrance in hemizygous males.
- TERT and TERC: autosomal dominant with variable penetrance; associated with adult-onset pulmonary fibrosis, cryptogenic cirrhosis, and hematologic disease; often familial pulmonary fibrosis kindreds.
- TINF2: often associated with severe DC and earlier onset; frequently associated with very short telomeres even in childhood.
- RTEL1 and PARN: biallelic mutations can cause HHS or severe DC phenotypes; heterozygous mutations may predispose to adult-onset pulmonary fibrosis or liver disease.
- CTC1: associated with Coats plus; early-onset retinal and cerebral vascular disease plus systemic features.
- WRAP53 (TCAB1), NOP10, NHP2: components of the telomerase ribonucleoprotein complex can cause DC, often with early mucocutaneous and marrow manifestations.

Penetrance is age-dependent and influenced by environmental exposures and genetic background. For example, family members with the same TERT mutation can present with IPF in one relative and marrow failure or asymptomatic short telomeres in another. Anticipation—earlier and more severe disease in successive generations—has been described and may reflect inherited shorter telomere length and compound genetic or epigenetic factors.

From a clinical standpoint, knowledge of the implicated gene affects counseling about expected organ risk, recommended surveillance, and transplant considerations. For example, a confirmed TERT mutation in a patient with pulmonary fibrosis prompts assessment for liver disease and bone marrow reserve before initiating procedures or immunosuppression. Identification of a DKC1 mutation in a child necessitates early hematologic and immunologic monitoring and genetic counseling for family members.

# Natural history, complications and cancer risk

The natural history of TBDs varies by genotype, age of onset, and environmental exposures. In severe pediatric forms, progressive marrow failure and life-limiting neurologic or pulmonary disease may occur in infancy or early childhood. In adults, organ-specific disease often progresses slowly but can accelerate. Pulmonary fibrosis can lead to progressive respiratory insufficiency over years; liver disease can progress to cirrhosis and portal hypertension; marrow failure can evolve to transfusion dependence, MDS, or AML.

Complications of TBDs derive from organ dysfunction and from increased sensitivity to cytotoxic stress. Hematologic complications include severe infections from neutropenia, hemorrhage from thrombocytopenia, and anemia-related end-organ ischemia. Transfusion dependence can result in iron overload, necessitating chelation if chronic. Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) offers a potential cure for marrow failure but is complicated by increased pulmonary toxicity, hepatic veno-occlusive disease, and poor tolerance of standard conditioning regimens. Reduced-intensity conditioning protocols and careful pre-transplant organ assessment are recommended.

Pulmonary complications include progressive fibrosis, respiratory failure, and increased perioperative and post-transplant morbidity. Immunosuppression and cytotoxic agents can exacerbate pulmonary and hepatic injury. Liver complications include portal hypertension, variceal bleeding, and hepatic decompensation; liver transplantation in TBDs carries additional complexities due to hematologic and pulmonary comorbidities.

Cancer risk is elevated in TBDs. There is an increased incidence of hematologic malignancies—MDS and AML are notably overrepresented—and solid tumors such as squamous cell carcinomas of the head and neck and skin cancers. Mechanistic models propose that critically short telomeres cause genomic instability that can both constrain cell proliferation via senescence and select for clones that bypass senescence and accumulate oncogenic mutations. Relative risk estimates vary by cohort and gene, but malignancy risk is an important long-term concern. Clinical surveillance should be individualized: for example, regular oral and dermatologic examinations, low threshold for biopsying suspicious lesions, and hematologic surveillance for clonal cytogenetic changes or rising cytopenias.

Management principles involve organ-directed therapy, avoidance of therapies with heightened toxicity risk in TBDs, genetic counseling, and surveillance for complications. Practical steps include baseline and periodic hematologic testing (CBC with differential and reticulocyte count), pulmonary evaluation (spirometry and diffusion capacity, with high-resolution CT when indicated), liver biochemistry and imaging, and cancer surveillance tailored to individual risks. Reproductive counseling is essential because offspring may inherit telomere-risk alleles and potentially present with earlier or more severe disease.

When interpreting telomere length and genetic testing, clinicians should integrate results with the clinical context. Telomere shortening is a biomarker but not determinative of clinical course; many individuals with short telomeres remain asymptomatic for years. Conversely, absence of identifiable pathogenic variants does not exclude a TBD. Multidisciplinary care including hematology, pulmonology, hepatology, medical genetics, and when needed, transplant teams, improves outcomes through coordinated surveillance and individualized therapy.

Summary

Telomere biology disorders represent a broad clinical spectrum from classic dyskeratosis congenita with mucocutaneous findings to severe multisystem pediatric syndromes and adult-onset organ-predominant disease such as pulmonary fibrosis and cryptogenic cirrhosis. Recognition requires attention to subtle mucocutaneous signs, family history, unexplained cytopenias, and early-onset organ dysfunction. Genotype informs likely organ predominance and age of onset but does not fully predict individual outcomes. Management centers on organ-directed care, careful choice of interventions given increased therapy-related risks, genetic counseling, and vigilant surveillance for complications including marrow failure and cancer. Early identification of TBDs enables tailored monitoring, mitigates iatrogenic harm, and guides family planning and transplant decisions.
