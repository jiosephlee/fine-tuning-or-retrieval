Introduction

Genetic testing has become an essential component of modern clinical medicine for the diagnosis, management, and counseling of patients with suspected inherited disorders. The rapid evolution of sequencing technologies and bioinformatic methods means that clinicians must understand the strengths, limitations, and interpretation frameworks that govern how test results are generated and reported. This chapter reviews diagnostic next-generation sequencing (NGS) modalities—targeted gene panels, whole-exome sequencing (WES), and whole-genome sequencing (WGS)—and complementary technologies for detecting copy-number variants (CNVs) and structural rearrangements. It then explains how variants are annotated and interpreted using population databases, in silico tools, and the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics/Association for Molecular Pathology (ACMG/AMP) classification framework. Finally, the chapter covers practical laboratory and clinical follow-up measures—Sanger confirmation, segregation analysis, functional assays to clarify variants of uncertain significance (VUS)—and genetic counseling considerations for patients and at-risk relatives.

# Next-generation sequencing methods: gene panels, whole-exome sequencing, whole-genome sequencing

Next-generation sequencing encompasses several laboratory strategies tailored to specific clinical questions. Targeted gene panels sequence a predefined set of genes associated with a particular phenotype (for example, cardiomyopathy, epilepsy, or hereditary cancer). Whole-exome sequencing captures and sequences the protein-coding exons of the genome (~1–2% of genomic sequence) where an estimated 85–90% of disease-causing single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) and small insertions/deletions have been found. Whole-genome sequencing sequences nearly all nuclear DNA, encompassing coding and noncoding regions, enabling detection of variants missed by capture-based approaches.

Targeted panels offer deep coverage of relevant genes, which increases sensitivity for low-level mosaicism and optimizes detection of sequence variants and small indels. Panels are cost-effective, provide more rapid turnaround times, and reduce incidental findings because sequencing is limited to clinically relevant genes. However, panels are limited by the content of the panel; newly discovered genes or atypical presentations involving genes outside the panel will not be detected. Panels may also have gaps in coverage due to difficult-to-sequence regions (high GC content, pseudogenes). For diseases with strong phenotypic specificity, a high-quality panel is often the first-line test.

WES is preferred when the phenotype is heterogeneous, when prior panel testing is non-diagnostic, or when a broad, hypothesis-free search is clinically justified. The principal advantage of WES is its breadth: it enables discovery of pathogenic variants in genes not yet associated with the phenotype at the time of testing and allows reanalysis as gene-disease associations evolve. Limitations of WES include incomplete or nonuniform exon capture (some exons poorly covered or missing), limited detection of CNVs and structural variants without specialized algorithms, difficulty with homologous regions and pseudogenes, and reduced sensitivity in repetitive regions and for intronic or regulatory variants outside captured intervals.

WGS provides the most comprehensive sequence data with relatively uniform coverage across the genome, better detection of structural variants (large deletions/duplications, balanced rearrangements), and the ability to interrogate noncoding regulatory regions that can influence gene expression. WGS also reduces capture-related biases and can better detect complex indels and tandem repeat expansions when combined with appropriate bioinformatic pipelines. Limitations include higher cost (though this is decreasing), larger data volume requiring more complex analysis and storage, and a greater likelihood of incidental findings. For clinical use, WGS requires rigorous validated pipelines and careful interpretation frameworks.

For all NGS modalities, key laboratory metrics are coverage (depth) and breadth. Coverage is commonly reported as mean depth (e.g., 100×) and percentage of targeted bases covered at a minimum depth (e.g., 20× or 10×). Clinical laboratories typically target coverage thresholds sufficient to achieve high sensitivity for heterozygous variants; for standard germline testing, 95–99% of targeted bases covered at ≥20× is a common benchmark, though assays vary. It is critical clinically to review regions of poor coverage (and strategies to address them, such as reflex Sanger sequencing) because false-negative results occur when pathogenic variants lie in inadequately covered regions.

# Copy-number variant detection, Sanger confirmation and segregation analysis

Copy-number variants (CNVs) — deletions and duplications that range from single exons to megabase-scale segments — are a common cause of genetic disease. CNV detection is achieved by several laboratory methods. Chromosomal microarray (CMA) remains the standard first-line test for many developmental disorders: high-resolution array comparative genomic hybridization (aCGH) or SNP arrays reliably detect CNVs down to the tens of kilobases depending on probe density. NGS-based CNV calling leverages read-depth, split-read, and paired-end mapping signals to infer CNVs from panel, WES, or WGS data. WGS provides the best sensitivity for CNVs and structural variants due to more uniform coverage and the availability of multiple orthogonal signals. Multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification (MLPA) is a targeted method frequently used to detect exon-level CNVs in specific genes with high sensitivity. Each method has limits: arrays struggle with small exon-level changes, WES-based CNV detection can be confounded by variable exon capture efficiency, and MLPA is limited to the genomic regions interrogated.

Sanger sequencing remains the gold standard for confirmation of many sequence-level variants detected by NGS, particularly for clinical reports. Historically, laboratories have reflexively confirmed NGS-detected variants by Sanger, especially for single heterozygous SNVs and small indels in clinically actionable genes. As NGS technologies and analytic pipelines matured, many labs now apply quality metrics to determine when Sanger confirmation is unnecessary (for example, variants with high depth and allele balance and passing all quality filters). Still, Sanger remains essential when NGS reads are ambiguous, when variants occur in homopolymer or low-complexity regions, when a pathogenic variant will directly influence major interventions, or when NGS data are of insufficient quality.

Segregation analysis assesses whether a variant co-segregates with disease within a family and is a powerful tool for strengthening or weakening pathogenicity assertions. Simple examples include demonstrating a variant occurred de novo in an affected proband (which supports pathogenicity) or showing that a variant is present in an unaffected older relative with high penetrance (which argues against pathogenicity). The ACMG/AMP framework explicitly includes segregation evidence: multiple affected relatives carrying the variant provides supporting evidence (PP1) with different weightings depending on the number of meioses and the strength of phenotype concordance, whereas lack of segregation can be strong evidence against pathogenicity. Properly performed segregation analysis requires accurate clinical phenotyping of relatives, appropriate sample consent, and awareness of confounders such as variable expressivity, reduced penetrance, phenocopies, and age-related onset that can make interpretation equivocal.

In clinical practice, confirmatory testing and segregation testing are balanced with timeliness, cost, and the clinical actionability of a result. Laboratories and clinicians should coordinate to select the most informative tests: reflex MLPA or targeted CNV testing when a single-exon deletion is suspected, Sanger confirmation when NGS metrics are borderline, and strategic testing of relatives (parents, affected siblings) to evaluate de novo status or co-segregation.

# Variant annotation, population frequency databases (gnomAD), in silico prediction tools

Variant annotation is the process of enriching raw variant calls with clinically relevant information: genomic coordinates, predicted effect on transcripts and proteins, allele frequencies in population databases, prior clinical assertions, conservation metrics, and scores from in silico algorithms predicting pathogenicity. Annotation pipelines map variants to canonical transcripts, determine consequence terms (missense, nonsense, frameshift, splice-site, synonymous), and provide context such as overlap with known functional domains.

Population frequency databases are critical to classify variants. The Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD) is the largest public resource, aggregating exome and genome sequences from tens of thousands to hundreds of thousands of unrelated individuals (release sizes evolve over time). gnomAD reports population-specific and overall allele frequencies and flags variants observed only in specific populations. A high allele frequency in an appropriate population is strong evidence against a variant being a fully penetrant pathogenic variant in a rare Mendelian disorder. ACMG criteria include BA1 (allele frequency is greater than expected for disorder) and BS1 (allele frequency is greater than expected, strong evidence against pathogenicity). The thresholds for BA1/BS1 are disease-dependent and should be set using disease prevalence, mode of inheritance, allelic heterogeneity, and penetrance. For example, a variant with allele frequency >5% in gnomAD is almost never disease-causing for a rare, severe Mendelian disorder, but disorders with founder variants or variable penetrance require careful gene-specific thresholds. Clinicians must also be mindful of gnomAD limitations: it includes some individuals with undiagnosed disease, has population substructure, and is less well-represented for many non-European ancestries.

ClinVar is another essential resource that aggregates clinical assertions from laboratories and clinicians. ClinVar submissions include variant classifications and sometimes the underlying evidence; however, inconsistencies can occur between submitters. The ClinGen resource provides gene- and disease-specific curation that refines interpretation for particular genes and syndromes.

In silico prediction tools provide computational assessments of potential deleteriousness, but they are supportive, not definitive. For missense variants, commonly used tools include SIFT (predicts tolerated vs. deleterious based on conservation and amino-acid properties) and PolyPhen-2 (predicts possibly/probably damaging using structural and comparative features). Meta-predictors such as CADD (Combined Annotation Dependent Depletion), REVEL, and MetaLR integrate multiple inputs and often perform better than single predictors. For splice variants, tools like MaxEntScan, SpliceAI, and NNSplice estimate the impact on splice sites or creation of cryptic splice sites. Conservation metrics (PhyloP, PhastCons) and protein-domain annotations provide additional evidence. The ACMG/AMP guidelines permit in silico evidence as supporting (PP3) or benign supporting (BP4) but caution against over-reliance; concordant predictions from multiple validated algorithms lend more weight than a single discordant score.

Annotation also includes population-specific annotations (subpopulation allele frequencies), functional domain maps, and references to published case reports or functional studies. Automated pipelines must be reviewed by clinical laboratory staff to ensure appropriate transcript selection and assessment of complex variants. Because databases and tools update frequently, reannotation and periodic reinterpretation can yield new insights into previously reported variants.

# ACMG/AMP criteria for variant classification (pathogenic, likely pathogenic, VUS, likely benign, benign)

The ACMG/AMP variant classification framework provides a standardized, semi-quantitative method for interpreting sequence variants by integrating multiple lines of evidence. It assigns evidence items to tiers of strength—very strong (PVS), strong (PS), moderate (PM), and supporting (PP) for pathogenic evidence, and standalone (BA), strong (BS), and supporting (BP) for benign evidence. Combining these evidence categories leads to five final classes: pathogenic, likely pathogenic, variant of uncertain significance (VUS), likely benign, and benign.

Pathogenic classifications commonly result from combinations such as: one very strong (PVS1) loss-of-function variant in a gene where loss of function (LoF) is a known mechanism of disease plus additional supporting evidence, or multiple strong criteria. PVS1 is applied to null variants (nonsense, frameshift, canonical ±1/2 splice sites, initiation codon loss, single- or multiexon deletions) when LoF is an established disease mechanism in the gene. The laboratory must consider exon location (escape from nonsense-mediated decay), alternative transcripts, and reading-frame effects when applying PVS1.

De novo occurrence (PS2) is strong evidence when confirmed by parental testing and in the context of a compatible phenotype. PS3 denotes well-established functional studies showing a damaging effect. PS4 refers to case-control data or enrichment in affected individuals. PM and PP criteria include allele frequency being absent or extremely rare in controls (PM2), co-segregation in multiple affected family members (PP1), multiple independent observations of the same variant in unrelated affected individuals (PS4 or PM3 in recessive conditions), computational evidence (PP3), and location in a mutational hotspot or functional domain (PM1).

Benign evidence includes BA1 (allele frequency incompatible with disease), BS1 (allele frequency greater than expected), BS2 (observed in healthy adult individuals for a dominant disorder), BS3 (well-established functional studies show no damaging effect), and BP4 (benign computational evidence). The combination rules specify how different strengths of evidence add up; for instance, one very strong plus one strong or two moderate could suffice for pathogenic. Laboratories should apply gene- and disease-specific specifications of the ACMG/AMP criteria; many loci require refinement of general thresholds based on penetrance, allelic heterogeneity, and disease prevalence. The Clinical Genome Resource (ClinGen) provides gene curation and variant interpretation expert panels that specify rules for particular genes.

Variants that do not meet criteria for pathogenic or benign categories are reported as variants of uncertain significance. Importantly, VUS should not be used for clinical management decisions such as predictive testing, and they should prompt additional steps (segregation studies, functional assays, reanalysis) to resolve uncertainty.

# Functional assays and research strategies to clarify VUS

Functional evidence can be decisive in classifying variants, especially when genetic and population data are inconclusive. Well-established functional assays that demonstrate damaging effects provide PS3-level evidence, while high-quality assays demonstrating no effect support BS3 (benign). The choice of assay must match the gene’s biology and disease mechanism. Examples include enzymatic activity assays for metabolic enzymes, cellular localization and trafficking studies for membrane proteins, electrophysiology for ion channel variants, reporter assays for transcriptional regulators, splice assays (minigene or RNA studies) for putative splicing variants, and model organism studies (zebrafish, fly, mouse) for developmental phenotypes.

RNA studies are particularly informative for variants that may affect splicing. Reverse transcription PCR and sequencing from patient RNA can show exon skipping, intron retention, or activation of cryptic splice sites. If patient tissue expressing the gene is not available, minigene assays in cell lines can recapitulate splicing outcomes. Interpretation must consider whether detected splice changes lead to frameshifts and nonsense-mediated decay, which may support PVS1-equivalent evidence if LoF is the mechanism.

For missense variants, functional characterization requires assays that reflect the effect of interest: enzymatic kinetics, ligand binding, stability/degradation, interaction with other proteins, or downstream pathway readouts. High-throughput saturation mutagenesis approaches, coupled with functional readouts (variant effect maps), are emerging to systematically measure the impact of all possible substitutions in a gene; these datasets can be powerful resources for variant interpretation but require validation for clinical use.

When functional work is not clinically available, collaboration with research laboratories may be pursued. Prior to research testing, appropriate consent and an understanding that research assays may not be performed in a CLIA-certified environment are necessary; if results will inform clinical care, confirmation in a validated clinical assay may be required. Functional assays must be well-controlled, reproducible, and disease-relevant to be weighed heavily. For example, an in vitro overexpression assay that does not recapitulate native protein context may be less persuasive than an assay that uses physiologic expression levels or patient-derived cells.

Additional research strategies include case aggregation, natural history studies, and family-based recruitment to enable segregation and case-control analyses. International data-sharing initiatives (ClinVar, Matchmaker Exchange) facilitate identification of additional patients with the same variant and phenotype, strengthening evidence for pathogenicity. Collectively, these approaches can move a VUS toward a more definitive classification.

# Genetic counselling considerations, cascade testing, and implications for relatives

Genetic testing carries implications beyond the index patient; counseling must address test purpose, possible outcomes (pathogenic, likely pathogenic, VUS, likely benign, benign), limitations, and potential psychosocial and familial consequences. Pre-test counseling should elicit an accurate family history to frame the choice of test (panel vs. exome vs. genome), discuss possible incidental or secondary findings (for example, actionable findings unrelated to the primary indication), and obtain informed consent for testing and for sharing results with at-risk relatives.

When a pathogenic or likely pathogenic variant is identified, cascade testing of at-risk relatives is commonly recommended to determine who carries the variant and who may benefit from surveillance, risk-reducing interventions, or reproductive counseling. Cascade testing is typically targeted: a single-site test for the known family variant is performed in relatives. Genetic counselors and clinicians should prioritize testing first-degree relatives and clinically relevant contacts based on inheritance pattern (e.g., autosomal dominant vertical transmission, autosomal recessive needs both parents and siblings). For de novo variants, parental testing informs recurrence risk and is part of postpartum counseling when relevant.

VUS results pose a particular counseling challenge. VUS should not be used to guide medical management, predictive testing in relatives, or reproductive decision-making. Counselors should explain the uncertainty, potential for reclassification over time, and options for further evaluation—such as segregation testing with affected relatives, functional studies if available, and recontact mechanisms. Family members should not undergo predictive testing based on a VUS; if a relative requests testing, they must be counseled that a negative or positive result will not be clinically actionable until the variant is reclassified.

Ethical considerations include testing minors for adult-onset conditions: standard counseling advises deferring predictive testing of minors for conditions without immediate medical benefit during childhood. Reproductive options with known pathogenic variants include prenatal diagnosis and preimplantation genetic testing; counseling should cover these options, including limits given residual risk and variant interpretation constraints.

Privacy, disclosure, and duty to warn relatives are practical and ethical issues. Clinicians should encourage patients to share actionable results with relatives and can provide family letters that explain the result and recommend testing. In jurisdictions where a clinician may have a duty to inform at-risk relatives of serious, actionable risk, institutional policies and legal frameworks determine obligations. Genetic information may have implications for insurance and employment, and counselors should review local protections (for example, GINA in the United States protects against discrimination by health insurers and employers but does not cover life or disability insurance).

Operationally, cascade testing programs and registries can facilitate family outreach and efficient testing workflows. Laboratories typically offer single-site testing at lower cost for relatives, but logistics require confirmatory sample handling and consent documentation. When a family shares a pathogenic variant across geographical locations or healthcare systems, clinicians should collaborate with genetic specialists to ensure consistent interpretation and management.

Conclusion

Diagnostic genetic testing requires integration of laboratory science, computational annotation, and careful clinical interpretation. Understanding the technical aspects of NGS modalities, appropriate use of CNV-detection tools, and the significance of confirmation and segregation testing are foundational. Variant classification under the ACMG/AMP framework provides a structured approach, but gene- and disease-specific specifications, population context, and functional data remain vital. Variants of uncertain significance need targeted strategies—segregation, functional studies, data sharing—to resolve uncertainty. Finally, genetic counseling and cascade testing are central to translating genetic diagnoses into patient-centered care while respecting ethical considerations and familial implications. Ongoing education, multidisciplinary collaboration, and data sharing are necessary to improve diagnostic yield and to provide accurate, actionable results for patients and their families.
