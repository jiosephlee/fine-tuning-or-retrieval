Chapter 17: Neurological Manifestations of B12 Deficiency and Multisystem Disorders

Introduction

Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is essential for nervous system integrity and hematopoiesis. Deficiency produces a spectrum of neurological injury ranging from subtle cognitive changes to severe myelopathy and peripheral neuropathy. In addition, a number of multisystem and inherited marrow failure syndromes produce neuropathic or neuromuscular manifestations that overlap clinically with B12-related disease. Understanding the molecular mechanisms of cobalamin-dependent pathways, the clinical patterns of neuropathy, and the electrophysiologic signatures that distinguish axonal from demyelinating injury is critical for accurate diagnosis and targeted management. This chapter reviews the pathophysiology of B12-related neural injury, the clinical phenotypes encountered, interpretation of electrodiagnostic testing, relevant differential diagnoses in systemic disease, treatment strategies and prognosis, and neuromuscular features associated with telomere biology disorders (TBDs) and inherited marrow failure syndromes.

# Mechanisms of B12-related neurological injury (myelin synthesis, methylation defects)

Cobalamin plays two enzymatic roles that are central to nervous system function: as a cofactor for methionine synthase (5-methyltetrahydrofolate—homocysteine methyltransferase) and for methylmalonyl-CoA mutase. Methionine synthase catalyzes the remethylation of homocysteine to methionine using 5-methyltetrahydrofolate as the methyl donor, regenerating tetrahydrofolate and providing methionine for S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) synthesis. SAM is the universal methyl donor for multiple methylation reactions including those necessary for myelin maintenance, neurotransmitter metabolism, and gene regulation. Impaired methionine synthase activity in B12 deficiency leads to decreased SAM and disturbed methylation reactions. Neuronal and oligodendroglial membrane lipids and proteins require ongoing methylation for myelin maintenance and repair; impaired methylation is a major mechanism for demyelination and myelin vacuolation seen with cobalamin deficiency.

The second important role, methylmalonyl-CoA mutase, converts methylmalonyl-CoA to succinyl-CoA in the metabolism of certain odd-chain fatty acids and branched-chain amino acids. Without adequate B12, methylmalonic acid (MMA) accumulates. Elevated MMA is thought to interfere with myelin stability by incorporating abnormal fatty acids into neuronal lipids or by exerting direct neurotoxic effects. Experimental models show that increased MMA and consequent abnormal metabolites can promote myelin breakdown and neuronal dysfunction.

Taken together, these pathways explain why B12 deficiency produces a mixed picture of myelopathy and peripheral neuropathy. The spinal cord pathology typically consists of selective involvement of the dorsal columns and lateral corticospinal tracts (subacute combined degeneration): dorsal column demyelination explains loss of vibration and proprioception, while corticospinal tract involvement explains weakness and spasticity. Peripheral nerves show a spectrum from predominantly demyelinating features to axonal degeneration, depending on the stage and severity of deficiency. Additional contributing mechanisms include oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, microvascular changes from elevated homocysteine, and impaired neuronal protein synthesis. The connections between biochemical derangements and structural damage explain the clinical heterogeneity: patients may present primarily with sensory ataxia, with combined sensory and motor deficits, or with predominantly axonal sensorimotor polyneuropathy.

# Clinical patterns: sensory, motor, combined and chronic progressive neuropathies

B12 deficiency produces several recognizable clinical patterns depending on which elements of the nervous system are most affected and on chronicity.

Sensory-predominant neuropathy and dorsal column dysfunction often present insidiously. Patients report numbness, paresthesias, and a loss of vibration and position sense beginning in a stocking-glove distribution but often with pronounced impairment of proprioception. Impaired joint position sense and vibration leads to sensory ataxia: patients may have a wide-based, unsteady gait, positive Romberg sign, and difficulty with tandem gait. Pain and small-fiber symptoms (burning pain) can occur but are less prominent than large-fiber dysfunction.

Motor involvement ranges from mild distal weakness to more diffuse paresis. Distal symmetric weakness is typical in peripheral polyneuropathy, whereas corticospinal tract involvement produces spasticity, hyperreflexia, and Babinski signs. In subacute combined degeneration, the coexistence of dorsal column loss and corticospinal tract dysfunction leads to a combination of sensory ataxia with upper motor neuron signs in the lower extremities. Hand weakness and loss of fine motor control may accompany sensory loss.

Combined neuropathies with both sensory and motor features are common. Distal symmetric sensorimotor polyneuropathy with reduced or absent deep tendon reflexes is a frequent presentation, mimicking other metabolic neuropathies such as diabetic polyneuropathy. Symptom onset may be subacute over weeks to months or slowly progressive over years. The chronic progressive neuropathy phenotype is especially likely in longer-standing deficiency; irreversible axonal loss and neuronal death increase the risk of incomplete recovery after treatment.

Autonomic involvement (orthostatic hypotension, urinary dysfunction) is less frequent than in some other neuropathies but can occur in severe or prolonged deficiency. Cognitive and psychiatric manifestations, including memory impairment, mood changes, and even psychosis or delirium, reflect central nervous system involvement and may coexist with peripheral neuropathy.

Clinical recognition requires attention to accompanying hematologic features (macrocytic anemia, hypersegmented neutrophils) but it is important to remember that neurologic symptoms can precede hematologic abnormalities in a substantial minority of patients. Therefore a high index of suspicion is warranted when neurological signs consistent with dorsal column dysfunction or a subacute sensorimotor polyneuropathy arise, particularly in patients with risk factors for cobalamin deficiency (e.g., pernicious anemia, gastrectomy, prolonged proton pump inhibitor or metformin use, vegan diet, malabsorption).

# Electromyography and nerve conduction study interpretation for axonal vs demyelinating neuropathies

Electrodiagnostic testing—nerve conduction studies (NCS) and electromyography (EMG)—provides objective characterization of peripheral nerve dysfunction and helps distinguish primarily axonal from demyelinating processes. In B12 deficiency the electrodiagnostic picture may vary with the stage of disease and with involvement of central tracts versus peripheral nerves.

Key NCS features of demyelinating neuropathy include markedly slowed nerve conduction velocities (NCV), prolonged distal motor latencies, increased temporal dispersion, and conduction block (a drop in CMAP amplitude between proximal and distal stimulation points). Sensory nerve action potentials (SNAPs) may be preserved in early acquired demyelination because sensory axons remain intact while myelin is affected. Electrophysiologic criteria for acquired demyelinating polyneuropathy define specific thresholds, for example NCV less than 70% of lower limit of normal in upper limbs or distal latency prolongation more than 130% of ULN, but precise cutoffs depend upon the laboratory's normative data.

Axonal neuropathy is characterized by reduced compound muscle action potential (CMAP) and SNAP amplitudes with relatively preserved conduction velocities and distal latencies (unless secondary demyelination is present). EMG demonstrates signs of denervation: fibrillation potentials, positive sharp waves, and reduced recruitment with large-amplitude, long-duration motor unit potentials reflecting chronic reinnervation. In length-dependent axonal polyneuropathies, a distal-to-proximal gradient of amplitude loss is typical, and sensory responses (SNAPs) are often reduced early.

In B12 deficiency the electrodiagnostic pattern can be mixed. Early in the course, demyelinating features related to deficits in myelin maintenance may predominate in both peripheral nerves and central tracts; later, chronic axonal degeneration becomes more apparent with reduced amplitudes and denervation on needle EMG. Distinguishing primary demyelination from secondary demyelination due to axonal loss requires careful interpretation: major clues include disproportionate slowing of conduction velocity relative to amplitude loss, the presence of conduction block and temporal dispersion (suggesting primary myelin injury), and comparison across multiple nerves and muscles to identify a length-dependent pattern.

Electrodiagnostic testing should be interpreted in the context of the clinical picture and laboratory studies. For example, a patient with clear dorsal column signs and normal NCS but evidence of subacute combined degeneration on imaging may have central rather than peripheral pathology; conversely, a patient with distal symmetric sensory loss and reduced SNAP amplitudes on NCS likely has peripheral axonal neuropathy. Repeat testing can be useful to document progression or recovery after treatment.

# Differential diagnosis of polyneuropathy in systemic disease

Polyneuropathy in the context of systemic disease has a broad differential diagnosis. Distinguishing B12-related neuropathy from other causes relies on clinical pattern, laboratory testing, electrodiagnostics, and consideration of systemic features and exposures.

Metabolic causes are common. Diabetes mellitus is the most frequent cause of distal symmetric polyneuropathy and typically produces a length-dependent sensorimotor axonal neuropathy. Uremia due to chronic kidney disease can produce a symmetric axonal neuropathy. Hypothyroidism may cause a mixed neuropathy, sometimes with entrapment neuropathies. Vitamin deficiencies other than B12, such as thiamine (B1) deficiency, cause predominantly small-fiber neuropathies and Wernicke-related central findings; vitamin E deficiency produces spinocerebellar and peripheral neuropathies with ataxia. Copper deficiency causes a myeloneuropathy with dorsal column dysfunction and axonal neuropathy and may be clinically indistinguishable from B12 deficiency; serum copper and ceruloplasmin should be measured when the presentation resembles subacute combined degeneration.

Toxic and medication-induced neuropathies must be considered. Chemotherapeutic agents (e.g., platinum compounds, taxanes, vinca alkaloids, bortezomib) produce dose-dependent axonal sensorimotor neuropathies. Alcohol-related neuropathy usually produces distal symmetric axonal loss. Heavy metals (lead, arsenic) and certain antibiotics (e.g., metronidazole) can cause neuropathy.

Inflammatory and immune-mediated neuropathies include Guillain-Barré syndrome (acute inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy, AIDP), chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy (CIDP), and vasculitic neuropathy. These conditions tend to show demyelinating features (AIDP/CIDP) or multifocal axonal features with painful asymmetric deficits (vasculitic neuropathy). Systemic autoimmune diseases such as systemic lupus erythematosus, rheumatoid arthritis, and sarcoidosis may produce neuropathies by immune mechanisms or by granulomatous infiltration.

Infectious causes include HIV-associated neuropathy, Lyme disease, leprosy, and diphtheria. Paraneoplastic neuropathies can precede cancer diagnosis and often present with subacute sensory neuronopathy (dorsal root ganglionopathy) producing profound sensory loss with preserved reflexes and patchy absent SNAPs.

Inherited neuropathies (Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease) are usually chronic and symmetric with onset in childhood or adolescence, but adult-onset forms exist and must be considered when family history or foot deformities are present.

Systemic features and laboratory testing guide prioritization. Macrocytic anemia, low serum B12 (<200 pg/mL), elevated methylmalonic acid (MMA) and homocysteine support cobalamin deficiency. Normal serum B12 levels in the low-normal range (200–350 pg/mL) with elevated MMA suggest functional deficiency. Reference ranges vary by lab, but commonly total serum B12: 200–900 pg/mL; methylmalonic acid: 0.08–0.56 µmol/L (lab-specific). When copper deficiency, hypothyroidism, diabetes, renal failure, toxins, or medications are identified, targeted management for the underlying cause is indicated. When the electrodiagnostic profile suggests demyelination, consider immune-mediated neuropathy and evaluate for CSF albuminocytologic dissociation, antiganglioside or paraneoplastic antibodies, and systemic autoimmune disease. Always consider overlapping etiologies; for example, a diabetic patient can concurrently have B12 deficiency because of metformin therapy.

# Management and prognosis of B12-related neurological deficits

Management of B12-related neurological disease centers on prompt diagnosis, removal or treatment of the underlying cause of deficiency, and timely cobalamin repletion. Because neurological injury can be irreversible if left untreated, clinicians should not delay therapy while obtaining all confirmatory tests when clinical suspicion is high.

Diagnosis: Measure serum B12 and, if available, methylmalonic acid (MMA) and homocysteine. Elevated MMA is the most sensitive indicator of tissue cobalamin deficiency. Anti-intrinsic factor antibodies and anti-parietal cell antibodies are useful when autoimmune pernicious anemia is suspected. Evaluate for malabsorption causes (history of gastric surgery, small intestinal disease, celiac disease), medications (metformin, proton pump inhibitors), and dietary insufficiency.

Treatment regimens vary by severity and clinical context. Traditional parenteral therapy includes intramuscular (IM) cyanocobalamin 1000 mcg daily for 1 week, then weekly for 4 weeks, then monthly for life in cases of irreversible malabsorption (e.g., pernicious anemia). An alternative is IM hydroxocobalamin 1000–2000 mcg every other day for 2 weeks, then monthly. High-dose oral therapy (e.g., 1000–2000 mcg/day) can be effective even for many patients with malabsorption because passive diffusion permits adequate absorption in some cases; however, parenteral therapy is preferred when there are severe neurologic deficits, malabsorption, or concerns about compliance. Treatment for underlying causes (e.g., stopping metformin if appropriate, treating H. pylori, dietary counseling) should accompany replacement.

Monitoring: Repeat neurological examination and laboratory markers guide response. Hematologic indices (mean corpuscular volume, hemoglobin) usually improve within days to weeks; neurological recovery is slower. Objective neurophysiologic improvement may take months. Measure MMA or homocysteine at baseline and after several months to document biochemical correction when uncertainty exists.

Prognosis depends on duration and severity of neurological involvement. Early treatment, especially within months of symptom onset, often results in substantial improvement and sometimes full recovery of sensory function and gait. Motor recovery can be slower and incomplete if axonal loss has occurred. Long-standing symptoms, particularly chronic proprioceptive loss and ataxia, carry a higher risk of persistent disability. Some central nervous system manifestations (e.g., established spastic paraparesis) may be only partially reversible. Persistent neuropathic pain and paresthesias are possible even after biochemical correction. Therefore early recognition and treatment are pivotal. Rehabilitation, including physical and occupational therapy, is important to maximize functional recovery and to prevent falls in patients with sensory ataxia.

Clinical decision points: In patients with neurologic signs suspicious for B12 deficiency, initiate replacement promptly. If serum B12 is borderline (200–350 pg/mL) and MMA is elevated, treat as deficient. When pernicious anemia is suspected, plan for lifelong therapy. If copper deficiency or other reversible causes are found, correct those conditions as management differs and mismanagement (e.g., giving B12 while missing copper deficiency) may delay appropriate therapy.

# Neuromuscular features associated with TBDs and marrow failure syndromes

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) and inherited marrow failure syndromes encompass a heterogeneous group of genetic diseases (e.g., dyskeratosis congenita, Shwachman-Diamond syndrome, Fanconi anemia) that primarily present with hematologic abnormalities but also have multisystem manifestations including neuromuscular involvement. The pattern and mechanism of neurologic involvement vary by disease, but several themes emerge: developmental anomalies, peripheral neuropathy, myopathy, neuromuscular junction dysfunction, and central nervous system involvement.

In dyskeratosis congenita and other TBDs, short telomeres impair proliferative capacity of stem cell compartments. Although the predominant clinical consequences are bone marrow failure, pulmonary fibrosis, and mucocutaneous findings, neurologic manifestations include developmental delay, cognitive impairment, and less commonly peripheral neuropathies. The neuropathy, when present, may be axonal and slowly progressive. Mechanisms are not fully elucidated but likely reflect impaired maintenance of long-lived cells and tissues subject to replicative stress, along with possible mitochondrial dysfunction and altered DNA damage responses.

Fanconi anemia is principally a DNA repair defect leading to bone marrow failure, congenital anomalies, and cancer predisposition. Neurologic and neuromuscular features may include developmental delay, structural brain abnormalities, peripheral neuropathy, and neuromuscular junction involvement in some patients. Some neuropathies may be secondary to chemotherapeutic exposures or bone marrow transplantation complications rather than intrinsic to the genetic disease.

Shwachman-Diamond syndrome, with exocrine pancreatic insufficiency and bone marrow dysfunction, has been associated with neuromuscular features including hypotonia, motor delay, and occasionally peripheral neuropathy. Again, these manifestations may derive from the multisystem nature of the disorder affecting growth, nutrition, and organ systems that support neuromuscular health.

More broadly, marrow failure syndromes predispose to neuromuscular complications through several mechanisms. Chronic anemia and hypoxia can lead to muscle weakness and reduced exercise tolerance. Nutritional deficiencies from malabsorption or poor intake (e.g., low B12, folate, vitamin D, or copper) can produce neuropathy or myopathy. Recurrent infections, chronic inflammation, and iron overload from transfusions may contribute to metabolic stress and neurotoxicity. Importantly, therapies for marrow failure—including chemotherapeutic agents, immune suppressants, and hematopoietic stem cell transplantation—are significant causes of neuropathic and myopathic complications. For instance, vincristine and other vinca alkaloids cause dose-related axonal neuropathies, while calcineurin inhibitors used post-transplant can cause tremor and neurotoxicity.

When evaluating a patient with a marrow failure syndrome and neurologic symptoms, clinicians must consider primary disease-related neuropathy, nutritional deficiencies (including B12), treatment-related neurotoxicity, paraneoplastic syndromes, and infectious or metabolic complications. Electrophysiologic studies clarify whether the neuropathy is axonal, demyelinating, or mixed; muscle biopsy or MRI may be required for myopathic processes. Genetic testing can identify the underlying syndrome and may predict extra-hematologic manifestations. Management requires a multidisciplinary approach, treating reversible contributors (nutritional deficits, toxins, infections), modifying cytotoxic therapies when possible, and providing symptomatic neurology and rehabilitation care. Prognosis depends on the specific syndrome, extent of neurologic involvement, and complications of systemic disease and its therapies.

Conclusion

Neurologic manifestations of vitamin B12 deficiency and of multisystem/genetic marrow failure disorders are clinically important and mechanistically informative. Cobalamin deficiency produces a spectrum of myelopathy and peripheral neuropathy driven by failures in methylation and abnormal metabolite accumulation with downstream myelin and neuronal injury. Electrodiagnostic testing helps distinguish axonal versus demyelinating patterns, but clinical context and laboratory confirmation are essential. The differential diagnosis of polyneuropathy in systemic disease is broad and includes metabolic, toxic, immune, infectious, and inherited causes; targeted evaluation and early treatment (especially for B12 deficiency) improve the likelihood of neurological recovery. Multisystem genetic disorders such as TBDs and inherited marrow failure syndromes can involve the peripheral and central nervous systems through both disease-intrinsic mechanisms and treatment-related complications, necessitating integrated care and long-term monitoring.
