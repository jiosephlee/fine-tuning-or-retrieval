Introduction

Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) is a chronic, progressive fibrotic interstitial lung disease (ILD) that primarily affects older adults and leads to respiratory failure and premature death. Although labelled "idiopathic," IPF reflects a final common pathway of recurrent alveolar epithelial injury and dysregulated repair resulting in progressive scarring and architectural distortion of the lung. Understanding the clinical presentation, natural history, underlying pathobiology, radiologic and histopathologic patterns, physiologic abnormalities, and the role of genetics is essential for accurate diagnosis, prognostication, and management decisions. This chapter reviews the clinical features and natural history of IPF, the current model of pathophysiology centered on alveolar epithelial injury and fibroblast activation, the high-resolution CT (HRCT) patterns that guide diagnosis (particularly the usual interstitial pneumonia pattern), pulmonary function and gas-exchange abnormalities, the differential diagnosis of fibrotic ILD and the essential role of multidisciplinary discussion, and the impact of genetic predisposition—especially telomere-related gene variants—on disease behavior and clinical care.

# Clinical features and natural history of IPF

IPF is typically a disease of middle-aged and older adults; most patients present after the age of 60 years, and incidence increases with age. Men are affected more commonly than women, and there is a strong association with a history of smoking. The most common presenting symptoms are progressive exertional dyspnea and a persistent, nonproductive cough. On physical examination, inspiratory "Velcro"-like crackles are common, particularly at the lung bases, and digital clubbing is present in a substantial proportion of patients. Systemic features such as fever, arthralgias, or significant weight loss are uncommon and should prompt consideration of alternative or concomitant diagnoses.

The natural history of IPF is variable but generally progressive. Many patients experience slowly worsening dyspnea and exercise limitation over months to years, but disease course can be heterogeneous: some progress steadily while others have prolonged stability punctuated by acute exacerbations—episodes of rapid respiratory deterioration with new widespread alveolar injury—that carry a high short-term mortality. Median survival historically has been quoted as 3–5 years from diagnosis in untreated cohorts, but contemporary cohorts treated with antifibrotic therapies (pirfenidone or nintedanib) show a slower rate of physiological decline and improved progression-free survival, though not cure. Predictors of poorer prognosis include older age, male sex, lower baseline forced vital capacity (FVC), lower diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide (DLCO), greater extent of fibrosis on imaging, presence of pulmonary hypertension, and rapid decline in lung function over time.

Complications of progressive fibrosis include respiratory failure, pulmonary hypertension due to obliteration of pulmonary vasculature and hypoxic vasoconstriction, superimposed infection, and increased risk for lung cancer. Acute exacerbations—defined clinically as unexplained worsening of respiratory symptoms over days to weeks with new bilateral radiographic opacities—are a major cause of mortality and remain poorly understood; they may represent accelerated alveolar injury on a background of fibrotic lung. Comorbid conditions such as coronary artery disease, gastroesophageal reflux, obstructive sleep apnea, and deconditioning significantly affect symptom burden and outcomes and warrant active management.

# Pathophysiology: alveolar epithelial injury, aberrant repair, fibroblast activation

Modern concepts of IPF pathogenesis emphasize repeated or persistent injury to the alveolar epithelium in genetically susceptible individuals, leading to dysregulated wound healing rather than chronic inflammation as the primary driver. The central elements of this model are alveolar epithelial cell (AEC) dysfunction, aberrant epithelial–mesenchymal interactions, persistent activation and expansion of (myo)fibroblasts, excessive extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition, and progressive architectural remodeling.

Alveolar epithelial injury and senescence: AECs (particularly type I and type II pneumocytes) are vulnerable to environmental insults (cigarette smoke, metal dusts, viral infections, gastroesophageal reflux–related microaspiration), oxidative stress, and endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress. Injured type II AECs, which serve as progenitors for alveolar repair and surfactant production, undergo apoptosis or enter a dysfunctional, senescent phenotype. Telomere shortening or defects in telomere maintenance (see genetics section) can render AECs prone to senescence and impaired regenerative capacity. Senescent AECs adopt a secretory phenotype that releases profibrotic mediators (transforming growth factor-beta [TGF-β], connective tissue growth factor, platelet-derived growth factor) and chemokines that recruit and activate mesenchymal cells.

Aberrant repair and epithelial–mesenchymal crosstalk: In contrast to a normal reparative sequence where type II AECs proliferate and re-differentiate to restore alveolar integrity, the dysfunctional epithelium in IPF fails to re-epithelialize alveolar units appropriately. Instead, injured epithelium secretes profibrotic signals that stimulate resident fibroblasts and recruit fibrocytes and circulating progenitors. TGF-β is a central cytokine that promotes fibroblast differentiation into myofibroblasts, induces epithelial–mesenchymal transition in some contexts, enhances ECM production (collagens I and III, fibronectin), and suppresses ECM degradation by upregulating tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases (TIMPs).

Fibroblast activation, myofibroblast persistence, and extracellular matrix remodeling: The myofibroblast is the effector cell that synthesizes and organizes the fibrotic ECM and generates contractile forces that deform the lung parenchyma. In IPF, fibroblastic foci—small aggregates of activated fibroblasts and myofibroblasts at the interface of normal and fibrotic lung—are characteristic histologic features and are associated with active fibrogenesis. Myofibroblasts in IPF display resistance to apoptosis and produce abundant collagen and other ECM components resulting in irreversible architectural distortion. The ECM becomes progressively cross-linked and stiffened, and matrix stiffness itself enhances profibrotic signaling (a feed-forward loop) via mechanotransduction pathways that further activate fibroblasts and alter epithelial behavior.

Vascular involvement and remodeling: Fibrotic remodeling is accompanied by changes in the pulmonary vasculature, including loss of capillary beds, vascular rarefaction, and remodeling of pulmonary arteries. These changes contribute to gas-exchange impairment and development of pulmonary hypertension, which has important prognostic implications.

Molecular pathways: Multiple molecular pathways contribute to IPF pathogenesis. In addition to TGF-β, other mediators include Wnt/β-catenin signaling, platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) dysregulation, chemokines (e.g., CXCL12), integrin-mediated TGF-β activation, and altered matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) activity. Cellular contributors beyond fibroblasts and epithelial cells—such as alveolar macrophages with profibrotic polarization, pericytes, and bone marrow–derived fibrocytes—also play roles. The result is a remodeled, stiff lung with loss of alveolar-capillary units and impaired gas exchange.

Clinical implication: The shift from an inflammation-driven to an epithelial injury/fibroproliferative model underlies the limited efficacy of broad-spectrum immunosuppression in IPF and the rationale for antifibrotic agents that target fibroblast activation and ECM pathways.

# High-resolution CT patterns (usual interstitial pneumonia [UIP], ground-glass, subpleural reticulation, honeycombing)

High-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) of the chest is central to the diagnostic evaluation of suspected IPF. HRCT patterns not only help establish or exclude the diagnosis but also provide prognostic information. The hallmark HRCT pattern associated with IPF is that of usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP). Accurate interpretation requires thin-section (≤1.5 mm) images, expiratory views when indicated, and attention to distributional features.

UIP pattern: The typical UIP pattern on HRCT is characterized by basal and subpleural predominance, reticular abnormalities, honeycombing, and absence of features suggesting an alternative diagnosis. Subpleural reticulation manifests as a network of fine lines near the pleural surfaces and along the periphery of the lobes, often with traction bronchiectasis representing distortion and retraction of airways. Honeycombing consists of clustered cystic air spaces with well-defined walls, typically 3–10 mm in diameter but sometimes larger, stacked in multiple layers at the lung periphery. Honeycombing is a key feature that when present in the appropriate distribution and in conjunction with reticulation supports a confident radiologic diagnosis of UIP. Traction bronchiectasis and bronchiolectasis indicate fibrotic lung distortion and are often accompanying features.

Ground-glass opacity (GGO): Ground-glass attenuation refers to increased lung density on HRCT that does not obscure underlying bronchial and vascular markings. In IPF, areas of GGO may be present but are generally less extensive than reticulation and honeycombing. When GGO is predominant or diffuse, alternative diagnoses (e.g., nonspecific interstitial pneumonia, cryptogenic organizing pneumonia, acute exacerbation) should be considered. In fibrotic ILD, ground-glass may represent fine fibrosis, active inflammation, or partial alveolar filling, and its distribution and accompanying features inform differential diagnosis.

Subpleural reticulation and distribution: Subpleural reticulation—fine linear opacities adjacent to the pleura—is a common early sign of UIP. The typical distribution of UIP is peripheral and basilar, often with relative sparing of the anterior upper lobes. As disease advances, reticulation coalesces into dense fibrosis and honeycomb change. The degree and extent of fibrosis on HRCT correlate with pulmonary physiology and outcomes.

Other patterns and differential considerations: Several HRCT patterns are classified in ILD guidelines: "UIP pattern," "probable UIP," "indeterminate for UIP," and "alternative diagnosis." Radiologic features that argue against UIP include extensive GGO out of proportion to reticulation, upper- or mid-lung predominance, peribronchovascular predominance, diffuse consolidation, nodular disease, cystic changes away from subpleural regions, and prominent lymphadenopathy. An HRCT showing a probable UIP pattern (e.g., subpleural, basal-predominant reticulation and traction bronchiectasis without honeycombing) may be sufficient for a working diagnosis in the right clinical context, while an indeterminate pattern or features suggesting an alternative diagnosis may prompt surgical lung biopsy or cryobiopsy and multidisciplinary review.

Quantification and serial imaging: Extent of fibrotic change on HRCT—by visual scoring or automated quantification—has prognostic relevance. Increasing honeycombing, traction bronchiectasis, or overall fibrosis extent over time predicts functional decline. HRCT is also used to evaluate for complications such as infection, lung cancer, or acute exacerbation.

Key diagnostic criteria and decision points: The 2018 ATS/ERS/JRS/ALAT guideline defines criteria for diagnosing IPF and the radiologic categories. A confident noninvasive diagnosis of IPF can be established when the HRCT shows a UIP pattern in the appropriate clinical setting (i.e., unexplained chronic fibrosing interstitial pneumonia in adults). In cases with probable or indeterminate patterns, input from a multidisciplinary team and consideration of histologic sampling are required. When features suggest an alternative diagnosis (e.g., features of connective tissue disease–related ILD), IPF is unlikely.

# Pulmonary function testing and gas-exchange abnormalities

Pulmonary function tests (PFTs) and gas-exchange assessments are essential for diagnosis, staging severity, monitoring progression, and evaluating response to therapy in IPF and other fibrotic ILDs.

Spirometry and lung volumes: IPF typically produces a restrictive ventilatory defect. Forced vital capacity (FVC) is the key spirometric parameter used clinically: baseline FVC and rate of decline inform prognosis and management. A reduced FVC (commonly <80% predicted) with a proportionally preserved or increased FEV1/FVC ratio supports restriction. Total lung capacity (TLC) measured by plethysmography or gas dilution is reduced in restriction; TLC is often more sensitive than FVC in early disease. Residual volume (RV) is often normal or reduced. Serial decline in FVC is clinically meaningful; commonly used thresholds for progression include an absolute decline in FVC of ≥10% predicted or a relative decline of 5–10% accompanied by clinical decline.

Diffusing capacity and arterial blood gases: Diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide (DLCO) is frequently reduced in IPF and often declines earlier than FVC because it reflects loss of alveolar-capillary surface area and capillary blood volume. DLCO values are typically markedly reduced; a DLCO <40% predicted indicates severe gas-exchange impairment. Reference ranges vary by laboratory, but a typical normal DLCO is approximately 80–120% predicted depending on age, sex, and hemoglobin. Arterial blood gas measurements often reveal hypoxemia, which is exertional early in the disease and may be present at rest in advanced cases. Oxygenation should be assessed at rest and with exertion; exercise-induced desaturation is common and correlates with prognosis. Ambulatory oxygen saturation measured during a 6-minute walk test (6MWT) is clinically useful.

6-minute walk test and exercise physiology: The 6MWT provides information on exercise capacity, oxygen desaturation on exertion, and symptom burden. Distance walked, lowest oxygen saturation during the test, and need for supplemental oxygen are prognostically relevant. A 6MWT distance less than approximately 250–300 meters and significant desaturation are associated with poorer outcomes. Cardiopulmonary exercise testing can further delineate limiting factors—ventilatory mechanics, gas exchange inefficiency, or circulatory limitation including pulmonary vascular disease—but is not required routinely.

Pulmonary hypertension and right heart function: Pulmonary hypertension (PH) is a common and important complication of advanced IPF and worsens prognosis. Noninvasive clues include disproportionate reduction in DLCO relative to FVC, echocardiographic evidence of elevated right-sided pressures or right ventricular dysfunction, and severe exertional desaturation. Definitive diagnosis requires right heart catheterization. Screening and management of PH in fibrotic ILD require careful multidisciplinary consideration because vasodilator therapies approved for pulmonary arterial hypertension may have uncertain benefit or potential harm in patients with fibrotic lung disease.

Functional staging and prognostication: Several staging systems and composite scores have been proposed (e.g., GAP index—Gender, Age, Physiology [FVC and DLCO]) to predict mortality and inform discussions about referral for lung transplantation. For example, the GAP score incorporates sex, age, FVC % predicted, and DLCO % predicted to stratify patients into stages with distinct 1-year mortality risks.

# Differential diagnosis of fibrotic ILD and role of multidisciplinary discussion

The differential diagnosis of a fibrotic ILD is broad and includes idiopathic forms (e.g., IPF, idiopathic nonspecific interstitial pneumonia), fibrotic forms of connective tissue disease–associated ILD (CTD-ILD), chronic hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP), occupational/environmental pneumoconioses, drug-induced fibrosis, sarcoidosis with fibrosis, and fibrotic lung disease secondary to known exposures or systemic disease. Distinguishing IPF from alternative causes is clinically crucial because management, prognosis, and transplant candidacy may differ.

Clinical and exposure history: A detailed clinical history is often the most informative element. Important items include duration and tempo of symptoms, smoking history, occupational and environmental exposures (bird proteins, mold, metal/wood dusts), medication history (e.g., amiodarone, bleomycin, methotrexate), family history of pulmonary fibrosis, symptoms or signs of connective tissue disease (e.g., arthralgias, sicca symptoms, Raynaud phenomenon, skin changes), and temporal association with exposures or systemic disease. Laboratory studies for autoimmune serologies (ANA, rheumatoid factor, anti-CCP, myositis-specific antibodies, etc.) guide evaluation for CTD-ILD; however, serologies should be interpreted in the clinical context because low-titer autoantibodies can be nonspecific in older adults.

Imaging nuances: HRCT patterns provide key discrimination. For example, a peribronchovascular predominant reticular pattern, pronounced ground-glass, or mosaic attenuation with air-trapping suggest alternative diagnoses (NSIP, HP, or small airways disease). Centrilobular nodularity, mosaic attenuation, and upper- or mid-lung predominance strongly raise the possibility of chronic HP. Recognizing patterns that are inconsistent with UIP is essential to avoid mislabeling IPF.

Histopathology and tissue sampling: When imaging and clinical data are indeterminate, histopathology from surgical lung biopsy or transbronchial lung cryobiopsy may provide diagnostic clarity. The histologic UIP pattern mirrors radiologic UIP—temporal and spatial heterogeneity, fibroblastic foci, and honeycomb change. Conversely, a uniform interstitial inflammation and fibrosis without honeycombing favors nonspecific interstitial pneumonia (NSIP). However, biopsy carries procedure-related risks and may be contraindicated in patients with advanced disease or comorbidities. The decision to biopsy requires individualized risk–benefit assessment.

Multidisciplinary discussion (MDD): Given the complexities and overlapping features among fibrotic ILDs, multidisciplinary discussion is considered the diagnostic gold standard. MDD involves pulmonologists, radiologists with thoracic expertise, pathologists, and often rheumatologists or other specialists as needed. The MDD synthesizes clinical history, serologic testing, HRCT, and histology (if available) to reach a consensus diagnosis and management plan. MDD improves diagnostic confidence and has been shown to change final diagnosis in a significant proportion of cases compared to single-discipline interpretation. Key decision points in MDD include whether the clinical–radiologic picture supports a diagnosis of IPF without biopsy, whether biopsy is required and safe, and whether features suggest an alternative diagnosis that would benefit from immunomodulatory therapy or exposure avoidance.

Practical considerations: In older patients with typical UIP pattern on HRCT and no features suggestive of alternative diagnosis, many centers accept a working diagnosis of IPF without tissue sampling. In younger patients, those with atypical imaging, or those with systemic features or positive autoimmune serologies, a more exhaustive search for secondary causes is warranted. Management strategies differ: antifibrotic therapy is indicated for IPF and has been evaluated in progressive fibrotic ILDs of other causes, while systemic immunosuppression may be beneficial in certain CTD-ILDs or cellular NSIP but is potentially harmful in IPF.

# Genetic predisposition to pulmonary fibrosis (TERT/TERC/PARN/RTEL1) and clinical implications

Familial aggregation of pulmonary fibrosis and identification of rare genetic variants have established genetic predisposition as an important contributor to pulmonary fibrosis susceptibility and phenotype. Telomere-related genes such as TERT (telomerase reverse transcriptase), TERC (telomerase RNA component), PARN (poly(A)-specific ribonuclease), and RTEL1 (regulator of telomere elongation helicase 1) are among the best-characterized genes associated with familial and sporadic pulmonary fibrosis. Mutations in these genes impair telomere maintenance, leading to shortened telomeres and cellular senescence, particularly in high-turnover tissues such as the alveolar epithelium and hematopoietic system.

Genetic findings and phenotype: Pathogenic variants in TERT and TERC are present in a subset of familial pulmonary fibrosis pedigrees and are also identified among sporadic IPF cases, more commonly in younger patients and those with a family history. PARN and RTEL1 mutations are similarly associated with telomere dysfunction and pulmonary fibrosis. Patients with telomere-related mutations often demonstrate shorter leukocyte telomere length (measured relative to age-matched controls), and telomere shortening correlates with earlier disease onset and more rapid progression. Extrapulmonary manifestations are common in telomere syndromes and include bone marrow failure (cytopenias, aplastic anemia), hepatic fibrosis or cirrhosis, premature gray hair, and immune dysfunction. These systemic features may precede, coincide with, or follow pulmonary disease and are important for screening and management.

Clinical implications: Awareness of telomere-related genetic predisposition has multiple implications for clinical care.

- Family screening and genetic counseling: In patients with familial pulmonary fibrosis (two or more affected relatives) or early-onset disease, referral for genetic counseling and consideration of targeted genetic testing is appropriate. Identifying a pathogenic variant informs counseling about inheritance (often autosomal dominant with variable penetrance), recurrence risk, and potential screening of at-risk relatives.

- Prognosis: Telomere-related mutations are associated with worse outcomes and may predict more rapid physiological decline. Short telomere length—irrespective of an identified mutation—is an independent predictor of worse survival in IPF cohorts.

- Treatment considerations: Telomere dysfunction influences response to therapies and procedural risks. For example, patients with telomere-related mutations may have increased sensitivity to cytotoxic or immunosuppressive agents and higher risk of bone marrow suppression. This has implications for using immunosuppressive therapies in ILD; historically, broad immunosuppression has not benefited IPF and may cause harm. In patients with telomere syndromes, particular caution is needed with myelosuppressive medications and procedural sedation.

- Lung transplantation: Telomere-related mutations affect pre- and post-transplant considerations. Pretransplant, bone marrow dysfunction and liver disease may complicate candidacy and perioperative risk. Post-transplant, patients with telomere disorders have higher risk of bone marrow suppression with standard immunosuppressive regimens, leading to cytopenias, infections, and need for regimen modification. Awareness of telomere biology can guide transplant center selection, pretransplant evaluation (including hematology and hepatology consultation), and tailoring of immunosuppression.

- Research and targeted therapies: Understanding telomere biology has stimulated interest in targeted interventions. Strategies include telomerase activators, androgen therapy (which may increase telomerase activity and has been used experimentally in dyskeratosis congenita), and agents aimed at modulating cellular senescence or DNA damage responses. However, these approaches are investigational, and routine use is not established.

Testing and interpretation: Genetic testing strategies include sequencing of candidate genes (TERT, TERC, PARN, RTEL1, and others) and measurement of telomere length via flow-FISH or PCR-based assays. Interpretation requires correlation with clinical phenotype and genetic counseling because variants of uncertain significance are common, and telomere length decreases with age. Decision points include whom to test (familial cases, early-onset, unexplained cytopenias, or extrapulmonary manifestations), and how results will influence management, family screening, and transplantation planning.

Ethical and psychosocial considerations: Genetic testing carries psychosocial and insurance implications for patients and family members. Pretest counseling should address potential outcomes, limitations of testing, and implications for relatives. Post-test support is important when pathogenic variants are identified.

Conclusion

Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis exemplifies a complex, age-associated fibroproliferative lung disease arising from aberrant epithelial injury responses and progressive ECM accumulation. Clinical recognition—integrating history, PFTs, HRCT, and, when needed, histopathology—combined with multidisciplinary discussion yields the most accurate diagnoses and appropriate management strategies. Contemporary understanding of the molecular and genetic underpinnings, including telomere-related gene mutations, has refined prognostication and influenced decisions around therapy and transplantation. Recognition of the heterogeneous natural history and the potential for acute exacerbations remains crucial for patient counseling, monitoring, and timely referral for advanced therapies, including lung transplantation.
