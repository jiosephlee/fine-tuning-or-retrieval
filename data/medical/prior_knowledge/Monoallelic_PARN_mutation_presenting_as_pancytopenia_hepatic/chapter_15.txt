Introduction

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) comprise a spectrum of inherited conditions characterized by defective maintenance of telomeres, resulting in premature telomere shortening and multisystem disease. Clinically, TBDs present with bone marrow failure, pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic dysfunction, mucocutaneous features, and increased cancer predisposition. Because telomeres influence replicative capacity and genomic stability across tissues, management of TBDs requires a multisystem, anticipatory approach that balances supportive care, organ-directed therapies, and definitive interventions such as hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) or solid-organ transplantation when organ failure ensues. In parallel, investigational therapies aimed at restoring telomere length or function are under active study. This chapter outlines practical management principles for TBDs, emphasizing surveillance strategies, supportive measures, transplantation considerations, pharmacologic approaches, and the role of clinical trials.

# Surveillance strategies for marrow, liver, lung and cancer risks

Surveillance in TBDs is driven by the natural history of organ involvement and the high variability in penetrance and age of onset. Surveillance objectives are early detection of organ dysfunction, timely institution of therapy, prevention of complications, and risk stratification for interventions that can exacerbate organ injury (for example, myeloablative conditioning for HSCT in a patient with subclinical pulmonary fibrosis).

Bone marrow (hematologic) surveillance
Patients with known or suspected TBDs require periodic complete blood counts (CBC) with differential and reticulocyte count. Frequency depends on baseline cytopenias: for stable counts, obtain CBC every 3–6 months; if there is progressive cytopenia or new symptoms, increase to monthly or more frequent monitoring. Key thresholds that prompt further evaluation or treatment include hemoglobin < 8–10 g/dL (varies by clinical context), absolute neutrophil count (ANC) < 0.5 × 10^9/L (severe neutropenia), and platelet count < 30–50 × 10^9/L (high bleeding risk). Evaluate for macrocytosis (mean corpuscular volume > 100 fL), which is common in TBDs, and for reticulocytopenia suggesting marrow failure rather than peripheral destruction.

Bone marrow examination (aspiration and biopsy) is indicated when progressive cytopenias are present, before initiating disease-modifying therapy (such as HSCT), and to assess cellularity, dysplasia, fibrosis, and clonal abnormalities. Cytogenetic analysis and next-generation sequencing panels for myeloid malignancy–associated genes are important because TBD patients have elevated risk of myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS) and acute myeloid leukemia (AML). Because some clonal cytogenetic changes in TBD-associated MDS may be indolent, integrate morphologic, cytogenetic, and clinical data when considering therapeutic escalation.

Liver surveillance
Liver disease in TBDs ranges from asymptomatic transaminase elevation to steatosis, nodular regenerative hyperplasia, portal hypertension, and cirrhosis. Baseline liver tests (ALT, AST, alkaline phosphatase, total bilirubin), synthetic markers (albumin, international normalized ratio [INR]), and platelet count should be obtained. For patients without liver disease, monitor these values at least annually; if abnormalities are detected, increase frequency to every 3–6 months and obtain imaging and specialist referral. Abdominal ultrasound with Doppler is a reasonable first-line imaging modality to evaluate liver morphology, splenomegaly, and portal flow. Cross-sectional imaging (contrast-enhanced CT or MRI) and elastography (transient elastography/FibroScan or MR elastography) can quantify fibrosis and portal hypertension.

Specific features that should trigger hepatology referral include: persistent transaminase elevation > 1.5–2× upper limit of normal, development of thrombocytopenia or splenomegaly suggesting portal hypertension, hypoalbuminemia, coagulopathy (INR > 1.2–1.5 unexplained by other causes), or imaging evidence of cirrhosis. Surveillance for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) follows standard cirrhosis practice if cirrhosis is present: ultrasound every 6 months ± serum alpha-fetoprotein. Consider viral hepatitis screening and avoidance of hepatotoxic drugs. Where available, consider assessment for portal hypertension (variceal screening endoscopy) when clinical or imaging signs indicate risk.

Pulmonary surveillance
Pulmonary disease in TBDs most commonly presents as pulmonary fibrosis, often with a usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP) pattern, and as early-onset emphysema in smokers. Baseline pulmonary evaluation for all patients should include pulmonary symptom review, physical exam, pulmonary function tests (PFTs) with spirometry, lung volumes, and single-breath diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide (DLCO). Obtain baseline high-resolution chest CT (HRCT) to detect radiographic fibrosis, even in asymptomatic individuals, because early interstitial changes can be subclinical. For patients with no pulmonary disease at baseline, PFTs and symptom assessment at least annually are reasonable; HRCT can be repeated every 12–24 months or sooner if symptoms or PFTs worsen.

Worsening criteria that mandate pulmonary referral and consideration of antifibrotic therapy or transplant evaluation include: decline in forced vital capacity (FVC) ≥ 10% predicted, decline in DLCO ≥ 15% predicted, or worsening respiratory symptoms/hypoxemia. Smoking cessation is essential; even minimal tobacco exposure may precipitate more severe respiratory disease in TBDs. Evaluate for pulmonary hypertension with echocardiography when clinically indicated. Because pulmonary complications influence candidacy for HSCT and transplant conditioning choices, integrate pulmonary surveillance results into overall management planning.

Cancer surveillance
TBDs confer increased risks for several cancers, notably myeloid malignancies (MDS/AML), head and neck squamous cell carcinomas (particularly oral), skin cancers, and gastrointestinal malignancies. Surveillance strategies should be individualized by genotype, family history, and organ involvement.

Routine skin and oral mucosa examinations by primary care, dermatology, and otolaryngology are recommended at least annually, more frequently if lesions are present or if high-risk features exist. Low threshold for biopsy of suspicious mucocutaneous lesions is appropriate given cancer risk. For hematologic malignancy surveillance, periodic CBC and bone marrow evaluation when cytopenias or morphologic abnormalities appear is key. For patients with known liver cirrhosis, HCC surveillance as above is indicated. Standard age-appropriate cancer screening (colonoscopy, Pap smear, mammography) should continue, with consideration for earlier or more frequent screening if family history or genotype suggests higher risk.

Genetic counseling and cascade testing for at-risk relatives are important both for early surveillance and for transplant donor selection, because related donors may harbor the same pathogenic variants.

# Supportive care: transfusions, infection prophylaxis, nutritional supplementation and organ-specific treatments

Supportive care in TBDs aims to maintain organ function, reduce complications of cytopenias, and optimize patients for definitive therapy when appropriate. Decisions balance benefits of symptom relief against long-term risks such as iron overload from repeated transfusions or infectious risks from immunosuppression.

Blood product support
Red cell transfusions: Indications mirror general transfusion practice but are individualized. Transfuse for symptomatic anemia or hemoglobin thresholds often guided by clinical context (for example, hemoglobin < 7 g/dL in stable outpatients without cardiac disease, or higher thresholds [8–9 g/dL] in symptomatic patients or those with cardiovascular disease). Chronic transfusion dependence should prompt iron overload monitoring. Ferritin and transferrin saturation are useful serial markers; ferritin > 1000 ng/mL or rising trend usually prompts consideration of iron chelation. Noninvasive iron quantification (cardiac and liver MRI T2*) is valuable when available.

Platelet transfusions: Administer for active bleeding, invasive procedures, or when platelet counts fall below institutional thresholds that account for bleeding risk — commonly < 10 × 10^9/L for prophylaxis in stable patients and < 50 × 10^9/L for major procedures. Refractoriness to transfused platelets can occur; evaluate for alloimmunization and consider HLA-matched products when needed.

Granulocyte colony-stimulating factors (G-CSF) can be used for severe neutropenia with infections, but with caution because G-CSF may accelerate clonal evolution in marrow failure and is not a definitive therapy in TBDs. Prolonged G-CSF use should involve hematology expertise.

Infection prophylaxis and management
Patients with cytopenias, particularly neutropenia, are at increased infection risk. Standard infection prophylaxis includes vaccination review and optimization: pneumococcal polysaccharide and conjugate vaccines, annual influenza vaccine, and other age-appropriate immunizations. Live vaccines should generally be avoided in patients who are significantly immunosuppressed.

Antibiotic prophylaxis (e.g., fluoroquinolone or TMP-SMX) is considered in patients with prolonged severe neutropenia (ANC < 0.5 × 10^9/L) or recurrent infections. Antifungal and antiviral prophylaxis regimens are individualized based on degree of immunosuppression, prior infections, and planned therapies. For patients receiving immunosuppressive therapy (e.g., for autoimmune cytopenias) or post-transplant, follow transplant/infectious disease guidelines for prophylaxis against Pneumocystis jirovecii, cytomegalovirus, and invasive fungal disease.

Nutritional supplementation and bone health
Patients with hematologic and hepatic disease often have nutritional deficiencies and may be at risk for osteoporosis. Evaluate and correct vitamin deficiencies, particularly folate and vitamin B12, though macrocytosis in TBDs is often due to marrow failure rather than classical nutritional deficiency. Monitor calcium, vitamin D, and bone mineral density; consider bisphosphonates when indicated. Because cirrhosis affects protein metabolism and variceal/ascites risk, involve nutritionists for tailored recommendations.

Organ-specific medical therapies
Pulmonary: Consider antifibrotic agents such as pirfenidone or nintedanib for progressive fibrotic interstitial lung disease. While evidence is extrapolated from idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) trials and observational reports in telomere-related disease, these agents may slow FVC decline. Monitor for drug-specific adverse effects (e.g., liver enzyme elevation with pirfenidone, gastrointestinal effects and bleeding risk with nintedanib). Supplemental oxygen and pulmonary rehabilitation are important for symptomatic relief. Vaccinate against pneumococcus and influenza.

Hepatic: Manage portal hypertension, variceal bleeding, and ascites per standard hepatology practice: nonselective beta-blockers, endoscopic surveillance and variceal band ligation, diuretics for ascites, and large-volume paracentesis when necessary. Avoid hepatotoxic medications and alcohol. For patients with portal hypertension but preserved synthetic function, monitor closely for progression. In advanced disease, refer early for liver transplant evaluation.

Mucocutaneous care: Address nail dystrophy, oral leukoplakia, and mucocutaneous lesions with dermatology/otolaryngology consultation. Surveillance and early biopsy of suspicious lesions reduce morbidity from malignant transformation.

Psychosocial and palliative care
Given the chronic progressive nature and multisystem involvement in TBDs, integrate psychosocial support, genetic counseling, and early palliative care discussions. Advance care planning should be revisited regularly and particularly when considering interventions with significant mortality or morbidity risk (e.g., HSCT or organ transplantation).

# Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation: indications, conditioning regimens, increased toxicity risk in TBD and reduced-intensity approaches

HSCT is the only curative therapy for hematologic manifestations (severe aplastic anemia, MDS, AML) in TBDs. However, HSCT in TBDs is complicated by increased sensitivity to DNA-damaging agents, higher rates of pulmonary and hepatic complications, and poor tolerance of conventional myeloablative conditioning regimens. Therefore, HSCT indications, donor selection, timing, and conditioning must be individualized with expertise from centers experienced in telomere disorders.

Indications for HSCT
Indications include severe, symptomatic bone marrow failure not responsive to immunosuppression; presence of high-risk MDS or AML with marrow failure; or transfusion dependence with progressive cytopenias. For aplastic anemia, consider HSCT earlier in younger patients with matched sibling donors. In TBDs, early transplant referral is prudent because progressive extra-hematologic organ dysfunction (pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic cirrhosis) may preclude safe HSCT later. Conversely, HSCT can exacerbate lung and liver injury, so thorough pre-transplant assessment is essential.

Donor selection
Related donors should undergo genetic testing for known pathogenic telomerase/telomere-related variants and telomere length assessment. Using donors who carry the same pathogenic variant risks poor graft function and late complications. For unrelated donors, consider HLA-matched unrelated donors or haploidentical approaches depending on availability. Umbilical cord blood has been used but may have delayed engraftment. Donor age is relevant because older donors may have shorter telomeres; younger donors without pathogenic variants are preferred.

Conditioning regimens and toxicity considerations
Conventional myeloablative conditioning (MAC) regimens often employ high-dose alkylating agents (cyclophosphamide, busulfan) and total body irradiation (TBI). In TBD patients, high DNA damage from such regimens can cause severe and sometimes fatal pulmonary, hepatic, and mucocutaneous toxicity. Reported complications include veno-occlusive disease (sinusoidal obstruction syndrome), acute respiratory distress syndrome, and poor engraftment.

Reduced-intensity conditioning (RIC) and nonmyeloablative regimens aim to reduce early organ toxicity while achieving engraftment. Common RIC backbones include fludarabine-based regimens combined with lower-dose alkylators (e.g., melphalan at reduced doses, busulfan at pharmacokinetically guided lower exposures) or treosulfan-based regimens, which may be less hepatotoxic. Avoidance or minimization of cyclophosphamide and TBI is often recommended in TBDs. Conditioning choices should account for pre-existing organ dysfunction: patients with pulmonary fibrosis may fare better with regimens minimizing pulmonary-toxic agents.

Graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) prophylaxis and management
Standard GVHD prophylaxis (calcineurin inhibitor plus methotrexate or mycophenolate) is used, though some centers favor post-transplant cyclophosphamide in haploidentical transplants. However, exposure to cyclophosphamide may be problematic in TBDs; thus alternatives should be considered. GVHD increases morbidity and mortality and may impair organ recovery; aggressive prevention and early treatment are important.

Outcomes and mitigation strategies
Outcomes after HSCT in TBDs have historically been worse than in other inherited bone marrow failure syndromes, driven by increased nonrelapse mortality from organ failure. Key strategies to mitigate risk include: comprehensive pre-transplant organ assessment (pulmonary function testing, HRCT, liver imaging and function tests), selection of less toxic conditioning regimens, use of younger, genetically appropriate donors, careful infection prophylaxis, and post-transplant surveillance for organ injury. Multidisciplinary discussion with transplant, pulmonology, hepatology, and genetics teams is essential. Some centers report acceptable outcomes using tailored reduced-intensity regimens and stringent donor selection.

Timing of HSCT is a clinical decision weighing progressive marrow failure against risks of transplant in a patient who may develop progressive lung or liver disease. Early consultation and planning, ideally before severe organ dysfunction develops, improve candidacy and outcomes.

# Lung and liver transplantation in telomere-related organ failure: candidacy considerations and outcomes

For patients with end-stage lung or liver disease due to telomere biology disorders, transplantation may be life-saving. However, telomere dysfunction raises unique considerations for candidacy and post-transplant outcomes including extra-pulmonary complications, bone marrow suppression, and impaired tissue repair.

Lung transplantation
Indications and evaluation: Standard indications for lung transplantation apply (progressive respiratory failure with limited life expectancy despite optimal medical therapy). Candidates should undergo thorough pre-transplant assessment including HRCT, PFTs, right heart catheterization when pulmonary hypertension is suspected, and evaluation for extrapulmonary disease. Particular attention must be paid to hematologic status (CBC, marrow reserve), liver function, renal function, and nutritional status.

Risks and outcomes: Patients with TBDs are at increased risk for post-transplant complications — especially severe bone marrow suppression in response to systemic immunosuppression, impaired wound healing, and infectious complications. Some series report higher rates of early post-transplant cytopenias requiring transfusions and growth factors, higher infection rates, and poorer survival compared with non-TBD interstitial lung disease cohorts. Nonetheless, carefully selected patients can derive meaningful survival and functional benefit from lung transplantation.

Perioperative management and immunosuppression: Tailor immunosuppressive regimens to minimize marrow toxicity: consider avoidance or dose reduction of agents with pronounced marrow suppression (e.g., mycophenolate mofetil), and vigilant monitoring of blood counts. Prophylactic antimicrobials and aggressive management of cytopenias are necessary. Preemptive coordination with hematology is important to manage transfusion dependence or to consider HSCT timing relative to lung transplant in rare combined-organ failure contexts.

Combined organ transplantation: For patients with concomitant severe liver disease, combined lung-liver transplantation is rare but may be considered at specialized centers. The complexity and risks are substantial and decision-making must be individualized.

Liver transplantation
Indications and evaluation: Liver transplant candidacy follows standard indications for end-stage liver disease (MELD score), hepatocellular carcinoma within criteria, or liver failure not amenable to medical therapy. As with lung transplant, pre-transplant evaluation must include hematologic assessment and exclusion of severe uncontrolled extrahepatic disease that would contraindicate transplant.

Risks and outcomes: Post-liver transplant challenges include bone marrow suppression from immunosuppression, increased susceptibility to infections, and wound-healing problems. Outcomes vary across series; some patients with telomere-associated liver disease have acceptable post-transplant survival, but morbidity is higher relative to non-TBD patients. Pre-existing pulmonary disease worsens outcomes, so assessing for coexistent pulmonary fibrosis is crucial — severe pulmonary disease may preclude safe liver transplantation or require combined assessment.

General candidacy considerations
- Multidisciplinary evaluation: Include transplant surgery, pulmonology/hepatology, hematology, genetics, infectious disease, and anesthesiology.
- Hematologic reserve: Significant bone marrow failure increases postoperative risks. Consider whether concurrent HSCT is needed or feasible.
- Extrahepatic and extrapulmonary disease: Severe extrapulmonary disease (e.g., advanced marrow failure, severe coronary disease) may exclude transplantation.
- Genetic counseling and donor considerations: For living-donor liver or lung lobectomy, evaluate donors genetically to avoid using carriers of the same pathogenic variant.

Pre- and post-transplant surveillance
Aggressive monitoring for cytopenias, infections, organ rejection, and secondary malignancies is required. Adjust immunosuppression in response to marrow suppression. Long-term follow-up should include continued screening for cancers and telomere-related complications.

# Pharmacologic approaches: androgens (danazol, nandrolone), sex hormones and their proposed mechanisms

Pharmacologic therapies that modulate telomerase activity or stimulate hematopoiesis have been investigated in TBDs. Androgens, particularly danazol and nandrolone, have the best-evidenced role to date in ameliorating cytopenias and potentially stabilizing telomere length in some patients. Understanding mechanisms, indications, monitoring, and adverse effects is critical.

Rationale and mechanisms
Androgens have long been used empirically for aplastic anemia because they stimulate erythropoiesis. In TBDs, androgens may also upregulate telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) expression, enhance telomerase activity, and thereby slow telomere attrition. Preclinical studies demonstrate that androgens increase TERT transcription via androgen receptor signaling and affect shelterin components and telomere-associated pathways. These effects may be more pronounced in certain genotypes.

Clinical evidence
A randomized controlled trial of danazol in patients with telomere diseases showed that danazol (800 mg daily) for 24 months produced hematologic responses in a substantial proportion of patients and was associated with telomere elongation measured in peripheral blood leukocytes in many participants. Smaller investigator-initiated studies and case series support hematologic improvement with nandrolone or oxymetholone. However, responses are variable and may depend on underlying genotype, extent of marrow failure, and prior treatments.

Indications and patient selection
Consider androgens for patients with symptomatic cytopenias who are not immediate candidates for HSCT, for those who are awaiting transplant, or when HSCT is contraindicated. Candidates should be counseled on potential benefits and significant side effects. Androgens may be particularly useful to reduce transfusion dependence and improve quality of life.

Dosing, duration, and monitoring
Danazol has been used at doses up to 600–800 mg daily in divided doses; nandrolone dosing varies. Start at lower doses when comorbidities make side effects more likely and titrate based on response and tolerance. Monitor CBC, liver function tests (ALT, AST, alkaline phosphatase, bilirubin), lipid profile, and coagulation parameters at baseline and periodically (every 1–3 months early, then every 3–6 months). Evaluate for hepatic adenomas or hepatocellular carcinoma with imaging when prolonged use is expected because danazol carries a risk of hepatic neoplasia. Menstrual irregularities, virilization, acne, and mood changes are common; in women, virilization can be significant. Androgens are contraindicated in pregnancy and teratogenic; ensure effective contraception for individuals with childbearing potential.

Adverse effects and contraindications
Major risks include hepatic dysfunction (including cholestasis and hepatic tumors), lipid abnormalities, and masculinizing effects. Thromboembolic events have been reported with danazol and require caution in patients with clotting risk factors. Other effects include acne, hair growth, mood changes, and decreased HDL cholesterol.

Sex hormones and other agents
Estrogen/progestin therapies have not demonstrated the same telomerase-stimulating effects as androgens and are not used to treat hematologic manifestations of TBDs. Other pharmacologic agents that modulate telomerase activity (e.g., small-molecule activators) are under investigation and will be discussed in the investigational therapies section.

Clinical decision points
- Use androgens when marrow failure is symptomatic or transfusion-dependent and when risks are acceptable.
- Consider androgens as a bridge to HSCT when transplant is planned but deferred.
- Monitor for liver toxicity and malignancy; reassess benefit at 3–6 month intervals.
- Avoid androgens in pregnancy and counsel regarding contraception.

# Enrollment in clinical trials and emerging therapies (telomerase activators, gene therapy)

Given the genetic basis of TBDs and the limits of current therapies, clinical trials exploring telomerase activation, gene therapy, and other disease-modifying approaches are critical. Enrollment in trials should be discussed with eligible patients as part of standard-of-care planning.

Telomerase activators and small molecules
Telomerase activators aim to augment telomerase activity and stabilize or lengthen telomeres in somatic cells. Agents under study include small molecules that increase TERT expression or telomerase enzymatic activity. Clinical translation faces challenges: ensuring sufficient activity in relevant stem cell compartments (hematopoietic, alveolar epithelial, hepatic progenitors), avoiding oncogenic effects of telomerase activation in premalignant cells, and achieving sustained benefit without intolerable toxicity.

Clinical trials of danazol provided proof-of-principle that pharmacologic intervention can increase telomere length in humans. Newer agents include synthetic oligonucleotides, small molecules, and peptide-based drugs designed to target telomerase or associated pathways. Trial endpoints commonly include hematologic response, telomere length dynamics, pulmonary function measures, and safety/tolerability.

Gene therapy and gene editing
Gene therapy approaches seek to correct causative mutations in telomerase components (e.g., TERT, TERC) or in shelterin complex genes using viral vectors, gene replacement, or CRISPR-based gene editing. Ex vivo gene therapy of hematopoietic stem cells with corrected telomerase genes followed by autologous transplant is an attractive concept because it avoids donor-related complications and GVHD. However, gene therapy must demonstrate durable engraftment, restoration of telomere maintenance, and an acceptable safety profile without insertional mutagenesis or off-target effects.

Preclinical models show promise, but translating to human trials requires demonstration of vector safety, regulated expression to avoid uncontrolled telomerase activation (which could promote oncogenesis), and capacity to correct extra-hematopoietic defects. Combined approaches that pair gene-corrected autologous HSCT with organ-directed therapies are conceptually possible but complex.

Cellular therapies
Approaches include ex vivo expansion of hematopoietic stem cells, mesenchymal stromal cell infusions to modulate immune responses and tissue repair, and iPSC (induced pluripotent stem cell) technology to generate corrected progenitors. Each approach faces hurdles related to durability, differentiation capacity, and safety.

Risk considerations in investigational therapies
The principal theoretical risk of telomerase activation is increased cancer risk because upregulation of telomerase is a hallmark of many cancers. Long-term surveillance for neoplasia is therefore a key component of any trial. Regulatory frameworks typically require preclinical carcinogenicity assessments and rigorous monitoring.

Practical considerations for trial enrollment
- Referral to centers specializing in TBDs and genetic bone marrow failure syndromes increases access to trials.
- Genetic and telomere length confirmation are prerequisites for most studies.
- Patients must be counseled about trial phases, objectives (safety vs efficacy), possible risks, and long-term follow-up requirements.
- Participation may affect eligibility for other therapies (e.g., HSCT), so coordinate trial enrollment with overall management planning.

Future directions and biomarkers
Development of robust biomarkers for telomere function (beyond leukocyte telomere length) and better understanding of genotype–phenotype correlations will refine patient selection for therapies. Surrogate endpoints that predict clinical benefit (e.g., improvement in hematopoietic stem cell fitness) are needed to accelerate drug development.

Conclusion

Management of telomere biology disorders is complex and requires multidisciplinary collaboration, individualized surveillance, and careful balancing of supportive care with definitive interventions. Early and proactive assessment of marrow, pulmonary, and hepatic involvement guides decision-making for HSCT and solid-organ transplantation. Androgens offer a pharmacologic option for hematologic improvement, while novel therapies aiming to restore telomere function are in development. Enrollment in specialized centers and clinical trials provides access to evolving treatments and expertise that can improve outcomes. Clinicians should maintain vigilance for organ-specific complications, malignancy risk, and treatment-related toxicities and engage patients in shared decision-making informed by genetic counseling.
