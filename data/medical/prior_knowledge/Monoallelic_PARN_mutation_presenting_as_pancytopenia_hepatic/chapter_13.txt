Introduction

Patients presenting with concurrent abnormalities across hematologic, hepatic and pulmonary systems pose a diagnostic challenge because multiple organ involvement can reflect a single unifying disorder, several coincident processes, or complications of an underlying systemic disease. A systematic, algorithmic approach is essential to avoid missing treatable causes and to prioritize investigations that are high-yield and least invasive. This chapter provides a structured framework to evaluate pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and pulmonary fibrosis individually and together, with emphasis on pathophysiology, common and uncommon etiologies, key diagnostic criteria, and a stepwise investigative strategy aimed at excluding reversible causes and recognizing syndromic, inherited or multisystem disorders.

# Algorithmic approach to pancytopenia

Pancytopenia—concurrent anemia, leukopenia and thrombocytopenia—reflects reduction of one or more hematopoietic lineages. Mechanisms include decreased marrow production, marrow replacement or infiltration, peripheral destruction or sequestration (hypersplenism), and increased consumption (e.g., hemophagocytic syndromes). A focused algorithm clarifies the likely pathophysiologic category and guides targeted testing.

Initial clinical evaluation and basic labs
Begin with history (onset, constitutional symptoms, bleeding, infections, medications, exposures, travel, alcohol), physical exam (lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly, stigmata of chronic liver disease), and baseline labs: complete blood count with differential and reticulocyte count, peripheral blood smear, basic metabolic panel, liver function tests, coagulation profile, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), haptoglobin, direct antiglobulin test (DAT), inflammatory markers (CRP/ESR), and urinalysis. Key values and concepts: reticulocyte count indicates marrow response—low reticulocyte index suggests hypoproliferation. LDH and indirect bilirubin with low haptoglobin suggest hemolysis. Peripheral smear morphology (blasts, dysplastic changes, teardrop cells) often guides whether marrow pathology or peripheral destruction predominates.

Distinguish hypoproliferative from peripheral destruction/sequestration
- Hypoproliferative marrow failure: low reticulocyte production, hypocellular or aplastic marrow on bone marrow biopsy, absent splenomegaly in many cases, causes include aplastic anemia, marrow toxic exposures, nutritional deficiency (B12/folate), and marrow infiltration.
- Peripheral destruction or sequestration: normal/high reticulocyte count for anemia, presence of hemolysis markers, splenomegaly suggests hypersplenism; immune-mediated cytopenias (AIHA, ITP), DIC, thrombotic microangiopathy (TTP/HUS) and massive splenic sequestration are examples.

Major diagnostic categories and specific considerations

Nutritional deficiency
Vitamin B12 and folate deficiencies cause ineffective hematopoiesis with macrocytic anemia and variable leukopenia and thrombocytopenia. Labs: macrocytosis (MCV >100 fL), low reticulocyte production, hypersegmented neutrophils. Serum B12 (normal roughly 200–900 pg/mL; values vary by lab), methylmalonic acid (elevated in true B12 deficiency), and homocysteine (elevated in both B12 and folate deficiency) are useful. Iron deficiency more commonly causes isolated anemia but can contribute to bicytopenia.

Marrow aplasia (aplastic anemia)
Idiopathic aplastic anemia reflects immune-mediated destruction of hematopoietic stem cells or direct injury (radiation, drugs, toxins, viral infections). Presentation: progressive pancytopenia with hypocellular marrow on bone marrow biopsy and fatty replacement. Key diagnostic criteria for severe aplastic anemia (commonly used) include at least two of: absolute neutrophil count (ANC) <0.5 x 10^9/L, platelet count <20 x 10^9/L, reticulocyte count <20 x 10^9/L, with hypocellular marrow. Evaluate for drugs (chloramphenicol, benzene, alkylating agents), viral causes (hepatitis viruses, parvovirus B19, EBV, CMV), and constitutional marrow failure syndromes (see inherited disorders section).

Marrow infiltration (leukemia, myelodysplasia, metastatic cancer, storage diseases)
Neoplastic infiltration by acute leukemia or myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS) often produces pancytopenia with variable peripheral smear findings (blasts, dysplastic neutrophils/platelets). Bone marrow is hypercellular with malignant or dysplastic cells. Other infiltrative causes include lymphoma, metastatic solid tumors, and storage disorders (e.g., Gaucher, Niemann-Pick). Bone marrow biopsy with morphology, flow cytometry, cytogenetics and molecular testing is essential.

Hemophagocytic syndromes (HLH)
Hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) and other macrophage activation syndromes cause severe cytopenias due to pathologic immune activation and phagocytosis of blood cells. Clinical features include fever, hepatosplenomegaly, hyperferritinemia (often >500 ng/mL, frequently >3000 ng/mL), hypertriglyceridemia, low fibrinogen, elevated soluble CD25 (sIL-2R), and hemophagocytosis on marrow/biopsy. Diagnostic criteria (HLH-2004) require 5 of 8 criteria: fever, splenomegaly, cytopenias in ≥2 lineages, hypertriglyceridemia and/or hypofibrinogenemia, hemophagocytosis, low/absent NK-cell activity, ferritin >500 μg/L, elevated sCD25.

Hypersplenism
Enlarged spleen can sequester erythrocytes, leukocytes and platelets, resulting in pancytopenia. Common causes include portal hypertension from chronic liver disease, hematologic malignancies, infections (e.g., malaria, schistosomiasis), and storage disorders. Features include splenomegaly on exam/imaging and peripheral smear showing relative abundance of mature cells but cytopenias. Correction of the underlying splenic enlargement or splenectomy in selected cases may improve counts.

Drugs and toxins
Drug-induced pancytopenia may result from direct marrow suppression or immune-mediated mechanisms. Common culprits: chemotherapeutic agents, antithyroid drugs (e.g., methimazole), anticonvulsants (e.g., carbamazepine), antibiotics (e.g., chloramphenicol), gold salts, and certain herbal supplements. Toxin exposures include benzene and insecticides. Time course, medication history, and sometimes bone marrow features (hypocellularity vs. myeloid arrest) help identify causation.

Stepwise diagnostic approach
- Step 1: Exclude immediate life-threatening causes: severe infection, overt hemolysis, active bleeding, TTP/HUS.
- Step 2: Review medications and exposures; stop suspected offending agents if safe.
- Step 3: Confirm pancytopenia and evaluate marrow response (reticulocyte count) and hemolysis markers.
- Step 4: Peripheral smear review for blasts, dysplasia, hemolysis.
- Step 5: Imaging for organomegaly (ultrasound/CT abdomen for splenomegaly) and lymphadenopathy.
- Step 6: Bone marrow aspiration and biopsy early if marrow failure, infiltration, blasts, or unclear cause—obtain flow cytometry, cytogenetics, molecular panels, iron stores, and storage disorder testing when indicated.
- Step 7: Directed tests: B12/folate, viral serologies (HIV, hepatitis B/C, parvovirus, EBV, CMV), autoimmune workup (ANA), ferritin/iron studies, and HLH panel if suspected.

Prognosis and treatment depend on cause; promptly identifying treatable etiologies (nutrient deficiency, drug toxicity, immune causes, infections) changes outcomes.

# Approach to hepatic fibrosis: metabolic, infectious, autoimmune, cholestatic, vascular and genetic causes

Hepatic fibrosis is the common pathway of chronic liver injury resulting in excessive deposition of extracellular matrix by activated hepatic stellate cells. Fibrosis may progress to cirrhosis, portal hypertension and hepatic insufficiency. Evaluation should characterize the pattern of injury (hepatocellular vs cholestatic), contributors to chronic hepatocyte injury, and potential reversible causes.

Pathophysiology overview
Fibrosis develops when chronic liver injury leads to persistent activation of stellate cells and myofibroblasts, producing collagen types I and III and other matrix components. Key drivers include oxidative stress, cytokine-mediated inflammation (e.g., TGF-β, PDGF), and sustained hepatocyte apoptosis or necrosis. Different etiologies produce characteristic patterns on histology: pericentral (zone 3) fibrosis in alcoholic and nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), periportal and bile-duct-centered fibrosis in cholestatic diseases, and vascular-centered fibrosis with sinusoidal congestion in congestive hepatopathy.

Metabolic causes
Nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) and its progressive form, NASH, are common causes of fibrosis in Western populations. Associated with obesity, insulin resistance, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and metabolic syndrome, NASH leads to hepatocellular ballooning, inflammation and pericellular fibrosis. Key clinical clues include metabolic risk factors, elevated ALT and AST (ALT more commonly > AST in early disease), imaging with hepatic steatosis, and noninvasive fibrosis assessment (FibroScan, serum fibrosis scores such as FIB-4, NAFLD fibrosis score). Hemochromatosis—iron overload—can produce hepatocellular injury and fibrosis; evaluate with transferrin saturation (TS%) and serum ferritin. A TS% >45% and ferritin >300 ng/mL in men or >200 ng/mL in women suggest iron overload; confirm with HFE mutation testing and liver iron quantification if needed.

Infectious causes
Chronic viral hepatitis B and C are major global causes of hepatic fibrosis. Serologic testing for HBsAg, anti-HBc, HBV DNA, and HCV antibody with HCV RNA quantification is essential. Chronic schistosomiasis and other helminth infections cause periportal fibrosis in endemic areas. Recurrent or chronic biliary infections may complicate cholestatic disorders and contribute to fibrosis.

Autoimmune causes
Autoimmune hepatitis (AIH) produces predominantly hepatocellular injury with interface hepatitis and can progress to rapid fibrosis if untreated. Serologic markers include ANA, anti–smooth muscle antibody (ASMA), anti–liver-kidney microsomal (anti-LKM) antibodies; hypergammaglobulinemia (elevated IgG) is common. Primary biliary cholangitis (PBC) affects small intrahepatic bile ducts, causing cholestatic liver enzymes (elevated alkaline phosphatase, GGT) and anti-mitochondrial antibodies (AMA). Primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC) affects large bile ducts, often in association with inflammatory bowel disease; cholangiography (MRCP/ERCP) shows multifocal strictures and beading.

Cholestatic etiologies
Obstructive cholestasis from bile duct stones, malignancy (cholangiocarcinoma, pancreatic head tumor), or strictures can cause progressive fibrosis if chronic. Intrahepatic cholestasis from drug-induced injury or genetic cholestatic disorders (e.g., progressive familial intrahepatic cholestasis in children) can also lead to fibrosis. Biochemically, cholestatic patterns show alkaline phosphatase and GGT elevations greater than transaminases; bilirubin elevation suggests advanced or obstructive disease.

Vascular causes
Budd-Chiari syndrome (hepatic vein outflow obstruction) and long-standing congestive hepatopathy from right heart failure cause sinusoidal congestion and centrilobular fibrosis. Nodular regenerative hyperplasia can produce portal hypertension without classic fibrosis. Vascular obliteration secondary to prothrombotic states must be considered; Doppler ultrasound and cross-sectional imaging assess hepatic venous outflow.

Genetic causes
Beyond hemochromatosis, a range of inherited disorders result in hepatic fibrosis. Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency causes panacinar emphysema and liver disease through accumulation of misfolded A1AT in hepatocytes, producing periportal and cirrhotic changes; serum A1AT levels and Pi phenotyping/genotyping are diagnostic. Wilson disease (ATP7B defect) leads to copper accumulation, causing hepatitis, fibrosis and neurologic manifestations. Serum ceruloplasmin is often low (<20 mg/dL), but confirm with 24-hour urinary copper or hepatic copper quantification. Lysosomal storage disorders (e.g., Gaucher) and congenital hepatic fibrosis (associated with polycystic kidney disease) are other genetic causes.

Integration of clinical, biochemical and noninvasive fibrosis assessment
Initial tests: liver enzymes (AST, ALT), cholestatic markers (alkaline phosphatase, GGT), bilirubin, albumin, INR, complete blood count for platelets (thrombocytopenia may reflect portal hypertension), and hepatitis serologies. Noninvasive risk stratification tools include transient elastography (FibroScan) to quantify liver stiffness; thresholds vary by etiology but higher stiffness correlates with higher fibrosis stage. Serologic scores—FIB-4, APRI—help determine need for further invasive testing. Indications for liver biopsy include diagnostic uncertainty, suspected autoimmune or metabolic liver disease requiring histologic staging or when noninvasive tests are discordant.

Key decision points
- If viral hepatitis identified: prompt antiviral therapy (HBV, HCV) can halt or reverse fibrosis progression.
- If autoimmune hepatitis suspected: immunosuppression can limit fibrosis.
- If cholestatic imaging suggests mechanical obstruction: relieve obstruction (endoscopic or surgical) to prevent fibrosis progression.
- For metabolic causes: lifestyle measures, glycemic control, and specific therapies (e.g., phlebotomy for hemochromatosis, zinc/penicillamine for Wilson disease) are disease-modifying.
- Evaluate for portal hypertension and complications (varices, ascites) when thrombocytopenia, splenomegaly or imaging suggest advanced fibrosis.

# Approach to pulmonary fibrosis: environmental exposures, connective tissue disease, drug-related, genetic predisposition, idiopathic

Pulmonary fibrosis (PF) denotes a heterogeneous group of interstitial lung diseases (ILDs) characterized by interstitial scarring, architectural distortion, and progressive gas-exchange impairment. Recognizing the pattern of ILD—usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP) versus nonspecific interstitial pneumonia (NSIP), organizing pneumonia, etc.—and identifying potential causes forms the basis of management and prognosis.

Clinical presentation and initial evaluation
Patients typically present with progressive exertional dyspnea, dry cough, bibasilar inspiratory crackles (velcro-like), and clubbing in many cases. Spirometry often shows a restrictive pattern with reduced forced vital capacity (FVC) and normal/low total lung capacity (TLC). Diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide (DLCO) is frequently reduced. High-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) is central to diagnosis; UIP pattern (subpleural basal-predominant reticulation with honeycombing and traction bronchiectasis) suggests idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) or chronic fibrotic ILD, whereas NSIP shows more ground-glass opacities and fine reticulation without prominent honeycombing.

Environmental and occupational exposures
Exposure history is pivotal. Known environmental causes include inorganic dusts (silica causing silicosis, coal mine dust), asbestos (asbestosis with pleural plaques and increased mesothelioma risk), and organic antigens causing hypersensitivity pneumonitis (farmer’s lung, bird fancier’s lung). Drug-induced pulmonary fibrosis can present similar to idiopathic forms and include agents such as amiodarone, bleomycin, methotrexate, nitrofurantoin, and some anti–TNF agents. Smoking history is relevant because it affects differential diagnosis and prognosis.

Connective tissue disease–associated ILD
Many connective tissue diseases (CTD)—systemic sclerosis, rheumatoid arthritis, polymyositis/dermatomyositis, mixed connective tissue disease and Sjögren’s syndrome—can cause ILD that ranges from NSIP to UIP patterns. Systemic sclerosis commonly causes restrictive disease and pulmonary hypertension. CTD-ILD often manifests with other systemic features (arthralgias, rash, Raynaud phenomenon, sicca symptoms) and serologic markers (ANA, anti-CCP, anti–Scl-70/anti-topoisomerase I, anti-Jo-1). Recognizing CTD is important because immunosuppression can be beneficial in some CTD-associated ILDs whereas it may be harmful in IPF.

Drug-related causes
A careful medication history should look for drugs known to cause pulmonary toxicity: chemotherapeutic agents (bleomycin, busulfan), antiarrhythmics (amiodarone), antibiotics (nitrofurantoin), immunomodulators (methotrexate, anti–TNF), and targeted cancer therapies. Temporal relationship between drug initiation and symptom onset, improvement with drug cessation, and characteristic radiologic or histologic patterns aid attribution.

Genetic predisposition and familial pulmonary fibrosis
Familial pulmonary fibrosis accounts for a minority of cases but is important in younger patients and those with family history. Mutations in telomerase complex genes (TERT, TERC), surfactant protein genes (SFTPC, SFTPA2), and other genes affecting alveolar epithelial cell function predispose to pulmonary fibrosis. Short telomere syndromes also link to bone marrow failure, liver disease and premature greying—making genetic causes particularly relevant when fibrosis is multisystem. Genetic testing may be indicated when clinical clues (family history, early onset, associated cytopenias or liver disease) are present.

Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF)
IPF is the prototype of progressive fibrosing ILD with a UIP pattern. Diagnostic criteria require exclusion of known causes of ILD (e.g., environmental exposure, CTD, drug toxicity) and the presence of a UIP pattern on HRCT or surgical lung biopsy when HRCT is nondiagnostic. The natural history is progressive with median survival approximately 3–5 years without antifibrotic therapy. Antifibrotic agents (pirfenidone, nintedanib) reduce decline in FVC and are indicated for IPF. Distinguishing IPF from other ILDs that have better responses to immunosuppression is clinically critical.

Approach to evaluation
- Detailed history: exposures, occupational history, medications, smoking, family history, systemic symptoms suggesting CTD.
- Pulmonary function testing: spirometry (restrictive pattern), lung volumes, DLCO; serial measurements track progression.
- HRCT chest: identify pattern (UIP vs NSIP vs organizing pneumonia) and extent of fibrosis. HRCT can obviate biopsy in classic UIP.
- Serologic testing for CTD: ANA, rheumatoid factor, anti-CCP, myositis panel, anti–Scl-70, anti-centromere, and others guided by clinical features.
- Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL): may help exclude infection and show lymphocytosis in hypersensitivity pneumonitis.
- Lung biopsy (surgical or transbronchial cryobiopsy) when HRCT is nondiagnostic and identification of a specific histopathology would change management.
- Screening for pulmonary hypertension with echocardiography when clinical suspicion or severe DLCO reduction exists.

Key decision points
- If a causative exposure, drug or CTD is identified, management targets the underlying cause (avoidance, drug cessation, immunosuppression where appropriate).
- For IPF and other progressive fibrosing ILDs not responsive to immunosuppression, antifibrotic therapy is indicated.
- Consider referral for lung transplantation for eligible patients with progressive disease despite optimal therapy.
- Monitor for complications: respiratory failure, pulmonary hypertension, and acute exacerbations (sudden worsening with new diffuse alveolar damage), which carry high mortality.

# Recognizing syndromic and multisystem inherited disorders

When hematologic, hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis coexist, inherited multisystem disorders or syndromes that affect fundamental cellular processes (iron metabolism, telomere maintenance, lysosomal storage, protein folding) should be considered. Recognizing these syndromic patterns allows targeted testing, family counseling, and disease-specific therapies.

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs)
Telomerase and telomere maintenance mutations (TERT, TERC, DKC1, and others) produce a spectrum of disease from dyskeratosis congenita to adult-onset presentations with pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis, bone marrow failure (pancytopenia), and premature graying. Telomere length testing (flow-FISH) and genetic testing for telomerase complex mutations establish diagnosis. Clinical clues include early-onset pulmonary fibrosis (<50–60 years), family history of pulmonary fibrosis or bone marrow failure, mucocutaneous features in some cases (nail dystrophy, oral leukoplakia), and combined organ involvement. Important implications: standard immunosuppressive regimens and myeloablative conditioning for transplantation carry greater risk in TBDs; reduced-intensity approaches and multidisciplinary planning are often required.

Hereditary hemochromatosis
HFE-related hemochromatosis (C282Y homozygosity or compound heterozygosity with H63D) and other iron-overload syndromes can cause hepatic fibrosis and cirrhosis, cardiomyopathy, diabetes mellitus and arthropathy. Hematologic presentation is less commonly pancytopenia; however, iron deposition may affect multiple organs. Diagnostic clues include elevated transferrin saturation and ferritin, family history, and liver iron quantification. Treatment with phlebotomy prevents progression; chelation is reserved for specific circumstances.

Lysosomal and other storage disorders
Gaucher disease, Niemann-Pick disease, and other storage disorders can cause hepatosplenomegaly, cytopenias from hypersplenism and marrow infiltration, and in some cases pulmonary involvement. Splenomegaly, bone crises, and characteristic enzyme assays (e.g., low glucocerebrosidase in Gaucher) or genetic testing confirm diagnosis. Enzyme replacement therapy or substrate reduction therapy may alter disease course in certain storage disorders.

Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency
A1AT deficiency causes emphysema and liver disease due to accumulation of abnormal A1AT in hepatocytes leading to hepatitis, fibrosis and cirrhosis. Affected individuals may have both liver and lung disease; diagnosis via serum A1AT level and phenotyping/genotyping. Management includes avoidance of hepatotoxins, augmentation therapy for emphysema in certain patients, and consideration for transplantation for advanced liver disease.

Congenital hepatic fibrosis and polycystic kidney disease (PKD)
Autosomal recessive PKD presents in children and can be associated with congenital hepatic fibrosis leading to portal hypertension; autosomal dominant PKD has adult presentation with cystic liver disease; both can have multisystem manifestations. Genetic testing may be indicated in suggestive clinical scenarios.

Integration into the differential for multisystem fibrosis
When liver and lung fibrosis are present with cytopenias, consider telomere syndromes first among inherited causes because of their triad of pulmonary fibrosis, bone marrow failure and hepatic fibrosis. Additional clues such as endocrinopathies, neurologic features, or characteristic dermatologic signs help prioritize testing. Multidisciplinary coordination (hepatology, pulmonology, hematology, medical genetics) is often required.

# Role of stepwise investigations to exclude reversible causes

A rational, stepwise investigation plan maximizes diagnostic yield while minimizing unnecessary invasive tests. Prioritize reversible and treatable causes, integrate noninvasive testing early, and escalate to invasive diagnostics when needed.

Principles of a stepwise approach
1. Triage and stabilization: address life-threatening problems first (severe cytopenias causing bleeding/infection, decompensated liver disease, respiratory failure). Supportive measures include transfusions, empiric antibiotics for neutropenic fever, and respiratory support as needed.
2. Focused history and directed physical exam: exposure, medication and family histories are often the most informative and can point to reversible etiologies such as drug toxicity or environmental exposures.
3. Baseline laboratory and imaging evaluation: CBC with smear and reticulocyte count; comprehensive metabolic panel including liver enzymes, albumin, bilirubin; coagulation tests; iron studies, B12/folate; serologies for viral hepatitis and HIV; autoimmune serologies; ferritin and transferrin saturation. Chest HRCT for pulmonary fibrosis and abdominal ultrasound for splenomegaly/hepatic morphology are high-yield early tests.
4. Noninvasive fibrosis assessment: transient elastography and serum fibrosis scores for liver; PFTs and DLCO for lung. These help stage disease and prioritize urgency of interventions.
5. Targeted etiologic testing: if viral hepatitis is suspected, obtain HBsAg and HCV RNA; if autoimmune disease suspected, test relevant antibodies; if iron overload suspected, obtain transferrin saturation and genetic testing for HFE. Consider toxicology screens and occupational exposure assessments.
6. Early bone marrow and/or liver biopsy when indicated: marrow biopsy is indicated for unexplained pancytopenia after initial noninvasive testing; liver biopsy is indicated if diagnosis remains uncertain and histology would change management. When both liver and bone marrow involvement are present, coordinate biopsies to reduce procedural risks and integrate findings.
7. Genetic testing: indicated when there is family history, early-onset disease, or multisystem involvement pointing to inherited conditions (telomere disorders, A1AT deficiency, Wilson disease, storage disorders). Telomere length testing and next-generation sequencing panels for bone marrow failure/ILD may be appropriate.
8. Multidisciplinary case review: complex multisystem disease benefits from combined expertise (hepatology, pulmonology, hematology, genetics, pathology, radiology) to synthesize data and determine management pathways.

Examples of prioritizing reversible causes
- If viral hepatitis or autoimmune hepatitis is identified early, initiation of antiviral therapy or immunosuppression can arrest fibrosis progression.
- If a suspect medication (e.g., methotrexate, nitrofurantoin) is the likely cause of cytopenias, liver injury or pulmonary fibrosis, drug cessation is a priority and may lead to improvement.
- In suspected hemochromatosis, early phlebotomy prevents progression to cirrhosis and cardiomyopathy.
- For hypersplenism from portal hypertension, managing portal pressure and considering splenic interventions may improve cytopenias and quality of life.

Laboratory reference points and diagnostic criteria (selected)
- Pancytopenia: no single numeric definition, but commonly considered when hemoglobin, ANC and platelet count are below laboratory normal ranges. Example normals: hemoglobin (adult male) 13.5–17.5 g/dL, ANC >1.8 x 10^9/L, platelet count 150–450 x 10^9/L.
- Severe aplastic anemia criteria (commonly used): bone marrow hypocellularity and at least two of: ANC <0.5 x 10^9/L, platelet <20 x 10^9/L, reticulocyte count <20 x 10^9/L.
- Hemochromatosis screening: transferrin saturation >45% suggests iron overload; confirmatory HFE genotyping and hepatic iron quantification if needed.
- Wilson disease: low ceruloplasmin (<20 mg/dL), elevated 24-hour urinary copper (>100 μg/day in symptomatic individuals), hepatic copper >250 μg/g dry weight on biopsy.
- HLH diagnosis: HLH-2004 criteria—5 of 8 features (fever, splenomegaly, cytopenias, hypertriglyceridemia/hypofibrinogenemia, hemophagocytosis, low NK-cell activity, ferritin >500 μg/L, elevated sCD25).
- Pulmonary function: restrictive physiology defined by reduced TLC <80% predicted; DLCO reduction is a sensitive early indicator of gas-exchange impairment.
- HRCT patterns: UIP pattern on HRCT (subpleural, basal predominance, reticular abnormality with honeycombing and traction bronchiectasis) is diagnostic of UIP in the appropriate clinical context.

Conclusion

Concurrent pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and pulmonary fibrosis require a disciplined diagnostic strategy that integrates careful history-taking, targeted laboratory testing, noninvasive imaging and fibrosis assessment, and timely invasive diagnostics when indicated. Prioritize reversible causes—nutritional deficiencies, infections, drug toxicities, autoimmune diseases and treatable metabolic disorders—before concluding on irreversible or genetic conditions. Be alert for syndromic presentations, particularly telomere biology disorders and storage diseases, that explain multisystem involvement and alter therapeutic decisions. Multidisciplinary collaboration and judicious use of genetic testing increasingly facilitate accurate diagnosis and tailored management in these complex patients.
