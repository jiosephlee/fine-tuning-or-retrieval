Introduction

Acute and chronic pancreatic injury may be accompanied by systemic inflammation, multiorgan dysfunction, pleural effusions, respiratory compromise, large-volume pancreatic fistula output, and prolonged nutritional failure. Supportive care and targeted nutritional management are central to stabilizing these patients, preventing complications, and optimizing recovery. This chapter outlines practical principles for respiratory support when pleural effusions or compromised gas exchange coexist with pancreatic disease, criteria for ICU-level monitoring and organ support, the evidence-based use of enteral nutrition (including nasogastric versus nasojejunal approaches) versus parenteral nutrition, metabolic and glycemic considerations during total parenteral nutrition (TPN), strategies to prevent and manage catheter-related bloodstream infections, and rehabilitation and dietary counseling for long-term pancreatic health.

# Respiratory support strategies for pleural effusion and compromised gas exchange

Pleural effusion is a common extra‑pancreatic complication of pancreatitis and pancreatic fistulae. Effusions can be reactive, transudative or exudative with a chemical pancreatitis-related pleuritis. Large effusions cause restrictive physiology, impaired ventilation-perfusion matching, hypoxemia, and atelectasis. Initial assessment should quantify respiratory compromise using clinical exam, oxygenation indices (pulse oximetry and arterial blood gas), and imaging (chest radiograph and ultrasound). Key parameters include SpO2, PaO2, respiratory rate, work of breathing, and presence of hypercapnia. Aim for SpO2 generally between 92–96% in most patients; in those at risk for hypercapnia targets may be lower.

Small, asymptomatic effusions usually require conservative management—analgesia, pulmonary toilet, and mobilization. Ultrasound is invaluable for bedside assessment of effusion size and guidance of thoracentesis. Therapeutic thoracentesis should be considered when the effusion contributes significantly to dyspnea or hypoxemia. Diagnostic thoracentesis is indicated when the effusion is moderate-to-large, unilateral, or atypical, or when infection is suspected. Pleural fluid analysis in pancreatitis often demonstrates amylase-rich, neutrophil-predominant exudate; the presence of infected pleural fluid or empyema necessitates prompt drainage.

Large or loculated effusions that reaccumulate rapidly, or effusions associated with sepsis, respiratory failure, or inability to oxygenate, may require chest tube drainage. Small-bore pigtail catheters placed under image guidance are frequently sufficient. In refractory recurrent effusions related to persistent pancreatic fluid leakage, surgical or interventional procedures addressing the pancreatic source may be required.

Management of compromised gas exchange beyond drainage includes optimization of oxygen delivery, ventilation strategies to minimize lung injury, and attention to pain, secretion clearance, and fluid balance. Supplemental oxygen is first-line for hypoxemia. High-flow nasal cannula (HFNC) provides heated, humidified oxygen at high flow rates and can improve oxygenation and reduce work of breathing in many patients. Noninvasive positive pressure ventilation (NIV) may be appropriate for hypercapnic respiratory failure or selected hypoxemic patients without contraindications (e.g., hemodynamic instability, inability to protect the airway, or high aspiration risk).

If invasive mechanical ventilation is required, lung-protective ventilation principles apply. Use low tidal volumes of approximately 6 mL/kg predicted body weight (PBW) and limit plateau pressures to <30 cm H2O. PBW is commonly calculated as follows: for males PBW = 50 + 0.91 × (height in cm − 152.4) kg, and for females PBW = 45.5 + 0.91 × (height in cm − 152.4) kg. Apply appropriate positive end expiratory pressure (PEEP) and consider recruitment maneuvers selectively. In patients with pancreatitis-associated acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), use standard ARDSNet protocols including conservative fluid management once perfusion is optimized. Prone positioning for at least 12–16 hours/day is indicated for moderate–severe ARDS (PaO2/FiO2 ratio <150 mmHg) if there are no contraindications.

Analgesia, antiemetics, and early mobilization are important adjuncts. Pain control reduces splinting and improves cough and pulmonary toilet; whenever feasible, multimodal analgesia and judicious use of regional techniques (e.g., epidural analgesia) can be helpful. Attention to fluid management is critical: excess crystalloids worsen pulmonary edema and pleural effusions, whereas overly aggressive restriction risks hypoperfusion—titrate fluid therapy to hemodynamics and markers of perfusion.

# Indications for ICU-level monitoring and organ support

Decisions to admit a patient with pancreatitis or pancreatic fistula to an intensive care unit hinge on physiologic instability, risk of rapid deterioration, and the need for organ support. Established clinical indications for ICU-level care include respiratory failure requiring HFNC, NIV, or invasive mechanical ventilation; hemodynamic instability necessitating vasopressors; severe metabolic derangements (e.g., refractory acidosis, hyperkalemia, severe hypocalcemia); renal failure requiring renal replacement therapy; altered mental status with airway compromise; and complicated infections including sepsis or infected pancreatic necrosis. In addition, high-output enteric or pancreatic fistulas (>200–500 mL/day, with variation depending on institutional practice) may require ICU care for fluid, electrolyte, and nutritional optimization.

Severity scoring systems such as the revised Atlanta classification and organ failure scores (e.g., Sequential Organ Failure Assessment, SOFA) help identify patients at risk of persistent organ dysfunction. Persistent single or multiple organ failure (lasting >48 h) correlates with mortality and is an indication for high-intensity monitoring. Early ICU admission benefits patients with rapidly progressive disease because it allows aggressive resuscitation, close hemodynamic monitoring (including arterial lines and advanced hemodynamic assessment when needed), prompt respiratory support, and timely initiation of nutrition and source control interventions.

ICU-level support also encompasses invasive monitoring for critically ill patients: continuous telemetry, arterial blood gas monitoring, central venous access for vasoactive infusions and monitoring, and the capacity to deliver TPN safely if enteral feeding is not possible. Decisions about initiating renal replacement therapy for complications such as severe electrolyte disturbances, uremia, or refractory volume overload should follow standard indications but may be reached sooner in the setting of active multiorgan dysfunction. Multidisciplinary critical care—involving surgery, gastroenterology, interventional radiology, nutrition, and infectious disease teams—is essential for managing the complex interplay between pancreatic pathology and systemic organ support needs.

# Enteral nutrition (nasogastric vs nasojejunal) versus parenteral nutrition: indications, benefits and risks

Nutritional support is a cornerstone of management in moderate-to-severe pancreatitis and in patients with pancreatic fistulas. The gut barrier function and mucosal integrity are preserved and improved by enteral nutrition (EN), which reduces bacterial translocation and infectious complications compared with parenteral nutrition (PN). Current practice favors early enteral feeding when feasible.

Timing and indications: For patients able to tolerate oral intake, early advancement to an oral diet as tolerated is appropriate. When oral intake is inadequate or contraindicated, EN should be initiated within 24–48 hours in moderately severe or severe pancreatitis, particularly in patients predicted to have prolonged inability to intake sufficient calories. Enteral feeding is indicated for patients with persistent vomiting, ileus or obstruction that precludes oral intake when EN can be advanced distal to the stomach; for those with high nutritional risk; or when the fistula output is expected to persist. Total parenteral nutrition is reserved for patients with contraindications to EN (complete bowel obstruction, ischemia, high-output proximal enteric fistula preventing distal feeding, severe intestinal dysmotility) or when EN has failed to achieve nutritional goals despite optimal attempts.

Nasogastric (NG) versus nasojejunal (NJ) feeding: Historically, NJ feeding was preferred because bypassing the stomach was thought to reduce pancreatic stimulation. However, randomized trials and meta-analyses have shown that NG feeding is often safe, well-tolerated, and as effective as NJ feeding in many patients with acute pancreatitis. NG feeding is easier to place, avoids endoscopic or fluoroscopic tube placement, and allows earlier initiation. NJ feeding remains appropriate for patients with persistent gastric outlet obstruction, severe gastroparesis, high aspiration risk, or when gastric feeding is poorly tolerated or increases pain or pancreatic secretions (though evidence for increased stimulation with NG feeding is limited).

Formula selection and trophic strategies: Disease-specific formulas are not required for pancreatitis, but polymeric, isotonic, standard enteral formulas are generally appropriate. Initiate EN at a low rate and advance as tolerated toward a goal of providing 60–70% of estimated energy needs within the first week, aiming for 25–30 kcal/kg/day in most non-obese adults or individualized targets using indirect calorimetry when available. For severely malnourished patients, start more cautiously to reduce risk of refeeding syndrome. Consider elemental or semi-elemental formulas in patients with malabsorption or high-output fistulae when digestion/absorption is impaired; these are absorbed more proximally and may reduce stool/fistula output.

Benefits of enteral nutrition: EN maintains gut mucosal integrity, reduces bacterial translocation and infectious complications (including infected necrosis), attenuates systemic inflammation, is cost-effective, and is associated with fewer catheter-related infections compared with PN. EN also supports bile flow and gut-associated lymphoid tissue function, important for host defenses.

Risks and complications of enteral feeding: Aspiration is the most serious risk, particularly with gastric feeding in patients with impaired airway protection. Diarrhea, tube dislodgement, sinusitis (with prolonged nasogastric tubes), and tube-related blockage are additional concerns. Monitor for worsening abdominal pain or increased fistula output after initiating EN; in many cases, distal enteral feeding reduces fistula output, but proximal feeding may exacerbate leakage in selected anatomies. Regular reassessment of tolerance and early involvement of nutrition specialists is critical.

Parenteral nutrition: Indications, benefits and risks—TPN provides full nutrient support intravenously and is necessary when EN is impossible, contraindicated, or insufficient to meet requirements. Indications include prolonged ileus, high-output proximal fistula without distal access, intractable vomiting despite decompression, short bowel unsuitable for enteral feeding, or when enteral access cannot be achieved. Benefits include precise nutrient delivery and avoidance of enteral feed intolerance.

However, PN is associated with higher rates of infectious complications, especially catheter-related bloodstream infections, metabolic complications (hyperglycemia, electrolyte disturbances), hepatobiliary dysfunction (steatosis, cholestasis), and gut mucosal atrophy if used long term. To reduce risks, aim to transition to EN as soon as feasible, use dedicated central venous access with strict aseptic care, and employ multimodal glycemic and metabolic monitoring.

Clinical decision points: In a patient with pancreatitis who cannot maintain adequate oral intake, attempt early enteral nutrition within 24–48 hours. Start with NG feeding unless specific contraindications to gastric feeding are present. If EN is not possible or fails after reasonable attempts, transition to TPN. For high-output fistulas where distal feeding is feasible (e.g., via NJ or percutaneous endoscopic jejunostomy), enteral feeding should be preferred because it often reduces fistula output and infection risk.

# Glycemic management and metabolic monitoring during TPN

TPN introduces a substantial carbohydrate load and alters metabolic milieu, so rigorous glycemic control and electrolyte monitoring are essential. Hyperglycemia in critically ill patients receiving TPN is common and is associated with increased infection risk and worse outcomes. In most critically ill patients the recommended glucose target is between 140–180 mg/dL (7.8–10.0 mmol/L). More stringent targets (e.g., 110–140 mg/dL) have not consistently shown outcome benefit and increase hypoglycemia risk.

Monitoring frequency and strategies: Blood glucose should be monitored frequently—initially every 4–6 hours and more often when insulin is being titrated, with bedside point-of-care checks. Continuous insulin infusions are appropriate for unstable patients; subcutaneous basal-bolus regimens may be used for stable patients receiving continuous TPN. Insulin can be added directly to the TPN bag in stable, predictable regimens, but this complicates titration because insulin and dextrose rates cannot be adjusted independently; adding insulin to the bag also precludes safe intermittent interruptions of TPN without causing hypoglycemia. For patients at high glycemic risk, an intravenous insulin infusion with hourly to 2-hourly monitoring until stable is preferred.

Electrolyte and metabolic monitoring: TPN predisposes to electrolyte abnormalities and refeeding syndrome, particularly in malnourished patients. Monitor serum electrolytes—potassium (normal 3.5–5.0 mmol/L), phosphate (normal 2.5–4.5 mg/dL or 0.81–1.45 mmol/L), magnesium (normal 1.7–2.2 mg/dL or 0.7–0.9 mmol/L)—daily initially and more often if abnormalities are present. Hypophosphatemia is an early marker of refeeding and can precipitously worsen after initiation of carbohydrate-containing nutrition. Thiamine deficiency can cause lactic acidosis and cardiac dysfunction; administer thiamine (e.g., 100 mg IV) prior to initiating feeding in malnourished patients. Regular monitoring of liver function tests and triglycerides is also necessary: hypertriglyceridemia (TG >400–500 mg/dL) can occur with lipid emulsions and increases pancreatitis risk; stop or reduce lipid infusion at very high levels. Typical laboratory monitoring frequency is daily for the first 3–5 days, then every 1–3 days as stable.

Caloric and macronutrient targets: Aim for energy provision around 25–30 kcal/kg/day for most non-obese adults, adjusted for clinical context and measured energy expenditure when available. Carbohydrate infusion rates should be titrated to avoid hyperglycemia and excessive CO2 production in ventilated patients; typical dextrose infusion ranges are individualized, generally not exceeding approximately 4–6 mg/kg/min for many adults to reduce hyperglycemia and hypercapnia risk. Lipid emulsions supply essential fatty acids; monitor triglycerides. Protein needs are increased with catabolic illness: target protein intake of 1.2–2.0 g/kg/day depending on severity, renal function, and catabolic state.

Prevention of refeeding syndrome: For patients with recent poor intake or significant weight loss, begin TPN at a reduced caloric rate (e.g., 50–70% of estimated needs) and advance slowly while monitoring electrolytes closely. Provide thiamine and B vitamins, and promptly correct hypophosphatemia, hypokalemia, and hypomagnesemia.

# Prevention and management of catheter-related bloodstream infections

Catheter-related bloodstream infections (CRBSIs) are a principal complication of central venous access required for TPN and are associated with significant morbidity and mortality. Prevention is more effective than treatment: adherence to insertion and maintenance bundles substantially reduces infection rates.

Preventive measures: Use the least-invasive central access necessary and prefer single-lumen catheters when possible. Choose insertion sites considering infection and complication risks; the subclavian site historically has lower infection rates than the femoral or internal jugular but carries higher mechanical complication risk—site selection must be individualized. Use maximal sterile barrier precautions at insertion (cap, mask, sterile gown, sterile gloves, and large sterile drape). Employ chlorhexidine 2%–2.5% in alcohol for skin antisepsis unless contraindicated. Use ultrasound guidance to reduce insertion complications, and remove unnecessary catheters promptly.

Maintenance precautions include sterile dressing changes with chlorhexidine-impregnated dressings where indicated, strict hub and line access hygiene (scrub the hub for at least 15 seconds with alcohol or chlorhexidine), and minimizing line manipulations. Consider antimicrobial-impregnated catheters or antimicrobial lock therapy in patients with recurrent infections despite optimal care, balancing benefits against resistance and toxicity. Use bundled checklists and staff education programs to sustain high compliance.

Recognition and diagnosis: Suspect CRBSI in any patient on parenteral lines with fever, rigors, unexplained hypotension, or local catheter site inflammation. Obtain blood cultures from the catheter and a peripheral site before starting antibiotics. Diagnostic criteria include growth of the same organism from catheter and peripheral blood cultures, or a differential time to positivity where cultures drawn from the catheter become positive at least 2 hours before peripheral cultures. In practice, management decisions often precede full microbiologic confirmation based on clinical status.

Management: Prompt empiric systemic antibiotics covering likely pathogens (including Gram-positive organisms such as Staphylococcus aureus and coagulase-negative staphylococci, and Gram-negative bacilli) are indicated in sepsis. Empiric therapy should be tailored to local patterns. Catheter removal is recommended in many situations: in septic shock, tunnel or port infections, persistent bacteremia despite 48–72 hours of appropriate antibiotics, infections with S. aureus or Candida species, or when complications such as endocarditis or thrombosis are suspected. For select patients with stable condition and less virulent organisms (e.g., coagulase-negative staphylococci) and a functioning catheter, catheter salvage with systemic antibiotics and antibiotic lock therapy may be considered under close monitoring.

Follow-up blood cultures should document clearance of bacteremia. Duration of therapy depends on organism and presence of complicated infection—e.g., 7–14 days after catheter removal for uncomplicated Gram-negative bacteremia, but longer courses are required for S. aureus bacteremia, endocarditis, or osteomyelitis.

# Rehabilitation and dietary counseling for long-term pancreatic health

Recovery from pancreatic injury and management of pancreatic fistulas extend beyond the acute hospital phase into long-term rehabilitation and dietary modification. Chronic pancreatic insufficiency (exocrine and endocrine), malnutrition, and altered quality of life are common sequelae. A structured, multidisciplinary approach including dietitians, physiotherapists, diabetes educators, pain management specialists, and gastroenterologists or pancreatic surgeons is essential.

Nutritional rehabilitation: Guidance must be individualized. For patients with exocrine pancreatic insufficiency (EPI), institute pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy (PERT). Typical starting doses for adults are 25,000–40,000 lipase units with meals and half that with snacks, titrated to symptoms and stool characteristics; maximum doses vary but some patients need up to 75,000–100,000 lipase units per meal under specialist guidance. Counsel patients to take enzyme preparations with meals and snacks and to adjust dosing based on stool frequency, consistency, steatorrhea, and weight trends. Proton pump inhibitors may augment enzyme effectiveness in some patients by reducing gastric acid degradation of enzymes.

Dietary recommendations: Contrary to older practice of prolonged fat restriction, patients with EPI should not be placed on excessively low-fat diets unless severe steatorrhea persists despite optimized PERT. Adequate dietary fat is important for caloric intake and absorption of fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K). Small, frequent meals can improve tolerance. Alcohol abstinence and smoking cessation must be emphasized—they accelerate disease progression and increase risk of recurrent pancreatitis. Nutritional counseling should include individualized caloric goals to restore or maintain body weight and composition.

Monitoring and supplementation: Regularly assess weight, body mass index, and markers of malnutrition including serum albumin and prealbumin (noting their limitations as acute-phase reactants). Screen for fat-soluble vitamin deficiencies and correct as indicated. Check glycemic status: pancreatogenic (type 3c) diabetes results from loss of insulin and glucagon secretion and can be labile; monitor fasting glucose, HbA1c, and consider continuous glucose monitoring for labile cases. Pancreatogenic diabetes often requires insulin therapy because of beta-cell deficiency; however, sensitizers like metformin can be used when appropriate. Bone health should be evaluated because chronic pancreatitis increases risk of osteoporosis; measure vitamin D and consider bone density scanning in at-risk patients.

Physical rehabilitation: Early mobilization and physiotherapy reduce deconditioning from prolonged hospitalization. Strength training and graded aerobic exercise aid in returning patients to baseline functional status and support glycemic control and weight maintenance. Address chronic pain with multimodal strategies, favoring non-opioid modalities when possible; long-term opioid dependence impairs rehabilitation.

Long-term follow-up: Arrange ongoing care with a gastroenterologist experienced in pancreatic disease. Surveillance for complications such as pseudocyst formation, biliary obstruction, and pancreatic malignancy (in chronic pancreatitis with longstanding disease and other risk factors) should be individualized. Educate patients about signs of malabsorption, steatorrhea, poor glycemic control, and infection that warrant prompt evaluation.

Psychosocial support and counseling: Chronic pancreatic disease carries significant quality-of-life implications. Provide resources for smoking and alcohol cessation, nutrition education, and mental health referrals as needed. Involve family in counseling about dietary modifications and enzyme administration where appropriate.

Conclusion

Supportive care and nutritional management in pancreatitis and pancreatic fistulas require an integrated approach centered on preserving organ function, preventing complications, and restoring nutrition. Early enteral nutrition is preferred when feasible, with TPN reserved for clear contraindications or failed enteral strategies, and meticulous glycemic and metabolic monitoring when TPN is used. Respiratory compromise from pleural effusions and ARDS demands timely drainage, respiratory support, and lung-protective ventilation. ICU-level monitoring is indicated for organ failure or anticipated rapid deterioration. Preventing catheter-related infections through insertion and maintenance bundles is crucial to reduce TPN-associated morbidity. Long-term recovery includes PERT for exocrine insufficiency, tailored dietary plans, rehabilitation to reverse deconditioning, surveillance for complications, and comprehensive psychosocial support. A multidisciplinary model yields the best outcomes for these complex patients.
