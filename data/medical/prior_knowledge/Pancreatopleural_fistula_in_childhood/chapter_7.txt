Introduction

Pleural fluid analysis is an essential component of evaluating patients with pleural effusion. Proper sample collection and a structured laboratory approach combining biochemical assays, cell counts, cytology, and microbiology allow clinicians to differentiate transudates from exudates, identify infection and empyema, detect malignancy, diagnose specific causes such as chylothorax or pancreatic effusion, and guide management (for example, when to drain). This chapter reviews practical aspects of pleural fluid specimen collection and handling, the interpretation of basic constituents (appearance, cell count, protein, LDH) including established diagnostic criteria, the clinical utility and cutoffs for additional biochemical markers (glucose, pH, triglycerides, bilirubin, amylase, lipase, adenosine deaminase), microbiologic testing strategies, the strengths and limits of cytologic evaluation for malignancy, and how to synthesize results into diagnostic algorithms.

# Specimen collection, handling and contraindications

Accurate pleural fluid testing begins with appropriate specimen acquisition and transport. Thoracentesis should be performed under ultrasound guidance when feasible to reduce complications. The initial fluid volume should be sufficient to permit multiple tests: typically at least 20–50 mL distributed into appropriate tubes (culture bottles, EDTA tube, sterile tube, and a tube with heparin/anaerobic syringe for pH). Recommended aliquots: 1–2 mL in EDTA for cell count and differential, 10–20 mL in blood culture bottles for bacterial culture, 10–20 mL in a sterile tube for chemistry and microbiology, and 2–3 mL placed immediately in an anaerobic syringe on ice for pH measurement.

Handling specifics that affect results:
- pH: Pleural fluid pH is labile when exposed to air and metabolically active cells. It should be collected anaerobically (air-tight syringe), transported on ice, and analyzed promptly (within 30 minutes when possible) to avoid artifactual alkalinization. If pH cannot be measured promptly, interpret with caution.
- Cell counts and differential: Use EDTA anticoagulated specimen and process within hours. Delay can lead to cell lysis and inaccurately low neutrophil counts.
- Microbiology: For suspected bacterial infection, inoculate pleural fluid directly into aerobic and anaerobic blood culture bottles at bedside — this improves yield versus plain sterile tubes. For suspected tuberculosis, send multiple specimens for AFB microscopy, mycobacterial culture, and nucleic acid amplification tests.
- Cytology: Send an adequate volume (often >20–40 mL) and inform the laboratory if malignancy is suspected so they can prepare cytospin and cell block specimens. Multiple samples increase sensitivity.
- Biochemistry: For tests such as glucose, LDH, protein, triglycerides, and amylase, pair pleural fluid samples with a contemporaneous serum sample to compute ratios and compare absolute values.

Contraindications and cautions:
Absolute contraindications to thoracentesis are few; localized skin infection at the puncture site and uncorrectable coagulopathy with active bleeding are relative contraindications. Recent anticoagulant use is not an absolute contraindication but requires risk assessment; guidelines typically advise correcting severe coagulopathy (e.g., INR >1.5 or platelets <50,000/µL) before large-volume procedures, though small-volume diagnostic thoracentesis may be performed cautiously. Suspicion of small or loculated effusion beneath septations on ultrasound may make thoracentesis less successful; tube thoracostomy or thoracoscopy may be needed. Large pulmonary bullae directly adherent to chest wall are a relative contraindication. Always weigh the potential diagnostic benefit against bleeding and pneumothorax risk.

# Basic constituents: appearance, cell count and differential, protein, LDH (Light's criteria)

Appearance
Gross inspection provides early clues. Clear, straw-colored fluid is common in simple transudates and many exudates. Cloudy, turbid, or purulent fluid suggests infection or empyema. Bloody fluid may indicate malignancy, pulmonary embolism with infarction, trauma, or iatrogenic bleeding. Milky fluid suggests chyle (chylothorax) or pseudochylothorax — biochemical tests (triglycerides and cholesterol) differentiate these. Greenish fluid can occur with infection by organisms producing pigments or with bile in biliopleural fistula.

Cell count and differential
Total nucleated cell count and differential guide the differential diagnosis. Normal pleural fluid is acellular or has low cellularity (often <1000 cells/µL). Typical patterns:
- Neutrophil predominance: acute processes such as bacterial parapneumonic effusion, pulmonary embolism (early), or esophageal rupture. Very high neutrophil counts and pus-like appearance are typical of empyema.
- Lymphocyte predominance: chronic inflammatory processes including tuberculosis, lymphoma, chylothorax, and malignancy (often adenocarcinoma). A lymphocyte fraction >50–60% raises possibility of TB or malignancy, but is not specific.
- Eosinophil predominance: pneumothorax, hemothorax, drug reactions, parasitic disease, or pulmonary embolism; eosinophils >10% often represent air or blood in pleural space rather than specific disease.
- Red blood cells: bloody or hemorrhagic effusions may have a high RBC count. A hematocrit >50% of peripheral blood hematocrit suggests hemothorax.

Protein and LDH — Light’s criteria
Distinguishing transudate from exudate is foundational because it narrows etiologies and directs further testing. Light’s criteria define an exudative effusion if any one of the following is present:
- Pleural fluid protein / serum protein > 0.5
- Pleural fluid LDH / serum LDH > 0.6
- Pleural fluid LDH > (2/3) × upper limit of normal (ULN) for serum LDH

For example, if the laboratory ULN for serum LDH is 250 U/L, the third criterion corresponds to pleural fluid LDH > 167 U/L. Light’s criteria are highly sensitive (near 98%) for identifying exudates but less specific; many patients with heart failure on diuretics will have “pseudo-exudates” that meet Light’s criteria despite transudative physiology. In those cases, calculating the serum-to-pleural fluid albumin gradient (serum albumin − pleural fluid albumin) >1.2 g/dL (or 12 g/L) suggests a transudate. Other practical approaches include measuring BNP to identify heart-failure–related effusions.

Interpretation nuance: Light’s criteria determine exudate vs transudate but do not provide diagnosis. If exudate, proceed with infection, malignancy, autoimmune, and other targeted tests. If transudate, evaluate for systemic causes (heart failure, hepatic hydrothorax, nephrotic syndrome).

# Biochemical markers: glucose, pH, triglycerides, bilirubin

Glucose
Pleural fluid glucose usually mirrors serum glucose because of free diffusion; normal pleural fluid glucose is similar to serum (typical serum glucose range 70–140 mg/dL depending on fasting). Pathologically low pleural fluid glucose occurs when increased metabolic activity (high leukocyte or bacterial activity), impaired transport by mesothelial cells, or consumption by malignant cells occurs. Common causes of low pleural fluid glucose (<60 mg/dL) include complicated parapneumonic effusions/empyema, rheumatoid pleuritis, tuberculous pleuritis, malignant effusions, and esophageal rupture. Very low levels (<30 mg/dL) strongly suggest rheumatoid pleuritis, empyema, or esophageal rupture. In practice, a low pleural fluid glucose in combination with low pH and neutrophil predominance supports complicated parapneumonic effusion or empyema.

pH
Normal pleural fluid pH is slightly alkaline compared to venous blood; typical values are around 7.60 when measured under anaerobic conditions immediately. However, measured pleural fluid pH in clinical practice often falls in the 7.40–7.55 range for simple effusions. Accurate interpretation requires proper sample handling (anaerobic, ice, rapid analysis).

Clinically important thresholds:
- pH < 7.20–7.25 in a parapneumonic effusion is widely used as an indication for tube thoracostomy (drainage), reflecting complicated infection and impaired resolution without drainage.
- pH < 7.30–7.32 in some settings also indicates increased risk and may prompt drainage depending on clinical context.
Low pleural fluid pH develops from accumulation of acidic metabolic products and CO2 in the pleural space due to bacterial metabolism and host cell activity, as well as impaired removal. Malignancy may also cause moderately low pH. Always correlate with clinical findings, glucose, and Gram stain/culture.

Triglycerides and cholesterol (chylothorax vs pseudochylothorax)
When pleural fluid appears milky, measure triglyceride and cholesterol levels to distinguish chylothorax (true chyle) from pseudochylothorax (cholesterol effusion in long-standing inflammatory effusions).
- Triglyceride > 110 mg/dL in pleural fluid is diagnostic of chylothorax in most series.
- Triglyceride < 50 mg/dL makes chylothorax unlikely.
- Triglyceride 50–110 mg/dL is indeterminate; lipoprotein electrophoresis for chylomicrons is definitive — the presence of chylomicrons confirms chylothorax.
Chylothorax commonly results from thoracic duct disruption or obstruction (trauma, malignancy, lymphoma). Pseudochylothorax (cholesterol effusion) typically has high cholesterol (>200 mg/dL) and low triglycerides.

Bilirubin
Pleural fluid bilirubin can help identify biliopleural fistula or bile leakage into pleural space after hepatic surgery or diaphragmatic injury. A pleural fluid bilirubin higher than serum bilirubin or a pleural fluid/serum bilirubin ratio >1 suggests presence of bile. This is uncommon but should be considered with right-sided pleural effusion after hepatobiliary injury or pancreatobiliary disease.

# Amylase and lipase measurement in pleural fluid: significance and thresholds

Pleural fluid amylase and lipase measurements can identify effusions related to pancreatic disease, esophageal perforation, or malignancy. Mechanisms for elevated pleural fluid amylase include direct transdiaphragmatic passage of pancreatic enzymes in pancreatitis-related effusions, mediastinal tracking from pancreatico-pleural fistula, or high serum amylase diffusion in esophageal rupture and malignancy.

Interpretation points:
- Elevated pleural fluid amylase is usually considered significant when pleural fluid amylase is greater than the upper limit of normal serum amylase, when pleural fluid/serum amylase ratio >1, or when absolute pleural fluid amylase is markedly elevated (many centers use pleural fluid amylase >250–1,000 U/L as a threshold, recognizing interlaboratory variability). Esophageal rupture and pancreaticopleural fistula often produce very high pleural fluid amylase (frequently >1000 U/L).
- If pleural fluid amylase is elevated and the isoenzyme analysis shows salivary-type predominance, esophageal rupture or malignancy is more likely; pancreatic-type predominance suggests pancreatic origin.
- Pleural fluid lipase tends to parallel amylase in pancreatic-related effusions; high lipase strengthens the case for pancreatic origin but is less widely standardized.

Clinical implications:
- A pleural effusion with very high amylase should prompt evaluation for pancreatitis, pancreaticopleural fistula (consider CT chest/abdomen, MRCP, or ERCP), or esophageal rupture (contrast esophagram, CT). In the setting of acute chest or abdominal pain, consider esophageal perforation urgently.

# Adenosine deaminase (ADA) utility and limitations for tuberculous pleuritis

Adenosine deaminase (ADA) is an enzyme produced by activated lymphocytes and macrophages. High pleural fluid ADA levels are a useful adjunct for diagnosing tuberculous pleuritis, particularly in regions with moderate to high tuberculosis (TB) prevalence.

Performance characteristics and cutoffs:
- Commonly used ADA cutoff: 40 U/L. At this level, sensitivity is generally high (about 90% or greater in many studies) and specificity moderate to high depending on prevalence and patient population.
- Higher cutoffs (e.g., 70–100 U/L) increase specificity but reduce sensitivity.
- In lymphocyte-predominant exudates with ADA >40 U/L and compatible clinical context (fever, night sweats, contact/exposure risk, endemic area), TB pleuritis is highly likely and empiric anti-TB therapy may be considered while awaiting confirmatory testing.
- Pleural fluid ADA is less useful in low-prevalence settings because the positive predictive value falls.

Limitations and pitfalls:
- False positives occur with empyema, rheumatoid pleuritis, lymphoma, some malignancies, and parapneumonic effusions — particularly if neutrophil-predominant and with high ADA from neutrophil activation. Certain lymphomas and empyemas can cause markedly elevated ADA.
- ADA is most informative when combined with lymphocyte-predominant effusion, clinical context, and additional testing (e.g., pleural fluid culture for Mycobacterium tuberculosis, pleural biopsy histology or culture, nucleic acid amplification tests).
- ADA testing should not be used in isolation to start lifelong TB therapy without further confirmatory evidence unless clinical urgency and risk justify empiric treatment.

# Microbiology: Gram stain, bacterial culture, mycobacterial tests, pleural fluid PCR (e.g., GeneXpert)

Gram stain and bacterial culture
Gram stain of pleural fluid provides rapid results but has limited sensitivity, often detecting organisms in only a minority of cases of parapneumonic effusion or empyema. Nevertheless, a positive Gram stain is specific and can direct early antimicrobial choice. Optimal yield requires inoculation of pleural fluid into blood culture bottles at the bedside; this improves detection of pathogens compared to plating on solid media alone. Empyema or frank pus typically yields positive cultures more commonly than non-purulent parapneumonic effusions.

Key microbiology practices:
- Inoculate both aerobic and anaerobic blood culture bottles with pleural fluid at bedside for suspected bacterial infection.
- For empyema, send an adequate volume; prior antibiotic therapy reduces culture yield but does not preclude growth if sufficient volume is cultured.
- Be aware of anaerobic pathogens, particularly in aspiration-related empyema; anaerobic cultures are important and require proper specimen handling.

Mycobacterial tests
Diagnosis of tuberculous pleuritis relies on a combination of microbiology and histology. Pleural fluid Ziehl–Neelsen acid-fast bacilli (AFB) smear has low sensitivity (<10–20%). Mycobacterial culture of pleural fluid increases sensitivity (but still often limited), and cultures may take several weeks (e.g., up to 6–8 weeks for solid media; liquid culture systems are faster). Pleural biopsy for histology and culture has higher sensitivity than pleural fluid culture and is often required when TB is strongly suspected.

Nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs) — e.g., Xpert MTB/RIF (GeneXpert)
- NAATs can detect M. tuberculosis complex DNA and rifampicin resistance rapidly. For pleural fluid, sensitivity is variable and generally lower than for sputum; reported sensitivities for pleural fluid range widely (often 40–70%), with higher specificity (~95%).
- A positive pleural fluid NAAT supports TB diagnosis quickly, but a negative result does not exclude TB pleuritis. Combining ADA, lymphocyte predominance, pleural biopsy, and culture increases diagnostic yield.
- GeneXpert on pleural tissue or pleural biopsy specimens often has better sensitivity than on pleural fluid.

Additional molecular diagnostics
- Broad-range molecular testing, targeted PCR for specific pathogens, and next-generation sequencing may be used in difficult cases, particularly when conventional cultures are negative. However, these tests are not routine and should be used in consultation with infectious disease and microbiology specialists.

# Cytology for malignancy: sensitivity, sampling considerations

Cytologic examination of pleural fluid is a principal, noninvasive test for diagnosing malignant effusions and is often the first-line test for suspected malignancy. Sensitivity and factors affecting yield:
- Overall sensitivity of pleural fluid cytology for malignancy is approximately 60% on the first sample, increasing to 70–80% with a second sample. Sensitivity varies by tumor type: adenocarcinomas (lung, breast, ovarian) tend to shed cells more readily and are more commonly detected than mesothelioma, which often yields low cytologic sensitivity.
- Tumor type matters: ovarian and breast adenocarcinoma and metastatic adenocarcinomas are among the most readily detected; mesothelioma is notoriously difficult — cytology alone often fails to make the diagnosis and pleural biopsy (closed needle or thoracoscopic) with histopathology and immunohistochemistry is required.
- Volume and preparation: Larger volume samples (e.g., >20–50 mL) and dedicated cytology processing (cytospin, cell block) improve detection. Communicate clinical suspicion to the laboratory so they prepare cell blocks and immunostains.
- Repeated thoracenteses increase sensitivity. If cytology is negative but clinical suspicion remains high, proceed to pleural biopsy (image-guided closed pleural biopsy or thoracoscopic biopsy), which has higher sensitivity for malignancy and TB.
- Immunocytochemistry and molecular testing on cell block preparations can subtype tumors and identify actionable mutations (e.g., EGFR, ALK) if sufficient material is available.

Clinical implication:
- A positive cytology establishes malignant pleural involvement and generally upstages cancer, often altering management (e.g., palliative drainage, pleurodesis). Negative cytology does not exclude malignancy, and further tissue diagnosis is often required when cancer is suspected.

# Interpreting results in the clinical context and diagnostic flowcharts

Integration of pleural fluid data with clinical history, imaging, and laboratory findings is essential. No single test provides a definitive diagnosis in most cases; interpretation uses combinations of cell counts, biochemical values, microbiology, and cytology.

A pragmatic diagnostic approach:
1. Initial classification: Apply Light’s criteria (using paired serum measurements) to determine transudate versus exudate. If transudate, pursue systemic causes (cardiac, hepatic, renal); if exudate, proceed with targeted workup.
2. Assess appearance and cells: Purulent or frankly purulent fluid or very low pH and glucose favors complicated parapneumonic effusion/empyema and prompts drainage and empiric antibiotics. A neutrophil-predominant, low-pH (<7.20), low-glucose (<60 mg/dL) effusion with positive Gram stain/culture is empyema. Lymphocyte-predominant exudate with ADA >40 U/L suggests tuberculous pleuritis in the right context.
3. Use biochemistry to direct specific causes:
   - Chylothorax: milky appearance + triglycerides >110 mg/dL or chylomicrons.
   - Pseudochylothorax: milky appearance + high cholesterol (>200 mg/dL) and low triglycerides.
   - Pancreatic or esophageal origin: markedly elevated pleural amylase (often > 250–1000 U/L) and/or high lipase; consider imaging for pancreaticopleural fistula or contrast study for esophageal rupture.
   - Bile staining: elevated pleural fluid bilirubin relative to serum suggests biliopleural communication.
4. Microbiology: If infection suspected, inoculate culture bottles and order Gram stain; a positive culture or Gram stain prompts targeted antibiotics and drainage decisions. For suspected TB, consider ADA, NAAT, pleural fluid culture, and pleural biopsy.
5. Malignancy: Send cytology (adequate volume, cell block). If cytology is positive, manage as malignant effusion; if negative but suspicion remains, perform pleural biopsy (thoracoscopy has the highest yield and allows pleurodesis).
6. Reassess and escalate: Negative or indeterminate tests should prompt reconsideration of differential and escalation to pleural biopsy, advanced imaging (CT, MRCP), or targeted interventions based on strong clinical clues.

Example decision nodes (conceptual flowcharts)
- Suspected parapneumonic effusion: if pleural fluid pH < 7.20 or glucose < 60 mg/dL or frank pus or positive Gram stain/culture → chest tube drainage + antibiotics. If pH > 7.20 and no purulence → consider conservative management with antibiotics and close follow-up.
- Suspected TB pleuritis: lymphocyte-predominant exudate + ADA > 40 U/L in endemic setting → high probability; consider empirical therapy while pursuing culture/biopsy. In low-prevalence settings or atypical features, obtain pleural biopsy.
- Milky effusion: measure triglycerides and cholesterol; if triglycerides >110 mg/dL or chylomicrons present → chylothorax → evaluate for trauma, malignancy, lymphoma, and thoracic duct obstruction/rupture. If triglycerides low but cholesterol high → pseudochylothorax → chronic inflammatory disease or rheumatoid pleuritis.

Limitations and clinical judgment
- Test performance depends on pretest probability and disease prevalence: a positive ADA in high-prevalence area is more likely true than in low-prevalence settings. Similarly, cytology sensitivity depends on tumor type and sampling.
- Laboratory variability: reference ranges and assay methodologies differ; always interpret results using local lab ULN and units and using paired serum values when required.
- Timeliness: some tests (e.g., cultures) take time — use rapid tests (Gram stain, NAAT, ADA) and clinical judgment for early management.
- Multidisciplinary coordination: close collaboration with pulmonology, infectious disease, thoracic surgery, radiology, and pathology improves diagnostic yield and patient outcomes.

Summary

Pleural fluid analysis requires systematic collection, prompt handling, and a rational testing sequence. Light’s criteria remain the gateway to categorizing effusions as transudates versus exudates; subsequent biochemical tests (glucose, pH, triglycerides, bilirubin, amylase/lipase) and cell counts narrow the differential. ADA and nucleic acid tests help identify tuberculous pleuritis, while microbiology with blood culture bottle inoculation improves pathogen detection in empyema. Cytology detects many malignant effusions but has imperfect sensitivity; pleural biopsy is frequently necessary. Interpreting results within the clinical context and following established decision points (e.g., low pH and glucose indicating need for drainage) ensures that laboratory data translate into effective, timely clinical action.
