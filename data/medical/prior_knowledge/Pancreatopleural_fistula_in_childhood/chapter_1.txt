Introduction

The pancreas is a composite gland with essential exocrine and endocrine roles in digestion and metabolic homeostasis. Its anatomic position, vascular supply, and ductal architecture underlie common clinical problems such as pancreatitis, ductal disruption with fluid tracking, pancreatic fistulae, and tumor spread. Understanding the gross and microanatomy, the physiology of secretion, and the local anatomic relationships is crucial for interpreting imaging, planning interventions, and anticipating routes for extrapancreatic extension of pancreatic fluid collections or fistulae. This chapter presents a focused, clinically oriented review of pancreatic anatomy and physiology, emphasizing the ductal system and pathways for ductal disruption and fluid tracking.

# Gross anatomy: head, neck, body, tail relationships

The pancreas is a retroperitoneal gland that extends transversely across the posterior abdominal wall from the curve of the duodenum on the right to the hilum of the spleen on the left. Conventionally it is divided into four parts: head (including the uncinate process), neck, body, and tail. The head is nestled in the C-loop of the duodenum; the descending portion of the duodenum wraps around the right lateral aspect of the head. The uncinate process projects posteriorly and medially from the lower part of the head and typically lies posterior to the superior mesenteric vessels. The neck overlies the superior mesenteric artery (SMA) and vein (SMV) where the mesenteric vessels cross anterior to the aorta; the pancreatic neck is a short constricted segment between head and body and is a frequent site for surgical transection. The body extends leftward across the midline, anterior to the aorta and the left renal vein, and the tail reaches the splenic hilum, often lying within the splenorenal (lienorenal) ligament. The tail is the mobile, most intraperitoneal portion and is the closest to the splenic vessels and the diaphragmatic surface of the spleen.

The pancreas is classically described as a secondarily retroperitoneal organ except for the tail, which is intraperitoneal when it lies within the splenorenal ligament. The anteroposterior thickness varies but is typically 2–3 cm in body; overall length ranges from 12–18 cm in adults. The gland lies approximately at the level of vertebral bodies L1–L2 for the head and L2–L3 for the tail; this positional relationship is important when localizing pathology relative to retroperitoneal fascial planes.

Clinically relevant features of the gross anatomy include the close relationship of the head and uncinate process to the intrapancreatic common bile duct and major vascular structures, predisposing to obstructive jaundice or vascular invasion by tumors in the head. The body and tail’s proximity to the splenic artery and vein, transverse colon and left kidney explains routes for hemorrhage, vascular thrombosis, and inflammatory extension into adjacent organs.

# Pancreatic ductal anatomy: main pancreatic duct (Wirsung), accessory duct (Santorini), ampulla of Vater

The ductal system drains the vast network of acini and small ducts into the gastrointestinal tract. The principal duct is the main pancreatic duct (duct of Wirsung), which forms by the convergence of small intercalated and intralobular ducts and runs the length of the gland from tail toward the head, then typically turns inferiorly to pass through the head and to terminate at the major duodenal papilla. The main duct receives tributaries from the pancreatic head, body and tail; its diameter varies by location and age but is normally less than about 3 mm in the head and often smaller in the body (a commonly cited normal upper limit is 3 mm in the head and 2 mm in the body). Dilatation of the main duct is a key diagnostic sign of obstructive processes such as chronic pancreatitis or neoplasm.

An accessory pancreatic duct (duct of Santorini) may persist; when present it drains part of the anterior head and sometimes empties into the minor duodenal papilla, located approximately 2 cm proximal and superior to the major papilla. Embryologic fusion of dorsal and ventral pancreatic buds normally produces a dominant duct of Wirsung. In pancreas divisum, dorsal and ventral ducts fail to fuse, resulting in the majority of pancreatic drainage occurring via the accessory duct into the minor papilla. This variant predisposes, in some patients, to outflow obstruction at the minor papilla and is an important anatomic consideration for ductal disruption and drainage procedures.

The distal termination of the main pancreatic duct is closely related to the distal common bile duct; together they form a variable ampullary complex at the major duodenal papilla. The common channel formed by the union of bile and pancreatic ducts is called the ampulla of Vater; muscular sphincteric control (sphincter of Oddi) modulates flow into the duodenum. The anatomic length of any common channel is variable; its configuration affects the risk of biliary reflux into the pancreatic duct and has implications for endoscopic interventions and for local tumor spread.

Ductal branching is fractal: progressively smaller intralobular and intercalated ducts drain each acinus. Duct wall epithelium transitions from simple cuboidal to columnar with increasing caliber of ducts. For clinicians, knowing the ductal map explains how a focal ductal leak in the head or tail can produce very different patterns of localized or tracking fluid collections and how endoscopic or percutaneous access to the duct depends on whether the duct(s) communicate and where the leak is located.

# Microanatomy: acinar cells, ductal cells, islets of Langerhans

The pancreatic parenchyma comprises exocrine acini and endocrine islets of Langerhans interspersed in a lobulated architecture bounded by connective tissue septa. Exocrine acinar cells form grape-like clusters (acini) that synthesize and secrete digestive proenzymes (zymogens) and fluid. Acinar cells are polarized epithelial cells with abundant rough endoplasmic reticulum at the basal aspect for protein synthesis and apically located zymogen granules containing trypsinogen, chymotrypsinogen, proelastase, procarboxypeptidases, and pro-phospholipase A2. Centroacinar cells and the smallest ducts (intercalated ducts) receive acinar secretions; centroacinar cells are unique transitional epithelial cells that logically are the initial ductal elements within the acinus.

Ductal cells modify the primary secretion by adding bicarbonate-rich fluid and by transporting chloride and water; these cells are important for neutralizing duodenal acid and for providing an optimal intraluminal environment for enzyme activity. Key molecular components include CFTR (cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator) chloride channels and apical Cl^-/HCO_3^- exchangers. Dysfunction of ductal bicarbonate secretion—genetic or acquired—contributes to protein plug formation, ductal obstruction, and pancreatitis.

The islets of Langerhans are clusters of endocrine cells scattered throughout the pancreas, most concentrated in the tail. Islets contain several cell types: beta cells (insulin-secreting, ~60–70% of islet cells), alpha cells (glucagon, ~20%), delta cells (somatostatin), PP cells (pancreatic polypeptide), and epsilon cells (ghrelin). Islet microvasculature is dense and fenestrated, providing rapid release of hormones into the portal circulation. Local paracrine interactions between acinar and islet cells exist, and inflammatory or surgical damage to the pancreatic parenchyma can produce endocrine insufficiency.

Pathophysiologically, premature intrapancreatic activation of zymogens—most notably conversion of trypsinogen to trypsin—triggers autodigestion, inflammation, and systemic responses characteristic of acute pancreatitis. Structural injury to acinar-ductal units can allow enzyme-rich fluid to escape the ductal system, tracking along fascial planes.

# Exocrine secretion physiology: enzyme synthesis, activation, secretion regulation (CCK, secretin, neural inputs)

Exocrine pancreatic secretion comprises a protein-rich enzymatic component and an aqueous bicarbonate-rich component. Enzyme synthesis occurs in acinar cells where digestive enzymes are synthesized as inactive zymogens, packaged into zymogen granules, and released by regulated exocytosis into the acinar lumen. The aqueous component is produced primarily by ductal and centroacinar cells and contains large amounts of bicarbonate (HCO_3^-) essential to neutralize gastric acid entering the duodenum.

Activation of zymogens normally occurs in the duodenal lumen: enterokinase (enteropeptidase) on the duodenal brush border cleaves a small peptide from trypsinogen to generate active trypsin, which then activates the other pancreatic zymogens. This spatial separation of zymogen secretion (pancreatic acini) and activation (duodenum) protects the pancreas from autodigestion. Any condition that brings enterokinase into contact with pancreatic secretions within the duct or acinar lumen—or that activates trypsinogen locally—can precipitate pancreatitis.

Regulation of secretion is a coordinated response to a meal and involves hormonal and neural inputs. The two principal hormones are cholecystokinin (CCK) and secretin. CCK is released from I-cells in the duodenal and jejunal mucosa in response to luminal fats and peptides; CCK acts principally on acinar cells (directly via CCK-A receptors and indirectly via vagal afferents and efferents) to stimulate enzyme secretion and to contract the gallbladder. Secretin is released from S-cells in response to duodenal acidification and acts mainly on ductal cells to stimulate bicarbonate and water secretion via cyclic AMP–mediated activation of CFTR and HCO_3^- transporters. The combined effects of CCK and secretin produce an enzyme-rich, alkaline pancreatic juice that optimizes intraluminal digestion.

Neural control is mediated through the autonomic nervous system. Vagal parasympathetic stimulation enhances both enzyme and fluid secretion; afferent vagal and enteric reflexes link luminal sensing to secretion. Sympathetic input tends to modulate blood flow and can inhibit pancreatic secretion under stress. Clinical pharmacologic modulation of these pathways—e.g., somatostatin analogs to inhibit secretion, secretin in diagnostic testing—leverages an understanding of these control mechanisms.

Normal pancreatic secretory output varies with stimuli and individual factors; basal resting secretions are low but postprandial enzyme secretion increases markedly. Secretin stimulation tests historically were used to assess exocrine function: expected pancreatic bicarbonate concentration in duodenal aspirate after secretin is typically >80 mEq/L in normal function (laboratory methods vary). In practice, fecal elastase and direct function testing are used for assessing exocrine insufficiency.

# Vascular supply and lymphatic drainage

Arterial supply to the pancreas is a rich anastomotic network derived mainly from the splenic artery for the body and tail and from the pancreaticoduodenal arcades for the head. The splenic artery runs along the superior border of the pancreas, giving multiple small pancreatic branches (dorsal pancreatic artery, great pancreatic artery) that supply the body and tail. The head and uncinate process receive blood from the superior pancreaticoduodenal artery (a branch of the gastroduodenal artery from the common hepatic artery) and the inferior pancreaticoduodenal artery (a branch of the superior mesenteric artery). These anterior and posterior pancreaticoduodenal arcades form robust collateral circulation between the celiac and SMA territories. Venous drainage follows the arterial supply: veins of the body and tail drain to the splenic vein, and veins from the head drain to the superior mesenteric vein; ultimately the splenic and superior mesenteric veins join to form the portal vein.

Clinically, the pancreatic vascular anatomy is important because pancreatic disease can involve or compress these vessels. For instance, inflammation or malignancy in the pancreatic head can compromise the portal venous confluence or SMV, while splenic vein thrombosis is a known complication of chronic pancreatitis affecting the tail/body with the consequence of left-sided portal hypertension and gastric varices.

Lymphatic drainage is complex and primarily follows the arterial distribution. Lymph from the head drains to pancreaticoduodenal and pyloric nodes and then to the superior mesenteric and celiac nodes. Lymph from the body and tail drains to pancreatico-splenic (splenic hilum) nodes and then to the celiac nodes. There are also connections to para-aortic (periaortic) nodes. This pattern of lymphatic spread underlies the staging and surgical planning for pancreatic neoplasms and explains why disease in different parts of the gland may metastasize to distinct nodal basins.

Innervation, relevant to physiology and pain, includes sympathetic fibers from the greater and lesser splanchnic nerves converging on the celiac plexus and parasympathetic fibers via the vagus. Pain in pancreatic disease often localizes to the epigastrium and may refer to the back via visceral afferents following sympathetic fibers.

# Anatomic relations to retroperitoneum, diaphragm, mediastinum and adjacent organs (stomach, spleen, left kidney, colon)

The pancreas’s position at the level of the posterior abdominal wall places it in intimate relation with multiple organs and fascial planes that serve as conduits for inflammatory fluid, blood, or pancreatic enzymes when ductal integrity is lost.

Anteriorly, the pancreas lies posterior to the stomach and lesser sac. The anterior surface of the body and tail is separated from the stomach by the lesser sac (omental bursa). Posteriorly, the pancreas abuts the retroperitoneum and structures such as the left kidney and adrenal gland (especially posterior to the body and tail), the splenic vein and artery (superior border), and the SMA/SMV at the neck region. The transverse colon and its mesocolon cross inferior to the body of the pancreas; inflammatory or neoplastic processes can involve the transverse colon or produce colonic fistulae.

Because most of the pancreas (head, neck, body) is secondarily retroperitoneal, pancreatic fluid collections—particularly those arising from the body and tail—tend to track within retroperitoneal spaces. The retroperitoneal spaces (anterior pararenal space, perirenal space, posterior pararenal space) and fascial planes—Gerota’s fascia, Zuckerkandl’s fascia, the posterior parietal peritoneum—determine the direction of spread. For example, fluid from the pancreatic tail commonly tracks into the left perirenal space and can extend along the psoas muscle or retroperitoneal fascial planes.

Superior extension through the diaphragmatic hiatuses and mediastinal hiatuses provides a route to the thoracic cavity: pancreatic fluid or inflammatory collections can ascend through the esophageal hiatus or aortic hiatus into the posterior mediastinum, resulting in mediastinal pseudocysts or pancreaticopleural fistulae. Direct irritation of the pleura by enzyme-rich fluid can cause large, often amylase-rich pleural effusions, most commonly on the left but potentially bilateral.

Laterally, the splenic hilum and spleen are adjacent to the tail; inflammation can cause splenic vein thrombosis, splenic infarction, or even subcapsular splenic hematoma. Medial relationships with the left kidney and adrenal allow retroperitoneal tracking to the flanks. Inferiorly, enzymatic fluid may erode into the transverse mesocolon and colon, producing colonic fistulae or hemorrhage.

Understanding these anatomic corridors is essential when interpreting CT or MRI imaging of pancreatic fluid collections and when planning drain placement: percutaneous access frequently exploits retroperitoneal windows to avoid transgression of peritoneal cavity or viscera.

# Common anatomic variants relevant to fistula formation

Several anatomic variants of pancreatic ductal anatomy and of peripancreatic structures have important clinical consequences for ductal obstruction and fistula formation.

Pancreas divisum is the most common congenital variant of ductal anatomy and occurs when dorsal and ventral pancreatic ducts fail to fuse. In classical pancreas divisum, the dorsal duct (draining most of the pancreas, particularly the body and tail) drains into the minor papilla, while the ventral duct drains the inferior head and uncinate process into the major papilla. Because the minor papilla is relatively small, some individuals experience relative outflow obstruction, recurrent pancreatitis, or isolated dorsal ductal hypertension that promotes ductal disruption. In pancreas divisum, leaks from the dorsal duct may therefore drain preferentially to the lesser sac or retroperitoneum and may be less accessible via standard endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) approaches unless the minor papilla is cannulated.

Anatomic variants of the ampullary complex—such as a long common channel between the bile duct and pancreatic duct—can modify intraductal pressure dynamics and predispose to reflux or combined biliary and pancreatic disease. Similarly, an accessory pancreatic duct that is functionally dominant can be a site of stenosis or leak.

Vascular variants, such as replaced right hepatic artery arising from the SMA coursing near the superior aspect of the pancreatic head, or aberrant splenic artery branches, may affect surgical dissection and increase the risk of intraoperative bleeding, which in turn can complicate postoperative collections and fistulae.

Pancreatic morphological variants such as annular pancreas (a ring of pancreatic tissue around the duodenum), ectopic pancreatic tissue, or atrophic segments from chronic pancreatitis change local architecture and may create abnormal ductal communication pathways. Postsurgical anatomy—after distal pancreatectomy or pancreaticoduodenectomy—alters the routes available for ductal leakage: leaks from a transected pancreas stump (commonly at the neck) may produce localized fluid collections that track along the plane of resection into the mesocolon, retroperitoneum, or peritoneal cavity depending on whether the stump is intraperitoneal.

Finally, congenital anomalies of the peritoneal reflections (persistent or atypical attachments of the transverse mesocolon, variations of the splenorenal ligament) can modulate the path of least resistance for fluid tracking between the lesser sac, left retroperitoneum, and thorax. Clinically, anticipating these variants helps when planning endoscopic drainage (notably, choice of major vs minor papilla cannulation in pancreas divisum), percutaneous catheter placement, or surgical approach to control ductal leaks.

Conclusion

A detailed understanding of pancreatic anatomy—macroscopic structure, ductal architecture, microanatomy, physiologic secretion, vascular and lymphatic networks, and peripancreatic relationships—is indispensable for diagnosing and managing pancreatic disease. The ductal system’s configuration and its anatomic relations to retroperitoneal and mediastinal fascial planes dictate where enzyme-rich fluid will collect and how it may form fistulas to adjacent cavities. Clinicians must integrate knowledge of normal anatomy and common variants with physiologic principles (e.g., CCK/secretin regulation, zymogen activation) to interpret clinical presentations, select appropriate imaging and interventions, and reduce complications when treating pancreatic pathology.
