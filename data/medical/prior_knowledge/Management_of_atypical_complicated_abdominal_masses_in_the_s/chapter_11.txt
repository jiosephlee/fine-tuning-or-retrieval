Introduction

Surgical procedures during pregnancy—whether obstetric or non-obstetric—pose unique challenges for anesthesiologists and perioperative teams because of simultaneous physiologic obligations to the mother and the fetus. The anesthetic plan must balance maternal safety, optimize uteroplacental perfusion and fetal oxygenation, and anticipate pregnancy-specific complications (aspiration risk, altered airway anatomy, hemodynamic lability, and changes in drug pharmacokinetics). Intraoperative fetal monitoring and timely documentation can guide intraoperative management and postoperative disposition, but monitoring strategies depend on gestational age, the procedure being performed, and institutional resources. This chapter outlines the physiologic considerations underlying anesthetic technique selection, positioning, monitoring of maternal oxygenation, hemodynamics and temperature, the principles and practical techniques for intraoperative fetal surveillance, indications and agents for uterine relaxation or tocolysis, and postoperative maternal–fetal monitoring.

# Choice of anesthetic technique (general vs regional) and physiologic implications

Selecting between general and regional anesthesia in a pregnant patient requires integrating gestational age, the nature and urgency of the surgery, maternal comorbidities, airway assessment, coagulation status, and fetal considerations. Whenever feasible and appropriate, regional anesthesia is preferred for many procedures because it avoids airway manipulation and the hemodynamic and respiratory consequences of general anesthesia and many intravenous anesthetic agents. However, regional anesthesia has its own physiologic implications and contraindications.

Physiologic changes in pregnancy relevant to anesthetic choice include increased oxygen consumption (approximately 20–30%), decreased functional residual capacity (FRC), increased minute ventilation, reduced gastric emptying, increased risk of aspiration, increased blood volume (plasma volume increases more than red cell mass causing dilutional anemia), and aortocaval compression by the gravid uterus. These factors influence the choice and management of both general and regional anesthesia. For example, decreased FRC and increased oxygen consumption shorten safe apnea time, increasing the urgency for rapid securement of the airway if general anesthesia is required. Pregnant patients are at increased risk for difficult airway due to mucosal edema and weight gain.

Regional anesthesia (neuraxial: spinal, epidural, or combined spinal–epidural) provides effective analgesia/anesthesia for many types of surgery, including lower extremity and lower abdominal procedures, and it is the preferred approach for cesarean delivery. Advantages of regional anesthesia include avoidance of airway instrumentation, reduced fetal exposure to systemic anesthetics, decreased incidence of postoperative thromboembolism when combined with early mobilization, and often superior postoperative analgesia. However, neuraxial blockade produces sympathetic blockade that can cause hypotension, which reduces uteroplacental perfusion if not promptly recognized and treated. Proper preloading or co-loading with fluid and early vasopressor use (phenylephrine is commonly used for spinal-induced hypotension in obstetric anesthesia) can mitigate this risk. Coagulation abnormalities, local infection at the site, patient refusal, or inability to cooperate are contraindications to neuraxial techniques. For major abdominal or thoracic procedures, regional anesthesia alone is often not feasible.

General anesthesia is indicated for procedures requiring full muscle relaxation, when regional anesthesia is contraindicated or insufficient, or in emergent situations. Physiologic effects to consider include the impact of intravenous and inhalational agents on uterine tone and fetal physiology: most anesthetic agents cross the placenta to varying degrees. Volatile anesthetics in clinical concentrations can cause uterine relaxation (useful for intrauterine manipulation but potentially increasing hemorrhage). Propofol, opioids, and benzodiazepines cross the placenta; fetal depression is usually transient for single-dose exposures, but prolonged infusions can have greater effects. Maternal hypotension during induction (due to vasodilation or myocardial depression) reduces uteroplacental perfusion and must be prevented with preoxygenation, use of rapid sequence induction in appropriate cases, and prompt vasopressor support. Rapid sequence induction is often recommended for pregnant patients at risk of aspiration; cricoid pressure may be used but must be balanced against its potential to impair laryngoscopic view and ventilation. Airway management plans should anticipate difficulty: have difficult airway equipment and personnel available.

Intraoperative anesthetic technique should be individualized, and the decision should be documented in the chart with the maternal and fetal considerations. When possible, consultation with obstetrics and neonatology prior to planned procedures is prudent.

# Maternal positioning to avoid aortocaval compression

Aortocaval compression is a physiologic phenomenon caused by the gravid uterus compressing the inferior vena cava and, to some extent, the abdominal aorta when the patient lies supine. This compression reduces venous return to the heart, decreasing preload, cardiac output, and uteroplacental perfusion; it can result in maternal hypotension and fetal hypoxia. The risk increases as the uterus enlarges, typically after 20 weeks’ gestation, and is most pronounced in the third trimester.

Clinical goals for positioning are to relieve compression of the great vessels while maintaining patient access for surgery. The standard technique is left uterine displacement (LUD). A left lateral tilt of 15 degrees is usually sufficient to relieve inferior vena cava compression; this can be achieved by placing a wedge under the right hip or by tilting the entire operating table. Some clinicians prefer a 15–30 degree tilt depending on the patient’s body habitus and intraoperative hemodynamic response. Complete left lateral decubitus positioning is ideal for prevention of compression but may be impractical for many surgical procedures.

For cesarean delivery under neuraxial anesthesia, placing the patient supine with a left tilt or wedge and careful monitoring is standard. In patients undergoing general anesthesia, a left tilt should be maintained throughout induction and emergence because even short periods of aortocaval compression during these vulnerable phases can have consequences. Intraoperative positioning must also take into account the potential for pressure injuries, peripheral nerve compression, and the needs of the surgical team; padding and frequent reassessment are necessary.

When left tilt is not feasible (certain neurosurgical or cardiac procedures require strict head/neck positioning), alternative strategies include manual displacement of the uterus by an assistant, gradual table tilt adjustments, and hemodynamic management with fluids and vasoactive drugs. If aortocaval compression is suspected (sudden hypotension and decreased end-tidal carbon dioxide or uterine tone), immediate leftward displacement and hemodynamic support are indicated.

# Monitoring maternal oxygenation, hemodynamics and temperature

Maintaining adequate maternal oxygenation, hemodynamic stability, and normothermia is essential for maternal safety and fetal oxygen delivery. Pregnancy physiologic changes modify normal ranges and require attention to specific targets.

Oxygenation: Maternal oxygen consumption increases in pregnancy, while FRC decreases, shortening the acceptable apnea period. Preoxygenation prior to induction of general anesthesia is critical. Continuous pulse oximetry is the standard; aim to maintain SpO2 ≥95% in pregnant patients to ensure adequate maternal arterial oxygen partial pressure and fetal oxygen delivery. If supplemental oxygen is required, low-flow oxygen via nasal cannula or face mask is typically used intraoperatively. In intubated patients, maintain adequate ventilation to keep maternal arterial oxygen and carbon dioxide within target ranges (see ventilation below).

Ventilation and carbon dioxide: Pregnancy is characterized by a state of chronic respiratory alkalosis due to increased minute ventilation, producing a baseline maternal PaCO2 of approximately 30 mmHg (normal nonpregnant PaCO2 ~40 mmHg). During general anesthesia with controlled ventilation, target end-tidal CO2 (ETCO2) is commonly set to approximate the lower maternal baseline: ETCO2 ≈ 30 mmHg or such that arterial PaCO2 is in the range of 30–35 mmHg. Note that ETCO2 underestimates PaCO2 by a variable gradient, but using ETCO2 values and periodic arterial blood gases when needed is reasonable. Hypocapnia (excessive hyperventilation with PaCO2 <25 mmHg) reduces uterine blood flow via vasoconstriction; hypercapnia compromises maternal and fetal oxygenation. Therefore, moderate hypocapnia consistent with pregnancy baseline is acceptable; avoid extremes. When regional anesthesia is used and the mother breathes spontaneously, the inherent hyperventilation of pregnancy typically maintains an appropriate PaCO2.

Hemodynamics: Maternal blood volume expands in pregnancy and systemic vascular resistance decreases; cardiac output increases. Uteroplacental perfusion is pressure-dependent and has no autoregulation, so maternal blood pressure is a key determinant. Maintain maternal mean arterial pressure (MAP) close to baseline—commonly within 10–20%—to preserve uteroplacental blood flow. There is no single universal MAP target, but avoiding MAP <65 mmHg is a reasonable minimum; for pregnant patients with chronic hypertension or preeclampsia, targets should be individualized to their baseline pressures. Prompt treatment of hypotension following neuraxial blockade (e.g., with phenylephrine or ephedrine) is important; phenylephrine is often first-line in obstetric anesthesia because it restores blood pressure rapidly and maintains fetal acid–base status better than ephedrine in many studies, although ephedrine retains a role particularly with bradycardia. Use vasopressors titrated to effect and consider continuous infusions for persistent hypotension. Cardiac output monitoring may be useful in selected high-risk cases.

Monitoring modalities: Standard monitors per the American Society of Anesthesiologists include continuous ECG, noninvasive blood pressure, pulse oximetry, capnography (for intubated or sedated patients), temperature, and urine output when appropriate. Invasive arterial blood pressure monitoring is indicated for major surgery, expected large fluid shifts, severe preeclampsia, or when frequent arterial blood gases are needed. Central venous pressure may be helpful for major resuscitation but has limited utility for routine cases. Advanced hemodynamic monitoring (esophageal Doppler, pulse contour analysis, cardiac output monitors) may be considered in high-risk patients.

Temperature: Maternal core temperature should be maintained within the normal physiologic range (approximately 36.5–37.5°C). Hypothermia increases the risk of coagulopathy, wound infection, delayed drug metabolism, and shivering that increases oxygen consumption. Actively warm patients with forced-air warming blankets, warmed intravenous fluids, and warmed irrigation fluids during lengthy procedures. Monitor temperature intraoperatively and address deviations promptly. Hyperthermia should also be avoided as maternal fever can be associated with fetal tachycardia and potential neurodevelopmental risks with prolonged exposure.

# Principles and techniques for intraoperative fetal monitoring and documentation

Goals and scope: Intraoperative fetal monitoring (IFM) aims to assess fetal well-being in real time during maternal surgery and anesthesia, detect acute fetal distress, and guide interventions. The decision to monitor intraoperatively depends on gestational age (fetal viability), type and urgency of surgery, expected maternal hemodynamic instability, and availability of personnel and neonatal support. In many institutions, continuous fetal heart rate (FHR) monitoring is considered for viable fetuses (generally ≥23–24 weeks’ gestation, though institutional viability thresholds vary) undergoing non-obstetric surgery when feasible. For previable fetuses or surgeries remote from the uterus with low risk to the fetus, intermittent monitoring or no intraoperative monitoring may be reasonable.

Monitoring modalities: The primary modality is external fetal heart rate monitoring with a cardiotocograph (CTG) that records fetal heart rate and uterine activity. Intraoperative use may be challenging due to surgical field, patient positioning, electrocautery, and interference from maternal heart rate; nevertheless, external ultrasound-based monitoring (Doppler) is commonly used. When external monitoring is inadequate (obesity, difficult placement) and if an obstetrician is present, internal fetal monitoring via fetal scalp electrode is an option during cesarean or when the membranes are ruptured and the cervix is dilated, but is rarely used in non-obstetric surgery.

Monitoring frequency and duration: For continuous monitoring, continuous CTG tracing for the entire procedure is ideal when fetal viability and resource availability allow, and when there is potential for rapid deterioration. If continuous monitoring is not feasible, obtain preoperative baseline fetal heart rate tracings (e.g., a nonstress test) and intraoperative intermittent checks before induction, after major anesthetic events (induction, significant hemodynamic changes), and immediately postoperatively.

Interpretation: Standard fetal heart rate baseline and reactivity parameters apply. Normal baseline FHR is 110–160 beats per minute (bpm). A reactive nonstress test (NST) criterion for fetuses ≥32 weeks is at least two accelerations of ≥15 bpm lasting ≥15 seconds within 20 minutes. For fetuses <32 weeks’ gestation, the threshold is two accelerations of ≥10 bpm lasting ≥10 seconds within 20 minutes (the 10×10 criterion). Abnormalities include baseline tachycardia (>160 bpm), baseline bradycardia (<110 bpm), reduced variability, recurrent variable decelerations, late decelerations (uteroplacental insufficiency), and prolonged decelerations (>2 minutes but <10 minutes). A prolonged deceleration of ≥10 minutes is considered a change in baseline and indicates severe compromise.

Documentation: Document fetal monitoring in the anesthetic record similarly to other intraoperative data. Record the baseline fetal heart rate when monitoring begins, frequency of fetal heart rate checks if intermittent, any significant changes (time, nature, duration), interventions performed in response (maternal repositioning, oxygen administration, fluids, vasopressors, tocolytics), and consultations (obstetrics, neonatology). Note the method of monitoring (external CTG, Doppler) and any technical limitations. Clear documentation is essential for continuity of care and medico-legal purposes.

Algorithm for abnormal tracings: When a nonreassuring fetal heart tracing is detected intraoperatively, the first steps are to optimize the maternal physiologic state, as most fetal compromise during surgery is secondary to maternal factors. Immediate interventions include ensuring left uterine displacement or left tilt to relieve aortocaval compression, increasing inspired oxygen concentration to 100%, increasing maternal blood pressure and cardiac output (fluids, vasopressors), correcting maternal hypoventilation or hyperventilation, and treating uterine hypertonus or tachysystole if present. If fetal bradycardia persists despite maternal optimization, and gestational age is viable, emergent obstetric evaluation and possible delivery (cesarean) may be required depending on the clinical context, surgical procedure, and maternal condition. Institutional protocols for intraoperative fetal distress should be established and rehearsed.

Limitations: Awareness of limitations is necessary. Continuous FHR monitoring in the operating room may be technically difficult during certain procedures or with the use of electrocautery and other equipment. Interference of maternal heart rate can confound tracings. CTG interpretation should be performed by personnel trained in fetal surveillance, and prompt communication between anesthesia and obstetric teams is essential.

# Use of tocolytics and uterine relaxants: indications, agents, and evidence

Definition and indications: Tocolytics and uterine relaxants are agents used to reduce uterine contractility. In the perioperative setting, indications include prevention or treatment of uterine contractions during or after non-obstetric surgery when uterine irritability is present, management of intraoperative uterine tetany (e.g., after manipulation), and facilitating certain fetal procedures or cesarean extensions. However, routine prophylactic tocolysis for non-obstetric surgery is not uniformly recommended and should be individualized; evidence for routine use is limited. Use is more common when uterine instrumentation occurs or there is a high risk of preterm labor.

Common agents and mechanisms:

- Beta-adrenergic agonists (terbutaline): Terbutaline is a selective beta-2 agonist that relaxes uterine smooth muscle by increasing cyclic AMP. It can be given subcutaneously (0.25 mg) or intravenously (titrated infusion). Side effects include maternal tachycardia, palpitations, hyperglycemia, hypokalemia, and pulmonary edema with excessive fluid administration. Terbutaline can cause fetal tachycardia. It is effective for short-term tocolysis but is not commonly used for prolonged maintenance therapy due to systemic side effects. In the United States, terbutaline for prolonged tocolysis carries safety cautions.

- Calcium channel blockers (nifedipine): Nifedipine inhibits uterine smooth muscle contractions by blocking L-type calcium channels. Oral immediate-release nifedipine (e.g., 10–20 mg loading followed by 10–20 mg every 3–6 hours) is widely used for tocolysis and is generally well tolerated. Adverse effects include maternal hypotension, headache, flushing, and tachycardia. In the intraoperative setting, caution is necessary because nifedipine-induced hypotension can compromise uteroplacental perfusion; if used intraoperatively, hemodynamic status must be monitored closely and vasopressors used as needed.

- Magnesium sulfate: Magnesium acts as a calcium antagonist and neuromuscular blocker and is used primarily for fetal neuroprotection before anticipated preterm delivery and for eclampsia prophylaxis/therapy. Magnesium has modest tocolytic effects but is not the most effective agent for tocolysis; it may be used in select situations. Side effects include maternal flushing, nausea, decreased deep tendon reflexes, respiratory depression at high levels, and effects on neonatal muscle tone if used near delivery.

- Nitroglycerin: Nitroglycerin provides rapid uterine relaxation by nitric oxide–mediated smooth muscle relaxation and systemic vasodilation. It is useful for short-term uterine relaxation in the operating room, for example to facilitate uterine manipulation, uterine relaxation during difficult fetal extraction, or management of uterine inversion. Intravenous boluses (50–100 micrograms) or short infusions can be titrated; sublingual or spray forms are alternatives in some settings. Because nitroglycerin causes systemic hypotension, it must be used cautiously with immediate hemodynamic monitoring and vasopressor support available. Nitroglycerin’s rapid onset and short duration make it attractive for short procedures requiring profound relaxation.

- Oxytocin antagonists (atosiban): Atosiban is an oxytocin receptor antagonist used for tocolysis in some countries; it is not approved in all jurisdictions (e.g., not approved widely in the United States). Atosiban selectively inhibits myometrial contractions mediated by oxytocin and has a favorable side-effect profile compared with beta-agonists. Its intraoperative utility is limited by availability and by the need for parenteral administration.

- Other agents: Indomethacin (a prostaglandin synthesis inhibitor) can be used as tocolysis, particularly before 32 weeks, but limited use intraoperatively is typical due to fetal effects (oligohydramnios, premature constriction of the ductus arteriosus) and maternal renal effects.

Evidence and practical guidance: The evidence for routine prophylactic tocolysis for non-obstetric surgery is weak. Most guidelines recommend that tocolytics not be used routinely but consider them when there is uterine irritability, contractions, cervical change, or procedures that manipulate the uterus. If uterine contractions occur intraoperatively, initial management should focus on optimizing maternal oxygenation and hemodynamics and relieving mechanical stimuli (positioning, removing incision tension). If contractions persist or are judged to threaten preterm labor, tocolytics can be administered in consultation with obstetrics. Choose the agent based on the clinical scenario: nitroglycerin for immediate, short-term relaxation during an operation; nifedipine or terbutaline for ongoing tocolysis; magnesium for neuroprotection if preterm delivery is anticipated before 32 weeks (note timing and standard dosing); and avoid NSAID tocolytics near term due to ductus arteriosus concerns.

Monitoring during tocolysis: Monitor maternal heart rate, blood pressure, urine output, and serum electrolytes as appropriate. For terbutaline, watch for maternal tachycardia and signs of pulmonary edema. For nifedipine or nitroglycerin that can cause hypotension, have vasopressors ready and titrate doses to maintain MAP near baseline. If magnesium sulfate is used, monitor deep tendon reflexes, respiratory rate, and serum magnesium levels in prolonged infusions.

# Postoperative monitoring in the immediate maternal–fetal period

The immediate postoperative period demands focused monitoring of both mother and fetus because anesthetic and surgical effects may persist and complications such as bleeding, hemodynamic instability, aspiration pneumonitis, and uterine activity may present after the procedure.

Maternal monitoring: Continue standard postoperative monitoring until the patient meets discharge criteria from the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU). Key parameters include oxygenation (pulse oximetry; maintain SpO2 ≥95%), ventilation (respiratory rate and capnography where applicable), hemodynamics (frequent blood pressure and heart rate checks), urine output, pain control, temperature, and neurologic status. For patients who received neuraxial anesthesia, monitor sensory and motor block regression and watch for high spinal block symptoms. For patients who received opioids or sedatives intraoperatively, ensure adequate reversal and monitoring for respiratory depression. Early mobilization, thromboprophylaxis where indicated, and attention to fluid balance are also important.

Fetal monitoring and obstetric evaluation: For viable fetuses, perform fetal heart rate assessment in the PACU as soon after surgery as is feasible. If continuous intraoperative monitoring was performed, compare immediate postoperative fetal status to intraoperative baseline and document. For fetuses at or near viability, prolonged monitoring may be advisable for several hours postoperatively to detect delayed fetal compromise. For preterm fetuses at risk of preterm birth, consider neonatal consultation and, if indicated, administration of antenatal corticosteroids and magnesium sulfate according to gestational age and anticipated delivery timing (corticosteroids generally recommended for anticipated delivery before 34 0/7 weeks; institutional protocols may vary).

Uterine activity and bleeding: Monitor for uterine contractions, vaginal bleeding, and abdominal pain. Uterine irritability and preterm labor can occur after surgery. Document uterine tone and bleeding status, and involve obstetrics if uterine contractions, cervical change, or bleeding are noted. Assess the possibility of surgical causes of bleeding in the abdomen versus obstetric causes.

Analgesia and medications: Provide multimodal analgesia that is safe in pregnancy and compatible with breastfeeding for the postpartum period. Regional techniques (e.g., epidural analgesia) can provide effective pain control and minimize systemic opioid use. If opioids are used, monitor for neonatal respiratory depression if delivery occurs soon after administration. Review all medications given perioperatively for fetal safety; counsel the patient and obstetric team if potentially teratogenic agents were used in the first trimester. Document time and doses of sedative and analgesic agents.

Disposition and follow-up: Determine postoperative disposition based on maternal stability, gestational age, and fetal status. Patients with any intraoperative or postoperative maternal hemodynamic instability, nonreassuring fetal monitoring, preterm labor, or significant obstetric concern should be admitted for further observation and obstetric review. For otherwise straightforward cases with stable maternal and fetal status, admission decisions should follow usual post-surgical criteria, but ensure obstetric follow-up within an appropriate timeframe (e.g., same day or 24–48 hours) for high-risk pregnancies or earlier if concerns exist.

Communication and documentation: Effective communication between anesthesia, surgery, obstetrics, and neonatology is essential. Document the anesthetic course, maternal intraoperative events and interventions, fetal monitoring results, medications with times and doses, and specific recommendations for postoperative fetal surveillance and follow-up. Explicit handoff instructions should include when to call obstetrics or neonatology, parameters for concern (e.g., new uterine contractions, fetal heart rate <110 or >160 bpm, maternal hypotension), and anticipated next steps.

Conclusion

Perioperative management of pregnant patients requires an integrated approach that considers physiologic changes of pregnancy, the effects of anesthetic techniques and medications on uteroplacental perfusion and fetal oxygenation, and careful maternal and fetal monitoring before, during, and after the procedure. Regional anesthesia is preferred when appropriate, but both regional and general anesthesia can be applied safely with attention to airway planning, hemodynamic targets, oxygenation, and temperature maintenance. Positioning to avoid aortocaval compression, appropriate maternal monitoring, prudent use of fetal monitoring based on viability and institutional resources, judicious use of tocolytics when clinically indicated, and coordinated postoperative surveillance are essential components of safe perioperative care for pregnant patients.
