Introduction

Understanding the population-level distribution and determinants of uterine leiomyomas (fibroids) and endometriosis is essential for clinical reasoning: it shapes pretest probabilities, informs the index of suspicion for particular presentations, guides the selection and interpretation of diagnostic tests, and influences counseling about prognosis and reproductive planning. Both conditions are common gynecologic disorders of reproductive-aged women, but they differ in their pathophysiology, typical age of onset, associations with race and body habitus, and relationships to parity and infertility. This chapter synthesizes current epidemiologic knowledge and known or proposed risk factors, compares patterns of presentation across the life course, and outlines how these data should modify the clinician’s pretest probabilities and differential diagnoses when evaluating pelvic pain, abnormal uterine bleeding, an adnexal mass, or infertility.

# Prevalence of uterine leiomyomas and adnexal endometriomas across age groups and ethnicities

Prevalence estimates for both leiomyomas and endometriosis vary by study design, case definition, and population sampled. Key determinants of reported prevalence are whether the study uses clinical diagnosis, imaging (transvaginal ultrasound or MRI), operative diagnosis, or pathologic confirmation. Estimates drawn from population-based imaging and surgical cohorts give complementary perspectives.

Uterine leiomyomas (fibroids)
Uterine leiomyomas are extremely common. When assessed by imaging or at hysterectomy/autopsy, lifetime cumulative prevalence approaches 70–80% among women by menopause. The prevalence increases with age during the reproductive years: by the late 30s and 40s many women have detectable leiomyomas. Clinically significant fibroids—those causing symptoms prompting medical attention (heavy menstrual bleeding, pelvic pressure, bulk symptoms, pain, or reproductive dysfunction)—are less frequent than imaging-detected leiomyomas; estimates suggest roughly 20–30% of reproductive-aged women develop clinically significant fibroids.

Ethnic differences are pronounced. Black (African-American) women develop fibroids at younger ages, have higher prevalence, larger and more numerous tumors, and greater symptom burden compared with White women. Relative risk estimates vary, but Black women commonly have 2–3 times the prevalence of clinically significant fibroids seen in White women in many cohorts. Asian and Hispanic women have intermediate prevalence in different studies. These racial/ethnic differences persist after adjustment for socioeconomic and health-behavior covariates in many analyses, suggesting biologic contributions.

Adnexal endometriomas and endometriosis
Endometriosis is less ubiquitous than leiomyomas when measured by imaging or surgery, but still common among women of reproductive age. Prevalence estimates in unselected reproductive-aged populations are often cited around 5–10%, with many reviews proposing an overall prevalence near 10%. Among specific clinical populations the prevalence is much higher: up to 20–50% among women with chronic pelvic pain and 20–50% (often higher in surgical cohorts) among women with infertility. Rates differ further by age: endometriosis typically presents in adolescence or early adulthood and is concentrated in reproductive years; it usually regresses after menopause when ovarian estrogen production falls.

Adnexal endometriomas—ovarian cysts formed by endometriotic implants within the ovary—constitute a subset of endometriosis cases. In surgical series of women with proven endometriosis, endometriomas are reported in roughly 17–44% depending on cohort composition. On imaging (TVUS), an ovarian endometrioma may be suggested by a unilocular “ground-glass” cyst; however, many women with endometriosis lack endometriomas, and small endometriotic implants may not be seen on imaging.

Ethnic patterns for endometriosis are complex. Historically, diagnosis of endometriosis was reported more often in White, higher socioeconomic status, and infertile populations—likely reflecting referral and detection bias. In contemporary, diverse population-based studies, endometriosis occurs across racial and ethnic groups; some data suggest modest differences in prevalence, but these are smaller and less consistent than for leiomyomas. Underdiagnosis in marginalized groups remains a concern.

# Known and proposed risk factors (genetic, hormonal, environmental, BMI)

Both leiomyomas and endometriosis are multifactorial diseases. Genetic predisposition, hormonal milieu, reproductive history, somatic mutations (in fibroids), and environmental exposures all contribute to risk. Many associations are plausible mechanistically and supported by epidemiologic data; others remain hypotheses.

Genetic factors
Leiomyomas: Familial aggregation is well-recognized. Women with a first-degree relative with fibroids have higher risk. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified multiple susceptibility loci linked to leiomyoma development, implicating pathways in steroid hormone signaling, extracellular matrix regulation, and cell proliferation. Additionally, somatic mutations in MED12 are present in a high proportion (approximately 40–70%) of uterine leiomyomas, highlighting tumor-specific genetic events that drive clonal growth.

Endometriosis: There is also familial aggregation and increased risk among first-degree relatives. GWAS have identified susceptibility loci associated with endometriosis risk, with implicated pathways including sex-steroid signaling, inflammation, and cell adhesion. Endometriosis is polygenic with modest effect sizes per allele. Genetic predisposition is one component of risk but interacts with environmental and reproductive factors.

Hormonal factors
Both conditions are estrogen-dependent to an extent, but the hormonal dynamics and clinical consequences differ.

Leiomyomas: Estrogen and progesterone promote fibroid growth. Fibroids are rare before menarche and tend to grow during reproductive years, often shrinking after menopause. Exogenous estrogen exposure (e.g., hormone replacement without progestin) can stimulate growth; the role of combined hormonal contraceptives is less clear, with some evidence that low-dose combined oral contraceptives may control symptoms rather than increase fibroid risk. Hyperestrogenic states—driven by peripheral conversion in adipose tissue, higher circulating estrogens, or altered metabolism—can contribute.

Endometriosis: Endometriotic implants rely on estrogen for proliferation and cause local aromatase-driven estrogen production; they also demonstrate progesterone resistance. Early menarche and shorter menstrual cycles (greater lifetime menstrual exposure) are associated with higher endometriosis risk, consistent with retrograde menstruation hypotheses and cumulative estrogen exposure.

Environmental exposures
Leiomyomas: Epidemiologic data suggest associations between fibroids and environmental factors such as low vitamin D levels and exposure to endocrine-disrupting chemicals (phthalates, bisphenol A) in some studies. Hypertension and metabolic factors have been associated with increased risk. Occupational or dietary exposures have variable evidence.

Endometriosis: Smoking is inversely associated with endometriosis in some studies (potentially mediated through anti-estrogenic effects), but smoking has many harmful effects and is not protective clinically. Exposure to environmental toxins—dioxins and other endocrine disruptors—has been hypothesized as a risk factor; data in humans are inconsistent but animal studies suggest plausible mechanisms.

Body mass index (BMI)
The relationship between BMI and these diseases differs.

Leiomyomas: Higher BMI is generally associated with increased risk of fibroids, though the relationship is not strictly linear in all studies. Adiposity contributes to peripheral estrogen production via aromatization, which may increase fibroid growth. However, some studies report attenuation of association after adjusting for detection bias (e.g., imaging sensitivity).

Endometriosis: Many epidemiologic studies report an inverse association between BMI and endometriosis—women with lower BMI have higher risk. This relationship is not fully understood; it may reflect hormonal milieu, differences in menstrual characteristics, or detection bias (healthcare-seeking behavior). BMI is a marker that should be integrated with other clinical features rather than used alone.

Other medical comorbidities
Leiomyomas: Hypertension and certain metabolic comorbidities, including insulin resistance and dyslipidemia, have been linked to increased fibroid risk in some cohorts.

Endometriosis: Autoimmune diseases and allergy phenotypes have shown variable associations in observational studies, potentially reflecting shared inflammatory pathways.

# Impact of parity and fertility history on disease occurrence

Reproductive history profoundly affects risk for both conditions through mechanisms linked to hormonal exposure, menstrual cycles, and structural changes to the uterus and pelvis.

Parity and leiomyomas
Parity is generally protective against the development of fibroids. Higher parity and increasing number of full-term pregnancies are associated with lower fibroid risk. The protective effect is thought to arise from several mechanisms: the high progesterone state of pregnancy may modulate fibroid growth, postpartum uterine involution may cause regression of small fibroids, and pregnancies interrupt ovulatory cycles thus reducing cumulative exposure to cyclic ovarian hormones. Timing matters: earlier first full-term pregnancy appears more protective. The degree of protection varies by study but is clinically significant when estimating fibroid probability.

Parity and endometriosis
Nulliparity is a risk factor for endometriosis; conversely, women with endometriosis frequently have reduced parity due to subfertility. Whether nulliparity is a cause or consequence is bidirectional: endometriosis can impair fertility through distorted pelvic anatomy, inflammation, and altered endometrial receptivity, while lower parity (and therefore fewer pregnancies) increases lifetime menstrual exposure and may promote disease initiation or progression. Women with a history of infertility—particularly unexplained infertility—have higher prevalence of endometriosis on laparoscopy.

Fertility treatment history
Assisted reproductive technologies and hormonal ovulation induction do not appear to increase the underlying risk of endometriosis (which usually precedes fertility treatments), but they can unmask previously undiagnosed disease as women undergo more intensive evaluation. For fibroids, size and location (submucosal or large intramural) are important determinants of fertility outcomes and may influence decisions regarding myomectomy before infertility treatment.

# Patterns of disease presentation in reproductive-aged vs older women

Temporal patterns of symptomatology and clinical features change across reproductive life stages for both disorders.

Uterine leiomyomas
Reproductive-aged women: Fibroids most commonly present in women in their 30s and 40s. Typical complaints include heavy menstrual bleeding (menorrhagia), prolonged or irregular menses, pelvic pressure or fullness, urinary frequency from bladder compression, constipation from rectal compression, dyspareunia, and infertility or recurrent pregnancy loss if fibroids distort the uterine cavity. Acute pelvic pain can occur with red degeneration in pregnancy or torsion of a pedunculated subserosal fibroid, but this is less common.

Perimenopausal and postmenopausal women: Fibroids tend to regress after menopause due to decreased ovarian estrogen production. New or rapidly enlarging fibroids in a postmenopausal woman are uncommon and raise concern for malignancy (e.g., leiomyosarcoma) or hormone exposure (e.g., unopposed estrogen therapy). Management priorities shift toward symptomatic control and evaluation for alternative diagnoses.

Adnexal endometriomas and endometriosis
Reproductive-aged women: Endometriosis is classically associated with dysmenorrhea (pain with menses), chronic pelvic pain (often cyclical), dyspareunia (particularly deep dyspareunia), and infertility. Symptoms often begin in adolescence or early adulthood; progressive pain and worsening dysmenorrhea are common presenting features. On pelvic examination, fixed retroverted uterus or adnexal tenderness may be present. Endometriomas may produce a palpable adnexal mass if large.

Older women: Endometriosis generally becomes less active after menopause, with symptomatic improvement for many, as estrogen levels decline. However, some postmenopausal women—especially those on estrogen replacement without progestin or with exogenous estrogen—may have persistent or recurrent disease. New-onset endometriosis in postmenopausal women is rare and warrants evaluation for estrogen exposure or malignant transformation of an endometrioma, although malignant transformation is uncommon.

Overlapping presentations and differential diagnosis
Both conditions can present with pelvic or abdominal pain and can coexist in the same patient. Distinguishing features: heavy menstrual bleeding and bulk symptoms favor fibroids; cyclical pelvic pain, dysmenorrhea, and infertility favor endometriosis. Imaging helps segregate the differential—uterine fibroids are readily detected by transvaginal ultrasound, whereas small peritoneal implants may be occult on imaging. Clinical judgment must integrate age, symptom pattern, parity, and ethnic background.

# Implications of epidemiology for pretest probability in differential diagnosis

Epidemiologic data should inform the clinician’s prior (pretest) probability when evaluating symptoms. Bayesian reasoning helps determine how much weight to place on history, physical exam, and diagnostic tests.

Using age and reproductive stage
For a reproductive-aged woman with heavy menstrual bleeding and pelvic mass, fibroids are common and should be high on the differential—particularly in Black women and those in their 30s–40s. In contrast, a young adolescent girl with progressive dysmenorrhea and cyclic pelvic pain is more likely to have endometriosis than fibroids. In postmenopausal patients, the pretest probability for new or symptomatic fibroids or active endometriosis is low, and alternative diagnoses (including malignancy for masses) must be considered.

Accounting for ethnicity and family history
For Black women presenting with abnormal uterine bleeding or pelvic pressure, the pretest probability for fibroids is substantially higher than for women of other ethnicities; this should lower the threshold for uterine imaging (transvaginal ultrasound) as a first-line test. A positive family history increases prior probability for both conditions and can justify earlier or more aggressive diagnostic evaluation.

Incorporating parity and fertility history
Nulliparous women with infertility and pelvic pain have a higher pretest probability for endometriosis; if infertility is the dominant issue, laparoscopy or detailed pelvic imaging for endometriomas and pelvic adhesions may be prioritized. Conversely, multiparous women with new bulk symptoms are less likely to have disease onset related to endometriosis, and fibroids may be the leading consideration.

Interpreting diagnostic tests in context
Imaging and laboratory tests must be interpreted against the background pretest probability. For example, a transvaginal ultrasound that identifies a submucosal fibroid in a woman with heavy bleeding and consistent demographic risk factors usually confirms the clinical diagnosis. However, a negative ultrasound does not exclude endometriosis because many implants are not visible; in a patient with high clinical suspicion (dysmenorrhea, dyspareunia, infertility), a normal ultrasound should not be used to rule out endometriosis. Surgical visualization (laparoscopy) remains the diagnostic gold standard for peritoneal endometriosis, while MRI may have greater sensitivity for deep infiltrating disease and complex adnexal endometriomas.

Quantifying pretest probabilities in practice
While precise numeric pretest probabilities vary by setting, clinicians can apply epidemiologic ranges to inform decision thresholds. Examples:

- In an asymptomatic reproductive-aged woman, the chance of occult fibroids on imaging is substantial (tens of percent), but the probability of clinically significant fibroids is lower (~20–30% lifetime clinical significance).
- In a woman with chronic pelvic pain and infertility, the probability of endometriosis may be as high as 20–50% and even higher in surgical cohorts; the presence of an ovarian "ground-glass" cyst on ultrasound increases the likelihood of endometrioma substantially.
- A Black woman in her 40s with heavy menstrual bleeding has a high pretest probability for fibroids; imaging is indicated early in evaluation.

Clinical decision points based on epidemiology
Epidemiology guides when to pursue which tests and referrals:

- Transvaginal ultrasound should be first-line imaging for suspected fibroids in symptomatic women because of high prevalence and good sensitivity for uterine lesions.
- In women with suspected endometriosis who have infertility, persistent cyclical pain, or complex adnexal masses, early gynecologic referral and consideration of laparoscopy or pelvic MRI are warranted given the higher pretest probability and potential impact on fertility management.
- In populations with higher baseline risk (e.g., Black women for fibroids), clinicians should proactively discuss symptom surveillance and earlier intervention when symptoms emerge.

Conclusion

Epidemiologic patterns for uterine leiomyomas and endometriosis—age distribution, ethnicity, parity effects, and known risk factors—provide a framework for clinical reasoning. Both diseases are common in reproductive-aged women but differ in typical age of onset, symptom clusters, and associations with demographic and reproductive factors. Integrating these population-level data with individual history, examination, and targeted testing refines pretest probabilities, helps prioritize appropriate diagnostics, and guides counseling about likely etiologies and implications for fertility and long-term management.
