Introduction

Pregnancy induces profound anatomic and physiologic changes in the female pelvis and abdomen that alter normal relationships among pelvic organs, neurovascular structures, peritoneal reflections, and potential spaces. Understanding these changes is essential for accurate localization and characterization of pelvic and abdominal masses in pregnant patients, for planning imaging and surgical approaches, and for avoiding iatrogenic injury during obstetric and gynecologic procedures. This chapter reviews baseline pelvic anatomy, the uterine segments and myometrial architecture, adnexal and parametrial anatomy, peritoneal spaces relevant to fluid and mass propagation, arterial and venous vascular supply, patterns of organ displacement across gestation, operative access considerations (laparotomy versus laparoscopy and incision selection), and relationships that are critical for obstetric delivery.

# Normal pelvic anatomy: uterus, ovaries, fallopian tubes, broad and round ligaments

The female pelvis contains the reproductive organs centrally: the uterus, fallopian tubes, and ovaries. The uterus is a thick-walled, muscular, midline organ normally anteverted and anteflexed over the urinary bladder. In the nonpregnant state the uterus is pear-shaped, approximately 7–8 cm long, 4–5 cm wide, and 2–3 cm thick; its cavity is a potential space lined by endometrium. The fallopian tubes (uterine tubes) extend laterally from the uterine cornua; each tube is approximately 10–12 cm and has several anatomic segments (interstitial/isthmic, ampullary, infundibular, fimbrial). The ovaries are almond-shaped, located in the ovarian fossa on the lateral pelvic wall, suspended by the utero-ovarian ligament medially and the suspensory ligament (infundibulopelvic ligament) laterally. The ovaries are the source of oocytes and major producers of estrogen and progesterone.

Peritoneal reflections create the broad ligament, a double fold of peritoneum draping over and supporting the uterus, fallopian tube, and ovaries from the lateral pelvic walls. The broad ligament extends from the lateral uterine margins to the pelvic sidewalls and is divided conceptually into the mesosalpinx (overlying the tube), mesovarium (supporting the ovary), and mesometrium (supporting the uterus). Within the folds of the broad ligament run important structures, including the uterine vessels in the base (cardinal ligament region) and lymphatics.

The round ligaments are paired fibromuscular bands arising at the uterine horns, coursing laterally and anteriorly through the inguinal canal to insert in the labia majora. They contain small vessels and lymphatics and are important in maintaining anteversion of the uterus. During pregnancy, they frequently become stretched and can cause characteristic round ligament pain.

Understanding the spatial relations of these structures—uterus centrally, tubes and ovaries laterally, broad ligament draping these elements, and ligamental attachments anchoring the uterus—forms the foundation for predicting displacement of adnexal masses and interpreting cross-sectional imaging.

# Uterine segments (fundus, corpus, isthmus, cervix) and myometrial architecture

The uterus can be anatomically and functionally divided into the fundus, corpus (body), isthmus (including the lower uterine segment in pregnancy), and cervix. The fundus is the dome-shaped superior aspect, bounded laterally by the tubal ostia; it is the most contractile region during labor. The corpus comprises the main body of the uterus and includes the endometrial cavity. The isthmus is a narrow region distal to the corpus, and during pregnancy it becomes the lower uterine segment—thin-walled and distensible—critical for vaginal delivery and for intrapartum surgical decisions (e.g., low transverse cesarean incision). The cervix is the fibromuscular lower portion that projects into the vagina; it undergoes remodeling, effacement, and dilation during labor.

The myometrium consists of interlacing smooth muscle fibers arranged in inner, middle, and outer layers, with distinct orientations. The innermost layer (subendometrial or stratum subvasculare) is more cellular and has a rich vascular plexus supporting the endometrium. The middle layer is thickest and contains circular and longitudinal muscle bundles responsible for the powerful contractions of labor. The outer layer is contiguous with the broad ligament and serosa. This layered architecture explains regional differences in contractility and healing: the fundus and corpus have more muscle mass and contractile force; the lower uterine segment develops predominantly by thinning and stretching of the isthmus and lower corpus rather than true muscular growth.

Clinically, myometrial thickness and integrity determine risks and approaches in surgery. The lower uterine segment in labor becomes thin (a few millimeters), and this thinness is the reason low transverse incisions at the lower segment have less contractile violent disruption and lower risk of uterine rupture in subsequent pregnancies than classical (vertical corporal) incisions that transect the thicker, contractile myometrium.

# Adnexal anatomy and parametrium

The adnexa include the fallopian tubes, ovaries, and associated connective tissues and ligaments. The parametrium is the connective tissue lateral to the uterus within the broad ligament that contains the uterine vessels, lymphatics, nerves, and connective tissue; the cardinal (Mackenrodt’s) ligament is the major component providing lateral support and housing the uterine artery in its base. The utero-ovarian ligament (also called the ovarian ligament) connects the ovary to the uterus at the uterine cornu; the suspensory ligament (infundibulopelvic ligament) contains the ovarian vessels as they course to the lateral pelvic wall.

Adnexal masses (follicular cysts, ovarian neoplasms, ectopic pregnancies) are anatomically constrained by these ligaments and peritoneal folds. For example, an ovarian mass typically displaces the uterus medially and can extend into the pelvis or abdomen depending on size. The broad ligament forms a pouch that can harbor cysts; a mass within the broad ligament may mimic ovarian origin. The parametrium's vascular and neural content implies that disease processes here may present with pelvic pain, bleeding, or neurologic symptoms and that surgical dissection risks major bleeding from the uterine artery and injury to the ureter, which courses under the uterine artery ("water under the bridge").

Important anatomic relations to remember: the ureter passes medially and inferiorly to the ovarian vessels and then courses under the uterine artery approximately 1.5–2 cm lateral to the cervix before entering the bladder. During pelvic surgery, particularly hysterectomy or pelvic lymphadenectomy, the ureter is at highest risk where it is close to the uterine artery at the cardinal ligament.

# Peritoneal spaces: pouch of Douglas, subphrenic spaces, paracolic gutters

The peritoneal cavity is divided into recesses and gutters that direct fluid, pus, blood, and neoplastic spread. In the pelvis the most important dependent space in the supine and upright patient is the rectouterine pouch (pouch of Douglas), situated between the posterior uterus and anterior rectum. It is the lowest point of the peritoneal cavity in the upright position and often the site of free fluid accumulation (hemoperitoneum, pelvic inflammatory exudate, or ascites). Clinically, culdocentesis (historical) and transvaginal ultrasound with culdocentesis supplement detect fluid in this pouch; it is also the target of posterior colpotomy in some surgical approaches.

The vesicouterine pouch sits anteriorly between the bladder and uterus. In pregnancy the vesicouterine pouch is displaced and often obliterated as the gravid uterus expands. The paravesical spaces lie lateral to the bladder and are relevant in pelvic lymphadenectomy and diversional procedures.

The subphrenic spaces (right and left) are suprahepatic recesses between the diaphragm and liver or spleen; right subphrenic space drains into the right paracolic gutter. The paracolic gutters are grooves lateral to the ascending and descending colon; the right paracolic gutter is larger and more continuous with the pelvic cavity than the left, creating a route for peritoneal fluid to travel from the pelvis to the upper abdomen and vice versa. This explains clinical patterns such as the preferential spread of pelvic infections or malignant cells to the right subphrenic space and right hemidiaphragm. In pregnancy, supine or recumbent positions and the enlarged uterus alter these dynamics, but the fundamental pathways remain important for interpreting imaging and anticipating the spread of intraperitoneal processes.

# Vascular supply: uterine, ovarian, and round ligament arteries; venous plexuses

Arterial supply to the uterus is mainly via the uterine arteries, branches of the internal iliac (hypogastric) arteries. Each uterine artery runs in the base of the broad ligament within the cardinal ligament toward the lateral uterine surface, then ascends along the uterus in the broad ligament and gives ascending branches to the body and fundus that anastomose with ovarian arteries. The ovarian (testicular homolog) arteries arise directly from the abdominal aorta (typically at the level of L2) and supply the ovaries and upper tube; they also contribute to the uterine fundus via tubal branches. The round ligament artery typically arises from the inferior epigastric or uterine artery and supplies the round ligament and adjacent tissues. During pregnancy, uterine blood flow increases dramatically—from approximately 50 mL/min in the nonpregnant state to as much as 500–800 mL/min at term—principally through hypertrophy and increased flow in the uterine and ovarian arteries.

Venous drainage parallels arterial supply and includes the uterovaginal plexus and ovarian veins. The uterine venous plexus drains to the internal iliac veins. The ovarian veins drain to the left renal vein (left ovarian vein) or directly to the inferior vena cava (right ovarian vein), accounting for asymmetry in conditions like ovarian vein thrombosis. The pelvic venous plexuses are valveless and communicate widely; in pregnancy they become engorged due to increased flow and partially due to compression of pelvic veins by the gravid uterus, predisposing to varicosities and hemorrhoids.

Clinically, these vascular patterns explain bleeding risks during adnexal surgery and cesarean delivery. Identification of the uterine artery within the cardinal ligament is crucial in hysterectomy to control bleeding; ligation or coagulation here must respect the nearby ureter. Knowledge that ovarian arterial supply arises from the aorta explains why ligation of the uterine artery alone may not devascularize the adnexa.

# Changes in organ position with uterine enlargement across gestation

The uterus undergoes predictable size and positional changes across gestation. At about 12 weeks’ gestation the uterine fundus rises above the pelvic brim and becomes palpable in the lower abdomen; by 20 weeks it reaches the level of the umbilicus; by term (≈36–40 weeks) it approaches the xiphoid process. These vertical changes displace surrounding organs: the bladder is elevated and compressed anteriorly and inferiorly; the intestines are swept upward and laterally into the upper abdomen; the diaphragm is elevated and functional residual capacity decreases. Adnexal organs (ovaries and fallopian tubes) are displaced laterally and cephalad, often to the parauterine or even abdominal locations depending on uterine enlargement.

The lower uterine segment forms as the isthmus stretches and thins beginning in the second trimester, creating the region between the contractile corpus and the cervix. The ureters, which in the nonpregnant state run close to the lateral fornices, may be displaced laterally by the growing uterus, and urinary stasis from ureteral compression (especially on the right) contributes to hydronephrosis of pregnancy. The appendix is displaced upward and laterally as the uterus enlarges, so appendicitis in pregnancy may present with higher and more lateral pain than in nonpregnant patients.

Understanding these shifts is vital for imaging interpretation: an adnexal mass identified in the upper abdomen may still be of ovarian origin in later pregnancy. Similarly, surgical planning must account for uterine size when predicting access routes and anticipating displaced structures such as the ureters, bladder, bowel, and major vessels.

# Anatomic considerations for surgical access (laparotomy vs laparoscopy) and incision choices

Choice of surgical approach in pregnant patients requires balancing maternal and fetal safety, gestational age, uterine size, pathology, and surgeon experience. Laparoscopy is feasible and increasingly used in pregnancy, particularly in the first and early second trimesters, for selected indications (e.g., adnexal torsion, appendicitis, some ovarian cysts). Advantages include reduced wound morbidity, less postoperative pain, and shorter hospital stay. Limitations include decreased working space as the uterus enlarges, risk of uterine injury during trocar placement, physiologic effects of pneumoperitoneum (e.g., increased intra-abdominal pressure, CO2 absorption, potential decreased venous return), and difficulty in controlling hemorrhage for large masses. Insufflation pressures are typically kept lower (e.g., 10–12 mmHg) than nonpregnant cases; patient positioning avoids aortocaval compression (left lateral tilt).

Open laparotomy remains indicated for large pelvic masses, advanced gestational age with little abdominal working space, suspected malignancy requiring staging, or cases where rapid conversion is anticipated. Incision choice depends on exposure needs and gestational age. A midline vertical incision provides excellent exposure of the upper and lower abdomen and is preferred when access to the upper abdomen is needed or when rapid entry is required; it allows extension superiorly and inferiorly and avoids transecting multiple muscle layers. A Pfannenstiel (low transverse) incision offers better cosmetic results and less postoperative pain but limited exposure for large masses or high fundal height; it is commonly used for cesarean delivery and lower pelvic operations.

When operating in pregnancy, the uterine fundal height guides incision planning. For masses confined to the pelvis at earlier gestations, a lower transverse incision may suffice. For suspected malignancy or masses extending into the upper abdomen, a midline incision is preferred. Additionally, the presence of an enlarged uterus shifts the peritoneal cavity upward: placement of abdominal wall incisions and trocars must account for uterine position to avoid injury. In laparoscopy, initial access techniques vary—open (Hasson) technique or optical trocars placed away from midline (e.g., Palmer’s point in the left upper quadrant) can reduce uterine trauma, especially after 12 weeks’ gestation when the fundus is above the umbilicus.

Key clinical decision points when choosing approach include gestational age and uterine size, evidence of malignancy, size and location of the mass, hemodynamic stability, and surgeon expertise. For example, laparoscopy is generally avoided after the early second trimester for large masses due to limited working space and increased uterine size, although experienced teams may still perform minimally invasive surgery with adaptations.

# Relationships relevant to obstetric delivery (lower uterine segment, internal os, pelvic outlet)

Obstetric delivery depends on the coordinated function and structural relations of the lower uterine segment, cervix (internal and external os), maternal pelvis, and fetal lie/presentation. The lower uterine segment forms in the third month of pregnancy as the isthmus stretches and thins into a distensible region that accommodates fetal descent. Its reduced contractility compared with the fundus allows it to provide a controlled passageway during labor. The internal os is the proximal opening of the cervical canal into the uterine cavity; cervical dilation occurs from the internal os outward during labor. Effective labor requires coordinated dilation of the internal os and effacement of the cervix.

Pelvic dimensions determine the ability of the fetal head to negotiate the birth canal. The pelvic inlet, midpelvis, and outlet contain key measurements used in clinical assessment. The pelvic outlet is bounded by the pubic symphysis anteriorly, ischial tuberosities laterally, and the coccyx posteriorly. Soft tissue elements—perineal musculature and pelvic floor—also influence delivery mechanics. Fetal station is described relative to the maternal ischial spines; engagement and descent reflect the relationship of the fetal presenting part to the pelvic inlet and outlet.

Cesarean incision selection is informed by these relationships. A low transverse uterine incision placed in the lower uterine segment avoids the contractile corpus and minimizes risk of uterine rupture in subsequent labor. Conversely, a classical vertical incision through the contractile upper segment is sometimes necessary for preterm or malpositioned fetuses or when access is limited but carries increased risk in future pregnancies due to higher chance of rupture. Recognition of the location of the internal os and the dynamic capacity of the lower uterine segment influences decisions such as trial of labor after cesarean, indications for cesarean delivery, and the safest incision type.

Understanding the interaction between fetal size, presentation, pelvic anatomy, and the dynamic remodeling of the lower uterine segment and cervix enables rational clinical decisions during labor and surgical delivery, including assessment of cephalopelvic disproportion, the timing of operative intervention, and risk counseling regarding future pregnancies.

# Integration for imaging and surgical planning

A working integration of the anatomic concepts above is required when interpreting imaging and planning intervention in pregnancy. Sonographers and radiologists must localize adnexal or pelvic masses relative to the gravid uterus, trace vascular pedicles (ovarian versus uterine) to identify origin, evaluate for free fluid in dependent peritoneal spaces such as the pouch of Douglas, and assess displacement of bowel and upper abdominal organs. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is safe in pregnancy without gadolinium and is particularly helpful when sonography is inconclusive; MR cross-sectional anatomy must be read with the understanding that organs are displaced cephalad and laterally by the gravid uterus.

Surgeons must anticipate vascular engorgement of uterine and pelvic veins, displaced ureters, and the variable location of the ovaries. Preoperative planning should include discussion of optimal approach (laparoscopy vs laparotomy), incision placement relative to fundal height, trocar positioning if laparoscopy is chosen, strategies to minimize uterine manipulation, and plans to rapidly control hemorrhage with uterine or ovarian vessel ligation. Knowledge of the anatomic sites where ureter and uterine artery are in close relation should inform dissection technique. Finally, fetal monitoring considerations and obstetric team availability are essential when performing nonobstetric pelvic surgery in pregnancy.

Reference ranges and clinical thresholds

Although much of pelvic anatomy is structural, some physiologic reference values and clinical thresholds are useful in peripartum management. Normal hemoglobin in pregnancy varies by trimester: first trimester approximately 11.6–14.8 g/dL, second trimester down to 9.7–14.8 g/dL due to hemodilution, and third trimester 9.5–15.0 g/dL (values can vary by laboratory; anemia in pregnancy is often defined as hemoglobin <11 g/dL in the first and third trimesters and <10.5 g/dL in the second trimester). White blood cell count increases normally in pregnancy and especially during labor; values up to 15,000–20,000/mm^3 can be physiologic. These ranges inform perioperative transfusion thresholds and diagnosis of infection.

Key clinical decision points include the optimal timing of elective surgery (ideally deferred to second trimester when feasible), criteria for choosing laparotomy versus laparoscopy (gestational age and uterine size, suspected malignancy, size/location of mass), and incision choice (midline vertical for maximal exposure or Pfannenstiel for limited pelvic procedures). For cesarean delivery, choice of uterine incision balances immediate exposure and future uterine integrity; low transverse is standard when feasible; classical should be reserved for specific indications.

Summary

A thorough understanding of pelvic and abdominal anatomy, the structural changes of the gravid uterus, vascular relationships, and peritoneal pathways is essential for safe and effective evaluation and management of pelvic and abdominal masses in pregnancy. Integration of these anatomic principles with imaging findings, gestational considerations, and operative planning optimizes maternal and fetal outcomes and minimizes iatrogenic injury during diagnostic and therapeutic interventions.

Chapter 2: Physiologic Changes of Pregnancy Relevant to Diagnosis and Management

Introduction

Pregnancy is a state of profound physiologic adaptation in virtually every organ system. These changes are necessary to support fetal growth and prepare the mother for labor and delivery, but they also alter the presentation of disease, confound interpretation of laboratory and imaging studies, and modify perioperative and critical care risk. A sound understanding of these adaptations is essential for safe clinical reasoning: what is “normal” for a pregnant woman often differs markedly from the nonpregnant baseline, and apparent deviations from nonpregnant reference ranges may reflect physiology rather than pathology. This chapter reviews the key systemic changes relevant to diagnosis and management, links the underlying pathophysiology to clinical signs and test results, and highlights common decision points where pregnancy-modified norms must inform care.

# Cardiovascular changes: increased blood volume, cardiac output, heart rate, blood pressure alterations

Cardiac output, blood volume, and systemic vascular resistance are among the most striking cardiovascular changes of pregnancy. Blood volume increases progressively beginning in the first trimester and peaks in the second to third trimester. Total blood volume typically rises by approximately 30% to 50%; plasma volume increases more than red cell mass, producing a relative haemodilution (see hematologic section). The rise in blood volume is mediated by pregnancy hormones (notably progesterone and estrogen), activation of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system, increased plasma proteins that alter oncotic pressure, and sodium and water retention.

Cardiac output increases in parallel with blood volume. By midpregnancy, cardiac output is typically 30% to 50% above nonpregnant levels, with the maximal increase evident by 20 to 24 weeks’ gestation and sustained through delivery. This rise reflects increases in both stroke volume and heart rate. Stroke volume increases early in gestation, and heart rate rises by about 10 to 20 beats per minute on average. During labor and immediately postpartum, transient further increases in cardiac output occur (autotransfusion from contracting uterus and mobilization of interstitial fluid). Clinically, these changes account for higher resting heart rates and may challenge interpretation of tachycardia; modest tachycardia may be physiologic whereas higher rates raise concern for hemorrhage, sepsis, or cardiomyopathy.

Systemic vascular resistance decreases due to vasodilatory effects of progesterone, nitric oxide production, and formation of the low-resistance uteroplacental circulation. As a result, blood pressure commonly falls in early pregnancy, reaching a nadir in the mid-second trimester (usually around 20 weeks). Systolic blood pressure is often unchanged or mildly reduced; diastolic pressure decreases more noticeably, typically by 5 to 15 mmHg. Mean arterial pressure may be slightly lower than baseline. After midpregnancy, vascular resistance rises toward term and blood pressure typically returns toward prepregnancy levels. Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (gestational hypertension, preeclampsia) represent important pathologic deviations from this expected course and require vigilance, but baseline expectations of lower midpregnancy blood pressures should be remembered when interpreting outpatient readings.

Physiologic implications and clinical reasoning: The combination of increased blood volume and decreased systemic vascular resistance means that pregnant women tolerate blood loss differently than nonpregnant adults; however, because of the haemodilutional anemia described below, signs of hypovolemia (tachycardia, orthostatic changes) may appear later and be less specific. Supine hypotension can result from uterine compression of the inferior vena cava (IVC) in late pregnancy; patients may present with lightheadedness or hypotension when supine, mitigated by left lateral tilt. Cardiac murmurs, mild peripheral edema, and splitting of heart sounds may be benign. New or severe dyspnea, persistent tachycardia, syncope, or chest pain warrant evaluation for pathological causes including cardiomyopathy, pulmonary embolism, or myocardial ischemia.

# Respiratory changes: increased oxygen consumption, diaphragmatic elevation, tidal volume changes

Pregnancy imposes increased metabolic demand, reflected in respiratory physiology. Maternal oxygen consumption rises by approximately 20% to 30% above baseline, driven by increased basal metabolic rate and the oxygen needs of the uterus and fetus. To meet this demand, ventilation increases primarily through an increase in tidal volume rather than respiratory rate. Tidal volume typically increases by about 30% to 40%, producing an increase in minute ventilation of roughly 30% to 50%. Resting respiratory rate may be unchanged or modestly increased.

A key laboratory consequence is a chronic respiratory alkalosis: with increased alveolar ventilation, arterial carbon dioxide tension (PaCO2) falls to a new steady-state of about $28$ to $32$ mmHg (normal nonpregnant PaCO2 ≈ $40$ mmHg). The reduced PaCO2 shifts the maternal bicarbonate equilibrium; renal bicarbonate excretion compensates so that serum bicarbonate typically decreases to around $18$ to $21$ mEq/L. Maternal arterial oxygen tension (PaO2) is often slightly higher than nonpregnant values (PaO2 ≈ $100$ to $105$ mmHg), but dyspnea is very commonly reported despite normal oxygenation and is frequently physiologic.

Mechanical displacement of the diaphragm occurs as the enlarging uterus elevates the diaphragm by several centimeters (average up to about 4 cm). Functional residual capacity (FRC) falls by approximately $10$ to $25\%$ by term because of decreased expiratory reserve volume and residual volume; however, inspiratory capacity may be preserved or slightly increased due to higher tidal volumes. The net effect is decreased pulmonary reserve: pregnant women desaturate more rapidly during apnea or hypoventilation (for example during intubation), making airway management riskier in the intrapartum and perioperative periods.

Clinical implications: Shortness of breath is common and often physiologic in pregnancy due to increased ventilatory drive and altered perception. However, clinicians must distinguish benign dyspnea from cardiopulmonary disease. Key red flags include hypoxemia (SpO2 < 95% at rest), pleuritic chest pain, unilateral leg swelling, or persistent tachypnea. The lower baseline PaCO2 affects interpretation of arterial blood gases: a PaCO2 of $40$ mmHg in pregnancy suggests hypoventilation or respiratory failure. Preoxygenation and rapid sequence induction are particularly important during anesthesia; preparations for rapid desaturation should be anticipated.

# Hematologic changes: physiologic anemia, leukocytosis, coagulation profile changes

Pregnancy is a hyperdynamic hematologic state. Plasma volume expands substantially (about 30% to 50%), while red blood cell mass increases by roughly 20% to 30% due to increased erythropoietin. Because plasma increases more than red cell mass, the net effect is a dilutional or “physiologic” anemia. Common benchmarks: the World Health Organization defines anemia in pregnancy as hemoglobin (Hgb) less than $11.0$ g/dL; many clinicians accept Hgb ≥ $11.0$ g/dL in the first and third trimesters and ≥ $10.5$ g/dL in the second trimester as normal. Typical hemoglobin values in pregnancy fall in the range of about $11$–$13$ g/dL; hematocrit commonly decreases to 32%–42% depending on gestational age. Iron requirements increase substantially, making pregnant women vulnerable to true iron-deficiency anemia; whereas mild reductions in Hgb may be physiologic, values below $10$ g/dL warrant evaluation and treatment.

Total leukocyte count increases during pregnancy, primarily due to neutrophilia. Normal leukocyte counts can rise to 12,000–15,000/µL or higher, particularly in the third trimester and during labor; counts up to 20,000/µL can sometimes be physiologic during labor or immediate postpartum. Lymphocyte and monocyte distributions shift, and an absolute neutrophilia reduces the specificity of leukocytosis for infection. Therefore, interpreting the white blood cell count requires clinical correlation.

Platelet counts may decline modestly by late pregnancy—a phenomenon called gestational thrombocytopenia—due to hemodilution and increased platelet consumption in the uteroplacental circulation. Most gestational thrombocytopenia is mild, with platelet counts remaining above 100,000/µL. Platelet counts below 100,000/µL raise concern for immune thrombocytopenic purpura, preeclampsia-associated thrombocytopenia, or other pathology and warrant further workup.

Coagulation changes produce a prothrombotic state. Plasma fibrinogen increases substantially (typical term values $400$–$650$ mg/dL). Several procoagulant factors (e.g., factors VII, VIII, X, von Willebrand factor) increase, while natural anticoagulants such as protein S decline; protein C activity is relatively preserved. D-dimer levels are commonly elevated throughout pregnancy, limiting their negative predictive value for venous thromboembolism (VTE) in pregnancy. The net balance is increased thrombotic risk: pregnant and puerperal women have higher incidences of deep venous thrombosis and pulmonary embolism compared with nonpregnant women. Conversely, bleeding risk is not uniformly reduced; obstetric hemorrhage remains a leading cause of maternal morbidity, and the hypercoagulable state coexists with situations (placental abruption, uterine atony) that predispose to hemorrhage.

Clinical implications: Recognize that modest anemia, leukocytosis, and thrombocytopenia may reflect normal pregnancy. Ferritin, iron studies, and peripheral smear are useful to distinguish dilutional versus iron-deficiency anemia. For thrombocytopenia, platelet counts below $100{,}000$/µL merit expedited evaluation; counts below $50{,}000$/µL increase bleeding risk for regional anesthesia and operative delivery. Elevated fibrinogen can mask early disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) because fibrinogen remains high until late; conversely, a falling fibrinogen in a pregnant patient is an ominous sign. Clinical thresholds for transfusion are individualized: in stable nonbleeding patients, transfusion is usually not indicated until Hgb is < $7$ g/dL, but in obstetric patients clinical context, ongoing bleeding, and fetal considerations may prompt different decisions (many obstetricians consider transfusion at higher Hgb thresholds when symptomatic or hemodynamically unstable).

# Renal and urinary changes affecting infection risk and laboratory interpretation

Renal physiology adapts markedly during pregnancy. Renal blood flow increases by approximately 50% and glomerular filtration rate (GFR) rises by about 40% to 60%, beginning early in pregnancy and peaking in the second trimester. The augmented GFR lowers serum creatinine and urea concentrations: typical pregnancy creatinine values fall to roughly $0.4$–$0.8$ mg/dL (nonpregnant reference ≈ $0.6$–$1.1$ mg/dL), and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) is similarly decreased. These lower baselines mean that a creatinine of $1.0$ mg/dL in a pregnant patient, though within normal nonpregnant limits, represents renal impairment and may signify acute kidney injury or preeclampsia-related renal dysfunction.

Physiologic dilatation of the renal pelvis and ureters occurs due to progesterone-mediated smooth muscle relaxation and mechanical compression from the enlarging uterus; ureteral dilation is more pronounced on the right. Ureteral stasis combined with glycosuria—because increased GFR and decreased renal tubular reabsorption can produce glucosuria even with normal blood glucose—elevates the risk of urinary tract infections (UTIs) and pyelonephritis. Asymptomatic bacteriuria is more common and has important implications: untreated asymptomatic bacteriuria in pregnancy increases the risk of pyelonephritis and preterm birth. Routine screening for asymptomatic bacteriuria is recommended in pregnancy, and positive cultures are treated even if asymptomatic.

Urinary frequency is a common symptom that reflects reduced bladder capacity and increased urinary output; however, symptoms such as dysuria, fever, flank pain, or systemic illness should prompt evaluation for infection. Interpretation of urinalysis is modified by physiologic changes: trace proteinuria can occur with gestation, particularly after exercise or in late pregnancy, but persistent proteinuria (e.g., > $300$ mg/24 hours) is abnormal and is a diagnostic criterion for preeclampsia when associated with hypertension. The urinary sediment is often bland, and microscopic hematuria is not expected; frank hematuria or significant sediment requires investigation.

Clinical implications: Recognize lower creatinine and BUN baselines. Maintain a low threshold to investigate pyelonephritis and treat asymptomatic bacteriuria. Be cautious interpreting glycosuria; screen for gestational diabetes by specific testing rather than relying on urine glucose. When assessing renal function, small absolute creatinine rises can be clinically meaningful in pregnancy and warrant prompt evaluation.

# Immunologic and inflammatory modulation in pregnancy

Pregnancy represents a complex immunologic state that is often characterized simplistically as immunosuppression; the reality is nuanced immunomodulation. The maternal immune system must tolerate the semiallogeneic fetus while still protecting the mother and fetus from infection. This balance is achieved through shifts in innate and adaptive immunity, altered cytokine profiles, and changes at the maternal–fetal interface.

Adaptive immunity in pregnancy exhibits a shift from T helper type 1 (Th1)-dominant responses toward a Th2-biased profile during much of gestation. Th1 responses are generally proinflammatory and effective against intracellular pathogens; Th2 responses favor humoral immunity and antibody production. This shift can alter susceptibility to and severity of certain infections—some viral infections may be more severe, while humoral immune responses remain intact. Regulatory T cells (Tregs) expand during pregnancy and are critical for maintaining tolerance to fetal antigens.

Innate immune function is also modulated. Neutrophil counts and activity increase, but neutrophil function may be altered. Complement activity increases. NK (natural killer) cell populations in peripheral blood change in phenotype; uterine NK cells at the decidua are essential for placentation and spiral artery remodeling rather than cytotoxicity. Overall, pregnancy is characterized by a dynamic immune state that varies by gestational age and is neither uniformly suppressed nor uniformly activated.

Inflammation plays a role in the timing of labor: pregnancy can be considered to transit from an anti-inflammatory milieu in early gestation, to a relatively proinflammatory state at term to facilitate labor and delivery. This shifting baseline affects interpretation of inflammatory markers (e.g., C-reactive protein) and the clinical course of inflammatory conditions.

Clinical implications: Because leukocytosis can be physiologic and immune responses are altered, clinical judgment is essential when evaluating suspected infection. Some infections carry greater maternal risk (e.g., influenza, varicella, Listeria, certain fungal pathogens) and warrant aggressive prevention and treatment. Vaccination strategies in pregnancy (influenza, Tdap) exploit the preserved humoral response to protect both mother and neonate. When interpreting fever and inflammatory markers, remember the modified immune baseline and assess for concerning features such as persistent fever, hypotension, or organ dysfunction that indicate true infection.

# Mechanical effects of the gravid uterus on adjacent organs (diaphragm, bladder, bowel)

The enlarging uterus exerts mechanical effects that produce predictable anatomic and functional changes. By the second trimester, the uterus rises out of the pelvis; by term it may reach the right costal margin. This expansion displaces and compresses adjacent structures.

Diaphragmatic elevation is one major consequence. The diaphragm can be displaced upward by several centimeters, reducing chest wall compliance and decreasing functional residual capacity (FRC). Although inspiratory capacity may be maintained by increased tidal volume, the reduced FRC lowers oxygen reserve and contributes to faster desaturation during apnea or hypoventilation. Diaphragmatic elevation may also alter breath sounds on auscultation and complicate ultrasound windows for thoracoabdominal imaging.

Bladder compression reduces bladder capacity and increases urinary frequency. The enlarging uterus may also impair ureteral drainage, particularly on the right side, thereby increasing susceptibility to hydronephrosis and pyelonephritis. Mechanical pressure on pelvic veins contributes to lower-extremity venous stasis, increasing the risk of varicose veins and deep venous thrombosis. Uterine enlargement and hormonal effects (progesterone-mediated smooth muscle relaxation) slow gastrointestinal motility and relax the lower esophageal sphincter, promoting gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), heartburn, and constipation. Displacement of the intestines can also change the location and character of abdominal pain; for example, appendicitis often presents with a more cephalad and lateral pain distribution as pregnancy progresses.

Compression of the inferior vena cava (IVC) by the gravid uterus when a woman lies supine reduces venous return, preload, and cardiac output, producing supine hypotension syndrome. Symptoms include dizziness, nausea, pallor, or syncope; many women report relief by position change (left lateral decubitus). In labor and when performing procedures, clinicians should avoid prolonged supine positioning and use left lateral tilt to maintain venous return.

Clinical implications: Expect urinary frequency and constipation as common, often physiologic complaints, but maintain suspicion for infection or surgical abdominal pathology when symptoms are severe or atypical. Intra-abdominal inflammatory processes may present with atypical physical findings due to organ displacement. For procedural planning, recognize the importance of patient positioning to avoid IVC compression and anticipate altered ventilation mechanics when intubation or general anesthesia is necessary.

# Implications of physiologic changes for symptom interpretation (dyspnea, anemia, fever)

The interplay of cardiovascular, respiratory, hematologic, renal, immunologic, and mechanical adaptations in pregnancy modifies how common symptoms and laboratory abnormalities should be interpreted.

Dyspnea: Shortness of breath is frequently reported in otherwise healthy pregnant women and may arise from increased ventilatory drive, elevated diaphragm, and increased oxygen consumption. Characteristically, physiologic dyspnea is present at rest or with minimal exertion, is chronic or gradually progressive, and is not associated with hypoxemia, chest pain, orthopnea, syncope, or focal neurologic signs. Objective hypoxemia (SpO2 < 95% on room air) or features suggestive of cardiopulmonary disease (rapid progression, hemoptysis, pleuritic chest pain, unilateral leg swelling) should prompt urgent evaluation for pulmonary embolism, peripartum cardiomyopathy, pneumonia, or other pathology. Because baseline PaCO2 is reduced in pregnancy, a normal PaCO2 (~$40$ mmHg) is abnormal in a pregnant patient and may signal respiratory failure.

Anemia: The dilutional physiologic anemia of pregnancy lowers hemoglobin and hematocrit. Nevertheless, true iron-deficiency anemia remains common and clinically important. Symptoms such as fatigue, tachycardia, dyspnea on exertion, pallor, and reduced exercise tolerance may be attributed to pregnancy but should trigger evaluation when disproportionate to gestational stage or when hemoglobin falls below expected ranges. Screening hemoglobin and ferritin during pregnancy identify iron deficiency early; ferritin below $30$ ng/mL commonly indicates depleted iron stores. Treatment with oral or intravenous iron is effective and reduces perinatal risks associated with severe maternal anemia.

Fever: Interpreting fever in pregnancy requires recognizing both altered immune responses and the high stakes of maternal infection. Maternal fever has implications for the fetus (risk of neural tube defects with early gestational hyperthermia, and fetal tachycardia or acidosis with intrauterine infection). Some infections are more severe in pregnancy (e.g., influenza) and warrant prompt treatment. However, baseline leukocytosis and elevated inflammatory markers may obscure the significance of mild fever. A pragmatic approach is to evaluate fever in pregnancy with a lower threshold for diagnostic testing and empiric therapy when appropriate, particularly for systemic symptoms or evidence of organ dysfunction. Obstetric infections (chorioamnionitis, postpartum endometritis) require specific vigilance in peripartum contexts.

Laboratory and imaging interpretation: Multiple common laboratory tests have pregnancy-altered baselines—lower creatinine and BUN, lower bicarbonate, lower PaCO2, higher fibrinogen and D-dimer, higher leukocyte counts, and altered hepatic enzymes in some contexts. These altered baselines limit the utility of standard thresholds (for example, absolute D-dimer cutoffs for VTE exclusion). Imaging interpretation must account for organ displacement (e.g., renal ultrasound for hydronephrosis may be harder to interpret). When imaging involves ionizing radiation, clinician must balance diagnostic yield with fetal exposure, using appropriate shielding and alternative modalities (ultrasound, MRI without gadolinium) when feasible.

Perioperative and critical care implications: The increased cardiac output and blood volume, reduced FRC, increased aspiration risk (due to GERD and delayed gastric emptying), and hypercoagulability all influence perioperative planning. Preparation for rapid desaturation and difficult airway management is essential; awake or modified rapid-sequence techniques and maximal preoxygenation are common strategies. VTE prophylaxis decisions take into account pregnancy-specific risk factors. In critical illness, pregnancy-specific targets (e.g., oxygenation goals of maternal SpO2 ≥ 95% to ensure adequate fetal oxygen delivery) may alter management priorities.

Conclusion

Pregnancy transforms baseline physiology across organ systems in predictable ways that are essential to recognize for safe clinical care. Many common signs and laboratory abnormalities that would be alarming in nonpregnant adults—mild anemia, leukocytosis, tachycardia, elevated fibrinogen—may be benign in pregnancy, yet the same physiologic changes also create novel vulnerabilities (reduced respiratory reserve, hypercoagulability, increased infection risk, and the potential for rapid deterioration during hemorrhage). Clinical judgment in pregnancy requires integrating these physiologic norms with careful attention to warning signs that indicate pathologic processes. Awareness of pregnancy-specific normal ranges and the ways physiologic adaptation shapes symptom presentation improves diagnostic accuracy, guides therapy, and enhances maternal and fetal safety.

Introduction

Uterine leiomyomas, commonly called fibroids, are benign smooth muscle neoplasms of the myometrium that are the most frequent pelvic tumor in women of reproductive age. While morphologically benign, leiomyomas are clinically important because of their high prevalence, potential to cause heavy menstrual bleeding, pelvic pain, reproductive dysfunction, and bulk-related symptoms that impair quality of life. Understanding the epidemiology, molecular pathogenesis, anatomic classification, morphologic variants, growth behavior and hormonal responsiveness of leiomyomas is essential for rational diagnosis and management. This chapter reviews these foundational topics, emphasizing the biologic mechanisms that connect cellular events to clinical presentation and decision-making.

# Epidemiology and risk factors (age, ethnicity, parity, BMI, hormonal influences)

Epidemiology
Leiomyomas are extremely common. Population and autopsy studies estimate that up to 70–80% of women will have at least one leiomyoma by the time of menopause, though only 20–40% become clinically significant (symptomatic or detected on imaging during life). Prevalence increases with age throughout the reproductive years, peaking in the 40s and typically regressing after menopause. Many women are asymptomatic and leiomyomas are often incidental findings.

Ethnicity and genetics
Race/ethnicity is a strong determinant of disease burden. Black (African-descended) women have higher incidence, earlier onset (often by a decade earlier), greater symptom burden, larger and more numerous lesions, and higher rates of myomectomy and hysterectomy compared with White women. Genetic predisposition plays a role: positive family history increases risk. Certain rare hereditary syndromes such as hereditary leiomyomatosis and renal cell carcinoma (FH mutations) produce multiple and early-onset leiomyomas.

Reproductive factors
Parity is inversely associated with risk: nulliparity or lower parity increases leiomyoma risk while pregnancies exert a protective effect long-term. The protection appears dose-dependent with increasing number of full-term pregnancies conferring greater reduction in risk. Early age at first term pregnancy is associated with lower lifetime incidence.

Body mass index and metabolic factors
Higher body mass index (BMI) is associated with increased leiomyoma risk, likely mediated through increased peripheral estrogen production from aromatization in adipose tissue and altered insulin/IGF axis. Metabolic syndrome features and insulin resistance have been correlated with higher prevalence.

Hormonal influences and reproductive lifespan
Endogenous estrogen and progesterone are central determinants. Early menarche and longer reproductive lifespan (later menopause) increase cumulative exposure to ovarian steroids and increase risk. Combined oral contraceptive pills have a complex relationship with leiomyomas: some studies show small protective effects when started early, while others show neutral effects. Progestin-only therapies and exogenous progestogens can stimulate leiomyoma growth in some contexts, and selective progesterone receptor modulators (SPRMs) demonstrate reduction of bleeding and size. Tamoxifen has been associated with increased leiomyoma growth effects in some reports. Overall, ovarian steroid exposure (estrogen and progesterone) is a major driver of growth, explaining the strong age and reproductive factor associations.

Other risk factors
Additional influences include vitamin D deficiency (epidemiologic association with increased risk), smoking (mixed data but generally not protective), and environmental endocrine disruptors (investigational). Hypertension has been variably linked to higher leiomyoma prevalence. The interplay between these factors is complex and likely mediated by both systemic hormonal effects and local uterine susceptibility.

# Cellular and molecular pathogenesis of leiomyoma formation

Cellular origin and clonal nature
Leiomyomas arise from the smooth muscle cells of the myometrium and typically behave as monoclonal neoplasms, arising from a single transformed myometrial progenitor or stem-like cell. Clonal analyses demonstrate that most fibroids in a uterus are genetically distinct, suggesting multiple independent transformation events rather than spread from a single lesion.

Driver mutations and molecular subgroups
Recent molecular studies identify recurrent somatic genetic alterations that define molecular subgroups of leiomyomas. The most frequent identified driver is mutation in the MED12 gene (mediator complex subunit 12), present in approximately 40–70% of sporadic leiomyomas depending on cohort. MED12 mutations disrupt transcriptional regulation and downstream pathways regulating cell proliferation.

Another subgroup is characterized by overexpression or rearrangements of HMGA2 (high mobility group AT-hook 2), often linked to cytogenetic rearrangements such as translocations; these lesions tend to be larger and occur in younger women. Less commonly, biallelic inactivation of fumarate hydratase (FH) defines the hereditary leiomyomatosis subgroup; FH-mutant leiomyomas have distinct metabolic dysregulation and may present earlier.

Other genetic alterations include deletions or rearrangements involving RAD51B, COL4A5–COL4A6 fusions, and chromothripsis-like events in rare tumors. These genetic drivers converge on pathways that modulate proliferation, extracellular matrix (ECM) production, and sensitivity to steroid hormones.

Growth signaling, extracellular matrix and microenvironment
Leiomyomas are not simply proliferating smooth muscle; a hallmark is excessive ECM production including collagen (types I and III), fibronectin, and proteoglycans. The ECM contributes to tumor stiffness, compressive effects and growth signaling via mechanotransduction—ECM stiffness activates cell-surface integrins and downstream signaling pathways (e.g., MAPK, PI3K/AKT) that further promote proliferation and resistance to apoptosis.

Growth factors and cytokines such as transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β), platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), epidermal growth factor (EGF), insulin-like growth factor (IGF) and others are overexpressed in leiomyomas relative to adjacent myometrium. TGF-β, in particular, drives ECM accumulation and fibrosis. Dysregulated microRNAs and epigenetic alterations (DNA methylation, histone modification) further modulate gene expression.

Hormones and receptor biology
Estrogen and progesterone are central to leiomyoma biology. Leiomyomas typically express estrogen receptors (ERα) and progesterone receptors (PR). Estrogen promotes proliferation and upregulates PR expression in fibroid cells; progesterone acting through PR stimulates further growth by increasing mitotic activity and enhancing secretion of growth factors while inhibiting apoptosis. Progesterone stimulates cyclin-dependent kinases and downregulates pro-apoptotic factors such as TNF-related apoptosis-inducing ligand (TRAIL). This underpins the clinical response of fibroids to agents that suppress gonadal steroid production (e.g., GnRH analogs), and the therapeutic effect of SPRMs.

Stem/progenitor cell hypothesis
Leiomyoma formation may originate from a rare myometrial stem/progenitor cell that acquires a driver mutation and clonally expands, recruiting differentiated smooth muscle cells and stromal components to form the mass. Evidence comes from side-population cell studies and in vitro models where a minority cell population shows progenitor markers and tumor-forming capacity when implanted in immunodeficient mice.

Integration to clinical behavior
Molecular subtype, the extent of ECM deposition, and hormonal milieu together determine growth behavior, symptom expression and response to therapy. For example, MED12-mutant tumors may have different growth kinetics and drug responsiveness compared with HMGA2-driven lesions. Understanding these pathways informs the rationale for hormonal therapies, uterine-sparing surgical approaches, and emerging targeted agents.

# Types by location: submucosal, intramural, subserosal, pedunculated

Anatomic classification by location
Leiomyomas are defined clinically and surgically by their relationship to the uterine cavity and serosal surface. The main location-based categories are:

- Submucosal: Projecting into the endometrial cavity beneath the endometrium. These may be sessile or pedunculated and may distort the cavity.
- Intramural: Confined within the myometrium, not projecting significantly into the cavity or serosa.
- Subserosal: Projecting outward from the serosal surface into the pelvic cavity.
- Pedunculated: A subtype that forms a stalk; can be pedunculated submucosal (prolapsing into the cavity or vagina) or pedunculated subserosal (on a pedicle into the peritoneal cavity).

Clinical significance of location
Location has direct implications for symptoms, fertility and management. Submucosal fibroids are most strongly associated with heavy menstrual bleeding (HMB) and reproductive dysfunction because they alter the endometrial surface, increase endometrial surface area, and may disrupt implantation. Even small submucosal lesions can cause significant bleeding or infertility.

Intramural fibroids, depending on size and proximity to the endometrium, can cause HMB and pelvic pain, and may impair fertility if they distort the endometrium. Historically, intramural fibroids larger than certain thresholds were considered detrimental to fertility, but contemporary evidence suggests that only those that distort the cavity significantly consistently impair outcomes.

Subserosal fibroids primarily cause bulk symptoms—pelvic pressure, urinary frequency, constipation—rather than bleeding, because they do not directly affect the endometrium. Pedunculated subserosal fibroids can become torsed and present with acute abdominal pain. Pedunculated submucosal fibroids may prolapse through the cervix and present with bleeding or discharge.

FIGO classification and practical use
The FIGO (International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics) leiomyoma subclassification system (described below in classification systems) integrates location and degree of intracavitary extension and is widely used because it correlates with symptoms and guides surgical planning (e.g., hysteroscopic resection for FIGO types 0–2).

# Morphologic variants: cystic degeneration, red (carneous) degeneration, hyaline degeneration, calcification

Degenerative changes overview
As leiomyomas enlarge, their tissue outgrows the blood supply and undergoes various degenerative changes. These changes influence imaging appearance, clinical presentation, and sometimes the urgency of treatment. Common types of degeneration include hyaline, myxoid/cystic, red (carneous), and calcification. Degeneration is not synonymous with malignancy but is a normal sequela of poor perfusion and tissue remodeling, particularly in large tumors and during pregnancy.

Hyaline degeneration
Hyaline degeneration is the most common type and is characterized by homogeneous, eosinophilic deposition of proteinaceous material within the tumor, replacing smooth muscle bundles. On gross inspection, hyalinized areas appear pale and firm. Hyaline degeneration typically causes no acute symptoms but can alter ultrasound and MRI characteristics—appearing as areas of low signal on T2-weighted MRI and of variable echogenicity on ultrasound.

Cystic/myxoid degeneration
Progression of hyaline change may lead to liquefaction, producing cystic or myxoid degeneration with mucinous material. Cystic degeneration can be focal or extensive and produces heterogeneous imaging with fluid components. Clinically, large cystic areas may fluctuate in size and rarely present as palpable mass with mixed consistency. Cystic degeneration can complicate imaging interpretation, mimicking adnexal cysts if exophytic.

Red (carneous) degeneration
Red degeneration results from acute venous thrombosis within the tumor causing hemorrhagic infarction. It is most commonly seen during pregnancy due to rapid tumor growth, increased vascular demands and altered blood flow. Clinically, red degeneration presents with acute localized abdominal pain, low-grade fever and leukocytosis; the uterus is tender and may have localized peritoneal signs. Imaging (MRI) may show hemorrhagic signal changes. Management during pregnancy is usually conservative analgesia and supportive care; surgical removal in pregnancy is generally avoided unless necessary.

Calcification
Calcific degeneration commonly occurs in postmenopausal women as mineralization of degenerated tissue. On radiographs and ultrasound, calcified fibroids produce characteristic shadowing and may be seen as echogenic foci. Calcified fibroids are typically stable and less hormonally responsive.

Other variants and unusual patterns
Less common morphological patterns include lipoleiomyomas (containing fat), bizarre (symplastic) leiomyomas with highly atypical-appearing cells but benign behavior, and cellular leiomyomas, which are densely cellular variants that may mimic sarcoma on imaging and histology. Differentiation from leiomyosarcoma is crucial but often requires careful histopathological and clinical correlation (rapid growth alone is not diagnostic of malignancy).

# Growth patterns and influence of pregnancy and hormones on size

Natural history and growth kinetics
Leiomyoma growth is variable and unpredictable. Longitudinal studies report heterogeneous behavior: some fibroids remain stable for years, some regress, and others grow at variable rates. Average reported annual growth rates range widely; a commonly referenced mean is a few percent to about 30% volume change per year depending on cohort, fibroid size, age and hormonal milieu. Smaller fibroids tend to exhibit more proportional growth than larger lesions, but large fibroids may grow substantially in absolute terms.

Age-dependent behavior
Growth is most active during the reproductive years and slows after menopause when ovarian steroid production declines. Perimenopausal unpredictable behavior is common—some lesions diminish as ovarian function wanes. In the early reproductive years, growth tends to correlate with cumulative estrogen and progesterone exposure.

Hormonal influence: estrogen and progesterone
Estrogen stimulates mitotic activity and upregulates progesterone receptors in leiomyoma cells, increasing their responsiveness to progesterone. Progesterone, paradoxically, has a strong pro-growth role in established fibroids: it promotes mitogenesis, ECM deposition and inhibits apoptosis. Pharmacologic suppression of ovarian function (e.g., gonadotropin-releasing hormone [GnRH] agonists or antagonists) typically induces leiomyoma shrinkage; however, size reduction is often temporary and tumors can regrow after therapy cessation. SPRMs and selective progestin receptor modulators can reduce bleeding and shrink fibroids by modulating PR-dependent signaling.

Effect of pregnancy
Pregnancy often causes rapid growth of leiomyomas, particularly in the first trimester and early pregnancy, driven by high circulating estrogen and progesterone and increased uterine blood flow. The degree of enlargement is variable; many fibroids enlarge modestly, while some may increase dramatically. Red degeneration is more common during pregnancy because of relative vascular insufficiency in rapidly expanding tumors. Postpartum involution frequently results in partial regression: many fibroids reduce in size after delivery due to decreased hormonal stimulation and uterine involution, though permanent shrinkage varies.

External hormonal therapies and drugs
Exogenous hormones alter leiomyoma behavior. Combined oral contraceptives may have neutral or modest protective effects; progestin-only therapies can sometimes maintain or increase fibroid size depending on the agent. GnRH analogs (agonists/antagonists) produce profound hypoestrogenism and shrink leiomyomas substantially (commonly 30–50% reduction in volume), used short-term preoperatively to improve anemia and reduce vascularity. Newer oral GnRH antagonists with add-back therapy can maintain symptom control while limiting hypoestrogenic adverse effects. SPRMs (e.g., ulipristal acetate) produce amenorrhea and fibroid shrinkage via antiproliferative and proapoptotic effects on leiomyoma cells; availability varies by region.

Clinical implications of growth behavior
Because growth is heterogeneous, clinical decisions are driven more by symptoms and impact on fertility than by size alone. Rapid growth raises concern and prompts careful evaluation, but even rapid growth is more often benign (degeneration, edema) than malignant. Imaging and histologic features that suggest sarcoma include postmenopausal new growth, heterogeneous necrosis, high vascularity, and suspicious imaging, but definitive diagnosis often requires histopathology. Management strategies must consider potential for pregnancy-related enlargement, desire for future fertility, and the reversible nature of hormone suppression.

# Clinical presentations: pain, bleeding, bulk symptoms, infertility

Heavy menstrual bleeding and anemia
Heavy menstrual bleeding (HMB) is a common presentation. Mechanisms include increased endometrial surface area caused by intracavitary or submucosal tumors, localized venous ectasia on the myometrial surface, altered uterine contractility leading to ineffective hemostasis, and increased expression of angiogenic and fibrinolytic factors in the adjacent endometrium. Submucosal leiomyomas, even small ones, disproportionately cause HMB. Chronic HMB can lead to iron deficiency anemia; normal adult female hemoglobin ranges are approximately 12.0–16.0 g/dL; anemia is often defined as hemoglobin <12 g/dL in non-pregnant women. Management of anemia is a key clinical decision point prior to surgical intervention.

Pain: dysmenorrhea and acute pain syndromes
Leiomyomas can cause dysmenorrhea related to uterine contractility and submucosal involvement. They also produce chronic pelvic pain due to mass effect. Acute pelvic pain may result from torsion of a pedunculated subserosal fibroid or red (carneous) degeneration, the latter often seen during pregnancy and accompanied by systemic signs. Degeneration-related pain tends to be localized and can be severe.

Bulk symptoms and pressure effects
Large intramural or subserosal fibroids produce bulk symptoms: urinary frequency and urgency due to bladder compression, hydronephrosis and renal impairment in severe compression of ureters (rare), constipation and tenesmus from rectal compression, back or leg pain from pelvic nerve compression, and abdominal distension with a palpable mass. These symptoms correlate with size, number and location, and may prompt surgical treatment even in absence of bleeding.

Reproductive dysfunction: infertility and pregnancy complications
Leiomyomas are associated with infertility and adverse pregnancy outcomes, but causation depends on location. Submucosal fibroids are most consistently associated with decreased fertility, increased miscarriage rates, and reduced implantation; hysteroscopic resection of submucosal fibroids improves pregnancy rates in many studies. Intramural fibroids that distort the endometrial cavity or are large may also impair fertility, though data are mixed regarding small intramural lesions not distorting the cavity. Mechanisms include mechanical distortion of the cavity, impaired endometrial receptivity, altered uterine contractility, local inflammation, and impediment to sperm or embryo transport. During pregnancy, leiomyomas increase the risk of pain, red degeneration, malpresentation, preterm labor, placenta previa (if submucosal or lower-uterine segment involvement), cesarean delivery, and postpartum hemorrhage.

Other presentations
Leiomyomas can present with abnormal vaginal discharge (especially if submucosal and necrotic), palpable pelvic mass during routine exam, or as incidental imaging findings. Rarely, leiomyomas may be the point of origin for acute gynecologic emergencies (torsion, hemorrhage). Differential diagnosis includes adenomyosis, adnexal masses, and uterine sarcomas.

Clinical decision points
Assessment should quantify bleeding (menstrual calendar, Pictorial Blood Loss Assessment Chart when available), evaluate hemoglobin and iron studies (ferritin <30 ng/mL often indicates iron deficiency), consider fertility goals, and correlate symptoms with imaging findings. Indications for intervention include significant HMB causing anemia or impacting quality of life, intractable pain, rapid growth with concern for malignancy, bulk symptoms impairing organ function, or infertility attributable to fibroids.

# Classification systems and staging relevant to management

FIGO leiomyoma subclassification
The FIGO (International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics) system provides a standardized anatomic classification from type 0 to 8, reflecting the degree of intracavitary and intramural involvement:

- Type 0: Pedunculated intracavitary (entirely within the uterine cavity, no myometrial invasion).
- Type 1: Submucosal with <50% intramural extension.
- Type 2: Submucosal with ≥50% intramural extension.
- Type 3: Contacts endometrium; 100% intramural but abutting cavity (no submucosal component).
- Type 4: Intramural.
- Type 5: Subserosal with ≥50% intramural.
- Type 6: Subserosal with <50% intramural.
- Type 7: Subserosal pedunculated.
- Type 8: Other (e.g., cervical, parasitic) or extrauterine tumors of uterine origin.

This anatomic subclassification is clinically useful because FIGO types 0–2 are amenable to hysteroscopic resection; types 3–5 may require combined approaches; types 6–7 are more likely to produce bulk symptoms and may be addressed laparoscopically or by other uterine-sparing techniques. Type 8 includes lesions where management may differ markedly (e.g., cervical fibroids can complicate delivery and surgical approaches).

Staging and size considerations for management
There is no universally adopted staging system equivalent to oncology stages for leiomyomas because they are benign and management is symptom-driven. However, categorization by size, number and location guides therapy. Practical decision thresholds include consideration of myomectomy or uterine artery embolization for symptomatic fibroids typically larger than a few centimeters or causing significant symptoms. For fertility-sparing surgery, intramural fibroids >4–5 cm or those distorting the cavity are often considered for removal, though evidence is nuanced and individualized.

Imaging-based assessment
Transvaginal ultrasound is the first-line imaging modality to identify number, size and approximate location. MRI offers superior mapping, especially for surgical planning, differentiating degeneration, and when multiple fibroids obscure anatomy. MRI can provide detailed spatial relationships to the endometrium and serosa and is useful in complex cases or when considering uterine-sparing approaches. Imaging classification using FIGO categories should be recorded to guide management.

Treatment-oriented classification systems
Some classification systems are tailored to guide specific therapies: for example, the FIGO types 0–2 classify hysteroscopic resectability, while other algorithms stratify candidates for uterine artery embolization versus myomectomy based on number/location/size and desire for future fertility. The presence of submucosal components and the cavity distortion index help predict improvement in fertility after resection.

Risk stratification for sarcoma
Because leiomyosarcoma is rare but clinically serious, clinicians must stratify risk preoperatively. Red flags include new rapid growth in a postmenopausal woman, atypical imaging features (marked heterogeneity, necrosis, irregular margins), persistent pain disproportionate to size, and systemic symptoms. However, preoperative differentiation between benign fibroid degeneration and sarcoma remains imperfect, and definitive diagnosis often requires histopathology. Thus, clinical context, imaging and age drive the threshold for en bloc removal and avoidance of morcellation when malignancy cannot be reasonably excluded.

Conclusion

Uterine leiomyomas are common, molecularly heterogeneous benign tumors whose clinical significance depends on location, size, growth behavior, and the hormonal milieu. A modern understanding integrates epidemiology, genetics (e.g., MED12, HMGA2, FH), ECM biology, and steroid hormone signaling to explain varied presentations and responses to therapy. Anatomical classification (notably FIGO types) links directly to symptomatology and procedural planning. Management decisions rest on symptom severity, fertility desires, and risk stratification for malignancy—with imaging and laboratory assessment (including hemoglobin and iron indices) providing practical thresholds for intervention. Familiarity with the pathophysiology and natural history allows the clinician to tailor interventions appropriately, balancing symptom control with preservation of reproductive potential and minimization of morbidity.

Introduction

Adnexal masses are detected with increasing frequency during pregnancy because of routine use of transvaginal and transabdominal ultrasound in early gestation. Most are benign and either represent physiologic structures or preexisting ovarian lesions that are discovered incidentally. However, a subset may be emergent (e.g., ectopic pregnancy) or predispose to complications such as torsion, rupture, or hemorrhage that can threaten maternal and fetal well-being. Understanding the spectrum of adnexal pathology in pregnancy, their expected behavior under the hormonal milieu of gestation, imaging characteristics, and principles of management are essential for clinicians who care for pregnant patients. This chapter reviews the common adnexal lesions encountered in pregnancy, focuses on endometriomas and their unique behavior in pregnancy, reviews emergent adnexal pathologies including ectopic pregnancy, contrasts benign and malignant ovarian tumors with risk stratification approaches applicable in pregnancy, details complications and their clinical presentation, and summarizes how pregnancy modifies the natural history of adnexal lesions.

# Common adnexal lesions in pregnancy: corpus luteum cysts, dermoids, serous/mucinous cystadenomas

Adnexal masses identified during pregnancy most commonly fall into a few diagnostic categories: physiologic cysts (especially corpus luteum cysts), benign germ cell tumors such as mature cystic teratomas (dermoids), and epithelial tumors such as serous and mucinous cystadenomas. The prevalence of adnexal masses detected on first-trimester ultrasound varies but is commonly cited as 1%–4% of pregnancies; the vast majority are benign and many resolve spontaneously.

Corpus luteum cysts
The corpus luteum is the luteinized remnant of the ovarian follicle formed after ovulation and is typically essential for progesterone production in early pregnancy until the luteal-placental shift occurs (around 8–10 weeks’ gestation). Corpus luteum cysts are functional cysts that range in size from a few centimeters to occasionally >10 cm. On ultrasound they are usually unilateral, thin-walled, may be anechoic or contain internal echoes from hemorrhage, and can demonstrate a “ring of fire” on color Doppler due to peripheral vascularity. Distinguishing a corpus luteum from other cystic lesions is often done by timing (early pregnancy), appearance, and sometimes by repeat imaging to document resolution. Most corpus luteum cysts are asymptomatic and resolve spontaneously by the second trimester as placental progesterone production becomes dominant. However, they can hemorrhage, rupture, or undergo torsion, and symptomatic lesions require urgent attention.

Mature cystic teratomas (dermoids)
Mature cystic teratomas are the most common benign ovarian neoplasm in reproductive-age women and are frequently encountered incidentally in pregnancy. They arise from germ cells and typically contain elements from multiple embryologic layers: sebaceous material, hair, teeth, and fat. On ultrasound they often present as complex masses with echogenic Rokitansky protuberances and acoustic shadowing from calcified components. Computed tomography and MRI more clearly demonstrate fat-fluid levels and calcifications; MRI is preferred in pregnancy when additional characterization is needed because it avoids ionizing radiation. Dermoids are usually slow-growing and benign but are notable for a risk of torsion because their solid components increase mass and weight. Rare complications include rupture leading to chemical peritonitis and malignant transformation (exceptionally rare, <2% overall), which is exceedingly uncommon during pregnancy.

Serous and mucinous cystadenomas
Epithelial ovarian neoplasms of the serous or mucinous types are common benign causes of adnexal masses. Serous cystadenomas typically are unilocular or multilocular cystic masses with thin septations; mucinous cystadenomas can be multiloculated and may reach very large sizes. On ultrasound they appear as large, anechoic or low-level echogenic cystic lesions with thin septations; mural nodularity is uncommon in benign forms. Mucinous tumors may demonstrate more complex multilocularity. In pregnancy, benign serous and mucinous cystadenomas are often managed expectantly if asymptomatic and without suspicious features, since many do not change significantly and the risk of malignancy is low. However, very large cysts (>10 cm) raise concern for torsion and may be considered for surgery in the second trimester.

General approach and imaging
Initial evaluation of an adnexal mass detected in pregnancy is with transvaginal ultrasound, supplemented by transabdominal views for large masses. Key sonographic features suggesting benign lesions include simple cystic appearance, thin septations, small size (<5–6 cm), unilocular morphology, and lack of solid papillary projections or vascularized mural nodules. Color Doppler can demonstrate flow but is not specific for malignancy; many benign cysts can have peripheral vascularity. MRI without gadolinium is a valuable second-line imaging modality when ultrasound is indeterminate. Tumor markers such as CA-125 are limited in pregnancy because physiologic elevations in the first trimester and late pregnancy reduce specificity; CA-125 may be elevated in pregnancy-related conditions and should not be used alone to diagnose malignancy.

# Endometriomas: pathophysiology, imaging features, behavior in pregnancy

Pathophysiology
Endometriomas are cystic ovarian lesions formed by the presence of ectopic endometrial tissue within the ovary. Endometriosis is a hormonally responsive chronic inflammatory condition characterized by implantation of functional endometrial glands and stroma outside the uterine cavity. In the ovary, repeated cyclic hemorrhage into ectopic implants causes accumulation of degraded blood products producing the classic “chocolate cyst.” The underlying mechanisms include retrograde menstruation, coelomic metaplasia, and immunologic or stem-cell–mediated processes. Endometriomas contain hemosiderin-laden macrophages and repeated cycles of bleeding and fibrosis, which can lead to adhesions and distortion of normal ovarian architecture.

Imaging features
On transvaginal ultrasound, endometriomas characteristically appear as unilocular or multilocular cystic ovarian masses with homogeneously low-level internal echoes (ground-glass echogenicity), reflecting old blood products. They are frequently bilateral. Small internal septations and peripheral vascularity may be present. On MRI, endometriomas show high signal intensity on T1-weighted images due to methemoglobin and variable low signal intensity on T2-weighted images (so-called “T2 shading” sign), which reflects concentrated blood products and chronic hemorrhage; the T2 shading sign is supportive but not pathognomonic. Fat-suppressed sequences can help differentiate endometriomas from dermoids: fat suppression reduces signal from fat but not from blood products. Use of gadolinium contrast is generally avoided in pregnancy; even without contrast, MRI remains helpful for characterization when ultrasound is indeterminate.

Behavior in pregnancy
Pregnancy alters the hormonal milieu in a manner that can affect endometriosis and endometriomas. Elevated progesterone and other pregnancy-related hormones often induce decidualization of endometrial tissues. Decidualization within an endometrioma can create mural nodules and increased vascularity that may mimic neoplasm on imaging. On ultrasound a decidualized endometrioma may show a vascularized papillary projection; MRI can help by showing characteristic blood-product signals in the cyst and demonstrating the non-neoplastic nature of mural nodules when correlated clinically. Clinically, many women report symptomatic improvement in endometriosis during pregnancy; however, the disease does not universally regress and can still cause complications. Endometriomas may undergo enlargement in pregnancy due to decidualization or, less commonly, may be complicated by rupture, hemorrhage, or torsion. Management is individualized: small, asymptomatic endometriomas without suspicious features are often observed; symptomatic lesions or those with indeterminate imaging features may require surgical intervention, ideally in the second trimester to minimize obstetric risks.

# Ectopic pregnancy and other emergent adnexal pathologies

Ectopic pregnancy
Ectopic pregnancy is the implantation of a fertilized ovum outside the uterine cavity, most commonly in the fallopian tube. It is a life-threatening obstetric emergency when rupture leads to intra-abdominal hemorrhage. Presenting features classically include abdominal pain, vaginal bleeding, and amenorrhea, but presentations vary. Serum beta-human chorionic gonadotropin (β-hCG) dynamics and transvaginal ultrasound are the cornerstones of diagnosis. A discriminatory zone is used so that an intrauterine pregnancy is expected to be visualized on transvaginal ultrasound when the serum β-hCG exceeds a threshold (commonly around 1500–2000 mIU/mL, though thresholds may vary by laboratory and equipment). If β-hCG is above the discriminatory zone and no intrauterine gestation is seen, ectopic pregnancy is highly likely. A definitive extrauterine mass on ultrasound confirms the diagnosis; an adnexal complex mass with a “bagel” or “ring of fire” appearance can represent an ectopic. Management depends on hemodynamic stability, size, presence of fetal cardiac activity, and patient preference. Options include medical management with single-dose methotrexate for selected stable patients with small, unruptured ectopic pregnancies and low/resolving β-hCG levels, and surgical management (laparoscopy preferred if feasible) for ruptured ectopic, hemodynamic instability, or contraindications to methotrexate.

Other emergent adnexal pathologies
Beyond ectopic pregnancy, adnexal masses in pregnancy can present emergently as ovarian torsion, rupture, hemorrhage into a cyst, or infection.

- Ovarian torsion results from partial or complete twisting of the ovary on its vascular pedicle, compromising venous outflow first and then arterial inflow, producing ischemia and necrosis if not promptly corrected. Pregnancy itself, particularly the first trimester when corpus luteum cysts are common, increases the risk of torsion because of enlarged ovaries and increased ligamentous laxity. Clinically, torsion presents with acute onset unilateral lower abdominal pain, nausea, and vomiting. Ultrasound may show an enlarged ovary with peripheral follicles, absent or diminished Doppler flow (though Doppler can be normal due to dual ovarian arterial supply), and free pelvic fluid. Management is urgent surgical detorsion and evaluation of ovarian viability; ovarian conservation is preferred when possible in pregnancy.

- Hemorrhage into a cyst typically manifests as acute abdominal pain with possible signs of intraperitoneal bleeding if rupture occurs. Hemorrhagic cysts often have heterogeneous internal echoes on ultrasound and may demonstrate fluid-fluid levels. Clinical stability, hemoglobin level, and imaging guide management; significant hemoperitoneum or instability necessitates urgent surgery.

- Rupture of an adnexal mass may cause acute abdomen and intraperitoneal hemorrhage. The appearance on ultrasound is nonspecific but free fluid is a key sign. Management parallels hemorrhage.

- Adnexal infection (e.g., tubo-ovarian abscess) is less common in pregnancy but may occur, often in the setting of sexually transmitted infection or pelvic inflammatory disease; presentation includes fever, pelvic pain, leukocytosis, and complex mass on imaging. Management requires broad-spectrum antibiotics and drainage if indicated, balancing fetal risks.

When evaluating emergent adnexal presentations in pregnancy, clinicians must balance the need for prompt diagnosis and intervention with concerns about fetal exposure to imaging and anesthesia. Ultrasound and MRI (without contrast) are safe imaging modalities; CT and ionizing radiation are generally avoided in pregnancy unless necessary.

# Benign versus malignant ovarian tumors: prevalence and risk stratification in pregnant patients

Prevalence and general considerations
The majority of ovarian tumors encountered during pregnancy are benign. Malignancy is rare, estimated in approximately 1–3 per 10,000 pregnancies, though rates depend on the population studied and detection methods. When malignancy does occur, it is most often epithelial cancers, germ cell tumors, or borderline tumors. Specific histologies such as dysgerminoma (a germ cell tumor) can present in young women and during pregnancy. Clinicians must remain vigilant, however, because early recognition and appropriate staging/management significantly affect outcomes.

Risk stratification
Robust risk stratification before intervention relies on integration of clinical findings, imaging characteristics, tumor markers (with caveats), and timing in pregnancy. Key elements:

- Clinical features: Pain, rapid growth, ascites, systemic symptoms, and large size (>10 cm) raise concern. However, many benign lesions can be large and asymptomatic.

- Imaging features: Ultrasound is primary. Features worrisome for malignancy include solid components, papillary excrescences/mural nodularity, thick irregular septations, multilocularity with heterogeneous internal echoes, and increased internal vascularity on Doppler. The presence of ascites or peritoneal implants suggests more advanced disease. MRI can further characterize complex masses and better delineate soft tissue components without ionizing radiation. Use of validated ultrasound scoring systems such as the IOTA (International Ovarian Tumor Analysis) simple rules and ADNEX model can aid preoperative risk assessment in nonpregnant patients; while these models were developed and validated mostly outside pregnancy, their sonographic criteria remain informative for pregnant patients with the caveat that physiologic pregnancy-related changes and decidualization may alter appearances.

- Tumor markers: CA-125 is of limited value in pregnancy because levels are physiologically elevated in the first trimester and may also increase in other pregnancy-related conditions. Typical nonpregnant reference ranges (CA-125 <35 U/mL) are not reliable in early pregnancy. Alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG), and inhibin are used for specific tumor types (e.g., AFP in yolk sac tumors, LDH in dysgerminoma), but pregnancy itself alters some markers (e.g., AFP increases during pregnancy). Interpretation requires consideration of gestational age and correlation with imaging.

Decision points for intervention
Management decisions balance maternal oncologic needs, obstetric risks, and fetal exposure. Key decision points include:

- Observation versus surgery: Small (<5–6 cm), simple-appearing cysts without suspicious features are usually observed with serial ultrasound because many resolve. Persistent or enlarging cysts, complex features, or symptoms may prompt surgery.

- Timing of surgery: If surgery is indicated during pregnancy for adnexal masses, the second trimester (ideally 16–20 weeks) is the preferred timing because organogenesis is complete and the uterus is not yet so large as to complicate access. Surgery in the first trimester increases the risk of miscarriage; surgery in the third trimester increases preterm labor risk and technical difficulty. However, emergent indications (torsion, rupture, hemorrhage, or suspected high-grade malignancy) require immediate intervention regardless of gestational age.

- Surgical approach: Minimally invasive surgery (laparoscopy) is feasible and safe during pregnancy when performed by experienced surgeons and with appropriate measures (lower insufflation pressures, left lateral tilt to avoid aortocaval compression). Laparotomy may be preferred for very large masses, suspected malignancy requiring staging, or hemodynamic instability.

- If malignancy is confirmed or highly suspected: Multidisciplinary care involving gynecologic oncology, maternal-fetal medicine, and neonatology is essential. The need for comprehensive staging or cytoreductive surgery must be weighed against fetal risks; in select situations chemotherapy may be administered during pregnancy (generally avoided in the first trimester) for certain tumor types.

Overall, the risk of malignancy in a pregnant patient with an adnexal mass is low, but structured risk stratification combining imaging and clinical context informs safe expectant management versus timely intervention.

# Complications: torsion, rupture, hemorrhage, infection

Ovarian torsion
Ovarian torsion is a gynecologic surgical emergency characterized by rotation of the ovary and often the fallopian tube along the vascular pedicle, leading to ischemia. Pregnancy increases the risk for torsion due to ovarian enlargement (physiologic cysts, corpus luteum, or tumors) and increased mobility. Typical presentation includes sudden, severe unilateral lower abdominal pain, often accompanied by nausea and vomiting. Vital sign abnormalities may be absent unless significant hemorrhage or systemic response has occurred. On ultrasound, torsed ovaries classically appear enlarged with peripheral displacement of follicles and heterogeneous stroma; Doppler flow may be reduced or absent, but normal flow does not exclude torsion due to collateral circulation. Prompt gynecologic evaluation and, if torsion suspected, surgical exploration is indicated. Detorsion and ovarian conservation are preferred whenever feasible, even if the ovary appears dusky; many ovaries regain function. Oophorectomy is reserved for nonviable tissue or when malignancy is suspected.

Rupture and hemorrhage
Rupture of an adnexal cyst releases its contents into the peritoneal cavity and may be accompanied by significant bleeding if vascularized tissue is involved (e.g., corpus luteum hemorrhage). Presentation ranges from sudden localized or generalized abdominal pain to peritoneal signs and hemodynamic instability in the setting of hemorrhage. Hemoglobin and hematocrit can decline; monitoring and fluid resuscitation are crucial. Ultrasound may detect intraperitoneal free fluid; CT is sensitive for active bleeding but generally avoided in pregnancy. Surgical management is determined by hemodynamic stability, ongoing bleeding, and gestational age; unstable patients require emergent operative intervention.

Infection
Infectious complications such as tubo-ovarian abscess are less common in pregnancy but important. Patients present with fever, pelvic or lower abdominal pain, elevated inflammatory markers (e.g., leukocytosis), and complex fluid collections on imaging. Management prioritizes maternal stabilization and infection control: broad-spectrum antibiotics with coverage for anaerobes and gram-negative organisms are first-line, with percutaneous or surgical drainage for large or refractory abscesses. If operative intervention is needed, pregnancy considerations regarding timing and fetal monitoring apply.

Other complications
- Adhesive disease and chronic pelvic pain from preexisting endometriosis or prior surgery may be exacerbated by pregnancy-related anatomical shifts.

- Venous thromboembolism can be a concern in postoperative pregnant patients; prophylaxis should be considered per obstetric and surgical guidelines.

Clinical recognition and timely intervention for these complications reduce maternal morbidity and preserve pregnancy when possible.

# Impact of pregnancy on adnexal lesion natural history

Hormonal environment and lesion behavior
Pregnancy induces a unique endocrine state—high circulating progesterone, elevated estrogens, human chorionic gonadotropin, and other placental hormones—that influences the biology of adnexal lesions. Functional cysts such as corpus luteum cysts are common early in pregnancy and often regress after the luteal-placental shift. Endometriosis often shows symptomatic improvement in many patients due to progesterone-dominant state causing decidualization and suppression of cyclic bleeding; however, existing endometriomas may persist or undergo decidual changes that complicate imaging interpretation. Dermoids and epithelial cystadenomas are generally hormonally insensitive and therefore may not markedly change during pregnancy, though some reports suggest modest growth attributable to increased vascularity and fluid shifts.

Growth patterns and spontaneous resolution
Small simple cysts (<5–6 cm) detected in the first trimester commonly resolve spontaneously by mid-gestation. Larger cysts (>10 cm) are less likely to resolve and have higher risks of torsion or obstruction during delivery, which may prompt consideration of removal. Serial ultrasound monitoring (e.g., every 4–8 weeks initially) helps document changes in size and morphology. Rapid enlargement, development of solid components, or new symptoms warrant closer assessment.

Decidualization and diagnostic challenges
Decidualization of ectopic endometrium and implants in pregnancy can create solid-appearing mural nodules that mimic neoplasm. This is especially relevant for endometriomas, where decidualized tissue may appear as vascularized papillary projections on ultrasound. Differentiation relies on a combination of imaging (MRI patterns of blood products), clinical context (history of endometriosis), and observation when safe. Awareness of decidualization prevents unnecessary premature surgery.

Immune modulation and tumor behavior
Pregnancy is an immunologically modulated state with relative tolerance to fetal antigens; theoretically this could influence tumor behavior, but clinical data are limited and heterogeneous. Some ovarian malignancies present during pregnancy and must be treated promptly; others may be managed with delayed intervention depending on histology and stage. In general, rapidly progressive tumors (e.g., high-grade epithelial cancers) can be aggressive regardless of pregnancy, whereas borderline tumors and many germ cell tumors have treatment options that can be temporized or administered in pregnancy under specialist guidance.

Obstetric implications
Large adnexal masses can complicate pregnancy and delivery. They may cause mechanical discomfort, obstruct labor if located in the pelvis at term, or require cesarean incision modification. Torsion and rupture can precipitate preterm labor. Surgical interventions themselves carry obstetric risks including miscarriage (higher in first-trimester surgery), preterm birth, and need for perioperative fetal monitoring. The second trimester represents a window in which many nonurgent but necessary surgeries can be performed with lower obstetric risk.

Surveillance strategies
Management strategies reflect lesion type, size, symptoms, and imaging features. For small uncomplicated lesions, surveillance with periodic ultrasound is reasonable: for example, repeat imaging at 6–8 weeks and then as clinically indicated. For complex or enlarging masses, multidisciplinary discussion with considerations for timing of surgery, anesthetic planning, and oncologic staging is necessary. In all cases, patient counseling should cover the natural history of the specific lesion, potential risks to pregnancy, and the balance of expectant versus interventional approaches.

Conclusion

Adnexal masses in pregnancy encompass a wide spectrum from physiologic cysts to neoplastic lesions. Most are benign and can be managed expectantly, but clinicians must recognize emergent pathologies like ectopic pregnancy and torsion and stratify risk for malignancy using clinical features and imaging. Pregnancy alters adnexal lesion biology through hormonal and immune changes that can both ameliorate symptoms (as in many cases of endometriosis) and complicate diagnosis (decidualization mimicking tumor). A careful, multidisciplinary approach that integrates safe imaging (transvaginal ultrasound and MRI without gadolinium), selective use of tumor markers with gestational context, and judicious timing of surgical intervention—preferably in the second trimester when feasible—optimizes maternal and fetal outcomes.

# Introduction

Uterine leiomyomas (fibroids) are monoclonal smooth muscle neoplasms of the myometrium that are common across reproductive age. Although many fibroids are asymptomatic, a significant subset undergo degenerative changes or complications that can produce acute or subacute clinical syndromes. Understanding the pathophysiology of fibroid degeneration, vascular compromise, torsion, infection, and mass-effect complications is essential for diagnosing and managing patients—especially during pregnancy, when fibroid biology and risk profiles differ. This chapter summarizes the mechanisms that underlie various types of fibroid degeneration, the vascular and inflammatory sequelae, the clinical features and diagnostic clues associated with torsion and secondary infection, and the potential for hemorrhage, organ compression, and systemic inflammatory responses. Emphasis is placed on linking histopathology and imaging correlates with clinical presentation and laboratory findings that guide decision-making.

# Types of degeneration (red, hyaline, cystic, myxoid) and pathologic correlates

Fibroid degeneration describes the spectrum of histologic and gross changes that occur when the internal environment of a leiomyoma is altered relative to its original well-vascularized, compact smooth-muscle architecture. Degeneration subtypes are classified by their predominant histologic pattern and typical clinical context.

Hyaline degeneration is the most common form and represents extracellular protein deposition and smooth muscle cell loss. Microscopically, there is homogenous, eosinophilic, acellular matrix deposition replacing muscle bundles; the process is thought to arise from chronic ischemia and impaired turnover of extracellular matrix. Grossly, hyalinized areas are pale, firm, and glassy. Hyaline degeneration usually causes gradual enlargement or firmness of the fibroid but rarely leads to acute pain or systemic inflammation.

Red degeneration (carneous degeneration) is most often described in pregnancy. It reflects acute venous thrombosis within the fibroid, followed by hemorrhagic infarction. Grossly the fibroid shows a red-brown, hemorrhagic cut surface; micropathology reveals coagulative necrosis with hemorrhage and variable inflammatory cell infiltration. Clinically, red degeneration often presents with acute localized abdominal pain, low-grade fever, and leukocytosis; pain can be persistent and poorly responsive to simple analgesia. The pathogenesis relates to rapid fibroid growth and altered uterine blood flow in pregnancy, leading to venous outflow obstruction and ischemic necrosis.

Cystic degeneration represents liquefaction of areas within a fibroid, typically following hyaline change. Microscopically there is loss of cellularity with cystic spaces filled by serous fluid. On imaging, cystic degeneration appears as anechoic or cystic areas within the mass on ultrasound, and as non-enhancing fluid collections on MRI. Cystic change can cause mass enlargement and produce compressive symptoms but usually lacks the intense inflammatory response seen in red degeneration.

Myxoid degeneration (also called myxomatous change) features accumulation of mucopolysaccharide-rich extracellular matrix between smooth muscle cells, giving a gelatinous appearance. Histology shows abundant basophilic mucoid material separating myocytes. Myxoid change can be focal or extensive; when extensive it may mimic sarcoma on imaging because of heterogeneous signal characteristics and rapid growth. Myxoid degeneration may predispose to cystic change or infection due to the altered extracellular milieu.

These degenerative patterns are not mutually exclusive; progressive ischemia often leads first to hyaline change, then cystic or myxoid transformation, and, if severe and acute, to red degeneration with hemorrhagic necrosis. Recognizing the expected histologic correlates helps interpret imaging and clinical signs.

# Vascular compromise leading to ischaemia and necrosis

Vascular compromise is central to most degenerative processes in fibroids. The blood supply of a fibroid derives from branches of uterine (and occasionally ovarian) arteries, but unlike the surrounding myometrium, fibroids often have relatively poor microvascular architecture and a propensity for venous stasis because of increased interstitial pressure and compressed intratumoral vessels as the mass enlarges.

Ischemia may be chronic or acute. Chronic suboptimal perfusion promotes hyaline and myxoid change through hypoxia-driven alterations in extracellular matrix turnover and cell death. Acute vascular events—venous thrombosis, arterial occlusion, or torsion of a pedicle—produce ischemic necrosis. Histologically, ischemia progresses from cell swelling and coagulative necrosis to infiltration by neutrophils and macrophages, eventual liquefaction, and organization. The time course of these events determines clinical presentation: chronic ischemia tends to cause gradual enlargement and nonspecific discomfort, while acute ischemia can produce severe localized pain, fever, and systemic inflammatory signs.

Pregnancy exacerbates vulnerability to vascular compromise. Increased uterine blood flow and rapid fibroid growth can outpace vascular development, and mechanical effects from uterine contractions or distortion of uterine architecture can cause venous outflow obstruction. Hormonal influences—especially elevated progesterone—may modify vascular tone and coagulation, increasing the likelihood of thrombosis within the fibroid. The physiologic hypercoagulable state of pregnancy further predisposes to intratumoral thrombotic events.

On imaging, vascular compromise may be suggested by absent or reduced internal flow on Doppler ultrasound, non-enhancement of portions of the mass on contrast MRI or CT, heterogeneous T2 signal with areas of hemorrhage, and peripheral rim enhancement if viable capsule remains. These features, combined with clinical signs, point to ischemic degeneration.

# Torsion of pedunculated subserosal fibroids: pathogenesis and clinical signs

Pedunculated subserosal fibroids project from the uterine serosal surface on a stalk (pedicle), which can undergo torsion. Torsion is a mechanical event in which rotation of the stalk impairs venous and arterial flow, producing acute ischemia of the fibroid. Pathogenesis is driven by several factors: a relatively long or narrow pedicle that permits rotation; increased uterine size or mobility (as in pregnancy); sudden changes in intra-abdominal pressure or body position; and adjacent pelvic organ movement.

The progression of vascular compromise after torsion commonly follows a venous-outflow obstruction pattern first—leading to congestion and hemorrhage—followed by arterial compromise and infarction. The timeline from torsion to complete necrosis can be rapid, producing an acute abdomen.

Clinically, torsion of a pedunculated fibroid typically presents with sudden, severe, focal abdominal or pelvic pain often accompanied by nausea, vomiting, and signs of peritoneal irritation if there is associated hemoperitoneum or inflammatory reaction. Fever and leukocytosis are common. The pain is frequently localized to the lower abdominal quadrant corresponding to the pedicle location and may be exacerbated by movement. On examination, there may be focal tenderness, a palpable pelvic mass, and occasionally peritoneal signs if necrosis elicits inflammation.

Diagnostic clues include the history of a known pedunculated fibroid, acute onset of pain, and imaging features: ultrasound may demonstrate a mass separate from the ovary with little or absent internal vascularity on Doppler; the pedicle may be visualized as a thin connection to the uterus. MRI is particularly useful, showing heterogeneous signal intensity with central non-enhancement and potential twisting of the pedicle (a “whirl sign” analogous to adnexal torsion). CT can identify a non-enhancing mass and associated inflammatory changes; in cases of hemorrhagic infarct, high-attenuation material may be seen. Because torsion can progress to sepsis or peritonitis, prompt recognition is essential.

Management decisions are guided by the severity of symptoms, gestational status, and imaging findings. In nonpregnant patients, surgical detorsion and resection (myomectomy) are often required. In pregnancy, conservative management with analgesia may be attempted for stable patients with mild symptoms, but persistent pain, peritonitis, or hemodynamic instability calls for surgical intervention, balancing maternal-fetal risks.

# Secondary infection of degenerating myomas

Degenerating fibroids are susceptible to secondary infection because necrotic tissue provides a nidus for microbial proliferation and because breakdown of tissue barriers and local vascular compromise impair immune cell access. Infection may complicate any severe degenerative process—particularly cystic or myxoid degeneration that creates fluid collections, or red degeneration where hemorrhagic necrosis is present.

Pathogens are typically polymicrobial and can include vaginal flora ascending into the necrotic tissue (anaerobes such as Bacteroides species, Prevotella, anaerobic streptococci), Enterobacteriaceae, and streptococci; in some cases skin flora or iatrogenic inoculation (after instrumentation) contributes. In pregnancy, the risk of ascending infection is increased by cervical dilation or membrane rupture, and obstetric pathogens (e.g., group A or B Streptococcus) may be involved.

Clinically, secondary infection should be suspected in a patient with a known fibroid who develops new or worsening localized pain, fever, rigors, malodorous vaginal discharge (if there is communication), leukocytosis, and elevated inflammatory markers. On examination there may be focal pelvic tenderness, fluctuance if an abscess has formed, and signs of systemic sepsis if infection is advanced.

Imaging is central to diagnosis: ultrasound may show complex cystic components with internal debris, gas echoes suggestive of anaerobic infection, and peripheral hyperemia. CT can better delineate gas within the lesion, rim-enhancing fluid collections, and surrounding inflammatory fat stranding. MRI provides superior soft-tissue characterization and can differentiate abscess (central restricted diffusion on diffusion-weighted imaging) from sterile necrosis.

Laboratory findings usually show leukocytosis (normal WBC: 4–11 × 10^9/L), elevated C-reactive protein (CRP; normal < 5 mg/L) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR; women: 0–20 mm/hr), and sometimes bacteremia. Blood cultures may be positive, particularly in fulminant infection.

Management principles include broad-spectrum antibiotics covering anaerobes and Gram-negative bacilli, image-guided drainage of abscess collections when accessible, and surgical resection of the infected fibroid when drainage is incomplete or the patient is unstable. In pregnant patients, the timing and mode of intervention must consider fetal maturity and maternal status; uncontrolled infection may threaten both patient and fetus and prompt urgent surgical management.

# Mass effect complications: urinary obstruction, bowel compression, respiratory compromise

Large fibroids can produce clinically important mass effects by exerting extrinsic compression on adjacent pelvic and abdominal organs. The severity of mass effect depends on fibroid size, location (subserosal versus intramural versus submucosal), and rate of growth. In pregnancy fibroids may increase in size and shift position, magnifying these risks.

Urinary tract obstruction: A fibroid that compresses the ureter—commonly a large lateral uterine or broad ligament fibroid—can cause unilateral or bilateral hydronephrosis and compromise renal function. Clinical manifestations include flank pain, recurrent urinary tract infections, and deterioration of renal function. Serum creatinine should be monitored (normal adult female serum creatinine approximately 45–90 μmol/L) when obstruction is suspected. Imaging with ultrasound can detect hydronephrosis; CT urography or MR urography better defines the level of obstruction and relationship to the mass. Persistent obstruction may require decompression—ureteral stenting or percutaneous nephrostomy—followed by definitive surgical removal of the compressing fibroid.

Bowel compression: Large anterior or posterior fibroids can cause constipation, fecal urgency, tenesmus, or rarely bowel obstruction if they impinge on the rectosigmoid colon. Symptoms may be progressive and relate to mass size and patient posture. On imaging, displacement or extrinsic compression of bowel loops is evident; significant obstruction may manifest with abdominal distension, vomiting, and radiographic signs of small or large bowel obstruction. Management includes bowel rest, decompression if necessary, and planning for myomectomy or hysterectomy if symptoms are refractory.

Respiratory compromise: Massive uterine enlargement from multiple or very large fibroids can elevate the diaphragm, reduce lung volumes, and precipitate dyspnea—this is most commonly an issue with giant fibroids occupying much of the abdominal cavity. Pregnancy can exacerbate this effect due to the enlarged gravid uterus. Clinical assessment of respiratory status and oxygenation, along with chest imaging when indicated, determines the need for intervention. Surgical reduction of uterine size may be required when conservative measures fail.

All mass-effect complications must be assessed in the context of patient stability and comorbidities. The decision to intervene surgically is driven by the severity of organ dysfunction, potential for irreversible damage (e.g., renal impairment), and the feasibility of temporizing measures (stenting, drainage).

# Potential for hemorrhage and hemodynamic instability

Fibroids can be associated with significant hemorrhage through several mechanisms: degeneration with secondary bleeding, rupture of superficial veins on the fibroid surface, intralesional bleeding (as in red degeneration), traumatic rupture of a pedunculated fibroid, or erosion into adjacent vascular structures. Additionally, associated vascular malformations or parasitic vascular supply from ovarian vessels can produce bleeding.

In pregnancy, increased vascularity and a hyperdynamic pelvic circulation heighten the risk. Rupture of a fibroid can lead to intraperitoneal hemorrhage with signs of hypovolemia—tachycardia, hypotension, pallor, diaphoresis, and acute abdomen—requiring rapid recognition and resuscitation. Massive bleeding can progress to hemorrhagic shock and coagulopathy; transfusion thresholds and resuscitation practices follow institutional and guideline-based protocols (e.g., transfuse red cells when hemoglobin is symptomatic or below target levels; consider massive transfusion protocols in ongoing hemorrhage).

Laboratory monitoring in suspected hemorrhage includes complete blood count (normal hemoglobin women 120–160 g/L), coagulation tests (prothrombin time, activated partial thromboplastin time), fibrinogen (normal 2–4 g/L), and arterial blood gases if unstable. Imaging with focused assessment with sonography for trauma (FAST) or abdominal ultrasound can quickly identify free fluid; CT angiography can localize active extravasation. In stable patients, uterine artery embolization can control bleeding by devascularizing the fibroid; in unstable patients, surgical exploration with myomectomy or hysterectomy may be necessary. In pregnancy, management must weigh fetal viability and maternal risk; intraoperative hemostatic techniques and multidisciplinary planning are critical.

# Biologic markers and inflammatory response associated with degeneration

Degenerative and infectious processes in fibroids elicit local and systemic inflammatory responses measurable by laboratory markers and cytokine profiles. Acute degenerative events—especially red degeneration and secondary infection—typically provoke neutrophilic leukocytosis (WBC > 11 × 10^9/L in many cases), elevated CRP concentrations, and increased ESR. Typical reference ranges include CRP < 5 mg/L, though values rise significantly with marked inflammation or infection. Procalcitonin can be useful in distinguishing bacterial infection from sterile inflammatory degeneration; procalcitonin levels rise in systemic bacterial infection and sepsis, whereas they are less elevated in sterile necrosis.

At the molecular level, ischemic and necrotic fibroid tissue upregulates proinflammatory mediators. Interleukin-6 (IL-6) commonly rises in systemic inflammation and stimulates hepatic production of acute phase reactants such as CRP and fibrinogen. Tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β) are also implicated in the local inflammatory milieu, promoting leukocyte recruitment and vascular permeability. These cytokines explain systemic manifestations such as fever, malaise, and anorexia. Elevated IL-6 and CRP correlate with symptom severity in some inflammatory gynecologic conditions and may help monitor response to therapy.

Microbiologic testing (blood cultures, aspirate cultures) assists in identifying infectious agents when infection is suspected. Gram stain and culture of drained purulent material guide targeted antibiotic therapy. Inflammatory markers and repeat imaging help evaluate treatment response. D-dimer and fibrin degradation products may be elevated in severe tissue necrosis or concurrent thrombotic events, but these are nonspecific.

Clinically, decision points include distinguishing sterile degeneration from infection. Key discriminants are presence of high-grade fever, rigors, purulent vaginal discharge, marked leukocytosis and procalcitonin elevation, imaging evidence of gas or rim-enhancing abscess, and bacteremia. Sterile red degeneration often presents with low-grade fever and less pronounced inflammatory marker elevation; antibiotics are not universally indicated unless secondary infection is suspected.

# Summary and clinical reasoning integration

Degenerative changes in uterine fibroids arise from a spectrum of vascular, mechanical, and biochemical disturbances that alter tissue viability and structure. Red, hyaline, cystic, and myxoid degeneration each have distinct histopathologic and imaging correlates and produce characteristic clinical syndromes. Vascular compromise—whether chronic ischemia, intratumoral thrombosis, or torsion of a pedicle—drives progression to necrosis and can precipitate pain, systemic inflammation, or secondary infection. Pedunculated subserosal fibroid torsion is a surgical emergency when it causes severe ischemia; secondary infection of necrotic myomas may require antibiotics and drainage in addition to definitive surgical therapy.

Large fibroids produce mass effects that may threaten renal, bowel, or respiratory function, and they carry a risk of hemorrhage severe enough to produce hemodynamic instability. Laboratory markers (WBC, CRP, procalcitonin, renal function tests, coagulation profile) and imaging (ultrasound, CT, MRI) are crucial to differentiate sterile degeneration from infection, to localize complications, and to guide urgency and type of intervention. Understanding the pathophysiology helps clinicians triage patients appropriately, select diagnostic tests that will most directly change management, and recognize when urgent surgical or radiologic interventions are required to prevent irreversible organ damage or life-threatening hemorrhage.

Introduction

Imaging of the pregnant patient with abdominal or pelvic symptoms poses unique diagnostic and safety challenges. Clinicians must balance the need for accurate characterization of masses or acute pathology against minimizing fetal risk from ionizing radiation, thermal effects and potentially teratogenic agents. Ultrasound and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) are the principal non-ionizing imaging modalities used in pregnancy. Ultrasound—transabdominal and transvaginal—remains the first-line modality for pelvic and many abdominal disorders because of its availability, real-time capability and lack of ionizing radiation. When ultrasound is inconclusive or additional tissue characterization is needed, non-contrast MRI is an excellent second-line modality because of its superior soft-tissue contrast and multiplanar capability without ionizing radiation. This chapter reviews practical techniques for optimizing ultrasound in pregnancy, sonographic appearances of common uterine and adnexal lesions, the role of color and power Doppler in assessing vascularity, MRI physics basics relevant to sequence selection and interpretation, MRI appearances of leiomyomas and adnexal lesions, safety considerations for MRI (including field strength and gadolinium), when CT may be considered and the fetal risks associated with ionizing radiation, and how to integrate imaging findings into clinical decision-making.

# Transabdominal and transvaginal ultrasound techniques and optimization in pregnancy

Ultrasound in pregnancy requires deliberate adjustment of technique to compensate for gravid anatomy and the goals of the examination. Transabdominal (TA) ultrasound uses a low-frequency curved-array transducer (typically 2–5 MHz) to evaluate large fields of view including the uterus, adnexa and the upper pelvis; transvaginal (TV) ultrasound uses a higher-frequency endovaginal probe (typically 5–9 MHz) to provide high-resolution images of pelvic structures close to the probe. Both approaches are often complementary: TA for overall survey and for larger masses or when the uterus has been displaced, TV for high-resolution assessment of uterine contour, endometrium/cervical canal and adnexal detail.

When performing TA ultrasound in pregnancy, patient positioning and probe selection are essential. A comfortably full bladder can be helpful in early pregnancy to act as an acoustic window, displacing bowel gas and allowing visualization of the pelvic organs; however, in later pregnancy a full bladder is usually unnecessary and can be uncomfortable. Probe frequency should be chosen to balance penetration and resolution; in obese patients or with deep-seated lesions a lower frequency may be required. Scan planes should be systematic: midline sagittal and multiple transverse images from fundus to cervix, and additional oblique planes as anatomy dictates. Sweeps should include coronal oblique views when evaluating adnexal masses that sit lateral to the uterus.

Transvaginal ultrasound provides superior spatial resolution for structures within the pelvis and should be used whenever detailed characterization of the adnexa or junctional zone is required and when patient tolerance permits. The TV probe is inserted into the anterior vagina with the patient in lithotomy position. Gentle systematic sweeps in sagittal and transverse orientations are used to inspect each ovary, the uterine fundus, corpus and cervix. Doppler interrogation is typically performed through the TV approach for small vascular structures.

Optimization strategies for image quality include adjusting gain, dynamic range, focal zones and depth. Reduce depth to place the structure of interest in the center of the image and set focal zones at or just below the structure to sharpen resolution. Time-gain compensation can help compensate for depth-dependent attenuation. If tissue-harmonic imaging is available, it often improves image contrast and reduces near-field artifacts in TA imaging. For TV studies, decreasing the field of view and increasing line density increases spatial resolution.

Patient safety and comfort considerations specific to pregnancy include minimizing mechanical index (MI) and thermal index (TI) where possible—both of which are displayed on modern ultrasound units. Although diagnostic ultrasound has an excellent safety record, the principle of ALARA (as low as reasonably achievable) applies. Avoid prolonged dwell time with spectral Doppler over the fetal head or abdomen, and limit high-power Doppler exposure in early pregnancy.

# Sonographic features of fibroids, endometriomas and ovarian cysts

Recognizing typical sonographic patterns of common pelvic masses reduces diagnostic uncertainty and guides management. Leiomyomas (uterine fibroids), endometriomas and functional or benign ovarian cysts are frequent in reproductive-age women and may coexist with pregnancy.

Leiomyomas. On ultrasound, leiomyomas are generally well-circumscribed, round-to-oval, solid masses arising from the myometrium. Classic sonographic features include a heterogeneous, hypoechoic mass compared with surrounding myometrium, with a well-defined pseudocapsule. Calcifications produce echogenic foci with posterior acoustic shadowing. Subserosal fibroids may be pedunculated and located external to the uterine contour; submucosal fibroids project into the endometrial cavity and can be better characterized with TV ultrasound or saline sonohysterography when not pregnant. In pregnancy, fibroids can change in size (typically increase slightly) because of pregnancy hormones and vascular changes; degenerative changes occur and alter sonographic appearance. Cystic degeneration produces mixed cystic and solid echotexture; red degeneration (hemorrhagic infarction) can produce central hypoechoic or heterogeneous areas with variable echogenicity depending on the age of hemorrhage. Distinguishing degenerated fibroids from adnexal pathology can be challenging; their uterine attachment and continuity of myometrial tissue help identify them.

Endometriomas. Endometriomas (ovarian "chocolate cysts") typically have a characteristic sonographic appearance: a unilocular or multilocular cystic ovarian mass with diffuse low-level internal echoes, often described as “ground-glass” echogenicity. Thin septations may be present; solid mural nodules are uncommon and, when present, should prompt careful evaluation because they raise concern for neoplasm. Endometriomas are usually avascular or have low peripheral vascularity on Doppler. They are commonly multiple and bilateral. In pregnancy their appearance may remain stable or change due to altered hormonal milieu.

Ovarian cysts. Functional ovarian cysts seen in reproductive-age women include follicular cysts and corpus luteum cysts. Follicular cysts are typically anechoic, unilocular and thin-walled with posterior acoustic enhancement. Corpus luteum cysts can be more complex with thicker walls, internal echoes and peripheral vascularity (“ring of fire” on color Doppler) and may measure several centimeters. Hemorrhagic cysts exhibit internal reticular, lace-like echogenic strands and may demonstrate fluid-fluid levels; acute hemorrhage can be echogenic and more solid-appearing. Dermoid cysts (mature cystic teratomas) often show heterogeneous echotexture with echogenic Rokitansky protuberances, dermoid mesh (linear hyperechoic interfaces), calcifications and posterior acoustic shadowing due to fat or bone. Simple cysts are usually benign if <5–10 cm and without concerning features. Complex cystic adnexal masses with solid components, papillary projections, nodularity, thick septations >3 mm, or increased vascularity on Doppler raise suspicion for neoplasm and warrant further evaluation.

# Role of color and power Doppler in characterizing vascularity

Color and power Doppler ultrasound provide physiologic information about blood flow that complements gray-scale morphology. Color Doppler encodes flow direction and velocity, while power Doppler maps total flow amplitude and is more sensitive to low-velocity flow but lacks directional information. Spectral Doppler can quantify flow velocities and waveform characteristics (e.g., peak systolic velocity, end-diastolic velocity, resistive index).

In pelvic imaging during pregnancy, Doppler is used to assess viability of adnexal structures (e.g., torsion), vascularity of masses to help differentiate benign from malignant lesions, and to examine uterine or placental blood flow when relevant. For adnexal torsion, absence or markedly reduced venous and arterial flow within the ovary supports the diagnosis, but normal Doppler flow does not exclude torsion because of dual ovarian arterial supply and early arterial flow preservation. Increasing ovarian enlargement with peripheral follicles, stromal edema and decreased venous outflow are complementary grayscale signs.

Doppler indices can assist in lesion characterization. Benign lesions such as hemorrhagic cysts, endometriomas and simple cysts tend to have low-flow or peripheral flow patterns with relatively high resistive indices (RI). Malignant ovarian neoplasms often demonstrate increased central vascularity with low RI (typically RI < 0.4–0.5) and low pulsatility index (PI), reflecting neoangiogenesis. However, overlap exists: corpus luteum cysts can have prominent peripheral flow with low RI (“ring of fire”), and inflammatory masses can be hypervascular. Power Doppler is particularly useful for detecting low-flow within solid components or septations and for mapping tumor vascularity, whereas color Doppler helps localize direction and turbulent flow.

Technical factors influence Doppler sensitivity: angle of insonation, pulse repetition frequency (PRF), wall filter settings, gain and probe frequency. To detect low-velocity flow reduce PRF, use low wall filters and increase color gain carefully to avoid noise. Power Doppler is preferred when flow is weak or in obese patients. Always document Doppler indices (peak systolic velocity, end-diastolic velocity, RI, PI) when using spectral Doppler as part of an objective record.

Doppler use in early pregnancy should adhere to ALARA principles; avoid prolonged spectral Doppler sampling over the fetal head and keep MI and TI as low as practical.

# MRI physics basics relevant to T1, T2, fat-saturation sequences and interpretation

Understanding basic MRI physics is important for selecting sequences and interpreting pelvic and abdominal pathology. MRI signal depends primarily on proton density and relaxation properties of tissues: longitudinal relaxation (T1) and transverse relaxation (T2). Sequences are designed to emphasize differences in these relaxation times.

T1-weighted sequences produce images in which fat has high signal (bright) and fluid is relatively low signal (dark). T1-weighted imaging is useful for detecting fat, subacute blood (methemoglobin), and assessing anatomy with good contrast between fat and soft tissues. T2-weighted sequences make fluid bright and are sensitive to edema, cystic components and inflammation. Most pelvic MRI protocols for characterization include both T1-weighted and T2-weighted sequences in multiple planes.

Fat-suppression techniques selectively reduce the high signal from fat to unmask other high-signal components such as fluid or blood. Chemical fat saturation (frequency-selective) exploits the difference in precessional frequency between fat and water protons; it works well at homogenous field conditions but can be less effective in regions with B0 inhomogeneity. Short tau inversion recovery (STIR) is an inversion recovery method that nulls fat signal and is relatively robust to inhomogeneity but is not compatible with gadolinium contrast evaluation because it also suppresses contrast-enhanced fat. Dixon methods (in-phase and out-of-phase imaging with subsequent reconstruction) allow both water-only and fat-only images and provide useful fat quantification and reliable fat-suppression across varying field conditions.

Other sequences commonly used include gradient-echo sequences for dynamic or cine imaging, diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) for assessing cellularity and restricted diffusion (helpful in characterizing malignant tissue or abscess), and susceptibility-weighted imaging which accentuates blood products and calcifications via magnetic susceptibility differences. DWI sequences produce apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) maps which are quantitative; tissues with restricted diffusion (low ADC) suggest high cellularity (e.g., many malignancies) or acute hemorrhage.

Important technical considerations include the specific absorption rate (SAR), which reflects energy deposition in tissue and is proportional to field strength and sequence parameters (flip angle, TR, etc.). Higher field strengths (e.g., 3 Tesla) produce higher SAR and potentially greater tissue heating—an important safety consideration in pregnancy. B0 (main magnetic field) and B1 (radiofrequency) inhomogeneities impact fat-saturation performance and image uniformity; sequence selection should account for these effects.

Interpretation principles: T1-hyperintense signal may indicate fat or subacute blood (methemoglobin), or proteinaceous content. T2-hyperintense signal generally indicates fluid or edema; T2 hypointensity in the myometrium or a lesion often reflects compact smooth muscle or fibrous tissue (e.g., leiomyoma). Combining multiple sequences (T1, T2, fat-sat, DWI) allows differentiating hemorrhagic, fatty, cystic and solid components.

# MRI features of leiomyomas, cystic degeneration, hemorrhage and adnexal lesions

MRI provides excellent tissue characterization of uterine and adnexal pathology and is particularly helpful when ultrasound is limited by body habitus, gravid uterus or complex appearances.

Leiomyomas. On MRI, typical leiomyomas are well-circumscribed masses arising from the myometrium with low-to-intermediate signal intensity on T2-weighted images relative to normal myometrium, and low-to-intermediate signal on T1-weighted images. The low T2 signal reflects compact smooth muscle and variable collagen. Degenerative changes alter signal characteristics: cystic degeneration produces high T2 signal centrally (reflecting fluid) with correspondingly low T1 signal; myxoid degeneration shows heterogeneous T2 hyperintensity. Red degeneration (hemorrhagic infarction) produces high signal on T1-weighted images due to intracellular and extracellular methemoglobin and variable T2 signal depending on stage. Adenomyosis is characterized by thickening of the junctional zone (>12 mm in many criteria) with small high-signal foci on T2 representing ectopic endometrial glands or small hemorrhagic foci; distinguishing small intramural fibroids from adenomyosis can be aided by evaluating the junctional zone contour and architecture.

Cystic degeneration within fibroids can produce large central areas of high T2 signal and variable T1 signal. When a fibroid undergoes hyaline degeneration, it may remain T2 hypointense but with increased heterogeneity. Pedunculated subserosal fibroids are identified by a pedicle connecting them to the uterus; this connection helps differentiate them from adnexal masses.

Hemorrhage. MRI is sensitive to blood products at different stages. Acute hemorrhage (oxyhemoglobin) may be isointense on T1 and hyperintense on T2. Subacute blood with intracellular methemoglobin becomes T1 hyperintense. Chronic blood products such as hemosiderin cause susceptibility effects and T2 hypointensity, which may be best seen on T2* or gradient-echo sequences. In the pelvis, hemorrhagic cysts (e.g., hemorrhagic ovarian cyst) demonstrate high T1 signal due to blood and variable T2 signal; fat-saturation sequences confirm whether T1 hyperintensity is due to fat (suppressed) or blood (not suppressed).

Adnexal lesions. MRI differentiates simple cysts, endometriomas, dermoids, and solid neoplasms. Simple cysts are uniformly T2 hyperintense and T1 hypointense, with no restricted diffusion or enhancement (in post-contrast studies). Endometriomas typically show high signal on T1-weighted images due to blood products that do not suppress with fat-sat, and variable shading on T2—often hypointense ("T2 shading") because of concentrated blood degradation products and protein. Dermoid cysts (mature teratomas) contain macroscopic fat and therefore show high T1 signal that suppresses with fat-saturation, and often show calcifications (signal voids) or Rokitansky protuberances. Ovarian torsion manifests as ovarian enlargement, peripheral displacement of follicles, stromal edema (T2 hyperintensity), and abnormal or absent perfusion on contrast-enhanced sequences; on DWI there may be restricted diffusion due to ischemia. Malignant ovarian masses often have solid components that are intermediate on T1 and variable on T2, may show restricted diffusion on DWI with low ADC values, and—when contrast is used—early enhancing solid components. In pregnancy, MRI protocols are usually non-contrast, so reliance on T1/T2 characteristics and DWI becomes more important.

# Safety of MRI in pregnancy: field strength considerations, contrast agents (gadolinium) and risks

MRI is generally considered safe in pregnancy when non-contrast techniques are used, but several safety issues require consideration: static magnetic field exposure, radiofrequency (RF) heating, acoustic noise, and the use of gadolinium-based contrast agents (GBCAs).

Field strength. Most clinical MRI in pregnancy is performed at 1.5 Tesla (T); 3T scanners provide higher signal-to-noise ratio and potentially improved spatial resolution but also increase SAR (and thus potential tissue heating) and may increase B1 inhomogeneity. Current guidelines suggest that MRI at 1.5T is safe throughout pregnancy for clinically indicated examinations. MRI at 3T can be used when the diagnostic benefit outweighs potential risks, but care should be taken to minimize SAR and monitor sequence parameters. In general, avoid nonessential high-SAR sequences (e.g., fast spin-echo with high flip angles) particularly in early pregnancy. The decision should involve discussion with radiology physicists for complex protocols.

RF heating and SAR. RF energy deposited during MRI can theoretically heat tissues. The fetus has immature thermoregulation, and temperature elevations are a concern. Scanner consoles display SAR estimates. Clinical practice aims to keep thermal index low and adhere to manufacturer recommendations. Shorter scan times, lower flip angles, and use of sequences with lower energy deposition reduce SAR.

Acoustic noise. MRI acoustic noise (rapid gradient switching) can be loud. There is limited evidence that acoustic noise could affect the fetal auditory system, especially during organogenesis, although most centers consider this risk low. Ear protection is impractical for the fetus; noise reduction by using quieter sequences and allowing maternal acoustic protection (headphones/earplugs) is reasonable.

Gadolinium-based contrast agents. GBCAs cross the placenta and enter fetal circulation; they are then excreted into the amniotic fluid and remain for prolonged periods. Animal studies have suggested potential fetal risks, and human observational data have raised concerns about an association between gadolinium exposure in pregnancy and a small increase in the risk of stillbirth, neonatal death, or certain inflammatory or rheumatologic conditions in early childhood. For these reasons, the general consensus and guidelines recommend avoiding gadolinium during pregnancy unless absolutely necessary—i.e., when the diagnostic information obtained with contrast will influence management and cannot be obtained by non-contrast MRI or ultrasound. If gadolinium is deemed essential, the lowest effective dose of a macrocyclic GBCA (which is more stable and less likely to release free gadolinium than linear agents) should be used. Informed consent documenting the risks and benefits is recommended. There is no routine recommendation for chelation or other interventions after in utero exposure.

Summary of practical safety points: use non-contrast MRI whenever possible; prefer 1.5T when diagnostic yield is adequate; tailor sequences to minimize SAR and scan duration; document indications and counseling if gadolinium is administered.

# When cross-sectional imaging (CT) is considered and associated fetal risks

Computed tomography (CT) uses ionizing radiation and is generally avoided in pregnancy for pelvic imaging when ultrasound and MRI are available. Nonetheless, CT has roles when rapid evaluation of certain acute conditions is necessary and when expected diagnostic benefit outweighs fetal radiation risk—for example, in suspected life-threatening maternal hemorrhage, complex trauma, pulmonary embolism when ventilation–perfusion scanning is unavailable or inconclusive, or when CT angiography is the fastest way to guide urgent intervention.

Radiation risk considerations revolve around stochastic effects (increased lifetime cancer risk) and deterministic effects (threshold-dose effects such as intellectual disability or malformations). Deterministic effects have threshold doses typically greater than 100 mGy. Most diagnostic CT examinations of the abdomen and pelvis deliver fetal doses far below this threshold. Typical fetal radiation exposures: a single abdominal/pelvic CT commonly results in fetal doses in the range of 10–50 mGy depending on technique, scanner, and maternal habitus, although there is wide variability. A fetal dose <50 mGy is generally not associated with a measurable increase in deterministic effects, but stochastic risk (small increase in childhood cancer risk) is proportional to dose and must be discussed. By comparison, radiation dose from a single chest CT is much lower to the pelvis and yields minimal fetal exposure unless imaging includes the abdomen/pelvis.

When CT is necessary, strategies to minimize fetal dose include limiting the scan range, shielding when possible (though internal fetal dose is not reduced by external shielding), using iterative reconstruction and dose-reduction algorithms, reducing tube voltage and current consistent with diagnostic quality, and avoiding multiphase imaging unless essential. Always document estimated fetal dose and provide counseling regarding risks. Repeat or multiple CT examinations increase cumulative dose and thus risks.

In pregnant patients, contrast-enhanced CT uses iodinated contrast agents that cross the placenta; current data do not show teratogenicity, but neonatal thyroid function may be transiently affected because of iodine load. Many guidelines recommend measuring neonatal thyroid function if significant iodinated contrast exposure occurred near delivery.

Overall, CT is reserved for situations where alternative imaging cannot provide timely or sufficient information for maternal care; when maternal indication is strong, maternal wellbeing takes precedence because maternal compromise poses the greatest risk to the fetus.

# Integration of imaging findings into clinical decision-making

Integration of imaging results into clinical management requires synthesis of sonographic and MRI data together with clinical presentation, laboratory values and gestational age. The approach to a pregnant patient with a pelvic mass or abdominal pain should be patient-centered, prioritizing maternal stabilization and then fetal considerations.

First-line evaluation typically begins with tailored ultrasound. If ultrasound clearly identifies a benign-appearing simple cyst (<5–10 cm with thin walls and anechoic content) in an asymptomatic patient, conservative management and follow-up imaging are often appropriate. Presence of acute pain, a very large adnexal mass, complex features (solid components, papillary projections, thick septations), or signs suggesting torsion (enlarged ovary, peripheral follicles, absent venous/arterial flow) prompt urgent gynecologic consultation and potentially surgical intervention. Doppler findings of absent or severely diminished flow support surgical consideration, but a normal Doppler does not exclude torsion—clinical suspicion remains paramount.

For uterine fibroids that are asymptomatic or cause mild symptoms, expectant management during pregnancy is common. When degeneration causes severe pain, supportive care is frequently effective; imaging helps exclude alternative causes. MRI is used when ultrasound is nondiagnostic—for example, to evaluate large or atypical uterine masses, define the location (submucosal vs intramural vs subserosal), or assess for red degeneration. MRI findings consistent with hemorrhagic degeneration (T1 hyperintensity) can explain acute pain and guide conservative therapy rather than immediate surgery.

When adnexal masses have concerning imaging features for neoplasm, MRI without contrast provides additional characterization: T1 hyperintensity that suppresses on fat-sat suggests a dermoid; persistent T1 hyperintensity not suppressed by fat-sat suggests blood (endometrioma); solid enhancing components (if contrast is used postpartum or in non-pregnant patient) and restricted diffusion raise suspicion for malignancy. If malignancy is suspected in pregnancy, multidisciplinary discussion including obstetrics, gynecologic oncology, neonatology and radiology is required to plan timing of intervention that balances maternal oncologic needs with fetal maturity.

Decision thresholds and specific interventions depend on gestational age. For example, in late pregnancy a mass may be managed expectantly until fetal viability or elective cesarean with concurrent surgical management. In early pregnancy suspected torsion or hemorrhage may demand urgent laparoscopy or laparotomy. Imaging informs these decisions: a small simple cyst in an asymptomatic first-trimester patient may be observed; a large complex mass causing pain or hemoperitoneum requires urgent action.

Documenting imaging limitations is essential: ultrasound may not visualize the entire pelvis when the gravid uterus displaces structures; MRI is superior for tissue characterization but typically performed without contrast in pregnancy. Radiology reports should explicitly state confidence levels, potential differential diagnoses, and recommended next steps (e.g., short-interval follow-up ultrasound, non-contrast MRI, or urgent surgical consultation).

Finally, counseling patients requires clear communication of risks and benefits. Discuss the significance of findings, the rationale for additional imaging (if needed), and potential maternal or fetal risks of imaging and any recommended procedures. When ionizing radiation or gadolinium is considered, provide documented informed consent and involve multidisciplinary teams.

Conclusion

Ultrasound and non-contrast MRI are complementary, safe, and powerful modalities for evaluating abdominal and pelvic masses in pregnancy when used thoughtfully. Mastery of transabdominal and transvaginal techniques plus appropriate use of Doppler optimizes ultrasound diagnostic yield. Knowledge of typical sonographic and MRI appearances of leiomyomas, endometriomas and adnexal lesions, together with MRI physics concepts (T1, T2, fat-sat, DWI) enables accurate lesion characterization and appropriate management planning. Safety considerations—especially regarding gadolinium and ionizing radiation—must guide imaging choices, always weighing diagnostic benefits against theoretical fetal risks. Integration of imaging findings with clinical context is essential for timely, patient-centered decision-making.

# Introduction

Evaluation of adnexal masses and suspected malignancy in reproductive-age and pregnant patients relies on integration of clinical history, imaging, and laboratory tests. Serum tumor markers can provide supportive information but are affected by physiologic and pathologic states common in pregnancy. This chapter reviews commonly used serum tumor markers and inflammatory laboratory tests in pregnancy, emphasizes physiologic alterations and benign causes of marker elevation—particularly CA-125—details interpretation pitfalls for single versus serial measurements, and places sensitivity/specificity of markers in the context of reproductive-age and pregnant patients. The goal is to equip the reader with pragmatic, evidence-based reasoning to interpret tumor marker results appropriately and to avoid overreliance on laboratory values alone.

# Commonly used markers: CA-125, CA 19-9, CEA, CA 15-3, LDH, beta-hCG contexts

Several serum markers are used in the evaluation of adnexal masses and gynecologic malignancies. Each has characteristic associations, strengths, and limitations.

CA-125
- CA-125 (cancer antigen 125) is a high-molecular-weight glycoprotein expressed by coelomic- and Mullerian-derived epithelia (ovary, fallopian tube, endometrium, peritoneum, pleura). It is most frequently used in ovarian epithelial carcinoma evaluation. The commonly quoted normal upper limit is 35 U/mL, but this cutpoint was derived from postmenopausal cohorts and has limited specificity in premenopausal women. CA-125 is the principal serum marker used in risk stratification models such as the Risk of Malignancy Index (RMI).

CA 19-9
- CA 19-9 is a carbohydrate antigen associated with gastrointestinal malignancies—most pertinently mucinous ovarian tumors when considering adnexal masses. The customary normal value is <37 U/mL. CA 19-9 may be elevated in benign gastrointestinal disease, pancreatitis, cholestasis, and cystic ovarian neoplasms, so elevations require contextual interpretation.

CEA
- Carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) is an oncofetal glycoprotein associated with colorectal cancer and mucinous tumors. Reference ranges vary; typical upper limits are ~3 ng/mL for nonsmokers and ~5 ng/mL for smokers. In adnexal mass workup, increased CEA raises concern for metastatic gastrointestinal primary or mucinous ovarian neoplasm.

CA 15-3
- CA 15-3 is linked to breast carcinoma and can be relevant when adnexal masses represent metastatic disease from breast cancer. The usual upper limit is ~30 U/mL. Clinical use is principally for monitoring known disease rather than initial diagnosis.

LDH
- Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) is a nonspecific marker of cell turnover and tissue injury. In gynecologic oncology, LDH is classically associated with dysgerminoma (a germ cell tumor) which often shows marked LDH elevation. Reference ranges depend on assay and laboratory, commonly approximately 140–280 U/L. Because LDH is widely distributed, elevations can arise from hemolysis, hepatic injury, muscle injury, and pregnancy-related physiology.

beta-hCG
- Beta-human chorionic gonadotropin (beta-hCG) is produced by trophoblasts and is the primary pregnancy hormone. It is also produced ectopically by certain germ cell tumors (e.g., choriocarcinoma, embryonal carcinoma). In nonpregnant individuals, beta-hCG is <5 mIU/mL. In pregnancy, values vary dramatically with gestational age: first-trimester levels rise rapidly and may peak in the first trimester at mean concentrations on the order of $10^4$–$10^5$ mIU/mL depending on gestational age and assay. When interpreting beta-hCG in an adnexal mass context, consider both physiologic pregnancy levels and tumor-associated ectopic production.

Clinical application
- No single marker is diagnostic. Markers are most useful when guided by clinical pretest probability and imaging features. For example, markedly elevated CA-125 in a postmenopausal woman with a complex solid adnexal mass is much more worrisome than a similar level in a first-trimester pregnant patient with a simple cyst. Conversely, an elevated LDH in a young woman with a solid ovarian mass raises suspicion for dysgerminoma and can redirect management toward fertility-sparing surgery.

# Physiologic variation of CA-125 across trimesters and causes of elevation in benign conditions

CA-125 levels are notably variable in reproductive-age women and are particularly labile during pregnancy. Understanding trimester-specific patterns and common benign causes of elevation is crucial to avoid misclassification.

Trimester variation
- First trimester: CA-125 often increases in early pregnancy. Several studies show median and mean CA-125 values often exceed the conventional upper limit (35 U/mL) in the first trimester, especially around the time of implantation and early placental development. Mechanisms proposed include decidual and endometrial remodeling, trophoblastic invasion, peritoneal irritation, and shedding of Mullerian-derived epithelium resulting in transient peritoneal antigen exposure.
- Second trimester: Levels generally decline during the second trimester and approach nonpregnant lower values. Many pregnant women have CA-125 within the reference range during mid-pregnancy.
- Third trimester and postpartum: CA-125 may rise again in late pregnancy and in the immediate postpartum period, likely reflecting placental separation, increased peritoneal fluid, and tissue remodeling at delivery.

Reported ranges
- Because of heterogeneity in study populations and assays, precise reference ranges in pregnancy vary. Some studies report that up to 20–40% of first-trimester pregnancies have CA-125 above 35 U/mL. Markedly elevated values (e.g., >200 U/mL) can occur with benign processes but are less common.

Benign causes of CA-125 elevation
- Endometriosis: Endometriotic implants and peritoneal inflammation commonly raise CA-125.
- Uterine leiomyomas (fibroids): Large fibroids—particularly those with degeneration—can elevate CA-125.
- Pelvic inflammatory disease, tubo-ovarian abscess, and recent pelvic surgery: Any peritoneal inflammation or irritation increases antigen expression and leakage into the circulation.
- Menstruation and ovulation: Physiologic cyclic shedding increases CA-125 transiently during menses and sometimes around ovulation.
- Ascites, pleural effusion, peritonitis: Serosal inflammation elevates CA-125.
- Pregnancy-related causes: First-trimester decidual changes, ectopic pregnancy, hydatidiform mole, and placental abruption can elevate CA-125.

Pathophysiology
- CA-125 is expressed on mesothelial and Mullerian epithelium; disruption or inflammation of these surfaces increases antigen release. Peritoneal inflammation enhances antigen diffusion into systemic circulation. In pregnancy, trophoblastic invasion and decidual reaction transiently increase antigen expression and peritoneal exposure, especially in early gestation and after delivery.

Clinical implication
- Because CA-125 lacks specificity in pregnancy and reproductive-age women, elevated values should prompt careful correlation with imaging, clinical signs of infection or endometriosis, timing in relation to menses or pregnancy, and consideration of alternative markers or serial measurements rather than immediate assumption of malignancy.

# Interpretation pitfalls of single vs serial measurements in pregnancy

Interpreting tumor marker data requires appreciation of biological variability, assay variability, and disease dynamics. This is particularly true in pregnant patients.

Pitfalls of single measurements
- A single elevated value may reflect physiologic variation, transient inflammation, assay interference, or laboratory error. In pregnancy, a first-trimester CA-125 above 35 U/mL is common and often benign. Relying on a single CA-125 to triage a pregnant patient to aggressive interventions can lead to unnecessary morbidity.
- Thresholds derived from nonpregnant populations do not translate directly to pregnant patients. For example, many risk prediction models that include CA-125 (e.g., RMI) were validated in nonpregnant cohorts and lose accuracy in pregnancy.

Benefits and considerations for serial measurements
- Serial measurement can help establish a trend. In ovarian epithelial cancers, CA-125 typically rises progressively; in contrast, benign causes often produce fluctuating or transient elevations. Demonstration of a persistent and rising CA-125 over time increases the pretest probability of malignancy.
- When using serial markers, consider assay consistency (use the same laboratory and methodology), timing relative to pregnancy events (implantation, miscarriage, delivery), and potential confounders (infection, hemorrhage).
- Quantitative kinetics can assist clinical reasoning. For instance, the half-life of CA-125 after tumor debulking is reported around 10 days; for malignant processes, doubling times vary widely but sustained increases are more concerning than single excursions. However, there are no universally validated kinetic cutoffs in pregnancy.

Specific scenarios
- Rising CA-125 in the second trimester, in concert with an enlarging complex adnexal mass on ultrasound, may warrant expedited evaluation.
- A high CA-125 in the first trimester with a simple cyst and no concerning ultrasound features may be best managed expectantly with repeat measurement later in pregnancy.
- For markers that are strictly tumor-associated (e.g., beta-hCG produced by choriocarcinoma), absolute values and trends are more interpretable but must still be considered within pregnancy physiology.

Laboratory and preanalytic considerations
- Hemolysis and sample handling can falsely elevate or interfere with some assays. Heterophile antibodies and human anti-mouse antibodies can produce spurious results in immunoassays. When results are discordant with clinical findings, repeat testing with a different platform or laboratory can be informative.

# Sensitivity and specificity considerations for malignancy in reproductive-age and pregnant patients

Understanding test performance metrics—sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV)—is essential. These metrics depend on disease prevalence and patient population.

Influence of reproductive age and pregnancy on test performance
- In postmenopausal women, CA-125 has better specificity for epithelial ovarian cancer because benign gynecologic conditions that raise CA-125 are less prevalent. In premenopausal women, benign causes of elevation (endometriosis, fibroids, menstruation, pregnancy) are common, decreasing specificity and reducing PPV.
- Pregnancy reduces the specificity of CA-125 further, particularly in the first trimester, because physiologic elevations are common.

Reported performance characteristics
- CA-125 sensitivity for epithelial ovarian cancer varies by stage; it is more sensitive for advanced-stage disease. Estimates in mixed-age cohorts report sensitivities around 80% for advanced disease but lower (~50–60%) for early-stage disease. Specificity in premenopausal populations can be as low as 60–70% using the 35 U/mL cutoff; specificity is higher in postmenopausal women (>90% in some series).
- When evaluating adnexal masses, combinations of markers and imaging scores (e.g., IOTA models, RMI) outperform single markers. RMI uses CA-125 multiplied by ultrasound score and menopausal status: RMI = U × M × CA-125, with commonly used cutoffs to stratify malignancy risk.
- For germ cell tumors and other rarer entities, marker sensitivities vary: LDH is sensitive for dysgerminoma but is nonspecific; beta-hCG has high sensitivity for choriocarcinoma and some embryonal carcinomas but is irrelevant for epithelial ovarian cancer.

Predictive values and prevalence
- PPV and NPV depend on disease prevalence. In a low-prevalence setting (young pregnant women), even a test with modest specificity yields many false positives and a low PPV. Thus, elevated tumor markers in pregnancy are more likely to be false positives than true malignancy.

Clinical thresholds and decision points
- No absolute cutoff excludes or confirms malignancy. However, markedly elevated results (e.g., CA-125 >200–500 U/mL) are more suspicious in the appropriate clinical context, though exceptions exist (massive ascites from benign liver disease or peritonitis can raise CA-125 dramatically).
- Use markers to modulate clinical suspicion rather than as definitive tests: integrate marker levels with imaging morphology, symptoms, and progression over time.

# Laboratory markers of infection and inflammation (WBC, CRP, procalcitonin) in pregnancy

Laboratory markers of systemic inflammation and infection—complete blood count (CBC) with white blood cell (WBC) count, C-reactive protein (CRP), and procalcitonin—help distinguish inflammatory or infectious causes of adnexal symptoms and can explain tumor marker elevations due to inflammation.

WBC
- Nonpregnant normal range: approximately 4,000–11,000 cells/µL.
- Physiologic changes in pregnancy: leukocytosis is common, especially in the third trimester and the immediate postpartum period. WBC counts of 12,000–15,000 cells/µL can be normal in late pregnancy; values up to 20,000 cells/µL may be seen with labor. A left shift (increased neutrophils) is common but must be interpreted in clinical context.
- Clinical implication: modest leukocytosis without clinical signs of infection is common and not diagnostic. Rising WBC with fever, localized signs, or markedly elevated inflammatory markers suggests infection.

CRP
- Typical reference range: <10 mg/L (laboratory-dependent).
- Pregnancy effect: CRP may be mildly elevated in pregnancy, though most pregnant women have CRP values within or slightly above the standard reference range. CRP is a sensitive marker of inflammation but not specific for infection.
- Clinical implication: an elevated CRP supports an inflammatory or infectious process, particularly when rising over serial measurements or accompanied by clinical signs. CRP is not diagnostic of malignancy but can help differentiate inflammatory causes of tumor marker elevation.

Procalcitonin
- Baseline noninfected reference: <0.05 ng/mL; values >0.5 ng/mL suggest bacterial infection in many clinical settings.
- Pregnancy effect: procalcitonin is less influenced by pregnancy than CRP or WBC, making it potentially more specific for bacterial infection. However, data are limited in pregnancy, and thresholds may need adjustment.
- Clinical implication: procalcitonin can aid in distinguishing bacterial infection from sterile inflammation or malignancy-related markers. Elevated procalcitonin in a pregnant patient with an adnexal mass, fever, and leukocytosis supports infectious etiologies such as tubo-ovarian abscess.

Integrative use
- When CA-125 is elevated, concomitant inflammatory markers can suggest whether peritoneal inflammation/infection is the likely driver. For example, an elevated CA-125 with markedly raised CRP/procalcitonin and clinical fever favors PID or abscess over malignancy. Conversely, isolated CA-125 elevation without inflammatory marker abnormalities and with suspicious imaging warrants further oncologic evaluation.

# Role of tumor markers as adjuncts, not diagnostic by themselves

Fundamentally, tumor markers are adjuncts that refine clinical probability but do not replace history, examination, imaging, and sometimes tissue diagnosis.

Principles of adjunctive use
- Pretest probability: The utility of any test is determined by the pretest probability of disease. In low-prevalence populations (pregnant women), even moderately sensitive or specific tests have limited PPV.
- Triaging and monitoring: Tumor markers can guide triage—whether to observe, obtain advanced imaging, or proceed to surgery—and can be used for postoperative surveillance when a tumor is biopsy-proven. In pregnancy, markers may help decide timing of intervention (urgent vs. delayed) by showing stability versus progression.
- Complementary to imaging: Imaging provides morphological information; markers add biologic activity context. For example, a complex solid adnexal mass with rising CA-125 increases concern; a simple cyst with stable low-level CA-125 is reassuring.

Limitations and pitfalls
- Lack of specificity: Many markers are raised in benign conditions, especially in reproductive-age and pregnant patients.
- Heterogeneity of tumors: Not all tumors produce classic markers. A normal marker does not rule out malignancy, particularly early-stage disease or marker-negative histologies.
- Assay variability and external factors: Different assays have different calibrations; heterophile antibodies and other interferences can produce spurious results.

Clinical approach and decision-making
- Use markers to modify, not determine, management. Leverage multidisciplinary input (radiology, gynecologic oncology, maternal-fetal medicine) when pregnancy introduces competing maternal-fetal considerations.
- When marker results are discordant with imaging, prioritize tissue diagnosis when safe and necessary. In pregnancy, consider gestational age, potential fetal risks of anesthesia and surgery, and alternative diagnostic options (MRI without gadolinium for further characterization).
- Establish monitoring plans: If expectant management is chosen, document baseline marker levels with planned intervals and consistent laboratory methodology, and pair serial marker assessment with serial imaging and clinical evaluation.

Practical thresholds and red flags
- Markedly elevated markers (e.g., CA-125 in the hundreds) are a red flag but not definitive; interpret in context. Rapidly rising serial values are more worrisome than isolated elevated measurements.
- For germ cell tumors, markedly elevated beta-hCG or alpha-fetoprotein (AFP; not covered centrally in this chapter but relevant) in the absence of pregnancy suggests tumor production and may prompt earlier surgical management.
- When infection markers are elevated alongside CA-125, prioritize identification and treatment of infectious causes before attributing CA-125 elevation to malignancy.

# Conclusion

Serum tumor markers and inflammatory laboratory tests can provide valuable adjunctive information in the evaluation of adnexal masses and possible malignancy during pregnancy, but their interpretation must be nuanced. CA-125, the most commonly used marker for ovarian epithelial cancer, is especially susceptible to physiologic elevation in early pregnancy and with benign pelvic or systemic inflammation. Other markers (CA 19-9, CEA, CA 15-3, LDH, beta-hCG) have more specific tumor associations but are also limited by false positives and dependence on tumor histology. Serial measurements, consistent laboratory methods, and integration with imaging and clinical findings improve interpretive value. In pregnancy, clinicians should emphasize cautious interpretation, use markers to guide but not dictate management, and enlist multidisciplinary consultation when uncertainty exists.

Chapter 8: Clinical Evaluation and Differential Diagnosis of Pelvic/Abdominal Masses in Pregnancy

Introduction

The discovery of a pelvic or abdominal mass in a pregnant patient presents a diagnostic challenge that requires integration of obstetric knowledge, anatomic considerations of the gravid uterus, and the physiological changes of pregnancy that alter presentation and interpretation of signs, symptoms, and laboratory data. A structured approach begins with a targeted history and symptom assessment, proceeds to a focused but modified physical examination, and synthesizes findings with laboratory and imaging studies to develop a prioritized differential diagnosis that balances maternal and fetal risk. This chapter outlines the essential clinical elements for evaluating pelvic and abdominal masses in pregnancy, details how to perform and interpret a bimanual pelvic examination in this context, provides a practical framework for differential diagnosis across likely organ systems, highlights red flags that mandate urgent intervention, reviews useful scoring and risk-stratification tools, and describes how to integrate clinical, laboratory, and imaging data into working diagnoses and management decisions.

# Key components of history and symptom assessment (pain characteristics, systemic symptoms, obstetric history)

A thorough history is the foundation of diagnosis. In pregnant patients with a pelvic or abdominal mass, distinguish among symptoms that point toward acute surgical abdomen, obstetric complications, or chronic/benign processes. Begin by characterizing pain: onset (sudden versus gradual), timing (constant versus intermittent/colicky), severity (scale 0–10), location and radiation, exacerbating and relieving factors, and associated features such as nausea, vomiting, bowel habit changes, urinary symptoms, vaginal bleeding, or discharge. Sudden, severe, focal pain that localizes to one lower quadrant and is associated with vomiting should raise concern for adnexal torsion, ruptured ovarian cyst, or acute appendicitis. Colicky, intermittent pain with palpable mass may suggest an obstructing process such as ureteral stone, obstructed bowel, or a fibroid undergoing degeneration. Diffuse abdominal pain with fever and peritoneal signs more likely reflects inflammatory or infectious processes (peritonitis, pelvic abscess, complicated appendicitis) and requires rapid evaluation.

Systemic symptoms refine differential and urgency. Fever and rigors indicate infection; tachycardia, hypotension, or altered mental status suggest sepsis and demand immediate action. Bleeding, vaginal fluid loss, and fetal movement reports are essential; fetal symptoms (decreased movements) may signal compromise. Obtain obstetric history early: gestational age, parity, prior pelvic surgery (adhesions, ectopic risk), prior cesarean delivery, history of pelvic inflammatory disease or endometriosis, known uterine fibroids or adnexal masses identified antenatally, infertility treatments (e.g., ovarian hyperstimulation), and results of recent prenatal ultrasounds. Gestational age narrows likely causes: adnexal masses discovered in the first trimester are often corpus luteum cysts or early adnexal pathology; mid-to-late pregnancy enlarging masses may be uterine fibroids that enlarge during pregnancy or degenerating leiomyomas. Ask about contraceptive and sexual history, as sexually transmitted infection history increases risk of tubo-ovarian abscess. Medication exposures including anticoagulants inform hemorrhagic risk.

Elicit gynecologic symptoms: abnormal vaginal bleeding, malodorous discharge, or postcoital bleeding. Urinary symptoms (dysuria, hematuria, frequency) may point to urinary tract infection or obstructing calculus and can mimic gynecologic pain. Gastrointestinal symptoms—anorexia, bowel obstruction features (obstipation, severe distension), melena—should not be overlooked, as gravid uterus can displace bowel and mask typical signs. Also query constitutional symptoms such as weight loss or night sweats, which might suggest chronic conditions (malignancy, tuberculosis) rather than an acute process.

When an adnexal or pelvic mass is known from prior imaging, review its documented characteristics (size, laterality, complexity, solid versus cystic, vascularity) and interval change. A previously stable small simple cyst is often managed conservatively, whereas a rapidly enlarging or complex mass warrants earlier intervention. Always document maternal comorbidities (coagulopathy, cardiovascular disease) that alter perioperative risk, and the patient’s preferences and obstetric plan (desire to continue pregnancy, mode and timing of delivery) because these affect management choices.

# Bimanual pelvic examination techniques and limitations in pregnancy

The bimanual pelvic examination remains a valuable bedside tool but must be adapted and interpreted cautiously in pregnancy. The goals are to assess uterine size and contour, cervical status, adnexal masses, tenderness, mobility, and signs of peritoneal irritation. In early pregnancy (first trimester), a bimanual exam can often detect uterine enlargement consistent with gestational age and may detect adnexal masses proximal to the uterus. In the second trimester and beyond, the gravid uterus enlarges into the abdominal cavity; consequently, pelvic bimanual palpation becomes less reliable for uterine size estimation and for assessing adnexal structures.

Technique in pregnancy follows conventional steps with patient positioning supine and a chaperone present. Explain the procedure and obtain consent. Insert two gloved, lubricated fingers into the vagina, palpate the cervix for consistency, effacement, dilation and tenderness. With the non-dominant hand on the lower abdomen, elevate the uterine fundus to approximate its position and attempt to palpate lateral adnexal structures between hands. Gently note any masses, tenderness, or restricted mobility. Bimanual palpation is more informative prior to 12–14 weeks; in later pregnancy, adnexal masses may be displaced cephalad or laterally and thus difficult to reach.

Be aware of limitations and potential for false reassurance or misinterpretation. The gravid uterus can mask or mimic adnexal masses: a lateral uterine fibroid may be mistaken for an adnexal mass; an enlarged, anteverted uterus can simulate a mass felt through the fornix. Vaginal bleeding or active labor signs require careful cervical examination but avoid aggressive manipulation. Pregnancy-related leukorrhea and cervical softening can complicate assessment of cervicitis. In cases where bimanual exam is inconclusive, rely promptly on imaging (transvaginal or transabdominal ultrasound) rather than repeated strenuous examinations.

Specific examination findings help direct differential. A tender, unilateral adnexal mass with adnexal rebound suggests torsion or tubo-ovarian abscess; a firm, irregular, non-mobile mass contiguous with the uterus suggests leiomyoma. A palpable, contiguous mass rising with the uterus points to uterine origin (fibroid, bicornuate uterus), whereas a mass separate from but adjacent to the uterus suggests adnexal or gastrointestinal source. Cervical motion tenderness increases likelihood of pelvic inflammatory disease but can be present with other inflammatory processes, including appendicitis or ectopic pregnancy. Always interpret findings in the context of gestational age and concurrent imaging.

# Framework for differential diagnosis: uterine, adnexal, gastrointestinal, urinary, and extra-abdominal sources

A systematic approach divides potential etiologies into uterine, adnexal, gastrointestinal, urinary, and extra-abdominal sources to organize thinking and testing.

Uterine sources: Uterine fibroids (leiomyomas) are the most common benign pelvic masses in pregnancy. They may increase in size under hormonal influence, although growth is variable. Symptoms include localized pain from red degeneration (a painful ischemic process often occurring in the second trimester) and mass effect causing urinary frequency or malpresentation. On examination, fibroids are typically firm, irregular, and contiguous with the uterus; ultrasound shows a well-defined, heterogeneous intramural or subserosal mass. Other uterine causes include uterine anomalies (e.g., bicornuate uterus) and rare uterine sarcomas that may present as rapidly enlarging masses with systemic symptoms.

Adnexal sources: In pregnancy, adnexal masses include corpus luteum cysts (common in early pregnancy and typically resolve by the second trimester), ovarian cysts (follicular, hemorrhagic, endometriomas), benign neoplasms (mature cystic teratoma), and malignant tumors (epithelial ovarian carcinoma, germ cell tumors) though malignancy is uncommon. Adnexal torsion is a gynecologic emergency characterized by sudden onset unilateral lower abdominal pain, often accompanied by nausea and vomiting. Torsion risk increases with larger adnexal masses, ovarian hyperstimulation, or pregnancy-related adnexal enlargement. Tubo-ovarian abscess and pelvic inflammatory disease, though less common in pregnancy, can produce complex, tender adnexal masses and systemic toxicity.

Gastrointestinal sources: Appendicitis is the most common non-obstetric surgical emergency in pregnancy and can present atypically because the appendix is displaced superiorly by the gravid uterus as pregnancy progresses. Pain migration, anorexia, nausea, and fever are typical but may be blunted. Intestinal obstruction (due to adhesions, volvulus, or external compression by a mass), diverticulitis, and neoplasms can present as palpable masses or abdominal distension. Pancreatitis, though rarely palpable as a mass, can cause severe epigastric pain that radiates to the back and systemic inflammatory response. Always consider GI causes when the mass is more abdominal than pelvic, when bowel sounds are altered, or when symptoms include significant bowel dysfunction.

Urinary sources: Distended bladder, hydronephrosis, or perinephric collections can mimic pelvic masses. Physiologic hydronephrosis of pregnancy commonly causes right-sided urinary tract dilation with mild flank pain; however, obstructing ureteral stones or pyonephrosis produce more severe pain and urinary symptoms. A palpable suprapubic mass may be a distended bladder in women with urinary retention (less common in pregnancy but possible in advanced labor or with pelvic mass causing outlet obstruction). Hematuria or severe flank pain should prompt urinalysis and renal imaging.

Extra-abdominal sources: Abdominal wall masses such as rectus sheath hematomas (especially in anticoagulated patients), ventral hernias, or lipomas can present as palpable masses and localized pain. Systemic conditions such as ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome (in patients who conceived with assisted reproduction) can cause bilateral ovarian enlargement and ascites. Lymphadenopathy or neoplastic metastases rarely present first as palpable abdominal masses but should be considered when systemic symptoms are present.

Cross-organ overlap and mimicry are common. For instance, an adnexal mass may cause urinary frequency by mass effect, and appendicitis can present with adnexal tenderness. Therefore, avoid anchoring bias: correlate history, exam, and imaging to avoid misattributing symptoms to pregnancy or to a known benign mass.

# Red flags warranting urgent intervention (torsion, rupture, severe hemorrhage, sepsis)

Timely identification of red flags is critical because delays can result in maternal morbidity and fetal compromise. Recognize the clinical patterns that demand urgent intervention.

Ovarian/adnexal torsion: Torsion classically presents with sudden, severe, unilateral lower abdominal pain often accompanied by nausea and vomiting. Physical exam may reveal localized peritoneal signs or a tender adnexal mass. Doppler ultrasound may show absent or diminished venous and arterial flow, but normal Doppler does not exclude torsion. High clinical suspicion merits urgent gynecologic consultation and often surgical exploration, because ovarian viability declines rapidly with prolonged ischemia.

Rupture and severe hemorrhage: Rupture of ovarian cysts, ectopic pregnancy, or vascular injury from mass necrosis can cause intraperitoneal hemorrhage. Presentations include sudden severe abdominal pain, hemodynamic instability (tachycardia, hypotension), syncope, and abdominal distension. Paracentesis or focused ultrasound demonstrating free intraperitoneal fluid may support the diagnosis. Immediate resuscitation, blood product availability, and surgical intervention are required.

Sepsis and pelvic abscess: Fever, tachycardia, hypotension, leukocytosis or leukopenia, elevated lactate, and altered mental status indicate sepsis. A tubo-ovarian abscess, complicated pyelonephritis, or perforated viscous may be the source. Use sepsis protocols (early antibiotics, source control, hemodynamic support) promptly. In pregnancy, consider fetal monitoring and involve obstetrics early.

Acute bowel obstruction or strangulation: Signs include severe abdominal pain, distension, vomiting, absence of flatus/feces, and systemic toxicity. The gravid uterus may complicate localization, but persistence of obstructive features or peritoneal signs necessitates surgical evaluation and likely operative management to prevent ischemia.

Acute appendicitis with perforation: Increasing pain intensity, peritonitis signs, fever, and leukocytosis may indicate perforation. Because diagnostic uncertainty is higher in pregnancy, maintain low threshold for surgical intervention if clinical and imaging data support appendicitis.

Cardiopulmonary compromise from large masses: Very large uterine fibroids or ovarian masses may compromise venous return or respiratory mechanics; in such cases, consider paracentesis for ascites or expedited management.

Any pregnant patient with hemodynamic instability, uncontrolled pain despite analgesia, peritoneal signs, or systemic inflammatory response should be treated emergently with coordinated multidisciplinary care (obstetrics, surgery, anesthesia, neonatology as needed).

# Use of clinical scoring systems and risk stratification tools

Clinical scoring systems can assist decision making by quantifying pre-test probability and standardizing evaluation, but most are developed for nonpregnant patients and must be applied cautiously.

Appendicitis: The Alvarado score (maximum 10 points) incorporates signs, symptoms, and lab findings (migration of pain, anorexia, nausea/vomiting, right lower quadrant tenderness, rebound, elevated temperature, leukocytosis, and left shift). A high score increases likelihood of appendicitis; however, physiologic leukocytosis and displacement of the appendix during pregnancy reduce specificity. Imaging (ultrasound followed by MRI if needed) should complement clinical scoring.

Ovarian mass malignancy risk: Several tools estimate risk that an adnexal mass is malignant. The Risk of Malignancy Index (RMI) incorporates ultrasound features, menopausal status, and serum CA-125. In pregnancy, CA-125 may be elevated and menopausal status is typically premenopausal, limiting RMI utility. More contemporary models include the International Ovarian Tumor Analysis (IOTA) simple rules and the ADNEX model, which use ultrasound morphology and patient factors to estimate malignancy risk. These models perform best with high-quality transvaginal ultrasound and are less validated in pregnancy; still, complex solid components, papillary projections, multilocularity, and increased vascularity on Doppler raise concern.

Sepsis: SOFA (Sequential Organ Failure Assessment) and qSOFA (quick SOFA: altered mentation, systolic blood pressure ≤100 mmHg, respiratory rate ≥22/min) help identify patients at risk of poor outcomes. qSOFA is a bedside screen; however, pregnancy physiologic adaptations (baseline tachypnea, lower blood pressure) can affect thresholds. Use clinical judgment and obstetric sepsis protocols when SIRS or qSOFA criteria are met.

Shock index: The shock index (heart rate divided by systolic blood pressure) helps detect early hemodynamic compromise. Normal shock index is approximately 0.5–0.7. In obstetrics, a shock index ≥0.9 is associated with increased risk of severe hemorrhage and need for transfusion.

Imaging-based risk stratification: Transvaginal ultrasound features that warrant expedited evaluation include solid components, papillary excrescences, multiloculated cysts >10 cm, internal septations >3 mm, and significant vascular flow on Doppler. For adnexal torsion, absence of Doppler flow is concerning but not definitive. In suspected appendicitis, graded compression ultrasound is first-line; MRI is preferred over CT when ultrasound is non-diagnostic, as MRI avoids ionizing radiation.

Use scoring systems as adjuncts rather than replacements for clinical judgment, and adjust interpretation according to gestational age and physiologic changes of pregnancy.

# Integration of clinical, laboratory and imaging data into working diagnoses

Constructing a working diagnosis requires synthesizing history, physical exam, laboratory results, and imaging with awareness of pregnancy-specific caveats. Start with a problem list and prioritize life-threatening conditions. Ask: does the presentation indicate an emergent surgical process, an obstetric complication, an infectious source, or a benign lesion amenable to observation?

Laboratory evaluation should be targeted. Obtain complete blood count (CBC), basic metabolic panel, liver enzymes if indicated, coagulation studies if bleeding suspected, blood type and screen, lactate for perfusion assessment, and urinalysis/urine culture for urinary tract sources. Reference ranges shift in pregnancy: hemoglobin concentration commonly falls due to physiologic hemodilution (normal nonpregnant female hemoglobin 12–16 g/dL; during pregnancy mild anemia is common), leukocyte count increases—normal range in pregnancy may be up to 12,000–16,000/μL and can rise further in labor. CRP and ESR are nonspecific markers of inflammation that can be elevated in many conditions and are less specific in pregnancy. Serum CA-125 may be elevated in early pregnancy and with peritoneal irritation and thus has limited specificity. Use serial laboratory trends (e.g., rising leukocytosis, increasing lactate) to gauge progression.

Imaging is indispensable. Transvaginal ultrasound is first-line for adnexal masses, providing detail on cyst morphology, septations, solid components, and Doppler flow. Transabdominal ultrasound complements transvaginal approach for larger masses and for uterine assessment. If ultrasound is non-diagnostic, MRI without gadolinium is safe in pregnancy and excellent for evaluating adnexal masses, appendicitis, bowel obstruction, and soft tissue characterization, particularly in the second and third trimesters when ultrasound windows are limited. Computed tomography (CT) involves ionizing radiation but may be used if MRI is unavailable and the maternal benefit outweighs fetal risk—use shielding and lowest feasible dose. When evaluating for hemorrhage, focused assessment with sonography in trauma (FAST) can rapidly detect free fluid.

Integrate findings into probabilities and plan management accordingly. For example, a small, simple unilateral cyst (<5 cm) on ultrasound in the first trimester with minimal symptoms can be managed expectantly with follow-up ultrasound because many resolve spontaneously. Conversely, a complex mass with solid components, septations, or rapid growth raises concern for neoplasm and may prompt surgical consultation and consideration of operative intervention in the second trimester, which is the safest window for non-obstetric abdominal surgery. In suspected torsion, emphasize clinical urgency: even if Doppler flow is present, persistent severe unilateral pain and compatible exam may require operative exploration.

Document maternal-fetal considerations in the working diagnosis. Assess fetal status appropriate to gestational age: fetal heart rate monitoring for viability and well-being in the context of maternal instability or sepsis. Balance maternal stabilization with fetal considerations—maternal resuscitation takes priority because maternal instability endangers both. In decision-making about timing and approach to surgery, engage a multidisciplinary team (obstetrics, maternal-fetal medicine, anesthesia, surgery, neonatology) early.

Finally, establish a follow-up plan tailored to risk. For low-risk masses managed expectantly, schedule interval imaging and clear return precautions for red-flag symptoms. For intermediate-risk lesions, arrange expedited imaging (MRI), tumor marker measurement with pregnancy caveats, and specialist consultation. For high-risk or unstable patients, prioritize resuscitation and timely surgical or interventional management.

Normal or commonly referenced laboratory ranges and diagnostic thresholds relevant in pregnancy:
- Hemoglobin: nonpregnant 12–16 g/dL; mild physiologic anemia is common in pregnancy.
- White blood cell count: nonpregnant 4,000–11,000/μL; in pregnancy, values up to 12,000–16,000/μL (or higher in labor) may be physiologic.
- Shock index: normal approximately 0.5–0.7; obstetric concern when ≥0.9.
- qSOFA: 1–3 points (altered mentation, systolic BP ≤100 mmHg, RR ≥22/min); pregnancy may alter thresholds—use as adjunct.
- CA-125: normal <35 U/mL in nonpregnant adults, but may be elevated in early pregnancy and peritoneal irritation.

Clinical decision points to remember: when there is hemodynamic instability, peritoneal signs, high clinical suspicion for torsion, perforation, or ongoing hemorrhage, prioritize timely surgical evaluation. When presentation is indeterminate, rely on ultrasound and MRI rather than prolonged observation if symptoms are progressing. Use risk-stratification tools to inform but not dictate care, and always interpret tests in the physiologic context of pregnancy.

Conclusion

Evaluating pelvic and abdominal masses in pregnancy requires methodical history-taking, careful but adapted physical examination, and judicious use of laboratory and imaging modalities. A framework that categorizes potential etiologies by organ system assists in developing a differential, while awareness of red flags ensures prompt intervention when maternal or fetal safety is threatened. Clinical scoring systems and imaging-based models can support decision-making, but pregnancy alters test characteristics and normal ranges; therefore, clinical judgment and multidisciplinary coordination remain central to delivering safe, effective care.

Introduction

Pregnancy presents unique challenges when non-obstetric pelvic or abdominal pathology requires consideration of operative management. The physiologic changes of pregnancy alter presentation, diagnostic accuracy, and perioperative risk for both the mother and the fetus. Decision-making must balance the maternal indications for surgery against fetal gestational age, the urgency of the maternal condition, and the potential effects of anesthesia, surgical stress, and intraoperative interventions on pregnancy outcomes. This chapter synthesizes evidence-based indications for operative intervention for pelvic and abdominal masses in pregnancy, addresses trimester-specific risks and timing preferences for non-obstetric abdominal surgery, contrasts operative and conservative management pathways, and details the essentials of counseling, multidisciplinary decision-making, documentation, and ethical aspects of shared decision-making.

# Indications for surgery: acute complications, suspected malignancy, refractory symptoms

Indications for operative intervention in pregnancy fall into three broad clinical categories: acute complications that threaten maternal health or viability of the organ involved, suspicion of malignancy where delay could compromise staging or outcomes, and refractory or progressively symptomatic lesions that fail conservative therapy and impair quality of life or create obstetric risks.

Acute complications mandate urgent or emergent intervention. Ovarian torsion is a prime example: twisting of the adnexal vascular pedicle produces venous outflow obstruction, progressive edema, ischemia, and ultimately hemorrhagic infarction. Clinically it presents with sudden, severe, unilateral lower abdominal pain often accompanied by nausea and vomiting. Ultrasound findings that raise concern include an enlarged, edematous ovary, peripheralization of follicles, absent or reduced Doppler venous and arterial flow, and free fluid. Torsion is a surgical emergency because prolonged ischemia can result in necrosis and infection, with attendant maternal sepsis and loss of ovarian function. Detorsion with cystectomy or ovary-sparing procedures is preferred when feasible; oophorectomy is reserved for nonviable tissue or hemodynamic instability.

Rupture and hemorrhage of an adnexal cyst or tumor can present as acute abdomen with peritoneal signs and hemodynamic instability. Hemodynamic compromise guides the urgency of laparotomy; blood loss parameters and need for transfusion are key decision points. Infection, such as abscess or infected necrosis within a mass, similarly requires prompt drainage and source control in addition to antimicrobial therapy.

Suspected malignancy is another major indication where delay could alter prognosis. Features that increase concern for malignancy in adnexal or pelvic masses include solid components, mural nodularity, papillary projections, septations thicker than 3 mm, rapid growth, presence of ascites, and elevated tumor markers when interpreted in context. In pregnancy, tumor markers have altered baselines (for example, human chorionic gonadotropin is elevated physiologically; CA-125 may be modestly increased in the first trimester and with peritoneal irritation), so interpretation must be cautious. Size thresholds commonly cited prompting consideration of surgery are masses greater than 5–10 cm, particularly if complex, growing, or symptomatic, because larger lesions carry greater risk of torsion, rupture, or malignancy. The decision to operate for malignancy must balance gestational age, need for accurate staging, and the potential for fertility- and pregnancy-sparing surgical approaches.

Refractory or progressive symptoms that materially affect maternal function or pregnancy course also justify surgical management. Persistent pain that is unresponsive to analgesia, bowel or urinary obstruction due to mass effect, or rapidly enlarging lesions that threaten uterine growth or obstruct labor are scenarios where the risk-benefit favors operative management. In all indications, maternal health and stability take precedence; maternal critical illness places fetal viability at risk, so intervention to secure maternal well-being is often the optimal route for both patients.

# Trimester-specific risks and preferred timing for non-obstetric abdominal surgery

Gestational age is a central determinant of perioperative risk and helps frame timing decisions for non-obstetric abdominal surgery. Each trimester carries distinct maternal-fetal considerations.

First trimester (conception through 14 weeks): The primary concern in the first trimester is teratogenicity and the baseline risk of spontaneous abortion. Organogenesis occurs approximately between weeks 3 and 10 of gestation; exposures during this window carry the highest theoretical risk for major congenital anomalies. However, modern anesthetic agents have not been convincingly linked to congenital malformations in humans when used for necessary surgery. The baseline miscarriage risk in recognized pregnancies is approximately 10% to 20% depending on population and maternal factors. Non-obstetric surgery in the first trimester may be associated with a small increased risk of miscarriage, particularly if underlying pathology or systemic illness (for example, severe infection or maternal hemodynamic instability) is present. When possible, elective procedures are deferred to the second trimester.

Second trimester (15 through 27 weeks): The second trimester is generally regarded as the safest window for elective non-obstetric surgery. Organogenesis is complete, the uterus is not yet so large as to complicate operative access, and the risk of preterm labor is lower than in the third trimester. Surgical positioning and respiratory mechanics are more manageable. Empirical data suggest lower rates of pregnancy loss and preterm birth when elective abdominal surgery occurs in the second trimester compared with the first or third. Therefore, when a procedure can be safely postponed, the second trimester is preferred for elective or semiurgent interventions.

Third trimester (28 weeks onward): The principal concerns in the third trimester are the increased risk of preterm labor, technical difficulties related to the gravid uterus, higher intra-abdominal pressure, and risks associated with perioperative fetal distress and prematurity. Surgeries in the late third trimester have higher rates of preterm delivery and neonatal morbidity if preterm birth occurs. For near-term pregnancies with pathology that can be delayed, it may be appropriate to plan surgery concurrent with delivery if obstetrically indicated (for example, performing cesarean delivery with simultaneous resection of a pelvic mass). When emergent maternal indications arise in the third trimester, the maternal condition dictates immediate management; when feasible, administration of corticosteroids to enhance fetal lung maturity (generally between 24 and 34 weeks) and planning for potential neonatal support should be incorporated.

Across trimesters, other specific risks must be considered. In pregnant patients, physiological increases in blood volume and cardiac output, decreased functional residual capacity, and changes in gastroesophageal sphincter tone increase aspiration risk. The impact of intra-abdominal pressure (pneumoperitoneum) with laparoscopy requires attention to insufflation pressures and maternal positioning. Current evidence supports the safety of laparoscopy in pregnancy when performed by experienced surgeons with pneumoperitoneum pressures generally kept at or below 12 mmHg, and with left lateral tilt to minimize aortocaval compression. Nevertheless, uterine size and gestational age inform the choice between laparoscopy and laparotomy.

# Conservative management alternatives and criteria for expectant vs operative approaches

Not all pelvic or abdominal masses in pregnancy require immediate surgery. Conservative management is often appropriate and may include expectant observation with serial imaging and symptom monitoring, medical therapy (for example, antibiotics for infection), percutaneous drainage for accessible collections, or temporizing procedures that preserve pregnancy until a safer operative window.

Criteria favoring expectant management include small, simple cysts (<5–6 cm) that are asymptomatic or minimally symptomatic, sonographic features consistent with a benign functional cyst (thin-walled, anechoic, unilocular), and stability or regression on serial imaging. Many functional ovarian cysts regress spontaneously during pregnancy. The decision to observe should include planned intervals for reassessment, typically with follow-up ultrasound in 4–8 weeks, and clear instructions for return if symptoms worsen.

Conversely, criteria favoring operative intervention include signs of acute complication (torsion, rupture with hemoperitoneum, hemodynamic instability), sonographic or clinical features suggesting malignancy as previously outlined (solid components, papillary projections, ascites, rapid growth), persistent or severe symptoms refractory to medical therapy, and mass size sufficiently large (commonly >8–10 cm) to appreciably raise risks of torsion, rupture, or obstructed labor. Infection not responsive to antibiotics or collections requiring definitive drainage are further indications for operative or percutaneous intervention.

The threshold for conversion from expectant to operative management depends on evolution of symptoms, interval growth, new suspicious features on imaging, or development of complications. Clinical vigilance is essential because the physiologic changes of pregnancy can mask some signs of deterioration (for example, leukocytosis is common), and imaging modalities have trade-offs in sensitivity and specificity during pregnancy. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) without gadolinium is often the preferred problem-solving tool when ultrasound is inconclusive and additional characterization is required, as it avoids ionizing radiation and generally provides superior soft-tissue contrast.

# Counseling on maternal and fetal risks, informed consent essentials

Informed consent in the context of operative intervention during pregnancy must be comprehensive, individualized, and documented. Counseling should explicitly address the maternal risks of the procedure, the potential fetal risks, the likely timing and urgency of surgical intervention, and the alternatives, including their risks and benefits.

Maternal risks include anesthesia-related complications, surgical bleeding, infection, thromboembolic events, organ injury, potential need for blood transfusion, and the general risks of the procedure being considered (for example, oophorectomy vs cystectomy). Maternal physiologic changes in pregnancy modify risk profiles: airway edema increases the risk of difficult intubation, increased blood volume can mask acute blood loss until decompensation, and a hypercoagulable state raises thromboembolism risk. Preoperative labs should include complete blood count (reference ranges: hemoglobin 12–16 g/dL in nonpregnant females but may be lower in pregnancy due to dilutional anemia; pregnant hemoglobin often ranges 11–14 g/dL), and crossmatch if significant hemorrhage is anticipated.

Fetal risks to communicate include miscarriage (particularly for first-trimester surgeries), preterm labor and delivery (notably for third-trimester interventions), potential for transient or permanent fetal neurodevelopmental effects related to intraoperative hypoxia or hemodynamic instability, and the possibility of neonatal complications if preterm delivery occurs. While modern anesthesia has not been shown to cause congenital malformations, perioperative maternal hypotension, hypoxia, fever, or prolonged uterine manipulations can adversely affect the fetus. Specific numerical risk estimates should be framed cautiously: baseline spontaneous abortion risk is roughly 10–20%; non-obstetric surgery may increase miscarriage risk modestly in the first trimester, but the absolute increase varies by underlying condition and is not precisely quantified. Risk of preterm delivery after nonobstetric surgery is higher in the third trimester; some series report increased relative risk but absolute risk depends on gestational age and maternal condition.

Discussion of alternatives should include conservative options, percutaneous interventions, and the option of deferring surgery to a safer gestational window if clinically appropriate and safe. If malignancy is suspected, the potential impact of delay on staging and prognosis should be reviewed. Counseling must also outline perioperative fetal monitoring plans, use of tocolytics (not routinely recommended for prophylaxis) and corticosteroid administration when preterm delivery risk exists (corticosteroids recommended for fetal lung maturation between 24 and 34 weeks when preterm birth is likely within 7 days).

Informed consent must cover contingency planning: for example, the potential need to proceed to laparotomy, possible oophorectomy, and the circumstances under which obstetric delivery might be performed concurrently. Consent should be obtained in language the patient understands, include assessment of capacity, and allow time for questions. Documentation should record the risks discussed, alternatives offered, the patient’s expressed preferences and understanding, and the decision reached.

# Multidisciplinary decision-making and documentation

Optimal perioperative care of pregnant patients with pelvic or abdominal masses requires coordinated multidisciplinary input. The team should include the obstetrician with expertise in high-risk pregnancies, the surgeon skilled in the planned procedure (gynecologic oncologist if malignancy suspected), anesthesiologist familiar with obstetric anesthesia, neonatology if viability and potential preterm delivery are considerations, radiology for diagnostic imaging input, and pathology if intraoperative frozen section may inform decisions. Nursing, blood bank, and critical care services should be integrated as needed.

Multidisciplinary decision-making begins with a shared assessment of urgency, maternal stability, gestational age, diagnostic uncertainty, and the potential benefits and harms of intervention versus conservative care. In cases of suspected malignancy, discussion should address whether fertility- or pregnancy-sparing approaches are oncologically safe and whether staging can be deferred. The anesthesiologist should evaluate airway, aspiration risk, and provide recommendations on positioning, monitoring, and perioperative fetal monitoring. Neonatology involvement is critical when the risk of preterm delivery is substantial; they advise on corticosteroid administration, magnesium sulfate for neuroprotection when appropriate (usually considered for deliveries before 32 weeks), and neonatal resuscitation readiness.

Documentation after such discussions should be thorough and should include the clinical indications, alternatives considered, the multidisciplinary team members involved, the shared decision-making process, the specifics of the consent obtained, contingency plans, and any agreed-upon thresholds that would prompt action (for example, specified levels of maternal instability that would trigger immediate laparotomy). This documentation is both medical record and medicolegal protection and provides continuity of care across teams and shifts.

# Ethical considerations and patient-centered risk tolerance

Ethical decision-making in the context of surgery during pregnancy requires balancing respect for maternal autonomy, beneficence for both patients (mother and fetus), nonmaleficence, and justice. The pregnant patient is the primary decision-maker; although fetal interests are ethically weighty, the mother’s autonomy must be respected. Clinicians should provide clear, unbiased information and recommend a course consistent with medical evidence while recognizing that patients may weigh risks to their own health and the fetus differently.

Values clarification is an important component of counseling. Some patients may prioritize minimizing any fetal risk, even at some increased maternal risk or postponing definitive cancer treatment; others may prioritize rapid treatment of a suspected malignancy to maximize maternal prognosis. Clinicians should explore patient goals, fears, and social context (support systems, childcare, ability to attend follow-up) and recognize that cultural or religious values may shape preferences. Where a patient’s decision potentially conflicts with medical recommendation, efforts should focus on empathetic engagement, provision of additional information, and negotiation. Coercion must be avoided; if a patient declines recommended intervention, clinicians should document the discussion, ensure comprehension, and outline plans for monitoring and reassessment.

Situations of extreme urgency, where maternal life is at risk, ethically justify immediate intervention even when that intervention carries significant fetal risk. The mother’s treatment should not be withheld to protect the fetus when maternal survival is imperiled. In such scenarios, interventions that simultaneously address maternal illness and obstetric considerations (for example, resuscitative hysterotomy when indicated) may be appropriate.

Finally, fair access to care is an ethical concern. Pregnant patients should have timely access to specialists and supportive services, and systems should minimize delays in diagnosis and treatment that could worsen outcomes. Counseling should aim to empower patients with information and support rather than directing decisions based on provider values.

Conclusion

Deciding on operative intervention for pelvic and abdominal masses in pregnancy requires a nuanced understanding of pathophysiology, altered maternal physiology, gestational age–specific risks, and the comparative benefits of operative versus conservative strategies. Clear communication, multidisciplinary planning, detailed informed consent, and attention to ethical principles enable clinicians to guide pregnant patients through complex choices. The clinician’s responsibility is to present evidence-based recommendations tailored to the patient’s clinical situation and values, prioritize maternal stabilization when necessary, and document the process and rationale thoroughly to support optimal maternal and fetal outcomes.

Introduction

Surgical management of uterine and adnexal masses during pregnancy requires balancing maternal surgical goals (complete resection, preservation of reproductive function, and control of hemorrhage) with fetal safety. Physiologic changes of pregnancy—enlarged gravid uterus, increased blood volume, altered coagulation, and displacement of intra-abdominal organs—create technical challenges that influence choice of exposure, access, hemostatic technique and intraoperative monitoring. This chapter reviews operative techniques used for myomectomy and adnexal surgery in pregnant patients, describing principles of open and minimally invasive access, stepwise operative technique for myomectomy and ovarian cystectomy, and adjunctive measures to reduce blood loss, minimize uterine manipulation and shorten operative time. Emphasis is placed on pathophysiology, safe surgical decision-making, and the translation of basic surgical principles into the unique context of pregnancy.

# Open laparotomy techniques: incision types, exposure in gravid abdomen

Open laparotomy remains the most widely used approach for large masses, uncertain pathology, or when rapid access and maximal exposure are needed. In pregnancy, incision choice is governed by gestational age, size and location of the mass, need for uterine exteriorization, and anticipated intraoperative maneuvers.

Midline vertical incision: A midline vertical incision—extending from just below the umbilicus toward the pubis when required—provides the most extensile exposure for an enlarged gravid uterus and supracolic pathology. It allows rapid entry, easy extension, and minimal bleeding through linea alba. For second- or third-trimester patients or when multiple quadrants must be accessed, a vertical midline incision offers superior exposure compared with transverse incisions. Care must be taken when closing a vertical incision to approximate rectus fascia and minimize wound complications; the vertical incision also has implications for future pregnancies (e.g., classical cesarean risk) that should be discussed preoperatively when possible.

Low transverse (Pfannenstiel) incision: A low transverse suprapubic incision is cosmetically favorable and often feasible in early pregnancy when the uterus is not markedly enlarged. It provides excellent access to lower-pelvic masses and the adnexa but is limited for large fundal leiomyomas or masses extending above the pelvic brim. The Pfannenstiel approach may be preferable when the anticipated operative field is confined to the lower pelvis and there is minimal need for wide exposure.

Joel-Cohen and transverse midline alternatives: In some centers the Joel-Cohen or Maylard modifications are used for improved exposure while maintaining a transverse skin crease. The Maylard requires transection of the rectus muscles and is not commonly chosen in pregnancy unless wide lateral exposure is required and the surgeon is familiar with the technique.

Principles of exposure and uterine handling: Adequate exposure requires patient positioning with left lateral tilt (15 degrees) to avoid aortocaval compression in the supine gravid patient. A Foley catheter drains the bladder to decrease injury risk when operating near the lower uterine segment or adnexa. When feasible, avoid exteriorizing the gravid uterus excessively; if exteriorization is necessary for exposure, it should be performed gently and for the shortest duration. Retractor selection (e.g., self-retaining retractors with blunt blades) and careful placement of surgical pads to support the uterus minimize pressure and maintain uteroplacental perfusion.

Hemodynamic considerations: Blood volume is increased during pregnancy but so is uterine blood flow, and placental perfusion is sensitive to maternal hypotension. Hemostasis is paramount; rapid control of bleeding limits transfusion needs and reduces the chance of uterine irritability. Preoperative planning includes type and crossmatch (common practical target is at least type and screen with crossmatch if significant bleeding is likely), and baseline labs: hemoglobin 12–16 g/dL (nonpregnant; pregnancy values may be slightly lower), hematocrit approximately 36–48% in nonpregnant women (physiologic hemodilution lowers hematocrit in pregnancy), platelet count 150–450 ×10^9/L, and coagulation studies as clinically indicated.

# Laparoscopic approaches: indications, port placement modifications, insufflation considerations and open Hasson entry

Laparoscopy during pregnancy can be safe and effective when patient selection and technique are adapted to gestational changes. Indications include symptomatic adnexal masses (torsion, large persistent cysts of uncertain character), small to moderate-size pedunculated fibroids causing pain, and situations where diagnostic uncertainty persists. Contraindications include hemodynamic instability and very large masses or uterine size that prohibit safe insufflation and visualization.

Timing and gestational age: The second trimester (approximately 14–20 weeks) is generally the optimal window for elective minimally invasive surgery because organogenesis is complete and the uterus is not yet too large to impede laparoscopic visualization. Emergency procedures (e.g., adnexal torsion) may be performed at any gestational age if clinically necessary.

Entry technique and port placement modifications: The gravid uterus alters standard port-entry landmarks. For safety, initial peritoneal access is often performed with an open (Hasson) technique or at alternative entry sites to avoid uterine injury.

- Open Hasson entry: The Hasson technique entails a small incision to the fascia under direct vision, blunt dissection down to the peritoneum, placement of a blunt trocar secured with sutures, and controlled establishment of pneumoperitoneum. This reduces the risk of blind insufflation-related uterine or visceral injury. Surgeons commonly place the Hasson trocar at an alternative site—commonly supraumbilical or at Palmer's point (3 cm below the left costal margin in the midclavicular line)—depending on uterine size. Direct visualization is essential during deep dissection.

- Palmer’s point and left upper quadrant entry: For patients with an enlarged uterus displacing the umbilicus cranially, left upper quadrant entry at Palmer’s point is a safe alternative. Care must be taken to avoid a distended stomach (preoperative nasogastric decompression if necessary) and to be aware of splenic anatomy.

- Optical trocars: When used, optical trocars allow visualization during penetration but still require careful consideration of uterine position and remaining working space.

Port placement: As pregnancy advances, ports are shifted cephalad. Typical modifications include placing the primary camera port 2–3 cm above the umbilicus in early pregnancy, and higher (subxiphoid region) in later pregnancy if necessary. Working ports are similarly placed with more cephalad and lateral distribution to optimize triangulation and avoid pressure on the uterus. All ports should be inserted at angles that minimize upward force and pressure on the uterine fundus.

Insufflation pressure considerations: Pneumoperitoneum increases intra-abdominal pressure and can reduce venous return, cardiac output and uteroplacental blood flow. Published experience supports using the lowest insufflation pressure that provides an adequate operative field. Common recommendations are to use carbon dioxide pneumoperitoneum at pressures of 10–12 mmHg, often targeting 8–12 mmHg. In late pregnancy or when venous return is a concern, lower pressures (8–10 mmHg) are preferred. Duration of pneumoperitoneum should be minimized, and gasless laparoscopy is an option in centers with the requisite equipment and experience.

Anesthetic and fetal considerations during laparoscopy: Consultation with anesthesia is essential. Maternal capnography and end-tidal CO2 monitoring are critical because CO2 absorption increases during pneumoperitoneum; hypercapnia should be avoided. Fetal monitoring protocols vary with gestational age; continuous fetal monitoring may be considered for viable fetuses perioperatively when feasible, and neonatology should be available if preterm delivery is a possibility. Tocolytics are not routinely indicated but may be used for uterine irritability in select situations in consultation with obstetrics.

# Steps of myomectomy: pedicle control, enucleation, uterine repair and hemostasis

Indications for myomectomy in pregnancy are limited and typically involve symptomatic fibroids causing refractory pain, degenerative change with sepsis risk, rapid growth with concern for malignancy, or pedunculated lesions at risk of torsion. When performed, a meticulous, stepwise approach reduces bleeding and preserves uterine integrity.

Preoperative planning: Preoperative imaging (ultrasound with Doppler, MRI if needed) helps define size, number, location and relationship of fibroids to the uterine cavity and placenta. Blood availability should be considered and the team prepared for rapid transfusion if necessary.

Pedicle control and devascularization: Control of blood supply is a critical first step. Techniques include:

- Local vasoconstrictor injection: Diluted vasopressin (commonly 0.05–0.5 U/mL) injected into the myometrium adjacent to the fibroid reduces bleeding by local vasoconstriction. In pregnancy, caution is required: systemic absorption can cause maternal bradycardia, hypertension, and coronary or uteroplacental vasospasm. Use small doses, dilute the solution, aspirate before injection, and monitor hemodynamics closely. Some centers avoid vasopressin in pregnancy or use lower concentrations.

- Temporary mechanical devascularization: Use of atraumatic clamps (e.g., bulldog clamps) on the uterine arteries or application of tourniquets/suture loops (e.g., around the lower uterine segment and fundus) may be used to decrease uterine perfusion temporarily during enucleation. These techniques reduce bleeding without systemic pharmacologic effects but require familiarity and caution regarding ischemic time.

- Occlusion of vascular pedicles in pedunculated fibroids: Ligation or clamping of a stalk is straightforward when present (see below under management of pedunculated lesions).

Enucleation: After devascularization, a precise incision over the most prominent portion of the fibroid is made (typically a linear serosal incision). The pseudocapsule is identified, and blunt-sharp dissection is used to shell out the fibroid. Gentle traction and countertraction facilitate dissection along the cleavage plane, minimizing myometrial damage. When multiple fibroids are present, prioritize removal of symptomatic or large lesions; extensive myomectomy with multiple uterine incisions can increase bleeding and risk uterine rupture in subsequent pregnancy, so surgical goals must be individualized.

Uterine repair: Layered, secure uterine closure restores anatomy and decreases risk of bleeding and future uterine rupture. Repairs generally include:

- Deep myometrial approximation: Interrupted or continuous absorbable sutures (e.g., polyglactin 1 or 0) to reapproximate deep myometrium, obliterating dead space. Multiple layers may redistribute tension and support hemostasis.

- Serosal closure: Reapproximate serosa with fine absorbable sutures (e.g., 2-0 or 3-0) taking care to invert serosal edges; some favor continuous locked suturing for a hemostatic seal, others prefer interrupted sutures to minimize ischemic tension.

- Avoid excessive suture bulk and ischemia: Excessively tight sutures or obliteration of the pseudocapsule can lead to poor healing and scarring. A balance is required between hemostasis and preservation of tissue viability.

Hemostasis: Achieve meticulous hemostasis before closure. Techniques include direct vessel ligation, use of electrosurgical bipolar coagulation for small bleeding points, and application of topical hemostatic agents as adjuncts. Adequate hemostasis limits hematoma formation, infection risk, and uterine irritability.

Postoperative considerations: Monitor for uterine contractions and bleeding. Decisions about postoperative tocolysis, thromboprophylaxis and duration of maternal-fetal monitoring depend on gestational age and intraoperative events.

# Management of pedunculated lesions and techniques to reduce blood loss (clamps, vasoconstrictors, hemostatic agents)

Pedunculated myomas or adnexal stalk lesions are often amenable to simpler approaches because of their discrete vascular pedicle.

Management of pedunculated myomas: Identify the base of the stalk and achieve secure hemostasis prior to transection. Techniques include:

- Ligation and excision: Double ligation of the stalk with absorbable suture proximal and distal to the intended transection site, then excise between ligatures. This provides reliable mechanical hemostasis without reliance on thermal energy.

- Clamping and oversewing: Apply an atraumatic clamp (e.g., Kelly or vascular clamp) to the stalk, then oversew the base with suture and transect. This is effective in open surgery; for laparoscopy, intracorporeal bougie-suture techniques or extracorporeal knot tying may be used.

- Bipolar coagulation: Bipolar energy can coagulate the pedicle before transection; it produces less lateral thermal spread than monopolar energy and is often preferred when vascular pedicles are small. Careful application with short bursts and visual confirmation of blanching is recommended.

Techniques to reduce blood loss more broadly:

- Mechanical vascular control: As above, temporary uterine artery occlusion with clamps or suture loops can significantly lower intraoperative blood loss. Some centers use tourniquet techniques around the lower uterine segment to decrease perfusion while the fibroid is enucleated; ischemia times should be minimized to limit myometrial injury.

- Local vasoconstrictors: Vasopressin injections can reduce bleeding at the dissection plane. Pregnant patients are at higher risk for systemic cardiovascular effects; therefore, doses are conservative, and anesthesia should be informed and prepared to treat hemodynamic changes.

- Topical hemostatic agents: Product classes include oxidized regenerated cellulose (e.g., Surgicel), gelatin-thrombin matrices (e.g., Floseal), and fibrin sealants. These adjuncts may be applied to raw myometrial surfaces to promote clot formation. They are adjunctive measures and do not substitute for proper surgical technique. Considerations in pregnancy include potential for local inflammation, adhesive disease, or thrombotic risk especially when using agents containing thrombin. Use absorbable agents and avoid overpacking.

- Hypotensive anesthesia: Some procedures use transient controlled hypotension to reduce blood loss in nonpregnant patients; in pregnancy this strategy is generally avoided because uteroplacental perfusion is pressure-dependent.

- Rapid but careful technique: Efficient enucleation and uterine repair shorten ischemic or clamp times and reduce cumulative blood loss.

# Ovarian cystectomy techniques in pregnancy and preservation of ovarian tissue

The majority of adnexal masses encountered in pregnancy are benign functional cysts or dermoids; however, persistent or suspicious lesions may require removal. The surgical objective is removal of the mass with preservation of ovarian tissue and function when possible.

Preoperative assessment: Ultrasound with Doppler is first-line for characterization of cysts. Tumor markers (e.g., CA-125) have limited specificity in pregnancy because levels may change; values should be interpreted cautiously. Indications for surgical management include torsion, large persistent cysts (commonly >6–10 cm depending on symptoms and morphology), rapid growth, or sonographic features suspicious for malignancy (solid components, papillary projections, septations, increased vascular flow).

Cystectomy technique: The general principles are similar to nonpregnant patients but adapted to minimize uterine manipulation and ovarian ischemia.

- Access and exposure: For laparoscopic cystectomy, port placement is modified cephalad as needed; for open cystectomy, the incision is chosen for adequate exposure with minimal uterine handling. If possible, avoid aggressive manipulation of the uterus.

- Incision on ovarian cortex: A cortical incision is made away from the hilum when possible. Sharp dissection delineates cyst wall from ovarian stroma.

- Enucleation: Use gentle blunt dissection with traction-countertraction to shell out the cyst. For endometriomas, careful cleavage along the capsule and preservation of ovarian tissue is important; however, endometrioma surgery in pregnancy is rarely required.

- Hemostasis and ovarian preservation: Hemostasis of the ovarian parenchyma can be achieved with fine sutures (e.g., 4-0 or 5-0 absorbable) placed to approximate ovarian tissue, thereby minimizing thermal injury from electrosurgery. Bipolar coagulation can be used selectively for small bleeding points but should be limited because thermal spread reduces ovarian reserve. For cysts with problematic bleeding or friable stroma, hemostatic agents may be applied. Intracorporeal suturing techniques that approximate the ovarian cortex preserve ovarian tissue architecture better than fulguration.

- Specimen retrieval: Remove the cyst intact or in an endoscopic retrieval bag to avoid spillage of cyst contents (particularly dermoid elements or mucinous cyst content). Avoid power morcellation in pregnancy because of risk of tissue dissemination and because the uterine size and fetal safety concerns complicate its use.

- Oophorectomy considerations: Reserve oophorectomy for cases where ovarian preservation is not feasible (e.g., extensive destruction of ovarian tissue, uncontrolled bleeding, or high suspicion for malignancy). When bilateral oophorectomy is considered, the implications for endocrine function and fetal effects must be discussed preoperatively if time allows.

# Strategies to shorten operative time and reduce uterine manipulation

Reducing operative time and minimizing uterine manipulation are important to limit fetal exposure to anesthesia, maternal blood loss, and uterine irritability that can provoke contractions. Strategies include:

- Preoperative planning and team coordination: Detailed preoperative mapping of lesion location via imaging, preparation of appropriate instruments, and briefing of the anesthesia and nursing teams reduce intraoperative delays. Having blood products available and ready tubing and warming devices prepared also streamlines care.

- Surgical approach selection: Choose the least invasive approach that allows safe and effective resection. When laparoscopy can accomplish the goals safely, it often leads to shorter recovery and less postoperative pain, but the operative time may be longer if the team is not experienced. Experienced laparoscopic teams can perform adnexal operations quickly with less uterine manipulation.

- Limit uterine handling: Use atraumatic graspers on adnexal structures rather than grasping the uterus. Avoid repeated exteriorization of the uterus during open procedures. When uterine palpation is necessary to locate fibroids, gentle external compression and palpation by the assistant can be used instead of internal traction.

- Efficient hemostasis: Apply devascularization techniques early in the case so dissection can proceed with minimal interruption. Preemptive local vasoconstrictor, appropriate use of clamps and early suturing decrease bleeding and the need for time-consuming cautery.

- Use of energy devices judiciously: Advanced bipolar or vessel-sealing devices can speed dissection and coagulation for small to moderate vessels, but remember their use must be balanced against potential thermal injury and effects on adjacent tissues.

- Parallel tasking by the operative team: While the primary surgeon completes the critical dissection, assistants can prepare suture material, hemostatic agents, and plans for specimen retrieval to avoid pauses.

- Limit number of lesions removed: When multiple small fibroids are present but asymptomatic, consider removing only the dominant or symptomatic lesions; extensive myomectomy increases operative time and may not confer benefit during pregnancy.

# Use and risks of electrosurgery and topical hemostatic adjuncts in pregnancy

Electrosurgery is a powerful tool for cutting and coagulation, but in pregnancy its use requires special caution because of potential effects on maternal tissues and theoretical concerns about fetal exposure.

Electrosurgery types and considerations:

- Monopolar electrosurgery: Monopolar current passes from the active electrode through tissues to a dispersive grounding pad. Monopolar energy can produce greater lateral thermal spread and poses a theoretical risk of distant current flow; it is generally avoided for direct uterine myometrial fulguration during pregnancy because of potential for deeper tissue thermal injury. If monopolar is used, use low power settings and short activation intervals, and avoid direct application to the gravid uterus where unnecessary.

- Bipolar electrosurgery: Bipolar devices confine current between two jaws, producing less lateral thermal spread and greater precision. Bipolar coagulation is preferred for coagulating small bleeding vessels on the uterus or ovaries. Vessel-sealing devices (advanced bipolar) can be efficient for pedicle control but should be used with awareness of lateral spread and potential for thermal injury to adjacent structures.

- Harmonic and ultrasonic devices: These use ultrasonic vibration to coagulate and cut tissue with minimal electric current. They can be associated with less smoke and may produce less lateral thermal spread, but data in pregnancy are limited. When used, application times should be minimized.

Risks and mitigation strategies: Thermal injury to the myometrium or ovarian cortex can impair tissue viability and fertility. In the uterus, deep thermal damage can create areas of necrosis that may compromise uterine integrity. To mitigate risks:

- Prefer mechanical hemostasis (suture ligation, clamps) when feasible, especially for larger vessels or where preservation of tissue is critical.

- Use bipolar rather than monopolar energy near the uterus and adnexa.

- Apply the lowest effective power settings and activate energy for the shortest duration necessary.

- Maintain distance from the fetus and placenta; identify placental location preoperatively to avoid inadvertent thermal injury when operating near the lower uterine segment.

Topical hemostatic adjuncts:

- Oxidized regenerated cellulose (e.g., Surgicel): Provides a scaffold for clot formation, is absorbable, and is commonly used on raw myometrial surfaces. It can be left in situ but should be applied sparingly to avoid mass effect.

- Gelatin-thrombin matrices (e.g., Floseal): These promote clot formation and are effective for diffuse oozing. They contain biologically active thrombin, and although topical, there is a theoretical risk of systemic coagulation if used excessively, especially in patients with predisposing clotting disorders.

- Fibrin sealants: Mimic the final steps of coagulation and create a fibrin clot. They can be effective adjuncts but are relatively expensive and may induce mild local inflammatory reactions.

Safety in pregnancy: Overall, topical hemostatic agents are considered safe for use in pregnancy when used appropriately. Adhesive formation is a theoretical concern after any intra-abdominal surgery. There is limited evidence of systemic fetal effects from topically applied agents, but prudence dictates minimal necessary use and avoidance of agents associated with higher thrombotic risk when maternal coagulopathy or thrombophilia exists.

Conclusion

Successful myomectomy and adnexal surgery during pregnancy require a nuanced appreciation of altered maternal anatomy and physiology, fetal considerations, and careful operative technique. Selection of open versus minimally invasive approaches is individualized based on lesion size, suspicion for malignancy, gestational age, and surgeon expertise. Key operative principles include minimizing uterine manipulation, achieving early devascularization, meticulous enucleation and layered uterine repair, and using mechanical hemostasis and topical adjuncts judiciously. Electrosurgical tools and hemostatic agents can shorten operative time and control bleeding but must be used with an understanding of their risks and limitations in pregnancy. Preoperative planning, multidisciplinary coordination (obstetrics, anesthesia, neonatology), and intraoperative vigilance for maternal hemodynamic stability and fetal well-being underpin safe surgical care in this unique population.

Introduction

Surgical procedures during pregnancy—whether obstetric or non-obstetric—pose unique challenges for anesthesiologists and perioperative teams because of simultaneous physiologic obligations to the mother and the fetus. The anesthetic plan must balance maternal safety, optimize uteroplacental perfusion and fetal oxygenation, and anticipate pregnancy-specific complications (aspiration risk, altered airway anatomy, hemodynamic lability, and changes in drug pharmacokinetics). Intraoperative fetal monitoring and timely documentation can guide intraoperative management and postoperative disposition, but monitoring strategies depend on gestational age, the procedure being performed, and institutional resources. This chapter outlines the physiologic considerations underlying anesthetic technique selection, positioning, monitoring of maternal oxygenation, hemodynamics and temperature, the principles and practical techniques for intraoperative fetal surveillance, indications and agents for uterine relaxation or tocolysis, and postoperative maternal–fetal monitoring.

# Choice of anesthetic technique (general vs regional) and physiologic implications

Selecting between general and regional anesthesia in a pregnant patient requires integrating gestational age, the nature and urgency of the surgery, maternal comorbidities, airway assessment, coagulation status, and fetal considerations. Whenever feasible and appropriate, regional anesthesia is preferred for many procedures because it avoids airway manipulation and the hemodynamic and respiratory consequences of general anesthesia and many intravenous anesthetic agents. However, regional anesthesia has its own physiologic implications and contraindications.

Physiologic changes in pregnancy relevant to anesthetic choice include increased oxygen consumption (approximately 20–30%), decreased functional residual capacity (FRC), increased minute ventilation, reduced gastric emptying, increased risk of aspiration, increased blood volume (plasma volume increases more than red cell mass causing dilutional anemia), and aortocaval compression by the gravid uterus. These factors influence the choice and management of both general and regional anesthesia. For example, decreased FRC and increased oxygen consumption shorten safe apnea time, increasing the urgency for rapid securement of the airway if general anesthesia is required. Pregnant patients are at increased risk for difficult airway due to mucosal edema and weight gain.

Regional anesthesia (neuraxial: spinal, epidural, or combined spinal–epidural) provides effective analgesia/anesthesia for many types of surgery, including lower extremity and lower abdominal procedures, and it is the preferred approach for cesarean delivery. Advantages of regional anesthesia include avoidance of airway instrumentation, reduced fetal exposure to systemic anesthetics, decreased incidence of postoperative thromboembolism when combined with early mobilization, and often superior postoperative analgesia. However, neuraxial blockade produces sympathetic blockade that can cause hypotension, which reduces uteroplacental perfusion if not promptly recognized and treated. Proper preloading or co-loading with fluid and early vasopressor use (phenylephrine is commonly used for spinal-induced hypotension in obstetric anesthesia) can mitigate this risk. Coagulation abnormalities, local infection at the site, patient refusal, or inability to cooperate are contraindications to neuraxial techniques. For major abdominal or thoracic procedures, regional anesthesia alone is often not feasible.

General anesthesia is indicated for procedures requiring full muscle relaxation, when regional anesthesia is contraindicated or insufficient, or in emergent situations. Physiologic effects to consider include the impact of intravenous and inhalational agents on uterine tone and fetal physiology: most anesthetic agents cross the placenta to varying degrees. Volatile anesthetics in clinical concentrations can cause uterine relaxation (useful for intrauterine manipulation but potentially increasing hemorrhage). Propofol, opioids, and benzodiazepines cross the placenta; fetal depression is usually transient for single-dose exposures, but prolonged infusions can have greater effects. Maternal hypotension during induction (due to vasodilation or myocardial depression) reduces uteroplacental perfusion and must be prevented with preoxygenation, use of rapid sequence induction in appropriate cases, and prompt vasopressor support. Rapid sequence induction is often recommended for pregnant patients at risk of aspiration; cricoid pressure may be used but must be balanced against its potential to impair laryngoscopic view and ventilation. Airway management plans should anticipate difficulty: have difficult airway equipment and personnel available.

Intraoperative anesthetic technique should be individualized, and the decision should be documented in the chart with the maternal and fetal considerations. When possible, consultation with obstetrics and neonatology prior to planned procedures is prudent.

# Maternal positioning to avoid aortocaval compression

Aortocaval compression is a physiologic phenomenon caused by the gravid uterus compressing the inferior vena cava and, to some extent, the abdominal aorta when the patient lies supine. This compression reduces venous return to the heart, decreasing preload, cardiac output, and uteroplacental perfusion; it can result in maternal hypotension and fetal hypoxia. The risk increases as the uterus enlarges, typically after 20 weeks’ gestation, and is most pronounced in the third trimester.

Clinical goals for positioning are to relieve compression of the great vessels while maintaining patient access for surgery. The standard technique is left uterine displacement (LUD). A left lateral tilt of 15 degrees is usually sufficient to relieve inferior vena cava compression; this can be achieved by placing a wedge under the right hip or by tilting the entire operating table. Some clinicians prefer a 15–30 degree tilt depending on the patient’s body habitus and intraoperative hemodynamic response. Complete left lateral decubitus positioning is ideal for prevention of compression but may be impractical for many surgical procedures.

For cesarean delivery under neuraxial anesthesia, placing the patient supine with a left tilt or wedge and careful monitoring is standard. In patients undergoing general anesthesia, a left tilt should be maintained throughout induction and emergence because even short periods of aortocaval compression during these vulnerable phases can have consequences. Intraoperative positioning must also take into account the potential for pressure injuries, peripheral nerve compression, and the needs of the surgical team; padding and frequent reassessment are necessary.

When left tilt is not feasible (certain neurosurgical or cardiac procedures require strict head/neck positioning), alternative strategies include manual displacement of the uterus by an assistant, gradual table tilt adjustments, and hemodynamic management with fluids and vasoactive drugs. If aortocaval compression is suspected (sudden hypotension and decreased end-tidal carbon dioxide or uterine tone), immediate leftward displacement and hemodynamic support are indicated.

# Monitoring maternal oxygenation, hemodynamics and temperature

Maintaining adequate maternal oxygenation, hemodynamic stability, and normothermia is essential for maternal safety and fetal oxygen delivery. Pregnancy physiologic changes modify normal ranges and require attention to specific targets.

Oxygenation: Maternal oxygen consumption increases in pregnancy, while FRC decreases, shortening the acceptable apnea period. Preoxygenation prior to induction of general anesthesia is critical. Continuous pulse oximetry is the standard; aim to maintain SpO2 ≥95% in pregnant patients to ensure adequate maternal arterial oxygen partial pressure and fetal oxygen delivery. If supplemental oxygen is required, low-flow oxygen via nasal cannula or face mask is typically used intraoperatively. In intubated patients, maintain adequate ventilation to keep maternal arterial oxygen and carbon dioxide within target ranges (see ventilation below).

Ventilation and carbon dioxide: Pregnancy is characterized by a state of chronic respiratory alkalosis due to increased minute ventilation, producing a baseline maternal PaCO2 of approximately 30 mmHg (normal nonpregnant PaCO2 ~40 mmHg). During general anesthesia with controlled ventilation, target end-tidal CO2 (ETCO2) is commonly set to approximate the lower maternal baseline: ETCO2 ≈ 30 mmHg or such that arterial PaCO2 is in the range of 30–35 mmHg. Note that ETCO2 underestimates PaCO2 by a variable gradient, but using ETCO2 values and periodic arterial blood gases when needed is reasonable. Hypocapnia (excessive hyperventilation with PaCO2 <25 mmHg) reduces uterine blood flow via vasoconstriction; hypercapnia compromises maternal and fetal oxygenation. Therefore, moderate hypocapnia consistent with pregnancy baseline is acceptable; avoid extremes. When regional anesthesia is used and the mother breathes spontaneously, the inherent hyperventilation of pregnancy typically maintains an appropriate PaCO2.

Hemodynamics: Maternal blood volume expands in pregnancy and systemic vascular resistance decreases; cardiac output increases. Uteroplacental perfusion is pressure-dependent and has no autoregulation, so maternal blood pressure is a key determinant. Maintain maternal mean arterial pressure (MAP) close to baseline—commonly within 10–20%—to preserve uteroplacental blood flow. There is no single universal MAP target, but avoiding MAP <65 mmHg is a reasonable minimum; for pregnant patients with chronic hypertension or preeclampsia, targets should be individualized to their baseline pressures. Prompt treatment of hypotension following neuraxial blockade (e.g., with phenylephrine or ephedrine) is important; phenylephrine is often first-line in obstetric anesthesia because it restores blood pressure rapidly and maintains fetal acid–base status better than ephedrine in many studies, although ephedrine retains a role particularly with bradycardia. Use vasopressors titrated to effect and consider continuous infusions for persistent hypotension. Cardiac output monitoring may be useful in selected high-risk cases.

Monitoring modalities: Standard monitors per the American Society of Anesthesiologists include continuous ECG, noninvasive blood pressure, pulse oximetry, capnography (for intubated or sedated patients), temperature, and urine output when appropriate. Invasive arterial blood pressure monitoring is indicated for major surgery, expected large fluid shifts, severe preeclampsia, or when frequent arterial blood gases are needed. Central venous pressure may be helpful for major resuscitation but has limited utility for routine cases. Advanced hemodynamic monitoring (esophageal Doppler, pulse contour analysis, cardiac output monitors) may be considered in high-risk patients.

Temperature: Maternal core temperature should be maintained within the normal physiologic range (approximately 36.5–37.5°C). Hypothermia increases the risk of coagulopathy, wound infection, delayed drug metabolism, and shivering that increases oxygen consumption. Actively warm patients with forced-air warming blankets, warmed intravenous fluids, and warmed irrigation fluids during lengthy procedures. Monitor temperature intraoperatively and address deviations promptly. Hyperthermia should also be avoided as maternal fever can be associated with fetal tachycardia and potential neurodevelopmental risks with prolonged exposure.

# Principles and techniques for intraoperative fetal monitoring and documentation

Goals and scope: Intraoperative fetal monitoring (IFM) aims to assess fetal well-being in real time during maternal surgery and anesthesia, detect acute fetal distress, and guide interventions. The decision to monitor intraoperatively depends on gestational age (fetal viability), type and urgency of surgery, expected maternal hemodynamic instability, and availability of personnel and neonatal support. In many institutions, continuous fetal heart rate (FHR) monitoring is considered for viable fetuses (generally ≥23–24 weeks’ gestation, though institutional viability thresholds vary) undergoing non-obstetric surgery when feasible. For previable fetuses or surgeries remote from the uterus with low risk to the fetus, intermittent monitoring or no intraoperative monitoring may be reasonable.

Monitoring modalities: The primary modality is external fetal heart rate monitoring with a cardiotocograph (CTG) that records fetal heart rate and uterine activity. Intraoperative use may be challenging due to surgical field, patient positioning, electrocautery, and interference from maternal heart rate; nevertheless, external ultrasound-based monitoring (Doppler) is commonly used. When external monitoring is inadequate (obesity, difficult placement) and if an obstetrician is present, internal fetal monitoring via fetal scalp electrode is an option during cesarean or when the membranes are ruptured and the cervix is dilated, but is rarely used in non-obstetric surgery.

Monitoring frequency and duration: For continuous monitoring, continuous CTG tracing for the entire procedure is ideal when fetal viability and resource availability allow, and when there is potential for rapid deterioration. If continuous monitoring is not feasible, obtain preoperative baseline fetal heart rate tracings (e.g., a nonstress test) and intraoperative intermittent checks before induction, after major anesthetic events (induction, significant hemodynamic changes), and immediately postoperatively.

Interpretation: Standard fetal heart rate baseline and reactivity parameters apply. Normal baseline FHR is 110–160 beats per minute (bpm). A reactive nonstress test (NST) criterion for fetuses ≥32 weeks is at least two accelerations of ≥15 bpm lasting ≥15 seconds within 20 minutes. For fetuses <32 weeks’ gestation, the threshold is two accelerations of ≥10 bpm lasting ≥10 seconds within 20 minutes (the 10×10 criterion). Abnormalities include baseline tachycardia (>160 bpm), baseline bradycardia (<110 bpm), reduced variability, recurrent variable decelerations, late decelerations (uteroplacental insufficiency), and prolonged decelerations (>2 minutes but <10 minutes). A prolonged deceleration of ≥10 minutes is considered a change in baseline and indicates severe compromise.

Documentation: Document fetal monitoring in the anesthetic record similarly to other intraoperative data. Record the baseline fetal heart rate when monitoring begins, frequency of fetal heart rate checks if intermittent, any significant changes (time, nature, duration), interventions performed in response (maternal repositioning, oxygen administration, fluids, vasopressors, tocolytics), and consultations (obstetrics, neonatology). Note the method of monitoring (external CTG, Doppler) and any technical limitations. Clear documentation is essential for continuity of care and medico-legal purposes.

Algorithm for abnormal tracings: When a nonreassuring fetal heart tracing is detected intraoperatively, the first steps are to optimize the maternal physiologic state, as most fetal compromise during surgery is secondary to maternal factors. Immediate interventions include ensuring left uterine displacement or left tilt to relieve aortocaval compression, increasing inspired oxygen concentration to 100%, increasing maternal blood pressure and cardiac output (fluids, vasopressors), correcting maternal hypoventilation or hyperventilation, and treating uterine hypertonus or tachysystole if present. If fetal bradycardia persists despite maternal optimization, and gestational age is viable, emergent obstetric evaluation and possible delivery (cesarean) may be required depending on the clinical context, surgical procedure, and maternal condition. Institutional protocols for intraoperative fetal distress should be established and rehearsed.

Limitations: Awareness of limitations is necessary. Continuous FHR monitoring in the operating room may be technically difficult during certain procedures or with the use of electrocautery and other equipment. Interference of maternal heart rate can confound tracings. CTG interpretation should be performed by personnel trained in fetal surveillance, and prompt communication between anesthesia and obstetric teams is essential.

# Use of tocolytics and uterine relaxants: indications, agents, and evidence

Definition and indications: Tocolytics and uterine relaxants are agents used to reduce uterine contractility. In the perioperative setting, indications include prevention or treatment of uterine contractions during or after non-obstetric surgery when uterine irritability is present, management of intraoperative uterine tetany (e.g., after manipulation), and facilitating certain fetal procedures or cesarean extensions. However, routine prophylactic tocolysis for non-obstetric surgery is not uniformly recommended and should be individualized; evidence for routine use is limited. Use is more common when uterine instrumentation occurs or there is a high risk of preterm labor.

Common agents and mechanisms:

- Beta-adrenergic agonists (terbutaline): Terbutaline is a selective beta-2 agonist that relaxes uterine smooth muscle by increasing cyclic AMP. It can be given subcutaneously (0.25 mg) or intravenously (titrated infusion). Side effects include maternal tachycardia, palpitations, hyperglycemia, hypokalemia, and pulmonary edema with excessive fluid administration. Terbutaline can cause fetal tachycardia. It is effective for short-term tocolysis but is not commonly used for prolonged maintenance therapy due to systemic side effects. In the United States, terbutaline for prolonged tocolysis carries safety cautions.

- Calcium channel blockers (nifedipine): Nifedipine inhibits uterine smooth muscle contractions by blocking L-type calcium channels. Oral immediate-release nifedipine (e.g., 10–20 mg loading followed by 10–20 mg every 3–6 hours) is widely used for tocolysis and is generally well tolerated. Adverse effects include maternal hypotension, headache, flushing, and tachycardia. In the intraoperative setting, caution is necessary because nifedipine-induced hypotension can compromise uteroplacental perfusion; if used intraoperatively, hemodynamic status must be monitored closely and vasopressors used as needed.

- Magnesium sulfate: Magnesium acts as a calcium antagonist and neuromuscular blocker and is used primarily for fetal neuroprotection before anticipated preterm delivery and for eclampsia prophylaxis/therapy. Magnesium has modest tocolytic effects but is not the most effective agent for tocolysis; it may be used in select situations. Side effects include maternal flushing, nausea, decreased deep tendon reflexes, respiratory depression at high levels, and effects on neonatal muscle tone if used near delivery.

- Nitroglycerin: Nitroglycerin provides rapid uterine relaxation by nitric oxide–mediated smooth muscle relaxation and systemic vasodilation. It is useful for short-term uterine relaxation in the operating room, for example to facilitate uterine manipulation, uterine relaxation during difficult fetal extraction, or management of uterine inversion. Intravenous boluses (50–100 micrograms) or short infusions can be titrated; sublingual or spray forms are alternatives in some settings. Because nitroglycerin causes systemic hypotension, it must be used cautiously with immediate hemodynamic monitoring and vasopressor support available. Nitroglycerin’s rapid onset and short duration make it attractive for short procedures requiring profound relaxation.

- Oxytocin antagonists (atosiban): Atosiban is an oxytocin receptor antagonist used for tocolysis in some countries; it is not approved in all jurisdictions (e.g., not approved widely in the United States). Atosiban selectively inhibits myometrial contractions mediated by oxytocin and has a favorable side-effect profile compared with beta-agonists. Its intraoperative utility is limited by availability and by the need for parenteral administration.

- Other agents: Indomethacin (a prostaglandin synthesis inhibitor) can be used as tocolysis, particularly before 32 weeks, but limited use intraoperatively is typical due to fetal effects (oligohydramnios, premature constriction of the ductus arteriosus) and maternal renal effects.

Evidence and practical guidance: The evidence for routine prophylactic tocolysis for non-obstetric surgery is weak. Most guidelines recommend that tocolytics not be used routinely but consider them when there is uterine irritability, contractions, cervical change, or procedures that manipulate the uterus. If uterine contractions occur intraoperatively, initial management should focus on optimizing maternal oxygenation and hemodynamics and relieving mechanical stimuli (positioning, removing incision tension). If contractions persist or are judged to threaten preterm labor, tocolytics can be administered in consultation with obstetrics. Choose the agent based on the clinical scenario: nitroglycerin for immediate, short-term relaxation during an operation; nifedipine or terbutaline for ongoing tocolysis; magnesium for neuroprotection if preterm delivery is anticipated before 32 weeks (note timing and standard dosing); and avoid NSAID tocolytics near term due to ductus arteriosus concerns.

Monitoring during tocolysis: Monitor maternal heart rate, blood pressure, urine output, and serum electrolytes as appropriate. For terbutaline, watch for maternal tachycardia and signs of pulmonary edema. For nifedipine or nitroglycerin that can cause hypotension, have vasopressors ready and titrate doses to maintain MAP near baseline. If magnesium sulfate is used, monitor deep tendon reflexes, respiratory rate, and serum magnesium levels in prolonged infusions.

# Postoperative monitoring in the immediate maternal–fetal period

The immediate postoperative period demands focused monitoring of both mother and fetus because anesthetic and surgical effects may persist and complications such as bleeding, hemodynamic instability, aspiration pneumonitis, and uterine activity may present after the procedure.

Maternal monitoring: Continue standard postoperative monitoring until the patient meets discharge criteria from the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU). Key parameters include oxygenation (pulse oximetry; maintain SpO2 ≥95%), ventilation (respiratory rate and capnography where applicable), hemodynamics (frequent blood pressure and heart rate checks), urine output, pain control, temperature, and neurologic status. For patients who received neuraxial anesthesia, monitor sensory and motor block regression and watch for high spinal block symptoms. For patients who received opioids or sedatives intraoperatively, ensure adequate reversal and monitoring for respiratory depression. Early mobilization, thromboprophylaxis where indicated, and attention to fluid balance are also important.

Fetal monitoring and obstetric evaluation: For viable fetuses, perform fetal heart rate assessment in the PACU as soon after surgery as is feasible. If continuous intraoperative monitoring was performed, compare immediate postoperative fetal status to intraoperative baseline and document. For fetuses at or near viability, prolonged monitoring may be advisable for several hours postoperatively to detect delayed fetal compromise. For preterm fetuses at risk of preterm birth, consider neonatal consultation and, if indicated, administration of antenatal corticosteroids and magnesium sulfate according to gestational age and anticipated delivery timing (corticosteroids generally recommended for anticipated delivery before 34 0/7 weeks; institutional protocols may vary).

Uterine activity and bleeding: Monitor for uterine contractions, vaginal bleeding, and abdominal pain. Uterine irritability and preterm labor can occur after surgery. Document uterine tone and bleeding status, and involve obstetrics if uterine contractions, cervical change, or bleeding are noted. Assess the possibility of surgical causes of bleeding in the abdomen versus obstetric causes.

Analgesia and medications: Provide multimodal analgesia that is safe in pregnancy and compatible with breastfeeding for the postpartum period. Regional techniques (e.g., epidural analgesia) can provide effective pain control and minimize systemic opioid use. If opioids are used, monitor for neonatal respiratory depression if delivery occurs soon after administration. Review all medications given perioperatively for fetal safety; counsel the patient and obstetric team if potentially teratogenic agents were used in the first trimester. Document time and doses of sedative and analgesic agents.

Disposition and follow-up: Determine postoperative disposition based on maternal stability, gestational age, and fetal status. Patients with any intraoperative or postoperative maternal hemodynamic instability, nonreassuring fetal monitoring, preterm labor, or significant obstetric concern should be admitted for further observation and obstetric review. For otherwise straightforward cases with stable maternal and fetal status, admission decisions should follow usual post-surgical criteria, but ensure obstetric follow-up within an appropriate timeframe (e.g., same day or 24–48 hours) for high-risk pregnancies or earlier if concerns exist.

Communication and documentation: Effective communication between anesthesia, surgery, obstetrics, and neonatology is essential. Document the anesthetic course, maternal intraoperative events and interventions, fetal monitoring results, medications with times and doses, and specific recommendations for postoperative fetal surveillance and follow-up. Explicit handoff instructions should include when to call obstetrics or neonatology, parameters for concern (e.g., new uterine contractions, fetal heart rate <110 or >160 bpm, maternal hypotension), and anticipated next steps.

Conclusion

Perioperative management of pregnant patients requires an integrated approach that considers physiologic changes of pregnancy, the effects of anesthetic techniques and medications on uteroplacental perfusion and fetal oxygenation, and careful maternal and fetal monitoring before, during, and after the procedure. Regional anesthesia is preferred when appropriate, but both regional and general anesthesia can be applied safely with attention to airway planning, hemodynamic targets, oxygenation, and temperature maintenance. Positioning to avoid aortocaval compression, appropriate maternal monitoring, prudent use of fetal monitoring based on viability and institutional resources, judicious use of tocolytics when clinically indicated, and coordinated postoperative surveillance are essential components of safe perioperative care for pregnant patients.

Introduction

Pregnant patients who undergo abdominal or pelvic surgery for adnexal masses, uterine fibroids (myomectomy), or other pelvic pathology require carefully tailored obstetric follow-up. The intersection of postoperative physiologic changes, potential impacts on the pregnant uterus and placenta, and the risks introduced by residual or recurrent pelvic masses mandates structured surveillance, clear thresholds for intervention, and anticipatory delivery planning. This chapter reviews practical postoperative fetal surveillance protocols, evidence-based medical strategies to reduce miscarriage or preterm birth risk (including progesterone and tocolytics), criteria that guide timing and mode of delivery after pregnancy-associated pelvic surgery or prior myomectomy, assessment of how residual masses influence labor mechanics, clinical considerations when placental location is affected by myomas, and counseling points on breastfeeding and timing of any further surgery. The emphasis is clinical reasoning—linking pathophysiology to monitoring choices and delivery decisions.

# Postoperative fetal surveillance protocols and frequency of obstetric follow-up

Pelvic or abdominal surgery during pregnancy can produce a range of fetal risks—preterm labor, fetal distress from intraoperative hemodynamic or oxygenation changes, and less commonly, placental injury. Surveillance should therefore be individualized on the basis of (1) gestational age at surgery, (2) type and extent of surgery (laparoscopy versus laparotomy; whether the uterine cavity was entered), (3) intraoperative events (maternal hypotension, hypoxia, substantial blood loss, uterine manipulation), and (4) fetal status in the immediate perioperative period.

Immediate postoperative period (first 24–72 hours). For viable fetuses (commonly defined as ≥20–24 weeks depending on local practice), an assessment of fetal well-being is mandatory after recovery from anesthesia. This typically includes a fetal heart rate auscultation or continuous fetal monitoring (cardiotocography) for patients ≥24 weeks with concern for contractions or nonreassuring maternal course. If the procedure occurred prior to viability, a transvaginal or transabdominal ultrasound to confirm cardiac activity is appropriate within 24 hours. For viable pregnancies with an uneventful surgery and no concerning intraoperative findings, one or two assessments of fetal heart rate (by Doppler) and a clinical maternal assessment (pain, uterine tenderness, bleeding) in the first 24 hours may suffice.

Short-term follow‑up (first 2–4 weeks). A formal obstetric visit within 1–2 weeks after surgery is commonly recommended. That visit should include a targeted ultrasound to confirm fetal growth appropriate to gestational age and to evaluate amniotic fluid, placental location (if not previously documented), and any residual pelvic masses. If the surgery was for adnexal masses or myomas, documentation of mass size and relation to cervix/placenta is important at this visit. If the patient reports uterine contractions, vaginal bleeding, or decreased fetal movement, more urgent evaluation (nonstress test [NST], biophysical profile [BPP]) is indicated.

Ongoing surveillance through pregnancy. Frequency of routine visits follows standard obstetric schedules (every 4 weeks until 28 weeks, every 2 weeks until 36 weeks, then weekly). However, pregnancies complicated by significant intraoperative events, significant residual pelvic masses, or any entrance into the uterine cavity during surgery should be considered higher risk and managed with increased surveillance. This may include more frequent growth ultrasounds (every 4 weeks or as clinically indicated), serial cervical length assessments (transvaginal ultrasound) when preterm birth risk exists, and periodic NSTs or BPPs beginning in the third trimester if there are concerns about placental perfusion or fetal growth restriction. Doppler studies (umbilical artery pulsatility index) are appropriate when growth restriction is suspected or there were intraoperative events suggesting compromised uteroplacental blood flow.

Indications for antenatal fetal surveillance modalities. Nonstress testing is indicated for decreased fetal movement, maternal conditions that predispose to fetal compromise, or concerns after surgery. A biophysical profile (score 8–10 normal) complements NST when fetal movements are reduced or when NST is nonreactive. Cervical length is used when assessing preterm birth risk: a short cervix is generally <25 mm before 24 weeks and is a validated predictor of spontaneous preterm birth. Ultrasound documentation of placental location becomes essential if the surgical field included the lower uterine segment or the uterus was manipulated.

Disposition and threshold for obstetric admission. Postoperative uterine contractions, cervical change, vaginal bleeding, oligohydramnios, or any nonreassuring fetal testing should prompt admission for obstetric monitoring and management. Clear discharge instructions must emphasize warning signs—regular uterine contractions, leaking fluid, heavy vaginal bleeding, or decreased fetal movement—so patients seek immediate care.

# Use of progesterone and other agents for miscarriage or preterm birth prevention: indications and evidence

Progesterone is the most widely studied pharmacologic intervention for prevention of recurrent early pregnancy loss and spontaneous preterm birth (sPTB). Its role differs depending on the clinical scenario: prevention of miscarriage in early pregnancy, prevention of sPTB in women with prior spontaneous preterm birth, and prevention in women with a short cervix on ultrasound.

Progesterone for recurrent early pregnancy loss. For women with a history of recurrent pregnancy loss (commonly defined as two or more consecutive losses), progesterone supplementation in the first trimester has been evaluated. Evidence supports a benefit in women with early pregnancy bleeding and prior losses, though randomized trials show mixed results. Clinical practice often uses vaginal micronized progesterone (e.g., 200 mg vaginally nightly) or oral dydrogesterone in the first trimester for women with threatened miscarriage and prior losses—recognizing that large, high-quality trials (e.g., PRISM trial) suggest benefit primarily in the subgroup with prior loss.

Progesterone to prevent spontaneous preterm birth. Two major contexts:

1) Women with prior spontaneous preterm birth. For women with a singleton pregnancy and a history of spontaneous preterm birth (prior spontaneous delivery <37 weeks), intramuscular 17-alpha hydroxyprogesterone caproate (17-OHPC) 250 mg weekly has been widely used historically to reduce recurrence. However, recent high-quality evidence (e.g., the PROLONG trial) has brought controversy—showing no significant benefit in heterogeneous populations—leading to reconsideration of universal 17-OHPC use. Current guidance tends toward individualized decision-making: consider progesterone for those with a clear prior spontaneous singleton preterm birth, after counseling on the mixed evidence and local guideline recommendations. Notably, 17-OHPC is given from 16–36 weeks gestation.

2) Women with a short cervix without prior preterm birth. Vaginal progesterone (e.g., 90 mg gel nightly or 200 mg micronized vaginal progesterone nightly) for women with a short cervical length (<25 mm before 24 weeks) reduces the rate of preterm birth and improves neonatal outcomes across several randomized trials and meta-analyses. This indication is well supported and commonly applied.

Choice of formulation. Vaginal progesterone is preferred for cervical shortening and is commonly used for threatened miscarriage. 17-OHPC (intramuscular) has been the standard in the U.S. for prior spontaneous preterm birth but, as above, requires individualized counseling. Oral formulations vary in bioavailability and are less consistently studied for sPTB prevention.

Safety considerations. Progesterone is generally well tolerated. There are no consistent data linking prenatal progesterone exposure to major congenital anomalies. Side effects may include local irritation (vaginal forms) or injection site reactions (IM 17-OHPC).

Tocolytics and adjuvant agents. Tocolysis aims to delay delivery long enough for antenatal corticosteroids to act (optimally 48 hours) or to permit in utero transfer. Common agents include nifedipine (oral calcium channel blocker), which is widely used and has evidence supporting short-term pregnancy prolongation and improved maternal tolerability compared with beta-agonists. Beta-agonists (e.g., terbutaline) are generally used less due to maternal side effects. Indomethacin (a prostaglandin-synthesis inhibitor) is an effective tocolytic but is generally limited to gestations <32 weeks and used short-term because of fetal risks (oligohydramnios, premature closure of ductus arteriosus) if used later. Atosiban (oxytocin receptor antagonist) is used in some countries and has a favorable side-effect profile but is not available in all jurisdictions.

Adjunctive measures. Corticosteroids for fetal lung maturation (e.g., betamethasone 12 mg IM x 2 doses 24 hours apart or dexamethasone 6 mg IM q12h x 4 doses) are recommended when delivery is anticipated before 34 weeks, with consideration up to 37 weeks in certain contexts. Magnesium sulfate for fetal neuroprotection is indicated for anticipated delivery before 32 weeks (commonly 4 g loading then 1 g/h infusion for up to 24 hours or until delivery) to reduce risk of cerebral palsy in the preterm infant.

Application to postoperative patients. Surgery itself is not an automatic indication for progesterone or tocolysis. Use of progesterone is driven by obstetric risk factors (e.g., short cervix, prior sPTB). Tocolysis after surgery is indicated if there is active preterm labor (regular uterine contractions with cervical change) and the goal is to delay delivery for steroid benefit or maternal stabilization. Routine prophylactic tocolysis after surgery is not recommended because benefits are unproven and risks exist. When early contractions occur immediately postoperatively, short-term tocolysis (e.g., nifedipine) may be used to blunt uterine activity while reassessing for other causes (infection, hematoma, uterine irritability).

# Criteria for timing and mode of delivery after prior myomectomy or pelvic surgery during pregnancy

Determining timing and mode of delivery after intrapartum or antepartum pelvic surgery (particularly myomectomy) requires evaluation of the uterine incision type, whether the uterine cavity was entered, extent and location of the myomectomy, and the number and depth of myometrial layers repaired.

Risk of uterine rupture after myomectomy. The principal concern is uterine rupture during labor. Rupture risk after myomectomy is generally low but not negligible and is influenced by operative factors: transmural or deep intramural myomas requiring entry into the uterine cavity, repair of multiple layers, large myomas (>5 cm) in the fundus or lower segment, and classical (vertical) uterine incisions increase rupture risk. Minimally invasive myomectomy with small fascial disruption seems to have lower rupture risk than extensive open myomectomy that involves a full-thickness incision. Published cumulative risk estimates vary; many series report uterine rupture rates after myomectomy between 0.5% and 2% depending on case mix.

Mode of delivery decision-making. Individualized counseling is essential. If the myomectomy involved only superficial, small intramural excisions without entry into the endometrial cavity and with a robust multilayer repair, a trial of labor after myomectomy (TOL) may be reasonable. When the uterine cavity was entered, large myomas were removed, the lower uterine segment was involved, or the repair was uncertain/multiple-layer repair was incomplete, many clinicians recommend cesarean delivery to avoid the risk of rupture during labor.

Key operative features that favor cesarean delivery include:

- Documented entry into the endometrial cavity at time of myomectomy. 
- Multiple deep intramural myomas requiring extensive myometrial excision.
- A classical—or vertical—fundal incision or other incision that disrupts the contractile myometrium significantly.
- Poor hemostatic or tissue quality compromising the repair.
- Uncertain or unavailable operative records to characterize the repair (absence of records often pushes toward cesarean in higher-risk scenarios).

Timing of delivery. In uncomplicated situations where a TOL is planned, timing is dictated by obstetric indications rather than a fixed post-myomectomy interval. No standard mandate requires scheduled cesarean at a specific gestational age solely due to prior myomectomy. If cesarean is chosen, scheduling around 39 weeks is typical unless other indications prompt earlier delivery. When concern for scar integrity is present (e.g., large prior uterine repair), some clinicians consider scheduled cesarean at 37–39 weeks to avoid spontaneous labor, but there is no universal consensus; decisions should balance neonatal maturity and rupture risk.

Informed consent and documentation. Before labor onset, clear counseling should include discussion of rupture risk (including signs/symptoms: sudden severe abdominal pain, vaginal bleeding, loss of fetal station, abnormal fetal heart tracing), alternatives, and the rationale for either trial of labor or planned cesarean. Availability of surgical and anesthesia back-up should be ensured for cases where the threshold for cesarean is low.

Implications of other pelvic surgeries. Surgeries that do not alter uterine integrity (e.g., adnexal surgery, pelvic lymphadenectomy) generally do not mandate cesarean delivery. However, the presence of residual pelvic masses in the lower pelvis or extensive adhesions that could obstruct descent may influence mode of delivery (see next section).

# Assessment of residual masses and their impact on labor and delivery (obstructed labor risk)

Residual pelvic masses—fundal myomas, lower segment myomas, adnexal masses, or postoperative hematomas—can mechanically obstruct labor, distort uterine contractility, and increase risk of malpresentation or labor dystocia. Assessing impact requires attention to size, number, location relative to the birth canal and cervix, and change over gestation.

Mass characteristics and clinical relevance. Large myomas (>5–6 cm) are more likely to influence pregnancy and labor, but even smaller myomas in critical locations can be clinically significant. Subserosal or fundal myomas typically have less effect on labor mechanics than intramural or submucosal myomas or those in the lower uterine segment/cervix. A lower-segment myoma near the internal os, a cervical myoma, or a large adnexal mass can physically block descent and produce obstructed labor. Multiple myomas that distort the uterine cavity can impede effective contractions, increasing the need for operative delivery.

Assessment modalities. Serial ultrasound is the primary imaging tool for assessing residual masses—documenting size, echotexture, vascularity, and relation to cervix and placenta. If ultrasound is limited, MRI offers superior soft tissue contrast and is safe in pregnancy (preferably without gadolinium), and may clarify whether a mass is likely to obstruct the birth canal. Imaging should be repeated if symptoms emerge (pain, increasing uterine tenderness, or suspicion for rapid growth/degeneration) or when planning delivery.

Predicting obstructed labor. No simple size threshold guarantees obstruction; rather, consider the triad of mass size, location, and mobility. A sessile, firm mass in the lower uterine segment or cervix that occupies a significant proportion of the diameter of the pelvic inlet is most concerning. Pre-labor planning includes assessing whether the mass is likely to prevent engagement of the fetal head. For example, a 5–10 cm mass low in the pelvis is more problematic than the same mass at the fundus.

Delivery planning for obstructive masses. If imaging suggests high likelihood of mechanical obstruction at term, a planned cesarean delivery is appropriate. For borderline cases, a plan for attempted labor with a low threshold for cesarean if descent does not progress is reasonable only when the team is prepared for urgent operative delivery. Intrapartum monitoring should include close assessment of labor progress and fetal status. Attempted vaginal delivery with instrumental assistance or cesarean extraction should be decided on a case-by-case basis. Myomectomy at the time of cesarean (cesarean myomectomy) may be feasible in select cases at experienced centers but carries increased hemorrhage risk; prior planning for blood products and surgical support is mandatory.

Special consideration for degenerating masses. Myomas can undergo red degeneration during pregnancy, causing pain and sometimes fever and leukocytosis—these episodes may transiently alter uterine contractility. Conservative management may be adequate for pain control; however, persistent irritation and uterine irritability near term might necessitate expedited delivery decisions.

# Placenta–myoma interactions, placental localization and monitoring

The placenta and uterine myomas may interact in ways that influence placental implantation, perfusion, and risk of abnormal placentation.

Placental location and displacement. Large myomas can displace the placenta toward a less ideal site. A placenta overlying a large submucosal or intramural myoma may be partially impeded from normal implantation. Placental location should be documented early (first- or early second-trimester ultrasound) and reassessed in the third trimester when placenta previa risk is evaluated. Placental migration is common: an apparent placenta previa identified in the second trimester often resolves by the third trimester as the lower uterine segment develops.

Placenta previa and accreta spectrum. Uterine surgery, including myomectomy with cavity entry, is a risk factor for abnormal placentation (placenta accreta spectrum), because scarring can impair normal decidualization and predispose to abnormal trophoblastic invasion. When placenta overlies a prior myomectomy site or uterine scar, the risk of placenta accreta increases. Therefore, in patients with prior uterine surgery and a placenta positioned low in the uterus, targeted ultrasound for accreta features and, if needed, MRI without gadolinium can be used for further assessment. Sonographic signs suggestive of accreta include loss of the clear zone between placenta and myometrium, placental lacunae with turbulent flow, and abnormal uterine-bladder interface.

Monitoring for placental insufficiency. Large intramural myomas can theoretically impair uterine blood flow to portions of the placenta, predisposing to fetal growth restriction or abnormal Doppler studies. If there is concern—based on serial growth charts, low fundal height, or abnormal Doppler velocimetry—then increased surveillance with growth ultrasounds every 2–4 weeks and periodic umbilical artery Doppler studies are warranted. The frequency depends on severity; abnormal Dopplers or growth restriction may prompt increased fetal surveillance (NST, BPP) and earlier delivery if compromise is progressive.

Management of suspected accreta. When placenta accreta is suspected antenatally, delivery planning must be multidisciplinary including maternal-fetal medicine, anesthesia, blood bank, interventional radiology (if available for balloon catheterization), and surgical teams. Timing of delivery is generally scheduled (often 34–36+6 weeks in controlled settings) to balance maternal risks and fetal maturity, with corticosteroids administered for lung maturity. These considerations fall outside of routine postoperative follow-up but are critical when placental pathology is identified after pelvic surgery.

# Counseling about breastfeeding and implications for further surgical planning postpartum

Breastfeeding and timing of subsequent pelvic surgery intersect through considerations of lactation physiology, medication safety, anesthesia and analgesia, and timing for optimal surgical outcomes.

Breastfeeding physiology and immediate postpartum period. Lactogenesis II typically begins 2–3 days postpartum, but early milk production and establishment of lactation benefit from skin-to-skin contact and frequent breastfeeding. Counseling should emphasize that early postoperative pain control regimens should be compatible with breastfeeding (e.g., acetaminophen and most opioids in short courses are considered compatible, but informed dosing and monitoring for sedation is essential). If maternal surgery is planned in the immediate postpartum period, impact on lactation initiation should be discussed.

Impact of medications. Many perioperative medications are compatible with breastfeeding. Local anesthetics and most general anesthetics are minimally excreted in breastmilk; guidelines generally allow breastfeeding to resume after anesthesia once the mother is alert and able to safely hold and feed the infant. Certain medications used postoperatively—some anticoagulants, methadone, or specific antidepressants—require counseling about risks and may necessitate temporary pumping and discarding of breastmilk or alternative feeding strategies.

Timing of elective pelvic surgery postpartum. For nonurgent elective surgery (including definitive myomectomy, adnexal surgery, or definitive repair of pelvic pathology), delaying for uterine involution and recovery is typical. Many surgeons recommend waiting at least 6–12 weeks postpartum; some prefer waiting 3–6 months to reduce vascularity and optimize tissue planes. Longer intervals (3–6 months) may also be chosen to allow completion of breastfeeding if the mother desires; surgery while lactating can sometimes impair milk supply transiently depending on stress, medications, and separation from the infant.

Breastfeeding and future fertility/surgical outcomes. Breastfeeding does not generally preclude future pelvic surgery; however, clinicians should discuss that breastfeeding at the time of elective surgery may necessitate interruption and temporary pumping to maintain supply. If a postpartum hysterectomy becomes necessary, early cessation of breastfeeding will affect lactation. Counseling about contraception postpartum is also relevant, especially for those who require interval surgery and wish to avoid conception shortly after procedures.

Coordination with pediatric care. If perioperative medications are expected to reach breastmilk and pose neonatal risks, coordinate with pediatric providers about monitoring for sedation or respiratory depression. Breastfeeding mothers who must take medications incompatible with breastfeeding should be provided a plan for milk expression and safe storage, and for when to resume direct breastfeeding.

Planning future pregnancies and surgeries. For women who plan additional pelvic surgery to address residual masses that impacted the pregnancy, timing and surgical approach can be influenced by lactational status. Hormonal milieu during lactation (prolactin elevated, estrogen lower) may influence tissue healing and bleeding risk minimally, but the primary considerations are maternal preference, breastfeeding logistics, and optimizing surgical conditions (less pelvic vascularity after involution). Counseling should include discussion of contraceptive plans if pregnancy delay is desired until after surgery.

Conclusion

Obstetric management after pelvic surgery in pregnancy requires integration of perioperative events, surgical details, imaging findings, and obstetric risk stratification. Vigilant early postoperative surveillance, targeted use of progesterone when indicated by obstetric risk factors, judicious application of tocolytics for active preterm labor, and individualized delivery planning—particularly after myomectomy—are the pillars of safe care. Understanding how residual masses and placental relationships affect labor mechanics and placental function allows anticipatory planning for mode and timing of delivery. Counseling about breastfeeding and timing of future surgery completes the continuum of care, ensuring safe maternal and neonatal outcomes while respecting patient preferences.

Introduction

Pregnancy alters physiology and pharmacokinetics in ways that influence the safety and efficacy of medications used for the assessment and perioperative management of pelvic masses. Clinicians caring for pregnant patients must balance maternal benefit with fetal safety, integrate gestational-age–dependent risks, and choose agents that minimize teratogenicity, fetal toxicity, and obstetric complications. This chapter reviews pharmacologic options commonly encountered when evaluating or operating on pregnant patients with pelvic masses: analgesics, antibiotics, progesterone formulations for miscarriage and preterm birth prevention, iron therapy, transfusion considerations, and drugs that are contraindicated or require caution. For each class, we emphasize mechanism of action, pharmacokinetic considerations in pregnancy, typical dosing principles, safety profiles by trimester, and relevant clinical decision points.

# Analgesics: paracetamol, NSAIDs (limitations in pregnancy), opioid considerations

Analgesia in pregnancy must achieve adequate maternal comfort while minimizing fetal risk. Drug absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion change in pregnancy—plasma volume and glomerular filtration rate increase, and protein binding may change—so dosing and intervals sometimes require adjustment. Always aim for the lowest effective dose and shortest effective duration; however, undertreated maternal pain has physiologic and psychological consequences that can adversely affect both mother and fetus.

Paracetamol (acetaminophen). Paracetamol is the first-line analgesic in pregnancy because of its favorable safety profile, wide therapeutic index, and effectiveness for mild-to-moderate pain and fever. Mechanism of action is not fully understood but involves central inhibition of prostaglandin synthesis (COX inhibition in the CNS), modulation of serotonergic pathways, and cannabinoid receptor-mediated effects. Typical adult dosing is 500–1000 mg every 4–6 hours as needed, with a maximum of 3–4 g/day (many guidelines now recommend not exceeding 3 g/day to limit hepatotoxicity). In pregnancy, standard dosing is used but clinicians should counsel on avoiding chronic high-dose use. Epidemiologic studies have raised concerns about potential associations between prolonged prenatal paracetamol exposure and neurodevelopmental outcomes (e.g., attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, autism spectrum disorder), but these studies are subject to confounding and have not established causality; current recommendations still endorse paracetamol for short-term use when clinically indicated.

Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs). NSAIDs inhibit cyclooxygenase enzymes (COX-1 and COX-2), reducing prostaglandin synthesis. NSAIDs are effective analgesics and antipyretics, and perioperatively they reduce opioid requirements. However, in pregnancy their use is gestational-age dependent and limited by specific fetal risks. In the first trimester, some studies suggest a small increased risk of miscarriage and certain congenital anomalies with high-dose or prolonged NSAID exposure, though findings are inconsistent. The major concern is in the late second and third trimesters: NSAIDs can cause premature constriction or closure of the fetal ductus arteriosus due to reduced prostaglandin E2, and they can impair fetal renal perfusion leading to oligohydramnios. They can also inhibit uterine contractions and prolong labor. Thus, NSAIDs are generally avoided after 30 weeks’ gestation (many guidelines recommend avoiding them after 32 weeks, and some specify avoidance after 20–30 weeks depending on agent and dose), and should be used cautiously in the second trimester. When indicated in early pregnancy, use the lowest effective dose for the shortest duration. If NSAIDs are used near term, fetal echocardiography may be considered if prolonged exposure is suspected and there are signs of oligohydramnios or abnormal fetal Dopplers.

Opioids. Opioids remain important for moderate-to-severe acute pain and for perioperative analgesia when nonopioid options are insufficient. Mechanistically, opioids act as agonists at mu, kappa, and delta opioid receptors to reduce ascending pain signaling and alter emotional pain perception. Short-term, intermittent opioid use during pregnancy (e.g., perioperative) has not been clearly linked to major congenital anomalies, but chronic opioid exposure, especially in the first trimester, has been associated in some studies with increased risk of certain malformations and adverse neonatal outcomes. Late-pregnancy opioid exposure poses the risk of neonatal respiratory depression at delivery and neonatal opioid withdrawal syndrome (NOWS) if exposure is prolonged. Clinical principles include: use the lowest effective dose for the shortest duration, avoid combination with benzodiazepines, counsel patients about potential neonatal effects, and ensure obstetric and neonatal teams are aware of opioid exposure near delivery. For acute perioperative pain, short-acting opioids (e.g., fentanyl, morphine in single perioperative doses) are commonly used; dosing must consider increased volume of distribution and altered clearance in pregnancy. Regional analgesia (e.g., neuraxial techniques) should be used when appropriate to reduce systemic opioid requirements.

Clinical decision points. For mild pain, prefer paracetamol. Reserve NSAIDs for early pregnancy only when benefits outweigh risks and avoid in the late second and third trimesters. Use opioids sparingly, with anticipatory planning for neonatal monitoring if exposure occurs close to delivery. Document gestational age and counsel the patient on fetal risks.

# Antibiotics: classes considered safe in pregnancy, selection for urinary or surgical infections

Antibiotic selection in pregnancy prioritizes agents with established safety profiles, appropriate penetration to the site of infection, and stewardship principles to limit resistance. Many commonly used antibiotics are safe in pregnancy; others are contraindicated or used only with caution because of potential teratogenicity, effects on fetal development, or neonatal toxicity.

Beta-lactams (penicillins and cephalosporins). These are first-line for many infections in pregnancy because of robust safety data. Mechanism: inhibition of bacterial cell wall synthesis via binding to penicillin-binding proteins. Penicillins (e.g., amoxicillin, ampicillin) and first- to third-generation cephalosporins (e.g., cefazolin, cephalexin, cefuroxime, ceftriaxone) are widely used. For urinary tract infections (UTIs) and surgical prophylaxis related to pelvic masses, cephazolin 1–2 g IV at induction is standard for many procedures. For complicated urinary infections or pyelonephritis, ceftriaxone 1–2 g IV daily is commonly used.

Macrolides. Azithromycin and erythromycin are generally considered safe when needed; clarithromycin has some concerns and is often avoided. Macrolides inhibit bacterial protein synthesis at the 50S ribosomal subunit. Use azithromycin for atypical infections or in penicillin-allergic patients when appropriate.

Nitrofurantoin and fosfomycin for UTI. Nitrofurantoin (macrocrystalline formulation, typically 100 mg twice daily for 5–7 days) is effective for lower UTIs and is generally safe during pregnancy except some recommend avoiding it at term (38–42 weeks) and during labor because of theoretical risk of neonatal hemolysis in G6PD deficiency and rare reports of maternal pulmonary reactions. Fosfomycin (single 3 g dose) is another option for uncomplicated cystitis. Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX) is effective for UTIs but has cautions: avoid in the first trimester (folate antagonist potentially associated with congenital anomalies) and near term (risk of neonatal kernicterus from displacement of bilirubin). If used for essential indications, folate supplementation and informed discussion are required.

Aminoglycosides. Agents such as gentamicin are associated with fetal ototoxicity and nephrotoxicity in prolonged therapy; however, short courses for severe infections can be used with careful monitoring when no safer alternative exists. They act by inhibiting the 30S ribosomal subunit. Use in pregnancy is reserved for severe infections (e.g., gram-negative sepsis) and combined with beta-lactams for synergy where indicated.

Metronidazole. Historically controversial in the first trimester, more recent data support its safety for treatment of trichomoniasis, bacterial vaginosis, and anaerobic infections; concern remains theoretical for mutagenicity but clinical use is common when indicated.

Fluoroquinolones. Generally avoided in pregnancy because of concerns from animal data regarding arthropathy in offspring and limited human safety data. In some severe, resistant infections where no alternatives exist, they may be used with caution.

Selection for urinary infections. For asymptomatic bacteriuria in pregnancy, screen and treat because untreated bacteriuria increases risk of pyelonephritis and preterm birth. First-line options include nitrofurantoin (avoid at term) and beta-lactams based on sensitivity (e.g., cephalexin). For acute pyelonephritis, hospitalize and treat with IV ceftriaxone or a beta-lactam; add gentamicin only if needed. Duration typically 7–14 days depending on clinical response.

Perioperative and surgical infections. Surgical prophylaxis for gynecologic and obstetric procedures commonly uses cefazolin 1–2 g IV within 60 minutes of incision; for beta-lactam allergy, clindamycin plus gentamicin is an option. For intra-abdominal or pelvic infections, select agents covering gram-negative bacilli and anaerobes—e.g., cefoxitin or a combination of ceftriaxone plus metronidazole. For severe sepsis, follow local sepsis protocols but favor beta-lactams with established pregnancy safety.

Clinical decision points. Always check local resistance patterns. For penicillin-allergic patients, ascertain the nature of the allergy because many labeled allergies are not true IgE–mediated reactions; skin testing or graded challenge may allow safer beta-lactam use. Avoid TMP-SMX in first trimester and near delivery, avoid fluoroquinolones and tetracyclines (tetracyclines cause permanent enamel and bone effects), and use aminoglycosides only when necessary for short durations with monitoring.

# Progesterone formulations for prevention of miscarriage/preterm birth: IM vs vaginal, dosing principles

Progesterone plays a central role in maintaining pregnancy by promoting uterine quiescence, modulating immune tolerance, and supporting decidualization. Pharmacologic progesterone supplementation is used in specific obstetric contexts: prevention of recurrent miscarriage in selected cases, and prevention of preterm birth in women with prior spontaneous preterm birth or a short cervix.

Formulations and routes. Progesterone is available in several formulations: vaginal (suppositories, tablets, gels), oral micronized progesterone, and intramuscular (IM) 17-hydroxyprogesterone caproate (17-OHPC). The pharmacokinetics differ by route: vaginal administration achieves high local endometrial and uterine tissue concentrations with minimal systemic exposure, whereas IM 17-OHPC achieves prolonged systemic levels with weekly dosing. Oral micronized progesterone is subject to extensive first-pass hepatic metabolism and may cause sedation due to metabolites.

Prevention of recurrent preterm birth. For women with a prior spontaneous preterm birth (typically defined as delivery before 37 weeks due to spontaneous labor or preterm premature rupture of membranes), IM 17-OHPC 250 mg weekly, starting at 16–20 weeks and continuing until 36 weeks, was historically widely used based on randomized data showing reduced recurrent preterm birth in some populations. However, large subsequent trials and meta-analyses have raised questions about efficacy in broader populations, and some regulatory bodies have modified recommendations. Current practice often individualizes therapy: 17-OHPC remains an option for women with a singleton gestation and a history of a prior spontaneous preterm birth, after counseling on benefits and uncertainties. Note that 17-OHPC is contraindicated in active thrombosis or certain hormone-sensitive conditions.

Vaginal progesterone for short cervix or prior preterm birth. Vaginal progesterone (commonly 200 mg nightly or 90 mg vaginal gel daily, depending on formulation) is effective for reducing preterm birth risk in women with a singleton pregnancy and a short cervix (usually defined as cervical length <25 mm by transvaginal ultrasound) identified in mid-pregnancy (typically 16–24 weeks). Vaginal progesterone is often favored in women with cervical shortening because it provides targeted uterine effects with minimal systemic exposure and is used up to 34–36 weeks depending on practice.

Prevention of miscarriage. The use of progesterone to prevent miscarriage (threatened miscarriage or recurrent miscarriage in the first trimester) has been debated. For women with threatened miscarriage and vaginal bleeding in early pregnancy, some trials show benefit of oral or vaginal progesterone in reducing miscarriage rates in specific subgroups (e.g., women with a history of miscarriage), while other trials show minimal or no benefit. When used, typical regimens include oral micronized progesterone 200–400 mg daily or vaginal progesterone 200–400 mg daily until reassuring viability is established, often through 12 weeks. For recurrent miscarriage thought to be due to luteal phase deficiency (a controversial diagnosis), progesterone supplementation is sometimes used empirically, commonly through the first trimester.

Dosing principles and monitoring. Route selection depends on the indication: vaginal for short cervix, IM for recurrent preterm birth historical indications, and oral or vaginal for first-trimester threatened miscarriage. Start timing is critical—prophylactic strategies for preterm birth begin in mid-gestation (16–24 weeks), whereas miscarriage prevention is considered in the first trimester. Duration follows the indication: until 34–36 weeks for preterm birth prevention in many protocols, and until 12 weeks for miscarriage prevention. Side effects include injection-site pain (IM), somnolence (oral), and local irritation (vaginal). Progesterone is generally considered safe in pregnancy; current data do not demonstrate major teratogenic risks. Always discuss evidence strength and alternatives with patients.

Clinical decision points. Use vaginal progesterone for a short cervix identified in midpregnancy. Consider 17-OHPC for select women with prior spontaneous preterm birth after informed discussion. Use progesterone for threatened miscarriage selectively, discussing uncertain efficacy. Check local guidelines and regulatory updates, as recommendations evolve with new evidence.

# Iron therapy: oral vs intravenous iron formulations and indications during pregnancy

Anemia is common in pregnancy due to physiologic hemodilution and increasing iron demands (for expansion of maternal red cell mass and fetal development). Normal hemoglobin in pregnancy is generally considered approximately 11.0–14.0 g/dL; the World Health Organization defines anemia in pregnancy as hemoglobin <11.0 g/dL in the first and third trimesters, and <10.5 g/dL in the second trimester. Key goals of iron therapy are to correct iron-deficiency anemia, restore maternal iron stores, reduce transfusion needs, and optimize maternal and fetal outcomes.

Oral iron. First-line therapy for most cases of iron-deficiency anemia in pregnancy is oral iron due to efficacy, safety, and convenience. Common formulations include ferrous sulfate 325 mg (contains approximately 65 mg elemental iron), ferrous fumarate, and ferrous gluconate (lower elemental iron content). Typical dosing is ferrous sulfate 325 mg once to three times daily, or lower-frequency dosing such as 60–120 mg elemental iron once daily or every other day to improve tolerability and absorption. Emerging evidence suggests alternate-day dosing may increase fractional absorption and reduce gastrointestinal side effects by allowing hepcidin levels to fall between doses. Side effects (nausea, constipation, abdominal cramping) are common and frequently limit adherence; strategies include taking with food (though this reduces absorption), using lower or alternate-day dosing, switching formulations, or using concomitant laxatives when needed. Oral iron requires weeks to months to correct hemoglobin and replenish ferritin; reticulocyte rise is often seen within 7–10 days and hemoglobin increases by approximately 1 g/dL every 2–4 weeks with effective therapy.

Indications for intravenous (IV) iron. IV iron is indicated when oral iron is not tolerated, when rapid repletion is required (e.g., late pregnancy or when surgery is planned and iron-deficiency anemia is moderate to severe), when malabsorption (e.g., prior bariatric surgery, inflammatory bowel disease) limits response, or when adherence is poor. IV iron formulations include iron sucrose, ferric carboxymaltose, and ferric derisomaltose (formerly iron dextran in older formulations). Ferric carboxymaltose and ferric derisomaltose allow larger single doses (e.g., 500–1000 mg per infusion) and more rapid repletion compared with iron sucrose, which typically requires multiple smaller doses (e.g., 200–300 mg per dose). Total dose calculations are typically done using the Ganzoni formula or simplified weight-based tables to reach a target ferritin and hemoglobin level.

Safety of IV iron. Modern IV iron formulations have a good safety profile, with low rates of serious anaphylaxis; severe hypersensitivity is now rare compared with older high-molecular-weight iron dextran. Common adverse effects are infusion-related reactions (flushing, myalgias, mild hypotension), which are usually self-limited. Administer IV iron in settings with resuscitation capability and observe patients during and after infusion per local protocols. In pregnancy, IV iron has been shown to increase hemoglobin and ferritin more rapidly than oral iron and to reduce transfusion needs in some studies. Administer IV iron when there is insufficient time for oral replenishment before expected delivery or surgery (e.g., substantial anemia discovered in the third trimester).

Laboratory targets and monitoring. Evaluate baseline hemoglobin, mean corpuscular volume (MCV), ferritin, reticulocyte count when possible. Ferritin <30 ng/mL (some use <15–30 ng/mL depending on assay) is consistent with iron deficiency in pregnancy, although inflammation can elevate ferritin. After initiating iron therapy, monitor hemoglobin in 2–4 weeks to assess response and again near delivery. Typical transfusion thresholds and decisions (below) will be influenced by response to iron and timing relative to delivery or surgery.

Clinical decision points. Start oral iron for mild-to-moderate iron-deficiency anemia, consider alternate-day dosing to improve absorption and reduce side effects, and switch to IV iron when oral therapy fails, is not tolerated, or when rapid correction is required before delivery or operative procedures.

# Blood transfusion thresholds and safety considerations in pregnancy

Pregnancy and the peripartum period present unique transfusion considerations due to physiologic anemia of pregnancy, increased blood volume, and the potential for obstetric hemorrhage. Indications for transfusion should be individualized based on hemodynamic status, ongoing blood loss, comorbidities, and gestational age, with the goal of optimizing maternal oxygen delivery and minimizing transfusion risks.

Laboratory thresholds. In nonpregnant critically ill adults, restrictive transfusion thresholds (transfuse for hemoglobin <7 g/dL) are commonly used. In obstetrics, data are less definitive, and thresholds are often more liberal. For stable pregnant patients without significant comorbidities, many centers use a transfusion threshold of hemoglobin <7 g/dL. However, in pregnancy, clinicians often consider transfusion when hemoglobin is below 7–8 g/dL for symptomatic anemia, and at higher levels for those with cardiovascular disease, ongoing bleeding, or inadequate physiologic adaptation. In acute hemorrhage, transfusion decisions are driven by blood loss, hemodynamics (tachycardia, hypotension), and clinical signs rather than fixed hemoglobin thresholds. Massive transfusion protocols (MTPs) for obstetric hemorrhage typically use balanced-component therapy (e.g., ratios approximating RBC:plasma:platelets of 1:1:1 or institution-specific protocols) and activate early when anticipated blood loss is substantial.

Safety considerations. Transfusion carries risks: transfusion reactions (acute hemolytic, febrile nonhemolytic, allergic/anaphylactic), transfusion-related acute lung injury (TRALI), transfusion-associated circulatory overload (TACO), alloimmunization, and infectious disease transmission (now rare). In pregnancy, alloimmunization (particularly anti-D in Rh-negative mothers who are exposed to Rh-positive fetal blood) is a specific concern. For Rh(D)-negative mothers who receive transfused Rh(D)-positive red cells (or are exposed to fetal-maternal hemorrhage), standard practice is to provide Rh(D) immunoglobulin (anti-D) prophylaxis per guidelines and to perform antibody screening. For any transfusion in a Rh(D)-negative pregnant patient, involve transfusion medicine and obstetrics to ensure appropriate administration of anti-D and follow-up titers.

Perioperative planning. For planned operations on pregnant patients with pelvic masses, optimize hemoglobin preoperatively with iron therapy or erythropoiesis-stimulating strategies when appropriate. When transfusion is anticipated, prepare ABO/Rh-matched blood, consider crossmatch and antibody screen in advance, and have MTP protocols ready for obstetric hemorrhage. Minimize transfusion exposure through cell-salvage techniques when permitted and appropriate—cell salvage has been used in obstetric surgery with particular attention to filtering and avoiding amniotic fluid contamination; many centers accept cell salvage with appropriate protocols.

Post-transfusion monitoring. After transfusion, monitor hemoglobin and clinical status, and watch for transfusion reactions. For neonates exposed to maternal transfusion medications (e.g., opioids near delivery), ensure neonatal teams are alerted.

Clinical decision points. Use restrictive transfusion thresholds in stable patients (e.g., transfuse for Hb <7 g/dL) but individualize for obstetric context—higher thresholds may be appropriate for symptomatic patients, cardiovascular disease, or ongoing loss. Early activation of MTPs in massive hemorrhage improves outcomes; ensure anti-D prophylaxis and antibody screening are handled appropriately.

# Drugs contraindicated or used with caution (e.g., GnRH analogues, certain chemotherapeutics, gadolinium)

Certain drugs carry high fetal risk or lack sufficient data to support safe use in pregnancy. When managing pelvic masses, clinicians must be familiar with agents that are contraindicated or require caution.

Gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) analogues. GnRH agonists (e.g., leuprolide) and antagonists suppress ovarian steroidogenesis and cause hypoestrogenism. They are contraindicated in pregnancy because they can disrupt the hormonal milieu necessary for maintenance of pregnancy and may have adverse effects on fetal development. If pregnancy is suspected or confirmed, GnRH analogues should be stopped. In the context of pelvic masses suspected to be hormonally responsive (e.g., fibroids), GnRH analogues are used to shrink lesions preoperatively in nonpregnant patients, but not in pregnant patients.

Methotrexate and certain chemotherapeutics. Methotrexate is a folate antagonist and a known abortifacient and teratogen; it is contraindicated in pregnancy and used for medical management of ectopic pregnancy precisely because it terminates trophoblastic tissue. Other chemotherapeutic agents have varying degrees of teratogenicity depending on class and timing: classic alkylating agents, antimetabolites (e.g., methotrexate), and some targeted agents are highly teratogenic in the first trimester. Anthracyclines (e.g., doxorubicin) have been used in pregnancy in the second and third trimesters for maternal indications when necessary, with careful counseling; however, decision-making must involve maternal-fetal medicine and oncology. For patients with malignant pelvic masses discovered in pregnancy, multidisciplinary discussion is essential to weigh maternal benefit against fetal risk and to plan timing (e.g., deferring certain agents until after the first trimester or until postpartum). For benign pelvic masses, chemotherapeutics are generally not relevant, but inadvertent exposure must be recognized as contraindicated.

Gadolinium-based contrast agents. Gadolinium crosses the placenta and distributes into amniotic fluid; some cohort studies suggest a possible association with an increased risk of stillbirth, neonatal death, or rheumatologic and skin conditions, though data are limited and causality is not established. Consequently, gadolinium-enhanced MRI is generally avoided during pregnancy unless the expected diagnostic benefit outweighs potential risk and no noncontrast MRI or alternative imaging can provide equivalent information. If gadolinium is used, use the lowest effective dose, and obtain informed consent.

Other agents to avoid or use with caution. Tetracyclines cause permanent fetal teeth discoloration and bone growth inhibition and are avoided after the first trimester (and generally avoided throughout pregnancy). Warfarin is teratogenic (nasal hypoplasia, stippled epiphyses) and is typically avoided in pregnancy in favor of low-molecular-weight heparin for anticoagulation, except in select cases managed by specialists. Misoprostol (prostaglandin E1 analogue) can cause uterine contractions and abortion and must be used with caution in pregnancy. Live-attenuated vaccines and certain immunomodulators are contraindicated.

Clinical decision points. For any agent with potential fetal toxicity, perform a risk–benefit analysis, involve obstetrics and relevant specialties, document counseling, and consider delaying therapy or using safer alternatives. Always consult up-to-date teratogen databases or a clinical pharmacology/toxicology service for specific agents.

Conclusion

Pharmacologic management of pregnant patients with pelvic masses requires a nuanced understanding of drug mechanisms, maternal–fetal pharmacokinetic changes, and trimester-specific risks. First-line analgesia favors paracetamol; NSAIDs are limited by gestational age; opioids are used only when necessary. Antibiotic selection should favor beta-lactams and other agents with strong safety data in pregnancy, with specific choices for urinary and surgical infections guided by sensitivity patterns and gestational age. Progesterone therapy has role-specific indications (short cervix vs prior preterm birth vs threatened miscarriage) with route and dosing tailored to the clinical scenario. Iron therapy should begin with oral iron when feasible, with IV iron reserved for intolerance, malabsorption, or need for rapid repletion. Blood transfusion thresholds are individualized in pregnancy, with special attention to Rh immunoprophylaxis and hemorrhage protocols. Finally, several drugs—GnRH analogues, methotrexate, many chemotherapeutics, and gadolinium—are contraindicated or require extreme caution. In all cases, multidisciplinary collaboration, informed consent, and reference to current guidelines are essential.

# Introduction

Pregnancy produces profound hematologic changes that have important implications for perioperative care of pregnant surgical patients. Plasma volume expands disproportionately to red cell mass, producing physiologic dilutional anemia that must be distinguished from pathologic iron deficiency. Pregnancy is also a hypercoagulable state, which modifies both bleeding risk and the response to transfusion and blood-conservation strategies. In the perioperative setting—especially for obstetric surgery such as cesarean delivery or for nonobstetric surgery during pregnancy—clinicians must integrate knowledge of pregnancy physiology, laboratory interpretation, iron-repletion strategies, transfusion thresholds, intraoperative blood-loss minimization, and postoperative monitoring to optimize maternal and fetal outcomes. This chapter reviews those topics in detail with emphasis on pathophysiology and clinical decision-making.

# Physiologic pregnancy anemia vs iron deficiency anemia: diagnosis and interpretation of ferritin and Hb

Pregnancy causes a predictable increase in plasma volume beginning in the first trimester, peaking in the second trimester, and persisting through the third trimester. Red cell mass also increases, but to a lesser extent. The net result is hemodilution and a fall in hemoglobin concentration. Normal hemoglobin reference values in pregnancy are adjusted for trimester: the World Health Organization defines anemia as hemoglobin <11.0 g/dL in the first and third trimesters and <10.5 g/dL in the second trimester. Typical nonpregnant references of 12–16 g/dL for women do not apply directly; clinicians must interpret Hb in the context of gestational age.

Physiologic (dilutional) anemia is characterized by a moderate reduction in Hb without iron-deficiency laboratory features. In contrast, iron deficiency anemia (IDA) results from inadequate iron stores to support expanded erythropoiesis and is the most common nutritional deficiency in pregnancy. Differentiating physiologic anemia from IDA relies on iron indices rather than hemoglobin alone. Serum ferritin, a marker of iron stores, is the single best initial test. In pregnancy, ferritin values are lower than in nonpregnant states due to hemodilution and increased iron utilization. Common diagnostic thresholds: ferritin <30 µg/L is generally diagnostic of iron deficiency in pregnancy; ferritin <15 µg/L indicates severe iron depletion. Ferritin values >70–100 µg/L make iron deficiency unlikely, though inflammation can raise ferritin and mask deficiency.

Transferrin saturation (TSAT) and serum iron complement ferritin. TSAT <20% supports iron deficiency. A low mean corpuscular volume (MCV <80 fL) and low mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH) can indicate microcytic iron deficiency, although early IDA can be normocytic. The reticulocyte count and reticulocyte hemoglobin content can help assess marrow response. Inflammation and infection complicate interpretation; ferritin is an acute-phase reactant and may be elevated despite iron deficiency. In such cases, a combination of low TSAT (<20%) and clinical risk factors (poor oral intake, interval since last pregnancy, heavy bleeding) suggests IDA despite ferritin values that are borderline or mildly elevated.

Clinical reasoning requires integrating timing (trimester), symptoms (fatigue, exertional dyspnea), objective signs, and iron studies. A pragmatic approach: in a pregnant patient with Hb <11 g/dL (or <10.5 g/dL in second trimester), measure ferritin and TSAT. If ferritin <30 µg/L or TSAT <20%, treat as iron deficiency. If ferritin is 30–70 µg/L and clinical suspicion is high (e.g., prior iron deficiency, heavy bleeding), consider treating or obtaining further studies such as CRP to interpret ferritin. If inflammation is present, a low TSAT assumes more weight. Early identification is crucial because iron repletion before surgery reduces perioperative transfusion needs and improves maternal reserves in the event of antepartum or intraoperative hemorrhage.

# Indications and dosing for intravenous iron formulations in pregnancy

Oral iron remains first-line for many pregnant patients with mild iron deficiency because of low cost and widespread availability. However, oral iron may be inadequately tolerated (gastrointestinal side effects), absorbed slowly, or insufficient when rapid repletion is needed (late pregnancy, short interval to surgery) or when malabsorption or ongoing bleeding exists. Intravenous (IV) iron is indicated in pregnancy when oral iron is not tolerated or ineffective, when rapid repletion is required (e.g., Hb significantly below target in late pregnancy or preoperative period), in severe IDA with Hb <8.0 g/dL (particularly when a rapid rise is desired), or when malabsorption or ongoing bleeding precludes oral therapy.

Several IV iron preparations are used clinically: iron sucrose, ferric carboxymaltose, ferric derisomaltose (also called iron isomaltoside), and low-molecular-weight iron dextran. Each differs in maximum single-dose allowed, infusion time, and risk profile. Key dosing principles include calculating total iron deficit and knowing practical single-dose limits.

A common method to estimate total iron deficit is the Ganzoni formula:
Total iron deficit (mg) = body weight (kg) × (target Hb − actual Hb) (g/dL) × 2.4 + iron stores (mg).
Typical target Hb in pregnancy for calculation is 12.0 g/dL and assumed iron stores component often 500 mg when repleting during pregnancy, though some clinicians use 300 mg. Because the formula can be cumbersome and conservative, many centers use simplified dosing tables or the following practical approaches:

- Ferric carboxymaltose (e.g., 750–1000 mg per infusion): allows large single doses (up to 1000 mg per session, maximum cumulative doses vary by jurisdiction and product labeling). A typical regimen for significant deficiency is 1000 mg IV over 15–30 minutes, repeated after one week if additional dose is required to meet total iron deficit. Ferric carboxymaltose is advantageous where rapid correction is needed and when a single or two infusions are preferable.
- Ferric derisomaltose / iron isomaltoside (e.g., up to 1000–1500 mg single dose depending on product): allows single high-dose infusion (often 1000–1500 mg in one session) to replete stores.
- Iron sucrose: typically administered as 200 mg IV over 15–60 minutes, repeated on alternate days to reach the total dose; total doses commonly 600–1200 mg depending on deficit. Infusion times tend to be longer and multiple visits are required.
- Low-molecular-weight iron dextran: can permit full replacement in a single infusion (total dose infusion), but because of historical concerns about anaphylaxis, many centers prefer non-dextran agents; when used, a test dose may be given per institutional policy, and staff trained to manage anaphylaxis are required.

Specific practical dosing examples: a 70 kg woman with Hb 8.0 g/dL and target Hb 12.0 g/dL has a deficit of weight × (12 − 8) × 2.4 = 70 × 4 × 2.4 = 672 mg, plus iron stores (~500 mg) for a total ~1172 mg. This could be delivered as a single 1000–1200 mg infusion with ferric derisomaltose or two 750–1000 mg ferric carboxymaltose infusions separated by one week, or as multiple 200 mg iron sucrose infusions.

Safety and contraindications: IV iron is generally safe in pregnancy when given with appropriate monitoring. Serious hypersensitivity reactions are uncommon but possible; clinicians must ensure resuscitation capability during infusion. Ferric carboxymaltose and iron isomaltoside have low rates of anaphylaxis. Iron dextran has higher anaphylaxis risk historically and is less commonly used. IV iron can cause transient hypophosphatemia, particularly with ferric carboxymaltose; this is usually asymptomatic but can be clinically relevant with repeated dosing. Timing relative to surgery: when urgent correction is required prior to delivery or surgery within days, IV iron is indicated because oral iron will not raise Hb rapidly enough. IV iron increases reticulocyte counts within days, with clinically meaningful Hb increases over 1–3 weeks; if surgery is imminent within 24–48 hours and severe anemia exists, transfusion strategies may also be necessary.

# Transfusion thresholds, risks, and indications in obstetric patients

Transfusion practice in obstetrics requires balancing the risks of anemia (maternal hemodynamic compromise, impaired oxygen delivery to mother and fetus) against transfusion-related risks (infection, transfusion reactions, transfusion-related acute lung injury (TRALI), transfusion-associated circulatory overload (TACO), and alloimmunization). Pregnancy-specific considerations include the possibility of maternal alloantibodies affecting the fetus and fetal-maternal blood exchange.

General transfusion thresholds in nonpregnant adults have trended toward restrictive strategies. A commonly used threshold for stable, non-bleeding adults is hemoglobin <7 g/dL to consider transfusion, with thresholds of 7–8 g/dL in patients with cardiovascular disease or risk factors. In obstetric patients, many centers adopt a slightly higher threshold because of the physiologic changes in pregnancy and the potential for rapid decompensation during blood loss; thresholds of Hb <8 g/dL often prompt consideration of transfusion in symptomatic patients, and symptomatic anemia (tachycardia, hypotension, ischemic symptoms) generally justifies transfusion regardless of absolute Hb.

Specific clinical indications in obstetrics include massive obstetric hemorrhage, symptomatic anemia in the perioperative period, and iron-refractory severe anemia when immediate improvement in oxygen-carrying capacity is required (e.g., before urgent surgery or when ongoing bleeding threatens hemodynamic stability). In massive obstetric hemorrhage, transfusion is guided by clinical status and massive transfusion protocols rather than fixed Hb values. A commonly used definition of massive transfusion is replacement of total blood volume within 24 hours, or transfusion of ≥10 units of red blood cells (RBCs) in 24 hours, or ≥4 units in 1 hour with ongoing bleeding. During massive bleeding, balanced transfusion with RBCs, plasma, and platelets in approximately 1:1:1 ratio is used to prevent coagulopathy.

Risks of transfusion in pregnancy include alloimmunization to red cell antigens that may affect future pregnancies (e.g., RhD immunization), febrile and allergic reactions, TRALI, TACO (pregnant patients are at increased risk due to expanded blood volume and possible preeclampsia), and transmission of infections (now rare). In addition, transfusion may complicate interpretation of newborn blood type and serology. For RhD-negative mothers, administration of Rh immune globulin (RhIG) is required after substantial fetomaternal hemorrhage or after transfusion of RhD-positive blood to an RhD-negative mother; transfusion services aim to avoid giving RhD-incompatible RBCs, but if unavoidable, immunoprophylaxis and hematology consultation are necessary.

Practical transfusion guidance: one unit of packed RBC typically raises hemoglobin by approximately 1.0 g/dL (10 g/L) in adults; in pregnancy this expectation is similar but can be affected by ongoing bleeding or hemodilution. Monitor Hb after transfusion within 30–60 minutes (or at the next clinically relevant time) to assess response. For obstetric patients with acute hemorrhage, decision to transfuse is clinical and immediate; laboratory Hb will lag and should not delay lifesaving transfusion. For stable patients with chronic or antenatal anemia, aim to correct iron deficiency with oral or IV iron before resorting to transfusion.

# Strategies to minimize intraoperative blood loss (surgical techniques, pharmacologic agents)

Minimizing intraoperative blood loss in pregnant surgical patients combines preoperative optimization (correction of anemia, cessation of anticoagulation when safe), meticulous surgical technique, and judicious use of pharmacologic agents. Preoperatively, ensure adequate iron stores, correct coagulopathy, and coordinate with anesthesia and transfusion services.

Surgical techniques that reduce blood loss include careful tissue handling, meticulous hemostasis with electrocautery, use of vascular clamps and temporary vascular control (e.g., uterine artery ligation or temporary occlusion), and avoidance of unnecessary dissection. In obstetric hemorrhage, specific mechanical interventions are important: uterine massage for atony, bimanual compression, intrauterine balloon tamponade, compression sutures (B-Lynch, Hayman, Cho), and selective arterial ligation (uterine arteries, internal iliac artery) can control hemorrhage while preserving the uterus. In nonobstetric abdominal or pelvic surgery during pregnancy, minimizing operative time and maintaining normothermia and normotension reduces bleeding risk.

Pharmacologic agents play a central role. Uterotonics—oxytocin, ergometrine (methylergometrine), and prostaglandins (sulprostone, carboprost tromethamine, misoprostol)—are first-line for uterine atony in postpartum hemorrhage (PPH). Oxytocin infusion protocols commonly use a bolus (e.g., 5–10 IU IV) followed by infusion (e.g., 20–40 IU in 1 L crystalloid at titrated rates); institutional protocols vary. Ergometrine is effective but contraindicated in hypertension and preeclampsia. Carboprost (250 mcg IM every 15–90 minutes, maximum 8 doses) is effective but contraindicated in asthma. Misoprostol (800–1000 mcg per rectum or sublingual) is an adjunct when parenteral agents are unavailable.

Antifibrinolytics—tranexamic acid (TXA)—are crucial in reducing blood loss in obstetric hemorrhage. The WOMAN trial demonstrated a reduction in death due to bleeding when TXA 1 g IV was given as soon as possible (within 3 hours of onset), with a second 1 g dose if bleeding persisted after 30 minutes or restarted within 24 hours. Therefore, standard practice for PPH is 1 g IV bolus of TXA, repeatable once. TXA is also used prophylactically in some high-risk surgeries but evidence is strongest for treatment of established hemorrhage. Dose adjustments for renal impairment are needed because TXA is renally excreted.

Topical hemostatic agents (oxidized regenerated cellulose, gelatin sponges, fibrin sealants) are useful adjuncts in oozing surfaces. Administration of recombinant activated factor VII has been used as a rescue therapy for uncontrolled hemorrhage but carries thrombotic risk; it is reserved for refractory cases after correction of coagulation abnormalities and multidisciplinary discussion. Use of pro-hemostatic blood components—platelets and fresh frozen plasma—should follow laboratory guidance (platelet counts, INR) and massive transfusion protocols.

Anesthetic techniques also influence blood loss. Regional anesthesia may reduce blood loss through lower systemic blood pressures but can mask early signs of hypovolemia. Close blood pressure control and avoidance of significant hypertension (which may exacerbate bleeding) are important.

# Use of cell salvage and blood conservation methods in obstetric surgery

Autologous cell salvage collects, filters, and returns the patient’s shed blood intraoperatively, reducing allogeneic transfusion requirements. Historically, the use of cell salvage in obstetrics was limited by concerns about returning amniotic fluid, fetal squames, and potential for amniotic fluid embolism. However, data accumulated over the past decade support the safety and utility of cell salvage in obstetric surgery when appropriate protocols are followed.

Key practical considerations for cell salvage in obstetrics: use of cell salvage devices with high-volume suction and processing, combined with leukocyte-depletion (WBC) filters during reinfusion, reduces the risk of particulate matter and amniotic components. Most guidelines recommend the use of a dedicated obstetric protocol, suctioning amniotic fluid and heavily contaminated fields separately where possible, and avoiding reinfusion unless processed blood meets safety checks. Reinfusion should occur only after centrifugation and washing of salvaged blood to remove plasma, free hemoglobin, and contaminants. Use of a leukocyte-depletion filter (e.g., 40 µm) immediately before reinfusion further decreases risk of fetal squames and amniotic fluid debris.

Efficacy: cell salvage reduces allogeneic blood transfusion volumes and can be particularly valuable when blood products are scarce, when patients decline allogeneic transfusion (e.g., for religious reasons), or in settings of massive obstetric hemorrhage. Safety data show low incidence of adverse outcomes when washing and filtration protocols are used; documented cases of amniotic fluid embolism related to salvaged blood are exceedingly rare when proper techniques are employed. There is theoretical concern about returning fetal red cells to Rh-negative mothers; therefore, cell salvage in RhD-negative mothers who deliver RhD-positive fetuses requires additional considerations. If salvaged blood contains fetal RBCs, Rh immune globulin should be administered per institutional protocols to prevent alloimmunization.

Other blood conservation techniques include acute normovolemic hemodilution (ANH), preoperative autologous donation, and controlled hypotension. ANH—removing whole blood immediately before surgery and replacing with crystalloid/colloid to maintain normovolemia, then reinfusing autologous blood later—reduces intraoperative loss of RBCs by diluting them at the time of bleeding. ANH is less common in pregnancy because pregnancy already produces physiologic hemodilution and because timing relative to delivery and fetal considerations complicate its use. Preoperative autologous donation is rarely practical in pregnant patients because it requires time and further reduces maternal iron stores. Controlled hypotension has limited applicability in obstetrics because uteroplacental perfusion is pressure-dependent; maintaining adequate maternal blood pressure is critical for fetal oxygenation. Therefore, cell salvage remains the most practical intraoperative autologous strategy in obstetric surgery.

Institutional protocols, staff training, and multidisciplinary planning are essential for safe implementation of cell salvage in obstetric settings. Indications should be predefined (anticipated blood loss >1000 mL, refusal of allogeneic blood, or difficulty obtaining compatible blood), and contingency plans for RhD-negative mothers must be in place.

# Strategies to minimize intraoperative blood loss (continued): multidisciplinary and logistical considerations

Beyond individual techniques and drugs, minimizing blood loss in pregnant surgical patients depends on multidisciplinary planning. For elective cesarean delivery or planned maternal surgery in pregnancy, preoperative optimization should involve obstetricians, anesthesiologists, hematologists/transfusion medicine experts, and blood bank services. Strategies include arranging crossmatched blood products in advance when risk is elevated, having uterotonics and TXA immediately available, ensuring cell salvage equipment and trained personnel are on site if indicated, and establishing clear triggers for escalation to massive transfusion protocols.

Intraoperative cell-saver use should be coordinated with the blood bank to ensure compatibility and immediate processing. Regular simulation training for obstetric hemorrhage—including checklists, role assignment, and rapid availability of blood products—improves team performance and reduces delays in treatment. Use of point-of-care coagulation testing (thromboelastography or rotational thromboelastometry) can guide targeted component therapy, allowing for focused replacement of fibrinogen, platelets, or plasma rather than empirical transfusion.

# Postoperative hematologic monitoring and iron repletion

After surgery, careful hematologic monitoring is essential. In patients who received transfusion or experienced significant perioperative blood loss, serial hemoglobin measurements are recommended: an initial check within 30–60 minutes after transfusion to assess response, and subsequent checks at 24 and 48 hours (or sooner if clinical status changes). For non-bleeding postoperative patients, a pragmatic schedule includes Hb assessment at 24 hours and again at 48–72 hours if clinically indicated. Monitor vital signs, urine output, and signs of ongoing bleeding. In those who received massive transfusion, monitor for coagulopathy and hypocalcemia (from citrate toxicity), and check platelet count, fibrinogen, PT/INR, aPTT, and electrolytes.

For iron repletion postoperatively, the strategy depends on the cause and severity of anemia and the patient’s tolerance of oral iron. If iron deficiency is confirmed or strongly suspected, and if oral therapy is tolerated and there is sufficient time before the next obstetric or surgical event, high-dose oral iron (e.g., ferrous sulfate 325 mg PO two or three times daily, providing elemental iron 65–195 mg/day) can be used. Newer approaches recommend alternate-day dosing (e.g., 60–100 mg elemental iron every other day) to enhance absorption and reduce hepcidin-mediated blockade, improving bioavailability and tolerability. However, in the immediate postoperative period, gastrointestinal absorption may be impaired and oral tolerance limited.

IV iron is the preferred option postoperatively when anemia is moderate-to-severe, when rapid repletion is desired, or when oral iron is not tolerated. Use the same dosing principles and preparations described earlier. Monitor ferritin and Hb after IV iron: an increase in reticulocyte count is expected within 3–7 days, with measurable Hb rise within 1–3 weeks. Aim for ferritin >30 µg/L and gradual normalization of Hb to trimester-appropriate targets; in some cases, clinicians target ferritin ≥50–70 µg/L to ensure robust stores.

In patients who received RBC transfusion, iron repletion may still be necessary because transfusion addresses immediate oxygen-carrying capacity but not iron stores; transfusion can transiently mask iron deficiency. If iron deficiency persists after transfusion or surgery, treat accordingly. In RhD-negative mothers who were exposed to fetal blood or transfused with RhD-positive blood, ensure appropriate RhIG administration and follow maternal antibody testing.

Long-term monitoring includes assessment of Hb and ferritin 4–6 weeks after discharge to confirm repletion and to plan maintenance therapy. Counseling on dietary iron, prenatal vitamin adherence, and follow-up for causes of iron loss (e.g., menorrhagia, GI lesions in nonpregnant patients) is part of comprehensive care.

# Summary and clinical decision points

Management of hematologic issues in pregnant surgical patients requires a layered approach: first, distinguish physiologic dilutional anemia from iron deficiency using Hb, ferritin (thresholds: ferritin <30 µg/L suggests deficiency; <15 µg/L severe), and TSAT (<20% supportive). Second, use IV iron when oral therapy is intolerable, ineffective, or too slow—select product and dose based on total iron deficit (Ganzoni formula or simplified dosing) and urgency; common regimens include ferric carboxymaltose 1000 mg single or two-dose strategies, or iron sucrose 200 mg repeated. Third, apply transfusion prudently: consider transfusion in symptomatic patients or Hb thresholds commonly around 7–8 g/dL depending on stability and comorbidities; in massive hemorrhage follow balanced transfusion and institutional massive transfusion protocols. Fourth, employ surgical measures, uterotonics, tranexamic acid (1 g IV, repeat once if needed), and cell salvage (with washing and leukocyte filtration) to minimize and manage blood loss. Finally, monitor postoperative Hb, coagulation, and iron indices, and provide appropriate iron repletion and follow-up to restore maternal reserves and reduce future transfusion needs. Multidisciplinary planning and institutional protocols tailored to obstetric care improve outcomes and safety.

Introduction

Uterine and adnexal masses encompass a broad spectrum of benign and malignant lesions whose accurate histopathologic classification is essential for guiding clinical management. Gross and microscopic examination provides the definitive diagnosis in many cases, distinguishes indolent from aggressive neoplasms, and explains radiologic appearances and clinical behavior. This chapter reviews the histopathology of common uterine and adnexal masses encountered in surgical specimens, emphasizing the diagnostic criteria used to separate benign smooth muscle tumors from sarcomas, the histologic correlates of degenerative change, the defining microscopic features of principal ovarian cyst types, and the practical role of frozen section and cytology in intraoperative decision-making. Where relevant, gross–microscopic–imaging correlations are highlighted to reinforce clinical reasoning.

# Histologic criteria for leiomyoma: smooth muscle bundles, collagen deposition

Leiomyomas (uterine fibroids) are benign smooth muscle neoplasms composed of well-differentiated smooth muscle cells arranged in whorled bundles. The canonical microscopic appearance is monotonous spindle-shaped cells with eosinophilic fibrillary cytoplasm, elongated “cigar-shaped” nuclei with blunt ends, and minimal nuclear pleomorphism. Cells are arranged in intersecting fascicles or swirling (whorled) patterns, often forming nodules separated by well-circumscribed capsules or compressed myometrium.

Between the smooth muscle bundles there is frequently variable extracellular matrix. Collagen deposition is common and may appear as pink, acellular bands or sheets on hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) sections. Some leiomyomas are collagen-rich (also termed cellular variants with increased collagen or “hyalinized” types), where smooth muscle cells are sparser and collagen predominates; others are cellular leiomyomas with dense cellularity and scant collagen. Mitotic activity is usually low, commonly quoted as less than 5 mitoses per 10 high-power fields (HPF), and nuclei lack atypia. The presence of significant nuclear atypia, increased mitotic rate, or coagulative tumor cell necrosis should prompt evaluation for malignancy.

Additional features sometimes seen in leiomyomas include vascular changes within the tumor (thick-walled hyalinized vessels), peripheral pseudocapsule formed by compressed adjacent myometrium, and entrapped benign endometrial glands in submucosal or intramural tumors. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) supports smooth muscle differentiation: tumor cells are typically positive for smooth muscle actin (SMA), desmin, and H-caldesmon, while being negative for markers of endometrial stromal or epithelial differentiation (e.g., CD10 negative). These IHC markers are most useful when morphology is ambiguous.

# Pathologic patterns of degeneration (ischaemic, hyaline, cystic, red) and their microscopic features

Leiomyomas frequently undergo degenerative changes as they outgrow their blood supply or are exposed to physiologic changes such as pregnancy. Recognizing the histologic patterns of degeneration is important so they are not misinterpreted as malignancy or infection.

Hyaline degeneration is the most common pattern. Grossly this appears as homogeneous, glassy, whitish areas. Microscopically, hyaline degeneration is characterized by the replacement of smooth muscle cells by eosinophilic, acellular, hyaline proteinaceous material that may be arranged as amorphous acellular zones between residual muscle bundles. The remaining tumor cells become attenuated and are often surrounded by hyalinized stroma. Hyaline change can be focal or diffuse and may be associated with calcification.

Cystic degeneration represents more extensive liquefactive change of a hyalinized leiomyoma and appears grossly as cystic spaces filled with serous or proteinaceous fluid. Microscopically, dilated spaces lined by flattened or denuded mesothelial-like cells can be seen; the surrounding stroma shows variable fibrosis and hemosiderin if prior hemorrhage occurred. Cystic change can mimic an ovarian cyst on imaging if subserosal and large.

Red (carneous) degeneration is typically seen during pregnancy and is due to acute hemorrhagic infarction resulting from venous thrombosis in the periphery of the tumor. Grossly, areas are dark red or brown. Microscopically, there is coagulative necrosis with hemorrhage, degenerated smooth muscle fibers, and variable inflammatory infiltrate including macrophages and neutrophils. Unlike coagulative tumor cell necrosis seen in malignant sarcomas, red degeneration usually shows hemorrhagic infarction with preservation of cell outlines in early phases and lacks the classic zonal eosinophilic necrosis with ghost outlines of cells that characterizes malignant necrosis.

Ischemic degeneration (often used interchangeably with red degeneration but broader) and infarct-type necrosis can occur in pedunculated or torsed tumors. Microscopy shows areas of ischemic change with ghost cells, karyorrhexis, and infiltration by inflammatory cells. Distinguishing ischemic/coagulative infarction from coagulative tumor cell necrosis (see below) may require correlation with clinical history (e.g., pregnancy, torsion) and review of the interface between viable tumor and necrosis: coagulative tumor cell necrosis displays abrupt transition from viable tumor to necrotic zone with cell ghosts and nuclear debris, and is more suggestive of malignancy when paired with atypia and mitoses.

Additional degenerative processes include myxoid degeneration, in which abundant myxoid extracellular matrix (pale basophilic mucinous material) separates smooth muscle bundles, and fatty change or calcification. Recognizing these patterns reduces misclassification of benign atypical changes as sarcoma.

# Features distinguishing benign leiomyoma from leiomyosarcoma and other sarcomas

Differentiating benign leiomyoma from leiomyosarcoma (LMS) and other uterine sarcomas is one of the most important tasks in gynecologic pathology because management and prognosis differ dramatically. The classic diagnostic triad for leiomyosarcoma comprises significant cytologic atypia, high mitotic activity, and coagulative tumor cell necrosis. Various sets of criteria exist, but commonly used thresholds and patterns are:

- Mitotic index: A commonly cited cutoff for concern is ≥10 mitoses per 10 high-power fields (HPF) for outright diagnosis of LMS in many institutions, though some diagnosticians consider lower counts (e.g., >5/10 HPF) with significant atypia and necrosis to be suspicious. It is imperative to evaluate mitoses in well-fixed, representative tumor areas and to avoid counting apoptotic figures.
- Cytologic atypia: Moderate to severe nuclear atypia characterized by marked pleomorphism, hyperchromasia, prominent nucleoli, and irregular nuclear contours supports malignancy. Scattered atypical cells alone in a largely bland tumor do not define LMS.
- Coagulative tumor cell necrosis (CTCN): CTCN shows abrupt transition from viable tumor to necrotic areas with preserved cellular outlines (ghost cells) and nuclear karyorrhexis. This type of necrosis is more specific for malignancy than hyaline/ischemic infarction, which tends to be associated with inflammation and gradual transition zones.

Tumors that do not meet all three criteria but raise concern are categorized as smooth muscle tumor of uncertain malignant potential (STUMP). Characteristics prompting a STUMP diagnosis include moderate atypia with low mitotic rate but focal necrosis, or high mitotic activity without atypia, or other unusual combinations. STUMP requires cautious clinical follow-up because behavior is unpredictable.

Other uterine sarcomas include:

- Endometrial stromal sarcoma (ESS): Typically composed of small, uniform cells resembling proliferative-phase endometrial stroma with a characteristic infiltrative, tongue-like pattern into myometrium and often lymphovascular invasion. Low-grade ESS shows limited atypia and mitotic activity but frequently harbors JAZF1-SUZ12 rearrangements; high-grade ESS demonstrates marked atypia and higher mitotic rates and may have YWHAE-NUTM2 fusions. Immunophenotype includes CD10 positivity and variable ER/PR.
- Undifferentiated uterine sarcoma: Highly pleomorphic, high mitotic activity, aggressive behavior, and lack of specific differentiation or molecular markers; a diagnosis of exclusion.
- Carcinosarcoma (malignant mixed Müllerian tumor): Biphasic neoplasm with malignant epithelial (carcinomatous) and malignant mesenchymal (sarcomatous) components. Historically treated as sarcoma but molecular and clinical behavior align more with high-grade carcinoma; cytokeratin and EMA highlight the epithelial component.

IHC and molecular tools can assist. H-caldesmon and desmin favor smooth muscle differentiation (leiomyoma/LMS) over endometrial stromal tumors, which are CD10 positive. Ki-67 proliferation index is higher in LMS than leiomyoma but no single cutoff is universally accepted. Molecular cytogenetic alterations (e.g., MED12 mutations in a sizable subset of leiomyomas) can support benignity, whereas complex karyotypes are more common in sarcomas.

Accurate sampling is crucial: leiomyomas can have focal atypia or increased mitoses that do not reflect overall malignancy; conversely, LMS can be focal within a larger mass. Pathologists typically sample extensively from tumors with worrisome gross features (areas of hemorrhage, necrosis, softening) to avoid underdiagnosis.

# Ovarian cyst histology: endometrioma, dermoid, serous and mucinous tumors

Ovarian cysts arise from various tissues and display distinct histologic architectures.

Endometrioma (endometriotic cyst)
Endometriomas are cysts formed by ectopic endometrial glands and stroma within the ovary, often containing altered blood products (“chocolate cysts”). Microscopically, the cyst lining shows endometrial-type glandular epithelium, which may be atrophic or proliferative, accompanied by endometrial-type stroma. Hemosiderin-laden macrophages and blood breakdown products are typical in the cyst contents and within the wall. Variable fibrosis occurs in the ovarian cortex, and foci of smooth muscle metaplasia may be present. Endometriotic implants can undergo malignant transformation (rarely) most commonly to endometrioid or clear cell carcinoma; identification of atypical hyperplasia or carcinoma in endometriosis warrants careful sampling.

Mature cystic teratoma (dermoid)
Mature cystic teratomas are germ cell tumors containing tissues derived from more than one germ layer, most commonly ectodermal derivatives. Grossly they are unilocular cysts containing sebaceous material and hair. Microscopically, the lining is often stratified squamous epithelium with underlying adnexal structures (hair follicles, sebaceous glands). Other mature tissues—respiratory epithelium, cartilage, bone, thyroid tissue, neural elements—may be present. Immature teratomas contain immature neuroepithelium and have malignant potential. Squamous carcinoma arising within a dermoid is the most common malignant transformation; presence of invasive atypical epithelial components requires adequate sampling to detect.

Serous tumors
Serous ovarian tumors form a spectrum from benign serous cystadenomas to borderline (atypical proliferative) serous tumors and serous carcinomas. Benign serous cystadenomas are cystic, lined by a single layer of columnar, often ciliated epithelial cells resembling fallopian tube epithelium, and show no stromal invasion. Borderline serous tumors exhibit epithelial proliferation with hierarchical branching papillae, epithelial stratification, mild-to-moderate cytologic atypia, but lack stromal invasion. Microinvasion or stromal invasion converts the lesion to low-grade serous carcinoma in certain contexts. High-grade serous carcinomas show markedly pleomorphic cells, high mitotic index, necrosis, and complex papillary and solid architecture; they often show diffuse p53 aberrant staining on IHC and are associated with TP53 mutations and BRCA alterations.

Psammoma bodies (laminated calcifications) are common in serous tumors, especially serous borderline and low-grade carcinomas. Immunophenotype includes WT1 positivity in serous lineage tumors.

Mucinous tumors
Mucinous cystadenomas are multilocular cysts lined by tall columnar mucin-secreting epithelium resembling endocervical or intestinal epithelium. Benign mucinous tumors have a single epithelial layer, while borderline tumors display epithelial proliferation and stratification without stromal invasion. Mucinous carcinomas show complex architecture with confluent glandular/epithelial proliferation and stromal invasion. Mucinous tumors can be enormous and sometimes represent metastases from the gastrointestinal tract; distinguishing primary ovarian from metastatic mucinous tumors relies on laterality (primary often unilateral), size (primary larger), complex architecture, and clinical correlation, sometimes aided by immunoprofile (CK7/CK20, CDX2).

In all ovarian tumors, adequate sampling is essential because borderline or malignant areas can be focal. Intraoperative assessment may be limited by sampling error, particularly in large mucinous tumors.

# Role of frozen section and cytology in intraoperative decision-making

Frozen section and intraoperative cytology provide rapid pathologic assessment to guide surgical management, especially decisions about extent of resection (conservative versus radical surgery) and staging procedures.

Frozen section is useful when deciding whether an adnexal mass that is resected at the time of laparotomy or laparoscopy is benign, borderline, or malignant. Its accuracy is high for distinguishing benign versus malignant lesions in many settings (sensitivity and specificity often >90%), but performance is lower for certain tumor types:

- High accuracy: Typical benign cysts (serous cystadenoma, dermoid), high-grade serous carcinoma.
- Lower accuracy: Mucinous tumors (due to heterogeneity and huge size), borderline tumors where subtle epithelial proliferation may be missed, and lesions with focal malignant transformation in a teratoma.

Limitations of frozen section include freezing artifacts that can obscure nuclear detail, sampling error (especially in large, multilocular mucinous masses), and interpretive difficulty in STUMP or borderline lesions. When frozen section yields “benign,” but clinical or imaging suspicion is high, some surgeons may choose to stage or to stage at a later definitive surgery. Conversely, a frozen section diagnosis of malignancy typically prompts comprehensive staging (total hysterectomy, bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy, omentectomy, lymph node sampling) in postmenopausal patients; in younger patients wishing fertility preservation, the decision must balance frozen section reliability and clinical risk.

Intraoperative cytology (peritoneal washings/ascitic fluid cytology, scrape cytology) supplements frozen section by detecting free malignant cells in the peritoneal cavity, which has staging implications. Positive peritoneal cytology historically upstaged certain ovarian and uterine cancers, although the staging systems and their significance have evolved. Cytology is rapid and sensitive for surface-spreading carcinomas but less useful for localized stromal tumors.

Best practice combines careful preoperative imaging, tumor markers (e.g., CA-125; normal range <35 U/mL), and intraoperative frozen section with an understanding of limitations. Communication between surgeon and pathologist about the clinical scenario and radiologic features improves decision-making.

# Correlation of gross pathology to imaging findings

Bridging gross pathological observations with imaging findings enhances interpretation and clinical decisions. Several consistent correlations between gross/microscopic pathology and radiologic appearances exist:

Leiomyomas:
- Hyaline degeneration: On MRI, hyalinized fibroids are typically low signal on both T1- and T2-weighted images (T2 dark) due to dense collagen and decreased water content. Grossly they are firm and pale.
- Cystic degeneration: Appears as high T2 signal intensity areas corresponding to fluid-filled cystic spaces within the mass; grossly cystic and soft.
- Red degeneration: On MRI, acute hemorrhagic/red degeneration shows high signal on T1-weighted images due to methemoglobin and variable T2 signal (so-called T1 hyperintense, T2 variable). Clinically associated with acute pain, most commonly in pregnancy.
- Calcified fibroids: Seen as echogenic foci with shadowing on ultrasound and signal voids on MRI; grossly gritty with calcification.
- Cellular leiomyomas: May show higher T2 signal than typical fibroids due to higher cellularity and less dense collagen.

Ovarian masses:
- Endometrioma: Classically shows “T2 shading” on MRI—progressive loss of T2 signal due to concentrated blood products (old hemorrhage) and high signal on T1-weighted images because of blood products. On ultrasound, endometriomas are often homogeneous, low-level internal echoes (“ground glass” echogenicity). Pathologically, these correspond to blood-filled cysts lined by endometrial epithelium and hemosiderin-laden macrophages.
- Mature cystic teratoma (dermoid): Imaging may show fat-fluid levels, Rokitansky protuberance, and calcified components. On CT and MRI, intratumoral macroscopic fat is diagnostic. Grossly, sebaceous material, hair, and occasionally bone/teeth reflect these imaging characteristics.
- Serous tumors: Typically appear as unilocular or multilocular cystic masses with thin septations; papillary excrescences projecting into the cyst lumen on imaging raise suspicion for borderline or malignant serous tumors and correspond histologically to epithelial papillae. Psammoma bodies may cause punctate calcifications on imaging.
- Mucinous tumors: Often multilocular cystic masses with variable signal intensity on MRI corresponding to differences in mucin content; huge size and multilocularity may be suggestive of mucinous tumors. If septal nodules or solid enhancing components are present on imaging, malignancy should be suspected correlating with invasive epithelial growth on histology.

Ascites and peritoneal implants on imaging often indicate malignant ovarian tumors with peritoneal dissemination; on pathology these correspond to surface tumor implants, omental metastases, and positive cytology. Tumor markers assist but are not diagnostic alone: CA-125 is often elevated in epithelial ovarian carcinomas (normal <35 U/mL), but can also be raised in endometriosis, menstruation, and benign conditions.

Imaging guides specimen handling and intraoperative strategy: radiologic suspicion for malignancy should prompt thorough sampling and alert the pathologist to focus on detecting invasive foci. Conversely, recognition that certain radiologic features (e.g., fat in a dermoid, T1 hyperintensity in endometrioma) correlate with benign pathology can prevent over-treatment.

Closing remarks

Understanding the histopathology of uterine and adnexal masses is essential for accurate diagnosis and appropriate clinical management. Recognizing the architecture of leiomyomas, patterns of degeneration, and the diagnostic criteria that separate benign from malignant smooth muscle tumors prevents misclassification. For adnexal masses, familiarity with the distinctive histologic features of endometriomas, dermoids, and serous and mucinous epithelial tumors, combined with prudent use of frozen section and cytology, allows the surgeon and pathologist to make informed intraoperative decisions. Integrating gross pathology with imaging and clinical context completes the diagnostic picture and optimizes patient care.

Introduction

Pelvic masses encountered in pregnancy encompass a spectrum of benign and malignant lesions, with uterine leiomyomas (fibroids) and adnexal masses (functional cysts, endometriomas, dermoids, paraovarian cysts, and neoplasms) being the most common. Their presence during pregnancy raises distinct concerns: mechanical effects on the pregnant pelvis and uterus, symptom exacerbation related to hormonal milieu, acute complications requiring urgent intervention, and potential implications for labor, delivery, and neonatal outcomes. Clinical management must balance maternal safety and optimal fetal outcomes, recognizing that many masses are managed conservatively during pregnancy and often regress postpartum. This chapter synthesizes evidence on rates and predictors of adverse obstetric outcomes associated with pelvic masses, delineates immediate complications and their clinical presentations, explores long-term maternal consequences including fertility and the need for delayed surgery, reviews the natural postpartum involution of leiomyomas and adnexal cysts, addresses neonatal risks related to maternal surgical interventions and timing, and offers guidance on postpartum surveillance imaging and indications for intervention.

# Rates and predictors of preterm labor, miscarriage and cesarean delivery associated with fibroids and adnexal masses

Uterine leiomyomas and adnexal masses influence pregnancy outcomes variably, depending on characteristics such as size, number, location, and growth dynamics. Large or submucosal fibroids, rapidly growing lesions, and adnexal masses that impinge on the uterine corpus or cervix are associated with higher risks.

Miscarriage: Several cohort and case-control studies indicate a modestly increased risk of first- and second-trimester pregnancy loss in women with fibroids. The magnitude varies; pooled estimates suggest an adjusted relative risk of early pregnancy loss of approximately 1.2–1.6 in some series. Submucosal fibroids and those that significantly distort the endometrial cavity confer the highest risk, presumably via local effects on implantation, endometrial receptivity, and trophoblast invasion. Adnexal masses are not major independent contributors to miscarriage unless they are associated with ovarian torsion, hemorrhage, or infection that destabilize maternal physiology.

Preterm labor and preterm birth: The presence of fibroids confers an increased risk of preterm labor and delivery. Large fibroids (>5–6 cm), multiple fibroids, rapidly enlarging fibroids, and those located in the lower uterine segment or near the uterine cervix are associated with a higher incidence of preterm labor, cervical insufficiency-type presentations, and preterm premature rupture of membranes. A typical pooled risk increase for preterm birth (before 37 weeks) is in the range of 1.2–1.5 compared with women without fibroids, with higher risks for more severe fibroid phenotypes. For adnexal masses, the association with spontaneous preterm labor is less consistent; symptomatic masses that cause acute abdomen or require surgical intervention are more likely to precipitate preterm delivery due to maternal stress, anesthesia, or iatrogenic preterm delivery.

Cesarean delivery: Fibroids are a well-established risk factor for cesarean delivery. Mechanisms include abnormal uterine contractility, malpresentation (breech, transverse, oblique) when fibroids occupy the uterine fundus or lower segment, mechanical obstruction of the birth canal by large lower-segment fibroids, and clinician preference driven by perceived risks of obstructed labor or hemorrhage. Reported cesarean rates among pregnant women with fibroids vary widely but are commonly 1.5–3 times higher than in comparable populations without fibroids. Predictors of cesarean include cervical or lower-segment fibroid location, size >5–6 cm, multiple fibroids, and prior cesarean or other obstetric indications. Adnexal masses seldom necessitate cesarean for mechanical obstruction unless exceptionally large or pedunculated in the pelvis; however, adnexal pathology may prompt cesarean if concurrent laparotomy or adnexal surgery is planned.

Clinical decision points: When counseling patients, it is important to stratify risk based on mass characteristics. For leiomyomas, factors increasing risk are: submucosal location, size >5–6 cm, number >3, and lower-segment location. For adnexal masses, size (commonly >5–10 cm depending on morphology), solid vs cystic nature, and symptomatic presentation guide risk estimates. Surveillance plans should be individualized; asymptomatic small masses typically require routine prenatal care with serial imaging only if clinically indicated, whereas larger or complex lesions merit multidisciplinary planning with obstetrics, maternal-fetal medicine, and gynecologic oncology or surgery as appropriate.

# Immediate complications: torsion, rupture, hemorrhage, infection, acute abdomen

Pelvic masses in pregnancy can present acutely with complications that constitute obstetric surgical emergencies. The pregnancy state alters the risk profile: increased vascularity, hormonal stimulation, and mechanical displacement can predispose to torsion, rupture, hemorrhage, and infection. Rapid identification and appropriate management are essential to limit maternal morbidity and fetal compromise.

Torsion: Ovarian torsion involves rotation of the ovary and often the fallopian tube around the vascular pedicle, leading to ischemia. Pregnancy is a recognized risk factor, particularly in the first and early second trimesters when corpus luteum cysts are common and ovarian mobility is higher. Preexisting adnexal masses increase the likelihood of torsion; cysts larger than 5–6 cm are at higher risk. Clinical presentation is acute unilateral pelvic or abdominal pain, often severe and intermittent early on as torsion may be intermittent. Nausea and vomiting are common. Fever and leukocytosis can occur but are not specific. Doppler ultrasound may show absent or diminished ovarian blood flow, but normal flow does not exclude torsion due to dual ovarian arterial supply and intermittent torsion. Prompt diagnosis and surgery (laparoscopy preferred when feasible) with detorsion and cystectomy or oophorectomy as needed should be considered, balancing gestational age and fetal considerations.

Rupture and hemorrhage: Hemorrhagic ovarian cyst rupture and bleeding from vascular leiomyomas can present with acute abdomen and hemodynamic instability. Rupture of a corpus luteum or hemorrhagic cyst commonly manifests with sudden unilateral lower abdomen pain, peritoneal signs, and sometimes syncope if intraperitoneal bleeding is significant. Hemodynamic assessment and serial hemoglobin/hematocrit measurements guide urgency. For fibroids, degeneration (red degeneration) may cause intratumoral hemorrhage and acute pain with systemic symptoms; intraperitoneal hemorrhage from fibroid erosion is less common but can be catastrophic if it occurs, particularly with large subserosal or pedunculated fibroids. Management ranges from conservative (analgesia, observation) for hemodynamically stable patients to urgent surgical intervention for ongoing hemorrhage.

Infection: Pelvic mass–related infection can include infected ovarian cysts, tubo-ovarian abscess, or secondary infection of a necrotic fibroid (especially after red degeneration). Presentations often include fever, localized pelvic pain, leukocytosis, and systemic signs. In pregnant patients, empiric broad-spectrum antibiotics tailored to likely pathogens are indicated while planning drainage or surgical management if there is an abscess or failure to respond to medical therapy. Imaging with ultrasound and, when necessary and safe, MRI can localize collections without ionizing radiation.

Acute abdomen: Distinguishing obstetric from non-obstetric causes of acute abdomen in pregnancy is challenging. Mass-related complications must be considered alongside appendicitis, cholecystitis, pancreatitis, bowel obstruction, and placental abruption. Clinical signs (localized peritonitis, rebound tenderness, persistence of severe pain), laboratory markers (white blood cell count, C-reactive protein), and imaging guide diagnosis. Ultrasound is first-line due to safety; MRI is valuable when ultrasound is inconclusive. Timely surgical consultation is essential because delayed intervention increases risks of sepsis, fetal compromise, and maternal morbidity.

Clinical reasoning in acute complications balances maternal hemodynamic stability, gestational age, fetal status, and the likelihood of salvageable ovarian tissue or control of hemorrhage. Laparoscopy can be safe in pregnancy, particularly in the first and early second trimesters, but decision-making must incorporate the surgeon's experience and anticipated complexity.

# Long-term maternal outcomes: fertility, recurrence, need for delayed surgery

Fertility: The impact of pelvic masses on fertility depends on lesion type and anatomic effects. Submucosal leiomyomas and those that distort the endometrial cavity impair implantation and are associated with reduced clinical pregnancy rates and increased miscarriage rates in assisted and spontaneous conception contexts. Intramural fibroids that do not deform the cavity may have a lesser or negligible effect; evidence is mixed. After pregnancy complicated by fibroids, most women retain fertility unless definitive surgery (myomectomy or hysterectomy) is performed. For adnexal masses, ovarian reserve can be affected if cystectomy entails significant removal of ovarian tissue or if ovarian torsion leads to oophorectomy. Use of ovarian-sparing techniques and careful surgical planning can mitigate long-term impacts on fertility. Preoperative counseling should include discussion of potential effects on fertility, alternatives, and fertility preservation as appropriate.

Recurrence: Leiomyomas can recur after myomectomy, with recurrence rates influenced by age, number of fibroids removed, and completeness of excision. Younger women tend to have higher recurrence rates due to longer remaining reproductive years and ongoing estrogen exposure. Recurrence after pregnancy is possible but many fibroids regress postpartum. Adnexal cysts like functional cysts typically do not recur if conservative management addresses the underlying hormonal milieu; endometriomas, however, have higher recurrence rates and may require staged management. Dermoids and neoplasms have different recurrence profiles based on histology and completeness of resection.

Need for delayed surgery: A substantial proportion of pelvic masses identified in pregnancy are managed conservatively with delayed postpartum surgery if symptomatic or suspicious for malignancy. Indications for postpartum intervention include persistent or enlarging masses, persistent pain, concern for neoplasm on imaging (solid components, papillary projections, septations, vascular flow patterns suggestive of malignancy), and masses that interfere with future pregnancies or delivery planning. Timing of delayed surgery is individualized: many surgeons wait 6–12 weeks postpartum to allow uterine involution and resolution of pregnancy-related anatomic changes, but earlier intervention may be warranted for persistent severe symptoms or suspicion of malignancy. For fibroids, postpartum myomectomy is often deferred due to increased vascularity and risk of hemorrhage during pregnancy; the postpartum period typically carries lower surgical risk once involution is underway. When hysterectomy is contemplated, counseling must consider parity, desire for future fertility, and overall gynecologic health.

Clinical decision points for delayed surgery include assessment of symptom burden, imaging characteristics (via ultrasound or MRI), tumor markers such as CA-125 when ovarian malignancy is a concern (bearing in mind normal ranges: CA-125 <35 U/mL in premenopausal women is commonly used as a cutoff but has many false positives), and the timing relative to breastfeeding and recovery. Multidisciplinary discussion with gynecologic oncology, reproductive endocrinology, and maternal-fetal medicine improves individualized care.

# Postpartum involution/regression of leiomyomas and adnexal cysts

Pregnancy induces hormonal and hemodynamic changes that affect pelvic masses, which often partially or fully regress after delivery. Understanding typical patterns of postpartum involution helps guide surveillance and avoids unnecessary early surgical interventions.

Leiomyomas: Many leiomyomas decrease in size postpartum due to the dramatic decline in circulating estrogen and progesterone and uterine involution. Studies report variable rates of regression; a substantial proportion (often cited as 30–60% depending on size and location) demonstrate measurable postpartum shrinkage, particularly small intramural and subserosal fibroids. Submucosal fibroids may persist or change less predictably if they have already distorted the cavity. Factors associated with postpartum regression include smaller baseline size (<5–6 cm), fewer fibroids, and older maternal age (less endogenous estrogen stimulation postpartum when ovarian function may be reduced temporarily). Lactation has been suggested to further suppress ovarian function and may contribute to slower fibroid regrowth in the early postpartum period. However, some fibroids may remain unchanged or even increase in size, particularly if growth during pregnancy was driven by local factors or if significant vascular supply persists.

Adnexal cysts: Functional ovarian cysts often resolve spontaneously in the postpartum period as the ovarian cycle reestablishes and corpus luteum cysts created in early pregnancy resolve. Corpus luteum cysts and simple cysts commonly regress within a few weeks to months. Endometriomas and dermoids are less likely to involute and may persist postpartum. For cysts that were conservative-managed during pregnancy, a planned reassessment at 6–12 weeks postpartum with pelvic ultrasound is common practice. Persistent complex cysts, cysts with solid components, or enlarging lesions warrant further evaluation with MRI and gynecologic oncology consultation.

Clinical implications: Anticipating regression avoids overtreatment. For patients with mildly symptomatic fibroids or adnexal masses that were stable during pregnancy, expectant management into the postpartum window is often reasonable. However, persistent symptoms (pain, bleeding outside the immediate postpartum period, or new mass effect) or concern about malignancy prompt earlier imaging and potential surgical planning. Breastfeeding status and its suppression of ovulation should be discussed as it may influence timing and extent of tumor regression.

# Neonatal outcomes related to maternal surgical interventions and timing

Surgical interventions for pelvic masses during pregnancy carry fetal risks that depend on gestational age, the urgency of surgery, anesthetic exposure, and procedural factors. Neonatal outcomes are tied both to direct effects of surgery and to decisions to deliver preterm in the context of maternal indications.

Timing of surgery: The risk-benefit calculus varies by trimester. First-trimester surgery carries higher theoretical risk of teratogenesis and miscarriage, although necessary interventions (e.g., for life-threatening maternal disease) may still be performed. Elective pelvic surgery is generally avoided during the first trimester when possible. The second trimester (preferably between 14–20 weeks) is considered the safest window for nonobstetric abdominal and pelvic surgery because organogenesis is complete, the uterus is still relatively small for surgical access, and the risk of preterm labor is lower than later in pregnancy. Third-trimester surgery increases the risks of preterm labor and delivery; if delivery is anticipated, coordination between surgical and obstetric teams is needed.

Fetal and neonatal risks: Immediate fetal risks include miscarriage, preterm birth, fetal distress during maternal hemodynamic compromise, and the potential effects of anesthetic agents. Contemporary general and regional anesthetic techniques are broadly considered safe when maternal physiology is optimized, but intraoperative hypotension, hypoxia, or significant hemorrhage can jeopardize fetal oxygenation. Preterm neonates face the expected sequelae of prematurity (respiratory distress syndrome, intraventricular hemorrhage, necrotizing enterocolitis, sepsis), with gestational age at delivery being the primary determinant of outcome.

Specific neonatal outcomes associated with interventions: When surgery is urgent and unavoidable in the second trimester, fetal loss rates are low but not negligible; reported rates vary by indication and series. Laparoscopic procedures have been performed successfully with low fetal morbidity when performed by experienced teams using intraoperative monitoring and techniques to minimize uterine manipulation and intraabdominal pressure (insufflation pressures typically kept at or below 12 mmHg). Intraoperative fetal monitoring is useful in viable pregnancies, though continuous monitoring may be impractical during many urgent procedures; pre- and postoperative assessment and monitoring are therefore critical. For procedures leading to iatrogenic preterm delivery, neonatal outcomes correspond to the degree of prematurity and the availability of neonatal intensive care.

Medication considerations: Perioperative medications (antibiotics, tocolytics, analgesics) have fetal safety profiles that must be considered. For example, prostaglandin-inducing agents and some NSAIDs have known fetal risks (e.g., premature closure of the ductus arteriosus), and the use of tocolytics such as nifedipine or indomethacin for perioperative uterine relaxation is practiced selectively. Steroids for fetal lung maturation should be administered when preterm delivery is anticipated between 24 and 34 weeks gestation, and a risk-benefit discussion is essential when surgery threatens imminent preterm birth.

Clinical decision points: Ideally, nonurgent pelvic surgery should be deferred until postpartum. When surgery during pregnancy is necessary for maternal indications (torsion, rupture, severe hemorrhage, or suspicion of malignancy requiring timely diagnosis), the second trimester is preferred. Multidisciplinary planning with obstetrics, anesthesia, neonatology, and surgery enables optimization of neonatal outcomes, including preparations for possible preterm delivery and neonatal intensive care. Counseling should include discussion of maternal risks, the likelihood of preterm birth, and expected neonatal sequelae given gestational age.

# Follow-up imaging and indications for postpartum intervention

Structured postpartum follow-up ensures that masses that regress are distinguished from those requiring intervention and that potential malignancies are appropriately identified and treated. Timing and modality of imaging should be tailored to the lesion type, symptoms, and prior imaging characteristics.

Timing and modality: For most women with pelvic masses identified during pregnancy and managed expectantly, the first postpartum imaging is typically scheduled at 6–12 weeks. This interval allows uterine involution and attenuation of pregnancy-related vascular changes, making imaging interpretation more reliable. Transvaginal and transabdominal ultrasound remain first-line for pelvic mass evaluation; MRI without gadolinium is useful as a second-line modality when ultrasound is inconclusive, when there are complex adnexal masses, or when better tissue characterization is required. CT is generally avoided for routine pelvic mass evaluation in reproductive-age women and is not preferred postpartum unless specific indications demand it.

Indications for repeat or earlier imaging: Persistent or worsening pelvic pain, new symptoms (e.g., abnormal bleeding, palpable mass), prior imaging showing complex features (solid components, papillary projections, thick septations, increased vascularity), significant size (>5–10 cm depending on morphology and location), and any features suggestive of malignancy warrant earlier and more detailed imaging. If imaging in pregnancy suggested complexity or growth, an expedited postpartum MRI may be indicated. Similarly, if adnexal mass markers (e.g., CA-125) were elevated or suspicious features were present, dedicated postpartum workup should not be delayed.

Criteria for intervention: Indications for postpartum surgical intervention include suspicion of malignancy based on imaging or clinical course, persistent symptomatic masses (pain, bleeding, urinary or gastrointestinal symptoms), risk of complications in future pregnancies (e.g., large fibroids obstructing labor), and patient preference after counseling on risks and benefits. For adnexal masses, size thresholds vary by center, but commonly cysts >5–10 cm, especially with complex features, are considered for resection. For fibroids, intervention is more individualized: myomectomy is considered for symptomatic relief (pain, heavy bleeding after uterine involution) or infertility related to cavity distortion. Hysterectomy is reserved for definitive management when childbearing is complete or when malignancy or intractable symptoms dictate this approach.

Preoperative evaluation and planning: Preoperative imaging should include mapping of fibroid size, number, and location relative to the cavity and cervix. For adnexal masses, MRI can provide tissue characterization to differentiate benign from malignant lesions and inform fertility-sparing surgical approaches. Laboratory workup may include tumor markers (CA-125, HE4, AFP, beta-hCG, LDH, inhibin B) as indicated by age and imaging features; interpretation must consider confounding factors like endometriosis and recent pregnancy. When a malignancy is suspected, referral to gynecologic oncology is appropriate for staging and management.

Follow-up after intervention: After surgical treatment, surveillance is dictated by histology and completeness of resection. Benign lesions generally require routine postoperative care, while malignant pathology mandates oncologic staging and tailored follow-up. For fertility-preserving surgeries, ovarian reserve testing (anti-Mullerian hormone, antral follicle counts) may be considered if ovarian tissue was affected.

Conclusion

Pelvic masses in pregnancy present a complex interplay of risks encompassing obstetric outcomes, acute maternal complications, and long-term gynecologic consequences. Clinical assessment must focus on lesion-specific characteristics—size, number, location, morphology—and symptomatology. Most masses can be managed conservatively during pregnancy, with vigilant surveillance and clear criteria for surgical intervention. The postpartum period offers an opportunity for re-evaluation after physiologic regression; many leiomyomas and functional adnexal cysts involute, while persistent, symptomatic, or suspicious lesions warrant timely imaging and, when indicated, surgical management. Multidisciplinary coordination and individualized counseling around timing and risks—especially when surgery during pregnancy is contemplated—optimize maternal and neonatal outcomes.

# Introduction

Urinary tract infections (UTIs) are one of the most common bacterial infections encountered in pregnancy. Physiologic changes of pregnancy predispose to urinary stasis and ascending infection, and infections during gestation carry distinct risks to both mother and fetus — including pyelonephritis, sepsis, and increased risk of preterm birth and low birth weight. Management must balance prompt eradication of pathogens with selection of antibiotics that are effective yet safe for the fetus, and prevention of progression to severe infection. This chapter reviews epidemiology, pregnancy-related anatomic and physiologic contributors to UTI risk, screening and treatment recommendations for asymptomatic bacteriuria, common pathogens and their resistance patterns, complications of untreated infection, antibiotic choices in pregnancy, and perioperative considerations when infection is present concurrently with operative interventions.

# Epidemiology and physiology increasing UTI risk during pregnancy

UTIs affect approximately 2–10% of pregnant women as symptomatic infections, while asymptomatic bacteriuria (ASB) can be detected in 2–11% depending on population and screening practices. The prevalence varies by socioeconomic factors, prior UTI history, parity, and comorbidities such as diabetes.

Physiologic and anatomic changes in pregnancy that increase UTI risk include hormonal, mechanical, and immunologic factors. Progesterone-mediated smooth muscle relaxation causes dilation of the ureters and reduced ureteral peristalsis; mechanical compression by the gravid uterus further contributes to urinary stasis, especially on the right side. Stasis reduces washout of bacteria and promotes ascent of organisms from the bladder toward the renal pelvis. Increased renal plasma flow and glomerular filtration rate lead to higher urinary glucose and amino acid excretion early in pregnancy, which can facilitate bacterial growth in some settings. Alterations in bladder capacity and incomplete bladder emptying — common in late pregnancy — further predispose to infection.

Immunologic changes of pregnancy are complex and not simply a global immunosuppression; there is modulation of innate and adaptive immunity that may alter susceptibility and the clinical presentation of infection. For example, leukocyte counts are often higher in pregnancy (physiologic leukocytosis) and febrile responses can be blunted, potentially delaying recognition. Finally, genitourinary microbiome changes and increased incidence of vesicoureteral reflux during pregnancy may facilitate ascending infections.

Clinically, these changes mean pregnant women are at increased risk of progression from lower UTI (cystitis) to upper UTI (pyelonephritis), and thus early detection and appropriate treatment are emphasized in obstetric care.

# Asymptomatic bacteriuria screening and treatment recommendations

Asymptomatic bacteriuria (ASB) is defined as the presence of a significant quantity of bacteria in an appropriately collected urine specimen in a patient without signs or symptoms of UTI. In pregnancy, ASB is clinically important because it is associated with increased risk of pyelonephritis and adverse obstetrical outcomes if left untreated.

Diagnostic threshold and testing
- The traditional microbiologic definition used in pregnancy is a single clean-catch voided urine culture growing a single uropathogen at ≥ 10^5 colony-forming units per milliliter (CFU/mL) in an asymptomatic woman. Many guidelines continue to recommend this threshold for screening and treatment decisions in pregnancy.
- For catheterized specimens, the threshold for significant bacteriuria is lower (≥ 10^2–10^3 CFU/mL) because catheter collection bypasses some contamination risks.
- Some centers use lower thresholds (≥ 10^3 CFU/mL) or require two consecutive positive cultures in nonpregnant populations, but in pregnancy one properly collected culture with ≥ 10^5 CFU/mL is generally sufficient to diagnose ASB.

Screening recommendations
- Universal screening for ASB with a urine culture is recommended at the first prenatal visit (often at 12–16 weeks gestation, or at the initial prenatal visit if earlier). Repeat screening later in pregnancy is not universally recommended, but should be considered in women at high risk (e.g., recurrent UTI, diabetes, sickle cell disease, structural urologic abnormalities).
- Dipstick testing alone (nitrite, leukocyte esterase) is insufficiently sensitive to exclude ASB in pregnancy; culture remains the gold standard.

Treatment of ASB
- Treatment of ASB in pregnancy reduces the incidence of pyelonephritis and is associated with lower rates of low birth weight and preterm delivery in randomized trials and observational studies.
- Recommended practice is to treat all pregnant women with ASB identified on culture with an appropriate antibiotic for a defined course, guided by susceptibility testing when available.
- Typical regimens: nitrofurantoin (100 mg twice daily for 5–7 days) or a beta-lactam (amoxicillin-clavulanate, or a cephalosporin such as cefalexin) for 3–7 days depending on the agent. Fosfomycin trometamol 3 g single dose is an alternative in some settings where susceptibility is expected and the organism is susceptible.
- After completing therapy, a test-of-cure urine culture is often obtained 1–2 weeks later to confirm eradication. If persistent bacteriuria is present, guided retreatment based on susceptibility and consideration of urology consult for anatomic evaluation are indicated.

Clinical decision points
- Treat all pregnant women with ASB detected on screening culture.
- Use local antibiogram and susceptibility data to select empiric therapy when culture results are pending; adjust once susceptibilities are known.
- Consider repeat cultures after treatment and periodic rescreening in high-risk women.

# Common pathogens (Escherichia coli, Klebsiella spp.) and antibiograms in pregnancy

Etiology
- The dominant uropathogen in pregnancy is Escherichia coli, accounting for approximately 70–90% of UTIs, including ASB and symptomatic infections. Other common organisms include Klebsiella species, Proteus mirabilis, Enterococcus faecalis, and group B Streptococcus (Streptococcus agalactiae). Staphylococcus saprophyticus is less common in pregnancy than in the general young female population.
- Group B Streptococcus deserves particular attention because colonization of the urinary tract can reflect heavy GBS colonization; detection has implications for intrapartum antibiotic prophylaxis. GBS bacteriuria at any concentration is a marker for heavy vaginal colonization and typically prompts intrapartum penicillin prophylaxis to prevent neonatal GBS disease.

Antibiograms and resistance considerations
- Local and regional resistance patterns vary substantially and should guide empiric therapy. E. coli resistance to commonly used antibiotics such as ampicillin and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX) is high in many settings; resistance to ampicillin often exceeds 40–50% in some regions. Resistance to ciprofloxacin and other fluoroquinolones has risen as well, making them less favored.
- Extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL)-producing Enterobacterales (often E. coli or Klebsiella) are an increasing concern. ESBL organisms are resistant to penicillins and most cephalosporins; carbapenems may be required for severe infections with ESBL organisms.
- Klebsiella species may also carry resistance determinants, including ESBLs and carbapenemases in high-risk settings.
- Enterococcus spp. are intrinsically resistant to cephalosporins and may require ampicillin or vancomycin depending on susceptibility.

Implications for pregnancy
- Because many first-line, safe-for-pregnancy agents (e.g., nitrofurantoin, certain beta-lactams) retain activity against the majority of community E. coli isolates, these remain reasonable empiric choices for ASB and uncomplicated cystitis in pregnant patients in many settings. However, where local resistance to nitrofurantoin or amoxicillin-clavulanate exceeds acceptable thresholds, selection must be tailored to culture results.
- Always obtain urine culture prior to starting antibiotics when feasible to enable de-escalation based on susceptibilities.

# Complications of untreated UTI: pyelonephritis, sepsis and preterm birth

Ascending infection and pyelonephritis
- Untreated lower urinary tract infection in pregnancy carries a higher risk of progression to acute pyelonephritis than in nonpregnant women due to ureteral dilation and urinary stasis. Pyelonephritis typically presents with fever, flank pain, costovertebral angle tenderness, nausea/vomiting, and systemic features. Laboratory findings include pyuria, bacteriuria, and often elevated inflammatory markers.
- Pyelonephritis in pregnancy is associated with significant maternal morbidity: dehydration, electrolyte abnormalities, bacteremia, renal impairment, and in severe cases septic shock. Hospitalization for intravenous antibiotics and supportive care is commonly required.

Sepsis
- Bacteremia may complicate pyelonephritis and progress to sepsis. Pregnancy-specific recognition of sepsis is important because physiologic changes in pregnancy modify the presentation. For example, baseline leukocytosis and increased heart rate complicate standard sepsis scoring. Obstetric early warning scores and pregnancy-adapted sepsis guidelines can improve early detection.
- Sepsis in pregnancy increases the risk of multiorgan dysfunction and may necessitate intensive care, vasopressor support, and source control. Maternal sepsis poses risks to the fetus, including fetal hypoxia and preterm delivery as a consequence of maternal instability.

Adverse obstetrical outcomes
- Untreated or severe UTIs are associated with increased risk of preterm labor, low birth weight, and perinatal morbidity. The mechanisms are multifactorial and may involve systemic inflammatory responses, cytokine-mediated uterine activity, and direct effects of bacteremia and endotoxemia.
- Pyelonephritis during pregnancy has been associated with a higher rate of preterm birth and low birth weight even when appropriately treated; prevention by screening and treatment of ASB is therefore an important obstetric intervention.

Other complications
- Recurrent UTIs may occur and merit investigation for urinary tract abnormalities, diabetes, or other predisposing factors. Rare complications include renal cortical abscess, emphysematous pyelonephritis (more common in diabetics), and perinephric abscesses, which are surgical problems requiring drainage.

Clinical decision points
- Recognize that fever and flank pain in a pregnant patient merit evaluation for pyelonephritis and prompt initiation of appropriate therapy.
- Consider hospital admission for pregnant patients with pyelonephritis, particularly if febrile, dehydrated, vomiting, or showing signs of systemic toxicity.
- Use pregnancy-adapted sepsis recognition tools and involve multidisciplinary teams early (obstetrics, infectious disease, critical care).

# Choice of antibiotics during pregnancy based on safety and sensitivity

Principles
- Select antibiotics that are both effective against likely pathogens and considered safe in pregnancy. Consider gestational age, microbiologic susceptibilities, severity of infection, and potential fetal effects. Whenever possible, obtain urine culture prior to starting antibiotics and tailor therapy based on susceptibilities.
- Avoid agents with known teratogenicity or neonatal toxicity when safer alternatives exist.

Commonly used agents and considerations
- Nitrofurantoin: Often used for ASB and cystitis (nitrofurantoin 100 mg twice daily for 5–7 days). It is generally considered safe in pregnancy for most of gestation. Avoid use at term (≥ 38–40 weeks) and during labor and delivery due to theoretical risk of hemolytic anemia in the neonate with glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency. Use with caution in the first trimester; while large studies have not consistently demonstrated major teratogenic risk, some prefer to avoid unnecessary exposures in very early pregnancy.
- Beta-lactams: Penicillins (e.g., amoxicillin) and cephalosporins (e.g., cephalexin, cefuroxime) are widely used and considered safe in pregnancy. Amoxicillin-clavulanate is often effective for community pathogens, although local resistance may limit utility. Duration typically 3–7 days for cystitis; longer for pyelonephritis.
- Fosfomycin trometamol: A single 3 g oral dose can be effective for uncomplicated cystitis and is considered safe in pregnancy. It is convenient and used where susceptibility is expected. If used for ASB or cystitis, test-of-cure culture is often recommended given single-dose regimen.
- Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX): Generally avoided in the first trimester because trimethoprim is a folate antagonist associated with potential neural tube defect risk; also avoided near term (last weeks of gestation) due to risk of kernicterus from displacement of bilirubin in the neonate. TMP-SMX may be used in middle pregnancy if no alternatives and if benefits outweigh risks, but this is generally reserved for specific indications and with specialist input.
- Aminoglycosides (e.g., gentamicin): Effective for severe Gram-negative infections and sometimes used in combination for pyelonephritis. However, aminoglycosides carry risks of fetal ototoxicity and nephrotoxicity; short courses may be used when benefits outweigh risks, often under specialist guidance and with monitoring.
- Fluoroquinolones: Generally avoided in pregnancy due to concerns about effects on fetal cartilage development in animal studies and potential adverse effects, despite limited human data. Reserved only for situations where no safer alternatives are available and after specialist consultation.
- Carbapenems: May be used for severe infections due to ESBL organisms in pregnancy when indicated; carbapenems are not known to be teratogenic and can be lifesaving for severe resistant infections.
- Vancomycin: Safe in pregnancy for Gram-positive infections such as Enterococcus (when susceptible) or MRSA; used for severe infections when indicated.

Treatment duration and routes
- ASB: oral therapy for 3–7 days depending on agent (e.g., 5–7 days for nitrofurantoin, 3 days for short-course beta-lactams may be inadequate — many recommend 5–7 days).
- Uncomplicated cystitis: 3–7 days depending on agent; fosfomycin single dose is an alternative.
- Acute pyelonephritis: initial parenteral therapy (e.g., IV ceftriaxone or ampicillin plus gentamicin depending on susceptibility and local practice) with clinical improvement allowing step-down to oral therapy for a total of typically 10–14 days or until full resolution.
- Severely ill or septic patients require IV, broad-spectrum empiric therapy covering Gram-negatives; choices should be guided by local antibiograms and pregnancy safety profiles, often involving third-generation cephalosporins (e.g., ceftriaxone) or carbapenems for resistant organisms.

Monitoring and stewardship
- Reassess therapy once culture and susceptibility data are available and de-escalate appropriately.
- Obtain test-of-cure cultures after treatment of ASB and consider repeat culture for symptomatic infections to ensure clearance.
- Recognize drug-specific maternal side effects (e.g., nitrofurantoin pulmonary toxicity is rare but possible; beta-lactam allergies) and adjust therapy accordingly.

# Perioperative considerations when concurrent infection exists

General principles
- The presence of a urinary tract infection or bacteriuria has implications for perioperative management in pregnant patients for both obstetric and non-obstetric procedures. The main goals are to reduce perioperative infectious risk, avoid maternal deterioration, and minimize fetal compromise.

Timing of surgery
- Elective procedures should ideally be postponed until UTIs are treated and the patient is clinically stable and afebrile with negative cultures when feasible. For urgent or emergent procedures, proceed with appropriate antibiotic therapy and source control while coordinating obstetric and perioperative teams.
- In obstetric practice, active infection (e.g., pyelonephritis with sepsis) is itself an indication for careful consideration regarding timing of delivery; delivery should be dictated by obstetrical indications and maternal stability rather than treating infection alone.

Antibiotic prophylaxis and therapy
- Perioperative prophylactic antibiotics should consider the current urinary pathogen and susceptibilities. If bacteriuria is present and surgery is necessary, perioperative antibiotics should cover the organism; this may require tailoring standard prophylactic regimens.
- For cesarean delivery in a patient with bacteriuria or recent UTI, standard perioperative prophylaxis (e.g., cefazolin) may be continued if organisms are susceptible. If bacteremia or resistant organisms are present, broaden regimens as indicated and consult infectious disease.
- For instrumentation of the urinary tract (e.g., catheterization, urologic procedures), ensure appropriate peri-procedural antibiotics per urology guidelines, particularly if bacteriuria is documented.

Anesthesia considerations
- Neuraxial anesthesia (spinal or epidural) is commonly used in obstetric operations. In the presence of systemic infection or bacteremia, there is theoretical concern that neuraxial procedures could introduce pathogens into the central nervous system, though evidence is limited. Most guidelines recommend caution: avoid neuraxial anesthesia in patients with uncontrolled systemic sepsis until infection is controlled. If bacteremia is suspected or documented, multidisciplinary discussion with anesthesiology is warranted; alternatives include general anesthesia or delaying neuraxial until infection is treated.
- For patients with localized urinary infection without systemic signs, neuraxial anesthesia is often acceptable.

Intraoperative and postoperative monitoring
- Monitor for signs of worsening maternal infection or sepsis intra- and post-operatively. Pregnancy complicates interpretation of vital signs; maintain a low threshold for escalation.
- Consider fetal monitoring intraoperatively for viable fetuses when feasible, especially if maternal infection or hemodynamic instability is present.
- Use thromboprophylaxis as indicated; pregnancy is a hypercoagulable state and infection increases thrombotic risk.

Device-related infection risk
- Indwelling urinary catheters increase the risk of catheter-associated urinary tract infection (CAUTI). Avoid unnecessary catheterization, use aseptic technique for insertion, and remove catheters as soon as clinically possible. Antimicrobial-impregnated catheters are not routinely recommended solely for pregnancy.

Special scenarios
- For urologic surgery in pregnant patients with active infection, coordinate with urology for source control (e.g., drainage of obstruction) and with obstetrics for fetal considerations. In obstructive uropathy with infection, urgent decompression (e.g., ureteral stenting or percutaneous nephrostomy) may be necessary to control sepsis.
- In cases of resistant organisms (e.g., ESBL producers), perioperative therapy may require IV agents with activity against these pathogens (e.g., carbapenems). Maternal safety and fetal considerations must be balanced.

Clinical decision points
- Postpone elective surgery until infection is treated when possible.
- Treat documented bacteriuria prior to planned instrumentation; tailor perioperative antibiotics to susceptibilities.
- Avoid neuraxial anesthesia in uncontrolled systemic infection; individualize decisions with anesthesiology.
- Ensure catheter avoidance and prompt removal to reduce CAUTI risk.

# Conclusion

Urinary tract infections in pregnancy require proactive screening, prompt and pregnancy-safe therapy, and vigilance for complications. Universal urine culture screening at the first prenatal visit with treatment of asymptomatic bacteriuria reduces the risk of pyelonephritis and adverse perinatal outcomes. Empiric antibiotic choices should reflect local susceptibility patterns and pregnancy safety profiles; nitrofurantoin, beta-lactams, and fosfomycin are commonly used, while TMP-SMX, fluoroquinolones, and aminoglycosides have specific contraindications or cautions depending on gestational age and severity of infection. Severe infections and sepsis necessitate hospitalization, parenteral therapy, and multidisciplinary care. Perioperative management must prioritize maternal stabilization, appropriate antibiotic coverage, and procedural timing, with attention to anesthetic risks when bacteremia is present. Understanding the pathophysiology, microbiology, and evidence-based management principles enables clinicians to reduce maternal and fetal morbidity associated with UTIs in pregnancy.

Reference ranges and key diagnostic criteria summarized
- ASB in pregnancy: urine culture with a single uropathogen at ≥ 10^5 CFU/mL in an asymptomatic patient.
- Physiologic leukocytosis in pregnancy: WBC up to approximately 12,000/µL–15,000/µL may be normal; interpret leukocytosis in clinical context.
- Pyelonephritis treatment duration: typically 10–14 days (initial IV then oral stepdown as clinically indicated).
- Screen all pregnant women with urine culture at first prenatal visit (12–16 weeks commonly).

Introduction

Understanding the population-level distribution and determinants of uterine leiomyomas (fibroids) and endometriosis is essential for clinical reasoning: it shapes pretest probabilities, informs the index of suspicion for particular presentations, guides the selection and interpretation of diagnostic tests, and influences counseling about prognosis and reproductive planning. Both conditions are common gynecologic disorders of reproductive-aged women, but they differ in their pathophysiology, typical age of onset, associations with race and body habitus, and relationships to parity and infertility. This chapter synthesizes current epidemiologic knowledge and known or proposed risk factors, compares patterns of presentation across the life course, and outlines how these data should modify the clinician’s pretest probabilities and differential diagnoses when evaluating pelvic pain, abnormal uterine bleeding, an adnexal mass, or infertility.

# Prevalence of uterine leiomyomas and adnexal endometriomas across age groups and ethnicities

Prevalence estimates for both leiomyomas and endometriosis vary by study design, case definition, and population sampled. Key determinants of reported prevalence are whether the study uses clinical diagnosis, imaging (transvaginal ultrasound or MRI), operative diagnosis, or pathologic confirmation. Estimates drawn from population-based imaging and surgical cohorts give complementary perspectives.

Uterine leiomyomas (fibroids)
Uterine leiomyomas are extremely common. When assessed by imaging or at hysterectomy/autopsy, lifetime cumulative prevalence approaches 70–80% among women by menopause. The prevalence increases with age during the reproductive years: by the late 30s and 40s many women have detectable leiomyomas. Clinically significant fibroids—those causing symptoms prompting medical attention (heavy menstrual bleeding, pelvic pressure, bulk symptoms, pain, or reproductive dysfunction)—are less frequent than imaging-detected leiomyomas; estimates suggest roughly 20–30% of reproductive-aged women develop clinically significant fibroids.

Ethnic differences are pronounced. Black (African-American) women develop fibroids at younger ages, have higher prevalence, larger and more numerous tumors, and greater symptom burden compared with White women. Relative risk estimates vary, but Black women commonly have 2–3 times the prevalence of clinically significant fibroids seen in White women in many cohorts. Asian and Hispanic women have intermediate prevalence in different studies. These racial/ethnic differences persist after adjustment for socioeconomic and health-behavior covariates in many analyses, suggesting biologic contributions.

Adnexal endometriomas and endometriosis
Endometriosis is less ubiquitous than leiomyomas when measured by imaging or surgery, but still common among women of reproductive age. Prevalence estimates in unselected reproductive-aged populations are often cited around 5–10%, with many reviews proposing an overall prevalence near 10%. Among specific clinical populations the prevalence is much higher: up to 20–50% among women with chronic pelvic pain and 20–50% (often higher in surgical cohorts) among women with infertility. Rates differ further by age: endometriosis typically presents in adolescence or early adulthood and is concentrated in reproductive years; it usually regresses after menopause when ovarian estrogen production falls.

Adnexal endometriomas—ovarian cysts formed by endometriotic implants within the ovary—constitute a subset of endometriosis cases. In surgical series of women with proven endometriosis, endometriomas are reported in roughly 17–44% depending on cohort composition. On imaging (TVUS), an ovarian endometrioma may be suggested by a unilocular “ground-glass” cyst; however, many women with endometriosis lack endometriomas, and small endometriotic implants may not be seen on imaging.

Ethnic patterns for endometriosis are complex. Historically, diagnosis of endometriosis was reported more often in White, higher socioeconomic status, and infertile populations—likely reflecting referral and detection bias. In contemporary, diverse population-based studies, endometriosis occurs across racial and ethnic groups; some data suggest modest differences in prevalence, but these are smaller and less consistent than for leiomyomas. Underdiagnosis in marginalized groups remains a concern.

# Known and proposed risk factors (genetic, hormonal, environmental, BMI)

Both leiomyomas and endometriosis are multifactorial diseases. Genetic predisposition, hormonal milieu, reproductive history, somatic mutations (in fibroids), and environmental exposures all contribute to risk. Many associations are plausible mechanistically and supported by epidemiologic data; others remain hypotheses.

Genetic factors
Leiomyomas: Familial aggregation is well-recognized. Women with a first-degree relative with fibroids have higher risk. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified multiple susceptibility loci linked to leiomyoma development, implicating pathways in steroid hormone signaling, extracellular matrix regulation, and cell proliferation. Additionally, somatic mutations in MED12 are present in a high proportion (approximately 40–70%) of uterine leiomyomas, highlighting tumor-specific genetic events that drive clonal growth.

Endometriosis: There is also familial aggregation and increased risk among first-degree relatives. GWAS have identified susceptibility loci associated with endometriosis risk, with implicated pathways including sex-steroid signaling, inflammation, and cell adhesion. Endometriosis is polygenic with modest effect sizes per allele. Genetic predisposition is one component of risk but interacts with environmental and reproductive factors.

Hormonal factors
Both conditions are estrogen-dependent to an extent, but the hormonal dynamics and clinical consequences differ.

Leiomyomas: Estrogen and progesterone promote fibroid growth. Fibroids are rare before menarche and tend to grow during reproductive years, often shrinking after menopause. Exogenous estrogen exposure (e.g., hormone replacement without progestin) can stimulate growth; the role of combined hormonal contraceptives is less clear, with some evidence that low-dose combined oral contraceptives may control symptoms rather than increase fibroid risk. Hyperestrogenic states—driven by peripheral conversion in adipose tissue, higher circulating estrogens, or altered metabolism—can contribute.

Endometriosis: Endometriotic implants rely on estrogen for proliferation and cause local aromatase-driven estrogen production; they also demonstrate progesterone resistance. Early menarche and shorter menstrual cycles (greater lifetime menstrual exposure) are associated with higher endometriosis risk, consistent with retrograde menstruation hypotheses and cumulative estrogen exposure.

Environmental exposures
Leiomyomas: Epidemiologic data suggest associations between fibroids and environmental factors such as low vitamin D levels and exposure to endocrine-disrupting chemicals (phthalates, bisphenol A) in some studies. Hypertension and metabolic factors have been associated with increased risk. Occupational or dietary exposures have variable evidence.

Endometriosis: Smoking is inversely associated with endometriosis in some studies (potentially mediated through anti-estrogenic effects), but smoking has many harmful effects and is not protective clinically. Exposure to environmental toxins—dioxins and other endocrine disruptors—has been hypothesized as a risk factor; data in humans are inconsistent but animal studies suggest plausible mechanisms.

Body mass index (BMI)
The relationship between BMI and these diseases differs.

Leiomyomas: Higher BMI is generally associated with increased risk of fibroids, though the relationship is not strictly linear in all studies. Adiposity contributes to peripheral estrogen production via aromatization, which may increase fibroid growth. However, some studies report attenuation of association after adjusting for detection bias (e.g., imaging sensitivity).

Endometriosis: Many epidemiologic studies report an inverse association between BMI and endometriosis—women with lower BMI have higher risk. This relationship is not fully understood; it may reflect hormonal milieu, differences in menstrual characteristics, or detection bias (healthcare-seeking behavior). BMI is a marker that should be integrated with other clinical features rather than used alone.

Other medical comorbidities
Leiomyomas: Hypertension and certain metabolic comorbidities, including insulin resistance and dyslipidemia, have been linked to increased fibroid risk in some cohorts.

Endometriosis: Autoimmune diseases and allergy phenotypes have shown variable associations in observational studies, potentially reflecting shared inflammatory pathways.

# Impact of parity and fertility history on disease occurrence

Reproductive history profoundly affects risk for both conditions through mechanisms linked to hormonal exposure, menstrual cycles, and structural changes to the uterus and pelvis.

Parity and leiomyomas
Parity is generally protective against the development of fibroids. Higher parity and increasing number of full-term pregnancies are associated with lower fibroid risk. The protective effect is thought to arise from several mechanisms: the high progesterone state of pregnancy may modulate fibroid growth, postpartum uterine involution may cause regression of small fibroids, and pregnancies interrupt ovulatory cycles thus reducing cumulative exposure to cyclic ovarian hormones. Timing matters: earlier first full-term pregnancy appears more protective. The degree of protection varies by study but is clinically significant when estimating fibroid probability.

Parity and endometriosis
Nulliparity is a risk factor for endometriosis; conversely, women with endometriosis frequently have reduced parity due to subfertility. Whether nulliparity is a cause or consequence is bidirectional: endometriosis can impair fertility through distorted pelvic anatomy, inflammation, and altered endometrial receptivity, while lower parity (and therefore fewer pregnancies) increases lifetime menstrual exposure and may promote disease initiation or progression. Women with a history of infertility—particularly unexplained infertility—have higher prevalence of endometriosis on laparoscopy.

Fertility treatment history
Assisted reproductive technologies and hormonal ovulation induction do not appear to increase the underlying risk of endometriosis (which usually precedes fertility treatments), but they can unmask previously undiagnosed disease as women undergo more intensive evaluation. For fibroids, size and location (submucosal or large intramural) are important determinants of fertility outcomes and may influence decisions regarding myomectomy before infertility treatment.

# Patterns of disease presentation in reproductive-aged vs older women

Temporal patterns of symptomatology and clinical features change across reproductive life stages for both disorders.

Uterine leiomyomas
Reproductive-aged women: Fibroids most commonly present in women in their 30s and 40s. Typical complaints include heavy menstrual bleeding (menorrhagia), prolonged or irregular menses, pelvic pressure or fullness, urinary frequency from bladder compression, constipation from rectal compression, dyspareunia, and infertility or recurrent pregnancy loss if fibroids distort the uterine cavity. Acute pelvic pain can occur with red degeneration in pregnancy or torsion of a pedunculated subserosal fibroid, but this is less common.

Perimenopausal and postmenopausal women: Fibroids tend to regress after menopause due to decreased ovarian estrogen production. New or rapidly enlarging fibroids in a postmenopausal woman are uncommon and raise concern for malignancy (e.g., leiomyosarcoma) or hormone exposure (e.g., unopposed estrogen therapy). Management priorities shift toward symptomatic control and evaluation for alternative diagnoses.

Adnexal endometriomas and endometriosis
Reproductive-aged women: Endometriosis is classically associated with dysmenorrhea (pain with menses), chronic pelvic pain (often cyclical), dyspareunia (particularly deep dyspareunia), and infertility. Symptoms often begin in adolescence or early adulthood; progressive pain and worsening dysmenorrhea are common presenting features. On pelvic examination, fixed retroverted uterus or adnexal tenderness may be present. Endometriomas may produce a palpable adnexal mass if large.

Older women: Endometriosis generally becomes less active after menopause, with symptomatic improvement for many, as estrogen levels decline. However, some postmenopausal women—especially those on estrogen replacement without progestin or with exogenous estrogen—may have persistent or recurrent disease. New-onset endometriosis in postmenopausal women is rare and warrants evaluation for estrogen exposure or malignant transformation of an endometrioma, although malignant transformation is uncommon.

Overlapping presentations and differential diagnosis
Both conditions can present with pelvic or abdominal pain and can coexist in the same patient. Distinguishing features: heavy menstrual bleeding and bulk symptoms favor fibroids; cyclical pelvic pain, dysmenorrhea, and infertility favor endometriosis. Imaging helps segregate the differential—uterine fibroids are readily detected by transvaginal ultrasound, whereas small peritoneal implants may be occult on imaging. Clinical judgment must integrate age, symptom pattern, parity, and ethnic background.

# Implications of epidemiology for pretest probability in differential diagnosis

Epidemiologic data should inform the clinician’s prior (pretest) probability when evaluating symptoms. Bayesian reasoning helps determine how much weight to place on history, physical exam, and diagnostic tests.

Using age and reproductive stage
For a reproductive-aged woman with heavy menstrual bleeding and pelvic mass, fibroids are common and should be high on the differential—particularly in Black women and those in their 30s–40s. In contrast, a young adolescent girl with progressive dysmenorrhea and cyclic pelvic pain is more likely to have endometriosis than fibroids. In postmenopausal patients, the pretest probability for new or symptomatic fibroids or active endometriosis is low, and alternative diagnoses (including malignancy for masses) must be considered.

Accounting for ethnicity and family history
For Black women presenting with abnormal uterine bleeding or pelvic pressure, the pretest probability for fibroids is substantially higher than for women of other ethnicities; this should lower the threshold for uterine imaging (transvaginal ultrasound) as a first-line test. A positive family history increases prior probability for both conditions and can justify earlier or more aggressive diagnostic evaluation.

Incorporating parity and fertility history
Nulliparous women with infertility and pelvic pain have a higher pretest probability for endometriosis; if infertility is the dominant issue, laparoscopy or detailed pelvic imaging for endometriomas and pelvic adhesions may be prioritized. Conversely, multiparous women with new bulk symptoms are less likely to have disease onset related to endometriosis, and fibroids may be the leading consideration.

Interpreting diagnostic tests in context
Imaging and laboratory tests must be interpreted against the background pretest probability. For example, a transvaginal ultrasound that identifies a submucosal fibroid in a woman with heavy bleeding and consistent demographic risk factors usually confirms the clinical diagnosis. However, a negative ultrasound does not exclude endometriosis because many implants are not visible; in a patient with high clinical suspicion (dysmenorrhea, dyspareunia, infertility), a normal ultrasound should not be used to rule out endometriosis. Surgical visualization (laparoscopy) remains the diagnostic gold standard for peritoneal endometriosis, while MRI may have greater sensitivity for deep infiltrating disease and complex adnexal endometriomas.

Quantifying pretest probabilities in practice
While precise numeric pretest probabilities vary by setting, clinicians can apply epidemiologic ranges to inform decision thresholds. Examples:

- In an asymptomatic reproductive-aged woman, the chance of occult fibroids on imaging is substantial (tens of percent), but the probability of clinically significant fibroids is lower (~20–30% lifetime clinical significance).
- In a woman with chronic pelvic pain and infertility, the probability of endometriosis may be as high as 20–50% and even higher in surgical cohorts; the presence of an ovarian "ground-glass" cyst on ultrasound increases the likelihood of endometrioma substantially.
- A Black woman in her 40s with heavy menstrual bleeding has a high pretest probability for fibroids; imaging is indicated early in evaluation.

Clinical decision points based on epidemiology
Epidemiology guides when to pursue which tests and referrals:

- Transvaginal ultrasound should be first-line imaging for suspected fibroids in symptomatic women because of high prevalence and good sensitivity for uterine lesions.
- In women with suspected endometriosis who have infertility, persistent cyclical pain, or complex adnexal masses, early gynecologic referral and consideration of laparoscopy or pelvic MRI are warranted given the higher pretest probability and potential impact on fertility management.
- In populations with higher baseline risk (e.g., Black women for fibroids), clinicians should proactively discuss symptom surveillance and earlier intervention when symptoms emerge.

Conclusion

Epidemiologic patterns for uterine leiomyomas and endometriosis—age distribution, ethnicity, parity effects, and known risk factors—provide a framework for clinical reasoning. Both diseases are common in reproductive-aged women but differ in typical age of onset, symptom clusters, and associations with demographic and reproductive factors. Integrating these population-level data with individual history, examination, and targeted testing refines pretest probabilities, helps prioritize appropriate diagnostics, and guides counseling about likely etiologies and implications for fertility and long-term management.

Introduction

Pregnancy presents a unique clinical context in which two biologically and ethically linked patients — the pregnant person and the fetus — must be considered simultaneously. Managing complex medical or surgical conditions in pregnancy therefore demands not only excellent clinical knowledge, but structured teamwork, clear communication, careful risk–benefit analysis, and ethically robust decision-making. This chapter outlines practical frameworks for multidisciplinary care, approaches to shared decision-making, documentation and informed consent that explicitly incorporate maternal and fetal risks, strategies for balancing maternal autonomy with fetal considerations, methods for applying clinical guidelines and appraising evidence in rare scenarios, and principles for follow-up coordination and contingency planning. The aim is to equip trainees with the conceptual tools and actionable steps necessary to lead or participate effectively in care of pregnant patients with complex needs.

# Composition and roles of multidisciplinary teams: obstetrics, gynaecology, surgery, radiology, anaesthesia, neonatology

Multidisciplinary care begins with assembling the right team and clarifying roles. The specific composition will vary by clinical problem, gestational age, acuity, and available resources, but commonly involved specialties include maternal–fetal medicine (MFM) or obstetrics, gynaecology, general surgery or subspecialty surgical teams, radiology (diagnostic and interventional), anaesthesia, neonatology, critical care, and allied health professionals (nursing, midwifery, pharmacy, social work, and ethics). Clear role definition reduces duplication, ambiguity, and delays in emergency situations.

Obstetrics/Maternal–Fetal Medicine: The obstetric team coordinates pregnancy-specific care, monitors fetal well-being, and advises on timing of delivery. MFM specialists provide expertise in high-risk pregnancy management, fetal surveillance (nonstress tests, biophysical profiles), and interpretation of gestational-age–specific risks. In many cases MFM functions as the clinical lead for the pregnancy and liaison between specialties.

Gynaecology/Surgical teams: For non-obstetric surgical conditions (appendicitis, biliary disease, adnexal pathology) or obstetric surgeries (cesarean delivery, uterine-sparing procedures), gynaecologists and surgical subspecialists determine operative indication, technique, and anticipated impact on pregnancy. They assess feasibility of minimally invasive vs open approaches and discuss perioperative anticoagulation and infection prophylaxis in pregnancy.

General Surgery/Subspecialty Surgery: When a pregnant patient requires general surgical care (e.g., appendectomy, cholecystectomy, trauma laparotomy), a general surgeon assumes responsibility for the surgical condition, working with obstetrics for fetal monitoring and delivery planning if needed. Subspecialists (vascular, neurosurgery, thoracic) are engaged for condition-specific expertise.

Radiology and Interventional Radiology: Radiologists advise on diagnostic imaging choices that balance diagnostic yield against fetal exposure. Interventional radiology may provide temporizing measures (e.g., embolization for postpartum hemorrhage or trauma) that can avoid or delay major surgery. Radiology input should include estimated fetal radiation dose and alternative modalities (ultrasound, MRI).

Anaesthesia: Anaesthetists evaluate perioperative maternal physiology, provide recommendations on anaesthetic technique that minimize fetal hypoxia/uteroplacental compromise, plan airway management (pregnancy increases aspiration risk and difficult airway likelihood), and manage analgesia and thromboprophylaxis. They coordinate with neonatology for anticipated neonatal resuscitation if preterm or emergent delivery is possible.

Neonatology and Paediatrics: Neonatologists advise about neonatal outcomes by gestational age, requirements for resuscitation, neonatal intensive care capacity, and counseling parents about prematurity risks when delivery is a possible outcome of maternal interventions.

Allied Health and Ancillary Services: Midwives and specialized nursing staff ensure bedside fetal monitoring and postpartum care; pharmacists review medication safety in pregnancy and lactation; social workers and mental health professionals support psychosocial decision-making; ethics consultants may be engaged for conflicts or high-stakes situations. Quality improvement and case management staff can facilitate coordination and follow-up.

Team function depends on clear leadership, regular communication (structured huddles, case conferences), and explicit documentation of responsibilities. The team should establish a single point of contact for the patient and a clear plan for escalation in emergencies. In urgent situations, predefined protocols that identify who makes time-sensitive decisions (e.g., obstetrics for immediate fetal resuscitation, surgeon for life-saving surgery) prevent delays.

# Shared decision-making principles and communication of uncertainty

Shared decision-making (SDM) is a collaborative process in which clinicians and patients exchange information, deliberate about options, and make a care plan that aligns with the patient’s values and preferences. SDM is particularly important in pregnancy because interventions may pose trade-offs between maternal benefit and fetal risk, and because decisions often occur under uncertainty.

Principles of SDM include: (1) acknowledge that a decision is required; (2) explain clinically reasonable options (including watchful waiting) and their likely outcomes; (3) describe uncertainties, probabilities, and potential trade-offs in plain language; (4) elicit the patient’s values, goals, and risk tolerance; and (5) arrive at a mutually acceptable plan, documenting the discussion and the patient’s preferences. Decision aids (booklets, pictographs, web tools) can help convey numerical risks and clarify options, and these are often underutilized in perinatal care.

Communicating uncertainty is a core skill. Clinicians should distinguish between well-quantified risks (e.g., frequency of a known complication) and uncertainties due to limited evidence (e.g., rare exposures in pregnancy). Use absolute risks rather than relative risks when possible. For example, instead of saying “this doubles the risk,” state baseline and new absolute risks: “If 1 in 1,000 pregnant people experience outcome X, this exposure increases it to 2 in 1,000.” When high-quality numerical estimates are unavailable, explain the nature of uncertainty: sample size limitations, lack of pregnancy-specific data, or variability in outcomes by gestational age. Offer best-available analogies (e.g., data from non-pregnant adults or animal studies) but be explicit about the limitations.

Culturally sensitive communication and attention to health literacy are essential. Use simple language, avoid medical jargon, pause to allow questions, and employ teach-back to confirm understanding. When language barriers exist, use professional interpreters. Document the patient’s expressed values (e.g., prioritizing maternal survival over fetal outcomes, desire to avoid preterm delivery) so that future team members can honor these preferences.

In time-sensitive settings, SDM still applies but must be streamlined: present the most relevant options, focus on immediate risks and benefits, and obtain assent when full deliberation is impossible, explaining that the decision may be revisited. In emergencies where the patient lacks capacity, predefined advance directives or known preferences should guide care, and proxies or ethics consultation may be necessary.

# Documentation of informed consent with maternal and fetal risk discussion

Informed consent in pregnancy must explicitly address risks and benefits to both the pregnant person and the fetus (when relevant). Good documentation is both a clinical and legal safeguard; it should be specific, contemporaneous, and accessible to all team members.

Elements of a comprehensive consent discussion include: diagnosis and proposed intervention; alternatives (including no treatment and temporizing measures); expected benefits; material risks to mother and fetus; likelihoods of important outcomes (numerical probabilities when possible); anticipated effects on pregnancy course and delivery timing; peri-procedural fetal monitoring plans; contingency plans if fetal compromise occurs; implications for breastfeeding and postpartum recovery; and the opportunity to ask questions and change one’s mind. Document who participated in the conversation (patient, partner, interpreter), that the patient had adequate time to consider options, and whether any materials or decision aids were used.

When recording risks, avoid vague phrases such as “low risk” without context. For example: “There is an approximately 1–2% risk of preterm labor requiring neonatal intensive care due to this procedure” is preferable. If numerical estimates are uncertain, state the best available estimate and its limits: “Based on limited observational data, the risk of major congenital anomaly from this exposure is estimated to be less than 1%, but data are sparse.”

Specific documentation recommendations:

- Use a structured consent form that includes a section for fetal risk discussion.
- Record the rationale for recommended timing of intervention relative to gestational age (e.g., delaying elective surgery until second trimester reduces miscarriage risk).
- Note discussions about alternatives and their risks (e.g., medical vs surgical therapy).
- If the patient declines recommended therapy, document the counseling provided, the patient’s decision, and the plan for follow-up and safety-net instructions.
- For urgent interventions where written consent cannot be obtained, document the clinical indications for proceeding, attempts to obtain consent, who authorized the intervention, and the reasons delaying consent was unsafe.

Consent is an ongoing process. Revisit and re-document consent when the clinical course changes or when interventions are deferred and reconsidered.

# Balancing maternal autonomy with fetal considerations

Ethical care in pregnancy must respect maternal autonomy while recognizing potential obligations to the fetus; these duties can sometimes appear to conflict. Modern ethical frameworks prioritize the pregnant person’s autonomy as the primary decision-maker for interventions affecting her body, with fetal considerations informing, but not overriding, maternal choices except in exceptional legal contexts.

Core ethical principles to apply are autonomy, beneficence, nonmaleficence, and justice. Maternal autonomy means that competent pregnant persons have the right to accept or refuse recommended interventions, even when refusal may increase fetal risk. Clinicians should explore underlying reasons for refusal—fear of fetal harm, cultural beliefs, misunderstanding—and provide information and support, but coercion or threats of legal action are ethically problematic and generally counterproductive. Instead, seek to reach a mutually acceptable plan through respectful dialogue and clear information sharing.

There are limited situations where conflicts become acute: for example, when a pregnant person refuses a life-saving intervention that would likely also improve fetal outcomes, or when medical teams believe that immediate delivery is required to save either mother or fetus. In most jurisdictions the legal authority to compel treatment is constrained, and clinicians should involve ethics consultation and legal counsel only when necessary. Prioritize de-escalation, continued engagement, and shared decision-making, documenting attempts to reach agreement.

Best practices for balancing maternal and fetal interests:

- Center informed consent on maternal values and autonomous choice, while transparently communicating how maternal decisions affect fetal outcomes.
- Use nonjudgmental, supportive language; attempt to clarify misunderstandings and provide incremental steps (e.g., small tests or temporizing measures).
- Engage multidisciplinary support (social work, psychiatry, chaplaincy, cultural mediators) for complex refusals.
- In emergencies where the patient lacks capacity, make decisions based on previously expressed wishes or, if unknown, in the patient’s best interest; fetal interests can be considered but do not automatically override maternal best interests.
- Document the reasoning, the patient’s stated values, and the team’s efforts to facilitate informed, voluntary decisions.

Ultimately, preserving trust in the therapeutic relationship is often more important for both maternal and fetal outcomes than adversarial approaches.

# Use of clinical guidelines and evidence appraisal in rare clinical scenarios

Clinical guidelines are indispensable for standardizing care, but pregnancy is often excluded from randomized controlled trials, and evidence for rare scenarios can be sparse. Clinicians must therefore be adept at applying guidelines while critically appraising the underlying evidence and adapting recommendations to pregnancy-specific physiology and fetal considerations.

When using guidelines, consider their scope, the population studied, and whether pregnancy-specific recommendations exist. For example, clinical practice guidelines (CPGs) often classify recommendations by strength (e.g., strong vs conditional) and by evidence quality (high, moderate, low, very low). In applying a guideline to a pregnant patient, ask: were pregnant persons included in the evidence base? Are there known physiologic or pharmacokinetic differences in pregnancy that alter benefit–harm trade-offs?

Evidence appraisal steps in rare scenarios:

1. Define the clinical question using a structured format (e.g., PICO: Patient/Problem, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome).
2. Seek the best available evidence: systematic reviews, registry data, observational studies, and case series may be the only sources for rare conditions in pregnancy. Check pregnancy-specific resources (teratology information services, pregnancy registries, specialty society guidelines).
3. Evaluate study limitations: small sample sizes, confounding in observational studies, publication bias, and indirectness of evidence (e.g., extrapolation from non-pregnant adults or animal data).
4. Translate evidence into practice by integrating physiologic changes of pregnancy (increased plasma volume, altered drug clearance, hypercoagulable state), timing-dependent fetal vulnerability (e.g., organogenesis in weeks 2–10), and gestational-age–dependent neonatal risk.
5. Use consensus and multidisciplinary expert opinion when high-quality evidence is absent. Document the rationale for deviating from standard non-pregnant guidelines.

In rare or high-stakes cases, consider rapid guideline adaptation methods: convene a concise multidisciplinary panel, perform targeted literature review, and produce a clear, time-limited recommendation for the local context. Where available, consult national teratology information services or registries that track exposures and outcomes.

Always communicate the uncertainty to the patient and document how recommendations were derived and the level of supporting evidence. Where interventions are novel or off-label in pregnancy, document that the decision involved extrapolation and the plan for data collection and follow-up, whenever feasible.

# Strategies for follow-up coordination and contingency planning

Complex pregnancies require carefully orchestrated follow-up and explicit contingency plans. Coordination must ensure continuity across disciplines, clarity about responsible clinicians, and rapid access to care when deterioration occurs.

Key elements of follow-up coordination:

- Designate a case coordinator or primary contact (often an MFM specialist, obstetrician, or nurse case manager) who maintains an up-to-date care plan accessible to all team members and the patient.
- Provide a written and verbal summary for the patient that outlines the diagnosis, agreed plan, warning signs, appointment schedule, medication instructions, and emergency contact numbers.
- Schedule follow-up visits and investigations proactively, with appointments aligned to gestational milestones (e.g., fetal surveillance intervals, timing of definitive surgery to optimize gestational age).
- Ensure interoperability of medical records or create a succinct shared “care snapshot” that succinctly documents key dates, medication lists, allergies, and contingency triggers to be posted in the record for emergency clinicians.
- Use reminders and outreach (telephone, text messages, home visits when needed) to reduce missed appointments, which is particularly important in high-risk pregnancies.

Contingency and escalation planning should include foreseeable complications and predetermined actions. For each major decision node (e.g., clinical deterioration, signs of preterm labor, hemorrhage, or fetal distress), define:

- Immediate actions (who to call, where to go, what monitoring to perform).
- Thresholds for escalation (e.g., tachycardia >120 beats/min despite fluid resuscitation, contractions leading to cervical change at <34 weeks).
- Anticipatory medication plans (e.g., steroids for fetal lung maturation when preterm delivery is likely between 24 and 34 weeks; typical regimen: betamethasone 12 mg IM q24h × 2 or dexamethasone 6 mg IM q12h × 4).
- Blood product and massive transfusion readiness for hemorrhage-prone conditions, with prenatal crossmatch ordering if surgery is anticipated.
- Neonatal care plan and NICU activation criteria based on gestational age and predicted neonatal needs.

Safety-netting instructions must be explicit and actionable: for example, “If you experience regular contractions every 10 minutes or less than 34 weeks, contact labor and delivery immediately.” Provide thresholds for fetal concerns (reduced fetal movements, loss of baseline variability on home monitoring if used) and maternal red flags (fever above 38.0°C, vaginal bleeding, severe abdominal pain, syncope).

Regular review and revision of the contingency plan is necessary as the pregnancy evolves. After each major clinical encounter, update the plan and ensure the patient and team members receive the changes. For patients at high risk of rapid deterioration, consider establishing a pre-emptive plan for in-hospital monitoring at certain gestational ages to permit timely intervention.

Conclusion

Managing complex conditions in pregnancy requires coordinated multidisciplinary teams, robust shared decision-making that acknowledges uncertainty, meticulous documentation of informed consent including maternal and fetal risks, respect for maternal autonomy balanced with fetal considerations, judicious use of and adaptation of clinical guidelines in the face of sparse evidence, and proactive follow-up with clear contingency plans. Clinicians should strive to create transparent, patient-centered processes that enable safe, ethical, and evidence-informed care tailored to the needs of both the pregnant person and the fetus.
