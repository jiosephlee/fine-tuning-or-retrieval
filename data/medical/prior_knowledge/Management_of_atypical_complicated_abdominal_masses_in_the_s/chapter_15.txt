Introduction

Uterine and adnexal masses encompass a broad spectrum of benign and malignant lesions whose accurate histopathologic classification is essential for guiding clinical management. Gross and microscopic examination provides the definitive diagnosis in many cases, distinguishes indolent from aggressive neoplasms, and explains radiologic appearances and clinical behavior. This chapter reviews the histopathology of common uterine and adnexal masses encountered in surgical specimens, emphasizing the diagnostic criteria used to separate benign smooth muscle tumors from sarcomas, the histologic correlates of degenerative change, the defining microscopic features of principal ovarian cyst types, and the practical role of frozen section and cytology in intraoperative decision-making. Where relevant, gross–microscopic–imaging correlations are highlighted to reinforce clinical reasoning.

# Histologic criteria for leiomyoma: smooth muscle bundles, collagen deposition

Leiomyomas (uterine fibroids) are benign smooth muscle neoplasms composed of well-differentiated smooth muscle cells arranged in whorled bundles. The canonical microscopic appearance is monotonous spindle-shaped cells with eosinophilic fibrillary cytoplasm, elongated “cigar-shaped” nuclei with blunt ends, and minimal nuclear pleomorphism. Cells are arranged in intersecting fascicles or swirling (whorled) patterns, often forming nodules separated by well-circumscribed capsules or compressed myometrium.

Between the smooth muscle bundles there is frequently variable extracellular matrix. Collagen deposition is common and may appear as pink, acellular bands or sheets on hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) sections. Some leiomyomas are collagen-rich (also termed cellular variants with increased collagen or “hyalinized” types), where smooth muscle cells are sparser and collagen predominates; others are cellular leiomyomas with dense cellularity and scant collagen. Mitotic activity is usually low, commonly quoted as less than 5 mitoses per 10 high-power fields (HPF), and nuclei lack atypia. The presence of significant nuclear atypia, increased mitotic rate, or coagulative tumor cell necrosis should prompt evaluation for malignancy.

Additional features sometimes seen in leiomyomas include vascular changes within the tumor (thick-walled hyalinized vessels), peripheral pseudocapsule formed by compressed adjacent myometrium, and entrapped benign endometrial glands in submucosal or intramural tumors. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) supports smooth muscle differentiation: tumor cells are typically positive for smooth muscle actin (SMA), desmin, and H-caldesmon, while being negative for markers of endometrial stromal or epithelial differentiation (e.g., CD10 negative). These IHC markers are most useful when morphology is ambiguous.

# Pathologic patterns of degeneration (ischaemic, hyaline, cystic, red) and their microscopic features

Leiomyomas frequently undergo degenerative changes as they outgrow their blood supply or are exposed to physiologic changes such as pregnancy. Recognizing the histologic patterns of degeneration is important so they are not misinterpreted as malignancy or infection.

Hyaline degeneration is the most common pattern. Grossly this appears as homogeneous, glassy, whitish areas. Microscopically, hyaline degeneration is characterized by the replacement of smooth muscle cells by eosinophilic, acellular, hyaline proteinaceous material that may be arranged as amorphous acellular zones between residual muscle bundles. The remaining tumor cells become attenuated and are often surrounded by hyalinized stroma. Hyaline change can be focal or diffuse and may be associated with calcification.

Cystic degeneration represents more extensive liquefactive change of a hyalinized leiomyoma and appears grossly as cystic spaces filled with serous or proteinaceous fluid. Microscopically, dilated spaces lined by flattened or denuded mesothelial-like cells can be seen; the surrounding stroma shows variable fibrosis and hemosiderin if prior hemorrhage occurred. Cystic change can mimic an ovarian cyst on imaging if subserosal and large.

Red (carneous) degeneration is typically seen during pregnancy and is due to acute hemorrhagic infarction resulting from venous thrombosis in the periphery of the tumor. Grossly, areas are dark red or brown. Microscopically, there is coagulative necrosis with hemorrhage, degenerated smooth muscle fibers, and variable inflammatory infiltrate including macrophages and neutrophils. Unlike coagulative tumor cell necrosis seen in malignant sarcomas, red degeneration usually shows hemorrhagic infarction with preservation of cell outlines in early phases and lacks the classic zonal eosinophilic necrosis with ghost outlines of cells that characterizes malignant necrosis.

Ischemic degeneration (often used interchangeably with red degeneration but broader) and infarct-type necrosis can occur in pedunculated or torsed tumors. Microscopy shows areas of ischemic change with ghost cells, karyorrhexis, and infiltration by inflammatory cells. Distinguishing ischemic/coagulative infarction from coagulative tumor cell necrosis (see below) may require correlation with clinical history (e.g., pregnancy, torsion) and review of the interface between viable tumor and necrosis: coagulative tumor cell necrosis displays abrupt transition from viable tumor to necrotic zone with cell ghosts and nuclear debris, and is more suggestive of malignancy when paired with atypia and mitoses.

Additional degenerative processes include myxoid degeneration, in which abundant myxoid extracellular matrix (pale basophilic mucinous material) separates smooth muscle bundles, and fatty change or calcification. Recognizing these patterns reduces misclassification of benign atypical changes as sarcoma.

# Features distinguishing benign leiomyoma from leiomyosarcoma and other sarcomas

Differentiating benign leiomyoma from leiomyosarcoma (LMS) and other uterine sarcomas is one of the most important tasks in gynecologic pathology because management and prognosis differ dramatically. The classic diagnostic triad for leiomyosarcoma comprises significant cytologic atypia, high mitotic activity, and coagulative tumor cell necrosis. Various sets of criteria exist, but commonly used thresholds and patterns are:

- Mitotic index: A commonly cited cutoff for concern is ≥10 mitoses per 10 high-power fields (HPF) for outright diagnosis of LMS in many institutions, though some diagnosticians consider lower counts (e.g., >5/10 HPF) with significant atypia and necrosis to be suspicious. It is imperative to evaluate mitoses in well-fixed, representative tumor areas and to avoid counting apoptotic figures.
- Cytologic atypia: Moderate to severe nuclear atypia characterized by marked pleomorphism, hyperchromasia, prominent nucleoli, and irregular nuclear contours supports malignancy. Scattered atypical cells alone in a largely bland tumor do not define LMS.
- Coagulative tumor cell necrosis (CTCN): CTCN shows abrupt transition from viable tumor to necrotic areas with preserved cellular outlines (ghost cells) and nuclear karyorrhexis. This type of necrosis is more specific for malignancy than hyaline/ischemic infarction, which tends to be associated with inflammation and gradual transition zones.

Tumors that do not meet all three criteria but raise concern are categorized as smooth muscle tumor of uncertain malignant potential (STUMP). Characteristics prompting a STUMP diagnosis include moderate atypia with low mitotic rate but focal necrosis, or high mitotic activity without atypia, or other unusual combinations. STUMP requires cautious clinical follow-up because behavior is unpredictable.

Other uterine sarcomas include:

- Endometrial stromal sarcoma (ESS): Typically composed of small, uniform cells resembling proliferative-phase endometrial stroma with a characteristic infiltrative, tongue-like pattern into myometrium and often lymphovascular invasion. Low-grade ESS shows limited atypia and mitotic activity but frequently harbors JAZF1-SUZ12 rearrangements; high-grade ESS demonstrates marked atypia and higher mitotic rates and may have YWHAE-NUTM2 fusions. Immunophenotype includes CD10 positivity and variable ER/PR.
- Undifferentiated uterine sarcoma: Highly pleomorphic, high mitotic activity, aggressive behavior, and lack of specific differentiation or molecular markers; a diagnosis of exclusion.
- Carcinosarcoma (malignant mixed Müllerian tumor): Biphasic neoplasm with malignant epithelial (carcinomatous) and malignant mesenchymal (sarcomatous) components. Historically treated as sarcoma but molecular and clinical behavior align more with high-grade carcinoma; cytokeratin and EMA highlight the epithelial component.

IHC and molecular tools can assist. H-caldesmon and desmin favor smooth muscle differentiation (leiomyoma/LMS) over endometrial stromal tumors, which are CD10 positive. Ki-67 proliferation index is higher in LMS than leiomyoma but no single cutoff is universally accepted. Molecular cytogenetic alterations (e.g., MED12 mutations in a sizable subset of leiomyomas) can support benignity, whereas complex karyotypes are more common in sarcomas.

Accurate sampling is crucial: leiomyomas can have focal atypia or increased mitoses that do not reflect overall malignancy; conversely, LMS can be focal within a larger mass. Pathologists typically sample extensively from tumors with worrisome gross features (areas of hemorrhage, necrosis, softening) to avoid underdiagnosis.

# Ovarian cyst histology: endometrioma, dermoid, serous and mucinous tumors

Ovarian cysts arise from various tissues and display distinct histologic architectures.

Endometrioma (endometriotic cyst)
Endometriomas are cysts formed by ectopic endometrial glands and stroma within the ovary, often containing altered blood products (“chocolate cysts”). Microscopically, the cyst lining shows endometrial-type glandular epithelium, which may be atrophic or proliferative, accompanied by endometrial-type stroma. Hemosiderin-laden macrophages and blood breakdown products are typical in the cyst contents and within the wall. Variable fibrosis occurs in the ovarian cortex, and foci of smooth muscle metaplasia may be present. Endometriotic implants can undergo malignant transformation (rarely) most commonly to endometrioid or clear cell carcinoma; identification of atypical hyperplasia or carcinoma in endometriosis warrants careful sampling.

Mature cystic teratoma (dermoid)
Mature cystic teratomas are germ cell tumors containing tissues derived from more than one germ layer, most commonly ectodermal derivatives. Grossly they are unilocular cysts containing sebaceous material and hair. Microscopically, the lining is often stratified squamous epithelium with underlying adnexal structures (hair follicles, sebaceous glands). Other mature tissues—respiratory epithelium, cartilage, bone, thyroid tissue, neural elements—may be present. Immature teratomas contain immature neuroepithelium and have malignant potential. Squamous carcinoma arising within a dermoid is the most common malignant transformation; presence of invasive atypical epithelial components requires adequate sampling to detect.

Serous tumors
Serous ovarian tumors form a spectrum from benign serous cystadenomas to borderline (atypical proliferative) serous tumors and serous carcinomas. Benign serous cystadenomas are cystic, lined by a single layer of columnar, often ciliated epithelial cells resembling fallopian tube epithelium, and show no stromal invasion. Borderline serous tumors exhibit epithelial proliferation with hierarchical branching papillae, epithelial stratification, mild-to-moderate cytologic atypia, but lack stromal invasion. Microinvasion or stromal invasion converts the lesion to low-grade serous carcinoma in certain contexts. High-grade serous carcinomas show markedly pleomorphic cells, high mitotic index, necrosis, and complex papillary and solid architecture; they often show diffuse p53 aberrant staining on IHC and are associated with TP53 mutations and BRCA alterations.

Psammoma bodies (laminated calcifications) are common in serous tumors, especially serous borderline and low-grade carcinomas. Immunophenotype includes WT1 positivity in serous lineage tumors.

Mucinous tumors
Mucinous cystadenomas are multilocular cysts lined by tall columnar mucin-secreting epithelium resembling endocervical or intestinal epithelium. Benign mucinous tumors have a single epithelial layer, while borderline tumors display epithelial proliferation and stratification without stromal invasion. Mucinous carcinomas show complex architecture with confluent glandular/epithelial proliferation and stromal invasion. Mucinous tumors can be enormous and sometimes represent metastases from the gastrointestinal tract; distinguishing primary ovarian from metastatic mucinous tumors relies on laterality (primary often unilateral), size (primary larger), complex architecture, and clinical correlation, sometimes aided by immunoprofile (CK7/CK20, CDX2).

In all ovarian tumors, adequate sampling is essential because borderline or malignant areas can be focal. Intraoperative assessment may be limited by sampling error, particularly in large mucinous tumors.

# Role of frozen section and cytology in intraoperative decision-making

Frozen section and intraoperative cytology provide rapid pathologic assessment to guide surgical management, especially decisions about extent of resection (conservative versus radical surgery) and staging procedures.

Frozen section is useful when deciding whether an adnexal mass that is resected at the time of laparotomy or laparoscopy is benign, borderline, or malignant. Its accuracy is high for distinguishing benign versus malignant lesions in many settings (sensitivity and specificity often >90%), but performance is lower for certain tumor types:

- High accuracy: Typical benign cysts (serous cystadenoma, dermoid), high-grade serous carcinoma.
- Lower accuracy: Mucinous tumors (due to heterogeneity and huge size), borderline tumors where subtle epithelial proliferation may be missed, and lesions with focal malignant transformation in a teratoma.

Limitations of frozen section include freezing artifacts that can obscure nuclear detail, sampling error (especially in large, multilocular mucinous masses), and interpretive difficulty in STUMP or borderline lesions. When frozen section yields “benign,” but clinical or imaging suspicion is high, some surgeons may choose to stage or to stage at a later definitive surgery. Conversely, a frozen section diagnosis of malignancy typically prompts comprehensive staging (total hysterectomy, bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy, omentectomy, lymph node sampling) in postmenopausal patients; in younger patients wishing fertility preservation, the decision must balance frozen section reliability and clinical risk.

Intraoperative cytology (peritoneal washings/ascitic fluid cytology, scrape cytology) supplements frozen section by detecting free malignant cells in the peritoneal cavity, which has staging implications. Positive peritoneal cytology historically upstaged certain ovarian and uterine cancers, although the staging systems and their significance have evolved. Cytology is rapid and sensitive for surface-spreading carcinomas but less useful for localized stromal tumors.

Best practice combines careful preoperative imaging, tumor markers (e.g., CA-125; normal range <35 U/mL), and intraoperative frozen section with an understanding of limitations. Communication between surgeon and pathologist about the clinical scenario and radiologic features improves decision-making.

# Correlation of gross pathology to imaging findings

Bridging gross pathological observations with imaging findings enhances interpretation and clinical decisions. Several consistent correlations between gross/microscopic pathology and radiologic appearances exist:

Leiomyomas:
- Hyaline degeneration: On MRI, hyalinized fibroids are typically low signal on both T1- and T2-weighted images (T2 dark) due to dense collagen and decreased water content. Grossly they are firm and pale.
- Cystic degeneration: Appears as high T2 signal intensity areas corresponding to fluid-filled cystic spaces within the mass; grossly cystic and soft.
- Red degeneration: On MRI, acute hemorrhagic/red degeneration shows high signal on T1-weighted images due to methemoglobin and variable T2 signal (so-called T1 hyperintense, T2 variable). Clinically associated with acute pain, most commonly in pregnancy.
- Calcified fibroids: Seen as echogenic foci with shadowing on ultrasound and signal voids on MRI; grossly gritty with calcification.
- Cellular leiomyomas: May show higher T2 signal than typical fibroids due to higher cellularity and less dense collagen.

Ovarian masses:
- Endometrioma: Classically shows “T2 shading” on MRI—progressive loss of T2 signal due to concentrated blood products (old hemorrhage) and high signal on T1-weighted images because of blood products. On ultrasound, endometriomas are often homogeneous, low-level internal echoes (“ground glass” echogenicity). Pathologically, these correspond to blood-filled cysts lined by endometrial epithelium and hemosiderin-laden macrophages.
- Mature cystic teratoma (dermoid): Imaging may show fat-fluid levels, Rokitansky protuberance, and calcified components. On CT and MRI, intratumoral macroscopic fat is diagnostic. Grossly, sebaceous material, hair, and occasionally bone/teeth reflect these imaging characteristics.
- Serous tumors: Typically appear as unilocular or multilocular cystic masses with thin septations; papillary excrescences projecting into the cyst lumen on imaging raise suspicion for borderline or malignant serous tumors and correspond histologically to epithelial papillae. Psammoma bodies may cause punctate calcifications on imaging.
- Mucinous tumors: Often multilocular cystic masses with variable signal intensity on MRI corresponding to differences in mucin content; huge size and multilocularity may be suggestive of mucinous tumors. If septal nodules or solid enhancing components are present on imaging, malignancy should be suspected correlating with invasive epithelial growth on histology.

Ascites and peritoneal implants on imaging often indicate malignant ovarian tumors with peritoneal dissemination; on pathology these correspond to surface tumor implants, omental metastases, and positive cytology. Tumor markers assist but are not diagnostic alone: CA-125 is often elevated in epithelial ovarian carcinomas (normal <35 U/mL), but can also be raised in endometriosis, menstruation, and benign conditions.

Imaging guides specimen handling and intraoperative strategy: radiologic suspicion for malignancy should prompt thorough sampling and alert the pathologist to focus on detecting invasive foci. Conversely, recognition that certain radiologic features (e.g., fat in a dermoid, T1 hyperintensity in endometrioma) correlate with benign pathology can prevent over-treatment.

Closing remarks

Understanding the histopathology of uterine and adnexal masses is essential for accurate diagnosis and appropriate clinical management. Recognizing the architecture of leiomyomas, patterns of degeneration, and the diagnostic criteria that separate benign from malignant smooth muscle tumors prevents misclassification. For adnexal masses, familiarity with the distinctive histologic features of endometriomas, dermoids, and serous and mucinous epithelial tumors, combined with prudent use of frozen section and cytology, allows the surgeon and pathologist to make informed intraoperative decisions. Integrating gross pathology with imaging and clinical context completes the diagnostic picture and optimizes patient care.
