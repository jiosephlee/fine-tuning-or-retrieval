# Introduction

Uterine leiomyomas (fibroids) are monoclonal smooth muscle neoplasms of the myometrium that are common across reproductive age. Although many fibroids are asymptomatic, a significant subset undergo degenerative changes or complications that can produce acute or subacute clinical syndromes. Understanding the pathophysiology of fibroid degeneration, vascular compromise, torsion, infection, and mass-effect complications is essential for diagnosing and managing patients—especially during pregnancy, when fibroid biology and risk profiles differ. This chapter summarizes the mechanisms that underlie various types of fibroid degeneration, the vascular and inflammatory sequelae, the clinical features and diagnostic clues associated with torsion and secondary infection, and the potential for hemorrhage, organ compression, and systemic inflammatory responses. Emphasis is placed on linking histopathology and imaging correlates with clinical presentation and laboratory findings that guide decision-making.

# Types of degeneration (red, hyaline, cystic, myxoid) and pathologic correlates

Fibroid degeneration describes the spectrum of histologic and gross changes that occur when the internal environment of a leiomyoma is altered relative to its original well-vascularized, compact smooth-muscle architecture. Degeneration subtypes are classified by their predominant histologic pattern and typical clinical context.

Hyaline degeneration is the most common form and represents extracellular protein deposition and smooth muscle cell loss. Microscopically, there is homogenous, eosinophilic, acellular matrix deposition replacing muscle bundles; the process is thought to arise from chronic ischemia and impaired turnover of extracellular matrix. Grossly, hyalinized areas are pale, firm, and glassy. Hyaline degeneration usually causes gradual enlargement or firmness of the fibroid but rarely leads to acute pain or systemic inflammation.

Red degeneration (carneous degeneration) is most often described in pregnancy. It reflects acute venous thrombosis within the fibroid, followed by hemorrhagic infarction. Grossly the fibroid shows a red-brown, hemorrhagic cut surface; micropathology reveals coagulative necrosis with hemorrhage and variable inflammatory cell infiltration. Clinically, red degeneration often presents with acute localized abdominal pain, low-grade fever, and leukocytosis; pain can be persistent and poorly responsive to simple analgesia. The pathogenesis relates to rapid fibroid growth and altered uterine blood flow in pregnancy, leading to venous outflow obstruction and ischemic necrosis.

Cystic degeneration represents liquefaction of areas within a fibroid, typically following hyaline change. Microscopically there is loss of cellularity with cystic spaces filled by serous fluid. On imaging, cystic degeneration appears as anechoic or cystic areas within the mass on ultrasound, and as non-enhancing fluid collections on MRI. Cystic change can cause mass enlargement and produce compressive symptoms but usually lacks the intense inflammatory response seen in red degeneration.

Myxoid degeneration (also called myxomatous change) features accumulation of mucopolysaccharide-rich extracellular matrix between smooth muscle cells, giving a gelatinous appearance. Histology shows abundant basophilic mucoid material separating myocytes. Myxoid change can be focal or extensive; when extensive it may mimic sarcoma on imaging because of heterogeneous signal characteristics and rapid growth. Myxoid degeneration may predispose to cystic change or infection due to the altered extracellular milieu.

These degenerative patterns are not mutually exclusive; progressive ischemia often leads first to hyaline change, then cystic or myxoid transformation, and, if severe and acute, to red degeneration with hemorrhagic necrosis. Recognizing the expected histologic correlates helps interpret imaging and clinical signs.

# Vascular compromise leading to ischaemia and necrosis

Vascular compromise is central to most degenerative processes in fibroids. The blood supply of a fibroid derives from branches of uterine (and occasionally ovarian) arteries, but unlike the surrounding myometrium, fibroids often have relatively poor microvascular architecture and a propensity for venous stasis because of increased interstitial pressure and compressed intratumoral vessels as the mass enlarges.

Ischemia may be chronic or acute. Chronic suboptimal perfusion promotes hyaline and myxoid change through hypoxia-driven alterations in extracellular matrix turnover and cell death. Acute vascular events—venous thrombosis, arterial occlusion, or torsion of a pedicle—produce ischemic necrosis. Histologically, ischemia progresses from cell swelling and coagulative necrosis to infiltration by neutrophils and macrophages, eventual liquefaction, and organization. The time course of these events determines clinical presentation: chronic ischemia tends to cause gradual enlargement and nonspecific discomfort, while acute ischemia can produce severe localized pain, fever, and systemic inflammatory signs.

Pregnancy exacerbates vulnerability to vascular compromise. Increased uterine blood flow and rapid fibroid growth can outpace vascular development, and mechanical effects from uterine contractions or distortion of uterine architecture can cause venous outflow obstruction. Hormonal influences—especially elevated progesterone—may modify vascular tone and coagulation, increasing the likelihood of thrombosis within the fibroid. The physiologic hypercoagulable state of pregnancy further predisposes to intratumoral thrombotic events.

On imaging, vascular compromise may be suggested by absent or reduced internal flow on Doppler ultrasound, non-enhancement of portions of the mass on contrast MRI or CT, heterogeneous T2 signal with areas of hemorrhage, and peripheral rim enhancement if viable capsule remains. These features, combined with clinical signs, point to ischemic degeneration.

# Torsion of pedunculated subserosal fibroids: pathogenesis and clinical signs

Pedunculated subserosal fibroids project from the uterine serosal surface on a stalk (pedicle), which can undergo torsion. Torsion is a mechanical event in which rotation of the stalk impairs venous and arterial flow, producing acute ischemia of the fibroid. Pathogenesis is driven by several factors: a relatively long or narrow pedicle that permits rotation; increased uterine size or mobility (as in pregnancy); sudden changes in intra-abdominal pressure or body position; and adjacent pelvic organ movement.

The progression of vascular compromise after torsion commonly follows a venous-outflow obstruction pattern first—leading to congestion and hemorrhage—followed by arterial compromise and infarction. The timeline from torsion to complete necrosis can be rapid, producing an acute abdomen.

Clinically, torsion of a pedunculated fibroid typically presents with sudden, severe, focal abdominal or pelvic pain often accompanied by nausea, vomiting, and signs of peritoneal irritation if there is associated hemoperitoneum or inflammatory reaction. Fever and leukocytosis are common. The pain is frequently localized to the lower abdominal quadrant corresponding to the pedicle location and may be exacerbated by movement. On examination, there may be focal tenderness, a palpable pelvic mass, and occasionally peritoneal signs if necrosis elicits inflammation.

Diagnostic clues include the history of a known pedunculated fibroid, acute onset of pain, and imaging features: ultrasound may demonstrate a mass separate from the ovary with little or absent internal vascularity on Doppler; the pedicle may be visualized as a thin connection to the uterus. MRI is particularly useful, showing heterogeneous signal intensity with central non-enhancement and potential twisting of the pedicle (a “whirl sign” analogous to adnexal torsion). CT can identify a non-enhancing mass and associated inflammatory changes; in cases of hemorrhagic infarct, high-attenuation material may be seen. Because torsion can progress to sepsis or peritonitis, prompt recognition is essential.

Management decisions are guided by the severity of symptoms, gestational status, and imaging findings. In nonpregnant patients, surgical detorsion and resection (myomectomy) are often required. In pregnancy, conservative management with analgesia may be attempted for stable patients with mild symptoms, but persistent pain, peritonitis, or hemodynamic instability calls for surgical intervention, balancing maternal-fetal risks.

# Secondary infection of degenerating myomas

Degenerating fibroids are susceptible to secondary infection because necrotic tissue provides a nidus for microbial proliferation and because breakdown of tissue barriers and local vascular compromise impair immune cell access. Infection may complicate any severe degenerative process—particularly cystic or myxoid degeneration that creates fluid collections, or red degeneration where hemorrhagic necrosis is present.

Pathogens are typically polymicrobial and can include vaginal flora ascending into the necrotic tissue (anaerobes such as Bacteroides species, Prevotella, anaerobic streptococci), Enterobacteriaceae, and streptococci; in some cases skin flora or iatrogenic inoculation (after instrumentation) contributes. In pregnancy, the risk of ascending infection is increased by cervical dilation or membrane rupture, and obstetric pathogens (e.g., group A or B Streptococcus) may be involved.

Clinically, secondary infection should be suspected in a patient with a known fibroid who develops new or worsening localized pain, fever, rigors, malodorous vaginal discharge (if there is communication), leukocytosis, and elevated inflammatory markers. On examination there may be focal pelvic tenderness, fluctuance if an abscess has formed, and signs of systemic sepsis if infection is advanced.

Imaging is central to diagnosis: ultrasound may show complex cystic components with internal debris, gas echoes suggestive of anaerobic infection, and peripheral hyperemia. CT can better delineate gas within the lesion, rim-enhancing fluid collections, and surrounding inflammatory fat stranding. MRI provides superior soft-tissue characterization and can differentiate abscess (central restricted diffusion on diffusion-weighted imaging) from sterile necrosis.

Laboratory findings usually show leukocytosis (normal WBC: 4–11 × 10^9/L), elevated C-reactive protein (CRP; normal < 5 mg/L) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR; women: 0–20 mm/hr), and sometimes bacteremia. Blood cultures may be positive, particularly in fulminant infection.

Management principles include broad-spectrum antibiotics covering anaerobes and Gram-negative bacilli, image-guided drainage of abscess collections when accessible, and surgical resection of the infected fibroid when drainage is incomplete or the patient is unstable. In pregnant patients, the timing and mode of intervention must consider fetal maturity and maternal status; uncontrolled infection may threaten both patient and fetus and prompt urgent surgical management.

# Mass effect complications: urinary obstruction, bowel compression, respiratory compromise

Large fibroids can produce clinically important mass effects by exerting extrinsic compression on adjacent pelvic and abdominal organs. The severity of mass effect depends on fibroid size, location (subserosal versus intramural versus submucosal), and rate of growth. In pregnancy fibroids may increase in size and shift position, magnifying these risks.

Urinary tract obstruction: A fibroid that compresses the ureter—commonly a large lateral uterine or broad ligament fibroid—can cause unilateral or bilateral hydronephrosis and compromise renal function. Clinical manifestations include flank pain, recurrent urinary tract infections, and deterioration of renal function. Serum creatinine should be monitored (normal adult female serum creatinine approximately 45–90 μmol/L) when obstruction is suspected. Imaging with ultrasound can detect hydronephrosis; CT urography or MR urography better defines the level of obstruction and relationship to the mass. Persistent obstruction may require decompression—ureteral stenting or percutaneous nephrostomy—followed by definitive surgical removal of the compressing fibroid.

Bowel compression: Large anterior or posterior fibroids can cause constipation, fecal urgency, tenesmus, or rarely bowel obstruction if they impinge on the rectosigmoid colon. Symptoms may be progressive and relate to mass size and patient posture. On imaging, displacement or extrinsic compression of bowel loops is evident; significant obstruction may manifest with abdominal distension, vomiting, and radiographic signs of small or large bowel obstruction. Management includes bowel rest, decompression if necessary, and planning for myomectomy or hysterectomy if symptoms are refractory.

Respiratory compromise: Massive uterine enlargement from multiple or very large fibroids can elevate the diaphragm, reduce lung volumes, and precipitate dyspnea—this is most commonly an issue with giant fibroids occupying much of the abdominal cavity. Pregnancy can exacerbate this effect due to the enlarged gravid uterus. Clinical assessment of respiratory status and oxygenation, along with chest imaging when indicated, determines the need for intervention. Surgical reduction of uterine size may be required when conservative measures fail.

All mass-effect complications must be assessed in the context of patient stability and comorbidities. The decision to intervene surgically is driven by the severity of organ dysfunction, potential for irreversible damage (e.g., renal impairment), and the feasibility of temporizing measures (stenting, drainage).

# Potential for hemorrhage and hemodynamic instability

Fibroids can be associated with significant hemorrhage through several mechanisms: degeneration with secondary bleeding, rupture of superficial veins on the fibroid surface, intralesional bleeding (as in red degeneration), traumatic rupture of a pedunculated fibroid, or erosion into adjacent vascular structures. Additionally, associated vascular malformations or parasitic vascular supply from ovarian vessels can produce bleeding.

In pregnancy, increased vascularity and a hyperdynamic pelvic circulation heighten the risk. Rupture of a fibroid can lead to intraperitoneal hemorrhage with signs of hypovolemia—tachycardia, hypotension, pallor, diaphoresis, and acute abdomen—requiring rapid recognition and resuscitation. Massive bleeding can progress to hemorrhagic shock and coagulopathy; transfusion thresholds and resuscitation practices follow institutional and guideline-based protocols (e.g., transfuse red cells when hemoglobin is symptomatic or below target levels; consider massive transfusion protocols in ongoing hemorrhage).

Laboratory monitoring in suspected hemorrhage includes complete blood count (normal hemoglobin women 120–160 g/L), coagulation tests (prothrombin time, activated partial thromboplastin time), fibrinogen (normal 2–4 g/L), and arterial blood gases if unstable. Imaging with focused assessment with sonography for trauma (FAST) or abdominal ultrasound can quickly identify free fluid; CT angiography can localize active extravasation. In stable patients, uterine artery embolization can control bleeding by devascularizing the fibroid; in unstable patients, surgical exploration with myomectomy or hysterectomy may be necessary. In pregnancy, management must weigh fetal viability and maternal risk; intraoperative hemostatic techniques and multidisciplinary planning are critical.

# Biologic markers and inflammatory response associated with degeneration

Degenerative and infectious processes in fibroids elicit local and systemic inflammatory responses measurable by laboratory markers and cytokine profiles. Acute degenerative events—especially red degeneration and secondary infection—typically provoke neutrophilic leukocytosis (WBC > 11 × 10^9/L in many cases), elevated CRP concentrations, and increased ESR. Typical reference ranges include CRP < 5 mg/L, though values rise significantly with marked inflammation or infection. Procalcitonin can be useful in distinguishing bacterial infection from sterile inflammatory degeneration; procalcitonin levels rise in systemic bacterial infection and sepsis, whereas they are less elevated in sterile necrosis.

At the molecular level, ischemic and necrotic fibroid tissue upregulates proinflammatory mediators. Interleukin-6 (IL-6) commonly rises in systemic inflammation and stimulates hepatic production of acute phase reactants such as CRP and fibrinogen. Tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β) are also implicated in the local inflammatory milieu, promoting leukocyte recruitment and vascular permeability. These cytokines explain systemic manifestations such as fever, malaise, and anorexia. Elevated IL-6 and CRP correlate with symptom severity in some inflammatory gynecologic conditions and may help monitor response to therapy.

Microbiologic testing (blood cultures, aspirate cultures) assists in identifying infectious agents when infection is suspected. Gram stain and culture of drained purulent material guide targeted antibiotic therapy. Inflammatory markers and repeat imaging help evaluate treatment response. D-dimer and fibrin degradation products may be elevated in severe tissue necrosis or concurrent thrombotic events, but these are nonspecific.

Clinically, decision points include distinguishing sterile degeneration from infection. Key discriminants are presence of high-grade fever, rigors, purulent vaginal discharge, marked leukocytosis and procalcitonin elevation, imaging evidence of gas or rim-enhancing abscess, and bacteremia. Sterile red degeneration often presents with low-grade fever and less pronounced inflammatory marker elevation; antibiotics are not universally indicated unless secondary infection is suspected.

# Summary and clinical reasoning integration

Degenerative changes in uterine fibroids arise from a spectrum of vascular, mechanical, and biochemical disturbances that alter tissue viability and structure. Red, hyaline, cystic, and myxoid degeneration each have distinct histopathologic and imaging correlates and produce characteristic clinical syndromes. Vascular compromise—whether chronic ischemia, intratumoral thrombosis, or torsion of a pedicle—drives progression to necrosis and can precipitate pain, systemic inflammation, or secondary infection. Pedunculated subserosal fibroid torsion is a surgical emergency when it causes severe ischemia; secondary infection of necrotic myomas may require antibiotics and drainage in addition to definitive surgical therapy.

Large fibroids produce mass effects that may threaten renal, bowel, or respiratory function, and they carry a risk of hemorrhage severe enough to produce hemodynamic instability. Laboratory markers (WBC, CRP, procalcitonin, renal function tests, coagulation profile) and imaging (ultrasound, CT, MRI) are crucial to differentiate sterile degeneration from infection, to localize complications, and to guide urgency and type of intervention. Understanding the pathophysiology helps clinicians triage patients appropriately, select diagnostic tests that will most directly change management, and recognize when urgent surgical or radiologic interventions are required to prevent irreversible organ damage or life-threatening hemorrhage.
