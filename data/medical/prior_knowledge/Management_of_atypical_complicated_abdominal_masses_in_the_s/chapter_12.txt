Introduction

Pregnant patients who undergo abdominal or pelvic surgery for adnexal masses, uterine fibroids (myomectomy), or other pelvic pathology require carefully tailored obstetric follow-up. The intersection of postoperative physiologic changes, potential impacts on the pregnant uterus and placenta, and the risks introduced by residual or recurrent pelvic masses mandates structured surveillance, clear thresholds for intervention, and anticipatory delivery planning. This chapter reviews practical postoperative fetal surveillance protocols, evidence-based medical strategies to reduce miscarriage or preterm birth risk (including progesterone and tocolytics), criteria that guide timing and mode of delivery after pregnancy-associated pelvic surgery or prior myomectomy, assessment of how residual masses influence labor mechanics, clinical considerations when placental location is affected by myomas, and counseling points on breastfeeding and timing of any further surgery. The emphasis is clinical reasoning—linking pathophysiology to monitoring choices and delivery decisions.

# Postoperative fetal surveillance protocols and frequency of obstetric follow-up

Pelvic or abdominal surgery during pregnancy can produce a range of fetal risks—preterm labor, fetal distress from intraoperative hemodynamic or oxygenation changes, and less commonly, placental injury. Surveillance should therefore be individualized on the basis of (1) gestational age at surgery, (2) type and extent of surgery (laparoscopy versus laparotomy; whether the uterine cavity was entered), (3) intraoperative events (maternal hypotension, hypoxia, substantial blood loss, uterine manipulation), and (4) fetal status in the immediate perioperative period.

Immediate postoperative period (first 24–72 hours). For viable fetuses (commonly defined as ≥20–24 weeks depending on local practice), an assessment of fetal well-being is mandatory after recovery from anesthesia. This typically includes a fetal heart rate auscultation or continuous fetal monitoring (cardiotocography) for patients ≥24 weeks with concern for contractions or nonreassuring maternal course. If the procedure occurred prior to viability, a transvaginal or transabdominal ultrasound to confirm cardiac activity is appropriate within 24 hours. For viable pregnancies with an uneventful surgery and no concerning intraoperative findings, one or two assessments of fetal heart rate (by Doppler) and a clinical maternal assessment (pain, uterine tenderness, bleeding) in the first 24 hours may suffice.

Short-term follow‑up (first 2–4 weeks). A formal obstetric visit within 1–2 weeks after surgery is commonly recommended. That visit should include a targeted ultrasound to confirm fetal growth appropriate to gestational age and to evaluate amniotic fluid, placental location (if not previously documented), and any residual pelvic masses. If the surgery was for adnexal masses or myomas, documentation of mass size and relation to cervix/placenta is important at this visit. If the patient reports uterine contractions, vaginal bleeding, or decreased fetal movement, more urgent evaluation (nonstress test [NST], biophysical profile [BPP]) is indicated.

Ongoing surveillance through pregnancy. Frequency of routine visits follows standard obstetric schedules (every 4 weeks until 28 weeks, every 2 weeks until 36 weeks, then weekly). However, pregnancies complicated by significant intraoperative events, significant residual pelvic masses, or any entrance into the uterine cavity during surgery should be considered higher risk and managed with increased surveillance. This may include more frequent growth ultrasounds (every 4 weeks or as clinically indicated), serial cervical length assessments (transvaginal ultrasound) when preterm birth risk exists, and periodic NSTs or BPPs beginning in the third trimester if there are concerns about placental perfusion or fetal growth restriction. Doppler studies (umbilical artery pulsatility index) are appropriate when growth restriction is suspected or there were intraoperative events suggesting compromised uteroplacental blood flow.

Indications for antenatal fetal surveillance modalities. Nonstress testing is indicated for decreased fetal movement, maternal conditions that predispose to fetal compromise, or concerns after surgery. A biophysical profile (score 8–10 normal) complements NST when fetal movements are reduced or when NST is nonreactive. Cervical length is used when assessing preterm birth risk: a short cervix is generally <25 mm before 24 weeks and is a validated predictor of spontaneous preterm birth. Ultrasound documentation of placental location becomes essential if the surgical field included the lower uterine segment or the uterus was manipulated.

Disposition and threshold for obstetric admission. Postoperative uterine contractions, cervical change, vaginal bleeding, oligohydramnios, or any nonreassuring fetal testing should prompt admission for obstetric monitoring and management. Clear discharge instructions must emphasize warning signs—regular uterine contractions, leaking fluid, heavy vaginal bleeding, or decreased fetal movement—so patients seek immediate care.

# Use of progesterone and other agents for miscarriage or preterm birth prevention: indications and evidence

Progesterone is the most widely studied pharmacologic intervention for prevention of recurrent early pregnancy loss and spontaneous preterm birth (sPTB). Its role differs depending on the clinical scenario: prevention of miscarriage in early pregnancy, prevention of sPTB in women with prior spontaneous preterm birth, and prevention in women with a short cervix on ultrasound.

Progesterone for recurrent early pregnancy loss. For women with a history of recurrent pregnancy loss (commonly defined as two or more consecutive losses), progesterone supplementation in the first trimester has been evaluated. Evidence supports a benefit in women with early pregnancy bleeding and prior losses, though randomized trials show mixed results. Clinical practice often uses vaginal micronized progesterone (e.g., 200 mg vaginally nightly) or oral dydrogesterone in the first trimester for women with threatened miscarriage and prior losses—recognizing that large, high-quality trials (e.g., PRISM trial) suggest benefit primarily in the subgroup with prior loss.

Progesterone to prevent spontaneous preterm birth. Two major contexts:

1) Women with prior spontaneous preterm birth. For women with a singleton pregnancy and a history of spontaneous preterm birth (prior spontaneous delivery <37 weeks), intramuscular 17-alpha hydroxyprogesterone caproate (17-OHPC) 250 mg weekly has been widely used historically to reduce recurrence. However, recent high-quality evidence (e.g., the PROLONG trial) has brought controversy—showing no significant benefit in heterogeneous populations—leading to reconsideration of universal 17-OHPC use. Current guidance tends toward individualized decision-making: consider progesterone for those with a clear prior spontaneous singleton preterm birth, after counseling on the mixed evidence and local guideline recommendations. Notably, 17-OHPC is given from 16–36 weeks gestation.

2) Women with a short cervix without prior preterm birth. Vaginal progesterone (e.g., 90 mg gel nightly or 200 mg micronized vaginal progesterone nightly) for women with a short cervical length (<25 mm before 24 weeks) reduces the rate of preterm birth and improves neonatal outcomes across several randomized trials and meta-analyses. This indication is well supported and commonly applied.

Choice of formulation. Vaginal progesterone is preferred for cervical shortening and is commonly used for threatened miscarriage. 17-OHPC (intramuscular) has been the standard in the U.S. for prior spontaneous preterm birth but, as above, requires individualized counseling. Oral formulations vary in bioavailability and are less consistently studied for sPTB prevention.

Safety considerations. Progesterone is generally well tolerated. There are no consistent data linking prenatal progesterone exposure to major congenital anomalies. Side effects may include local irritation (vaginal forms) or injection site reactions (IM 17-OHPC).

Tocolytics and adjuvant agents. Tocolysis aims to delay delivery long enough for antenatal corticosteroids to act (optimally 48 hours) or to permit in utero transfer. Common agents include nifedipine (oral calcium channel blocker), which is widely used and has evidence supporting short-term pregnancy prolongation and improved maternal tolerability compared with beta-agonists. Beta-agonists (e.g., terbutaline) are generally used less due to maternal side effects. Indomethacin (a prostaglandin-synthesis inhibitor) is an effective tocolytic but is generally limited to gestations <32 weeks and used short-term because of fetal risks (oligohydramnios, premature closure of ductus arteriosus) if used later. Atosiban (oxytocin receptor antagonist) is used in some countries and has a favorable side-effect profile but is not available in all jurisdictions.

Adjunctive measures. Corticosteroids for fetal lung maturation (e.g., betamethasone 12 mg IM x 2 doses 24 hours apart or dexamethasone 6 mg IM q12h x 4 doses) are recommended when delivery is anticipated before 34 weeks, with consideration up to 37 weeks in certain contexts. Magnesium sulfate for fetal neuroprotection is indicated for anticipated delivery before 32 weeks (commonly 4 g loading then 1 g/h infusion for up to 24 hours or until delivery) to reduce risk of cerebral palsy in the preterm infant.

Application to postoperative patients. Surgery itself is not an automatic indication for progesterone or tocolysis. Use of progesterone is driven by obstetric risk factors (e.g., short cervix, prior sPTB). Tocolysis after surgery is indicated if there is active preterm labor (regular uterine contractions with cervical change) and the goal is to delay delivery for steroid benefit or maternal stabilization. Routine prophylactic tocolysis after surgery is not recommended because benefits are unproven and risks exist. When early contractions occur immediately postoperatively, short-term tocolysis (e.g., nifedipine) may be used to blunt uterine activity while reassessing for other causes (infection, hematoma, uterine irritability).

# Criteria for timing and mode of delivery after prior myomectomy or pelvic surgery during pregnancy

Determining timing and mode of delivery after intrapartum or antepartum pelvic surgery (particularly myomectomy) requires evaluation of the uterine incision type, whether the uterine cavity was entered, extent and location of the myomectomy, and the number and depth of myometrial layers repaired.

Risk of uterine rupture after myomectomy. The principal concern is uterine rupture during labor. Rupture risk after myomectomy is generally low but not negligible and is influenced by operative factors: transmural or deep intramural myomas requiring entry into the uterine cavity, repair of multiple layers, large myomas (>5 cm) in the fundus or lower segment, and classical (vertical) uterine incisions increase rupture risk. Minimally invasive myomectomy with small fascial disruption seems to have lower rupture risk than extensive open myomectomy that involves a full-thickness incision. Published cumulative risk estimates vary; many series report uterine rupture rates after myomectomy between 0.5% and 2% depending on case mix.

Mode of delivery decision-making. Individualized counseling is essential. If the myomectomy involved only superficial, small intramural excisions without entry into the endometrial cavity and with a robust multilayer repair, a trial of labor after myomectomy (TOL) may be reasonable. When the uterine cavity was entered, large myomas were removed, the lower uterine segment was involved, or the repair was uncertain/multiple-layer repair was incomplete, many clinicians recommend cesarean delivery to avoid the risk of rupture during labor.

Key operative features that favor cesarean delivery include:

- Documented entry into the endometrial cavity at time of myomectomy. 
- Multiple deep intramural myomas requiring extensive myometrial excision.
- A classical—or vertical—fundal incision or other incision that disrupts the contractile myometrium significantly.
- Poor hemostatic or tissue quality compromising the repair.
- Uncertain or unavailable operative records to characterize the repair (absence of records often pushes toward cesarean in higher-risk scenarios).

Timing of delivery. In uncomplicated situations where a TOL is planned, timing is dictated by obstetric indications rather than a fixed post-myomectomy interval. No standard mandate requires scheduled cesarean at a specific gestational age solely due to prior myomectomy. If cesarean is chosen, scheduling around 39 weeks is typical unless other indications prompt earlier delivery. When concern for scar integrity is present (e.g., large prior uterine repair), some clinicians consider scheduled cesarean at 37–39 weeks to avoid spontaneous labor, but there is no universal consensus; decisions should balance neonatal maturity and rupture risk.

Informed consent and documentation. Before labor onset, clear counseling should include discussion of rupture risk (including signs/symptoms: sudden severe abdominal pain, vaginal bleeding, loss of fetal station, abnormal fetal heart tracing), alternatives, and the rationale for either trial of labor or planned cesarean. Availability of surgical and anesthesia back-up should be ensured for cases where the threshold for cesarean is low.

Implications of other pelvic surgeries. Surgeries that do not alter uterine integrity (e.g., adnexal surgery, pelvic lymphadenectomy) generally do not mandate cesarean delivery. However, the presence of residual pelvic masses in the lower pelvis or extensive adhesions that could obstruct descent may influence mode of delivery (see next section).

# Assessment of residual masses and their impact on labor and delivery (obstructed labor risk)

Residual pelvic masses—fundal myomas, lower segment myomas, adnexal masses, or postoperative hematomas—can mechanically obstruct labor, distort uterine contractility, and increase risk of malpresentation or labor dystocia. Assessing impact requires attention to size, number, location relative to the birth canal and cervix, and change over gestation.

Mass characteristics and clinical relevance. Large myomas (>5–6 cm) are more likely to influence pregnancy and labor, but even smaller myomas in critical locations can be clinically significant. Subserosal or fundal myomas typically have less effect on labor mechanics than intramural or submucosal myomas or those in the lower uterine segment/cervix. A lower-segment myoma near the internal os, a cervical myoma, or a large adnexal mass can physically block descent and produce obstructed labor. Multiple myomas that distort the uterine cavity can impede effective contractions, increasing the need for operative delivery.

Assessment modalities. Serial ultrasound is the primary imaging tool for assessing residual masses—documenting size, echotexture, vascularity, and relation to cervix and placenta. If ultrasound is limited, MRI offers superior soft tissue contrast and is safe in pregnancy (preferably without gadolinium), and may clarify whether a mass is likely to obstruct the birth canal. Imaging should be repeated if symptoms emerge (pain, increasing uterine tenderness, or suspicion for rapid growth/degeneration) or when planning delivery.

Predicting obstructed labor. No simple size threshold guarantees obstruction; rather, consider the triad of mass size, location, and mobility. A sessile, firm mass in the lower uterine segment or cervix that occupies a significant proportion of the diameter of the pelvic inlet is most concerning. Pre-labor planning includes assessing whether the mass is likely to prevent engagement of the fetal head. For example, a 5–10 cm mass low in the pelvis is more problematic than the same mass at the fundus.

Delivery planning for obstructive masses. If imaging suggests high likelihood of mechanical obstruction at term, a planned cesarean delivery is appropriate. For borderline cases, a plan for attempted labor with a low threshold for cesarean if descent does not progress is reasonable only when the team is prepared for urgent operative delivery. Intrapartum monitoring should include close assessment of labor progress and fetal status. Attempted vaginal delivery with instrumental assistance or cesarean extraction should be decided on a case-by-case basis. Myomectomy at the time of cesarean (cesarean myomectomy) may be feasible in select cases at experienced centers but carries increased hemorrhage risk; prior planning for blood products and surgical support is mandatory.

Special consideration for degenerating masses. Myomas can undergo red degeneration during pregnancy, causing pain and sometimes fever and leukocytosis—these episodes may transiently alter uterine contractility. Conservative management may be adequate for pain control; however, persistent irritation and uterine irritability near term might necessitate expedited delivery decisions.

# Placenta–myoma interactions, placental localization and monitoring

The placenta and uterine myomas may interact in ways that influence placental implantation, perfusion, and risk of abnormal placentation.

Placental location and displacement. Large myomas can displace the placenta toward a less ideal site. A placenta overlying a large submucosal or intramural myoma may be partially impeded from normal implantation. Placental location should be documented early (first- or early second-trimester ultrasound) and reassessed in the third trimester when placenta previa risk is evaluated. Placental migration is common: an apparent placenta previa identified in the second trimester often resolves by the third trimester as the lower uterine segment develops.

Placenta previa and accreta spectrum. Uterine surgery, including myomectomy with cavity entry, is a risk factor for abnormal placentation (placenta accreta spectrum), because scarring can impair normal decidualization and predispose to abnormal trophoblastic invasion. When placenta overlies a prior myomectomy site or uterine scar, the risk of placenta accreta increases. Therefore, in patients with prior uterine surgery and a placenta positioned low in the uterus, targeted ultrasound for accreta features and, if needed, MRI without gadolinium can be used for further assessment. Sonographic signs suggestive of accreta include loss of the clear zone between placenta and myometrium, placental lacunae with turbulent flow, and abnormal uterine-bladder interface.

Monitoring for placental insufficiency. Large intramural myomas can theoretically impair uterine blood flow to portions of the placenta, predisposing to fetal growth restriction or abnormal Doppler studies. If there is concern—based on serial growth charts, low fundal height, or abnormal Doppler velocimetry—then increased surveillance with growth ultrasounds every 2–4 weeks and periodic umbilical artery Doppler studies are warranted. The frequency depends on severity; abnormal Dopplers or growth restriction may prompt increased fetal surveillance (NST, BPP) and earlier delivery if compromise is progressive.

Management of suspected accreta. When placenta accreta is suspected antenatally, delivery planning must be multidisciplinary including maternal-fetal medicine, anesthesia, blood bank, interventional radiology (if available for balloon catheterization), and surgical teams. Timing of delivery is generally scheduled (often 34–36+6 weeks in controlled settings) to balance maternal risks and fetal maturity, with corticosteroids administered for lung maturity. These considerations fall outside of routine postoperative follow-up but are critical when placental pathology is identified after pelvic surgery.

# Counseling about breastfeeding and implications for further surgical planning postpartum

Breastfeeding and timing of subsequent pelvic surgery intersect through considerations of lactation physiology, medication safety, anesthesia and analgesia, and timing for optimal surgical outcomes.

Breastfeeding physiology and immediate postpartum period. Lactogenesis II typically begins 2–3 days postpartum, but early milk production and establishment of lactation benefit from skin-to-skin contact and frequent breastfeeding. Counseling should emphasize that early postoperative pain control regimens should be compatible with breastfeeding (e.g., acetaminophen and most opioids in short courses are considered compatible, but informed dosing and monitoring for sedation is essential). If maternal surgery is planned in the immediate postpartum period, impact on lactation initiation should be discussed.

Impact of medications. Many perioperative medications are compatible with breastfeeding. Local anesthetics and most general anesthetics are minimally excreted in breastmilk; guidelines generally allow breastfeeding to resume after anesthesia once the mother is alert and able to safely hold and feed the infant. Certain medications used postoperatively—some anticoagulants, methadone, or specific antidepressants—require counseling about risks and may necessitate temporary pumping and discarding of breastmilk or alternative feeding strategies.

Timing of elective pelvic surgery postpartum. For nonurgent elective surgery (including definitive myomectomy, adnexal surgery, or definitive repair of pelvic pathology), delaying for uterine involution and recovery is typical. Many surgeons recommend waiting at least 6–12 weeks postpartum; some prefer waiting 3–6 months to reduce vascularity and optimize tissue planes. Longer intervals (3–6 months) may also be chosen to allow completion of breastfeeding if the mother desires; surgery while lactating can sometimes impair milk supply transiently depending on stress, medications, and separation from the infant.

Breastfeeding and future fertility/surgical outcomes. Breastfeeding does not generally preclude future pelvic surgery; however, clinicians should discuss that breastfeeding at the time of elective surgery may necessitate interruption and temporary pumping to maintain supply. If a postpartum hysterectomy becomes necessary, early cessation of breastfeeding will affect lactation. Counseling about contraception postpartum is also relevant, especially for those who require interval surgery and wish to avoid conception shortly after procedures.

Coordination with pediatric care. If perioperative medications are expected to reach breastmilk and pose neonatal risks, coordinate with pediatric providers about monitoring for sedation or respiratory depression. Breastfeeding mothers who must take medications incompatible with breastfeeding should be provided a plan for milk expression and safe storage, and for when to resume direct breastfeeding.

Planning future pregnancies and surgeries. For women who plan additional pelvic surgery to address residual masses that impacted the pregnancy, timing and surgical approach can be influenced by lactational status. Hormonal milieu during lactation (prolactin elevated, estrogen lower) may influence tissue healing and bleeding risk minimally, but the primary considerations are maternal preference, breastfeeding logistics, and optimizing surgical conditions (less pelvic vascularity after involution). Counseling should include discussion of contraceptive plans if pregnancy delay is desired until after surgery.

Conclusion

Obstetric management after pelvic surgery in pregnancy requires integration of perioperative events, surgical details, imaging findings, and obstetric risk stratification. Vigilant early postoperative surveillance, targeted use of progesterone when indicated by obstetric risk factors, judicious application of tocolytics for active preterm labor, and individualized delivery planning—particularly after myomectomy—are the pillars of safe care. Understanding how residual masses and placental relationships affect labor mechanics and placental function allows anticipatory planning for mode and timing of delivery. Counseling about breastfeeding and timing of future surgery completes the continuum of care, ensuring safe maternal and neonatal outcomes while respecting patient preferences.
