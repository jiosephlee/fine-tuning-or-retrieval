Chapter 8: Clinical Evaluation and Differential Diagnosis of Pelvic/Abdominal Masses in Pregnancy

Introduction

The discovery of a pelvic or abdominal mass in a pregnant patient presents a diagnostic challenge that requires integration of obstetric knowledge, anatomic considerations of the gravid uterus, and the physiological changes of pregnancy that alter presentation and interpretation of signs, symptoms, and laboratory data. A structured approach begins with a targeted history and symptom assessment, proceeds to a focused but modified physical examination, and synthesizes findings with laboratory and imaging studies to develop a prioritized differential diagnosis that balances maternal and fetal risk. This chapter outlines the essential clinical elements for evaluating pelvic and abdominal masses in pregnancy, details how to perform and interpret a bimanual pelvic examination in this context, provides a practical framework for differential diagnosis across likely organ systems, highlights red flags that mandate urgent intervention, reviews useful scoring and risk-stratification tools, and describes how to integrate clinical, laboratory, and imaging data into working diagnoses and management decisions.

# Key components of history and symptom assessment (pain characteristics, systemic symptoms, obstetric history)

A thorough history is the foundation of diagnosis. In pregnant patients with a pelvic or abdominal mass, distinguish among symptoms that point toward acute surgical abdomen, obstetric complications, or chronic/benign processes. Begin by characterizing pain: onset (sudden versus gradual), timing (constant versus intermittent/colicky), severity (scale 0–10), location and radiation, exacerbating and relieving factors, and associated features such as nausea, vomiting, bowel habit changes, urinary symptoms, vaginal bleeding, or discharge. Sudden, severe, focal pain that localizes to one lower quadrant and is associated with vomiting should raise concern for adnexal torsion, ruptured ovarian cyst, or acute appendicitis. Colicky, intermittent pain with palpable mass may suggest an obstructing process such as ureteral stone, obstructed bowel, or a fibroid undergoing degeneration. Diffuse abdominal pain with fever and peritoneal signs more likely reflects inflammatory or infectious processes (peritonitis, pelvic abscess, complicated appendicitis) and requires rapid evaluation.

Systemic symptoms refine differential and urgency. Fever and rigors indicate infection; tachycardia, hypotension, or altered mental status suggest sepsis and demand immediate action. Bleeding, vaginal fluid loss, and fetal movement reports are essential; fetal symptoms (decreased movements) may signal compromise. Obtain obstetric history early: gestational age, parity, prior pelvic surgery (adhesions, ectopic risk), prior cesarean delivery, history of pelvic inflammatory disease or endometriosis, known uterine fibroids or adnexal masses identified antenatally, infertility treatments (e.g., ovarian hyperstimulation), and results of recent prenatal ultrasounds. Gestational age narrows likely causes: adnexal masses discovered in the first trimester are often corpus luteum cysts or early adnexal pathology; mid-to-late pregnancy enlarging masses may be uterine fibroids that enlarge during pregnancy or degenerating leiomyomas. Ask about contraceptive and sexual history, as sexually transmitted infection history increases risk of tubo-ovarian abscess. Medication exposures including anticoagulants inform hemorrhagic risk.

Elicit gynecologic symptoms: abnormal vaginal bleeding, malodorous discharge, or postcoital bleeding. Urinary symptoms (dysuria, hematuria, frequency) may point to urinary tract infection or obstructing calculus and can mimic gynecologic pain. Gastrointestinal symptoms—anorexia, bowel obstruction features (obstipation, severe distension), melena—should not be overlooked, as gravid uterus can displace bowel and mask typical signs. Also query constitutional symptoms such as weight loss or night sweats, which might suggest chronic conditions (malignancy, tuberculosis) rather than an acute process.

When an adnexal or pelvic mass is known from prior imaging, review its documented characteristics (size, laterality, complexity, solid versus cystic, vascularity) and interval change. A previously stable small simple cyst is often managed conservatively, whereas a rapidly enlarging or complex mass warrants earlier intervention. Always document maternal comorbidities (coagulopathy, cardiovascular disease) that alter perioperative risk, and the patient’s preferences and obstetric plan (desire to continue pregnancy, mode and timing of delivery) because these affect management choices.

# Bimanual pelvic examination techniques and limitations in pregnancy

The bimanual pelvic examination remains a valuable bedside tool but must be adapted and interpreted cautiously in pregnancy. The goals are to assess uterine size and contour, cervical status, adnexal masses, tenderness, mobility, and signs of peritoneal irritation. In early pregnancy (first trimester), a bimanual exam can often detect uterine enlargement consistent with gestational age and may detect adnexal masses proximal to the uterus. In the second trimester and beyond, the gravid uterus enlarges into the abdominal cavity; consequently, pelvic bimanual palpation becomes less reliable for uterine size estimation and for assessing adnexal structures.

Technique in pregnancy follows conventional steps with patient positioning supine and a chaperone present. Explain the procedure and obtain consent. Insert two gloved, lubricated fingers into the vagina, palpate the cervix for consistency, effacement, dilation and tenderness. With the non-dominant hand on the lower abdomen, elevate the uterine fundus to approximate its position and attempt to palpate lateral adnexal structures between hands. Gently note any masses, tenderness, or restricted mobility. Bimanual palpation is more informative prior to 12–14 weeks; in later pregnancy, adnexal masses may be displaced cephalad or laterally and thus difficult to reach.

Be aware of limitations and potential for false reassurance or misinterpretation. The gravid uterus can mask or mimic adnexal masses: a lateral uterine fibroid may be mistaken for an adnexal mass; an enlarged, anteverted uterus can simulate a mass felt through the fornix. Vaginal bleeding or active labor signs require careful cervical examination but avoid aggressive manipulation. Pregnancy-related leukorrhea and cervical softening can complicate assessment of cervicitis. In cases where bimanual exam is inconclusive, rely promptly on imaging (transvaginal or transabdominal ultrasound) rather than repeated strenuous examinations.

Specific examination findings help direct differential. A tender, unilateral adnexal mass with adnexal rebound suggests torsion or tubo-ovarian abscess; a firm, irregular, non-mobile mass contiguous with the uterus suggests leiomyoma. A palpable, contiguous mass rising with the uterus points to uterine origin (fibroid, bicornuate uterus), whereas a mass separate from but adjacent to the uterus suggests adnexal or gastrointestinal source. Cervical motion tenderness increases likelihood of pelvic inflammatory disease but can be present with other inflammatory processes, including appendicitis or ectopic pregnancy. Always interpret findings in the context of gestational age and concurrent imaging.

# Framework for differential diagnosis: uterine, adnexal, gastrointestinal, urinary, and extra-abdominal sources

A systematic approach divides potential etiologies into uterine, adnexal, gastrointestinal, urinary, and extra-abdominal sources to organize thinking and testing.

Uterine sources: Uterine fibroids (leiomyomas) are the most common benign pelvic masses in pregnancy. They may increase in size under hormonal influence, although growth is variable. Symptoms include localized pain from red degeneration (a painful ischemic process often occurring in the second trimester) and mass effect causing urinary frequency or malpresentation. On examination, fibroids are typically firm, irregular, and contiguous with the uterus; ultrasound shows a well-defined, heterogeneous intramural or subserosal mass. Other uterine causes include uterine anomalies (e.g., bicornuate uterus) and rare uterine sarcomas that may present as rapidly enlarging masses with systemic symptoms.

Adnexal sources: In pregnancy, adnexal masses include corpus luteum cysts (common in early pregnancy and typically resolve by the second trimester), ovarian cysts (follicular, hemorrhagic, endometriomas), benign neoplasms (mature cystic teratoma), and malignant tumors (epithelial ovarian carcinoma, germ cell tumors) though malignancy is uncommon. Adnexal torsion is a gynecologic emergency characterized by sudden onset unilateral lower abdominal pain, often accompanied by nausea and vomiting. Torsion risk increases with larger adnexal masses, ovarian hyperstimulation, or pregnancy-related adnexal enlargement. Tubo-ovarian abscess and pelvic inflammatory disease, though less common in pregnancy, can produce complex, tender adnexal masses and systemic toxicity.

Gastrointestinal sources: Appendicitis is the most common non-obstetric surgical emergency in pregnancy and can present atypically because the appendix is displaced superiorly by the gravid uterus as pregnancy progresses. Pain migration, anorexia, nausea, and fever are typical but may be blunted. Intestinal obstruction (due to adhesions, volvulus, or external compression by a mass), diverticulitis, and neoplasms can present as palpable masses or abdominal distension. Pancreatitis, though rarely palpable as a mass, can cause severe epigastric pain that radiates to the back and systemic inflammatory response. Always consider GI causes when the mass is more abdominal than pelvic, when bowel sounds are altered, or when symptoms include significant bowel dysfunction.

Urinary sources: Distended bladder, hydronephrosis, or perinephric collections can mimic pelvic masses. Physiologic hydronephrosis of pregnancy commonly causes right-sided urinary tract dilation with mild flank pain; however, obstructing ureteral stones or pyonephrosis produce more severe pain and urinary symptoms. A palpable suprapubic mass may be a distended bladder in women with urinary retention (less common in pregnancy but possible in advanced labor or with pelvic mass causing outlet obstruction). Hematuria or severe flank pain should prompt urinalysis and renal imaging.

Extra-abdominal sources: Abdominal wall masses such as rectus sheath hematomas (especially in anticoagulated patients), ventral hernias, or lipomas can present as palpable masses and localized pain. Systemic conditions such as ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome (in patients who conceived with assisted reproduction) can cause bilateral ovarian enlargement and ascites. Lymphadenopathy or neoplastic metastases rarely present first as palpable abdominal masses but should be considered when systemic symptoms are present.

Cross-organ overlap and mimicry are common. For instance, an adnexal mass may cause urinary frequency by mass effect, and appendicitis can present with adnexal tenderness. Therefore, avoid anchoring bias: correlate history, exam, and imaging to avoid misattributing symptoms to pregnancy or to a known benign mass.

# Red flags warranting urgent intervention (torsion, rupture, severe hemorrhage, sepsis)

Timely identification of red flags is critical because delays can result in maternal morbidity and fetal compromise. Recognize the clinical patterns that demand urgent intervention.

Ovarian/adnexal torsion: Torsion classically presents with sudden, severe, unilateral lower abdominal pain often accompanied by nausea and vomiting. Physical exam may reveal localized peritoneal signs or a tender adnexal mass. Doppler ultrasound may show absent or diminished venous and arterial flow, but normal Doppler does not exclude torsion. High clinical suspicion merits urgent gynecologic consultation and often surgical exploration, because ovarian viability declines rapidly with prolonged ischemia.

Rupture and severe hemorrhage: Rupture of ovarian cysts, ectopic pregnancy, or vascular injury from mass necrosis can cause intraperitoneal hemorrhage. Presentations include sudden severe abdominal pain, hemodynamic instability (tachycardia, hypotension), syncope, and abdominal distension. Paracentesis or focused ultrasound demonstrating free intraperitoneal fluid may support the diagnosis. Immediate resuscitation, blood product availability, and surgical intervention are required.

Sepsis and pelvic abscess: Fever, tachycardia, hypotension, leukocytosis or leukopenia, elevated lactate, and altered mental status indicate sepsis. A tubo-ovarian abscess, complicated pyelonephritis, or perforated viscous may be the source. Use sepsis protocols (early antibiotics, source control, hemodynamic support) promptly. In pregnancy, consider fetal monitoring and involve obstetrics early.

Acute bowel obstruction or strangulation: Signs include severe abdominal pain, distension, vomiting, absence of flatus/feces, and systemic toxicity. The gravid uterus may complicate localization, but persistence of obstructive features or peritoneal signs necessitates surgical evaluation and likely operative management to prevent ischemia.

Acute appendicitis with perforation: Increasing pain intensity, peritonitis signs, fever, and leukocytosis may indicate perforation. Because diagnostic uncertainty is higher in pregnancy, maintain low threshold for surgical intervention if clinical and imaging data support appendicitis.

Cardiopulmonary compromise from large masses: Very large uterine fibroids or ovarian masses may compromise venous return or respiratory mechanics; in such cases, consider paracentesis for ascites or expedited management.

Any pregnant patient with hemodynamic instability, uncontrolled pain despite analgesia, peritoneal signs, or systemic inflammatory response should be treated emergently with coordinated multidisciplinary care (obstetrics, surgery, anesthesia, neonatology as needed).

# Use of clinical scoring systems and risk stratification tools

Clinical scoring systems can assist decision making by quantifying pre-test probability and standardizing evaluation, but most are developed for nonpregnant patients and must be applied cautiously.

Appendicitis: The Alvarado score (maximum 10 points) incorporates signs, symptoms, and lab findings (migration of pain, anorexia, nausea/vomiting, right lower quadrant tenderness, rebound, elevated temperature, leukocytosis, and left shift). A high score increases likelihood of appendicitis; however, physiologic leukocytosis and displacement of the appendix during pregnancy reduce specificity. Imaging (ultrasound followed by MRI if needed) should complement clinical scoring.

Ovarian mass malignancy risk: Several tools estimate risk that an adnexal mass is malignant. The Risk of Malignancy Index (RMI) incorporates ultrasound features, menopausal status, and serum CA-125. In pregnancy, CA-125 may be elevated and menopausal status is typically premenopausal, limiting RMI utility. More contemporary models include the International Ovarian Tumor Analysis (IOTA) simple rules and the ADNEX model, which use ultrasound morphology and patient factors to estimate malignancy risk. These models perform best with high-quality transvaginal ultrasound and are less validated in pregnancy; still, complex solid components, papillary projections, multilocularity, and increased vascularity on Doppler raise concern.

Sepsis: SOFA (Sequential Organ Failure Assessment) and qSOFA (quick SOFA: altered mentation, systolic blood pressure ≤100 mmHg, respiratory rate ≥22/min) help identify patients at risk of poor outcomes. qSOFA is a bedside screen; however, pregnancy physiologic adaptations (baseline tachypnea, lower blood pressure) can affect thresholds. Use clinical judgment and obstetric sepsis protocols when SIRS or qSOFA criteria are met.

Shock index: The shock index (heart rate divided by systolic blood pressure) helps detect early hemodynamic compromise. Normal shock index is approximately 0.5–0.7. In obstetrics, a shock index ≥0.9 is associated with increased risk of severe hemorrhage and need for transfusion.

Imaging-based risk stratification: Transvaginal ultrasound features that warrant expedited evaluation include solid components, papillary excrescences, multiloculated cysts >10 cm, internal septations >3 mm, and significant vascular flow on Doppler. For adnexal torsion, absence of Doppler flow is concerning but not definitive. In suspected appendicitis, graded compression ultrasound is first-line; MRI is preferred over CT when ultrasound is non-diagnostic, as MRI avoids ionizing radiation.

Use scoring systems as adjuncts rather than replacements for clinical judgment, and adjust interpretation according to gestational age and physiologic changes of pregnancy.

# Integration of clinical, laboratory and imaging data into working diagnoses

Constructing a working diagnosis requires synthesizing history, physical exam, laboratory results, and imaging with awareness of pregnancy-specific caveats. Start with a problem list and prioritize life-threatening conditions. Ask: does the presentation indicate an emergent surgical process, an obstetric complication, an infectious source, or a benign lesion amenable to observation?

Laboratory evaluation should be targeted. Obtain complete blood count (CBC), basic metabolic panel, liver enzymes if indicated, coagulation studies if bleeding suspected, blood type and screen, lactate for perfusion assessment, and urinalysis/urine culture for urinary tract sources. Reference ranges shift in pregnancy: hemoglobin concentration commonly falls due to physiologic hemodilution (normal nonpregnant female hemoglobin 12–16 g/dL; during pregnancy mild anemia is common), leukocyte count increases—normal range in pregnancy may be up to 12,000–16,000/μL and can rise further in labor. CRP and ESR are nonspecific markers of inflammation that can be elevated in many conditions and are less specific in pregnancy. Serum CA-125 may be elevated in early pregnancy and with peritoneal irritation and thus has limited specificity. Use serial laboratory trends (e.g., rising leukocytosis, increasing lactate) to gauge progression.

Imaging is indispensable. Transvaginal ultrasound is first-line for adnexal masses, providing detail on cyst morphology, septations, solid components, and Doppler flow. Transabdominal ultrasound complements transvaginal approach for larger masses and for uterine assessment. If ultrasound is non-diagnostic, MRI without gadolinium is safe in pregnancy and excellent for evaluating adnexal masses, appendicitis, bowel obstruction, and soft tissue characterization, particularly in the second and third trimesters when ultrasound windows are limited. Computed tomography (CT) involves ionizing radiation but may be used if MRI is unavailable and the maternal benefit outweighs fetal risk—use shielding and lowest feasible dose. When evaluating for hemorrhage, focused assessment with sonography in trauma (FAST) can rapidly detect free fluid.

Integrate findings into probabilities and plan management accordingly. For example, a small, simple unilateral cyst (<5 cm) on ultrasound in the first trimester with minimal symptoms can be managed expectantly with follow-up ultrasound because many resolve spontaneously. Conversely, a complex mass with solid components, septations, or rapid growth raises concern for neoplasm and may prompt surgical consultation and consideration of operative intervention in the second trimester, which is the safest window for non-obstetric abdominal surgery. In suspected torsion, emphasize clinical urgency: even if Doppler flow is present, persistent severe unilateral pain and compatible exam may require operative exploration.

Document maternal-fetal considerations in the working diagnosis. Assess fetal status appropriate to gestational age: fetal heart rate monitoring for viability and well-being in the context of maternal instability or sepsis. Balance maternal stabilization with fetal considerations—maternal resuscitation takes priority because maternal instability endangers both. In decision-making about timing and approach to surgery, engage a multidisciplinary team (obstetrics, maternal-fetal medicine, anesthesia, surgery, neonatology) early.

Finally, establish a follow-up plan tailored to risk. For low-risk masses managed expectantly, schedule interval imaging and clear return precautions for red-flag symptoms. For intermediate-risk lesions, arrange expedited imaging (MRI), tumor marker measurement with pregnancy caveats, and specialist consultation. For high-risk or unstable patients, prioritize resuscitation and timely surgical or interventional management.

Normal or commonly referenced laboratory ranges and diagnostic thresholds relevant in pregnancy:
- Hemoglobin: nonpregnant 12–16 g/dL; mild physiologic anemia is common in pregnancy.
- White blood cell count: nonpregnant 4,000–11,000/μL; in pregnancy, values up to 12,000–16,000/μL (or higher in labor) may be physiologic.
- Shock index: normal approximately 0.5–0.7; obstetric concern when ≥0.9.
- qSOFA: 1–3 points (altered mentation, systolic BP ≤100 mmHg, RR ≥22/min); pregnancy may alter thresholds—use as adjunct.
- CA-125: normal <35 U/mL in nonpregnant adults, but may be elevated in early pregnancy and peritoneal irritation.

Clinical decision points to remember: when there is hemodynamic instability, peritoneal signs, high clinical suspicion for torsion, perforation, or ongoing hemorrhage, prioritize timely surgical evaluation. When presentation is indeterminate, rely on ultrasound and MRI rather than prolonged observation if symptoms are progressing. Use risk-stratification tools to inform but not dictate care, and always interpret tests in the physiologic context of pregnancy.

Conclusion

Evaluating pelvic and abdominal masses in pregnancy requires methodical history-taking, careful but adapted physical examination, and judicious use of laboratory and imaging modalities. A framework that categorizes potential etiologies by organ system assists in developing a differential, while awareness of red flags ensures prompt intervention when maternal or fetal safety is threatened. Clinical scoring systems and imaging-based models can support decision-making, but pregnancy alters test characteristics and normal ranges; therefore, clinical judgment and multidisciplinary coordination remain central to delivering safe, effective care.
