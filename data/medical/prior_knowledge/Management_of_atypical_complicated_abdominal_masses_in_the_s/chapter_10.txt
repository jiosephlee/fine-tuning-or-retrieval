Introduction

Surgical management of uterine and adnexal masses during pregnancy requires balancing maternal surgical goals (complete resection, preservation of reproductive function, and control of hemorrhage) with fetal safety. Physiologic changes of pregnancy—enlarged gravid uterus, increased blood volume, altered coagulation, and displacement of intra-abdominal organs—create technical challenges that influence choice of exposure, access, hemostatic technique and intraoperative monitoring. This chapter reviews operative techniques used for myomectomy and adnexal surgery in pregnant patients, describing principles of open and minimally invasive access, stepwise operative technique for myomectomy and ovarian cystectomy, and adjunctive measures to reduce blood loss, minimize uterine manipulation and shorten operative time. Emphasis is placed on pathophysiology, safe surgical decision-making, and the translation of basic surgical principles into the unique context of pregnancy.

# Open laparotomy techniques: incision types, exposure in gravid abdomen

Open laparotomy remains the most widely used approach for large masses, uncertain pathology, or when rapid access and maximal exposure are needed. In pregnancy, incision choice is governed by gestational age, size and location of the mass, need for uterine exteriorization, and anticipated intraoperative maneuvers.

Midline vertical incision: A midline vertical incision—extending from just below the umbilicus toward the pubis when required—provides the most extensile exposure for an enlarged gravid uterus and supracolic pathology. It allows rapid entry, easy extension, and minimal bleeding through linea alba. For second- or third-trimester patients or when multiple quadrants must be accessed, a vertical midline incision offers superior exposure compared with transverse incisions. Care must be taken when closing a vertical incision to approximate rectus fascia and minimize wound complications; the vertical incision also has implications for future pregnancies (e.g., classical cesarean risk) that should be discussed preoperatively when possible.

Low transverse (Pfannenstiel) incision: A low transverse suprapubic incision is cosmetically favorable and often feasible in early pregnancy when the uterus is not markedly enlarged. It provides excellent access to lower-pelvic masses and the adnexa but is limited for large fundal leiomyomas or masses extending above the pelvic brim. The Pfannenstiel approach may be preferable when the anticipated operative field is confined to the lower pelvis and there is minimal need for wide exposure.

Joel-Cohen and transverse midline alternatives: In some centers the Joel-Cohen or Maylard modifications are used for improved exposure while maintaining a transverse skin crease. The Maylard requires transection of the rectus muscles and is not commonly chosen in pregnancy unless wide lateral exposure is required and the surgeon is familiar with the technique.

Principles of exposure and uterine handling: Adequate exposure requires patient positioning with left lateral tilt (15 degrees) to avoid aortocaval compression in the supine gravid patient. A Foley catheter drains the bladder to decrease injury risk when operating near the lower uterine segment or adnexa. When feasible, avoid exteriorizing the gravid uterus excessively; if exteriorization is necessary for exposure, it should be performed gently and for the shortest duration. Retractor selection (e.g., self-retaining retractors with blunt blades) and careful placement of surgical pads to support the uterus minimize pressure and maintain uteroplacental perfusion.

Hemodynamic considerations: Blood volume is increased during pregnancy but so is uterine blood flow, and placental perfusion is sensitive to maternal hypotension. Hemostasis is paramount; rapid control of bleeding limits transfusion needs and reduces the chance of uterine irritability. Preoperative planning includes type and crossmatch (common practical target is at least type and screen with crossmatch if significant bleeding is likely), and baseline labs: hemoglobin 12–16 g/dL (nonpregnant; pregnancy values may be slightly lower), hematocrit approximately 36–48% in nonpregnant women (physiologic hemodilution lowers hematocrit in pregnancy), platelet count 150–450 ×10^9/L, and coagulation studies as clinically indicated.

# Laparoscopic approaches: indications, port placement modifications, insufflation considerations and open Hasson entry

Laparoscopy during pregnancy can be safe and effective when patient selection and technique are adapted to gestational changes. Indications include symptomatic adnexal masses (torsion, large persistent cysts of uncertain character), small to moderate-size pedunculated fibroids causing pain, and situations where diagnostic uncertainty persists. Contraindications include hemodynamic instability and very large masses or uterine size that prohibit safe insufflation and visualization.

Timing and gestational age: The second trimester (approximately 14–20 weeks) is generally the optimal window for elective minimally invasive surgery because organogenesis is complete and the uterus is not yet too large to impede laparoscopic visualization. Emergency procedures (e.g., adnexal torsion) may be performed at any gestational age if clinically necessary.

Entry technique and port placement modifications: The gravid uterus alters standard port-entry landmarks. For safety, initial peritoneal access is often performed with an open (Hasson) technique or at alternative entry sites to avoid uterine injury.

- Open Hasson entry: The Hasson technique entails a small incision to the fascia under direct vision, blunt dissection down to the peritoneum, placement of a blunt trocar secured with sutures, and controlled establishment of pneumoperitoneum. This reduces the risk of blind insufflation-related uterine or visceral injury. Surgeons commonly place the Hasson trocar at an alternative site—commonly supraumbilical or at Palmer's point (3 cm below the left costal margin in the midclavicular line)—depending on uterine size. Direct visualization is essential during deep dissection.

- Palmer’s point and left upper quadrant entry: For patients with an enlarged uterus displacing the umbilicus cranially, left upper quadrant entry at Palmer’s point is a safe alternative. Care must be taken to avoid a distended stomach (preoperative nasogastric decompression if necessary) and to be aware of splenic anatomy.

- Optical trocars: When used, optical trocars allow visualization during penetration but still require careful consideration of uterine position and remaining working space.

Port placement: As pregnancy advances, ports are shifted cephalad. Typical modifications include placing the primary camera port 2–3 cm above the umbilicus in early pregnancy, and higher (subxiphoid region) in later pregnancy if necessary. Working ports are similarly placed with more cephalad and lateral distribution to optimize triangulation and avoid pressure on the uterus. All ports should be inserted at angles that minimize upward force and pressure on the uterine fundus.

Insufflation pressure considerations: Pneumoperitoneum increases intra-abdominal pressure and can reduce venous return, cardiac output and uteroplacental blood flow. Published experience supports using the lowest insufflation pressure that provides an adequate operative field. Common recommendations are to use carbon dioxide pneumoperitoneum at pressures of 10–12 mmHg, often targeting 8–12 mmHg. In late pregnancy or when venous return is a concern, lower pressures (8–10 mmHg) are preferred. Duration of pneumoperitoneum should be minimized, and gasless laparoscopy is an option in centers with the requisite equipment and experience.

Anesthetic and fetal considerations during laparoscopy: Consultation with anesthesia is essential. Maternal capnography and end-tidal CO2 monitoring are critical because CO2 absorption increases during pneumoperitoneum; hypercapnia should be avoided. Fetal monitoring protocols vary with gestational age; continuous fetal monitoring may be considered for viable fetuses perioperatively when feasible, and neonatology should be available if preterm delivery is a possibility. Tocolytics are not routinely indicated but may be used for uterine irritability in select situations in consultation with obstetrics.

# Steps of myomectomy: pedicle control, enucleation, uterine repair and hemostasis

Indications for myomectomy in pregnancy are limited and typically involve symptomatic fibroids causing refractory pain, degenerative change with sepsis risk, rapid growth with concern for malignancy, or pedunculated lesions at risk of torsion. When performed, a meticulous, stepwise approach reduces bleeding and preserves uterine integrity.

Preoperative planning: Preoperative imaging (ultrasound with Doppler, MRI if needed) helps define size, number, location and relationship of fibroids to the uterine cavity and placenta. Blood availability should be considered and the team prepared for rapid transfusion if necessary.

Pedicle control and devascularization: Control of blood supply is a critical first step. Techniques include:

- Local vasoconstrictor injection: Diluted vasopressin (commonly 0.05–0.5 U/mL) injected into the myometrium adjacent to the fibroid reduces bleeding by local vasoconstriction. In pregnancy, caution is required: systemic absorption can cause maternal bradycardia, hypertension, and coronary or uteroplacental vasospasm. Use small doses, dilute the solution, aspirate before injection, and monitor hemodynamics closely. Some centers avoid vasopressin in pregnancy or use lower concentrations.

- Temporary mechanical devascularization: Use of atraumatic clamps (e.g., bulldog clamps) on the uterine arteries or application of tourniquets/suture loops (e.g., around the lower uterine segment and fundus) may be used to decrease uterine perfusion temporarily during enucleation. These techniques reduce bleeding without systemic pharmacologic effects but require familiarity and caution regarding ischemic time.

- Occlusion of vascular pedicles in pedunculated fibroids: Ligation or clamping of a stalk is straightforward when present (see below under management of pedunculated lesions).

Enucleation: After devascularization, a precise incision over the most prominent portion of the fibroid is made (typically a linear serosal incision). The pseudocapsule is identified, and blunt-sharp dissection is used to shell out the fibroid. Gentle traction and countertraction facilitate dissection along the cleavage plane, minimizing myometrial damage. When multiple fibroids are present, prioritize removal of symptomatic or large lesions; extensive myomectomy with multiple uterine incisions can increase bleeding and risk uterine rupture in subsequent pregnancy, so surgical goals must be individualized.

Uterine repair: Layered, secure uterine closure restores anatomy and decreases risk of bleeding and future uterine rupture. Repairs generally include:

- Deep myometrial approximation: Interrupted or continuous absorbable sutures (e.g., polyglactin 1 or 0) to reapproximate deep myometrium, obliterating dead space. Multiple layers may redistribute tension and support hemostasis.

- Serosal closure: Reapproximate serosa with fine absorbable sutures (e.g., 2-0 or 3-0) taking care to invert serosal edges; some favor continuous locked suturing for a hemostatic seal, others prefer interrupted sutures to minimize ischemic tension.

- Avoid excessive suture bulk and ischemia: Excessively tight sutures or obliteration of the pseudocapsule can lead to poor healing and scarring. A balance is required between hemostasis and preservation of tissue viability.

Hemostasis: Achieve meticulous hemostasis before closure. Techniques include direct vessel ligation, use of electrosurgical bipolar coagulation for small bleeding points, and application of topical hemostatic agents as adjuncts. Adequate hemostasis limits hematoma formation, infection risk, and uterine irritability.

Postoperative considerations: Monitor for uterine contractions and bleeding. Decisions about postoperative tocolysis, thromboprophylaxis and duration of maternal-fetal monitoring depend on gestational age and intraoperative events.

# Management of pedunculated lesions and techniques to reduce blood loss (clamps, vasoconstrictors, hemostatic agents)

Pedunculated myomas or adnexal stalk lesions are often amenable to simpler approaches because of their discrete vascular pedicle.

Management of pedunculated myomas: Identify the base of the stalk and achieve secure hemostasis prior to transection. Techniques include:

- Ligation and excision: Double ligation of the stalk with absorbable suture proximal and distal to the intended transection site, then excise between ligatures. This provides reliable mechanical hemostasis without reliance on thermal energy.

- Clamping and oversewing: Apply an atraumatic clamp (e.g., Kelly or vascular clamp) to the stalk, then oversew the base with suture and transect. This is effective in open surgery; for laparoscopy, intracorporeal bougie-suture techniques or extracorporeal knot tying may be used.

- Bipolar coagulation: Bipolar energy can coagulate the pedicle before transection; it produces less lateral thermal spread than monopolar energy and is often preferred when vascular pedicles are small. Careful application with short bursts and visual confirmation of blanching is recommended.

Techniques to reduce blood loss more broadly:

- Mechanical vascular control: As above, temporary uterine artery occlusion with clamps or suture loops can significantly lower intraoperative blood loss. Some centers use tourniquet techniques around the lower uterine segment to decrease perfusion while the fibroid is enucleated; ischemia times should be minimized to limit myometrial injury.

- Local vasoconstrictors: Vasopressin injections can reduce bleeding at the dissection plane. Pregnant patients are at higher risk for systemic cardiovascular effects; therefore, doses are conservative, and anesthesia should be informed and prepared to treat hemodynamic changes.

- Topical hemostatic agents: Product classes include oxidized regenerated cellulose (e.g., Surgicel), gelatin-thrombin matrices (e.g., Floseal), and fibrin sealants. These adjuncts may be applied to raw myometrial surfaces to promote clot formation. They are adjunctive measures and do not substitute for proper surgical technique. Considerations in pregnancy include potential for local inflammation, adhesive disease, or thrombotic risk especially when using agents containing thrombin. Use absorbable agents and avoid overpacking.

- Hypotensive anesthesia: Some procedures use transient controlled hypotension to reduce blood loss in nonpregnant patients; in pregnancy this strategy is generally avoided because uteroplacental perfusion is pressure-dependent.

- Rapid but careful technique: Efficient enucleation and uterine repair shorten ischemic or clamp times and reduce cumulative blood loss.

# Ovarian cystectomy techniques in pregnancy and preservation of ovarian tissue

The majority of adnexal masses encountered in pregnancy are benign functional cysts or dermoids; however, persistent or suspicious lesions may require removal. The surgical objective is removal of the mass with preservation of ovarian tissue and function when possible.

Preoperative assessment: Ultrasound with Doppler is first-line for characterization of cysts. Tumor markers (e.g., CA-125) have limited specificity in pregnancy because levels may change; values should be interpreted cautiously. Indications for surgical management include torsion, large persistent cysts (commonly >6–10 cm depending on symptoms and morphology), rapid growth, or sonographic features suspicious for malignancy (solid components, papillary projections, septations, increased vascular flow).

Cystectomy technique: The general principles are similar to nonpregnant patients but adapted to minimize uterine manipulation and ovarian ischemia.

- Access and exposure: For laparoscopic cystectomy, port placement is modified cephalad as needed; for open cystectomy, the incision is chosen for adequate exposure with minimal uterine handling. If possible, avoid aggressive manipulation of the uterus.

- Incision on ovarian cortex: A cortical incision is made away from the hilum when possible. Sharp dissection delineates cyst wall from ovarian stroma.

- Enucleation: Use gentle blunt dissection with traction-countertraction to shell out the cyst. For endometriomas, careful cleavage along the capsule and preservation of ovarian tissue is important; however, endometrioma surgery in pregnancy is rarely required.

- Hemostasis and ovarian preservation: Hemostasis of the ovarian parenchyma can be achieved with fine sutures (e.g., 4-0 or 5-0 absorbable) placed to approximate ovarian tissue, thereby minimizing thermal injury from electrosurgery. Bipolar coagulation can be used selectively for small bleeding points but should be limited because thermal spread reduces ovarian reserve. For cysts with problematic bleeding or friable stroma, hemostatic agents may be applied. Intracorporeal suturing techniques that approximate the ovarian cortex preserve ovarian tissue architecture better than fulguration.

- Specimen retrieval: Remove the cyst intact or in an endoscopic retrieval bag to avoid spillage of cyst contents (particularly dermoid elements or mucinous cyst content). Avoid power morcellation in pregnancy because of risk of tissue dissemination and because the uterine size and fetal safety concerns complicate its use.

- Oophorectomy considerations: Reserve oophorectomy for cases where ovarian preservation is not feasible (e.g., extensive destruction of ovarian tissue, uncontrolled bleeding, or high suspicion for malignancy). When bilateral oophorectomy is considered, the implications for endocrine function and fetal effects must be discussed preoperatively if time allows.

# Strategies to shorten operative time and reduce uterine manipulation

Reducing operative time and minimizing uterine manipulation are important to limit fetal exposure to anesthesia, maternal blood loss, and uterine irritability that can provoke contractions. Strategies include:

- Preoperative planning and team coordination: Detailed preoperative mapping of lesion location via imaging, preparation of appropriate instruments, and briefing of the anesthesia and nursing teams reduce intraoperative delays. Having blood products available and ready tubing and warming devices prepared also streamlines care.

- Surgical approach selection: Choose the least invasive approach that allows safe and effective resection. When laparoscopy can accomplish the goals safely, it often leads to shorter recovery and less postoperative pain, but the operative time may be longer if the team is not experienced. Experienced laparoscopic teams can perform adnexal operations quickly with less uterine manipulation.

- Limit uterine handling: Use atraumatic graspers on adnexal structures rather than grasping the uterus. Avoid repeated exteriorization of the uterus during open procedures. When uterine palpation is necessary to locate fibroids, gentle external compression and palpation by the assistant can be used instead of internal traction.

- Efficient hemostasis: Apply devascularization techniques early in the case so dissection can proceed with minimal interruption. Preemptive local vasoconstrictor, appropriate use of clamps and early suturing decrease bleeding and the need for time-consuming cautery.

- Use of energy devices judiciously: Advanced bipolar or vessel-sealing devices can speed dissection and coagulation for small to moderate vessels, but remember their use must be balanced against potential thermal injury and effects on adjacent tissues.

- Parallel tasking by the operative team: While the primary surgeon completes the critical dissection, assistants can prepare suture material, hemostatic agents, and plans for specimen retrieval to avoid pauses.

- Limit number of lesions removed: When multiple small fibroids are present but asymptomatic, consider removing only the dominant or symptomatic lesions; extensive myomectomy increases operative time and may not confer benefit during pregnancy.

# Use and risks of electrosurgery and topical hemostatic adjuncts in pregnancy

Electrosurgery is a powerful tool for cutting and coagulation, but in pregnancy its use requires special caution because of potential effects on maternal tissues and theoretical concerns about fetal exposure.

Electrosurgery types and considerations:

- Monopolar electrosurgery: Monopolar current passes from the active electrode through tissues to a dispersive grounding pad. Monopolar energy can produce greater lateral thermal spread and poses a theoretical risk of distant current flow; it is generally avoided for direct uterine myometrial fulguration during pregnancy because of potential for deeper tissue thermal injury. If monopolar is used, use low power settings and short activation intervals, and avoid direct application to the gravid uterus where unnecessary.

- Bipolar electrosurgery: Bipolar devices confine current between two jaws, producing less lateral thermal spread and greater precision. Bipolar coagulation is preferred for coagulating small bleeding vessels on the uterus or ovaries. Vessel-sealing devices (advanced bipolar) can be efficient for pedicle control but should be used with awareness of lateral spread and potential for thermal injury to adjacent structures.

- Harmonic and ultrasonic devices: These use ultrasonic vibration to coagulate and cut tissue with minimal electric current. They can be associated with less smoke and may produce less lateral thermal spread, but data in pregnancy are limited. When used, application times should be minimized.

Risks and mitigation strategies: Thermal injury to the myometrium or ovarian cortex can impair tissue viability and fertility. In the uterus, deep thermal damage can create areas of necrosis that may compromise uterine integrity. To mitigate risks:

- Prefer mechanical hemostasis (suture ligation, clamps) when feasible, especially for larger vessels or where preservation of tissue is critical.

- Use bipolar rather than monopolar energy near the uterus and adnexa.

- Apply the lowest effective power settings and activate energy for the shortest duration necessary.

- Maintain distance from the fetus and placenta; identify placental location preoperatively to avoid inadvertent thermal injury when operating near the lower uterine segment.

Topical hemostatic adjuncts:

- Oxidized regenerated cellulose (e.g., Surgicel): Provides a scaffold for clot formation, is absorbable, and is commonly used on raw myometrial surfaces. It can be left in situ but should be applied sparingly to avoid mass effect.

- Gelatin-thrombin matrices (e.g., Floseal): These promote clot formation and are effective for diffuse oozing. They contain biologically active thrombin, and although topical, there is a theoretical risk of systemic coagulation if used excessively, especially in patients with predisposing clotting disorders.

- Fibrin sealants: Mimic the final steps of coagulation and create a fibrin clot. They can be effective adjuncts but are relatively expensive and may induce mild local inflammatory reactions.

Safety in pregnancy: Overall, topical hemostatic agents are considered safe for use in pregnancy when used appropriately. Adhesive formation is a theoretical concern after any intra-abdominal surgery. There is limited evidence of systemic fetal effects from topically applied agents, but prudence dictates minimal necessary use and avoidance of agents associated with higher thrombotic risk when maternal coagulopathy or thrombophilia exists.

Conclusion

Successful myomectomy and adnexal surgery during pregnancy require a nuanced appreciation of altered maternal anatomy and physiology, fetal considerations, and careful operative technique. Selection of open versus minimally invasive approaches is individualized based on lesion size, suspicion for malignancy, gestational age, and surgeon expertise. Key operative principles include minimizing uterine manipulation, achieving early devascularization, meticulous enucleation and layered uterine repair, and using mechanical hemostasis and topical adjuncts judiciously. Electrosurgical tools and hemostatic agents can shorten operative time and control bleeding but must be used with an understanding of their risks and limitations in pregnancy. Preoperative planning, multidisciplinary coordination (obstetrics, anesthesia, neonatology), and intraoperative vigilance for maternal hemodynamic stability and fetal well-being underpin safe surgical care in this unique population.
