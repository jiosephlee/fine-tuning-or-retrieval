Introduction

Imaging of the pregnant patient with abdominal or pelvic symptoms poses unique diagnostic and safety challenges. Clinicians must balance the need for accurate characterization of masses or acute pathology against minimizing fetal risk from ionizing radiation, thermal effects and potentially teratogenic agents. Ultrasound and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) are the principal non-ionizing imaging modalities used in pregnancy. Ultrasound—transabdominal and transvaginal—remains the first-line modality for pelvic and many abdominal disorders because of its availability, real-time capability and lack of ionizing radiation. When ultrasound is inconclusive or additional tissue characterization is needed, non-contrast MRI is an excellent second-line modality because of its superior soft-tissue contrast and multiplanar capability without ionizing radiation. This chapter reviews practical techniques for optimizing ultrasound in pregnancy, sonographic appearances of common uterine and adnexal lesions, the role of color and power Doppler in assessing vascularity, MRI physics basics relevant to sequence selection and interpretation, MRI appearances of leiomyomas and adnexal lesions, safety considerations for MRI (including field strength and gadolinium), when CT may be considered and the fetal risks associated with ionizing radiation, and how to integrate imaging findings into clinical decision-making.

# Transabdominal and transvaginal ultrasound techniques and optimization in pregnancy

Ultrasound in pregnancy requires deliberate adjustment of technique to compensate for gravid anatomy and the goals of the examination. Transabdominal (TA) ultrasound uses a low-frequency curved-array transducer (typically 2–5 MHz) to evaluate large fields of view including the uterus, adnexa and the upper pelvis; transvaginal (TV) ultrasound uses a higher-frequency endovaginal probe (typically 5–9 MHz) to provide high-resolution images of pelvic structures close to the probe. Both approaches are often complementary: TA for overall survey and for larger masses or when the uterus has been displaced, TV for high-resolution assessment of uterine contour, endometrium/cervical canal and adnexal detail.

When performing TA ultrasound in pregnancy, patient positioning and probe selection are essential. A comfortably full bladder can be helpful in early pregnancy to act as an acoustic window, displacing bowel gas and allowing visualization of the pelvic organs; however, in later pregnancy a full bladder is usually unnecessary and can be uncomfortable. Probe frequency should be chosen to balance penetration and resolution; in obese patients or with deep-seated lesions a lower frequency may be required. Scan planes should be systematic: midline sagittal and multiple transverse images from fundus to cervix, and additional oblique planes as anatomy dictates. Sweeps should include coronal oblique views when evaluating adnexal masses that sit lateral to the uterus.

Transvaginal ultrasound provides superior spatial resolution for structures within the pelvis and should be used whenever detailed characterization of the adnexa or junctional zone is required and when patient tolerance permits. The TV probe is inserted into the anterior vagina with the patient in lithotomy position. Gentle systematic sweeps in sagittal and transverse orientations are used to inspect each ovary, the uterine fundus, corpus and cervix. Doppler interrogation is typically performed through the TV approach for small vascular structures.

Optimization strategies for image quality include adjusting gain, dynamic range, focal zones and depth. Reduce depth to place the structure of interest in the center of the image and set focal zones at or just below the structure to sharpen resolution. Time-gain compensation can help compensate for depth-dependent attenuation. If tissue-harmonic imaging is available, it often improves image contrast and reduces near-field artifacts in TA imaging. For TV studies, decreasing the field of view and increasing line density increases spatial resolution.

Patient safety and comfort considerations specific to pregnancy include minimizing mechanical index (MI) and thermal index (TI) where possible—both of which are displayed on modern ultrasound units. Although diagnostic ultrasound has an excellent safety record, the principle of ALARA (as low as reasonably achievable) applies. Avoid prolonged dwell time with spectral Doppler over the fetal head or abdomen, and limit high-power Doppler exposure in early pregnancy.

# Sonographic features of fibroids, endometriomas and ovarian cysts

Recognizing typical sonographic patterns of common pelvic masses reduces diagnostic uncertainty and guides management. Leiomyomas (uterine fibroids), endometriomas and functional or benign ovarian cysts are frequent in reproductive-age women and may coexist with pregnancy.

Leiomyomas. On ultrasound, leiomyomas are generally well-circumscribed, round-to-oval, solid masses arising from the myometrium. Classic sonographic features include a heterogeneous, hypoechoic mass compared with surrounding myometrium, with a well-defined pseudocapsule. Calcifications produce echogenic foci with posterior acoustic shadowing. Subserosal fibroids may be pedunculated and located external to the uterine contour; submucosal fibroids project into the endometrial cavity and can be better characterized with TV ultrasound or saline sonohysterography when not pregnant. In pregnancy, fibroids can change in size (typically increase slightly) because of pregnancy hormones and vascular changes; degenerative changes occur and alter sonographic appearance. Cystic degeneration produces mixed cystic and solid echotexture; red degeneration (hemorrhagic infarction) can produce central hypoechoic or heterogeneous areas with variable echogenicity depending on the age of hemorrhage. Distinguishing degenerated fibroids from adnexal pathology can be challenging; their uterine attachment and continuity of myometrial tissue help identify them.

Endometriomas. Endometriomas (ovarian "chocolate cysts") typically have a characteristic sonographic appearance: a unilocular or multilocular cystic ovarian mass with diffuse low-level internal echoes, often described as “ground-glass” echogenicity. Thin septations may be present; solid mural nodules are uncommon and, when present, should prompt careful evaluation because they raise concern for neoplasm. Endometriomas are usually avascular or have low peripheral vascularity on Doppler. They are commonly multiple and bilateral. In pregnancy their appearance may remain stable or change due to altered hormonal milieu.

Ovarian cysts. Functional ovarian cysts seen in reproductive-age women include follicular cysts and corpus luteum cysts. Follicular cysts are typically anechoic, unilocular and thin-walled with posterior acoustic enhancement. Corpus luteum cysts can be more complex with thicker walls, internal echoes and peripheral vascularity (“ring of fire” on color Doppler) and may measure several centimeters. Hemorrhagic cysts exhibit internal reticular, lace-like echogenic strands and may demonstrate fluid-fluid levels; acute hemorrhage can be echogenic and more solid-appearing. Dermoid cysts (mature cystic teratomas) often show heterogeneous echotexture with echogenic Rokitansky protuberances, dermoid mesh (linear hyperechoic interfaces), calcifications and posterior acoustic shadowing due to fat or bone. Simple cysts are usually benign if <5–10 cm and without concerning features. Complex cystic adnexal masses with solid components, papillary projections, nodularity, thick septations >3 mm, or increased vascularity on Doppler raise suspicion for neoplasm and warrant further evaluation.

# Role of color and power Doppler in characterizing vascularity

Color and power Doppler ultrasound provide physiologic information about blood flow that complements gray-scale morphology. Color Doppler encodes flow direction and velocity, while power Doppler maps total flow amplitude and is more sensitive to low-velocity flow but lacks directional information. Spectral Doppler can quantify flow velocities and waveform characteristics (e.g., peak systolic velocity, end-diastolic velocity, resistive index).

In pelvic imaging during pregnancy, Doppler is used to assess viability of adnexal structures (e.g., torsion), vascularity of masses to help differentiate benign from malignant lesions, and to examine uterine or placental blood flow when relevant. For adnexal torsion, absence or markedly reduced venous and arterial flow within the ovary supports the diagnosis, but normal Doppler flow does not exclude torsion because of dual ovarian arterial supply and early arterial flow preservation. Increasing ovarian enlargement with peripheral follicles, stromal edema and decreased venous outflow are complementary grayscale signs.

Doppler indices can assist in lesion characterization. Benign lesions such as hemorrhagic cysts, endometriomas and simple cysts tend to have low-flow or peripheral flow patterns with relatively high resistive indices (RI). Malignant ovarian neoplasms often demonstrate increased central vascularity with low RI (typically RI < 0.4–0.5) and low pulsatility index (PI), reflecting neoangiogenesis. However, overlap exists: corpus luteum cysts can have prominent peripheral flow with low RI (“ring of fire”), and inflammatory masses can be hypervascular. Power Doppler is particularly useful for detecting low-flow within solid components or septations and for mapping tumor vascularity, whereas color Doppler helps localize direction and turbulent flow.

Technical factors influence Doppler sensitivity: angle of insonation, pulse repetition frequency (PRF), wall filter settings, gain and probe frequency. To detect low-velocity flow reduce PRF, use low wall filters and increase color gain carefully to avoid noise. Power Doppler is preferred when flow is weak or in obese patients. Always document Doppler indices (peak systolic velocity, end-diastolic velocity, RI, PI) when using spectral Doppler as part of an objective record.

Doppler use in early pregnancy should adhere to ALARA principles; avoid prolonged spectral Doppler sampling over the fetal head and keep MI and TI as low as practical.

# MRI physics basics relevant to T1, T2, fat-saturation sequences and interpretation

Understanding basic MRI physics is important for selecting sequences and interpreting pelvic and abdominal pathology. MRI signal depends primarily on proton density and relaxation properties of tissues: longitudinal relaxation (T1) and transverse relaxation (T2). Sequences are designed to emphasize differences in these relaxation times.

T1-weighted sequences produce images in which fat has high signal (bright) and fluid is relatively low signal (dark). T1-weighted imaging is useful for detecting fat, subacute blood (methemoglobin), and assessing anatomy with good contrast between fat and soft tissues. T2-weighted sequences make fluid bright and are sensitive to edema, cystic components and inflammation. Most pelvic MRI protocols for characterization include both T1-weighted and T2-weighted sequences in multiple planes.

Fat-suppression techniques selectively reduce the high signal from fat to unmask other high-signal components such as fluid or blood. Chemical fat saturation (frequency-selective) exploits the difference in precessional frequency between fat and water protons; it works well at homogenous field conditions but can be less effective in regions with B0 inhomogeneity. Short tau inversion recovery (STIR) is an inversion recovery method that nulls fat signal and is relatively robust to inhomogeneity but is not compatible with gadolinium contrast evaluation because it also suppresses contrast-enhanced fat. Dixon methods (in-phase and out-of-phase imaging with subsequent reconstruction) allow both water-only and fat-only images and provide useful fat quantification and reliable fat-suppression across varying field conditions.

Other sequences commonly used include gradient-echo sequences for dynamic or cine imaging, diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) for assessing cellularity and restricted diffusion (helpful in characterizing malignant tissue or abscess), and susceptibility-weighted imaging which accentuates blood products and calcifications via magnetic susceptibility differences. DWI sequences produce apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) maps which are quantitative; tissues with restricted diffusion (low ADC) suggest high cellularity (e.g., many malignancies) or acute hemorrhage.

Important technical considerations include the specific absorption rate (SAR), which reflects energy deposition in tissue and is proportional to field strength and sequence parameters (flip angle, TR, etc.). Higher field strengths (e.g., 3 Tesla) produce higher SAR and potentially greater tissue heating—an important safety consideration in pregnancy. B0 (main magnetic field) and B1 (radiofrequency) inhomogeneities impact fat-saturation performance and image uniformity; sequence selection should account for these effects.

Interpretation principles: T1-hyperintense signal may indicate fat or subacute blood (methemoglobin), or proteinaceous content. T2-hyperintense signal generally indicates fluid or edema; T2 hypointensity in the myometrium or a lesion often reflects compact smooth muscle or fibrous tissue (e.g., leiomyoma). Combining multiple sequences (T1, T2, fat-sat, DWI) allows differentiating hemorrhagic, fatty, cystic and solid components.

# MRI features of leiomyomas, cystic degeneration, hemorrhage and adnexal lesions

MRI provides excellent tissue characterization of uterine and adnexal pathology and is particularly helpful when ultrasound is limited by body habitus, gravid uterus or complex appearances.

Leiomyomas. On MRI, typical leiomyomas are well-circumscribed masses arising from the myometrium with low-to-intermediate signal intensity on T2-weighted images relative to normal myometrium, and low-to-intermediate signal on T1-weighted images. The low T2 signal reflects compact smooth muscle and variable collagen. Degenerative changes alter signal characteristics: cystic degeneration produces high T2 signal centrally (reflecting fluid) with correspondingly low T1 signal; myxoid degeneration shows heterogeneous T2 hyperintensity. Red degeneration (hemorrhagic infarction) produces high signal on T1-weighted images due to intracellular and extracellular methemoglobin and variable T2 signal depending on stage. Adenomyosis is characterized by thickening of the junctional zone (>12 mm in many criteria) with small high-signal foci on T2 representing ectopic endometrial glands or small hemorrhagic foci; distinguishing small intramural fibroids from adenomyosis can be aided by evaluating the junctional zone contour and architecture.

Cystic degeneration within fibroids can produce large central areas of high T2 signal and variable T1 signal. When a fibroid undergoes hyaline degeneration, it may remain T2 hypointense but with increased heterogeneity. Pedunculated subserosal fibroids are identified by a pedicle connecting them to the uterus; this connection helps differentiate them from adnexal masses.

Hemorrhage. MRI is sensitive to blood products at different stages. Acute hemorrhage (oxyhemoglobin) may be isointense on T1 and hyperintense on T2. Subacute blood with intracellular methemoglobin becomes T1 hyperintense. Chronic blood products such as hemosiderin cause susceptibility effects and T2 hypointensity, which may be best seen on T2* or gradient-echo sequences. In the pelvis, hemorrhagic cysts (e.g., hemorrhagic ovarian cyst) demonstrate high T1 signal due to blood and variable T2 signal; fat-saturation sequences confirm whether T1 hyperintensity is due to fat (suppressed) or blood (not suppressed).

Adnexal lesions. MRI differentiates simple cysts, endometriomas, dermoids, and solid neoplasms. Simple cysts are uniformly T2 hyperintense and T1 hypointense, with no restricted diffusion or enhancement (in post-contrast studies). Endometriomas typically show high signal on T1-weighted images due to blood products that do not suppress with fat-sat, and variable shading on T2—often hypointense ("T2 shading") because of concentrated blood degradation products and protein. Dermoid cysts (mature teratomas) contain macroscopic fat and therefore show high T1 signal that suppresses with fat-saturation, and often show calcifications (signal voids) or Rokitansky protuberances. Ovarian torsion manifests as ovarian enlargement, peripheral displacement of follicles, stromal edema (T2 hyperintensity), and abnormal or absent perfusion on contrast-enhanced sequences; on DWI there may be restricted diffusion due to ischemia. Malignant ovarian masses often have solid components that are intermediate on T1 and variable on T2, may show restricted diffusion on DWI with low ADC values, and—when contrast is used—early enhancing solid components. In pregnancy, MRI protocols are usually non-contrast, so reliance on T1/T2 characteristics and DWI becomes more important.

# Safety of MRI in pregnancy: field strength considerations, contrast agents (gadolinium) and risks

MRI is generally considered safe in pregnancy when non-contrast techniques are used, but several safety issues require consideration: static magnetic field exposure, radiofrequency (RF) heating, acoustic noise, and the use of gadolinium-based contrast agents (GBCAs).

Field strength. Most clinical MRI in pregnancy is performed at 1.5 Tesla (T); 3T scanners provide higher signal-to-noise ratio and potentially improved spatial resolution but also increase SAR (and thus potential tissue heating) and may increase B1 inhomogeneity. Current guidelines suggest that MRI at 1.5T is safe throughout pregnancy for clinically indicated examinations. MRI at 3T can be used when the diagnostic benefit outweighs potential risks, but care should be taken to minimize SAR and monitor sequence parameters. In general, avoid nonessential high-SAR sequences (e.g., fast spin-echo with high flip angles) particularly in early pregnancy. The decision should involve discussion with radiology physicists for complex protocols.

RF heating and SAR. RF energy deposited during MRI can theoretically heat tissues. The fetus has immature thermoregulation, and temperature elevations are a concern. Scanner consoles display SAR estimates. Clinical practice aims to keep thermal index low and adhere to manufacturer recommendations. Shorter scan times, lower flip angles, and use of sequences with lower energy deposition reduce SAR.

Acoustic noise. MRI acoustic noise (rapid gradient switching) can be loud. There is limited evidence that acoustic noise could affect the fetal auditory system, especially during organogenesis, although most centers consider this risk low. Ear protection is impractical for the fetus; noise reduction by using quieter sequences and allowing maternal acoustic protection (headphones/earplugs) is reasonable.

Gadolinium-based contrast agents. GBCAs cross the placenta and enter fetal circulation; they are then excreted into the amniotic fluid and remain for prolonged periods. Animal studies have suggested potential fetal risks, and human observational data have raised concerns about an association between gadolinium exposure in pregnancy and a small increase in the risk of stillbirth, neonatal death, or certain inflammatory or rheumatologic conditions in early childhood. For these reasons, the general consensus and guidelines recommend avoiding gadolinium during pregnancy unless absolutely necessary—i.e., when the diagnostic information obtained with contrast will influence management and cannot be obtained by non-contrast MRI or ultrasound. If gadolinium is deemed essential, the lowest effective dose of a macrocyclic GBCA (which is more stable and less likely to release free gadolinium than linear agents) should be used. Informed consent documenting the risks and benefits is recommended. There is no routine recommendation for chelation or other interventions after in utero exposure.

Summary of practical safety points: use non-contrast MRI whenever possible; prefer 1.5T when diagnostic yield is adequate; tailor sequences to minimize SAR and scan duration; document indications and counseling if gadolinium is administered.

# When cross-sectional imaging (CT) is considered and associated fetal risks

Computed tomography (CT) uses ionizing radiation and is generally avoided in pregnancy for pelvic imaging when ultrasound and MRI are available. Nonetheless, CT has roles when rapid evaluation of certain acute conditions is necessary and when expected diagnostic benefit outweighs fetal radiation risk—for example, in suspected life-threatening maternal hemorrhage, complex trauma, pulmonary embolism when ventilation–perfusion scanning is unavailable or inconclusive, or when CT angiography is the fastest way to guide urgent intervention.

Radiation risk considerations revolve around stochastic effects (increased lifetime cancer risk) and deterministic effects (threshold-dose effects such as intellectual disability or malformations). Deterministic effects have threshold doses typically greater than 100 mGy. Most diagnostic CT examinations of the abdomen and pelvis deliver fetal doses far below this threshold. Typical fetal radiation exposures: a single abdominal/pelvic CT commonly results in fetal doses in the range of 10–50 mGy depending on technique, scanner, and maternal habitus, although there is wide variability. A fetal dose <50 mGy is generally not associated with a measurable increase in deterministic effects, but stochastic risk (small increase in childhood cancer risk) is proportional to dose and must be discussed. By comparison, radiation dose from a single chest CT is much lower to the pelvis and yields minimal fetal exposure unless imaging includes the abdomen/pelvis.

When CT is necessary, strategies to minimize fetal dose include limiting the scan range, shielding when possible (though internal fetal dose is not reduced by external shielding), using iterative reconstruction and dose-reduction algorithms, reducing tube voltage and current consistent with diagnostic quality, and avoiding multiphase imaging unless essential. Always document estimated fetal dose and provide counseling regarding risks. Repeat or multiple CT examinations increase cumulative dose and thus risks.

In pregnant patients, contrast-enhanced CT uses iodinated contrast agents that cross the placenta; current data do not show teratogenicity, but neonatal thyroid function may be transiently affected because of iodine load. Many guidelines recommend measuring neonatal thyroid function if significant iodinated contrast exposure occurred near delivery.

Overall, CT is reserved for situations where alternative imaging cannot provide timely or sufficient information for maternal care; when maternal indication is strong, maternal wellbeing takes precedence because maternal compromise poses the greatest risk to the fetus.

# Integration of imaging findings into clinical decision-making

Integration of imaging results into clinical management requires synthesis of sonographic and MRI data together with clinical presentation, laboratory values and gestational age. The approach to a pregnant patient with a pelvic mass or abdominal pain should be patient-centered, prioritizing maternal stabilization and then fetal considerations.

First-line evaluation typically begins with tailored ultrasound. If ultrasound clearly identifies a benign-appearing simple cyst (<5–10 cm with thin walls and anechoic content) in an asymptomatic patient, conservative management and follow-up imaging are often appropriate. Presence of acute pain, a very large adnexal mass, complex features (solid components, papillary projections, thick septations), or signs suggesting torsion (enlarged ovary, peripheral follicles, absent venous/arterial flow) prompt urgent gynecologic consultation and potentially surgical intervention. Doppler findings of absent or severely diminished flow support surgical consideration, but a normal Doppler does not exclude torsion—clinical suspicion remains paramount.

For uterine fibroids that are asymptomatic or cause mild symptoms, expectant management during pregnancy is common. When degeneration causes severe pain, supportive care is frequently effective; imaging helps exclude alternative causes. MRI is used when ultrasound is nondiagnostic—for example, to evaluate large or atypical uterine masses, define the location (submucosal vs intramural vs subserosal), or assess for red degeneration. MRI findings consistent with hemorrhagic degeneration (T1 hyperintensity) can explain acute pain and guide conservative therapy rather than immediate surgery.

When adnexal masses have concerning imaging features for neoplasm, MRI without contrast provides additional characterization: T1 hyperintensity that suppresses on fat-sat suggests a dermoid; persistent T1 hyperintensity not suppressed by fat-sat suggests blood (endometrioma); solid enhancing components (if contrast is used postpartum or in non-pregnant patient) and restricted diffusion raise suspicion for malignancy. If malignancy is suspected in pregnancy, multidisciplinary discussion including obstetrics, gynecologic oncology, neonatology and radiology is required to plan timing of intervention that balances maternal oncologic needs with fetal maturity.

Decision thresholds and specific interventions depend on gestational age. For example, in late pregnancy a mass may be managed expectantly until fetal viability or elective cesarean with concurrent surgical management. In early pregnancy suspected torsion or hemorrhage may demand urgent laparoscopy or laparotomy. Imaging informs these decisions: a small simple cyst in an asymptomatic first-trimester patient may be observed; a large complex mass causing pain or hemoperitoneum requires urgent action.

Documenting imaging limitations is essential: ultrasound may not visualize the entire pelvis when the gravid uterus displaces structures; MRI is superior for tissue characterization but typically performed without contrast in pregnancy. Radiology reports should explicitly state confidence levels, potential differential diagnoses, and recommended next steps (e.g., short-interval follow-up ultrasound, non-contrast MRI, or urgent surgical consultation).

Finally, counseling patients requires clear communication of risks and benefits. Discuss the significance of findings, the rationale for additional imaging (if needed), and potential maternal or fetal risks of imaging and any recommended procedures. When ionizing radiation or gadolinium is considered, provide documented informed consent and involve multidisciplinary teams.

Conclusion

Ultrasound and non-contrast MRI are complementary, safe, and powerful modalities for evaluating abdominal and pelvic masses in pregnancy when used thoughtfully. Mastery of transabdominal and transvaginal techniques plus appropriate use of Doppler optimizes ultrasound diagnostic yield. Knowledge of typical sonographic and MRI appearances of leiomyomas, endometriomas and adnexal lesions, together with MRI physics concepts (T1, T2, fat-sat, DWI) enables accurate lesion characterization and appropriate management planning. Safety considerations—especially regarding gadolinium and ionizing radiation—must guide imaging choices, always weighing diagnostic benefits against theoretical fetal risks. Integration of imaging findings with clinical context is essential for timely, patient-centered decision-making.
