Introduction

Pregnancy alters physiology and pharmacokinetics in ways that influence the safety and efficacy of medications used for the assessment and perioperative management of pelvic masses. Clinicians caring for pregnant patients must balance maternal benefit with fetal safety, integrate gestational-age–dependent risks, and choose agents that minimize teratogenicity, fetal toxicity, and obstetric complications. This chapter reviews pharmacologic options commonly encountered when evaluating or operating on pregnant patients with pelvic masses: analgesics, antibiotics, progesterone formulations for miscarriage and preterm birth prevention, iron therapy, transfusion considerations, and drugs that are contraindicated or require caution. For each class, we emphasize mechanism of action, pharmacokinetic considerations in pregnancy, typical dosing principles, safety profiles by trimester, and relevant clinical decision points.

# Analgesics: paracetamol, NSAIDs (limitations in pregnancy), opioid considerations

Analgesia in pregnancy must achieve adequate maternal comfort while minimizing fetal risk. Drug absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion change in pregnancy—plasma volume and glomerular filtration rate increase, and protein binding may change—so dosing and intervals sometimes require adjustment. Always aim for the lowest effective dose and shortest effective duration; however, undertreated maternal pain has physiologic and psychological consequences that can adversely affect both mother and fetus.

Paracetamol (acetaminophen). Paracetamol is the first-line analgesic in pregnancy because of its favorable safety profile, wide therapeutic index, and effectiveness for mild-to-moderate pain and fever. Mechanism of action is not fully understood but involves central inhibition of prostaglandin synthesis (COX inhibition in the CNS), modulation of serotonergic pathways, and cannabinoid receptor-mediated effects. Typical adult dosing is 500–1000 mg every 4–6 hours as needed, with a maximum of 3–4 g/day (many guidelines now recommend not exceeding 3 g/day to limit hepatotoxicity). In pregnancy, standard dosing is used but clinicians should counsel on avoiding chronic high-dose use. Epidemiologic studies have raised concerns about potential associations between prolonged prenatal paracetamol exposure and neurodevelopmental outcomes (e.g., attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, autism spectrum disorder), but these studies are subject to confounding and have not established causality; current recommendations still endorse paracetamol for short-term use when clinically indicated.

Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs). NSAIDs inhibit cyclooxygenase enzymes (COX-1 and COX-2), reducing prostaglandin synthesis. NSAIDs are effective analgesics and antipyretics, and perioperatively they reduce opioid requirements. However, in pregnancy their use is gestational-age dependent and limited by specific fetal risks. In the first trimester, some studies suggest a small increased risk of miscarriage and certain congenital anomalies with high-dose or prolonged NSAID exposure, though findings are inconsistent. The major concern is in the late second and third trimesters: NSAIDs can cause premature constriction or closure of the fetal ductus arteriosus due to reduced prostaglandin E2, and they can impair fetal renal perfusion leading to oligohydramnios. They can also inhibit uterine contractions and prolong labor. Thus, NSAIDs are generally avoided after 30 weeks’ gestation (many guidelines recommend avoiding them after 32 weeks, and some specify avoidance after 20–30 weeks depending on agent and dose), and should be used cautiously in the second trimester. When indicated in early pregnancy, use the lowest effective dose for the shortest duration. If NSAIDs are used near term, fetal echocardiography may be considered if prolonged exposure is suspected and there are signs of oligohydramnios or abnormal fetal Dopplers.

Opioids. Opioids remain important for moderate-to-severe acute pain and for perioperative analgesia when nonopioid options are insufficient. Mechanistically, opioids act as agonists at mu, kappa, and delta opioid receptors to reduce ascending pain signaling and alter emotional pain perception. Short-term, intermittent opioid use during pregnancy (e.g., perioperative) has not been clearly linked to major congenital anomalies, but chronic opioid exposure, especially in the first trimester, has been associated in some studies with increased risk of certain malformations and adverse neonatal outcomes. Late-pregnancy opioid exposure poses the risk of neonatal respiratory depression at delivery and neonatal opioid withdrawal syndrome (NOWS) if exposure is prolonged. Clinical principles include: use the lowest effective dose for the shortest duration, avoid combination with benzodiazepines, counsel patients about potential neonatal effects, and ensure obstetric and neonatal teams are aware of opioid exposure near delivery. For acute perioperative pain, short-acting opioids (e.g., fentanyl, morphine in single perioperative doses) are commonly used; dosing must consider increased volume of distribution and altered clearance in pregnancy. Regional analgesia (e.g., neuraxial techniques) should be used when appropriate to reduce systemic opioid requirements.

Clinical decision points. For mild pain, prefer paracetamol. Reserve NSAIDs for early pregnancy only when benefits outweigh risks and avoid in the late second and third trimesters. Use opioids sparingly, with anticipatory planning for neonatal monitoring if exposure occurs close to delivery. Document gestational age and counsel the patient on fetal risks.

# Antibiotics: classes considered safe in pregnancy, selection for urinary or surgical infections

Antibiotic selection in pregnancy prioritizes agents with established safety profiles, appropriate penetration to the site of infection, and stewardship principles to limit resistance. Many commonly used antibiotics are safe in pregnancy; others are contraindicated or used only with caution because of potential teratogenicity, effects on fetal development, or neonatal toxicity.

Beta-lactams (penicillins and cephalosporins). These are first-line for many infections in pregnancy because of robust safety data. Mechanism: inhibition of bacterial cell wall synthesis via binding to penicillin-binding proteins. Penicillins (e.g., amoxicillin, ampicillin) and first- to third-generation cephalosporins (e.g., cefazolin, cephalexin, cefuroxime, ceftriaxone) are widely used. For urinary tract infections (UTIs) and surgical prophylaxis related to pelvic masses, cephazolin 1–2 g IV at induction is standard for many procedures. For complicated urinary infections or pyelonephritis, ceftriaxone 1–2 g IV daily is commonly used.

Macrolides. Azithromycin and erythromycin are generally considered safe when needed; clarithromycin has some concerns and is often avoided. Macrolides inhibit bacterial protein synthesis at the 50S ribosomal subunit. Use azithromycin for atypical infections or in penicillin-allergic patients when appropriate.

Nitrofurantoin and fosfomycin for UTI. Nitrofurantoin (macrocrystalline formulation, typically 100 mg twice daily for 5–7 days) is effective for lower UTIs and is generally safe during pregnancy except some recommend avoiding it at term (38–42 weeks) and during labor because of theoretical risk of neonatal hemolysis in G6PD deficiency and rare reports of maternal pulmonary reactions. Fosfomycin (single 3 g dose) is another option for uncomplicated cystitis. Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX) is effective for UTIs but has cautions: avoid in the first trimester (folate antagonist potentially associated with congenital anomalies) and near term (risk of neonatal kernicterus from displacement of bilirubin). If used for essential indications, folate supplementation and informed discussion are required.

Aminoglycosides. Agents such as gentamicin are associated with fetal ototoxicity and nephrotoxicity in prolonged therapy; however, short courses for severe infections can be used with careful monitoring when no safer alternative exists. They act by inhibiting the 30S ribosomal subunit. Use in pregnancy is reserved for severe infections (e.g., gram-negative sepsis) and combined with beta-lactams for synergy where indicated.

Metronidazole. Historically controversial in the first trimester, more recent data support its safety for treatment of trichomoniasis, bacterial vaginosis, and anaerobic infections; concern remains theoretical for mutagenicity but clinical use is common when indicated.

Fluoroquinolones. Generally avoided in pregnancy because of concerns from animal data regarding arthropathy in offspring and limited human safety data. In some severe, resistant infections where no alternatives exist, they may be used with caution.

Selection for urinary infections. For asymptomatic bacteriuria in pregnancy, screen and treat because untreated bacteriuria increases risk of pyelonephritis and preterm birth. First-line options include nitrofurantoin (avoid at term) and beta-lactams based on sensitivity (e.g., cephalexin). For acute pyelonephritis, hospitalize and treat with IV ceftriaxone or a beta-lactam; add gentamicin only if needed. Duration typically 7–14 days depending on clinical response.

Perioperative and surgical infections. Surgical prophylaxis for gynecologic and obstetric procedures commonly uses cefazolin 1–2 g IV within 60 minutes of incision; for beta-lactam allergy, clindamycin plus gentamicin is an option. For intra-abdominal or pelvic infections, select agents covering gram-negative bacilli and anaerobes—e.g., cefoxitin or a combination of ceftriaxone plus metronidazole. For severe sepsis, follow local sepsis protocols but favor beta-lactams with established pregnancy safety.

Clinical decision points. Always check local resistance patterns. For penicillin-allergic patients, ascertain the nature of the allergy because many labeled allergies are not true IgE–mediated reactions; skin testing or graded challenge may allow safer beta-lactam use. Avoid TMP-SMX in first trimester and near delivery, avoid fluoroquinolones and tetracyclines (tetracyclines cause permanent enamel and bone effects), and use aminoglycosides only when necessary for short durations with monitoring.

# Progesterone formulations for prevention of miscarriage/preterm birth: IM vs vaginal, dosing principles

Progesterone plays a central role in maintaining pregnancy by promoting uterine quiescence, modulating immune tolerance, and supporting decidualization. Pharmacologic progesterone supplementation is used in specific obstetric contexts: prevention of recurrent miscarriage in selected cases, and prevention of preterm birth in women with prior spontaneous preterm birth or a short cervix.

Formulations and routes. Progesterone is available in several formulations: vaginal (suppositories, tablets, gels), oral micronized progesterone, and intramuscular (IM) 17-hydroxyprogesterone caproate (17-OHPC). The pharmacokinetics differ by route: vaginal administration achieves high local endometrial and uterine tissue concentrations with minimal systemic exposure, whereas IM 17-OHPC achieves prolonged systemic levels with weekly dosing. Oral micronized progesterone is subject to extensive first-pass hepatic metabolism and may cause sedation due to metabolites.

Prevention of recurrent preterm birth. For women with a prior spontaneous preterm birth (typically defined as delivery before 37 weeks due to spontaneous labor or preterm premature rupture of membranes), IM 17-OHPC 250 mg weekly, starting at 16–20 weeks and continuing until 36 weeks, was historically widely used based on randomized data showing reduced recurrent preterm birth in some populations. However, large subsequent trials and meta-analyses have raised questions about efficacy in broader populations, and some regulatory bodies have modified recommendations. Current practice often individualizes therapy: 17-OHPC remains an option for women with a singleton gestation and a history of a prior spontaneous preterm birth, after counseling on benefits and uncertainties. Note that 17-OHPC is contraindicated in active thrombosis or certain hormone-sensitive conditions.

Vaginal progesterone for short cervix or prior preterm birth. Vaginal progesterone (commonly 200 mg nightly or 90 mg vaginal gel daily, depending on formulation) is effective for reducing preterm birth risk in women with a singleton pregnancy and a short cervix (usually defined as cervical length <25 mm by transvaginal ultrasound) identified in mid-pregnancy (typically 16–24 weeks). Vaginal progesterone is often favored in women with cervical shortening because it provides targeted uterine effects with minimal systemic exposure and is used up to 34–36 weeks depending on practice.

Prevention of miscarriage. The use of progesterone to prevent miscarriage (threatened miscarriage or recurrent miscarriage in the first trimester) has been debated. For women with threatened miscarriage and vaginal bleeding in early pregnancy, some trials show benefit of oral or vaginal progesterone in reducing miscarriage rates in specific subgroups (e.g., women with a history of miscarriage), while other trials show minimal or no benefit. When used, typical regimens include oral micronized progesterone 200–400 mg daily or vaginal progesterone 200–400 mg daily until reassuring viability is established, often through 12 weeks. For recurrent miscarriage thought to be due to luteal phase deficiency (a controversial diagnosis), progesterone supplementation is sometimes used empirically, commonly through the first trimester.

Dosing principles and monitoring. Route selection depends on the indication: vaginal for short cervix, IM for recurrent preterm birth historical indications, and oral or vaginal for first-trimester threatened miscarriage. Start timing is critical—prophylactic strategies for preterm birth begin in mid-gestation (16–24 weeks), whereas miscarriage prevention is considered in the first trimester. Duration follows the indication: until 34–36 weeks for preterm birth prevention in many protocols, and until 12 weeks for miscarriage prevention. Side effects include injection-site pain (IM), somnolence (oral), and local irritation (vaginal). Progesterone is generally considered safe in pregnancy; current data do not demonstrate major teratogenic risks. Always discuss evidence strength and alternatives with patients.

Clinical decision points. Use vaginal progesterone for a short cervix identified in midpregnancy. Consider 17-OHPC for select women with prior spontaneous preterm birth after informed discussion. Use progesterone for threatened miscarriage selectively, discussing uncertain efficacy. Check local guidelines and regulatory updates, as recommendations evolve with new evidence.

# Iron therapy: oral vs intravenous iron formulations and indications during pregnancy

Anemia is common in pregnancy due to physiologic hemodilution and increasing iron demands (for expansion of maternal red cell mass and fetal development). Normal hemoglobin in pregnancy is generally considered approximately 11.0–14.0 g/dL; the World Health Organization defines anemia in pregnancy as hemoglobin <11.0 g/dL in the first and third trimesters, and <10.5 g/dL in the second trimester. Key goals of iron therapy are to correct iron-deficiency anemia, restore maternal iron stores, reduce transfusion needs, and optimize maternal and fetal outcomes.

Oral iron. First-line therapy for most cases of iron-deficiency anemia in pregnancy is oral iron due to efficacy, safety, and convenience. Common formulations include ferrous sulfate 325 mg (contains approximately 65 mg elemental iron), ferrous fumarate, and ferrous gluconate (lower elemental iron content). Typical dosing is ferrous sulfate 325 mg once to three times daily, or lower-frequency dosing such as 60–120 mg elemental iron once daily or every other day to improve tolerability and absorption. Emerging evidence suggests alternate-day dosing may increase fractional absorption and reduce gastrointestinal side effects by allowing hepcidin levels to fall between doses. Side effects (nausea, constipation, abdominal cramping) are common and frequently limit adherence; strategies include taking with food (though this reduces absorption), using lower or alternate-day dosing, switching formulations, or using concomitant laxatives when needed. Oral iron requires weeks to months to correct hemoglobin and replenish ferritin; reticulocyte rise is often seen within 7–10 days and hemoglobin increases by approximately 1 g/dL every 2–4 weeks with effective therapy.

Indications for intravenous (IV) iron. IV iron is indicated when oral iron is not tolerated, when rapid repletion is required (e.g., late pregnancy or when surgery is planned and iron-deficiency anemia is moderate to severe), when malabsorption (e.g., prior bariatric surgery, inflammatory bowel disease) limits response, or when adherence is poor. IV iron formulations include iron sucrose, ferric carboxymaltose, and ferric derisomaltose (formerly iron dextran in older formulations). Ferric carboxymaltose and ferric derisomaltose allow larger single doses (e.g., 500–1000 mg per infusion) and more rapid repletion compared with iron sucrose, which typically requires multiple smaller doses (e.g., 200–300 mg per dose). Total dose calculations are typically done using the Ganzoni formula or simplified weight-based tables to reach a target ferritin and hemoglobin level.

Safety of IV iron. Modern IV iron formulations have a good safety profile, with low rates of serious anaphylaxis; severe hypersensitivity is now rare compared with older high-molecular-weight iron dextran. Common adverse effects are infusion-related reactions (flushing, myalgias, mild hypotension), which are usually self-limited. Administer IV iron in settings with resuscitation capability and observe patients during and after infusion per local protocols. In pregnancy, IV iron has been shown to increase hemoglobin and ferritin more rapidly than oral iron and to reduce transfusion needs in some studies. Administer IV iron when there is insufficient time for oral replenishment before expected delivery or surgery (e.g., substantial anemia discovered in the third trimester).

Laboratory targets and monitoring. Evaluate baseline hemoglobin, mean corpuscular volume (MCV), ferritin, reticulocyte count when possible. Ferritin <30 ng/mL (some use <15–30 ng/mL depending on assay) is consistent with iron deficiency in pregnancy, although inflammation can elevate ferritin. After initiating iron therapy, monitor hemoglobin in 2–4 weeks to assess response and again near delivery. Typical transfusion thresholds and decisions (below) will be influenced by response to iron and timing relative to delivery or surgery.

Clinical decision points. Start oral iron for mild-to-moderate iron-deficiency anemia, consider alternate-day dosing to improve absorption and reduce side effects, and switch to IV iron when oral therapy fails, is not tolerated, or when rapid correction is required before delivery or operative procedures.

# Blood transfusion thresholds and safety considerations in pregnancy

Pregnancy and the peripartum period present unique transfusion considerations due to physiologic anemia of pregnancy, increased blood volume, and the potential for obstetric hemorrhage. Indications for transfusion should be individualized based on hemodynamic status, ongoing blood loss, comorbidities, and gestational age, with the goal of optimizing maternal oxygen delivery and minimizing transfusion risks.

Laboratory thresholds. In nonpregnant critically ill adults, restrictive transfusion thresholds (transfuse for hemoglobin <7 g/dL) are commonly used. In obstetrics, data are less definitive, and thresholds are often more liberal. For stable pregnant patients without significant comorbidities, many centers use a transfusion threshold of hemoglobin <7 g/dL. However, in pregnancy, clinicians often consider transfusion when hemoglobin is below 7–8 g/dL for symptomatic anemia, and at higher levels for those with cardiovascular disease, ongoing bleeding, or inadequate physiologic adaptation. In acute hemorrhage, transfusion decisions are driven by blood loss, hemodynamics (tachycardia, hypotension), and clinical signs rather than fixed hemoglobin thresholds. Massive transfusion protocols (MTPs) for obstetric hemorrhage typically use balanced-component therapy (e.g., ratios approximating RBC:plasma:platelets of 1:1:1 or institution-specific protocols) and activate early when anticipated blood loss is substantial.

Safety considerations. Transfusion carries risks: transfusion reactions (acute hemolytic, febrile nonhemolytic, allergic/anaphylactic), transfusion-related acute lung injury (TRALI), transfusion-associated circulatory overload (TACO), alloimmunization, and infectious disease transmission (now rare). In pregnancy, alloimmunization (particularly anti-D in Rh-negative mothers who are exposed to Rh-positive fetal blood) is a specific concern. For Rh(D)-negative mothers who receive transfused Rh(D)-positive red cells (or are exposed to fetal-maternal hemorrhage), standard practice is to provide Rh(D) immunoglobulin (anti-D) prophylaxis per guidelines and to perform antibody screening. For any transfusion in a Rh(D)-negative pregnant patient, involve transfusion medicine and obstetrics to ensure appropriate administration of anti-D and follow-up titers.

Perioperative planning. For planned operations on pregnant patients with pelvic masses, optimize hemoglobin preoperatively with iron therapy or erythropoiesis-stimulating strategies when appropriate. When transfusion is anticipated, prepare ABO/Rh-matched blood, consider crossmatch and antibody screen in advance, and have MTP protocols ready for obstetric hemorrhage. Minimize transfusion exposure through cell-salvage techniques when permitted and appropriate—cell salvage has been used in obstetric surgery with particular attention to filtering and avoiding amniotic fluid contamination; many centers accept cell salvage with appropriate protocols.

Post-transfusion monitoring. After transfusion, monitor hemoglobin and clinical status, and watch for transfusion reactions. For neonates exposed to maternal transfusion medications (e.g., opioids near delivery), ensure neonatal teams are alerted.

Clinical decision points. Use restrictive transfusion thresholds in stable patients (e.g., transfuse for Hb <7 g/dL) but individualize for obstetric context—higher thresholds may be appropriate for symptomatic patients, cardiovascular disease, or ongoing loss. Early activation of MTPs in massive hemorrhage improves outcomes; ensure anti-D prophylaxis and antibody screening are handled appropriately.

# Drugs contraindicated or used with caution (e.g., GnRH analogues, certain chemotherapeutics, gadolinium)

Certain drugs carry high fetal risk or lack sufficient data to support safe use in pregnancy. When managing pelvic masses, clinicians must be familiar with agents that are contraindicated or require caution.

Gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) analogues. GnRH agonists (e.g., leuprolide) and antagonists suppress ovarian steroidogenesis and cause hypoestrogenism. They are contraindicated in pregnancy because they can disrupt the hormonal milieu necessary for maintenance of pregnancy and may have adverse effects on fetal development. If pregnancy is suspected or confirmed, GnRH analogues should be stopped. In the context of pelvic masses suspected to be hormonally responsive (e.g., fibroids), GnRH analogues are used to shrink lesions preoperatively in nonpregnant patients, but not in pregnant patients.

Methotrexate and certain chemotherapeutics. Methotrexate is a folate antagonist and a known abortifacient and teratogen; it is contraindicated in pregnancy and used for medical management of ectopic pregnancy precisely because it terminates trophoblastic tissue. Other chemotherapeutic agents have varying degrees of teratogenicity depending on class and timing: classic alkylating agents, antimetabolites (e.g., methotrexate), and some targeted agents are highly teratogenic in the first trimester. Anthracyclines (e.g., doxorubicin) have been used in pregnancy in the second and third trimesters for maternal indications when necessary, with careful counseling; however, decision-making must involve maternal-fetal medicine and oncology. For patients with malignant pelvic masses discovered in pregnancy, multidisciplinary discussion is essential to weigh maternal benefit against fetal risk and to plan timing (e.g., deferring certain agents until after the first trimester or until postpartum). For benign pelvic masses, chemotherapeutics are generally not relevant, but inadvertent exposure must be recognized as contraindicated.

Gadolinium-based contrast agents. Gadolinium crosses the placenta and distributes into amniotic fluid; some cohort studies suggest a possible association with an increased risk of stillbirth, neonatal death, or rheumatologic and skin conditions, though data are limited and causality is not established. Consequently, gadolinium-enhanced MRI is generally avoided during pregnancy unless the expected diagnostic benefit outweighs potential risk and no noncontrast MRI or alternative imaging can provide equivalent information. If gadolinium is used, use the lowest effective dose, and obtain informed consent.

Other agents to avoid or use with caution. Tetracyclines cause permanent fetal teeth discoloration and bone growth inhibition and are avoided after the first trimester (and generally avoided throughout pregnancy). Warfarin is teratogenic (nasal hypoplasia, stippled epiphyses) and is typically avoided in pregnancy in favor of low-molecular-weight heparin for anticoagulation, except in select cases managed by specialists. Misoprostol (prostaglandin E1 analogue) can cause uterine contractions and abortion and must be used with caution in pregnancy. Live-attenuated vaccines and certain immunomodulators are contraindicated.

Clinical decision points. For any agent with potential fetal toxicity, perform a risk–benefit analysis, involve obstetrics and relevant specialties, document counseling, and consider delaying therapy or using safer alternatives. Always consult up-to-date teratogen databases or a clinical pharmacology/toxicology service for specific agents.

Conclusion

Pharmacologic management of pregnant patients with pelvic masses requires a nuanced understanding of drug mechanisms, maternal–fetal pharmacokinetic changes, and trimester-specific risks. First-line analgesia favors paracetamol; NSAIDs are limited by gestational age; opioids are used only when necessary. Antibiotic selection should favor beta-lactams and other agents with strong safety data in pregnancy, with specific choices for urinary and surgical infections guided by sensitivity patterns and gestational age. Progesterone therapy has role-specific indications (short cervix vs prior preterm birth vs threatened miscarriage) with route and dosing tailored to the clinical scenario. Iron therapy should begin with oral iron when feasible, with IV iron reserved for intolerance, malabsorption, or need for rapid repletion. Blood transfusion thresholds are individualized in pregnancy, with special attention to Rh immunoprophylaxis and hemorrhage protocols. Finally, several drugs—GnRH analogues, methotrexate, many chemotherapeutics, and gadolinium—are contraindicated or require extreme caution. In all cases, multidisciplinary collaboration, informed consent, and reference to current guidelines are essential.
