# Introduction

Urinary tract infections (UTIs) are one of the most common bacterial infections encountered in pregnancy. Physiologic changes of pregnancy predispose to urinary stasis and ascending infection, and infections during gestation carry distinct risks to both mother and fetus — including pyelonephritis, sepsis, and increased risk of preterm birth and low birth weight. Management must balance prompt eradication of pathogens with selection of antibiotics that are effective yet safe for the fetus, and prevention of progression to severe infection. This chapter reviews epidemiology, pregnancy-related anatomic and physiologic contributors to UTI risk, screening and treatment recommendations for asymptomatic bacteriuria, common pathogens and their resistance patterns, complications of untreated infection, antibiotic choices in pregnancy, and perioperative considerations when infection is present concurrently with operative interventions.

# Epidemiology and physiology increasing UTI risk during pregnancy

UTIs affect approximately 2–10% of pregnant women as symptomatic infections, while asymptomatic bacteriuria (ASB) can be detected in 2–11% depending on population and screening practices. The prevalence varies by socioeconomic factors, prior UTI history, parity, and comorbidities such as diabetes.

Physiologic and anatomic changes in pregnancy that increase UTI risk include hormonal, mechanical, and immunologic factors. Progesterone-mediated smooth muscle relaxation causes dilation of the ureters and reduced ureteral peristalsis; mechanical compression by the gravid uterus further contributes to urinary stasis, especially on the right side. Stasis reduces washout of bacteria and promotes ascent of organisms from the bladder toward the renal pelvis. Increased renal plasma flow and glomerular filtration rate lead to higher urinary glucose and amino acid excretion early in pregnancy, which can facilitate bacterial growth in some settings. Alterations in bladder capacity and incomplete bladder emptying — common in late pregnancy — further predispose to infection.

Immunologic changes of pregnancy are complex and not simply a global immunosuppression; there is modulation of innate and adaptive immunity that may alter susceptibility and the clinical presentation of infection. For example, leukocyte counts are often higher in pregnancy (physiologic leukocytosis) and febrile responses can be blunted, potentially delaying recognition. Finally, genitourinary microbiome changes and increased incidence of vesicoureteral reflux during pregnancy may facilitate ascending infections.

Clinically, these changes mean pregnant women are at increased risk of progression from lower UTI (cystitis) to upper UTI (pyelonephritis), and thus early detection and appropriate treatment are emphasized in obstetric care.

# Asymptomatic bacteriuria screening and treatment recommendations

Asymptomatic bacteriuria (ASB) is defined as the presence of a significant quantity of bacteria in an appropriately collected urine specimen in a patient without signs or symptoms of UTI. In pregnancy, ASB is clinically important because it is associated with increased risk of pyelonephritis and adverse obstetrical outcomes if left untreated.

Diagnostic threshold and testing
- The traditional microbiologic definition used in pregnancy is a single clean-catch voided urine culture growing a single uropathogen at ≥ 10^5 colony-forming units per milliliter (CFU/mL) in an asymptomatic woman. Many guidelines continue to recommend this threshold for screening and treatment decisions in pregnancy.
- For catheterized specimens, the threshold for significant bacteriuria is lower (≥ 10^2–10^3 CFU/mL) because catheter collection bypasses some contamination risks.
- Some centers use lower thresholds (≥ 10^3 CFU/mL) or require two consecutive positive cultures in nonpregnant populations, but in pregnancy one properly collected culture with ≥ 10^5 CFU/mL is generally sufficient to diagnose ASB.

Screening recommendations
- Universal screening for ASB with a urine culture is recommended at the first prenatal visit (often at 12–16 weeks gestation, or at the initial prenatal visit if earlier). Repeat screening later in pregnancy is not universally recommended, but should be considered in women at high risk (e.g., recurrent UTI, diabetes, sickle cell disease, structural urologic abnormalities).
- Dipstick testing alone (nitrite, leukocyte esterase) is insufficiently sensitive to exclude ASB in pregnancy; culture remains the gold standard.

Treatment of ASB
- Treatment of ASB in pregnancy reduces the incidence of pyelonephritis and is associated with lower rates of low birth weight and preterm delivery in randomized trials and observational studies.
- Recommended practice is to treat all pregnant women with ASB identified on culture with an appropriate antibiotic for a defined course, guided by susceptibility testing when available.
- Typical regimens: nitrofurantoin (100 mg twice daily for 5–7 days) or a beta-lactam (amoxicillin-clavulanate, or a cephalosporin such as cefalexin) for 3–7 days depending on the agent. Fosfomycin trometamol 3 g single dose is an alternative in some settings where susceptibility is expected and the organism is susceptible.
- After completing therapy, a test-of-cure urine culture is often obtained 1–2 weeks later to confirm eradication. If persistent bacteriuria is present, guided retreatment based on susceptibility and consideration of urology consult for anatomic evaluation are indicated.

Clinical decision points
- Treat all pregnant women with ASB detected on screening culture.
- Use local antibiogram and susceptibility data to select empiric therapy when culture results are pending; adjust once susceptibilities are known.
- Consider repeat cultures after treatment and periodic rescreening in high-risk women.

# Common pathogens (Escherichia coli, Klebsiella spp.) and antibiograms in pregnancy

Etiology
- The dominant uropathogen in pregnancy is Escherichia coli, accounting for approximately 70–90% of UTIs, including ASB and symptomatic infections. Other common organisms include Klebsiella species, Proteus mirabilis, Enterococcus faecalis, and group B Streptococcus (Streptococcus agalactiae). Staphylococcus saprophyticus is less common in pregnancy than in the general young female population.
- Group B Streptococcus deserves particular attention because colonization of the urinary tract can reflect heavy GBS colonization; detection has implications for intrapartum antibiotic prophylaxis. GBS bacteriuria at any concentration is a marker for heavy vaginal colonization and typically prompts intrapartum penicillin prophylaxis to prevent neonatal GBS disease.

Antibiograms and resistance considerations
- Local and regional resistance patterns vary substantially and should guide empiric therapy. E. coli resistance to commonly used antibiotics such as ampicillin and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX) is high in many settings; resistance to ampicillin often exceeds 40–50% in some regions. Resistance to ciprofloxacin and other fluoroquinolones has risen as well, making them less favored.
- Extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL)-producing Enterobacterales (often E. coli or Klebsiella) are an increasing concern. ESBL organisms are resistant to penicillins and most cephalosporins; carbapenems may be required for severe infections with ESBL organisms.
- Klebsiella species may also carry resistance determinants, including ESBLs and carbapenemases in high-risk settings.
- Enterococcus spp. are intrinsically resistant to cephalosporins and may require ampicillin or vancomycin depending on susceptibility.

Implications for pregnancy
- Because many first-line, safe-for-pregnancy agents (e.g., nitrofurantoin, certain beta-lactams) retain activity against the majority of community E. coli isolates, these remain reasonable empiric choices for ASB and uncomplicated cystitis in pregnant patients in many settings. However, where local resistance to nitrofurantoin or amoxicillin-clavulanate exceeds acceptable thresholds, selection must be tailored to culture results.
- Always obtain urine culture prior to starting antibiotics when feasible to enable de-escalation based on susceptibilities.

# Complications of untreated UTI: pyelonephritis, sepsis and preterm birth

Ascending infection and pyelonephritis
- Untreated lower urinary tract infection in pregnancy carries a higher risk of progression to acute pyelonephritis than in nonpregnant women due to ureteral dilation and urinary stasis. Pyelonephritis typically presents with fever, flank pain, costovertebral angle tenderness, nausea/vomiting, and systemic features. Laboratory findings include pyuria, bacteriuria, and often elevated inflammatory markers.
- Pyelonephritis in pregnancy is associated with significant maternal morbidity: dehydration, electrolyte abnormalities, bacteremia, renal impairment, and in severe cases septic shock. Hospitalization for intravenous antibiotics and supportive care is commonly required.

Sepsis
- Bacteremia may complicate pyelonephritis and progress to sepsis. Pregnancy-specific recognition of sepsis is important because physiologic changes in pregnancy modify the presentation. For example, baseline leukocytosis and increased heart rate complicate standard sepsis scoring. Obstetric early warning scores and pregnancy-adapted sepsis guidelines can improve early detection.
- Sepsis in pregnancy increases the risk of multiorgan dysfunction and may necessitate intensive care, vasopressor support, and source control. Maternal sepsis poses risks to the fetus, including fetal hypoxia and preterm delivery as a consequence of maternal instability.

Adverse obstetrical outcomes
- Untreated or severe UTIs are associated with increased risk of preterm labor, low birth weight, and perinatal morbidity. The mechanisms are multifactorial and may involve systemic inflammatory responses, cytokine-mediated uterine activity, and direct effects of bacteremia and endotoxemia.
- Pyelonephritis during pregnancy has been associated with a higher rate of preterm birth and low birth weight even when appropriately treated; prevention by screening and treatment of ASB is therefore an important obstetric intervention.

Other complications
- Recurrent UTIs may occur and merit investigation for urinary tract abnormalities, diabetes, or other predisposing factors. Rare complications include renal cortical abscess, emphysematous pyelonephritis (more common in diabetics), and perinephric abscesses, which are surgical problems requiring drainage.

Clinical decision points
- Recognize that fever and flank pain in a pregnant patient merit evaluation for pyelonephritis and prompt initiation of appropriate therapy.
- Consider hospital admission for pregnant patients with pyelonephritis, particularly if febrile, dehydrated, vomiting, or showing signs of systemic toxicity.
- Use pregnancy-adapted sepsis recognition tools and involve multidisciplinary teams early (obstetrics, infectious disease, critical care).

# Choice of antibiotics during pregnancy based on safety and sensitivity

Principles
- Select antibiotics that are both effective against likely pathogens and considered safe in pregnancy. Consider gestational age, microbiologic susceptibilities, severity of infection, and potential fetal effects. Whenever possible, obtain urine culture prior to starting antibiotics and tailor therapy based on susceptibilities.
- Avoid agents with known teratogenicity or neonatal toxicity when safer alternatives exist.

Commonly used agents and considerations
- Nitrofurantoin: Often used for ASB and cystitis (nitrofurantoin 100 mg twice daily for 5–7 days). It is generally considered safe in pregnancy for most of gestation. Avoid use at term (≥ 38–40 weeks) and during labor and delivery due to theoretical risk of hemolytic anemia in the neonate with glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency. Use with caution in the first trimester; while large studies have not consistently demonstrated major teratogenic risk, some prefer to avoid unnecessary exposures in very early pregnancy.
- Beta-lactams: Penicillins (e.g., amoxicillin) and cephalosporins (e.g., cephalexin, cefuroxime) are widely used and considered safe in pregnancy. Amoxicillin-clavulanate is often effective for community pathogens, although local resistance may limit utility. Duration typically 3–7 days for cystitis; longer for pyelonephritis.
- Fosfomycin trometamol: A single 3 g oral dose can be effective for uncomplicated cystitis and is considered safe in pregnancy. It is convenient and used where susceptibility is expected. If used for ASB or cystitis, test-of-cure culture is often recommended given single-dose regimen.
- Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX): Generally avoided in the first trimester because trimethoprim is a folate antagonist associated with potential neural tube defect risk; also avoided near term (last weeks of gestation) due to risk of kernicterus from displacement of bilirubin in the neonate. TMP-SMX may be used in middle pregnancy if no alternatives and if benefits outweigh risks, but this is generally reserved for specific indications and with specialist input.
- Aminoglycosides (e.g., gentamicin): Effective for severe Gram-negative infections and sometimes used in combination for pyelonephritis. However, aminoglycosides carry risks of fetal ototoxicity and nephrotoxicity; short courses may be used when benefits outweigh risks, often under specialist guidance and with monitoring.
- Fluoroquinolones: Generally avoided in pregnancy due to concerns about effects on fetal cartilage development in animal studies and potential adverse effects, despite limited human data. Reserved only for situations where no safer alternatives are available and after specialist consultation.
- Carbapenems: May be used for severe infections due to ESBL organisms in pregnancy when indicated; carbapenems are not known to be teratogenic and can be lifesaving for severe resistant infections.
- Vancomycin: Safe in pregnancy for Gram-positive infections such as Enterococcus (when susceptible) or MRSA; used for severe infections when indicated.

Treatment duration and routes
- ASB: oral therapy for 3–7 days depending on agent (e.g., 5–7 days for nitrofurantoin, 3 days for short-course beta-lactams may be inadequate — many recommend 5–7 days).
- Uncomplicated cystitis: 3–7 days depending on agent; fosfomycin single dose is an alternative.
- Acute pyelonephritis: initial parenteral therapy (e.g., IV ceftriaxone or ampicillin plus gentamicin depending on susceptibility and local practice) with clinical improvement allowing step-down to oral therapy for a total of typically 10–14 days or until full resolution.
- Severely ill or septic patients require IV, broad-spectrum empiric therapy covering Gram-negatives; choices should be guided by local antibiograms and pregnancy safety profiles, often involving third-generation cephalosporins (e.g., ceftriaxone) or carbapenems for resistant organisms.

Monitoring and stewardship
- Reassess therapy once culture and susceptibility data are available and de-escalate appropriately.
- Obtain test-of-cure cultures after treatment of ASB and consider repeat culture for symptomatic infections to ensure clearance.
- Recognize drug-specific maternal side effects (e.g., nitrofurantoin pulmonary toxicity is rare but possible; beta-lactam allergies) and adjust therapy accordingly.

# Perioperative considerations when concurrent infection exists

General principles
- The presence of a urinary tract infection or bacteriuria has implications for perioperative management in pregnant patients for both obstetric and non-obstetric procedures. The main goals are to reduce perioperative infectious risk, avoid maternal deterioration, and minimize fetal compromise.

Timing of surgery
- Elective procedures should ideally be postponed until UTIs are treated and the patient is clinically stable and afebrile with negative cultures when feasible. For urgent or emergent procedures, proceed with appropriate antibiotic therapy and source control while coordinating obstetric and perioperative teams.
- In obstetric practice, active infection (e.g., pyelonephritis with sepsis) is itself an indication for careful consideration regarding timing of delivery; delivery should be dictated by obstetrical indications and maternal stability rather than treating infection alone.

Antibiotic prophylaxis and therapy
- Perioperative prophylactic antibiotics should consider the current urinary pathogen and susceptibilities. If bacteriuria is present and surgery is necessary, perioperative antibiotics should cover the organism; this may require tailoring standard prophylactic regimens.
- For cesarean delivery in a patient with bacteriuria or recent UTI, standard perioperative prophylaxis (e.g., cefazolin) may be continued if organisms are susceptible. If bacteremia or resistant organisms are present, broaden regimens as indicated and consult infectious disease.
- For instrumentation of the urinary tract (e.g., catheterization, urologic procedures), ensure appropriate peri-procedural antibiotics per urology guidelines, particularly if bacteriuria is documented.

Anesthesia considerations
- Neuraxial anesthesia (spinal or epidural) is commonly used in obstetric operations. In the presence of systemic infection or bacteremia, there is theoretical concern that neuraxial procedures could introduce pathogens into the central nervous system, though evidence is limited. Most guidelines recommend caution: avoid neuraxial anesthesia in patients with uncontrolled systemic sepsis until infection is controlled. If bacteremia is suspected or documented, multidisciplinary discussion with anesthesiology is warranted; alternatives include general anesthesia or delaying neuraxial until infection is treated.
- For patients with localized urinary infection without systemic signs, neuraxial anesthesia is often acceptable.

Intraoperative and postoperative monitoring
- Monitor for signs of worsening maternal infection or sepsis intra- and post-operatively. Pregnancy complicates interpretation of vital signs; maintain a low threshold for escalation.
- Consider fetal monitoring intraoperatively for viable fetuses when feasible, especially if maternal infection or hemodynamic instability is present.
- Use thromboprophylaxis as indicated; pregnancy is a hypercoagulable state and infection increases thrombotic risk.

Device-related infection risk
- Indwelling urinary catheters increase the risk of catheter-associated urinary tract infection (CAUTI). Avoid unnecessary catheterization, use aseptic technique for insertion, and remove catheters as soon as clinically possible. Antimicrobial-impregnated catheters are not routinely recommended solely for pregnancy.

Special scenarios
- For urologic surgery in pregnant patients with active infection, coordinate with urology for source control (e.g., drainage of obstruction) and with obstetrics for fetal considerations. In obstructive uropathy with infection, urgent decompression (e.g., ureteral stenting or percutaneous nephrostomy) may be necessary to control sepsis.
- In cases of resistant organisms (e.g., ESBL producers), perioperative therapy may require IV agents with activity against these pathogens (e.g., carbapenems). Maternal safety and fetal considerations must be balanced.

Clinical decision points
- Postpone elective surgery until infection is treated when possible.
- Treat documented bacteriuria prior to planned instrumentation; tailor perioperative antibiotics to susceptibilities.
- Avoid neuraxial anesthesia in uncontrolled systemic infection; individualize decisions with anesthesiology.
- Ensure catheter avoidance and prompt removal to reduce CAUTI risk.

# Conclusion

Urinary tract infections in pregnancy require proactive screening, prompt and pregnancy-safe therapy, and vigilance for complications. Universal urine culture screening at the first prenatal visit with treatment of asymptomatic bacteriuria reduces the risk of pyelonephritis and adverse perinatal outcomes. Empiric antibiotic choices should reflect local susceptibility patterns and pregnancy safety profiles; nitrofurantoin, beta-lactams, and fosfomycin are commonly used, while TMP-SMX, fluoroquinolones, and aminoglycosides have specific contraindications or cautions depending on gestational age and severity of infection. Severe infections and sepsis necessitate hospitalization, parenteral therapy, and multidisciplinary care. Perioperative management must prioritize maternal stabilization, appropriate antibiotic coverage, and procedural timing, with attention to anesthetic risks when bacteremia is present. Understanding the pathophysiology, microbiology, and evidence-based management principles enables clinicians to reduce maternal and fetal morbidity associated with UTIs in pregnancy.

Reference ranges and key diagnostic criteria summarized
- ASB in pregnancy: urine culture with a single uropathogen at ≥ 10^5 CFU/mL in an asymptomatic patient.
- Physiologic leukocytosis in pregnancy: WBC up to approximately 12,000/µL–15,000/µL may be normal; interpret leukocytosis in clinical context.
- Pyelonephritis treatment duration: typically 10–14 days (initial IV then oral stepdown as clinically indicated).
- Screen all pregnant women with urine culture at first prenatal visit (12–16 weeks commonly).
