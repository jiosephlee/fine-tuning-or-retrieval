Introduction

Pregnancy presents unique challenges when non-obstetric pelvic or abdominal pathology requires consideration of operative management. The physiologic changes of pregnancy alter presentation, diagnostic accuracy, and perioperative risk for both the mother and the fetus. Decision-making must balance the maternal indications for surgery against fetal gestational age, the urgency of the maternal condition, and the potential effects of anesthesia, surgical stress, and intraoperative interventions on pregnancy outcomes. This chapter synthesizes evidence-based indications for operative intervention for pelvic and abdominal masses in pregnancy, addresses trimester-specific risks and timing preferences for non-obstetric abdominal surgery, contrasts operative and conservative management pathways, and details the essentials of counseling, multidisciplinary decision-making, documentation, and ethical aspects of shared decision-making.

# Indications for surgery: acute complications, suspected malignancy, refractory symptoms

Indications for operative intervention in pregnancy fall into three broad clinical categories: acute complications that threaten maternal health or viability of the organ involved, suspicion of malignancy where delay could compromise staging or outcomes, and refractory or progressively symptomatic lesions that fail conservative therapy and impair quality of life or create obstetric risks.

Acute complications mandate urgent or emergent intervention. Ovarian torsion is a prime example: twisting of the adnexal vascular pedicle produces venous outflow obstruction, progressive edema, ischemia, and ultimately hemorrhagic infarction. Clinically it presents with sudden, severe, unilateral lower abdominal pain often accompanied by nausea and vomiting. Ultrasound findings that raise concern include an enlarged, edematous ovary, peripheralization of follicles, absent or reduced Doppler venous and arterial flow, and free fluid. Torsion is a surgical emergency because prolonged ischemia can result in necrosis and infection, with attendant maternal sepsis and loss of ovarian function. Detorsion with cystectomy or ovary-sparing procedures is preferred when feasible; oophorectomy is reserved for nonviable tissue or hemodynamic instability.

Rupture and hemorrhage of an adnexal cyst or tumor can present as acute abdomen with peritoneal signs and hemodynamic instability. Hemodynamic compromise guides the urgency of laparotomy; blood loss parameters and need for transfusion are key decision points. Infection, such as abscess or infected necrosis within a mass, similarly requires prompt drainage and source control in addition to antimicrobial therapy.

Suspected malignancy is another major indication where delay could alter prognosis. Features that increase concern for malignancy in adnexal or pelvic masses include solid components, mural nodularity, papillary projections, septations thicker than 3 mm, rapid growth, presence of ascites, and elevated tumor markers when interpreted in context. In pregnancy, tumor markers have altered baselines (for example, human chorionic gonadotropin is elevated physiologically; CA-125 may be modestly increased in the first trimester and with peritoneal irritation), so interpretation must be cautious. Size thresholds commonly cited prompting consideration of surgery are masses greater than 5–10 cm, particularly if complex, growing, or symptomatic, because larger lesions carry greater risk of torsion, rupture, or malignancy. The decision to operate for malignancy must balance gestational age, need for accurate staging, and the potential for fertility- and pregnancy-sparing surgical approaches.

Refractory or progressive symptoms that materially affect maternal function or pregnancy course also justify surgical management. Persistent pain that is unresponsive to analgesia, bowel or urinary obstruction due to mass effect, or rapidly enlarging lesions that threaten uterine growth or obstruct labor are scenarios where the risk-benefit favors operative management. In all indications, maternal health and stability take precedence; maternal critical illness places fetal viability at risk, so intervention to secure maternal well-being is often the optimal route for both patients.

# Trimester-specific risks and preferred timing for non-obstetric abdominal surgery

Gestational age is a central determinant of perioperative risk and helps frame timing decisions for non-obstetric abdominal surgery. Each trimester carries distinct maternal-fetal considerations.

First trimester (conception through 14 weeks): The primary concern in the first trimester is teratogenicity and the baseline risk of spontaneous abortion. Organogenesis occurs approximately between weeks 3 and 10 of gestation; exposures during this window carry the highest theoretical risk for major congenital anomalies. However, modern anesthetic agents have not been convincingly linked to congenital malformations in humans when used for necessary surgery. The baseline miscarriage risk in recognized pregnancies is approximately 10% to 20% depending on population and maternal factors. Non-obstetric surgery in the first trimester may be associated with a small increased risk of miscarriage, particularly if underlying pathology or systemic illness (for example, severe infection or maternal hemodynamic instability) is present. When possible, elective procedures are deferred to the second trimester.

Second trimester (15 through 27 weeks): The second trimester is generally regarded as the safest window for elective non-obstetric surgery. Organogenesis is complete, the uterus is not yet so large as to complicate operative access, and the risk of preterm labor is lower than in the third trimester. Surgical positioning and respiratory mechanics are more manageable. Empirical data suggest lower rates of pregnancy loss and preterm birth when elective abdominal surgery occurs in the second trimester compared with the first or third. Therefore, when a procedure can be safely postponed, the second trimester is preferred for elective or semiurgent interventions.

Third trimester (28 weeks onward): The principal concerns in the third trimester are the increased risk of preterm labor, technical difficulties related to the gravid uterus, higher intra-abdominal pressure, and risks associated with perioperative fetal distress and prematurity. Surgeries in the late third trimester have higher rates of preterm delivery and neonatal morbidity if preterm birth occurs. For near-term pregnancies with pathology that can be delayed, it may be appropriate to plan surgery concurrent with delivery if obstetrically indicated (for example, performing cesarean delivery with simultaneous resection of a pelvic mass). When emergent maternal indications arise in the third trimester, the maternal condition dictates immediate management; when feasible, administration of corticosteroids to enhance fetal lung maturity (generally between 24 and 34 weeks) and planning for potential neonatal support should be incorporated.

Across trimesters, other specific risks must be considered. In pregnant patients, physiological increases in blood volume and cardiac output, decreased functional residual capacity, and changes in gastroesophageal sphincter tone increase aspiration risk. The impact of intra-abdominal pressure (pneumoperitoneum) with laparoscopy requires attention to insufflation pressures and maternal positioning. Current evidence supports the safety of laparoscopy in pregnancy when performed by experienced surgeons with pneumoperitoneum pressures generally kept at or below 12 mmHg, and with left lateral tilt to minimize aortocaval compression. Nevertheless, uterine size and gestational age inform the choice between laparoscopy and laparotomy.

# Conservative management alternatives and criteria for expectant vs operative approaches

Not all pelvic or abdominal masses in pregnancy require immediate surgery. Conservative management is often appropriate and may include expectant observation with serial imaging and symptom monitoring, medical therapy (for example, antibiotics for infection), percutaneous drainage for accessible collections, or temporizing procedures that preserve pregnancy until a safer operative window.

Criteria favoring expectant management include small, simple cysts (<5–6 cm) that are asymptomatic or minimally symptomatic, sonographic features consistent with a benign functional cyst (thin-walled, anechoic, unilocular), and stability or regression on serial imaging. Many functional ovarian cysts regress spontaneously during pregnancy. The decision to observe should include planned intervals for reassessment, typically with follow-up ultrasound in 4–8 weeks, and clear instructions for return if symptoms worsen.

Conversely, criteria favoring operative intervention include signs of acute complication (torsion, rupture with hemoperitoneum, hemodynamic instability), sonographic or clinical features suggesting malignancy as previously outlined (solid components, papillary projections, ascites, rapid growth), persistent or severe symptoms refractory to medical therapy, and mass size sufficiently large (commonly >8–10 cm) to appreciably raise risks of torsion, rupture, or obstructed labor. Infection not responsive to antibiotics or collections requiring definitive drainage are further indications for operative or percutaneous intervention.

The threshold for conversion from expectant to operative management depends on evolution of symptoms, interval growth, new suspicious features on imaging, or development of complications. Clinical vigilance is essential because the physiologic changes of pregnancy can mask some signs of deterioration (for example, leukocytosis is common), and imaging modalities have trade-offs in sensitivity and specificity during pregnancy. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) without gadolinium is often the preferred problem-solving tool when ultrasound is inconclusive and additional characterization is required, as it avoids ionizing radiation and generally provides superior soft-tissue contrast.

# Counseling on maternal and fetal risks, informed consent essentials

Informed consent in the context of operative intervention during pregnancy must be comprehensive, individualized, and documented. Counseling should explicitly address the maternal risks of the procedure, the potential fetal risks, the likely timing and urgency of surgical intervention, and the alternatives, including their risks and benefits.

Maternal risks include anesthesia-related complications, surgical bleeding, infection, thromboembolic events, organ injury, potential need for blood transfusion, and the general risks of the procedure being considered (for example, oophorectomy vs cystectomy). Maternal physiologic changes in pregnancy modify risk profiles: airway edema increases the risk of difficult intubation, increased blood volume can mask acute blood loss until decompensation, and a hypercoagulable state raises thromboembolism risk. Preoperative labs should include complete blood count (reference ranges: hemoglobin 12–16 g/dL in nonpregnant females but may be lower in pregnancy due to dilutional anemia; pregnant hemoglobin often ranges 11–14 g/dL), and crossmatch if significant hemorrhage is anticipated.

Fetal risks to communicate include miscarriage (particularly for first-trimester surgeries), preterm labor and delivery (notably for third-trimester interventions), potential for transient or permanent fetal neurodevelopmental effects related to intraoperative hypoxia or hemodynamic instability, and the possibility of neonatal complications if preterm delivery occurs. While modern anesthesia has not been shown to cause congenital malformations, perioperative maternal hypotension, hypoxia, fever, or prolonged uterine manipulations can adversely affect the fetus. Specific numerical risk estimates should be framed cautiously: baseline spontaneous abortion risk is roughly 10–20%; non-obstetric surgery may increase miscarriage risk modestly in the first trimester, but the absolute increase varies by underlying condition and is not precisely quantified. Risk of preterm delivery after nonobstetric surgery is higher in the third trimester; some series report increased relative risk but absolute risk depends on gestational age and maternal condition.

Discussion of alternatives should include conservative options, percutaneous interventions, and the option of deferring surgery to a safer gestational window if clinically appropriate and safe. If malignancy is suspected, the potential impact of delay on staging and prognosis should be reviewed. Counseling must also outline perioperative fetal monitoring plans, use of tocolytics (not routinely recommended for prophylaxis) and corticosteroid administration when preterm delivery risk exists (corticosteroids recommended for fetal lung maturation between 24 and 34 weeks when preterm birth is likely within 7 days).

Informed consent must cover contingency planning: for example, the potential need to proceed to laparotomy, possible oophorectomy, and the circumstances under which obstetric delivery might be performed concurrently. Consent should be obtained in language the patient understands, include assessment of capacity, and allow time for questions. Documentation should record the risks discussed, alternatives offered, the patient’s expressed preferences and understanding, and the decision reached.

# Multidisciplinary decision-making and documentation

Optimal perioperative care of pregnant patients with pelvic or abdominal masses requires coordinated multidisciplinary input. The team should include the obstetrician with expertise in high-risk pregnancies, the surgeon skilled in the planned procedure (gynecologic oncologist if malignancy suspected), anesthesiologist familiar with obstetric anesthesia, neonatology if viability and potential preterm delivery are considerations, radiology for diagnostic imaging input, and pathology if intraoperative frozen section may inform decisions. Nursing, blood bank, and critical care services should be integrated as needed.

Multidisciplinary decision-making begins with a shared assessment of urgency, maternal stability, gestational age, diagnostic uncertainty, and the potential benefits and harms of intervention versus conservative care. In cases of suspected malignancy, discussion should address whether fertility- or pregnancy-sparing approaches are oncologically safe and whether staging can be deferred. The anesthesiologist should evaluate airway, aspiration risk, and provide recommendations on positioning, monitoring, and perioperative fetal monitoring. Neonatology involvement is critical when the risk of preterm delivery is substantial; they advise on corticosteroid administration, magnesium sulfate for neuroprotection when appropriate (usually considered for deliveries before 32 weeks), and neonatal resuscitation readiness.

Documentation after such discussions should be thorough and should include the clinical indications, alternatives considered, the multidisciplinary team members involved, the shared decision-making process, the specifics of the consent obtained, contingency plans, and any agreed-upon thresholds that would prompt action (for example, specified levels of maternal instability that would trigger immediate laparotomy). This documentation is both medical record and medicolegal protection and provides continuity of care across teams and shifts.

# Ethical considerations and patient-centered risk tolerance

Ethical decision-making in the context of surgery during pregnancy requires balancing respect for maternal autonomy, beneficence for both patients (mother and fetus), nonmaleficence, and justice. The pregnant patient is the primary decision-maker; although fetal interests are ethically weighty, the mother’s autonomy must be respected. Clinicians should provide clear, unbiased information and recommend a course consistent with medical evidence while recognizing that patients may weigh risks to their own health and the fetus differently.

Values clarification is an important component of counseling. Some patients may prioritize minimizing any fetal risk, even at some increased maternal risk or postponing definitive cancer treatment; others may prioritize rapid treatment of a suspected malignancy to maximize maternal prognosis. Clinicians should explore patient goals, fears, and social context (support systems, childcare, ability to attend follow-up) and recognize that cultural or religious values may shape preferences. Where a patient’s decision potentially conflicts with medical recommendation, efforts should focus on empathetic engagement, provision of additional information, and negotiation. Coercion must be avoided; if a patient declines recommended intervention, clinicians should document the discussion, ensure comprehension, and outline plans for monitoring and reassessment.

Situations of extreme urgency, where maternal life is at risk, ethically justify immediate intervention even when that intervention carries significant fetal risk. The mother’s treatment should not be withheld to protect the fetus when maternal survival is imperiled. In such scenarios, interventions that simultaneously address maternal illness and obstetric considerations (for example, resuscitative hysterotomy when indicated) may be appropriate.

Finally, fair access to care is an ethical concern. Pregnant patients should have timely access to specialists and supportive services, and systems should minimize delays in diagnosis and treatment that could worsen outcomes. Counseling should aim to empower patients with information and support rather than directing decisions based on provider values.

Conclusion

Deciding on operative intervention for pelvic and abdominal masses in pregnancy requires a nuanced understanding of pathophysiology, altered maternal physiology, gestational age–specific risks, and the comparative benefits of operative versus conservative strategies. Clear communication, multidisciplinary planning, detailed informed consent, and attention to ethical principles enable clinicians to guide pregnant patients through complex choices. The clinician’s responsibility is to present evidence-based recommendations tailored to the patient’s clinical situation and values, prioritize maternal stabilization when necessary, and document the process and rationale thoroughly to support optimal maternal and fetal outcomes.
