Chapter 2: Physiologic Changes of Pregnancy Relevant to Diagnosis and Management

Introduction

Pregnancy is a state of profound physiologic adaptation in virtually every organ system. These changes are necessary to support fetal growth and prepare the mother for labor and delivery, but they also alter the presentation of disease, confound interpretation of laboratory and imaging studies, and modify perioperative and critical care risk. A sound understanding of these adaptations is essential for safe clinical reasoning: what is “normal” for a pregnant woman often differs markedly from the nonpregnant baseline, and apparent deviations from nonpregnant reference ranges may reflect physiology rather than pathology. This chapter reviews the key systemic changes relevant to diagnosis and management, links the underlying pathophysiology to clinical signs and test results, and highlights common decision points where pregnancy-modified norms must inform care.

# Cardiovascular changes: increased blood volume, cardiac output, heart rate, blood pressure alterations

Cardiac output, blood volume, and systemic vascular resistance are among the most striking cardiovascular changes of pregnancy. Blood volume increases progressively beginning in the first trimester and peaks in the second to third trimester. Total blood volume typically rises by approximately 30% to 50%; plasma volume increases more than red cell mass, producing a relative haemodilution (see hematologic section). The rise in blood volume is mediated by pregnancy hormones (notably progesterone and estrogen), activation of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system, increased plasma proteins that alter oncotic pressure, and sodium and water retention.

Cardiac output increases in parallel with blood volume. By midpregnancy, cardiac output is typically 30% to 50% above nonpregnant levels, with the maximal increase evident by 20 to 24 weeks’ gestation and sustained through delivery. This rise reflects increases in both stroke volume and heart rate. Stroke volume increases early in gestation, and heart rate rises by about 10 to 20 beats per minute on average. During labor and immediately postpartum, transient further increases in cardiac output occur (autotransfusion from contracting uterus and mobilization of interstitial fluid). Clinically, these changes account for higher resting heart rates and may challenge interpretation of tachycardia; modest tachycardia may be physiologic whereas higher rates raise concern for hemorrhage, sepsis, or cardiomyopathy.

Systemic vascular resistance decreases due to vasodilatory effects of progesterone, nitric oxide production, and formation of the low-resistance uteroplacental circulation. As a result, blood pressure commonly falls in early pregnancy, reaching a nadir in the mid-second trimester (usually around 20 weeks). Systolic blood pressure is often unchanged or mildly reduced; diastolic pressure decreases more noticeably, typically by 5 to 15 mmHg. Mean arterial pressure may be slightly lower than baseline. After midpregnancy, vascular resistance rises toward term and blood pressure typically returns toward prepregnancy levels. Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (gestational hypertension, preeclampsia) represent important pathologic deviations from this expected course and require vigilance, but baseline expectations of lower midpregnancy blood pressures should be remembered when interpreting outpatient readings.

Physiologic implications and clinical reasoning: The combination of increased blood volume and decreased systemic vascular resistance means that pregnant women tolerate blood loss differently than nonpregnant adults; however, because of the haemodilutional anemia described below, signs of hypovolemia (tachycardia, orthostatic changes) may appear later and be less specific. Supine hypotension can result from uterine compression of the inferior vena cava (IVC) in late pregnancy; patients may present with lightheadedness or hypotension when supine, mitigated by left lateral tilt. Cardiac murmurs, mild peripheral edema, and splitting of heart sounds may be benign. New or severe dyspnea, persistent tachycardia, syncope, or chest pain warrant evaluation for pathological causes including cardiomyopathy, pulmonary embolism, or myocardial ischemia.

# Respiratory changes: increased oxygen consumption, diaphragmatic elevation, tidal volume changes

Pregnancy imposes increased metabolic demand, reflected in respiratory physiology. Maternal oxygen consumption rises by approximately 20% to 30% above baseline, driven by increased basal metabolic rate and the oxygen needs of the uterus and fetus. To meet this demand, ventilation increases primarily through an increase in tidal volume rather than respiratory rate. Tidal volume typically increases by about 30% to 40%, producing an increase in minute ventilation of roughly 30% to 50%. Resting respiratory rate may be unchanged or modestly increased.

A key laboratory consequence is a chronic respiratory alkalosis: with increased alveolar ventilation, arterial carbon dioxide tension (PaCO2) falls to a new steady-state of about $28$ to $32$ mmHg (normal nonpregnant PaCO2 ≈ $40$ mmHg). The reduced PaCO2 shifts the maternal bicarbonate equilibrium; renal bicarbonate excretion compensates so that serum bicarbonate typically decreases to around $18$ to $21$ mEq/L. Maternal arterial oxygen tension (PaO2) is often slightly higher than nonpregnant values (PaO2 ≈ $100$ to $105$ mmHg), but dyspnea is very commonly reported despite normal oxygenation and is frequently physiologic.

Mechanical displacement of the diaphragm occurs as the enlarging uterus elevates the diaphragm by several centimeters (average up to about 4 cm). Functional residual capacity (FRC) falls by approximately $10$ to $25\%$ by term because of decreased expiratory reserve volume and residual volume; however, inspiratory capacity may be preserved or slightly increased due to higher tidal volumes. The net effect is decreased pulmonary reserve: pregnant women desaturate more rapidly during apnea or hypoventilation (for example during intubation), making airway management riskier in the intrapartum and perioperative periods.

Clinical implications: Shortness of breath is common and often physiologic in pregnancy due to increased ventilatory drive and altered perception. However, clinicians must distinguish benign dyspnea from cardiopulmonary disease. Key red flags include hypoxemia (SpO2 < 95% at rest), pleuritic chest pain, unilateral leg swelling, or persistent tachypnea. The lower baseline PaCO2 affects interpretation of arterial blood gases: a PaCO2 of $40$ mmHg in pregnancy suggests hypoventilation or respiratory failure. Preoxygenation and rapid sequence induction are particularly important during anesthesia; preparations for rapid desaturation should be anticipated.

# Hematologic changes: physiologic anemia, leukocytosis, coagulation profile changes

Pregnancy is a hyperdynamic hematologic state. Plasma volume expands substantially (about 30% to 50%), while red blood cell mass increases by roughly 20% to 30% due to increased erythropoietin. Because plasma increases more than red cell mass, the net effect is a dilutional or “physiologic” anemia. Common benchmarks: the World Health Organization defines anemia in pregnancy as hemoglobin (Hgb) less than $11.0$ g/dL; many clinicians accept Hgb ≥ $11.0$ g/dL in the first and third trimesters and ≥ $10.5$ g/dL in the second trimester as normal. Typical hemoglobin values in pregnancy fall in the range of about $11$–$13$ g/dL; hematocrit commonly decreases to 32%–42% depending on gestational age. Iron requirements increase substantially, making pregnant women vulnerable to true iron-deficiency anemia; whereas mild reductions in Hgb may be physiologic, values below $10$ g/dL warrant evaluation and treatment.

Total leukocyte count increases during pregnancy, primarily due to neutrophilia. Normal leukocyte counts can rise to 12,000–15,000/µL or higher, particularly in the third trimester and during labor; counts up to 20,000/µL can sometimes be physiologic during labor or immediate postpartum. Lymphocyte and monocyte distributions shift, and an absolute neutrophilia reduces the specificity of leukocytosis for infection. Therefore, interpreting the white blood cell count requires clinical correlation.

Platelet counts may decline modestly by late pregnancy—a phenomenon called gestational thrombocytopenia—due to hemodilution and increased platelet consumption in the uteroplacental circulation. Most gestational thrombocytopenia is mild, with platelet counts remaining above 100,000/µL. Platelet counts below 100,000/µL raise concern for immune thrombocytopenic purpura, preeclampsia-associated thrombocytopenia, or other pathology and warrant further workup.

Coagulation changes produce a prothrombotic state. Plasma fibrinogen increases substantially (typical term values $400$–$650$ mg/dL). Several procoagulant factors (e.g., factors VII, VIII, X, von Willebrand factor) increase, while natural anticoagulants such as protein S decline; protein C activity is relatively preserved. D-dimer levels are commonly elevated throughout pregnancy, limiting their negative predictive value for venous thromboembolism (VTE) in pregnancy. The net balance is increased thrombotic risk: pregnant and puerperal women have higher incidences of deep venous thrombosis and pulmonary embolism compared with nonpregnant women. Conversely, bleeding risk is not uniformly reduced; obstetric hemorrhage remains a leading cause of maternal morbidity, and the hypercoagulable state coexists with situations (placental abruption, uterine atony) that predispose to hemorrhage.

Clinical implications: Recognize that modest anemia, leukocytosis, and thrombocytopenia may reflect normal pregnancy. Ferritin, iron studies, and peripheral smear are useful to distinguish dilutional versus iron-deficiency anemia. For thrombocytopenia, platelet counts below $100{,}000$/µL merit expedited evaluation; counts below $50{,}000$/µL increase bleeding risk for regional anesthesia and operative delivery. Elevated fibrinogen can mask early disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) because fibrinogen remains high until late; conversely, a falling fibrinogen in a pregnant patient is an ominous sign. Clinical thresholds for transfusion are individualized: in stable nonbleeding patients, transfusion is usually not indicated until Hgb is < $7$ g/dL, but in obstetric patients clinical context, ongoing bleeding, and fetal considerations may prompt different decisions (many obstetricians consider transfusion at higher Hgb thresholds when symptomatic or hemodynamically unstable).

# Renal and urinary changes affecting infection risk and laboratory interpretation

Renal physiology adapts markedly during pregnancy. Renal blood flow increases by approximately 50% and glomerular filtration rate (GFR) rises by about 40% to 60%, beginning early in pregnancy and peaking in the second trimester. The augmented GFR lowers serum creatinine and urea concentrations: typical pregnancy creatinine values fall to roughly $0.4$–$0.8$ mg/dL (nonpregnant reference ≈ $0.6$–$1.1$ mg/dL), and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) is similarly decreased. These lower baselines mean that a creatinine of $1.0$ mg/dL in a pregnant patient, though within normal nonpregnant limits, represents renal impairment and may signify acute kidney injury or preeclampsia-related renal dysfunction.

Physiologic dilatation of the renal pelvis and ureters occurs due to progesterone-mediated smooth muscle relaxation and mechanical compression from the enlarging uterus; ureteral dilation is more pronounced on the right. Ureteral stasis combined with glycosuria—because increased GFR and decreased renal tubular reabsorption can produce glucosuria even with normal blood glucose—elevates the risk of urinary tract infections (UTIs) and pyelonephritis. Asymptomatic bacteriuria is more common and has important implications: untreated asymptomatic bacteriuria in pregnancy increases the risk of pyelonephritis and preterm birth. Routine screening for asymptomatic bacteriuria is recommended in pregnancy, and positive cultures are treated even if asymptomatic.

Urinary frequency is a common symptom that reflects reduced bladder capacity and increased urinary output; however, symptoms such as dysuria, fever, flank pain, or systemic illness should prompt evaluation for infection. Interpretation of urinalysis is modified by physiologic changes: trace proteinuria can occur with gestation, particularly after exercise or in late pregnancy, but persistent proteinuria (e.g., > $300$ mg/24 hours) is abnormal and is a diagnostic criterion for preeclampsia when associated with hypertension. The urinary sediment is often bland, and microscopic hematuria is not expected; frank hematuria or significant sediment requires investigation.

Clinical implications: Recognize lower creatinine and BUN baselines. Maintain a low threshold to investigate pyelonephritis and treat asymptomatic bacteriuria. Be cautious interpreting glycosuria; screen for gestational diabetes by specific testing rather than relying on urine glucose. When assessing renal function, small absolute creatinine rises can be clinically meaningful in pregnancy and warrant prompt evaluation.

# Immunologic and inflammatory modulation in pregnancy

Pregnancy represents a complex immunologic state that is often characterized simplistically as immunosuppression; the reality is nuanced immunomodulation. The maternal immune system must tolerate the semiallogeneic fetus while still protecting the mother and fetus from infection. This balance is achieved through shifts in innate and adaptive immunity, altered cytokine profiles, and changes at the maternal–fetal interface.

Adaptive immunity in pregnancy exhibits a shift from T helper type 1 (Th1)-dominant responses toward a Th2-biased profile during much of gestation. Th1 responses are generally proinflammatory and effective against intracellular pathogens; Th2 responses favor humoral immunity and antibody production. This shift can alter susceptibility to and severity of certain infections—some viral infections may be more severe, while humoral immune responses remain intact. Regulatory T cells (Tregs) expand during pregnancy and are critical for maintaining tolerance to fetal antigens.

Innate immune function is also modulated. Neutrophil counts and activity increase, but neutrophil function may be altered. Complement activity increases. NK (natural killer) cell populations in peripheral blood change in phenotype; uterine NK cells at the decidua are essential for placentation and spiral artery remodeling rather than cytotoxicity. Overall, pregnancy is characterized by a dynamic immune state that varies by gestational age and is neither uniformly suppressed nor uniformly activated.

Inflammation plays a role in the timing of labor: pregnancy can be considered to transit from an anti-inflammatory milieu in early gestation, to a relatively proinflammatory state at term to facilitate labor and delivery. This shifting baseline affects interpretation of inflammatory markers (e.g., C-reactive protein) and the clinical course of inflammatory conditions.

Clinical implications: Because leukocytosis can be physiologic and immune responses are altered, clinical judgment is essential when evaluating suspected infection. Some infections carry greater maternal risk (e.g., influenza, varicella, Listeria, certain fungal pathogens) and warrant aggressive prevention and treatment. Vaccination strategies in pregnancy (influenza, Tdap) exploit the preserved humoral response to protect both mother and neonate. When interpreting fever and inflammatory markers, remember the modified immune baseline and assess for concerning features such as persistent fever, hypotension, or organ dysfunction that indicate true infection.

# Mechanical effects of the gravid uterus on adjacent organs (diaphragm, bladder, bowel)

The enlarging uterus exerts mechanical effects that produce predictable anatomic and functional changes. By the second trimester, the uterus rises out of the pelvis; by term it may reach the right costal margin. This expansion displaces and compresses adjacent structures.

Diaphragmatic elevation is one major consequence. The diaphragm can be displaced upward by several centimeters, reducing chest wall compliance and decreasing functional residual capacity (FRC). Although inspiratory capacity may be maintained by increased tidal volume, the reduced FRC lowers oxygen reserve and contributes to faster desaturation during apnea or hypoventilation. Diaphragmatic elevation may also alter breath sounds on auscultation and complicate ultrasound windows for thoracoabdominal imaging.

Bladder compression reduces bladder capacity and increases urinary frequency. The enlarging uterus may also impair ureteral drainage, particularly on the right side, thereby increasing susceptibility to hydronephrosis and pyelonephritis. Mechanical pressure on pelvic veins contributes to lower-extremity venous stasis, increasing the risk of varicose veins and deep venous thrombosis. Uterine enlargement and hormonal effects (progesterone-mediated smooth muscle relaxation) slow gastrointestinal motility and relax the lower esophageal sphincter, promoting gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), heartburn, and constipation. Displacement of the intestines can also change the location and character of abdominal pain; for example, appendicitis often presents with a more cephalad and lateral pain distribution as pregnancy progresses.

Compression of the inferior vena cava (IVC) by the gravid uterus when a woman lies supine reduces venous return, preload, and cardiac output, producing supine hypotension syndrome. Symptoms include dizziness, nausea, pallor, or syncope; many women report relief by position change (left lateral decubitus). In labor and when performing procedures, clinicians should avoid prolonged supine positioning and use left lateral tilt to maintain venous return.

Clinical implications: Expect urinary frequency and constipation as common, often physiologic complaints, but maintain suspicion for infection or surgical abdominal pathology when symptoms are severe or atypical. Intra-abdominal inflammatory processes may present with atypical physical findings due to organ displacement. For procedural planning, recognize the importance of patient positioning to avoid IVC compression and anticipate altered ventilation mechanics when intubation or general anesthesia is necessary.

# Implications of physiologic changes for symptom interpretation (dyspnea, anemia, fever)

The interplay of cardiovascular, respiratory, hematologic, renal, immunologic, and mechanical adaptations in pregnancy modifies how common symptoms and laboratory abnormalities should be interpreted.

Dyspnea: Shortness of breath is frequently reported in otherwise healthy pregnant women and may arise from increased ventilatory drive, elevated diaphragm, and increased oxygen consumption. Characteristically, physiologic dyspnea is present at rest or with minimal exertion, is chronic or gradually progressive, and is not associated with hypoxemia, chest pain, orthopnea, syncope, or focal neurologic signs. Objective hypoxemia (SpO2 < 95% on room air) or features suggestive of cardiopulmonary disease (rapid progression, hemoptysis, pleuritic chest pain, unilateral leg swelling) should prompt urgent evaluation for pulmonary embolism, peripartum cardiomyopathy, pneumonia, or other pathology. Because baseline PaCO2 is reduced in pregnancy, a normal PaCO2 (~$40$ mmHg) is abnormal in a pregnant patient and may signal respiratory failure.

Anemia: The dilutional physiologic anemia of pregnancy lowers hemoglobin and hematocrit. Nevertheless, true iron-deficiency anemia remains common and clinically important. Symptoms such as fatigue, tachycardia, dyspnea on exertion, pallor, and reduced exercise tolerance may be attributed to pregnancy but should trigger evaluation when disproportionate to gestational stage or when hemoglobin falls below expected ranges. Screening hemoglobin and ferritin during pregnancy identify iron deficiency early; ferritin below $30$ ng/mL commonly indicates depleted iron stores. Treatment with oral or intravenous iron is effective and reduces perinatal risks associated with severe maternal anemia.

Fever: Interpreting fever in pregnancy requires recognizing both altered immune responses and the high stakes of maternal infection. Maternal fever has implications for the fetus (risk of neural tube defects with early gestational hyperthermia, and fetal tachycardia or acidosis with intrauterine infection). Some infections are more severe in pregnancy (e.g., influenza) and warrant prompt treatment. However, baseline leukocytosis and elevated inflammatory markers may obscure the significance of mild fever. A pragmatic approach is to evaluate fever in pregnancy with a lower threshold for diagnostic testing and empiric therapy when appropriate, particularly for systemic symptoms or evidence of organ dysfunction. Obstetric infections (chorioamnionitis, postpartum endometritis) require specific vigilance in peripartum contexts.

Laboratory and imaging interpretation: Multiple common laboratory tests have pregnancy-altered baselines—lower creatinine and BUN, lower bicarbonate, lower PaCO2, higher fibrinogen and D-dimer, higher leukocyte counts, and altered hepatic enzymes in some contexts. These altered baselines limit the utility of standard thresholds (for example, absolute D-dimer cutoffs for VTE exclusion). Imaging interpretation must account for organ displacement (e.g., renal ultrasound for hydronephrosis may be harder to interpret). When imaging involves ionizing radiation, clinician must balance diagnostic yield with fetal exposure, using appropriate shielding and alternative modalities (ultrasound, MRI without gadolinium) when feasible.

Perioperative and critical care implications: The increased cardiac output and blood volume, reduced FRC, increased aspiration risk (due to GERD and delayed gastric emptying), and hypercoagulability all influence perioperative planning. Preparation for rapid desaturation and difficult airway management is essential; awake or modified rapid-sequence techniques and maximal preoxygenation are common strategies. VTE prophylaxis decisions take into account pregnancy-specific risk factors. In critical illness, pregnancy-specific targets (e.g., oxygenation goals of maternal SpO2 ≥ 95% to ensure adequate fetal oxygen delivery) may alter management priorities.

Conclusion

Pregnancy transforms baseline physiology across organ systems in predictable ways that are essential to recognize for safe clinical care. Many common signs and laboratory abnormalities that would be alarming in nonpregnant adults—mild anemia, leukocytosis, tachycardia, elevated fibrinogen—may be benign in pregnancy, yet the same physiologic changes also create novel vulnerabilities (reduced respiratory reserve, hypercoagulability, increased infection risk, and the potential for rapid deterioration during hemorrhage). Clinical judgment in pregnancy requires integrating these physiologic norms with careful attention to warning signs that indicate pathologic processes. Awareness of pregnancy-specific normal ranges and the ways physiologic adaptation shapes symptom presentation improves diagnostic accuracy, guides therapy, and enhances maternal and fetal safety.
