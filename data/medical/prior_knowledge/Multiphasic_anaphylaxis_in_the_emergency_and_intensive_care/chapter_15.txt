Introduction

Anaphylaxis is an acute, potentially life-threatening systemic hypersensitivity reaction that requires prompt recognition, immediate treatment, and thoughtful planning for post-event observation and follow-up. From a public health and emergency medicine perspective, the frequency of anaphylaxis and the risk of recurrence after apparent clinical resolution are central determinants of observation policy. Observation policies aim to detect recurrent or biphasic reactions, ensure stabilization of organ systems, permit patient education and delivery of secondary prevention (epinephrine autoinjector, individualized action plan), and determine the need for allergy referral. At the same time, prolonged hospitalization or extended emergency-department (ED) observation increases resource use, costs, and patient burden. This chapter synthesizes population-level epidemiology of anaphylaxis and biphasic reactions, critical factors that influence estimates, and guideline-based, risk-stratified approaches to observation, disposition, and follow-up that clinicians can apply in routine practice.

# Incidence and common triggers of anaphylaxis in different populations

Epidemiology

Reported incidence of anaphylaxis varies widely according to study methodology, case definitions, data sources (ED visits, hospital admissions, population registries), and geographic region. Contemporary population-based estimates typically fall in the range of approximately 30–100 episodes per 100,000 person-years, with many high-quality studies clustering around 40–60 per 100,000 person-years. ED visit rates for anaphylaxis have generally increased over recent decades in numerous countries, although whether this reflects a true increase in incidence versus improved recognition and coding practices is debated.

Age and sex patterns

Age-specific incidence shows bimodal tendencies in many cohorts: young children (commonly food-triggered) and adults in the middle-aged bracket (often drug- or insect-venom–triggered) show peaks. In pediatric populations, food is the predominant trigger; young infants and toddlers frequently present after accidental or undiagnosed exposures. In adults, medications (antibiotics, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, anaesthetic agents) and insect stings become more common contributors. Sex distribution varies by age: food-allergic anaphylaxis is often more common in boys in childhood but tends to be more frequent among adult females in some series.

Common triggers by population and setting

- Foods: The leading cause of anaphylaxis in children and an important cause in young adults. Common culprits are peanuts, tree nuts (walnut, almond, cashew), shellfish, fish, milk, egg, and sesame. Food-induced reactions often occur rapidly after ingestion and frequently present with cutaneous and respiratory symptoms.

- Medications: A major cause in adults and hospitalized patients. Beta-lactam antibiotics, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, radiocontrast media, muscle relaxants and monoclonal antibodies are prominent triggers. Medication reactions may present with predominant cardiovascular collapse or bronchospasm.

- Insect venom: Hymenoptera (bees, wasps, hornets, yellow jackets) produce systemic reactions often seen in adults and associated with severe cardiovascular compromise.

- Latex and environmental exposures: Less common in many populations but important in certain health-care and occupational settings.

- Idiopathic or exercise-induced: A fraction of anaphylaxis cases (idiopathic anaphylaxis) have no identified trigger after evaluation. Exercise-associated anaphylaxis can be cofactor-dependent (often food-dependent).

Contextual and social determinants

Geographic differences in diet, prescribing practices, prevalence of venomous insects, and access to health care shape observed incidence patterns. Socioeconomic factors influence recognition and presentation: differences in access to primary care and allergists affect rates of ED presentation versus outpatient management.

# Reported incidence and timing of biphasic reactions and factors affecting estimates

Definition and clinical significance

A biphasic anaphylactic reaction is recurrence of symptoms after initial clinical resolution without re-exposure to the trigger. Biphasic reactions can range from mild cutaneous symptoms to severe airway or cardiovascular compromise and are the primary rationale for observation after resolution. Understanding their incidence, timing, and risk factors underpins evidence-based observation durations.

Reported incidence and sources of variability

Estimates of biphasic reaction incidence vary markedly across studies, from less than 1% to more than 20%. Many contemporary ED- and hospital-based cohorts report rates in the 2–8% range when using strict clinical criteria; older or heterogeneous datasets report higher figures. Sources of variability include:

- Case definition heterogeneity: Some studies include mild, transient urticaria as biphasic events, while others count only clinically significant recurrence requiring intervention (e.g., epinephrine, IV fluids, or airway support).

- Duration of follow-up: Short observation periods or incomplete post-discharge follow-up underestimate biphasic events that present later. Conversely, studies with long surveillance windows may capture unrelated recurrent symptoms.

- Treatment differences: Use of systemic corticosteroids and timing/doses of epinephrine at initial presentation may modify biphasic risk, complicating comparisons across cohorts with different practice patterns.

- Selection bias: Studies limited to admitted patients may reflect more severe initial reactions and higher biphasic rates.

Timing of biphasic reactions

The time from initial symptom resolution to recurrence is variable. Reported time windows span from 1 hour up to 72 hours, but most clinically relevant reviews indicate that the majority of biphasic reactions occur within 1–8 hours of initial resolution, with a substantial proportion (commonly cited as 60–80%) occurring within 4–10 hours. Severe biphasic reactions are more likely to occur earlier within that window. Rare late recurrences beyond 24–48 hours occur but are uncommon.

Risk factors associated with biphasic reactions

Multiple features correlate with increased biphasic risk, though the predictive power of individual factors is modest:

- Severity of initial reaction: Patients with hypotension, respiratory failure, or need for airway intervention have higher biphasic rates.

- Delayed administration of epinephrine: Longer intervals between symptom onset and first epinephrine dose are linked with higher risk.

- Need for multiple doses of epinephrine or intensive resuscitation: Requiring repeated intramuscular or intravenous epinephrine indicates more severe or refractory physiology and correlates with recurrence.

- Persistent or recurrent symptoms during initial observation period: Ongoing symptoms prior to documented resolution increase probability of relapse.

- Unknown or difficult-to-avoid allergen: Idiopathic anaphylaxis or reactions with unidentified triggers sometimes have higher recurrence.

- Comorbidities: Asthma (particularly poorly controlled), cardiovascular disease, beta-blocker use, and older age are associated with a greater likelihood of severe or recurrent reactions.

- Lack of early corticosteroid therapy: Evidence is inconsistent about steroids preventing biphasic reactions; observational associations exist but are not causative.

Because none of these factors precisely predict biphasic reactions, guidelines emphasize combined clinical judgment and risk stratification rather than rigid rules.

# Risk-stratified observation recommendations from major organizations and rationale

Principles guiding recommendations

Observation recommendations balance three priorities: patient safety (detection of recurrence), efficient use of health resources, and patient-centered care (minimizing unnecessary hospitalization). Guideline bodies use available evidence—predominantly observational studies, not randomized controlled trials—to recommend observation durations keyed to initial severity, response to therapy, comorbidities, and social circumstances.

Representative recommendations (summary of prevailing positions)

- World Allergy Organization (WAO): WAO’s position statements recommend observation tailored to severity and risk factors, commonly suggesting a minimum observation window (often around 4–6 hours) for mild-to-moderate reactions that resolve promptly after treatment. For severe reactions (hypotension, respiratory compromise, need for multiple epinephrine doses), extended observation or admission (often 12–24 hours or longer) is advised.

- Resuscitation Councils and national guidelines (e.g., Resuscitation Council UK, Australasian Resuscitation Council): These organizations commonly endorse a baseline observation period of 6 hours for patients whose symptoms have resolved with a single epinephrine dose and who have no other risk factors. If any of the following are present—severe initial reaction, requirement for multiple epinephrine doses, ongoing symptoms, significant comorbidity (e.g., poorly controlled asthma), beta-blocker therapy, delayed presentation or epinephrine administration—then longer observation (12–24 hours) or inpatient admission is recommended.

- Allergy/immunology societies (e.g., American Academy of Allergy, Asthma & Immunology; European Academy of Allergy and Clinical Immunology): These bodies emphasize early intramuscular epinephrine as best practice, the uncertain preventive role of corticosteroids, and advocate risk-adapted observation with a low threshold for admission in severe cases.

Common rationales across organizations

- The highest risk of clinically significant biphasic reactions occurs within the first several hours after initial resolution; thus, a window of several hours of observation is important.

- The absolute rate of biphasic reactions is low, particularly after mild, rapidly resolving episodes, so extended universal admission is unnecessary and resource-inefficient.

- Patients with high-risk features have a materially increased probability of recurrence and worse outcomes if recurrence occurs; therefore, more prolonged observation is justified.

- Patient education and rapid access to epinephrine on discharge are essential components of a safe outpatient strategy.

# Practical observation periods and criteria for admission vs discharge

Risk stratification approach

Practical application uses a graded risk-assessment to decide observation duration.

Low-risk patients (candidate for short observation and discharge if stable)
- Features: Mild-to-moderate anaphylaxis limited to cutaneous and mild gastrointestinal symptoms or bronchospasm that responds promptly to a single dose of intramuscular epinephrine and supportive care; vital signs stable; no hypotension; no need for repeated epinephrine; comorbid conditions controlled; reliable follow-up and social supports.
- Suggested observation period: commonly 4–6 hours after last dose of epinephrine and after complete symptom resolution. Some guidelines suggest 6–8 hours as a conservative minimum.

Intermediate-risk patients (consider extended observation or short admission)
- Features: More severe initial symptoms but stabilized; need for more than one dose of epinephrine but now stable; transient hypotension corrected; moderate persistent asthma or cardiovascular comorbidity; delayed administration of epinephrine; incomplete access to reliable emergency care or social support.
- Suggested observation period: frequently 12–24 hours, with clinician discretion based on trend of symptoms and comorbidities.

High-risk patients (admit or observe in monitored setting)
- Features: Initial cardiovascular collapse or respiratory failure; persistent hypotension requiring ongoing vasopressor support; recurrent or persistent symptoms despite therapy; need for intravenous epinephrine; ongoing airway compromise or requirement for supplemental oxygen/high-flow support; high-risk comorbidities (unstable cardiac disease, severe asthma, beta-blocker or ACE inhibitor use limiting epinephrine response); uncertain follow-up or inability to access emergency care.
- Suggested disposition: hospital admission in a monitored bed (step-down or ICU as indicated) for at least 24 hours with readiness to treat recurrent events.

Specific clinical criteria applied before discharge

Before discharge, ensure all of the following are met:

1. Complete resolution of signs and symptoms without ongoing need for pharmacologic support (e.g., no need for ongoing bronchodilators, vasopressors, or oxygen beyond baseline).

2. A defined observation interval elapsed after the last dose of epinephrine—commonly 4–6 hours for low-risk cases, extended up to 24 hours for higher-risk cases depending on guidelines and clinical assessment.

3. No recurrent symptoms during the observation period.

4. Vitals stable and within acceptable ranges for age: for adults, systolic blood pressure preferably >90 mmHg and no orthostatic hypotension; for children, systolic blood pressure exceeding age-appropriate thresholds (commonly cited pediatric hypotension cutoff: systolic BP < 70 + 2 × age in years for infants/young children).

5. Ability to tolerate oral intake and ambulate as appropriate; airway symptoms resolved and respiratory status stable on room air or baseline oxygen.

6. Adequate social support and ability to seek and obtain emergency care rapidly if recurrence occurs.

7. Provision of education and prescription(s) (see follow-up section) prior to discharge.

Documented discharge instructions should include explicit warnings about biphasic reactions, signs warranting immediate return (respiratory distress, lightheadedness, recurrent hives with cardiovascular symptoms), and instructions for using an epinephrine autoinjector.

Use of corticosteroids and antihistamines in observation decisions

- Antihistamines (H1 blockers) ameliorate cutaneous and pruritic symptoms but do not prevent anaphylaxis progression; they are adjunctive.

- Systemic corticosteroids are often administered with the intent of reducing biphasic risk, but high-quality evidence demonstrating prevention of biphasic reactions is lacking. Some guidelines recommend steroids as adjunct therapy, especially for patients with severe reactions, but clinicians should not rely on steroids to replace adequate observation periods.

# Strategies to balance patient safety with healthcare resource utilization

Triage and resource stewardship

A tiered, risk-based approach conserves resources while maintaining safety. Key strategies include:

- Standardized risk assessment tools and local protocols: Implementing institution-specific algorithms that map clinical findings to observation duration reduces variability, improves throughput, and ensures high-risk patients receive appropriate monitoring.

- Short-stay observation units: For patients at intermediate or lower risk who need several hours of observation, placement in an ED observation unit rather than an inpatient bed reduces costs and preserves hospital capacity.

- Rapid response to deterioration: Equip observation areas with staff trained in recognizing and managing anaphylaxis recurrence and with ready access to epinephrine and resuscitation equipment.

- Shared decision-making: Engage patients (or caregivers) in discussion of risks and benefits, particularly when evidence is uncertain (e.g., borderline cases). Document the discussion and the rationale for chosen disposition.

- Use of telemedicine and early outpatient follow-up: For selected low-risk patients discharged after adequate observation, arrange early virtual check-ins within 24–48 hours to reassess symptom recurrence and adherence to instructions.

- Education to reduce future ED utilization: Deliver targeted education (recognition of early signs, epinephrine administration, avoidance strategies), which can reduce future emergency encounters and improve quality of life.

Operational considerations

- Ensure rapid access to epinephrine autoinjectors at discharge, or confirm that patients have and can use their existing device.

- Provide written individualized emergency action plans in the patient’s preferred language and at an appropriate literacy level.

- Standardize documentation and coding for anaphylaxis to improve quality metrics and enable institutional data collection for continuous improvement.

Cost-effectiveness considerations

Although some rare biphasic reactions are severe, the low absolute incidence in low-risk patients supports selective observation rather than universal prolonged admission. Analyses generally favor observation periods guided by severity: brief observation for low-risk patients and admission for those at higher risk. Institutions should balance medicolegal risk, local outcome data, and patient preferences when defining their protocols.

# Follow-up pathways: allergy referral, patient education and provision of emergency medications

Allergy/immunology referral and diagnostic testing

Every patient who experienced anaphylaxis should be referred for specialist allergy evaluation unless a clear, unavoidable iatrogenic cause is identified and already being managed. The purposes of referral include identification of the trigger, counseling on avoidance strategies, education regarding emergency treatment, and planning for immunotherapy when appropriate (e.g., venom immunotherapy).

Timing and modality of testing

- Skin testing and specific IgE testing should generally occur after a suitable interval from the acute event (commonly recommended wait of 4–6 weeks) to reduce the risk of false-negative testing due to transient immune suppression post-event. Timing can vary by agent and clinical scenario.

- Drug allergy evaluation often requires an allergist-supervised protocol, which may include skin testing, serum specific IgE where available, and graded drug challenges. For certain agents (e.g., radiocontrast, common antibiotics), there are evidence-based testing pathways.

- Venom allergy evaluation (specific IgE and skin testing) is typically indicated for systemic reactions to stings and guides consideration of venom immunotherapy, which markedly reduces the risk of future systemic reactions.

Patient education and action plans

Education is a cornerstone of post-anaphylaxis care. Effective education covers:

- Recognition of early signs and symptoms of anaphylaxis and biphasic reactions: respiratory difficulty, throat tightness, sudden lightheadedness/syncope, progression of skin symptoms with systemic features.

- Immediate use of intramuscular epinephrine: instruct patients and caregivers that epinephrine is the first-line treatment and should be administered promptly; if symptoms persist or recur after 5–15 minutes, repeat dosing is indicated.

- How and when to seek emergency care after epinephrine administration: call emergency services immediately after administering epinephrine even if symptoms improve.

- Avoidance strategies tailored to the identified or suspected trigger (reading labels, asking about ingredients in restaurants, medication reconciliation).

- An individualized anaphylaxis emergency action plan written and reviewed in the ED and reinforced at follow-up.

Provision and prescribing of emergency medications

- Epinephrine autoinjector dosing and prescriptions: ensure patients leave with an epinephrine autoinjector (or multiple devices where indicated) and instructions on its use. Common autoinjector doses:

  - 0.15 mg for children weighing approximately 15–30 kg.

  - 0.3 mg for children/adults weighing approximately 30–60 kg.

  - 0.5 mg autoinjectors are available in some markets for larger adults; dosing should be individualized.

  Clinicians should adhere to local formulary options and consider device availability when prescribing. Patients requiring multiple autoinjectors (e.g., due to distance from medical care, severe prior reactions, or large body size) should be supplied accordingly.

- Adjunctive medications: Prescribe oral H1 antihistamines and, when appropriate, short courses of systemic corticosteroids for adjunctive control of symptoms (e.g., prednisone 0.5–1 mg/kg for short courses), recognizing that steroids have unproven efficacy in preventing biphasic reactions. Inhaled beta-2 agonist prescriptions are appropriate for patients with reactive airways disease.

Logistics and access

- Ensure patients have prescriptions filled or provided prior to discharge; where possible, provide an autoinjector in the ED if access or affordability would otherwise delay possession.

- Provide training and demonstration of autoinjector use (ideally using a trainer device) and written/visual materials.

- Arrange allergist referral and provide a clear timeline: many centers aim for evaluation within 1–6 weeks depending on local capacity; urgent referral is required for severe or unclear cases.

- Discuss workplace, school, or travel implications with documentation (e.g., medical alert identification, school action plans).

Conclusion

Observation after anaphylaxis must be individualized according to the severity of the index event, response to treatment, comorbidities, and social context. Epidemiologic data indicate that while biphasic reactions can occur, the absolute risk in low-risk patients is modest and frontloading of observation resources for high-risk presentations is reasonable. Clinicians should apply standardized, evidence-informed protocols that specify minimum observation intervals, clear criteria for admission, and mandatory discharge components (education, epinephrine autoinjector prescription, and allergy referral). Combining risk stratification with patient-centered education and timely specialist follow-up achieves the dual aims of patient safety and judicious use of health-care resources.
