Introduction

Immediate hypersensitivity (Type I hypersensitivity) is an evolutionarily ancient immune program that protects against parasites but can cause pathologic responses to otherwise innocuous antigens (allergens). Clinically, Type I reactions manifest rapidly after allergen exposure and range from localized urticaria and allergic rhinitis to life‑threatening systemic anaphylaxis. Understanding the cellular players, antibody classes, receptor signalling, downstream mediators and amplifying pathways is essential to interpret clinical findings, choose diagnostic tests, and apply rational therapy in allergic disease. This chapter reviews the classification of hypersensitivity reactions with emphasis on Type I mechanisms, details the processes of sensitization and IgE biology, explicates mast cell and basophil FcεRI signalling and degranulation, describes complement and other amplifying inflammatory pathways, summarizes the key cytokines and chemokines that shape allergic inflammation, and links major mediators (histamine, tryptase, prostaglandins, leukotrienes, platelet‑activating factor) to their clinical effects.

# Classification of hypersensitivity reactions (Gell and Coombs) with emphasis on immediate (Type I) reactions

The Gell and Coombs classification organizes immune‑mediated tissue injury into four mechanistic types. This schema remains clinically useful for mapping immune mechanisms to presentations, although many clinical syndromes involve mixed mechanisms.

- Type I (Immediate, IgE‑mediated): Antigen‑specific IgE bound to high‑affinity Fc receptors on mast cells and basophils triggers rapid mediator release following allergen cross‑linking. Time course is minutes to hours. Examples: anaphylaxis, allergic asthma exacerbation, allergic rhinitis, urticaria, angioedema (some forms).
- Type II (Antibody‑mediated cytotoxicity): IgG or IgM antibodies directed against cell surface or matrix antigens lead to complement activation, opsonization, or ADCC. Examples: autoimmune hemolytic anemia, transfusion reactions, some drug‑induced cytopenias.
- Type III (Immune complex): Deposition of antigen–antibody complexes activates complement and neutrophil‑mediated inflammation (immune complex vasculitis). Examples: serum sickness, certain forms of glomerulonephritis.
- Type IV (Delayed‑type, T‑cell mediated): Antigen‑specific T lymphocytes and macrophages effect tissue injury over 48–72 hours. Subtypes include classical Th1 DTH and Th17/neutrophil‑driven responses; contact dermatitis and tuberculin reactions are classic examples.

Type I responses are distinguished by their dependence on IgE, tissue mast cells and blood basophils, and the very rapid onset owing to preformed mediators in granules as well as newly synthesized lipid mediators. Clinically, the rapidity of onset guides initial management (epinephrine for anaphylaxis) and the diagnostic approach (measurement of serum tryptase within hours, skin testing and specific IgE assays later).

# Role of IgE, antigen presentation and sensitisation

Sensitization describes the induction of allergen‑specific IgE and its loading onto effector cells before clinical reactions occur. The major steps include allergen uptake, antigen presentation, T helper differentiation toward a Th2 phenotype, B cell class switching to IgE, and binding of IgE to FcεRI on mast cells and basophils.

Allergen uptake and presentation: Epithelial barriers (skin, airway, gut) and resident antigen‑presenting cells (APCs)—dendritic cells, Langerhans cells—capture environmental proteins. The context of exposure (epithelial cytokines, protease activity of the allergen, co‑exposures such as adjuvants) shapes the immune outcome. APCs process proteins and present peptide fragments in the context of MHC class II to naive CD4+ T cells in secondary lymphoid organs.

T cell polarization to Th2: Several signals favor Th2 differentiation: IL‑4 is central in driving the Th2 program; epithelial‑derived cytokines such as thymic stromal lymphopoietin (TSLP), IL‑25 and IL‑33 promote dendritic cell conditioning that biases toward Th2 responses. Genetic predisposition, age, microbial exposures (the hygiene hypothesis), and the nature of the allergen influence this polarization.

B cell switching to IgE: Under the influence of Th2 cytokines—principally IL‑4 and IL‑13—B cells undergo class switch recombination to IgE. Interactions with CD40L on Th cells are required. The resulting plasma cells secrete allergen‑specific IgE into the circulation.

IgE binding and sensitization: Secreted IgE binds with very high affinity (Kd ~10^-10 M) to FcεRI on mast cells and basophils, effectively coating these cells with allergen‑specific receptors. FcεRI expression is upregulated by the presence of IgE, creating a feed‑forward amplification—higher serum IgE increases receptor density and cellular sensitivity. Tissue mast cells often remain long‑lived and can carry IgE for weeks to months.

Serologic testing and normal ranges: Total serum IgE has wide interindividual and age‑dependent variability; approximate “normal” adult ranges often quoted are <100–150 IU/mL, but values must be interpreted in clinical context. Allergen‑specific IgE tests (ImmunoCAP and others) report quantitative values and classes that correlate imperfectly with clinical reactivity; positive specific IgE indicates sensitization but not necessarily clinical allergy. Skin prick testing provides a functional in vivo readout of immediate hypersensitivity and is often more sensitive than serum tests, but both have roles depending on medication use and skin conditions.

# Mast cell and basophil activation: FcεRI cross‑linking and downstream signalling

The immediate effector response in Type I hypersensitivity is driven by degranulation of mast cells and basophils after cross‑linking of FcεRI by multivalent allergen. FcεRI is a tetrameric (αβγ2) receptor on mast cells and basophils (and trimeric αγ2 forms on other cells) with a high‑affinity IgE‑binding α chain and signaling subunits containing immunoreceptor tyrosine‑based activation motifs (ITAMs).

Receptor cross‑linking and early phosphorylation events: When allergen simultaneously binds two or more IgE molecules fixed to FcεRI, receptors are brought into close proximity and ITAMs are phosphorylated by Src family kinases—primarily Lyn. Phosphorylated ITAMs recruit Syk tyrosine kinase, a critical proximal signaling enzyme. Syk amplification is required for downstream propagation of the activation signal.

Signalosomes and adapter proteins: Activated Syk phosphorylates scaffolding and adapter proteins such as LAT (linker for activation of T cells) and SLP‑76, organizing multiprotein complexes that recruit enzymes including phospholipase Cγ (PLCγ) and phosphoinositide 3‑kinase (PI3K). These complexes coordinate calcium mobilization, actin cytoskeleton remodeling, and vesicle trafficking.

Second messenger cascades: PLCγ hydrolyzes phosphatidylinositol 4,5‑bisphosphate to generate diacylglycerol (DAG) and inositol 1,4,5‑trisphosphate (IP3). IP3 drives release of calcium from endoplasmic reticulum stores, followed by store‑operated calcium entry through channels such as Orai1, producing sustained intracellular calcium elevation. Calcium and DAG together activate protein kinase C (PKC) isoforms and other calcium‑dependent enzymes that orchestrate exocytosis of preformed granule contents.

Degranulation: The rise in intracellular calcium and cytoskeletal changes promote fusion of secretory granules with the plasma membrane, releasing preformed mediators such as histamine, proteases (including tryptase and chymase), and heparin within minutes. Degranulation is a regulated, energy‑dependent process mediated by SNARE proteins and other fusion machinery.

De novo synthesis of lipid mediators and cytokines: Parallel to rapid degranulation, activation of PLA2 (phospholipase A2) liberates arachidonic acid for conversion into prostaglandins (via cyclooxygenases) and leukotrienes (via 5‑lipoxygenase). Gene transcription pathways (MAPK, NF‑κB, and AP‑1) induce synthesis and release of cytokines and chemokines over hours.

Regulatory receptors and negative signals: FcεRI signaling is modulated by co‑receptors and inhibitory pathways. FcγRIIb (an inhibitory IgG Fc receptor containing ITIM motifs) delivers negative signals when co‑aggregated, reducing degranulation. Other receptors—such as Siglecs, CD200R, and inhibitory cytokine receptors—can attenuate responses. Pharmacologic agents (e.g., omalizumab) reduce free IgE, downregulate FcεRI expression, and blunt cellular responsiveness.

Differences between mast cells and basophils: Mast cells are tissue resident, long‑lived, and heterogeneous (mucosal vs connective tissue types) with differential mediator content and receptor expression. Basophils circulate and are recruited to tissues; they also express FcεRI and can release histamine, LTC4, and Th2‑polarizing cytokines (notably IL‑4) rapidly upon activation. Both cell types contribute to immediate and late phase allergic responses.

# Complement and other amplifying inflammatory pathways

Complement can amplify Type I hypersensitivity though it is not typically the primary initiating mechanism. Key complement fragments—C3a and C5a, termed anaphylatoxins—bind to their G protein–coupled receptors on mast cells, basophils, neutrophils and other immune cells to enhance mediator release and leukocyte recruitment.

Anaphylatoxins as co‑activators: C3a and C5a can induce direct degranulation of mast cells and basophils or synergize with FcεRI signaling to lower the threshold for activation. For example, C5a is a potent chemoattractant and activator of neutrophils and a contributor to vasodilation and increased microvascular permeability in inflammatory foci.

Alternative complement activation: Immune complex formation (Type III mechanisms) or pathogen‑triggered complement activation can generate anaphylatoxins that potentiate allergic inflammation. Complement activation products also upregulate adhesion molecules on endothelium, promoting leukocyte extravasation into allergen‑exposed tissues.

Coagulation and kinin systems: Other proteolytic cascades (coagulation cascade, kallikrein‑kinin system) intersect with allergic pathways. Bradykinin increases vascular permeability and pain; it contributes to angioedema in hereditary or ACE inhibitor–associated contexts. Platelet‑activating factor (PAF) is generated by multiple cell types and contributes both to bronchospasm and vascular collapse in severe anaphylaxis.

Innate immune amplifiers: Pattern recognition receptors, alarmins (TSLP, IL‑33), and epithelial cell–derived chemotactic factors amplify type 2 inflammation and skew the microenvironment toward allergic responses. These innate signals can sustain and perpetuate the response beyond the immediate phase.

# Cytokines and chemokines in allergic inflammation

A Th2‑skewed cytokine milieu defines the adaptive arm of allergic immunity; innate cytokines from barrier cells further condition the response. The principal cytokines and their effects are:

- IL‑4: Drives naive CD4+ T cell differentiation into Th2 cells and mediates B cell class switching to IgE. IL‑4 promotes expression of vascular cell adhesion molecules and contributes to mucus production.
- IL‑13: Shares many functions with IL‑4 (via overlapping receptor components) and is particularly important for airway hyperresponsiveness, goblet cell metaplasia, mucus hypersecretion, and tissue remodeling.
- IL‑5: Key eosinophil growth, differentiation, activation and survival factor. IL‑5–mediated eosinophilia contributes to tissue damage in asthma, eosinophilic esophagitis, and other eosinophilic disorders.
- IL‑3 and GM‑CSF: Promote growth and activation of basophils, eosinophils, and mast cells.
- TNF‑α: Produced by mast cells and other cells; increases endothelial activation and sustains inflammation.
- IL‑9: Supports mast cell proliferation and mucosal mast cell expansion.
- TSLP, IL‑25, IL‑33: Epithelial‑derived “alarmins” that activate type 2 innate lymphoid cells (ILC2s) and condition dendritic cells to drive Th2 polarization. They are central to the bridge between barrier dysfunction and type 2 immunity.

Chemokines direct cellular recruitment and positioning. Eotaxins (CCL11, CCL24, CCL26) selectively recruit eosinophils via CCR3. CCL17 (TARC) and CCL22 recruit Th2 cells via CCR4. CXCL8 (IL‑8) attracts neutrophils in mixed inflammatory patterns.

Late‑phase response and chronicity: The immediate degranulation phase is followed hours later by a late phase driven by newly synthesized cytokines and chemokines, leading to cellular infiltration (eosinophils, basophils, Th2 lymphocytes) and sustained tissue inflammation. Chronic exposure produces remodeling: subepithelial fibrosis, smooth muscle hypertrophy/hyperplasia, and persistent mucus production, particularly relevant in chronic asthma.

Therapeutic targeting: Biological therapies exploit these cytokine pathways—anti‑IL‑5 (mepolizumab, reslizumab), anti‑IL‑5Rα (benralizumab) for eosinophilic disease; anti‑IL‑4Rα (dupilumab) blocks IL‑4/IL‑13 signaling and reduces IgE production and mucus; anti‑TSLP and anti‑IL‑33 therapies are under evaluation. Understanding which cytokines predominate in a patient’s phenotype guides therapy selection.

# Clinical correlates of mediator effects (histamine, tryptase, prostaglandins, leukotrienes, platelet‑activating factor)

The clinical manifestations of immediate hypersensitivity reflect the cumulative effects of preformed granule mediators and newly synthesized lipid mediators and cytokines.

Histamine: A small, rapidly acting amine stored in mast cell and basophil granules. Histamine acts through at least four G protein–coupled receptors:

- H1 receptor: Mediates bronchoconstriction, increased vascular permeability, endothelial contraction (wheal and flare), pruritus via nerve endings, and vasodilation. H1 antagonists (antihistamines) are effective for urticaria, allergic rhinitis and itch but provide limited protection in systemic anaphylaxis.
- H2 receptor: Involved in gastric acid secretion and some vasodilatory effects. H2 blockers historically used adjunctively in anaphylaxis.
- H3 and H4 receptors: Modulate neuronal transmission and immune cell chemotaxis; H4 is implicated in eosinophil and mast cell recruitment and pruritus.

Clinically, histamine is responsible for the immediate wheal and flare seen on skin testing, nasal congestion, pruritus, and bronchospasm to a degree. Antihistamines mitigate many cutaneous symptoms but do not reverse airway obstruction or hypotension in severe anaphylaxis—epinephrine is required.

Tryptase and proteases: Tryptase (a tetrameric serine protease) is one of the most specific markers of mast cell activation. Baseline serum tryptase varies by individual; a commonly used upper limit is approximately 11.4 ng/mL, though reference ranges vary by assay and population. In systemic anaphylaxis, tryptase typically rises within 1–3 hours of symptom onset and peaks around 1–6 hours; a suggested criterion for significant mast cell activation is an increase of acute tryptase over baseline by at least 20% + 2 ng/mL (i.e., acute ≥ baseline × 1.2 + 2 ng/mL). Elevated tryptase supports, but does not definitively prove, mast cell degranulation as the cause of acute events, since tryptase can be normal in food‑triggered anaphylaxis and other contexts. Tryptase and other proteases (chymase) have local tissue effects—matrix remodeling, activation of protease‑activated receptors, and promotion of inflammation.

Prostaglandins: Mast cells produce prostaglandin D2 (PGD2) via cyclooxygenase pathway—PGD2 is a bronchoconstrictor, vasodilator and chemoattractant for Th2 cells and eosinophils. PGD2 metabolites correlate with mast cell activation and are implicated in airway narrowing in asthma. Cyclooxygenase inhibitors (NSAIDs) can alter prostaglandin balance and, paradoxically, worsen certain types of urticaria or trigger aspirin‑exacerbated respiratory disease by shunting arachidonic acid to leukotriene pathways.

Leukotrienes: Cysteinyl leukotrienes (LTC4, LTD4, LTE4) are powerful and slow‑acting bronchoconstrictors, increase vascular permeability and promote mucus secretion. They are synthesized via 5‑lipoxygenase rapidly after activation and contribute to prolonged bronchospasm that often responds to leukotriene receptor antagonists (montelukast) or 5‑lipoxygenase inhibitors. The relative persistence and potency of leukotrienes make them major mediators in asthma exacerbations and some systemic allergic responses.

Platelet‑activating factor (PAF): PAF is a potent phospholipid mediator produced by mast cells, basophils, neutrophils, platelets and endothelial cells. PAF induces bronchoconstriction, vasodilation, increased vascular permeability and platelet aggregation. High PAF levels correlate with severity of anaphylaxis; PAF acetylhydrolase (the enzyme that degrades PAF) deficiency or low activity has been associated with more severe anaphylaxis in some studies. PAF is also implicated in cardiovascular collapse during severe reactions.

Temporal phases and organ effects: The immediate phase (minutes) is dominated by histamine and preformed mediators causing urticaria, angioedema, bronchospasm and hypotension. The late phase (hours) involves leukotrienes, prostaglandins, cytokines and recruited inflammatory cells causing sustained airway inflammation, tissue swelling and sometimes biphasic anaphylactic phenomena. Organ‑specific effects include:

- Skin: Urticaria (wheals) from localized vasodilation and increased permeability; pruritus from histamine and neuropeptides. Angioedema reflects deeper dermal/subcutaneous vascular leakage.
- Respiratory tract: Upper airway edema and bronchospasm produce stridor, wheeze and airflow obstruction. Mucus hypersecretion is driven by IL‑13 and leukotrienes.
- Cardiovascular: Vasodilation, increased vascular permeability and reduced venous return lead to hypotension and shock. Myocardial depressant effects of mediators and mediator‑induced arrhythmias can occur.
- Gastrointestinal: Smooth muscle contraction causes cramping, vomiting and diarrhea.

Diagnostic implications and laboratory use: Serum tryptase measurement is helpful when obtained appropriately (ideally within 1–3 hours of symptom onset, with a paired baseline sample). Measurement of allergen‑specific IgE and skin testing (prick or intradermal) are useful for identifying sensitization to suspected allergens once the patient is stable. Pulmonary function testing with bronchoprovocation may reveal airway hyperresponsiveness in asthma. Biomarkers like fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO) reflect eosinophilic airway inflammation driven by IL‑13.

Conclusion

Type I hypersensitivity is a multi‑step process that begins with sensitization and IgE production and culminates in rapid effector responses mediated by mast cells and basophils. FcεRI signalling, downstream calcium and kinase cascades, and the generation of preformed and newly synthesized mediators produce the characteristic clinical spectrum—from cutaneous manifestations to life‑threatening anaphylaxis. Complement and other innate pathways amplify responses while cytokines and chemokines orchestrate late‑phase inflammation and chronic remodeling. Recognizing mediator‑specific effects guides acute management (epinephrine for anaphylaxis; antihistamines and inhaled bronchodilators for milder symptoms) and informs targeted chronic therapies (anti‑IgE, anti‑IL‑5, anti‑IL‑4/13, leukotriene modifiers). A mechanistic understanding aids appropriate diagnostic testing, risk stratification and personalized therapeutic strategies in allergic disease.

Introduction

Mast cells are tissue-resident immune effector cells that occupy key sentinel positions at the interface between the host and the external environment. They are best known for their central role in IgE-mediated immediate hypersensitivity and anaphylaxis, but their biology extends widely into host defense, tissue remodeling, and chronic inflammatory disease. Understanding mast cell types, development, the contents of their secretory granules and the profile of mediators they generate de novo is essential for interpreting the systemic physiology of mediator release, for recognizing and diagnosing mast cell activation syndromes, and for rational pharmacologic intervention. This chapter integrates the cellular and molecular basis of mast cell function with the clinical effects of their mediators, the regulation of activation thresholds, the laboratory detection of mediator release, and the principal pharmacologic targets used to block mediator actions.

# Mast cell types, distribution and lifecycle

Mast cells arise from hematopoietic progenitors in the bone marrow, circulate in an immature form, and complete differentiation in peripheral tissues. Unlike circulating granulocytes, mature mast cells are tissue-resident and do not recirculate. Key growth and differentiation signals include stem cell factor (SCF, the ligand for the receptor tyrosine kinase KIT) and several cytokines (notably IL-3, IL-4, IL-9, IL-33). KIT signaling is central to mast cell survival, proliferation, maturation and chemotaxis; genetic or pharmacologic perturbations of KIT profoundly alter mast cell numbers and function.

In humans, mast cells are classically divided by their protease content and location into two major phenotypes: tryptase-only mast cells (often designated MCT) and tryptase-plus-chymase mast cells (MCTC). MCT cells are more common in mucosal sites such as the gastrointestinal tract, lung mucosa and are often associated with allergic inflammation. MCTC cells predominate in connective tissues including skin and perivascular regions and contain tryptase, chymase, carboxypeptidase A and serine proteases plus abundant heparin in their granules. This protease profile influences how mast cells contribute to tissue-specific responses—e.g., chymase can activate matrix remodeling and modulate angiotensin II metabolism, while tryptase activates protease-activated receptors (PARs) on endothelium and nerve endings.

Anatomic distribution places mast cells at strategic sites: around small blood vessels and lymphatics, in the dermis, at mucosal surfaces (airways, gut), and adjacent to nerves. This makes their mediators especially effective at altering vascular tone and permeability, modulating smooth muscle, and engaging sensory neurons. Mast cells are long-lived compared with many leukocytes, with lifespan dependent on tissue cues; their numbers are dynamically regulated by local proliferation, recruitment of progenitors, and survival signals (SCF, IL-4, IL-9). In disease states such as systemic mastocytosis clonal expansion driven by activating KIT mutations (commonly KIT D816V) leads to markedly increased mast cell burden and constitutive activation.

# Preformed mediators (histamine, tryptase, heparin) and de novo mediators (leukotrienes, prostaglandins, cytokines)

Mast cell activation induces a biphasic secretory program. Immediately upon activation (seconds to minutes) there is exocytosis of cytoplasmic granules releasing preformed mediators. Over minutes to hours, enzymes and synthetic pathways generate new lipid mediators and cytokines that sustain and shape the inflammatory response.

Preformed mediators

- Histamine: Stored in dense granules complexed with proteoglycans (heparin, chondroitin sulfate). Release leads to rapid effects on vascular and bronchial smooth muscle via histamine receptors (H1, H2, H3, H4). Histamine is a prototypic mediator of pruritus, flushing, vasodilation, and increased microvascular permeability.

- Tryptase: A serine protease abundant in human mast cell granules, especially in MCT and MCTC subtypes. Tryptase exists as tetramers stabilized by interactions with proteoglycans and is relatively resistant to proteolysis and inactivation. Tryptase can activate protease-activated receptor-2 (PAR-2) on endothelial and nerve cells, cleave extracellular matrix components, modulate coagulation factors, and serve as a clinically useful biomarker of mast cell activation because of its relative specificity for mast cells.

- Heparin and other proteoglycans: Anionic polymers that stabilize granule proteases and have vasoactive and anticoagulant properties following release. Heparin contributes to the granule architecture and binds positively charged mediators.

Other preformed mediators include small quantities of serotonin (in rodents more than in humans), and stored cytokines such as TNF-alpha in some contexts.

De novo mediators

- Leukotrienes: Synthesized from arachidonic acid via 5-lipoxygenase (5-LO) into leukotriene C4 (LTC4), which is rapidly metabolized to LTD4 and LTE4. These cysteinyl leukotrienes are potent bronchoconstrictors, increase vascular permeability, and enhance mucus secretion. They are central to the sustained phase of bronchospasm and edema in allergic responses.

- Prostaglandins: Particularly prostaglandin D2 (PGD2) produced by cyclooxygenase (COX) and PGD synthase in mast cells. PGD2 causes bronchoconstriction, vasodilation, and recruits Th2 cells and eosinophils (via CRTH2 receptor), contributing to late-phase allergic inflammation.

- Platelet-activating factor (PAF): A phospholipid mediator generated rapidly that induces bronchoconstriction, increases vascular permeability, and can produce hypotension. High PAF levels correlate with more severe anaphylaxis in some studies.

- Cytokines and chemokines: Activated mast cells transcribe and secrete a wide range of cytokines (IL-4, IL-5, IL-6, IL-13, TNF-alpha, GM-CSF) and chemokines (CCL2/MCP-1, CCL3/MIP-1alpha). These mediators take hours to be produced and underlie cell recruitment (eosinophils, basophils, neutrophils), shifts in adaptive immunity toward Th2 responses, and chronic tissue changes.

- Nitric oxide (NO): Mast cells can induce endothelial NO production indirectly; mast cell-derived mediators can also modulate NO pathways contributing to vasodilation.

The pattern of mediator release (which mediators, quantities, and timing) differs by stimulus and mast cell subtype. IgE cross-linking typically causes rapid degranulation with histamine and tryptase release followed by lipid mediator synthesis; certain toll-like receptor (TLR) stimuli can favor cytokine production with limited degranulation.

# Effects of mediators on vascular tone, permeability, bronchial smooth muscle and the nervous system

The systemic physiologic manifestations of mast cell activation derive from the combined actions of their mediators on the vasculature, airways, and peripheral nervous system.

Vascular tone and permeability

Histamine acts on vascular endothelium and smooth muscle via H1 and H2 receptors. H1 receptor activation on endothelium increases intracellular calcium and induces endothelial cell contraction, creating interendothelial gaps that permit extravasation of plasma proteins and fluid — the basis for urticaria and angioedema. H1-mediated endothelial activation also promotes expression of adhesion molecules and chemokine release. H2 receptors on vascular smooth muscle cause vasodilation via cAMP increases, contributing to flushing and hypotension. The net effect of massive histamine release is a fall in systemic vascular resistance with increased vascular leak, third-spacing of intravascular volume, and intravascular hypovolemia.

Tryptase contributes to vascular effects by activating PAR-2 and remodeling the extracellular matrix; it also can enhance vascular permeability indirectly by generating kinins or modifying complement components. Heparin released from granules exerts anticoagulant effects locally and systemically, which can contribute to bleeding tendencies in some massive mast cell activation scenarios.

Leukotrienes (LTC4/D4/E4) and PGD2 potentiate vascular permeability and recruit leukocytes; PAF increases capillary leak and can produce hypotension. The combined action of these mediators during systemic anaphylaxis leads to rapid edema (including laryngeal edema) and distributive shock.

Bronchial smooth muscle

Cysteinyl leukotrienes are among the most potent bronchoconstrictors produced by inflammatory cells, acting on CysLT1 and CysLT2 receptors on airway smooth muscle and contributing to bronchospasm, increased mucus secretion, and airway hyperresponsiveness. Histamine, acting via H1 receptors on airway smooth muscle, produces immediate bronchoconstriction, though its effect is generally shorter-lived than leukotrienes. PGD2 also causes bronchoconstriction and is a chemoattractant for Th2 cells and eosinophils, promoting sustained airway inflammation. Nitric oxide and PAF can exacerbate lower airway dysfunction in severe reactions.

Nervous system

Mast cell mediators directly and indirectly interact with the peripheral nervous system. Histamine and tryptase stimulate sensory C-fibers and trigger neurogenic inflammation, which results in itching, burning, and reflex bronchoconstriction. Tryptase activation of PAR-2 on neurons increases nociceptive signaling and can potentiate pain and pruritus. Cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-alpha sensitize nociceptors and modulate central neuronal circuits, contributing to systemic symptoms such as malaise. Mast cells are also situated adjacent to nerves and can participate in bidirectional communication: neuronal mediators (substance P, CGRP) can trigger mast cell degranulation, and mast cell mediators modulate neuronal activity.

Cardiovascular collapse during severe anaphylaxis can result from profound vasodilation and increased vascular permeability producing hypovolemia, direct negative inotropic effects of mediators, and arrhythmogenic influences (e.g., histamine-induced coronary vasospasm). Pulmonary vasoconstriction from mediator effects can further impair oxygenation and right ventricular function.

# Regulation of mast cell activation and thresholds for degranulation

Mast cell activation is regulated at multiple levels: receptor engagement and signal strength, intracellular signaling cascades, cross-talk with other cells and mediators, and the balance of activating versus inhibitory receptors.

Receptors and primary activation pathways

The high-affinity IgE receptor, FcεRI, is the canonical pathway for classical allergic activation. Cross-linking of IgE bound to FcεRI by multivalent antigen induces phosphorylation of the receptor ITAMs by Lyn kinase, recruitment and activation of Syk kinase, and initiation of downstream signaling through LAT/SLP-76 leading to phospholipase C gamma (PLCγ) activation, inositol trisphosphate (IP3)-mediated calcium release, and protein kinase C activation. Calcium mobilization is essential for granule exocytosis. PI3K signaling contributes to cytoskeletal rearrangements and sustained mediator production.

Non-IgE triggers include complement split products (C3a, C5a) acting on their receptors, direct activation by drugs (e.g., radiocontrast, some antibiotics and opioids) via mechanisms that may involve MRGPRX2 (Mas-related G-protein–coupled receptor X2), physical stimuli (pressure), and pathogen-associated molecular patterns (TLRs) that can favor cytokine production without full degranulation.

Threshold modulation

The propensity of a mast cell to degranulate in response to a given stimulus depends on several modulatory influences:

- IgE load: The density of surface-bound IgE enhances sensitivity to antigen. High levels of specific IgE lower activation thresholds.

- Cytokine milieu: IL-4 and IL-33 can “prime” mast cells, increasing FcεRI expression and augmenting mediator synthesis. IL-10 and TGF-beta provide inhibitory signals.

- Co-receptors: Engagement of inhibitory receptors such as FcγRIIb (via IgG immune complexes) recruits phosphatases (SHP-1/2) that attenuate FcεRI signaling. Siglecs and other ITIM-bearing receptors similarly dampen activation.

- Intracellular signaling regulators: Proteins that modulate Syk, BTK (Bruton's tyrosine kinase), PI3K, and downstream calcium signaling set the amplitude of response. Pharmacologic inhibitors of Syk or BTK reduce degranulation and mediator production.

- Medications and comorbid conditions: Beta-blockers reduce the compensatory adrenergic response during anaphylaxis and may worsen outcomes. ACE inhibitors can augment bradykinin-mediated angioedema by blocking bradykinin degradation, potentially lowering the threshold for severe edema in mast cell–mediated or bradykinin-mediated processes. Chronic corticosteroids and anti-IgE therapy (omalizumab) can raise activation thresholds by lowering IgE levels and reducing FcεRI expression.

- Receptor desensitization and endocytosis: Following stimulation, receptor internalization and negative feedback mechanisms reduce further responses; repeated low-level stimulation can lead to altered responsiveness.

Collectively, the balance of activating and inhibitory influences determines whether a given stimulus produces limited local symptoms, a late-phase inflammatory response, or fulminant systemic mediator release as in anaphylaxis.

# Laboratory detection of mediator release (tryptase, histamine, metabolites)

Accurate laboratory documentation of mast cell activation is important for diagnosing anaphylaxis and mast cell activation syndromes, for distinguishing immunologic from non-immunologic mechanisms, and for following patients with mastocytosis. Several mediators are clinically useful biomarkers; each has specific kinetics, specimen requirements and interpretive caveats.

Tryptase

Tryptase is the most widely used biomarker of mast cell activation because it is relatively specific to mast cells and stable compared with free histamine. Baseline (total) tryptase primarily reflects mast cell burden; elevated baseline tryptase may indicate increased mast cell numbers as in systemic mastocytosis. Typical reference ranges vary by lab, but a commonly used upper limit of normal is approximately 11.4 ng/mL. For suspected anaphylaxis, an increase in serum total tryptase drawn during or shortly after the event supports mast cell activation. The diagnostic threshold commonly used is an acute tryptase that exceeds baseline by the formula:

acute tryptase ≥ (1.2 × baseline tryptase) + 2 ng/mL.

Because tryptase peaks later than histamine—usually within 1–2 hours after symptom onset, and remains elevated for up to 6–8 hours—optimal sampling for diagnosis is: an acute specimen drawn as soon as possible within 1–3 hours after symptom onset (ideally within 30–120 minutes) and a baseline specimen ≥24 hours after resolution of symptoms (or at least 48 hours later) to establish baseline. Interpretation must consider that not all anaphylactic events produce large tryptase rises (e.g., many food-induced anaphylaxis episodes show small or absent tryptase elevations), and that baseline tryptase can be elevated for reasons other than mastocytosis (familial hypertryptasemia due to increased TPSAB1 copy number, renal impairment).

Histamine and metabolites

Plasma histamine rises very rapidly after degranulation but has a short half-life (minutes) and is labile, making it difficult to measure reliably in routine clinical practice. Proper specimen handling (immediate chilling, use of appropriate anticoagulants and rapid assay) is critical. Because of these challenges, measurement of stable histamine metabolites is often preferred.

Urinary N-methylhistamine (and other methylated metabolites) offers a more stable integrated measure of histamine release over hours. Elevated urinary N-methylhistamine in a timed or 24-hour urine collection supports mast cell activation; reference ranges depend on the assay and are usually normalized to creatinine. Similarly, 24-hour urinary prostaglandin D2 metabolite (11-beta-PGF2alpha) and leukotriene metabolites (urinary LTE4) can reflect mast cell mediator synthesis. Urinary LTE4 may be elevated in aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease and in severe anaphylaxis involving leukotriene production.

Other markers

Plasma or urinary levels of PAF and PAF acetylhydrolase have been investigated; low PAF acetylhydrolase activity has been associated in some series with severe anaphylaxis, but routine clinical assays are not widely available.

Laboratory sampling considerations

- Timing: Choose analytes with appropriate kinetics. Histamine requires immediate sampling (within minutes) and rapid processing. Tryptase has a wider window (1–6 hours). Urinary metabolites reflect integrated release over longer periods.

- Baseline comparisons: Interpretation often relies on comparison to a reliable baseline measurement obtained after recovery.

- Preanalytical handling: Many mediators are labile; follow lab instructions for collection tubes, temperature, and timing.

- Specificity: Elevated markers indicate mast cell activation but must be interpreted in context. Tryptase elevation is more specific for mast cell degranulation, whereas histamine can derive from other cells (e.g., basophils) and metabolites can be influenced by other conditions.

# Pharmacologic targets to block mediator actions

Therapeutic interventions in mast cell–mediated disease and anaphylaxis address three conceptual targets: immediate reversal of life-threatening physiologic derangements, blockade of mediator effects, and reduction of future activation or mediator production.

Immediate, life-saving therapy

- Epinephrine: The first-line treatment for systemic anaphylaxis. Epinephrine acts on α1-adrenergic receptors to counteract vasodilation and increase vascular tone, on β1 receptors to support cardiac output, and on β2 receptors to induce bronchodilation and reduce mediator release from mast cells. Intramuscular epinephrine (0.3–0.5 mg of 1:1000 solution in adults) into the anterolateral thigh is recommended urgently. Repeat dosing every 5–10 minutes may be necessary. Intravenous epinephrine is reserved for refractory or peri-arrest situations and requires careful titration.

Blocking mediator actions

- Antihistamines: H1 receptor antagonists (e.g., cetirizine, loratadine, diphenhydramine) blunt histamine-mediated pruritus, urticaria and some bronchoconstriction; they do not reverse airway obstruction or hypotension in severe anaphylaxis and are adjunctive therapies. H2 receptor antagonists (e.g., ranitidine, famotidine) can provide additional control of vasodilation and gastric acid-related symptoms when combined with H1 blockers, particularly for cutaneous and gastrointestinal manifestations.

- Bronchodilators: Inhaled short-acting β2-agonists (albuterol/salbutamol) provide symptomatic relief of bronchospasm. Systemic beta-agonists are not substitutes for epinephrine in anaphylaxis because they lack α-adrenergic vasoconstrictive effects.

- Leukotriene pathway inhibitors: Leukotriene receptor antagonists (e.g., montelukast acting at CysLT1 receptors) reduce leukotriene-mediated bronchoconstriction and are useful in chronic management of asthma and aspirin-sensitive disease. 5-LO inhibitors (e.g., zileuton) block leukotriene synthesis but have more limited clinical use due to monitoring requirements.

- Prostaglandin/COX modulation: Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) inhibit COX and reduce prostaglandin production, but their use is nuanced: in aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease, aspirin/NSAIDs can paradoxically worsen symptoms by shunting arachidonic acid toward leukotriene production. Selective prostaglandin receptor antagonists or CRTH2 antagonists are investigational.

- Corticosteroids: Systemic corticosteroids (e.g., prednisone) are used to prevent or reduce protracted or biphasic reactions via anti-inflammatory and transcriptional suppression of cytokine production. They have delayed onset and are not effective for acute reversal of airway compromise or hypotension. Typical practice in emergency management of anaphylaxis involves administration of corticosteroids (e.g., methylprednisolone) after stabilization to reduce risk of late-phase reactions.

- Antagonists of PAF: No widely available, proven clinical PAF antagonists exist for routine practice, though the pathway is of research interest because of associations between PAF and anaphylaxis severity.

Mast cell stabilization and receptor blockade

- Cromolyn sodium and other mast cell stabilizers: Cromolyn reduces mast cell degranulation by interfering with chloride channels and calcium influx; clinically it is used mainly for prophylaxis in allergic rhinitis and some forms of mast cell activation but has limited efficacy in acute settings.

- Anti-IgE therapy: Omalizumab, a monoclonal antibody that binds circulating IgE, reduces free IgE and downregulates FcεRI expression on mast cells and basophils; it is effective in allergic asthma and chronic spontaneous urticaria and can reduce the frequency of severe reactions in selected patients. It raises activation thresholds over weeks to months.

- Kinase inhibitors and targeted agents: Because KIT signaling is central to mast cell survival and proliferation, KIT inhibitors (imatinib, midostaurin, avapritinib) are used in neoplastic mast cell disorders (systemic mastocytosis) with specific mutation profiles. Small molecule inhibitors of signaling kinases (Syk inhibitors, BTK inhibitors) can reduce FcεRI-dependent activation and are under investigation for allergic and mast cell–driven diseases. Caution is warranted: some kinase inhibitors have off-target effects that can alter mediator release.

Supportive and adjunctive therapies

- Intravenous fluids and vasopressors: Aggressive intravascular volume resuscitation is often required because of increased vascular permeability and relative hypovolemia in anaphylaxis. Norepinephrine may be needed for refractory distributive shock.

- Anticoagulation considerations: Heparin released from mast cells can have anticoagulant effects; monitoring and management of coagulopathy depend on clinical context.

Special considerations and emerging therapies

- MRGPRX2 antagonists: Since some non-IgE drugs produce mast cell degranulation via MRGPRX2, antagonists to this receptor are a potential approach to prevent pseudoallergic reactions.

- Cytokine-targeted biologics: Agents that neutralize cytokines involved in mast cell priming or Th2 inflammation (anti-IL-5, anti-IL-4Rα) are effective in certain eosinophilic and allergic disorders and may indirectly reduce mast cell–driven pathology.

- Gene and molecular approaches: For hereditary or clonal mast cell disorders, therapies aimed at underlying molecular drivers (e.g., KIT D816V inhibitors) are increasingly available and tailored based on mutation testing.

Clinical decision points

- In suspected anaphylaxis: administer intramuscular epinephrine without delay; adjunctive therapies (oxygen, intravenous fluids, H1/H2 antihistamines, inhaled bronchodilators, corticosteroids) should not replace epinephrine.

- For recurrent mast cell activation without anaphylaxis: combine trigger avoidance, H1/H2 antihistamines, leukotriene receptor antagonists, and consider omalizumab or mast cell–directed agents if symptoms persist.

- In patients with elevated baseline tryptase or suspected mastocytosis: evaluate for systemic mast cell disease and consider hematology referral and KIT mutation testing, as treatment options differ.

Conclusion

Mast cells are multifunctional effector cells whose rapid release of preformed mediators and synthesis of potent lipid mediators and cytokines produce the clinical picture of immediate hypersensitivity and contribute to chronic allergic disease. Understanding mast cell subtypes, mediator profiles, anatomic localization, and the molecular regulation of activation is essential for interpreting clinical presentations from urticaria to life-threatening anaphylaxis, selecting appropriate laboratory tests (with their timing and limitations), and choosing targeted pharmacologic strategies. The expanding therapeutic armamentarium — from traditional antihistamines and epinephrine to monoclonal antibodies and kinase inhibitors — reflects growing insight into the signaling pathways that control mast cell biology.

Introduction

Anaphylaxis is an acute, systemic hypersensitivity reaction characterized by rapid mediator release from activated mast cells and basophils. The ensuing pathophysiologic cascade simultaneously targets the cardiovascular and respiratory systems and can rapidly progress to life‑threatening hypotension, airway compromise, and hypoxaemia. Understanding how normal cardiopulmonary physiology is perturbed by vasoactive and bronchoconstrictive mediators is essential for clinical recognition, triage, and rational intervention. This chapter reviews the normal cardiovascular and respiratory functions most relevant to shock and hypoxaemia, then details how mediators produce vasodilation, increased vascular permeability, bronchoconstriction and upper airway edema. It explains the consequences for tissue perfusion and oxygen delivery, describes compensatory mechanisms and why they fail in severe reactions, and contrasts the pathophysiology of single‑phase, biphasic, multiphasic and refractory anaphylaxis to inform monitoring and treatment decisions.

# Normal cardiovascular and respiratory physiology relevant to shock and hypoxaemia

Cardiovascular physiology in the context of shock centers on the determinants of systemic oxygen delivery (DO2), which is the product of cardiac output (CO) and arterial oxygen content (CaO2). Arterial oxygen content is given by the equation
CaO2 = (SaO2 × Hb × 1.34) + (0.0031 × PaO2),
where SaO2 is arterial oxygen saturation, Hb is hemoglobin concentration (g/dL), and PaO2 is arterial oxygen tension (mmHg). Under normal conditions, CaO2 is maintained by normal hemoglobin (adult male 13.5–17.5 g/dL; adult female 12.0–15.5 g/dL), near‑maximal oxygen saturation (SaO2 ≥ 95%), and adequate PaO2 (normal 80–100 mmHg). Cardiac output, the other major determinant of DO2, is the product of stroke volume and heart rate and depends on preload, afterload and contractility. Systolic blood pressure in adults is normally 90–140 mmHg; mean arterial pressure (MAP) of at least 65 mmHg is commonly targeted to preserve organ perfusion.

Microcirculatory flow is not a simple linear function of macrocirculatory variables. Capillary recruitment, arteriolar tone, endothelial integrity and plasma oncotic pressure govern effective tissue perfusion and oxygen extraction. Normal systemic vascular resistance (SVR) maintains perfusion pressure and organ blood flow distribution; reflex sympathetic activation can rapidly augment SVR and heart rate during perturbations.

Respiratory physiology relevant to hypoxaemia begins with ventilation, gas exchange and matching of ventilation (V) to perfusion (Q). Alveolar gas exchange efficiency depends on adequate alveolar ventilation (PaCO2 normal 35–45 mmHg) and preserved diffusion surface and capillary flow. Hypoxaemia may result from hypoventilation, diffusion limitation, shunt (perfusion without ventilation), low inspired oxygen, or V/Q mismatch. In acute bronchospasm and airway edema, V/Q mismatch and shunt predominate as airways and alveoli become under‑ventilated relative to perfusion, producing hypoxaemia and, if severe, hypercapnia.

Normal compensatory responses to mild reductions in DO2 include tachycardia, increased contractility (β1‑adrenergic), peripheral vasoconstriction (α‑adrenergic), increased respiratory rate, and mobilization of intravascular volume through constriction of venous capacitance vessels. These mechanisms can maintain perfusion for a time but are limited by myocardial oxygen supply, catecholamine reserve, intravascular volume and ongoing pathologic processes.

# Vasodilation, increased vascular permeability and distributive shock mechanics

Anaphylaxis produces a profound distributive shock that stems from two interrelated vascular phenomena: marked vasodilation and increased microvascular permeability. Mast cells and basophils activated by IgE cross‑linking, direct complement activation, or other mechanisms release a complex mixture of mediators—histamine, platelet‑activating factor (PAF), prostaglandin D2 (PGD2), leukotrienes (LTC4, LTD4, LTE4), nitric oxide (NO) via inducible nitric oxide synthase stimulation, and various cytokines and chemokines. These mediators act on vascular smooth muscle and endothelium to reduce systemic vascular resistance and to allow rapid extravasation of plasma.

Histamine causes arteriolar and venular smooth muscle relaxation through endothelial H1 receptor stimulation and NO release; this produces a fall in SVR and pooling of blood in the venous capacitance beds, reducing venous return (preload) and cardiac output. PAF contributes to vasodilation and also induces platelet aggregation and further inflammatory mediator generation. Leukotrienes, while primarily bronchoconstrictive, can contribute to increased vascular permeability, and prostaglandins have variable vascular effects that often augment vasodilation. Collectively, these factors can drop MAP precipitously.

Increased vascular permeability occurs through several mechanisms: endothelial cell contraction and retraction producing intercellular gaps; disruption of intercellular junctions including cadherin and tight junction complexes; and glycocalyx shedding. The result is a rapid shift of plasma fluid and proteins into the interstitium and extravascular compartments. Clinically, this causes hemoconcentration, decreased intravascular volume and worsening hypotension despite peripheral edema. The combination of vasodilation and plasma loss creates the classic distributive shock of anaphylaxis—low SVR with circulating volume deficit and inadequate tissue perfusion.

Microcirculatory dysfunction also develops: endothelial activation promotes leukocyte and platelet adhesion, capillary plugging, and heterogeneous blood flow, which further impairs oxygen extraction despite sometimes-preserved or even increased bulk cardiac output. This maldistribution of flow, with shunting and no‑flow areas within capillary beds, results in tissue hypoxia disproportionate to measured systemic hemodynamics.

# Bronchoconstriction and upper airway edema mechanisms

Bronchoconstriction in anaphylaxis is a multifactorial process driven by direct smooth muscle constrictors, neural reflexes, and inflammatory cell recruitment. Key mediators include histamine, leukotrienes (particularly LTC4, LTD4 and LTE4), prostaglandin D2 and PAF. Histamine acts on H1 receptors on bronchial smooth muscle to increase intracellular calcium and cause constriction; leukotrienes are more potent and longer‑lasting bronchoconstrictors than histamine, and their action on CysLT receptors produces sustained bronchospasm and increased bronchial hyperresponsiveness. PAF can directly induce bronchial smooth muscle contraction and enhance the response to other mediators.

Neural mechanisms amplify bronchoconstriction: airway sensory nerves (C‑fibers) activated by mediators can trigger vagally mediated bronchoconstrictor reflexes and mucus secretion. Bronchial mucosal edema—resulting from the same increased permeability that affects systemic capillaries—narrows airway lumens, increases resistance, and reduces airway compliance. Excessive mucus production further occludes airways. In the small airways, even modest reductions in radius produce large increases in resistance according to Poiseuille’s relation, so that diffuse bronchospasm and edema can rapidly produce severe airflow limitation and ventilatory failure.

Upper airway edema (laryngeal and pharyngeal) arises primarily from increased permeability of the submucosal microvasculature and resultant fluid extravasation into loose connective tissue spaces. The larynx and pharynx are particularly susceptible because loose submucosal tissue allows rapid swelling that can compromise the glottic aperture. Histamine, bradykinin (sometimes generated via complement and contact system activation), and leukotrienes mediate vasodilation and endothelial gap formation locally. Angioedema in some contexts may be mediated by bradykinin rather than histamine—this is a mechanistic distinction relevant to treatment choices in nonallergic angioedema but is less central to IgE‑mediated anaphylaxis where histamine predominates. Regardless of mediator, upper airway edema is dangerous because it can obstruct both spontaneous ventilation and the ability to intubate.

From a physiologic standpoint, bronchospasm increases airway resistance and work of breathing, leading to tachypnea, dynamic hyperinflation and, in severe cases, respiratory muscle fatigue and hypoventilation with rising PaCO2. Upper airway obstruction preferentially compromises oxygenation because it limits airflow into the alveoli even if perfusion is maintained, producing a large shunt fraction and rapid desaturation.

# Effects on tissue perfusion and oxygen delivery

The combination of reduced intravascular volume, decreased venous return, and arteriolar dilation diminishes cardiac preload and can reduce cardiac output. When cardiac output falls, DO2 declines according to DO2 = CO × CaO2. Even when cardiac output is preserved or increased (as in distributive states with high-output shock), the maldistribution of flow and microcirculatory shunting can impair effective oxygen delivery at the tissue level. Endothelial activation impairs autoregulation and promotes capillary leak and interstitial edema, which increases diffusion distances for oxygen and impairs oxygen extraction.

Hypoxaemia from airway obstruction and bronchospasm reduces CaO2 by decreasing SaO2 and PaO2; hypoxaemia compounds the effects of hypotension by reducing oxygen content available for tissues. Anemia (low Hb) or decreased hemoglobin saturation will multiplicatively reduce DO2. Tissue hypoxia stimulates anaerobic metabolism and lactate production; an elevated serum lactate (normal <2 mmol/L) is a marker of global or regional tissue hypoperfusion.

The myocardium is vulnerable to both supply and demand imbalance in anaphylaxis. Hypotension and coronary vasospasm (mediated by histamine and other vasoconstrictive mediators in coronary vessels) can precipitate myocardial ischemia. In addition, tachycardia and increased contractile demand increase myocardial oxygen consumption; together these can precipitate arrhythmias or pump failure. Clinically significant reductions in cerebral perfusion pressure can lead to syncope, confusion or loss of consciousness.

Microvascular stasis, leukocyte plugging and platelet aggregation further compromise oxygen extraction despite restoration of systemic blood pressure. Thus, achieving normalization of MAP alone does not guarantee effective tissue oxygenation—adequate intravascular volume, correction of hypoxaemia and reversal of mediator effects are all necessary for recovery.

# Compensatory responses and failure modes in severe anaphylaxis

The body launches rapid compensatory responses to hypotension and hypoxaemia: sympathetic activation increases heart rate and systemic vasoconstriction (except where local mediators oppose alpha‑adrenergic tone), and renin–angiotensin–aldosterone and vasopressin systems are engaged to conserve intravascular volume. Increased respiratory drive attempts to correct hypoxaemia. However, mediators released in anaphylaxis can blunt or overwhelm these compensations.

Failure modes include refractory hypotension, where vasopressors and fluids are ineffective because widespread endothelial failure, ongoing mediator release and severe capillary leak prevent restoration of effective intravascular volume and SVR. Myocardial depression can occur from direct actions of mediators (e.g., PAF has negative inotropic effects) and from ischemia secondary to hypotension or coronary vasospasm, leading to low‑output states that are not responsive to vasoconstriction alone. Patients on beta‑blockers may fail to mount an adequate tachycardic or inotropic response and may be particularly prone to refractory shock; prostaglandin and PAF effects are not countered by beta‑agonism in these patients.

Airway failure is another major failure mode: progressive laryngeal edema can obstruct airflow, and severe bronchospasm can produce near‑complete ventilatory obstruction. Respiratory muscle fatigue and hypercapnic respiratory failure may ensue; hypoxaemia rapidly worsens if mechanical ventilation cannot be established.

Arrhythmias and cardiac arrest may arise from severe hypoxaemia, ischemia, or direct effects of mediators or medications. Coronary vasospasm or thrombosis (Kounis syndrome) can precipitate myocardial infarction with attendant pump failure. Disseminated intravascular coagulation and multiorgan dysfunction can follow severe, prolonged anaphylaxis, reflecting systemic endothelial injury and widespread inflammation.

Clinical decision points arise from understanding these failure modes: early intramuscular epinephrine to reverse mediator effects and support perfusion; aggressive intravascular volume replacement to correct capillary leak–induced hypovolaemia; early airway assessment and low threshold for securing the airway when progressive stridor, hoarseness, or rapidly declining oxygenation are present; consideration of intravenous vasopressors (epinephrine, norepinephrine) and adjuncts such as glucagon when β‑blockade is present; and monitoring for myocardial ischemia and arrhythmia.

# Pathophysiologic distinctions between single‑phase, biphasic, multiphasic and refractory courses

Anaphylaxis is heterogeneous in temporal pattern. A single‑phase anaphylactic reaction is the most common presentation: rapid onset of symptoms after exposure, with resolution after treatment and no recurrence. Biphasic anaphylaxis describes a clinical course in which symptoms recur after apparent resolution, usually within 1–72 hours, most commonly within 8–10 hours. Multiphasic reactions involve two or more recurrences over a protracted period. Refractory anaphylaxis refers to severe anaphylaxis that does not respond adequately to initial standard therapy (intramuscular epinephrine, supplemental oxygen, airway support and crystalloid resuscitation) and requires escalating interventions including repeated intravenous epinephrine, vasopressors, and often intensive care.

The mechanisms underlying biphasic and multiphasic reactions are incompletely elucidated. Proposed explanations include persistent or delayed absorption of the allergen (e.g., from depot preparations or ingested food), ongoing de novo synthesis of mediators (particularly leukotrienes and cytokines) after initial mast cell degranulation, secondary waves of inflammatory cell activation and recruitment, and incomplete initial treatment allowing residual mediator activity. Some evidence implicates continued antigen exposure or ongoing antigen release from tissue stores as a driver of delayed recurrences. The degree of initial severity appears to correlate with risk of biphasic reactions, as does delayed administration of epinephrine.

Refractory anaphylaxis often reflects either a massive mediator load or patient‑specific modifiers that blunt therapeutic responses. Contributing factors include delayed or inadequate epinephrine dosing, concomitant use of beta‑blockers (which reduce epinephrine efficacy), ACE inhibitors (which may worsen angioedema by increasing bradykinin), underlying mast cell disorders such as systemic mastocytosis, or biphasic mediator release with ongoing production. Pathophysiologically, refractory responses may represent sustained vasodilatory mediator generation, severe capillary leak resulting in refractory intravascular depletion, direct myocardial depression, or a combination of these. The presence of comorbid cardiac disease or advanced age worsens the reserve to tolerate hypotension and hypoxaemia.

From a clinical standpoint, recognition of these distinctions guides monitoring and disposition: patients who had severe initial hypotension, delayed epinephrine, persistent symptoms, or need for multiple epinephrine doses have higher risk for biphasic or multiphasic courses and often warrant prolonged observation (commonly 4–24 hours or longer depending on risk factors). Refractory cases require ICU‑level care, invasive hemodynamic monitoring, and consideration of alternative pharmacologic strategies (e.g., glucagon in beta‑blocked patients, continuous infusion of epinephrine or other vasopressors, and advanced airway/ventilatory support).

Therapeutic rationale must be grounded in the pathophysiology: epinephrine is the first‑line therapy because of its combined alpha‑adrenergic vasoconstrictive effect (restores SVR and reduces mucosal edema), beta‑1 effects (increase heart rate and contractility to augment CO) and beta‑2 effects (bronchodilation and decreased mediator release from mast cells). Intramuscular administration into the mid‑anterolateral thigh (adult dose 0.3–0.5 mg of 1:1000 solution) provides rapid and reliable absorption for most patients; intravenous bolus epinephrine (1:10,000 concentration) is reserved for severe hypotension or cardiac arrest because of the risk of arrhythmia and hypertension. Large‑volume isotonic crystalloid resuscitation (e.g., 1–2 L in adults initially, guided by response; in children typically 20 mL/kg boluses) is necessary to counter capillary leak and venous pooling. Supplemental oxygen and bronchodilators (inhaled β2 agonists) address hypoxaemia and bronchospasm, but neither replaces epinephrine for systemic mediator reversal. Antihistamines and corticosteroids are adjuncts: H1 and H2 blockers may blunt cutaneous and some cardiovascular effects of histamine but have delayed onset and do not reverse airway obstruction or hypotension. Corticosteroids may reduce the risk of protracted or biphasic reactions by suppressing later inflammatory mediator synthesis, but their immediate effect is limited, and they should not replace epinephrine.

Summary

Anaphylaxis disrupts normal cardiopulmonary physiology through mediator‑driven vasodilation, capillary leak, bronchospasm and airway edema. These processes reduce effective intravascular volume, alter microcirculatory flow, impair oxygen delivery and can overwhelm compensatory mechanisms, leading to refractory shock, respiratory failure, and cardiac complications. Timely therapeutic interventions are directed at reversing mediator effects (primarily with epinephrine), restoring intravascular volume and oxygenation, and anticipating complications based on the likely temporal pattern of the reaction. A mechanistic understanding of these processes informs rational choices about medication selection, routes of administration, fluid resuscitation targets and monitoring intensity.

Introduction

Anaphylaxis is an acute, potentially life‑threatening systemic hypersensitivity reaction that evolves rapidly and requires prompt recognition and treatment. For clinicians in the emergency department, hospital wards, outpatient clinics, or prehospital settings, the ability to identify both typical and atypical presentations, rapidly assess severity, institute early resuscitation, and make triage decisions is essential to preventing morbidity and mortality. This chapter reviews the diagnostic criteria and organ‑system manifestations of anaphylaxis, describes common and atypical clinical features, explores severity grading and triage principles, outlines immediate monitoring and documentation priorities, and lists the major alternative diagnoses to consider. The emphasis is on translating pathophysiology into bedside clinical reasoning so that early life‑saving decisions (particularly administration of intramuscular epinephrine and airway management) are made without delay.

# Diagnostic criteria for anaphylaxis

Diagnosis of anaphylaxis is primarily clinical. Several internationally accepted definitions and criteria exist; the most widely used is the 2005 NIAID/FAAN clinical criteria that were designed to maximize sensitivity for early identification. According to those criteria, anaphylaxis is highly likely when any one of the following three clinical scenarios occurs minutes to hours after exposure to a likely allergen:

1. Acute onset (minutes to several hours) of skin, mucosal, or both skin and mucosal tissue involvement (e.g., generalized hives, pruritus, flushing, swollen lips, tongue, uvula) AND at least one of the following:
   - Respiratory compromise: dyspnea, wheeze, bronchospasm, stridor, hypoxemia.
   - Reduced blood pressure (BP) or associated symptoms of end‑organ dysfunction: hypotonia (collapse), syncope, incontinence.

2. Two or more of the following that occur rapidly after exposure to a likely allergen:
   - Involvement of the skin or mucosa,
   - Respiratory compromise,
   - Reduced blood pressure or associated symptoms,
   - Persistent gastrointestinal symptoms (crampy abdominal pain, vomiting).

3. Reduced blood pressure after exposure to a known allergen for that patient:
   - Infants and children: low systolic blood pressure (age‑specific) or a greater than 30% decrease from baseline.
   - Adults: systolic blood pressure (SBP) less than 90 mm Hg or greater than a 30% decrease from that person’s baseline.

Key elements embedded in these criteria: the multisystem nature of the reaction (skin plus respiratory, circulatory, or GI symptoms), the rapid timing after exposure, and the recognition that severe cardiovascular collapse may occur with minimal cutaneous signs. Clinicians should not require urticaria to be present to make the diagnosis.

Organ‑system features to elicit in the diagnostic assessment

- Airway: sensation of throat tightness, dysphagia, stridor, progressive hoarseness, tongue or oropharyngeal swelling. These findings reflect upper‑airway mucosal and submucosal edema and may progress rapidly to obstruction.

- Breathing: cough, chest tightness, shortness of breath, wheeze, bronchospasm, hypoxemia, or respiratory arrest. Lower airway involvement results from bronchoconstriction, mucosal edema, and mucus production.

- Circulation: dizziness, presyncope, syncope, hypotension, tachycardia, shock, pallor, collapse. Cardiovascular effects include vasodilation, increased vascular permeability with intravascular volume loss, and direct effects on the heart (arrhythmias, myocardial ischemia) mediated by mediators such as histamine, leukotrienes, and platelet‑activating factor.

- Skin and mucosa: generalized urticaria (hives), angioedema, flushing, pruritus. Skin findings are common but may be absent in severe cases.

- Gastrointestinal: abdominal pain, cramping, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea. These symptoms are caused by smooth muscle contraction and increased vascular permeability in the gut.

- Neurologic: agitation, confusion, altered mental status, loss of consciousness from hypotension or hypoxia, and occasionally seizures.

Time course: Symptoms typically develop within minutes but can present after several hours (e.g., delayed food‑dependent reactions). Biphasic reactions (recurrence of symptoms without re‑exposure) can occur hours after apparent resolution; the reported risk varies, commonly cited as 1–20%.

# Common presenting symptoms and signs

Understanding the common presenting features and their pathophysiology helps anticipate progression and prioritize interventions.

Urticaria and pruritus
Urticaria (hives) and widespread pruritus are among the most frequent signs. Urticaria results from dermal microvascular hyperpermeability induced by histamine, prostaglandins, and other mediators released from activated mast cells and basophils. Urticarial lesions are typically raised, erythematous, and intensely itchy; they often migrate. Presence of urticaria supports a diagnosis of an immediate hypersensitivity reaction but is not required.

Angioedema
Angioedema is deeper dermal and submucosal swelling due to fluid extravasation. It can affect the face, lips, eyelids, and, critically, the upper airway including the tongue, floor of mouth, and larynx. Rapidly progressive angioedema and any signs of upper‑airway compromise (stridor, voice change, drooling) should prompt urgent airway evaluation and early consideration of airway control.

Hypotension and circulatory collapse
Hypotension and shock in anaphylaxis arise from systemic vasodilation, increased vascular permeability leading to third‑spacing of intravascular volume, and sometimes direct myocardial depression. Clinically this manifests as lightheadedness, syncope, tachycardia (compensatory), weak pulses, cool clammy skin, and decreased urine output. For adults, an absolute threshold commonly used is SBP < 90 mm Hg, or a drop >30% from baseline; for children, age‑specific hypotension thresholds apply (for example, infants: SBP < 70 mm Hg + [2 × age]; toddlers/children use published pediatric tables).

Bronchospasm and respiratory compromise
Lower airway involvement produces bronchospasm, wheeze, increased work of breathing, hypoxemia, and respiratory failure in severe cases. Wheeze may not always be audible and absence of wheeze does not exclude severe bronchospasm—silent chest in a cyanotic, hypoventilating patient indicates critical obstruction. Tachypnea, accessory muscle use, and oxygen desaturation are important clues.

Gastrointestinal symptoms
GI manifestations such as severe crampy abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea occur commonly and reflect smooth muscle contraction and vasodilation in the GI tract. In some cases, GI symptoms may predominate (particularly with food‑induced anaphylaxis), which can mislead the clinician away from the correct diagnosis.

Neurologic features
Neurologic symptoms are generally secondary to hypoxia or hypotension: confusion, agitation, syncope, and seizures. These signs should alert clinicians to severe systemic compromise even when other signs are subtle.

Putting the features together: while cutaneous signs are common and helpful, anaphylaxis can present without skin involvement and may present as sudden circulatory collapse, severe bronchospasm, or predominantly GI complaints. Therefore, a high index of suspicion is required based on exposure history and rapid evolution of symptoms.

# Atypical presentations and pitfalls in recognition

Atypical presentations can delay diagnosis and treatment. Awareness of these pitfalls reduces missed or under‑treated anaphylaxis.

Absence of skin signs
As many as 10–20% of severe anaphylactic reactions may lack cutaneous manifestations. Relying on the presence of hives will miss some fulminant cases where hypotension or respiratory arrest occur first. Always consider anaphylaxis in any rapid multisystem reaction after known or suspected exposure, even without urticaria.

Predominant GI presentation
Food‑triggered anaphylaxis, particularly in adolescents and young adults, may present with severe abdominal pain, vomiting, and diarrhea before other signs develop. This may be misattributed to gastroenteritis or toxin ingestion. A key clue is the temporal relation to ingestion and accompanying pruritus, wheeze, or lightheadedness.

Delayed presentations
Reactions may be delayed by several hours in certain contexts (e.g., alpha‑gal allergy related to red meat, or cofactor‑dependent food anaphylaxis such as exercise‑induced anaphylaxis). Medications or biologics may cause delayed serum sickness‑type reactions, which are immunologically distinct from anaphylaxis and present later.

Confounding by sedation or concurrent illness
Elderly patients or those who are intoxicated, sedated, or have chronic neurologic disease may not report symptoms; hypotension or altered mental status may be interpreted as primary cardiac or neurologic events. Similarly, patients with underlying asthma may have wheeze from baseline disease; look for rapid change from usual symptoms or associated features like angioedema or hypotension.

Anaphylaxis in the perioperative setting and with anesthesia
Anaphylaxis can occur under general anesthesia when the patient is unable to report symptoms. The first signs may be hypotension, bronchospasm evidenced by increased peak inspiratory pressures or difficult ventilation, and tachycardia. Cutaneous signs may be occult due to drapes. Diagnosis requires high suspicion and clear communication among the anesthetic and surgical teams.

Overlapping signs with other causes of shock or respiratory distress
Acute pulmonary edema, severe asthma exacerbation, septic shock, vasovagal syncope, myocardial infarction, and pulmonary embolism can mimic aspects of anaphylaxis. Differentiation requires attention to timing, exposure history, presence or absence of cutaneous/mucosal findings, and responses to treatment (e.g., rapid improvement after epinephrine supports anaphylaxis).

Medication‑related confounders
Beta‑blocker therapy can blunt tachycardia and response to epinephrine, sometimes making hypotension refractory; concomitant ACE inhibitor or ARB may exacerbate angioedema. Chronic urticaria or mast cell disorders may cause baseline skin changes complicating recognition of new hives.

# Severity assessment scales and triage decisions

Grading severity guides urgency of treatment, disposition, and monitoring. Several severity grading systems exist; two commonly referenced frameworks are the World Allergy Organization (WAO) severity grading and the more concise emergency oriented categorizations (mild, moderate, severe).

WAO severity grading (simplified)
- Grade 1 (Mild): Cutaneous/mucosal signs only (urticaria, flushing, swollen lips/eyes).
- Grade 2 (Moderate): Mild respiratory, cardiovascular, or GI signs (wheeze, mild hypotension with symptoms, vomiting).
- Grade 3 (Severe): Severe respiratory compromise (laryngeal edema, bronchospasm), marked hypotension, loss of consciousness.
- Grade 4 (Cardiac/respiratory arrest): Arrest.

Emergency triage categories (practical)
- Severe/Life‑threatening: Any of the following—airway compromise (stridor, severe oropharyngeal swelling), severe bronchospasm with hypoxia (SpO2 < 90% on room air or increasing respiratory effort), hypotension (SBP < 90 mm Hg or ≥ 30% drop from baseline), collapse, loss of consciousness. These patients require immediate intramuscular epinephrine, airway assessment and likely airway intervention, high flow oxygen, intravenous access, fluid resuscitation, and continuous monitoring. Triage priority: immediate resuscitation bay/critical care.
- Moderate: Multiple organ systems involved (e.g., significant urticaria + wheeze + vomiting) without hypotension or respiratory failure. These patients need prompt epinephrine, observation, and may need escalation if not improving.
- Mild: Limited cutaneous/mucosal signs only without other system involvement. These patients still require assessment, epinephrine if progression occurs, and observation; many will be observed for several hours due to risk of progression.

Clinical decision points for triage and disposition
- Epinephrine: Administer intramuscular epinephrine without delay for any patient meeting diagnostic criteria or with high clinical suspicion of anaphylaxis. Do not wait for objective hypotension or allergy testing.
- Airway: Early involvement of anesthesia/ENT/airway teams is indicated for progressive oropharyngeal swelling, voice change, drooling, stridor, or rapidly worsening respiratory distress. Consider early intubation before severe edema makes airway control impossible.
- Refractory hypotension: If hypotension persists after IM epinephrine and fluids, escalate to intravenous epinephrine infusion and ICU care. Beta‑blocker–treated patients may need glucagon.
- Observation period: Due to risk of biphasic reactions, observe patients after resolution of symptoms. Common practice is 4–6 hours for mild-to-moderate reactions and 12–24 hours for severe reactions; institutional policies vary. Consider longer observation for those with risk factors for biphasic reactions (previous severe anaphylaxis, need for multiple epinephrine doses, delayed epinephrine administration, protracted or refractory symptoms).
- Disposition: Admission to monitored setting (e.g., observation unit, step‑down, ICU) is indicated for severe reactions, those requiring multiple epinephrine doses, persistent symptoms, or significant comorbidity (cardiopulmonary disease, pregnancy). Patients with mild reactions who are well after observation and who can be reliably educated may be discharged with epinephrine auto‑injector prescriptions and specialist follow‑up.

# Initial monitoring requirements and documentation

Monitoring in the initial phase serves two purposes: detect deterioration early and document response to therapy. Monitoring requirements differ with severity.

Vital signs and frequency
- Continuous monitoring for patients with suspected moderate to severe anaphylaxis.
- Obtain baseline vital signs immediately: heart rate, blood pressure, respiratory rate, temperature, oxygen saturation, and level of consciousness.
- Reassess vital signs frequently after treatment (every 5–15 minutes initially) until stable. Document trends, not just single values.

Pulse oximetry and oxygen therapy
- Apply pulse oximetry immediately. Target peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2) typically ≥ 94% in adults; higher targets in pregnancy where possible.
- Administer high‑flow oxygen (e.g., nonrebreather at 10–15 L/min) to any patient with respiratory distress, hypoxemia, or cardiovascular compromise.

Cardiac monitoring
- Continuous cardiac monitoring (ECG) is indicated for moderate to severe reactions, hypotension, arrhythmia risk (known heart disease), or patients receiving intravenous epinephrine.
- Obtain a 12‑lead ECG if chest pain, arrhythmia, or ischemia is suspected; anaphylaxis can coexist with myocardial ischemia or Takotsubo cardiomyopathy.

Intravenous access and fluids
- Establish at least one large‑bore IV for patients with significant symptoms or hypotension. Give isotonic crystalloid fluid boluses (e.g., 500–1000 mL in adults, repeat as needed) to counteract relative hypovolemia due to vasodilation and capillary leak.
- For children, use weight‑based boluses (e.g., 20 mL/kg isotonic crystalloid).

Monitoring for biphasic reactions
- Continue observations for an appropriate interval (commonly 4–24 hours depending on severity). Patients should be advised about delayed reactions.

Documentation: essential items to record
Accurate, contemporaneous documentation improves clinical continuity and medicolegal clarity. Record the following:
- Time of symptom onset and relationship to potential exposures (food, drug, insect sting, contrast, physical exertion).
- Initial signs and symptoms by organ system and their timing.
- Baseline vital signs and subsequent values with timestamped reassessments.
- Treatments given: doses, routes, and times (particularly epinephrine—dose and route are critical).
- Response to each therapy (improvement, no change, deterioration).
- Allergies and past history of anaphylaxis, baseline medications (e.g., beta‑blockers), and comorbidities (asthma, cardiovascular disease).
- Airway assessment findings and interventions (e.g., supplemental oxygen, intubation).
- Disposition decision and rationale, observation plan, prescriptions (e.g., epinephrine auto‑injector), and specialist follow‑up arrangements.

Laboratory testing and imaging
Initial diagnosis is clinical; however, certain tests may be useful:
- Serum tryptase: peaked within 1–2 hours after onset and can help confirm mast cell activation retrospectively. Obtain the first sample as soon as possible (ideally within 1–4 hours), and a baseline sample at least 24 hours later or during convalescence to compare. A significant elevation is considered when acute tryptase > baseline + 2 µg/L or greater than laboratory reference range (normal tryptase generally < 11.4 µg/L but lab‑specific).
- Complete blood count, electrolytes, blood glucose, arterial blood gas if respiratory compromise or shock present.
- Chest X‑ray if aspiration, pulmonary edema, or other pulmonary pathology is suspected.
Note that normal tryptase does not exclude anaphylaxis, particularly in food‑triggered reactions.

# Initial differential diagnostic considerations

Many acute illnesses can present with features overlapping anaphylaxis. Early differentiation guides therapy: epinephrine is key for anaphylaxis and will often help differentiate clinically by response. Important alternative diagnoses include:

Vasovagal (neurally mediated) syncope
Often preceded by prodrome (pallor, diaphoresis, nausea), with bradycardia or transient hypotension. Cutaneous flushing and urticaria are absent. Recovery is usually rapid when supine; however, vasovagal episodes rarely cause bronchospasm or angioedema. A history of prolonged standing or emotional distress may suggest vasovagal etiology.

Acute severe asthma exacerbation
Presents with wheeze, dyspnea, and hypoxemia. In asthma the onset may not be temporally linked to allergen ingestion or injection; absence of skin/mucosal signs or hypotension favors asthma. That said, coexistence is possible. Bronchodilators and systemic corticosteroids are mainstays for asthma; epinephrine can help bronchospasm when anaphylaxis is suspected.

Septic shock
May present with hypotension, fever, and altered mental status. Septic patients typically do not have rapid onset after an exposure and usually have signs of infection, elevated white blood cell counts, or other infectious sources. Response to fluid resuscitation and pressors, as well as laboratory markers (lactate), help distinguish septic shock.

Cardiogenic shock and acute coronary syndromes
Acute myocardial infarction, arrhythmias, or cardiogenic shock may present with hypotension, chest pain, and syncope. Distinguishing features include ECG changes, elevated cardiac enzymes, known ischemic heart disease, and lack of urticaria/angioedema. Anaphylaxis can rarely cause myocardial ischemia or arrhythmia (Kounis syndrome), complicating the picture.

Pulmonary embolism
Acute dyspnea, chest pain, tachycardia, and hypoxemia may mimic anaphylaxis. Pulmonary embolism typically lacks cutaneous features and has risk factors (recent surgery, immobilization). D‑dimer and imaging (CT pulmonary angiography) are diagnostic tools after stabilization.

Angioedema due to bradykinin (ACE inhibitors, hereditary angioedema)
Produces facial and oropharyngeal swelling without urticaria or pruritus and usually without bronchospasm or hypotension. Onset can be slower and not related to allergen exposure; ACE inhibitor–mediated angioedema often has gradual onset over hours and lacks response to epinephrine and antihistamines. Hereditary angioedema typically has recurrent episodes and family history; complement testing (C4, C1 esterase inhibitor) can aid diagnosis.

Anxiety/panic attacks
May produce dyspnea, tachycardia, chest discomfort, and paresthesias. Cutaneous wheals, hypotension, angioedema, and GI symptoms are not typical. Panic attacks usually do not progress to hypotension or respiratory failure.

Mast cell disorders and systemic mastocytosis
Patients with mast cell activation syndromes may have recurrent anaphylaxis‑like episodes, often with very high baseline tryptase. A history of recurrent idiopathic episodes and skin findings like telangiectasia or urticaria pigmentosa can offer clues.

Medication side effects and toxic exposures
Vasodilatory drug reactions (e.g., nitrates), overdoses (opioids causing respiratory depression and hypotension), and histamine‑releasing drugs (vancomycin “red man syndrome”) can mimic parts of anaphylaxis. Red man syndrome is typically linked to rapid vancomycin infusion and causes flushing and pruritus but not hypotension or systemic involvement unless severe.

Key approach to differential diagnosis
- Use the exposure and timing history: a clear temporal relationship to an identifiable trigger increases likelihood of anaphylaxis.
- Consider the presence or absence of skin/mucosal symptoms, pattern of respiratory compromise (upper‑airway vs lower‑airway), degree and rapidity of hypotension, and response to a trial of intramuscular epinephrine.
- When in doubt and anaphylaxis is plausible, administer epinephrine and observe the response—delay in epinephrine is associated with worse outcomes.

Conclusion

Rapid clinical recognition of anaphylaxis depends on awareness of typical and atypical presentations across organ systems, use of validated clinical criteria, early severity grading, immediate monitoring, and consideration of alternative diagnoses. The priority is early intramuscular epinephrine administration and stabilization of the airway, breathing, and circulation. Thorough documentation and appropriate observation or escalation of care reduce the risk of preventable deterioration and enable accurate follow‑up, allergist referral, and secondary prevention strategies.

# Introduction

Anaphylaxis is a rapid, life-threatening systemic hypersensitivity reaction characterized by airway compromise, circulatory collapse, and cutaneous and gastrointestinal manifestations. Prompt recognition and immediate administration of intramuscular epinephrine are foundational to management; epinephrine acts through alpha-1, beta-1 and beta-2 adrenergic receptors to reverse life-threatening pathophysiology. Adjunctive agents — antihistamines, bronchodilators, corticosteroids, supplemental oxygen, intravenous fluids, and targeted vasopressors — play supportive roles but are not substitutes for epinephrine. This chapter integrates the pharmacology, pharmacokinetics, dosing strategies, monitoring, and practical decision-making for epinephrine and commonly used adjunctive therapies in the emergency treatment of anaphylaxis.

# Epinephrine pharmacodynamics: alpha-1, beta-1 and beta-2 effects and clinical implications

Epinephrine (adrenaline) is a nonselective adrenergic agonist with activity at alpha-1, beta-1 and beta-2 receptors; its clinical effects derive from this mixed profile and the receptor density of target tissues. Alpha-1 stimulation produces vasoconstriction of peripheral arterioles and venules, increasing systemic vascular resistance and venous return. In anaphylaxis, alpha-1 effects oppose pathologic vasodilation and capillary leak (mediated by histamine, prostaglandins, and platelet-activating factor), thereby restoring intravascular volume, increasing blood pressure, and reducing mucosal edema in the larynx and upper airway.

Beta-1 receptor agonism increases heart rate, myocardial contractility, and conduction velocity. These effects augment cardiac output, supporting perfusion in hypotensive patients. However, beta-1 stimulation also increases myocardial oxygen consumption, which can precipitate ischemia in patients with coronary artery disease or compromised oxygen delivery.

Beta-2 receptor stimulation causes bronchodilation and stabilization of mast cells, decreasing mediator release. Beta-2 effects relieve bronchospasm and improve airflow in anaphylaxis-induced airway obstruction. Beta-2–mediated vasodilation in skeletal muscle is offset by alpha-1 systemic vasoconstriction when full-spectrum epinephrine is used, so the net hemodynamic effect is variable depending on dose and route.

Clinically, the combined receptor activity is beneficial: alpha-1 counters hypotension and angioedema, beta-1 supports cardiac output, and beta-2 relieves bronchospasm and directly reduces mediator release. The balance of effects depends on concentration at receptor sites: lower systemic exposures (as achieved with intramuscular dosing) tend to favor beta-2 bronchodilation and reduce the magnitude of alpha-1–driven vasoconstriction compared with bolus IV administration. Understanding this helps frame why intramuscular epinephrine is first-line in the emergency setting and why intravenous epinephrine is reserved for refractory or life-threatening circulatory collapse.

# Recommended intramuscular dosing: 0.01 mg/kg (max 0.5 mg) and common auto-injector doses (0.15 mg, 0.3 mg, 0.5 mg); rationale for IM over other routes initially

The recommended initial dose of epinephrine for anaphylaxis in adults and children is intramuscular (IM) epinephrine administered into the mid-anterolateral thigh at a dose of 0.01 mg/kg, with a usual maximum of 0.5 mg per dose. For adults, typical doses are 0.3–0.5 mg IM. For children, weight-based dosing is essential: 0.15 mg is commonly used for children approximately 10–25 kg, while 0.3 mg is used for larger children and most adults. Auto-injectors are available in standard fixed doses: 0.15 mg (pediatric), 0.3 mg (adult), and in some markets 0.5 mg (high-dose adult). Auto-injector choice should be matched to patient size; manufacturers’ labeling and local guidelines inform selection.

Intramuscular injection into the mid-outer thigh achieves rapid and reliable absorption because of rich vascular supply and muscle mass at that site. Pharmacokinetic studies have shown that IM epinephrine attains higher and earlier peak plasma concentrations than subcutaneous injection. Compared with IV administration, IM reduces the risk of dangerous high peak plasma concentrations, abrupt hypertension, tachyarrhythmias, and myocardial ischemia while still producing clinically effective reversal of airway edema, bronchospasm and hypotension in the majority of patients.

IM is preferred initially because it is simple, rapid, and safe to administer in prehospital and emergency settings, including by patients and caregivers with auto-injectors. The risk–benefit calculus strongly favors IM epinephrine for nearly all cases of suspected anaphylaxis because delays in epinephrine administration are consistently associated with worse outcomes. Repeated IM dosing every 5–10 minutes is acceptable if symptoms persist; if there is inadequate response after 1–3 IM doses or there is severe cardiovascular collapse, escalation to intravenous therapy should be considered (see below).

# Indications, techniques and dosing for intravenous epinephrine boluses and continuous infusions (when to escalate)

Intravenous (IV) epinephrine is indicated when intramuscular administration fails to reverse life-threatening symptoms (persistent hypotension, cardiovascular collapse, severe or progressive airway compromise, or refractory bronchospasm), or when rapid titratable hemodynamic support is required (e.g., peri-arrest states). IV epinephrine carries substantially greater risk of adverse cardiovascular effects and requires careful dosing, monitoring, and experienced personnel.

Two IV strategies are commonly used: small IV boluses for acute deterioration and continuous IV infusion for ongoing titratable support.

IV boluses: For severe hypotension or cardiovascular collapse not responsive to IM epinephrine, small incremental IV boluses of epinephrine are administered. A commonly used bolus range is 10–100 micrograms (0.01–0.1 mg) given slowly IV, titrated to effect. Typical practice is to start with 10–20 microgram (0.01–0.02 mg) aliquots and escalate rapidly as needed, watching for increased heart rate, blood pressure, and signs of myocardial ischemia or arrhythmia. In cardiac arrest (anaphylaxis-induced arrest), the standard resuscitation dose is 1 mg IV of 1:10,000 epinephrine every 3–5 minutes, but that is a separate protocol-driven indication.

Continuous infusion: When persistent vasopressor support is required, prepare a continuous infusion with a known concentration to allow precise titration. Frequently used preparations include diluting 1 mg of epinephrine in 100 mL of normal saline or D5W to create a solution of 10 micrograms/mL, or 1 mg in 250 mL for a concentration of 4 micrograms/mL. Starting infusion rates commonly range from 0.05 to 0.1 micrograms/kg/min (roughly 3–7 micrograms/min for a 70 kg adult) and may be titrated upward based on hemodynamic response. Many guidelines recommend titrating to restore systolic blood pressure and end-organ perfusion, with infusion rates sometimes reaching 0.5–1.0 micrograms/kg/min in refractory cases. Use of a syringe pump or infusion pump is essential for safety. Continuous monitoring with ECG, invasive or frequent noninvasive blood pressure measurement, and trained personnel is mandatory.

Escalation triggers: Escalate from IM to IV epinephrine when there is: persistent hypotension (systolic blood pressure <90 mmHg or inadequate perfusion) despite adequate IM doses; signs of progressive respiratory compromise despite IM epinephrine and adjunctive bronchodilators; circulatory collapse or impending cardiac arrest; or recurrent severe symptoms despite repeated appropriate IM dosing. Because IV epinephrine can precipitate life-threatening arrhythmias and severe hypertension, it should only be given in controlled settings with continuous monitoring and immediate availability for airway management and advanced cardiovascular care.

# Antihistamines (H1 and H2): mechanisms, dosing and limits (symptomatic relief vs life-saving effect)

Antihistamines target histamine-mediated manifestations of anaphylaxis but do not address the primary life-threatening pathophysiology driven by massive mediator release (including histamine, tryptase, platelet-activating factor, leukotrienes and prostaglandins). H1 antihistamines (e.g., diphenhydramine, cetirizine) block histamine H1 receptors and therefore act on urticaria, pruritus and flushing; H2 antihistamines (e.g., ranitidine historically; famotidine clinically preferred now) block histamine H2 receptors and can provide additional symptomatic relief of cutaneous signs and, in some studies, augment suppression of gastric acid secretion.

Typical dosing for H1 antihistamines in the acute setting is diphenhydramine 25–50 mg IV or IM in adults, or weight-based dosing in children (approximately 1 mg/kg IV up to 25–50 mg). For oral agents when IV is not available, first-generation agents may be given orally but onset is slower. H2 antihistamines are commonly given as famotidine 20 mg IV or ranitidine 50 mg IV in older practice; note that ranitidine has been withdrawn in many jurisdictions and famotidine is the preferred agent. The combination of H1 + H2 blockade may reduce cutaneous manifestations more effectively than H1 alone.

Important clinical limits: Antihistamines relieve pruritus and hives but do not rapidly reverse airway obstruction, bronchospasm, hypotension, or prevent death from anaphylaxis. They are adjunctive therapies only and must never delay or replace epinephrine. Sedating H1 antihistamines (first-generation) can cause drowsiness and may complicate neurological assessment. IV antihistamines may cause local tissue irritation and, rarely, hypotension when administered too rapidly.

# Bronchodilators (beta-2 agonists) for bronchospasm

Bronchospasm in anaphylaxis results from mediator-induced bronchial smooth muscle contraction, mucosal edema and airway hyperreactivity. Inhaled beta-2 agonists such as albuterol (salbutamol) provide targeted bronchodilation by stimulating beta-2 receptors in bronchial smooth muscle. They are effective as adjunctive therapy for wheeze and bronchospasm but do not treat the vasodilation, capillary leak, or laryngeal edema that are life-threatening in severe anaphylaxis.

Common dosing is nebulized albuterol 2.5–5 mg for adults and children, given via a nebulizer with oxygen or air as the driving gas; repeated or continuous nebulization may be required for persistent bronchospasm. Metered-dose inhaler (MDI) with spacer can be used (e.g., 4–8 puffs, each puff delivering ~100 micrograms) when the patient is cooperative and able to inhale. Ipratropium bromide (anticholinergic bronchodilator) may be added for additional bronchodilation in severe bronchospasm. Beta-2 agonists do not replace epinephrine and should be used in concert with systemic epinephrine for anaphylaxis.

Be mindful that systemic absorption of inhaled beta-2 agonists can cause tachycardia and tremor, and their efficacy may be limited if airway obstruction is primarily due to laryngeal edema rather than lower airway bronchospasm.

# Corticosteroids: mechanism, historical rationale for use in preventing biphasic reactions, and evidence base questioning routine use

Corticosteroids (e.g., methylprednisolone, prednisone) exert broad anti-inflammatory effects by modifying gene transcription to decrease production of inflammatory cytokines, inhibit leukocyte trafficking, stabilize cellular membranes, and reduce late-phase inflammatory responses. Historically, corticosteroids have been included in anaphylaxis management with the rationale that they could reduce the risk of biphasic or protracted reactions that occur hours after the initial event by dampening late-phase mediator release and inflammation.

Typical emergency dosing is methylprednisolone 40–125 mg IV in adults (commonly 125 mg IV), with oral prednisone 40–60 mg used for follow-up. In children, weight-based dosing is used (e.g., methylprednisolone 1–2 mg/kg IV). Doses are selected to achieve prompt systemic anti-inflammatory effect but corticosteroids have a delayed onset (hours) for many of their genomic effects, so they do not acutely treat airway obstruction or hypotension.

Evidence base: Contemporary systematic reviews and observational studies have not demonstrated consistent benefit of corticosteroids in preventing biphasic anaphylactic reactions. Randomized controlled trial data are sparse, and existing studies are limited by heterogeneity and low power. Some studies suggest no clear reduction in the incidence of biphasic reactions with routine corticosteroid use; others show possible modest reductions in symptom recurrence but with unclear clinical significance. Because corticosteroids have low immediate value in reversing life-threatening physiology, they are considered adjunctive and are often used for potential late benefits and for treatment of prolonged/refractory symptoms. Clinical judgment should guide their use: corticosteroids may be reasonable in patients with severe or protracted anaphylaxis, significant asthma history, or those admitted for observation. Corticosteroids should not delay or replace epinephrine, airway management, IV fluids or vasopressors.

# Adjunctive measures: IV fluids, oxygen, vasopressors, glucagon in beta-blocked patients

IV fluids: Anaphylaxis often produces profound vasodilation and increased vascular permeability leading to intravascular volume depletion. Rapid intravascular volume repletion with isotonic crystalloid (normal saline or lactated Ringer’s) is a cornerstone of supportive care. For children, initial bolus is typically 20 mL/kg and may be repeated. For adults, initial boluses of 1–2 liters (20–30 mL/kg in many adults) should be infused rapidly, with repeated boluses guided by clinical response, blood pressure, urine output, and perfusion. Large-volume resuscitation is commonly required; remember that epinephrine’s vasoconstrictive effects can be less effective without adequate preload.

Oxygen and airway management: Administer high-flow oxygen to all patients with suspected anaphylaxis, particularly if hypoxemic, tachypneic, or showing signs of respiratory distress. Early airway evaluation is critical because progressive laryngeal edema can rapidly make intubation difficult. Prepare for advanced airway management when there is stridor, hoarseness, worsening hypoxemia, signs of complete airway obstruction, or progressive respiratory failure. Equipment and experienced personnel for difficult airway management and surgical airway should be available in severe cases.

Vasopressors: When hypotension is refractory to IM epinephrine and fluid resuscitation, vasopressors such as norepinephrine may be needed in addition to or instead of continuous epinephrine infusion, depending on hemodynamics and the clinical scenario. Norepinephrine provides potent alpha-1 vasoconstriction with some beta-1 support and is useful when systemic vasodilation predominates. Vasopressin or phenylephrine are alternatives in selected situations, but epinephrine remains the principal agent for anaphylaxis because of its beta-2 bronchodilatory and mast cell–stabilizing properties. Choice of vasopressor should be guided by monitoring and hemodynamic goals.

Glucagon in beta-blocked patients: Patients taking beta-adrenergic blockers may have attenuated response to epinephrine because beta receptors are pharmacologically blocked. In such cases, glucagon can be life-saving. Glucagon exerts positive inotropic and chronotropic effects by stimulating adenylate cyclase via G-protein–coupled receptors independent of beta-adrenergic receptors, increasing intracellular cyclic AMP in cardiac myocytes. Recommended dosing for severe anaphylaxis in beta-blocked patients is an initial IV bolus of glucagon 1–5 mg in adults (typical 3–5 mg) given slowly (over several minutes) followed by an infusion of 1–5 mg per hour (e.g., 1–5 mg/hour, titrated), or infusion rates reported as 5–15 micrograms/kg/min in some protocols. Glucagon often causes nausea and vomiting; antiemetic prophylaxis should be considered, and the pressor effect may be transient, so intensive monitoring and additional vasopressors may be needed.

# Common adverse effects and monitoring during vasoactive therapy

Epinephrine and other vasoactive therapies can produce significant adverse effects that require proactive monitoring and management. Common and serious effects include tachyarrhythmias (supraventricular tachycardia, ventricular arrhythmias), hypertension (which can cause intracranial hemorrhage in susceptible patients), myocardial ischemia and infarction due to increased myocardial oxygen demand, pulmonary edema from increased afterload and catecholamine-mediated increased capillary permeability, tremor, anxiety, hyperglycemia, and lactic acidosis.

Specific risks depend on dose and route: IV boluses and high-rate infusions of epinephrine carry higher risk of arrhythmia and hypertension than IM dosing. Patients with underlying coronary artery disease, uncontrolled hypertension, or arrhythmogenic substrates are at higher risk for ischemic complications. Concomitant medications (e.g., MAO inhibitors, tricyclic antidepressants, beta-blockers) can alter responses to catecholamines and complicate management.

Monitoring during treatment must include continuous ECG, frequent blood pressure measurement (noninvasive at short intervals or invasive arterial monitoring when titrating vasopressors), pulse oximetry, and observation for respiratory status with readiness to secure the airway. Serial assessment of perfusion (mental status, capillary refill, urine output) and hemodynamic endpoints guide ongoing therapy. Laboratory monitoring includes blood glucose, arterial blood gas if hypoxemia or severe respiratory compromise is present, electrolytes (especially potassium, because catecholamines can shift potassium intracellularly), and lactate as a marker of tissue perfusion. Normal laboratory ranges important for acute management include serum potassium 3.5–5.0 mmol/L and blood glucose 70–140 mg/dL (values will vary depending on local lab references); marked deviations may reflect therapy effects or severity of illness.

When continuous infusions are used, titrate to effect and de-escalate as soon as clinically appropriate. Avoid abrupt cessation of high-dose infusions without hemodynamic replacement because of potential rebound hypotension. Anticipate and manage common side effects: treat arrhythmias per ACLS guidelines, manage severe hypertension with careful down-titration of vasopressors, and monitor for myocardial ischemia with serial ECGs and troponin measurement if clinically indicated.

# Practical considerations and clinical decision points

Timely recognition and epinephrine administration are the most important determinants of outcome in anaphylaxis. IM epinephrine in the mid-anterolateral thigh at 0.01 mg/kg (up to 0.5 mg) should be administered immediately when anaphylaxis is suspected. Reassess and repeat IM dosing every 5–10 minutes as clinically indicated. Escalate to IV bolus and/or continuous infusion when there is persistent hypotension, ongoing cardiovascular instability, refractory respiratory compromise, or failure to respond to multiple IM doses. Prepare for aggressive airway management early when there are signs of progressive airway edema.

Adjunctive agents — H1/H2 antihistamines, inhaled beta-2 bronchodilators, corticosteroids — are valuable for symptomatic relief and potential mitigation of late-phase responses but are not life-saving in the acute phase and must not delay epinephrine or definitive hemodynamic support. Administer rapid IV crystalloid boluses and supplemental oxygen as standard supportive measures, and consider glucagon when beta-blockade is present. Continuous monitoring, titratable infusions via pumps, and escalation to advanced cardiovascular care are necessary for patients requiring IV epinephrine.

Finally, educate patients and caregivers about early signs of anaphylaxis, the importance of carrying and knowing how to use an epinephrine auto-injector at appropriate dose, and the need for urgent medical evaluation after any epinephrine use because of risk of biphasic or protracted reactions. Documentation of the event, allergen identification, referral for allergy follow-up and consideration of prescription of an appropriate-dose auto-injector are essential steps after the acute phase.

Introduction

Anaphylaxis is a life-threatening, systemic hypersensitivity reaction characterized by rapid onset airway compromise, bronchospasm, and circulatory collapse. Management hinges on prompt epinephrine administration and simultaneous supportive measures to secure the airway, maintain oxygenation, and restore tissue perfusion. In the critically ill patient who either fails initial therapy or presents with severe airway or hemodynamic compromise, decisive critical-care interventions — early recognition of impending airway obstruction, appropriate airway techniques for angioedema, graduated oxygen-delivery escalation, judicious fluid resuscitation, and titrated vasoactive support — are essential to prevent irreversible hypoxemia and end-organ injury. This chapter synthesizes pathophysiology with practical clinical decision-making for airway and circulatory support in anaphylactic shock, with emphasis on when and how to escalate interventions and how to monitor response.

# Assessment for impending airway obstruction and indications for early intubation

The airway in anaphylaxis can deteriorate rapidly because of laryngeal edema, angioedema of the soft tissues, and mucosal swelling compounded by bronchospasm. Clinical assessment must therefore be dynamic and proactive rather than reactive. Key features that indicate impending airway obstruction include progressive stridor (especially inspiratory stridor), hoarseness or muffled voice, drooling or difficulty handling secretions, progressive dysphagia, visible swelling of the face, lips, tongue, or oropharynx, and any signs of respiratory distress such as use of accessory muscles, tachypnea with hypoxia, or paradoxical breathing. Acute change in voice quality, failure to phonate, or increasing difficulty with bag-mask ventilation are particularly ominous. Consciousness level is also crucial: declining mental status, inability to cooperate, or fatigue from respiratory effort may necessitate airway control. Objective measures that inform the decision include oxygen saturation (target SpO2 ≥ 94% in most patients), arterial blood gas with rising carbon dioxide and falling pH, and escalating oxygen requirements despite supplemental oxygen.

Decision points for early intubation must balance the risk of a difficult airway against the risk of sudden complete obstruction. Indications for urgent or early intubation include: evidence of progressive upper-airway edema (stridor, oropharyngeal swelling), persistent hypoxemia (SpO2 < 90% despite high-flow oxygen), respiratory failure with hypercapnia or respiratory fatigue, inability to protect the airway due to altered mental status, and the need for prolonged mechanical ventilation for severe bronchospasm or hemodynamic instability. In angioedema, once edema is manifest and progressive, intubation should generally be performed before a "can't intubate, can't oxygenate" situation evolves, because conventional endotracheal intubation may become impossible as edema progresses.

Clinical reasoning requires anticipation: if the history indicates early facial or oropharyngeal involvement (e.g., recent exposure to a known allergen with rapid lip or tongue swelling) or there is a trend of worsening symptoms, prepare for definitive airway early. Preparation includes assembling the most experienced airway operator, ensuring full monitoring, pre-oxygenation strategies, availability of advanced airway equipment (videolaryngoscope, flexible fiberoptic scope), and planning for surgical airway backup.

# Techniques for airway management in angioedema (awake intubation, surgical airway backup)

Angioedema poses unique challenges: the anatomy may be distorted, the mucosa friable and edematous, and bag-mask ventilation may be ineffective. The safe approach emphasizes maintaining spontaneous ventilation when possible and using techniques that minimize further trauma or loss of airway patency.

Awake intubation is often the preferred strategy when time and patient cooperation permit. Awake techniques preserve spontaneous ventilation and allow assessment of airway patency during the procedure. For awake intubation in angioedema, topical anesthesia of the upper airway (e.g., lidocaine nebulization, topical sprays, or transtracheal block) and cautious sedation with agents that preserve respiratory drive (low-dose ketamine, dexmedetomidine titration) are options. Flexible fiberoptic bronchoscopy through the nasal or oral route is a commonly used awake technique; it provides direct visualization and can negotiate around swollen tissues. Videolaryngoscopy may also be effective in awake or lightly sedated patients and can improve glottic visualization compared with direct laryngoscopy, but it can be limited if supraglottic edema obscures views.

When performing awake intubation, meticulous preparation is essential: pre-oxygenation, positioning, suction available for copious secretions, and a stepwise plan that anticipates failure. The operator should use smaller-diameter endotracheal tubes if necessary to pass a swollen glottic inlet, and consider a tube exchanger or bougie to facilitate placement. Avoid excessive manipulation that can exacerbate bleeding or edema. If pharmacologic measures are being administered (epinephrine, antihistamines, steroids), these should proceed concurrently, but should not delay securing the airway.

Surgical airway backup must be planned for every difficult-angioedema airway. If intubation fails or is impossible and oxygenation cannot be maintained by mask ventilation or supraglottic devices, cricothyroidotomy is the recommended emergent approach in adults. The time-critical nature of "can't intubate, can't oxygenate" scenarios demands that equipment for emergency cricothyroidotomy (scalpel, bougie, cuffed tracheostomy or tracheal cannula kits) be immediately available and that the team be prepared to perform it without delay. In some settings where neck anatomy permits and the situation is not immediately catastrophic, awake tracheostomy in the operating room is an option for anticipated extremely difficult airways; however, in acute anaphylaxis with rapid deterioration, cricothyroidotomy remains the faster, life-saving intervention.

Throughout, the airway strategy must be integrated with ongoing resuscitation: high-flow oxygen, epinephrine administration, and hemodynamic support. After successful airway control, reassess ventilation and oxygenation, and secure the tube definitively. Be aware that angioedema may continue to progress after intubation, so endotracheal tube size and position should be chosen to allow for potential edema-related narrowing, and readiness for surgical airway conversion should remain.

# Oxygen delivery methods and escalation (nasal cannula, non-rebreather, non-invasive ventilation, mechanical ventilation)

Oxygen therapy in anaphylaxis follows a graded escalation based on severity of hypoxemia and respiratory compromise. The first step is always high-concentration oxygen to maintain adequate oxygen delivery to tissues. Initial options and clinical reasoning for escalation are as follows.

Low-flow devices such as nasal cannula (typically 1–6 L/min) provide fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2) increments up to approximately 44% depending on flow and patient inspiratory flow. Nasal cannula may be adequate for patients with mild hypoxemia or as a bridge while preparing higher-level support. However, in many anaphylactic patients with airway edema or bronchospasm, higher FiO2 is required.

A non-rebreather mask (NRB) with reservoir at 10–15 L/min can deliver an FiO2 approaching 0.60–0.90 when fitted snugly and the patient has moderate inspiratory flow, and should be used for patients with more significant hypoxemia or early respiratory distress. It is a rapid, noninvasive method to increase oxygenation while decisions about advanced airway are made.

High-flow nasal cannula (HFNC) systems can deliver warmed, humidified oxygen at flows up to 60 L/min with adjustable FiO2 up to near 1.0. HFNC offers advantages in selected patients: it can provide some degree of positive airway pressure, reduce work of breathing, and improve oxygenation without the need for an interface that may be poorly tolerated. HFNC may be useful in patients with significant hypoxemia or bronchospasm who are cooperative and do not have imminent airway compromise. However, HFNC should not delay definitive airway management when there is progressive upper-airway obstruction.

Non-invasive ventilation (NIV) such as bilevel positive airway pressure (BiPAP) or continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) can support ventilation and reduce work of breathing in selected patients with hypercapnic respiratory failure or severe bronchospasm. Positive pressure can help stent airways open and improve alveolar ventilation. However, NIV is contraindicated when there is risk of loss of airway protective reflexes, copious secretions, or progressive upper-airway obstruction from angioedema. In acute angioedema with upper-airway swelling, NIV risks aspiration and mask leak; thus avoidance is advised when airway compromise is present or suspected.

When noninvasive measures fail or are contraindicated, endotracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation are required. Mechanical ventilation strategy should be individualized. In the setting of bronchospasm, allow prolonged expiratory time to prevent air trapping and auto-PEEP (e.g., set inspiratory:expiratory ratio of 1:3 or greater), use lower respiratory rates, and consider higher inspiratory flow rates to shorten inspiratory time. Tidal volume recommendations for adults generally aim for lung-protective ventilation: 6–8 mL/kg predicted body weight, but severe bronchospasm may necessitate lower effective tidal volumes to avoid breath stacking. In severe cases, permissive hypercapnia may be acceptable to avoid dynamic hyperinflation, provided pH remains tolerable and hemodynamics are stable. Monitor for barotrauma and ventilator-associated complications. In cases of profound hypoxemia with refractory bronchospasm, adjuncts such as inhaled bronchodilators via inline nebulizer, intravenous magnesium sulfate, and neuromuscular blockade to facilitate ventilation may be used as clinically indicated.

Throughout escalation, continuous pulse oximetry, capnography if available (end-tidal CO2), and frequent clinical reassessment are essential. Use objective thresholds to guide escalation: SpO2 < 90% on NRB or rising PaCO2/respiratory acidosis on arterial blood gas are clear signals to escalate to invasive ventilation.

# Guidelines for initial fluid resuscitation volumes and rationale (crystalloids vs colloids)

Anaphylactic shock is characterized by vasodilation, increased vascular permeability, and relative intravascular volume depletion due to fluid extravasation into the interstitium. Rapid intravascular volume restoration is a cornerstone of resuscitation because severe hypotension may be refractory to vasopressors if the intravascular volume is low. Fluid therapy must be prompt, substantial, and guided by response.

Guideline-recommended initial fluid management typically begins with rapid crystalloid boluses. A commonly used initial bolus is 20 mL/kg of isotonic crystalloid (e.g., normal saline or balanced crystalloids) given rapidly, and in adults consideration of larger initial boluses (e.g., 1–2 liters) depending on severity and body habitus. If hypotension persists or there are signs of ongoing hypoperfusion, repeat boluses up to a cumulative fluid volume may be needed; many protocols suggest up to 30 mL/kg in distributive shock as an early resuscitation target. The rationale centers on restoring preload to support cardiac output in the setting of systemic vasodilation and third-spacing due to increased capillary permeability.

Crystalloids are preferred as first-line because they are readily available, cost-effective, and have strong evidence as initial resuscitation fluid in most forms of shock. Balanced crystalloids (e.g., lactated Ringer’s or Plasma-Lyte) may be favored over normal saline in some settings to avoid hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis when large volumes are anticipated, though evidence specific to anaphylactic shock is limited.

Colloids (e.g., albumin) have theoretic appeal because they remain intravascular for longer and might better restore oncotic pressure; however, they are not routinely recommended for initial resuscitation of anaphylactic shock. There is limited evidence of mortality benefit, higher cost, potential for allergic reactions, and in some cases concern for exacerbating pulmonary edema. Colloids may be considered if very large volumes of crystalloids are required and hypoalbuminemia or ongoing capillary leak is suspected, but this is an individualized decision.

Clinical reasoning must account for comorbidities: patients with heart failure or renal impairment are at higher risk of fluid overload; in these patients, more cautious titration of fluid with earlier use of vasopressors and invasive hemodynamic monitoring is appropriate. Clear endpoints for fluid resuscitation include improvements in blood pressure (e.g., mean arterial pressure ≥ 65 mmHg), normalization or downward trend in lactate, improved mental status, urine output ≥ 0.5 mL/kg/hr, and improved skin perfusion. Aim for rapid boluses with reassessment after each bolus rather than fixed large-volume infusions without response assessment.

# Use of vasopressors and inotropes beyond epinephrine

Epinephrine is the primary pharmacologic treatment for anaphylaxis because it has alpha-adrenergic vasoconstrictive effects to counteract vasodilation and capillary leak, beta-1 inotropic and chronotropic effects to support cardiac output, and beta-2 bronchodilatory effects. Intramuscular epinephrine (0.3–0.5 mg of 1:1000 or 0.3–0.5 mL of 1 mg/mL) into the lateral thigh is first-line for most pre-hospital and initial hospital care. Intravenous epinephrine is reserved for patients with severe anaphylaxis or cardiovascular collapse and should be titrated carefully due to risk of arrhythmia or myocardial ischemia.

When hypotension persists despite adequate epinephrine (and adequate intravascular volume), additional vasoactive agents should be considered. Norepinephrine is the vasopressor of choice for persistent hypotension in distributive shock states because of its strong alpha-1 vasoconstrictive activity and modest beta-1 support, which increases systemic vascular resistance and improves coronary perfusion without excessive tachycardia. Typical starting doses are in the range of 0.05–0.1 mcg/kg/min and titrated upward to achieve MAP ≥ 65 mmHg; adult dosing is often expressed in mcg/min with titration to effect.

Vasopressin (typically 0.03 units/min infusion) can be a useful adjunct in catecholamine-refractory distributive shock to restore vascular tone via non-adrenergic pathways. It is particularly useful when high-dose catecholamines provoke adverse effects or tachyarrhythmias. Phenylephrine, a pure alpha-agonist, can raise blood pressure but may reduce cardiac output because of reflex bradycardia and increased afterload; its use is mainly in select situations where tachyarrhythmia precludes other agents.

Inotropic support with agents such as dobutamine may be necessary when myocardial dysfunction or severe pump failure contributes to hypotension despite restored afterload. Dobutamine increases cardiac contractility and can improve cardiac output but may cause vasodilation and hypotension at lower doses; it should be used with or after vasopressor support to avoid worsening hypotension. Milrinone (a phosphodiesterase inhibitor) is another inotrope with vasodilatory effects and may be used in refractory cardiogenic components but is less commonly used acutely in anaphylaxis.

Selection and sequencing of vasopressors requires clinical judgment. In the collapsed patient with severe hypotension despite epinephrine and fluids, initiate norepinephrine early while ensuring adequate intravascular volume. If cardiac output is low and hypotension persists, add inotrope support. Continue to reassess for reversible causes such as ongoing hemorrhage, tamponade, or tension pneumothorax. Be vigilant for adverse effects: arrhythmias, ischemia, and excessive vasoconstriction that may worsen tissue perfusion.

# Monitoring hemodynamic response and tissue perfusion in shock

Monitoring guides resuscitation and helps determine adequacy of intervention. Initial monitoring includes continuous electrocardiography, pulse oximetry, noninvasive blood pressure, and frequent clinical assessments (mental status, skin perfusion, capillary refill). Beyond these, more advanced and objective measures are indicated in severe or refractory anaphylactic shock.

Arterial blood pressure monitoring via an indwelling arterial catheter provides beat-to-beat hemodynamic data, which is particularly valuable when titrating vasoactive infusions and when noninvasive blood pressure measurements are unreliable. Target mean arterial pressure (MAP) is generally ≥ 65 mmHg in most adults, with individualized targets for patients with chronic hypertension or other comorbidities.

Laboratory markers include serum lactate, which reflects tissue hypoperfusion when elevated; normal lactate is typically < 2 mmol/L. Serial lactate measurements can track response to resuscitation: declining lactate suggests improving perfusion. Arterial blood gases and mixed/central venous oxygen saturation provide additional insights into oxygen delivery and utilization. Central venous oxygen saturation (ScvO2) monitoring — with a target generally ≥ 70% in many protocols — can indicate whether oxygen delivery is sufficient for metabolic demands, although interpretation requires context regarding hemoglobin, cardiac output, and oxygen extraction.

Urine output is a simple, practical perfusion marker; a target of ≥ 0.5 mL/kg/hr in adults is commonly used. Mental status and skin perfusion (warm vs cool extremities, capillary refill time) also provide rapid bedside information about perfusion. Capillary refill < 2 seconds is often considered reassuring, though it is operator-dependent and less reliable in some patient populations.

Assessment of fluid responsiveness is central to guiding further fluid administration. Static measures such as central venous pressure (CVP) are poor predictors of responsiveness. Dynamic measures and bedside functional tests are preferred: pulse pressure variation and stroke volume variation during controlled mechanical ventilation (with tidal volumes ≥ 8 mL/kg) are predictive of fluid responsiveness, as are passive leg raise (PLR) tests that transiently increase venous return and can be coupled with stroke volume or cardiac output monitoring to observe response. Point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) is invaluable: evaluation of left ventricular function, inferior vena cava (IVC) collapsibility, and direct assessment of volume status and cardiac output guides resuscitation and differentiates distributive from cardiogenic causes. An IVC that is small and collapsible suggests low preload, but interpretation must consider spontaneous versus mechanical ventilation and intra-abdominal pressures.

For the sickest patients, comprehensive hemodynamic monitoring with pulmonary artery catheterization or advanced pulse-contour-based cardiac output monitoring can be used to measure cardiac output, pulmonary artery pressures, and mixed venous oxygen saturation to tailor therapy. These invasive monitors are not required for most patients but may inform management in refractory shock or when complex cardiac dysfunction is suspected.

Finally, reassessment frequency should be high: after each intervention (bolus fluid, vasopressor titration, mechanical ventilation changes), re-evaluate clinical and objective markers. Persistent hypotension, rising lactate, decreasing urine output, or worsening mental status despite appropriate therapy indicate need for escalation or re-evaluation for alternative etiologies.

Conclusion

Airway compromise and circulatory collapse in anaphylaxis demand rapid, coordinated critical-care interventions. Early recognition of impending airway obstruction and proactive airway management, including awake techniques and preparedness for surgical airway, can be lifesaving. Oxygen delivery should be escalated promptly according to clinical need, and mechanical ventilation tailored to the pathophysiology of bronchospasm or pulmonary edema. Aggressive initial crystalloid boluses (e.g., 20 mL/kg, with consideration up to 30 mL/kg) restore preload, while vasopressors such as norepinephrine and adjuncts (vasopressin, inotropes) address refractory vasodilatory shock. Continuous, multimodal monitoring of hemodynamics and tissue perfusion — including arterial pressure, lactate, urine output, ScvO2, and bedside ultrasound — guides resuscitation and reduces the risk of both under- and over-treatment. Integrating pathophysiology with practical airway and circulatory strategies enables timely, effective management of severe anaphylactic shock.

Introduction

Anaphylaxis is an acute, potentially life-threatening systemic hypersensitivity reaction characterized by rapid onset and involvement of multiple organ systems. While many episodes resolve after prompt recognition and treatment, clinicians must also anticipate atypical temporal patterns of recurrence. These include biphasic anaphylaxis, multiphasic anaphylaxis, and refractory anaphylaxis. Each pattern carries distinct clinical implications for monitoring, resource use, and risk communication with patients and caregivers. This chapter defines these patterns operationally, reviews the range of reported incidence rates and methodological causes for variability, examines suggested predictors and risk factors, describes typical time-course patterns and windows for recurrence, outlines limitations of the existing evidence base, and draws practical implications for observation strategies and allocation of clinical resources.

# Operational definitions: biphasic vs multiphasic vs refractory anaphylaxis

Clear, operational definitions are essential for consistency in research and clinical practice. Although consensus statements exist (e.g., from professional allergy and immunology societies), definitions used in the literature vary, which contributes to heterogeneity in reported incidence and study conclusions. Below are definitions commonly applied in contemporary clinical and research contexts.

Biphasic anaphylaxis
Biphasic anaphylaxis refers to the recurrence of symptoms consistent with anaphylaxis after an initial episode has resolved without further exposure to the inciting allergen. By convention, a biphasic event fulfills three principal criteria: (1) there is an initial anaphylactic reaction that meets standard diagnostic criteria; (2) the initial event shows documented clinical improvement or resolution with therapy; and (3) a second phase of symptoms occurs after a symptom-free period, without re-exposure to the trigger. The second phase typically mirrors the first in organ systems involved (e.g., cardiovascular compromise, bronchospasm, cutaneous signs) but may be less or more severe. Definitions vary regarding the minimum symptom-free interval required to call a recurrence "biphasic" (common choices are ≥1 hour or ≥4 hours), and whether the second phase must require specific therapy (e.g., additional epinephrine) to qualify. Many studies require that the recurrence be clinically significant—prompting re-treatment or physician assessment—rather than minor, self-limited symptoms.

Multiphasic anaphylaxis
Multiphasic anaphylaxis denotes recurrence of anaphylactic symptoms on more than two discrete occasions (i.e., three or more phases), again without re-exposure to the trigger and with intervening symptom-free intervals. Multiphasic patterns are less commonly reported and can present as intermittent relapses over hours to days. The clinical importance of distinguishing multiphasic from biphasic patterns lies in recognizing that relapses may be unpredictable in timing and severity, sometimes necessitating prolonged or repeated therapy and extended monitoring.

Refractory anaphylaxis
Refractory anaphylaxis describes anaphylaxis that is unresponsive to initial standard therapy, typically defined as persistent or progressive signs of systemic anaphylaxis despite adequate doses of intramuscular (IM) epinephrine and other supportive measures. Operationally, refractory cases are often characterized by the requirement for repeated doses of epinephrine (e.g., more than three IM doses or continuous intravenous infusion) and ongoing hemodynamic or respiratory compromise that persists despite appropriate management. Definitions vary: some authors define refractoriness as failure to respond to two or more doses of epinephrine; others emphasize the need for continuous infusion or escalation to advanced cardiovascular support. Refractory anaphylaxis should prompt consideration of alternative or coexisting diagnoses (e.g., vasodilatory shock of another etiology, acute cardiogenic events, severe asthma exacerbation) and of adjunctive therapies and escalation to critical care.

Clinical diagnosis of anaphylaxis
Because biphasic, multiphasic, and refractory forms are temporal variations of anaphylaxis, the baseline diagnostic criteria for anaphylaxis apply. Commonly used clinical criteria include acute onset of illness with skin and/or mucosal involvement plus either respiratory compromise or reduced blood pressure; or two or more of the following after exposure to a likely allergen: skin/mucosal symptoms, respiratory compromise, reduced blood pressure or associated symptoms, and persistent gastrointestinal symptoms. For hypotension, age-appropriate thresholds apply (e.g., systolic blood pressure <90 mmHg in adults or a >30% decrease from baseline). Laboratory markers (e.g., serum tryptase) can support diagnosis but do not substitute for clinical criteria; tryptase is often elevated in anaphylaxis but a normal level does not exclude it.

# Reported incidence ranges and methodological reasons for variability

Reported incidence rates for biphasic anaphylaxis vary widely across studies, ranging from near 0% up to ~20% or higher depending on patient population and study methodology. Multiphasic anaphylaxis is less frequently quantified, and refractory anaphylaxis is uncommon but disproportionately represented in severe anaphylaxis cohorts. Several methodological factors account for this variability.

Heterogeneity in operational definitions
As noted, differing definitions of what constitutes a biphasic episode (minimum symptom-free interval, requirement for objective re-treatment) produce divergent estimates. Studies requiring a longer symptom-free interval or re-treatment with epinephrine typically report lower incidence, while those that include minor recurrent symptoms report higher rates.

Study population and setting
Incidence varies by setting: emergency department and hospital-based cohorts (which capture more severe reactions) report higher biphasic rates than outpatient immunology clinic series. Pediatric populations may have different patterns compared with adults. Anaphylaxis secondary to medications or intravenous exposures (e.g., radiocontrast, perioperative agents) tends to show higher rates of biphasic reactions than food-induced anaphylaxis in some series.

Ascertainment and follow-up duration
Short observation windows will miss late recurrences. Studies that limit observation to the emergency department stay often underestimate incidence relative to those with inpatient observation for 24 hours or structured post-discharge follow-up. Conversely, studies relying on patient self-report without objective verification may overestimate minor recurrences.

Treatment variability
Differences in acute management influence recurrence incidence. Prompt, adequate epinephrine administration and use of intravenous corticosteroids or longer antihistamine courses may correlate with lower recurrence rates in some reports. Conversely, underuse of epinephrine in initial management is associated with higher recurrence in several observational analyses.

Retrospective versus prospective design
Retrospective chart reviews are subject to incomplete documentation and under-detection of recurrences, particularly if patients sought care elsewhere. Prospective studies with predefined monitoring protocols yield more reliable estimates but are fewer and often limited by sample size.

Definition of clinical significance
Some studies count any recurrent symptom as biphasic anaphylaxis; others restrict to recurrences requiring further epinephrine, systemic corticosteroids, or hospitalization. The clinical implication is important: counting trivial or subjective recurrences inflates incidence but may not reflect outcomes that matter for resource allocation.

Diagnostic uncertainty and comorbidities
Overlap with other clinical entities (e.g., vasovagal syncope, asthma exacerbation, urticaria flares) can result in misclassification. Studies using rigorous diagnostic criteria and objective physiologic endpoints (e.g., hypotension, hypoxia, laryngeal edema confirmed on exam) will produce different incidence figures compared with looser clinical capture methods.

Given these methodological variations, clinicians should interpret reported incidence ranges with caution and consider the context—patient population, setting, and definitions—when applying literature findings to clinical practice.

# Suggested predictors and risk factors (delay in epinephrine, severe initial reaction, multiple doses, patient factors)

Several candidate predictors and risk factors for biphasic, multiphasic, and refractory anaphylaxis have been proposed and studied. Most evidence arises from observational cohorts and case series; therefore, associations may not be causal. Nevertheless, these factors are useful for clinical risk stratification.

Delay in epinephrine administration
Delayed administration of intramuscular epinephrine during the initial event is consistently associated with worse outcomes and a higher likelihood of prolonged or recurrent anaphylaxis. Multiple retrospective series show that patients who received epinephrine late or not at all were more likely to experience biphasic reactions and to require additional interventions. The pathophysiologic rationale is that timely epinephrine halts mediator release and counteracts life-threatening physiologic derangements (vasodilation, increased vascular permeability, bronchial smooth muscle constriction). Delays may allow a more intense or sustained immune cascade, increasing the risk of relapse.

Severity of initial reaction
Greater severity at presentation—manifested by hypotension, respiratory compromise (stridor, wheeze, hypoxemia), altered mental status, or requirement for multiple epinephrine doses—is associated with higher risk of biphasic and refractory patterns. Severe initial reactions likely reflect larger mediator burden, greater tissue involvement, or coexisting factors (e.g., beta-blocker use) that exacerbate hemodynamic instability. Several studies report that patients requiring >1 dose of epinephrine or vasopressors initially are more likely to have recurrence.

Requirement for multiple epinephrine doses during the initial episode
The need for repeated epinephrine during the first hour correlates with persistence of mediators or pharmacologic tachyphylaxis and predicts refractory courses. Patients receiving multiple doses are more likely to need ICU-level care and to have biphasic or multiphasic recurrences. This association supports closer observation in such patients.

Coexisting cardiopulmonary disease and medications
Comorbidities such as cardiovascular disease (ischemic heart disease, heart failure), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease or severe asthma, and older age are linked to more severe or refractory presentations. Certain medications influence response: beta-blockers may blunt response to epinephrine and are associated with refractory hypotension; angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors may predispose to angioedema or more pronounced hypotension in some settings. Concurrent use of selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors and other agents has been investigated but with inconsistent findings.

Atopic history and baseline mast cell disorders
A history of severe allergic disease (e.g., previous anaphylaxis) may predict recurrence, although the relationship is complex. Underlying mast cell disorders or elevated baseline tryptase (systemic mastocytosis, monoclonal mast cell activation syndrome) increase the risk of severe and recurrent anaphylaxis. Baseline tryptase >11.4 ng/mL is commonly used as a threshold suggesting mast cell disorders, but value interpretation requires clinical context and repeat testing. These patients are more prone to biphasic or protracted courses.

Type of trigger
Some triggers are more commonly associated with biphasic or severe reactions. For example, drug-induced and insect-sting–induced anaphylaxis may have higher rates of protracted or biphasic courses compared with food-induced anaphylaxis in certain series. IV exposures, perioperative agents, and radiocontrast reactions may produce more severe hemodynamic compromise and higher recurrence risk.

Laboratory markers
Post-event elevated serum tryptase (measured within 1–4 hours of onset and compared with baseline) suggests significant mast cell activation and has been associated with increased severity; however, data linking tryptase to biphasic recurrence are mixed. No single laboratory marker reliably predicts biphasic anaphylaxis in routine practice.

Demographic factors
Evidence about sex, race, and age is mixed. Some studies suggest older age correlates with increased risk of biphasic or refractory reactions, possibly via comorbidity burden and medication use. Pediatric populations have unique considerations—children may present with predominant respiratory symptoms and variable recurrence patterns.

In summary, delay in initial epinephrine, severity of the first reaction (including requirement for multiple epinephrine doses), certain triggers (drugs, intravenous exposures), comorbid cardiopulmonary disease, medications that blunt response to epinephrine (e.g., beta-blockers), and baseline mast cell disorders are among the strongest candidate predictors of biphasic, multiphasic, and refractory anaphylaxis. These risk factors should guide clinical decision-making regarding observation intensity and disposition.

# Time course patterns and typical windows for recurrence (hours to days)

Understanding temporal patterns of recurrence is crucial for setting observation durations and discharge instructions. Recurrence windows vary across individuals and settings, but several general patterns and typical timeframes have been consistently reported.

Immediate and early recurrence
Recurrent or persistent symptoms may appear within minutes to a few hours of the initial event. This early phase often reflects incomplete response to therapy or ongoing exposure/absorption of the allergen. In this context, recurrence typically manifests within the first 1–4 hours. Many studies consider a biphasic event to occur when recurrence happens after a minimum symptom-free interval of 1–4 hours.

Most common recurrence window
Multiple observational studies report that the majority of biphasic recurrences occur within 1–12 hours of symptom resolution, with a clustering between 4 and 10 hours in many cohorts. Consequently, several clinical guidelines recommend observation for at least 4–6 hours for patients with risk factors and up to 24 hours for higher-risk cases.

Late recurrence
Late biphasic or multiphasic recurrences occurring beyond 24 hours are uncommon but documented. Some case reports describe relapses within 24–72 hours or even several days later, especially in patients with protracted courses or delayed absorption from depot-type exposures (e.g., intradermal drug depots) or ongoing antigen exposure. Such late recurrences tend to be less frequent but can be clinically significant.

Protracted anaphylaxis versus biphasic
It is important to distinguish between biphasic recurrence and protracted anaphylaxis. Protracted anaphylaxis refers to an unremitting episode lasting many hours to days without a symptom-free interval. The clinical management involves continued therapy and often inpatient or ICU-level care. This differs from true biphasic or multiphasic patterns, which feature discrete symptom-free intervals.

Factors influencing timing
The timing of recurrence is influenced by trigger type (e.g., foods versus intravenous medications), amount and route of exposure, patient comorbidities, and adequacy of initial therapy (especially epinephrine). For example, reactions to ingested food allergens may have relatively rapid onset and resolution, whereas drug-induced reactions with ongoing release or slow redistribution can cause later relapses.

Clinical implication
Because most recurrences occur within the first 4–12 hours, observation periods within this window capture most biphasic events, especially in low-to-moderate risk patients. However, given the tails of the distribution and the presence of late recurrences in some higher-risk individuals, tailored observation durations and clear discharge instructions are essential.

# Evidence limitations and influence on clinical observation policies

The evidence base for biphasic, multiphasic, and refractory anaphylaxis has important limitations that affect clinical policy and practice.

Observational and retrospective data predominance
Most of the literature consists of retrospective chart reviews and small prospective cohorts. Randomized controlled trials comparing observation durations or specific interventions to prevent recurrence are lacking. Observational studies are subject to biases: selection bias (sicker patients are more likely to be observed and therefore have recurrences detected), information bias (incomplete documentation), and confounding (e.g., more aggressive treatment in sicker patients).

Heterogeneous definitions
As previously discussed, lack of standardized definitions across studies complicates meta-analysis and guideline development. Without consistent criteria for biphasic events (symptom-free interval, severity threshold), pooling data yields uncertain conclusions.

Limited power for rare events
True refractory anaphylaxis and multiphasic courses are relatively uncommon; small studies and heterogeneity reduce ability to identify reliable predictors or to quantify absolute risks accurately. Many studies report associations with wide confidence intervals, limiting precision.

Variable follow-up
Differences in follow-up methods (inpatient observation vs. telephone follow-up vs. chart review) influence detection. Post-discharge events that do not return to the index institution are often missed in retrospective series.

Lack of standardized treatment protocols
Divergence in acute management (timing and dosing of epinephrine, adjunctive therapies like corticosteroids) makes it difficult to isolate the effect of initial treatment on recurrence risk. This heterogeneity also complicates assessment of whether adjunctive therapies (e.g., corticosteroids) reduce biphasic risk—evidence is inconclusive.

Confounding by indication
Patients receiving more aggressive treatment (including hospitalization and longer observation) are inherently more likely to have severe disease and thus more likely to have recurrence; simply observing higher recurrence in hospitalized groups does not mean hospitalization increases risk.

Implications for observation policies
Given these limitations, guideline development has balanced the low absolute incidence of clinically significant biphasic events in many settings against the resource implications of prolonged observation for all patients. Many professional statements recommend risk-stratified observation: shorter ED observation (e.g., 4–6 hours) for low-risk patients who respond promptly to epinephrine and longer observation (12–24 hours or hospitalization) for higher-risk patients (severe initial reaction, delayed epinephrine, need for multiple epinephrine doses, refractory physiology, comorbidities). Policies also emphasize the need for clear discharge instructions regarding signs of recurrence, availability of epinephrine autoinjectors, and prompt access to care.

Because the evidence is imperfect, institutions should develop local pathways that consider resource constraints, local epidemiology, and acceptability of risk. Prospective data collection and standardized definitions are needed to refine observation recommendations further.

# Implications for monitoring strategies and resource allocation

Translating the available evidence into operational clinical pathways requires balancing patient safety with efficient use of healthcare resources. Monitoring strategies should be tailored based on initial severity, risk factors for recurrence, and local capabilities.

Risk-stratified observation
A tiered approach is practical. Low-risk patients—those with mild cutaneous symptoms only, prompt complete resolution after a single IM epinephrine dose, no respiratory or cardiovascular compromise, no significant comorbidities, and reliable access to follow-up—may be observed for a shorter interval (commonly 4–6 hours) before discharge with education and a prescription for an epinephrine autoinjector. Higher-risk patients—those with hypotension, respiratory compromise, need for repeated epinephrine doses, delayed initial epinephrine administration, suspicion for underlying mast cell disorder, or significant comorbidities—should be observed for longer (12–24 hours) or admitted for inpatient care. Refractory cases require ICU-level monitoring and access to advanced hemodynamic and airway support.

Monitoring modalities and thresholds
While continuous cardiorespiratory monitoring is not necessary for all patients, it is indicated for those with severe initial reactions or ongoing symptoms. Pulse oximetry, frequent vital sign checks (e.g., every 15–30 minutes initially), and observation for airway compromise are standard. For patients with hypotension requiring vasopressors or fluid resuscitation, invasive monitoring and critical care admission may be warranted.

Medication availability and staff training
Facilities should ensure immediate availability of epinephrine in appropriate formulations (IM autoinjector equivalents or pre-filled syringes), and staff must be trained in recognizing anaphylaxis and delivering timely epinephrine. Protocols for escalation (repeat epinephrine dosing, IV epinephrine infusion, adjunctive therapies, airway management) should be in place, particularly in settings where severe reactions are more likely (perioperative suites, radiology with contrast administration, inpatient infusion centers).

Resource allocation considerations
Widespread 24-hour observation for all anaphylaxis patients would strain resources and might not meaningfully reduce poor outcomes for low-risk patients given the low incidence of clinically significant late biphasic events. Conversely, inadequate observation of high-risk patients could result in preventable morbidity or mortality. Institutions must therefore allocate resources to identify and manage high-risk patients, invest in staff education, and provide systems for reliable outpatient follow-up and access to epinephrine autoinjectors.

Discharge planning and patient education
Regardless of observation length, discharge planning must emphasize recognition of recurrent symptoms, instructions for epinephrine administration, provision of an epinephrine autoinjector, and follow-up with an allergist/immunologist. Patients should be counseled on when to return for care (e.g., new or worsening respiratory symptoms, hypotension, persistent gastrointestinal symptoms) and given written action plans.

Quality improvement and data collection
To refine local practice, institutions should collect prospective data using standardized definitions, monitor timing and frequency of recurrences, and audit adherence to epinephrine administration guidelines. Such data can inform iterative adjustment of observation protocols and resource allocation.

Summary

Biphasic, multiphasic, and refractory anaphylaxis are important temporal variations of anaphylaxis with distinct implications for monitoring and management. Definitions vary across studies, contributing to a wide range of reported incidences. Delay in epinephrine, severity of the initial reaction, need for multiple epinephrine doses, certain triggers, comorbid conditions, and underlying mast cell disorders are among the most consistent risk factors identified. Most recurrences occur within the first 4–12 hours, but late relapses can occur. Evidence limitations—heterogeneous definitions, predominantly observational data, and variable follow-up—necessitate risk-stratified observation strategies that balance patient safety with efficient resource use. Clear protocols, staff training, availability of epinephrine, and robust discharge education are essential components of high-quality anaphylaxis care. Further prospective, standardized research is needed to refine prediction of recurrence and to optimize observation and resource allocation policies.

Introduction

Anaphylaxis and other mast cell activation syndromes (MCAS) are clinical diagnoses defined by an acutely evolving constellation of mucocutaneous, respiratory, cardiovascular, and/or gastrointestinal signs and symptoms caused by rapid mediator release from mast cells (and basophils). Laboratory testing does not replace a clinical diagnosis but can provide objective evidence of mast cell activation, help distinguish anaphylaxis from mimics, and guide further investigation for underlying disorders such as systemic mastocytosis or hereditary alpha-tryptasemia. Understanding the biology and kinetics of mast cell mediators, the optimal timing of specimen collection, how to interpret results in the context of an individual patient, and the practical constraints of the acute care environment is essential for clinicians who manage these patients. This chapter reviews the major laboratory tests used in suspected anaphylaxis and mast cell activation, the biologic rationale for each, recommended sampling schedules, interpretation including quantitative decision rules, and limitations that affect clinical use.

# Tryptase biology: sources, kinetics after mast cell activation and baseline considerations

Tryptase is a serine protease stored in mast cell secretory granules and is released predominantly by mast cells during degranulation. It exists in several isoforms; the two most clinically relevant are alpha- and beta-tryptase. Beta-tryptase is predominantly the enzymatically active form released during acute degranulation; alpha-tryptase tends to be constitutively secreted and contributes to baseline concentrations. Basophils contain little to no tryptase, so circulating tryptase is considered a relatively specific marker for mast cell activation.

Kinetics. After an episode of mast cell degranulation, serum tryptase typically begins to rise within minutes, is often measurable within 15–30 minutes, and reaches a peak usually between 1 and 4 hours after symptom onset. In many patients, tryptase concentrations fall toward baseline over 12–24 hours, although the exact time course varies with the severity of activation, the patient’s baseline level, and renal clearance. Because tryptase is measured in serum, sample processing (allowing clotting, then centrifugation) and timing matter. The peak is commonly observed between 1–2 hours for many cases of anaphylaxis but can be later; some protocols capture an early sample (within 1–2 hours) and a second sample at 2–4 hours if feasible.

Baseline tryptase. Individuals have a personal baseline serum tryptase, reflecting constitutive secretion and total body mast cell burden. In the general population, many laboratories use an upper limit of normal of approximately 11.4 ng/mL for total tryptase measured by common commercial immunoassays (units vary by assay and laboratory; values are reported in ng/mL). Persistently elevated baseline tryptase (commonly defined as >20 ng/mL) raises concern for systemic mastocytosis or clonal mast cell disorders and should prompt specialist referral and further workup. Mild elevations above the population reference range but substantially below 20 ng/mL can reflect hereditary alpha-tryptasemia (an inherited copy-number variation in TPSAB1) or other conditions; therefore, interpretation requires knowledge of the baseline.

Physiologic and pathologic modifiers. Baseline and stimulated tryptase are influenced by a number of factors: renal insufficiency can slow clearance and elevate baseline concentrations; myeloid or mast-cell lineage hematologic disorders can produce persistently elevated levels; recent severe allergic or inflammatory events can transiently raise baseline. Medications do not substantially interfere with most immunoassays for tryptase, but pre-analytical factors (time to centrifugation, storage temperature) matter.

# Recommended sampling schedule for tryptase

Because tryptase kinetics are time-dependent, a planned sampling schedule improves the chance of documenting mast cell activation. The recommended approach in suspected anaphylaxis is to obtain at least two samples: an acute (event) sample and a baseline sample obtained after full clinical resolution.

Acute-phase sampling. Obtain the first serum tryptase as soon as possible after symptom onset and ideally within 1–2 hours. A useful target is within 1 hour of the peak of the clinical reaction, but practically within the first 1–2 hours is acceptable. If only one acute sample is feasible in the clinical setting, take it as early as possible; if two acute samples are possible, obtain the first within 1–2 hours of onset and a second at 2–4 hours to capture the expected peak and the initial decline. In many centers, a single acute sample transiently elevated compared with baseline is accepted for confirmation of mast cell activation, but multiple acute samples increase certainty.

Baseline (convalescent) sampling. A baseline serum tryptase should be obtained at least 24 hours after complete resolution of symptoms and cardiovascular stability, and ideally several days after the episode. Many guidelines recommend waiting at least 24–48 hours and preferably 48–72 hours post-resolution to ensure return to true baseline and avoid detecting a residual post-event elevation. Because baseline values are used for the decision rule (see next section), an accurate baseline is crucial; if baseline is elevated, repeat testing or hematology/immunology referral is indicated.

Practical considerations. In the acute emergency department (ED) setting, competing priorities (airway, breathing, circulation) and logistical factors (phlebotomy availability, lab turnaround) can delay sampling. Clinicians should document the exact time of symptom onset (or the earliest reliable time), the time(s) of sample collection, and whether the patient received treatments (epinephrine, fluids, steroids, antihistamines) before sampling. Tryptase remains a useful test even when sampling is delayed, but the sensitivity for detecting an elevation diminishes as time from symptom onset increases—particularly beyond 4–6 hours.

# Interpretation of elevated tryptase: relative vs absolute increases and thresholds suggestive of mast cell activation or systemic mastocytosis

Absolute thresholds. Many laboratories use a reference upper limit of normal for total tryptase around 11.4 ng/mL (assay-dependent). An isolated acute tryptase above that upper reference limit supports mast cell activation if correlated with compatible clinical findings. For concern about systemic mastocytosis, commonly used thresholds include a persistent baseline tryptase ≥20 ng/mL as one major minor criterion that warrants further hematologic workup (bone marrow biopsy, KIT mutation testing) in the appropriate clinical context.

Relative (delta) criteria. Because individuals have different baseline tryptase levels, relying solely on an absolute cutoff can miss clinically important activations in patients with low baseline values. A commonly used quantitative decision rule for significant mast cell activation is an increase of acute tryptase over baseline by at least $2 + 1.2 \times \text{baseline}$ ng/mL. Explicitly, an acute tryptase is considered significantly elevated if:

acute tryptase − baseline tryptase > $2 + 1.2 \times \text{baseline}$

This rule increases sensitivity for detecting activation across a range of baselines. For example, a patient with baseline tryptase of 5 ng/mL would meet the criterion if the acute tryptase exceeds $2 + 1.2 \times 5 = 8$ ng/mL (i.e., an acute level >13 ng/mL); a patient with baseline 2 ng/mL would reach significance with acute level >$2 + 1.2 \times 2 = 4.4$ ng/mL (i.e., >6.4 ng/mL). Using the delta criterion reduces false negatives in patients with elevated baseline tryptase and avoids overcalling trivial increases in patients with very low baseline values.

Clinical context matters. Tryptase elevation alone is not diagnostic of anaphylaxis in an asymptomatic patient. Interpretation requires integration with the clinical picture: presence of rapidly developing cutaneous signs, respiratory compromise, hypotension, or gastrointestinal symptoms supports that an elevated tryptase reflects clinically relevant mast cell activation. Conversely, in patients without suggestive symptoms, an isolated marginal tryptase elevation warrants repeat baseline testing and consideration of alternative etiologies.

Thresholds suggestive of systemic mastocytosis and hereditary conditions. Persistent baseline tryptase ≥20 ng/mL is a red flag for clonal mast cell disease (systemic mastocytosis); very high values substantially above this threshold are uncommon in isolated anaphylaxis and indicate the need for hematologic referral. Mild but persistent elevations (e.g., baseline 11–20 ng/mL) may reflect hereditary alpha-tryptasemia or other causes and should prompt specialist evaluation. Genetic testing for TPSAB1 copy-number variation may be considered in patients with reproducibly elevated baseline tryptase and appropriate symptoms.

# Other laboratory tests: histamine/metabolites, complete blood count, lactate, arterial blood gas, electrolytes

Histamine and metabolites. Histamine is another classic mediator released with mast cell (and basophil) activation. Plasma histamine rises very rapidly but has a very short half-life (on the order of minutes), making it difficult to capture in peripheral blood unless drawn promptly and handled properly (collected on ice, processed immediately). Because of these pre-analytic challenges, plasma histamine assays have limited practical utility in many routine ED settings.

Urinary and plasma metabolites of histamine and prostaglandin D2 are more stable and useful for retrospective confirmation. Urine concentrations of N-methylhistamine (also called 1-methylhistamine or methylhistamine) collected in a timed urine (often 24-hour) or spot urine (normalized to creatinine) can remain elevated longer than plasma histamine and are often used in research or outpatient evaluation of episodic mast cell activation. Similarly, urinary 11β-PGF2α is a stable urinary metabolite of prostaglandin D2 and may rise after mast cell activation. These tests are assay- and laboratory-dependent, have variable reference ranges, and are generally sent in the outpatient setting or for research rather than for immediate ED decision-making.

Complete blood count (CBC). CBC performed during and after an anaphylactic episode can contribute to clinical assessment. Leukocytosis, often with neutrophilia and relative lymphopenia, is common in acute physiologic stress. Eosinophil counts may be suppressed during the acute phase due to corticosterone-mediated demargination and do not reliably diagnose anaphylaxis. Hemoconcentration (elevated hemoglobin/hematocrit) can indicate significant plasma extravasation and intravascular volume loss. Platelet counts are usually unaffected acutely but may be helpful in evaluating disseminated intravascular coagulation in severe cases.

Lactate and arterial/venous blood gas. Serum lactate is a marker of tissue hypoperfusion and anaerobic metabolism and can be useful in assessing the severity of circulatory compromise during anaphylaxis. Normal venous lactate is typically <2 mmol/L; higher values suggest systemic hypoperfusion. Arterial blood gas (ABG) or venous blood gas can quantify hypoxemia, acid–base disturbances, or hypercapnia in respiratory compromise. Oxygenation status (PaO2 on ABG) and lactate trends can guide resuscitation and disposition decisions.

Electrolytes and renal function. Basic metabolic panel: sodium 135–145 mmol/L, potassium 3.5–5.0 mmol/L, chloride, bicarbonate, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine. Severe anaphylaxis with hypotension and hypoperfusion can cause acute kidney injury and electrolyte abnormalities; hypocalcemia may also be observed in severe cases. Monitoring electrolytes is pragmatic for fluid and vasopressor management. Elevated creatinine may also affect tryptase clearance and complicate interpretation.

Other tests. Cardiac troponin can rise transiently in severe hypotension or in Kounis syndrome (allergic coronary syndrome); consider obtaining if chest pain or ECG abnormalities occur. Coagulation studies may be useful if hemorrhagic or severe systemic inflammatory complications are suspected.

# Limitations: timing, stability, assay variability and differential diagnoses causing elevated tryptase

Timing. The single greatest practical limitation in using biochemical markers of mast cell activation is timing. Tryptase has optimal sensitivity when sampled within the first 1–4 hours after symptom onset; plasma histamine has an even narrower window. Delays in obtaining acute samples decrease sensitivity and increase the risk of false-negative results. Conversely, baseline samples too soon after resolution may be confounded by residual elevation.

Stability and pre-analytic handling. Tryptase is measured in serum; blood should be allowed to clot and then centrifuged according to laboratory protocols. Samples should be stored and transported per manufacturer recommendations (many assays tolerate refrigerated transport but prolonged delays or repeated freeze–thaw cycles can affect results). Plasma histamine requires immediate cooling and rapid processing, often beyond the capacity of many EDs. Urine metabolite tests require correct collection timing and creatinine normalization.

Assay variability. Different commercial assays and laboratory methods have different calibrators and reference ranges. Laboratories commonly report total tryptase measured by immunoassay, but assays differ in analytic sensitivity and specificity; therefore, absolute cutoffs (e.g., 11.4 ng/mL) should be interpreted in light of the assay-specific reference interval reported by the performing laboratory. When comparing acute and baseline tryptase values, use the same assay and ideally the same laboratory to avoid inter-assay variability.

Differential diagnoses causing elevated tryptase. Elevated tryptase is not pathognomonic for anaphylaxis. Causes of raised tryptase include systemic mastocytosis and other clonal mast cell disorders, hereditary alpha-tryptasemia, end-stage renal disease (reduced clearance), severe myeloid malignancies, myelodysplastic syndromes, severe systemic inflammation, and, rarely, other hematologic malignancies that release tryptase or tryptase-like proteins. Technical factors and assay cross-reactivity may also produce spurious elevations. A persistently elevated baseline requires a different diagnostic pathway than a transient post-event rise.

Sensitivity and specificity. Tryptase elevation has good specificity for mast cell activation when the delta criterion is applied and when correlated with compatible clinical signs, but sensitivity is incomplete. Many cases of clinically diagnosed anaphylaxis—particularly those dominated by isolated mucocutaneous or bronchospastic symptoms without profound hypotension—may not produce a measurable rise in serum tryptase. Therefore, a normal tryptase does not exclude anaphylaxis.

# Reporting and documentation for later specialist review

Accurate documentation and clear reporting are essential so later reviewers (allergy/immunology or hematology) can interpret laboratory results in context. Important elements to document in the medical record and on the laboratory requisition include: the exact time of symptom onset (or best estimate), the time of each blood draw, treatments given prior to sampling (dose and time of epinephrine, antihistamines, corticosteroids, vasopressors, fluids), the clinical features present at the time of sampling (hypotension, hypoxemia, respiratory distress, urticaria, angioedema, GI symptoms), and whether the sample is intended as an acute/event sample or a baseline/convalescent sample.

When sending tryptase, indicate on the requisition whether the sample is an “acute” or “baseline” specimen and provide the time of symptom onset. If multiple samples are sent, label them clearly (e.g., “acute 1 hr,” “acute 3 hr,” “baseline >24 hr”), and ensure they are run by the same laboratory and assay. Photocopying or including a brief clinical summary with the sample can help the laboratory prioritize handling and assist specialists interpreting results later.

Follow-up planning. Clinicians should communicate with the patient and arrange outpatient follow-up to obtain a true baseline tryptase (≥24–48 hours after full resolution). If the baseline is elevated or the clinical history is suggestive of an underlying mast cell disorder, arrange timely referral to allergy/immunology and/or hematology for further evaluation, which may include repeat baseline tryptase, KIT mutation testing, bone marrow biopsy, or genetic testing for hereditary alpha-tryptasemia.

Conclusion

Laboratory testing—chiefly serum tryptase—can provide objective support for a diagnosis of mast cell activation in suspected anaphylaxis and can reveal underlying chronic mast cell disorders when baseline levels are elevated. Optimal use requires an appreciation of mast cell mediator biology (sources, isoforms, and kinetics), a planned sampling strategy (acute sample ideally within 1–2 hours, peak usually 1–4 hours, and baseline ≥24–48 hours after resolution), and application of both absolute and relative interpretation rules (including the $2 + 1.2 \times \text{baseline}$ criterion). Other tests (histamine/metabolites, CBC, lactate, blood gases, electrolytes) provide complementary information but have practical limitations. Awareness of assay variability, pre-analytic requirements, and alternative causes of tryptase elevation is necessary to avoid misinterpretation. Clear documentation of timing, clinical context, and treatments is crucial so specialist reviewers can accurately interpret results and recommend further evaluation when indicated.

# Introduction

Mast cells are tissue-resident hematopoietic cells derived from the myeloid lineage that play key roles in innate and adaptive immunity, host defense against parasites, and modulation of allergic inflammation. They contain preformed mediators (histamine, proteases such as tryptase and chymase), lipid mediators (prostaglandins, leukotrienes), and a wide range of cytokines and chemokines that are released on activation. Disorders of mast cells encompass a spectrum from isolated cutaneous disease to systemic clonal proliferation and disorders of inappropriate mast cell mediator release. These disorders are clinically important because they produce a wide range of symptoms—cutaneous, gastrointestinal, cardiovascular, neurologic, and skeletal—and because they predispose to recurrent and sometimes life-threatening anaphylaxis.

This chapter provides a clinical-pathophysiologic overview of mast cell disorders with emphasis on classification, diagnostic criteria for systemic mastocytosis (SM), the molecular underpinnings (particularly KIT mutations), typical organ involvement and manifestations, bone marrow histopathology, and implications for anaphylaxis risk, monitoring and long-term management. The goal is to give a foundation for understanding how these entities present, how they are investigated, and how the findings influence acute and chronic treatment decisions.

# Classification of mast cell disorders (cutaneous mastocytosis, systemic mastocytosis, mast cell activation syndrome)

Mast cell disorders are categorized by the distribution of mast cell involvement and by whether they represent a clonal neoplastic process. Broadly, they include cutaneous mastocytosis (CM), systemic mastocytosis (SM) and mast cell activation syndrome (MCAS), with further sub-classifications within systemic disease that reflect disease burden and prognosis.

Cutaneous mastocytosis is the most common form in children and can also occur in adults. In children it typically presents as urticaria pigmentosa (maculopapular cutaneous mastocytosis), solitary mastocytoma, or diffuse cutaneous mastocytosis. Pediatric disease is often limited to the skin and frequently regresses spontaneously during adolescence. In adults, cutaneous findings are more frequently associated with systemic involvement; therefore adult-onset cutaneous manifestations raise concern for systemic disease.

Systemic mastocytosis is a clonal mast cell disorder in which mast cells infiltrate extracutaneous organs (bone marrow is most commonly involved). The World Health Organization (WHO) classifies SM into several categories reflecting clinical behavior and associated hematologic disease: indolent systemic mastocytosis (ISM), smoldering SM (SSM), aggressive SM (ASM), systemic mastocytosis with an associated hematologic neoplasm (SM-AHN), and mast cell leukemia (MCL). Indolent SM is characterized primarily by mediator-related symptoms and limited organ dysfunction; aggressive forms exhibit organ damage attributable to mast cell infiltration (cytopenias, malabsorption, hepatic dysfunction, large-volume splenomegaly, portal hypertension). SM-AHN includes additional clonal hematologic neoplasms (for example chronic myelomonocytic leukemia, myelodysplastic syndromes).

Mast cell activation syndrome is a heterogeneous group of disorders defined by episodic symptoms attributable to mast cell mediator release without necessarily demonstrating clonality or mast cell proliferation. MCAS is typically subdivided into primary (clonal, e.g., KIT-mutant mast cells but without meeting full criteria for SM), secondary (reactive, e.g., mast cell activation driven by allergy, autoimmune disease, or other underlying trigger), and idiopathic (no identifiable clonal marker or trigger). Diagnostic rigor is important because the term MCAS has been applied variably; true MCAS requires objective evidence of mast cell mediator release and clinically significant response to mast cell–directed therapy (see section on diagnostic criteria below).

Understanding the classification is clinically important: pediatric cutaneous disease has different prognosis and management than adult-onset cutaneous lesions, and the presence or absence of clonality (KIT mutation, aberrant CD25 expression) directs both prognostication and consideration of targeted therapy. Distinguishing MCAS from SM has implications for monitoring for progressive organ involvement and for long-term therapies that suppress mast cell burden.

# WHO diagnostic criteria for systemic mastocytosis: major and minor criteria overview

Diagnosis of systemic mastocytosis follows WHO criteria that combine morphologic, immunophenotypic, biochemical, and molecular findings into a framework of one major and four minor criteria. The diagnosis of SM requires either the major criterion plus one minor criterion, or at least three minor criteria.

The major criterion is defined by histologic documentation of multifocal dense infiltrates of mast cells in bone marrow or other extracutaneous organs. Specifically, these are collections or aggregates of mast cells composed of at least 15 mast cells per aggregate as appreciated in hematoxylin-eosin stained sections or through mast cell tryptase immunostaining.

The four minor criteria are: (1) more than 25% of mast cells in the infiltrate are spindle-shaped or show atypical morphology on histology, or there is the presence of >25% atypical mast cells in marrow smears; (2) detection of an activating point mutation at codon 816 of KIT (most commonly D816V) in bone marrow or another extracutaneous organ; (3) mast cells in bone marrow or blood expressing aberrant surface markers not found on normal mast cells, typically CD25 and/or CD2 in addition to KIT (CD117); and (4) a persistently elevated baseline serum tryptase level >20 ng/mL (measured on samples drawn during clinical stability, not during acute mast cell activation).

Thus, a single focal bone marrow mast cell aggregation plus one of these minor criteria establishes systemic disease; alternatively, three minor criteria in the absence of the major criterion suffice. It is important to recognize the timing and pre-analytic constraints of some tests: baseline serum tryptase should be measured outside of acute events (because tryptase rises transiently during anaphylaxis), and molecular testing requires sufficient sensitivity to detect the often low allele burden of KIT D816V in marrow or peripheral blood. These criteria integrate morphology, immunophenotype, genetics and biochemical evidence of mast cell activation and have been repeatedly validated in clinical practice.

# Role of KIT mutations (eg, D816V) and other molecular markers

KIT, a receptor tyrosine kinase encoded by the KIT proto-oncogene (CD117), is a key regulator of mast cell growth, differentiation, survival and activation. Stem cell factor (SCF) is the ligand for KIT. Activating mutations in KIT lead to constitutive kinase activity, independent of SCF, and thereby drive clonal expansion of mast cells in many cases of systemic mastocytosis.

The most common and clinically relevant mutation in adult SM is the D816V substitution in exon 17 of KIT. This mutation confers constitutive activation and is present in the majority of adult SM cases, including most indolent SM and almost all advanced SM cases. Detection of KIT D816V serves both as a diagnostic minor criterion and as a target for therapy, though it also has therapeutic implications because many early generation tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) do not inhibit KIT D816V effectively.

Molecular testing for KIT D816V requires sensitive methods because the mutant allele may be present at a low variant allele fraction, particularly in indolent disease. Techniques include allele-specific PCR, quantitative PCR, digital droplet PCR, and next-generation sequencing panels with deep coverage. Detection in bone marrow aspirate or enriched mast cell fractions increases sensitivity; peripheral blood testing with highly sensitive assays can sometimes detect the mutation and serve as a less invasive screening tool.

Beyond D816V, other KIT mutations may occur, especially in pediatric cutaneous mastocytosis or in rare adult cases, and non-D816V mutations can influence responsiveness to kinase inhibitors. Additionally, other molecular markers may be present in SM-AHN or advanced SM, and concurrent somatic mutations in genes associated with myeloid neoplasms (for example SRSF2, ASXL1, RUNX1) are associated with more aggressive disease and poorer prognosis. These additional mutations are often sought with broad myeloid next-generation sequencing panels when SM is suspected or when an associated hematologic neoplasm is present, as they inform prognosis and therapeutic choices.

Immunophenotypic markers include CD117 (KIT) which is expressed on normal mast cells, but aberrant expression of CD25 and/or CD2 on mast cells is a minor criterion for SM. CD30 expression has been described in some cases of advanced SM and may have diagnostic or prognostic relevance. Flow cytometry and immunohistochemical stains are complementary in detecting aberrant antigen expression.

# Typical organ involvement (skin, bone marrow, liver, spleen, GI tract) and clinical manifestations

Mast cell disorders produce a spectrum of organ-specific signs and symptoms that reflect both mediator-related effects and tissue infiltration. Clinical manifestations can be grouped by primary organ systems.

Skin: Cutaneous involvement may be the first clue to a mast cell disorder. Urticaria pigmentosa (maculopapular cutaneous mastocytosis) presents as hyperpigmented macules or papules that often urticate (Darier sign) after stroking. Solitary mastocytomas are localized nodules often seen in children. Diffuse cutaneous mastocytosis causes widespread erythema, blistering, and increased risk of systemic mediator release. Pruritus, flushing, and dermatographism are common mediator-related symptoms. In adults, the presence of cutaneous lesions commonly coexists with systemic disease.

Bone marrow and hematopoietic system: The bone marrow is the most commonly involved extracutaneous organ. Bone marrow infiltration may be patchy and is the focus for histopathologic diagnosis. Symptoms related to marrow involvement include cytopenias (anemia, leukopenia, thrombocytopenia) when infiltration is extensive and mast cell burden is high. Bone involvement includes osteopenia and osteoporosis, particularly in adults with SM; pathogenic mechanisms include mast cell-derived mediators (e.g., histamine and cytokines) and local osteoclast activation. Patients may suffer pathologic fractures and bone pain. Bone mineral density testing (DEXA) is recommended in adults with SM; values are interpreted using standard T-scores with osteoporosis defined as T-score ≤ -2.5. Vertebral compression fractures are an important clinical consequence.

Liver and spleen: Mast cell infiltration can produce hepatomegaly, portal fibrosis, cholestatic liver chemistry abnormalities, and ultimately portal hypertension in aggressive disease. Splenomegaly (sometimes massive) is common in advanced SM and may be accompanied by hypersplenism leading to cytopenias. Liver function tests (AST, ALT, alkaline phosphatase, bilirubin) and imaging may reveal organomegaly or structural damage; progressive hepatic dysfunction is a marker of aggressive disease.

Gastrointestinal tract: GI symptoms are among the most frequent mediator-related complaints and include abdominal pain, cramping, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, and malabsorption leading to weight loss and nutrient deficiencies. Symptoms can be episodic and related to mast cell mediator release or chronic owing to mucosal infiltration and damage. Chronic diarrhea and malabsorption may require nutritional assessment and management.

Cardiovascular and anaphylaxis: Mast cell mediators (histamine, platelet-activating factor, prostaglandin D2, leukotrienes) produce vasodilation and increased vascular permeability, which underlie flushing, hypotension and anaphylaxis. Patients with SM and some forms of MCAS are at increased risk for severe, recurrent anaphylaxis, often with atypical triggers (e.g., insect venoms, medications) and sometimes with no identifiable trigger. Baseline tryptase tends to be higher in clonal mast cell disorders, and an elevated baseline tryptase is associated with greater anaphylaxis risk, though normal tryptase does not exclude risk.

Other organ systems: Neuropsychiatric symptoms (anxiety, cognitive fog), musculoskeletal pain, and constitutional symptoms (fatigue, weight loss) are commonly reported and are often multifactorial—mediator effects, chronic inflammation, sleep disturbance, and comorbid conditions contribute. Pulmonary involvement is uncommon but may present as bronchospasm. Renal and neurologic complications are rare but can occur in advanced disease.

Clinically, distinguishing mediator-driven symptoms from organ dysfunction due to mast cell infiltration is important because it guides urgency and type of therapy: antihistamines and mediator-directed therapies can control many symptoms, while organ damage from infiltrative disease may require cytoreductive therapy.

# Bone marrow biopsy and histopathologic findings (mast cell aggregates, CD25/CD2 expression, morphology)

Bone marrow biopsy with aspiration and immunohistochemistry is central to diagnosing systemic mastocytosis. Because mast cell infiltration can be focal and patchy, adequate sampling and careful histologic examination are necessary. The bone marrow evaluation includes morphology on H&E stains, special stains for mast cell tryptase and/or Giemsa, immunohistochemistry for CD117 (KIT), and flow cytometry to characterize aberrant antigen expression.

Histopathologically, the major diagnostic feature is multifocal dense infiltrates (aggregates) of mast cells in bone marrow or other extracutaneous organs. The WHO definition specifies aggregates of 15 or more mast cells per cluster. Morphologically, neoplastic mast cells are often spindle-shaped with elongated cytoplasmic processes and atypical nuclear contours; in smoldering and aggressive disease, increased numbers of atypical mast cells can be seen in marrow smears. In indolent disease, more rounded and mature-appearing mast cells may predominate, but abnormal clustering remains a key clue.

Immunophenotyping is crucial because neoplastic mast cells commonly express aberrant antigens not seen on normal mast cells. Normal mast cells are CD117 (KIT) positive and typically negative for CD25 and CD2. In SM, mast cells frequently express CD25 and/or CD2; detection of CD25 expression on mast cells in bone marrow is a minor WHO diagnostic criterion. Flow cytometry may also reveal expression of CD30 in some cases, particularly advanced disease. The profile CD117+/CD25+ (and sometimes CD2+) on mast cells is highly suggestive of clonality.

Mast cell tryptase immunostaining helps visualize both individual mast cells and their aggregates; tryptase is a useful stain because it highlights mast cells even when morphology is subtle. Quantification of mast cell burden and description of the pattern of infiltration (focal aggregates versus diffuse infiltration) are part of the pathology report and are used in the SM diagnostic algorithm and in classification (e.g., criteria for B- and C-findings that define smoldering or aggressive disease).

Because marrow infiltration can be patchy, a negative marrow biopsy does not completely exclude systemic disease especially if other minor criteria such as a KIT D816V mutation or elevated baseline tryptase are present; repeat sampling or testing of other extracutaneous sites may be needed in selected cases. Aspirates can also be useful for cytogenetics and molecular testing to identify concurrent myeloid neoplasms.

# Implications of mast cell disorders for anaphylaxis risk, monitoring and long-term management

Mast cell disorders carry an elevated risk of anaphylaxis compared with the general population, and this risk is particularly pronounced among patients with clonal mast cell disease and/or elevated baseline serum tryptase. Clinical management therefore has two parallel aims: prevention and prompt treatment of acute mast cell–mediator events (notably anaphylaxis), and control of chronic symptoms and organ damage through mediator suppression or reduction of mast cell burden when indicated.

Risk of anaphylaxis: Patients with SM (especially those with KIT D816V or other clonal markers) and some types of MCAS have recurrent severe anaphylaxis that may be triggered by hymenoptera venom (notably bee and wasp stings), medications (opioids, radiocontrast, neuromuscular blockers), and less commonly foods. In many cases of SM-associated anaphylaxis, no clear trigger is identified. Baseline serum tryptase correlates imperfectly with anaphylaxis risk, but extremely elevated baseline tryptase concentrations (for example, >20 ng/mL) raise concern for underlying clonal mast cell disease and heightened risk. For diagnosing anaphylaxis specifically related to mast cell activation, a pragmatic biochemical criterion is an acute rise in serum tryptase above baseline by at least 20% of baseline plus 2 ng/mL (i.e., acute tryptase > baseline × 1.2 + 2 ng/mL) measured within 3–4 hours of symptom onset.

Monitoring: Patients with diagnosed SM or established MCAS require a tailored monitoring strategy. Baseline assessments often include serum tryptase, complete blood count with differential (to assess cytopenias suggesting marrow involvement), liver function tests, alkaline phosphatase, bilirubin, coagulation profile if clinically indicated, and imaging (ultrasound or cross-sectional imaging) for organomegaly. Bone mineral density testing (DEXA) is indicated in adults because of the risk of osteoporosis. Periodic reassessment of blood counts, liver chemistry and tryptase (frequency individualized by disease subtype and therapy) is appropriate to detect progression to smoldering or aggressive disease. In SM-AHN or advanced SM, more intensive monitoring and hematologic evaluation are required.

Acute management: For anaphylactic episodes, standard emergency management applies—immediate intramuscular epinephrine is first-line, along with airway, breathing and circulation support, oxygen, intravenous fluids, and adjunctive therapies (H1 and H2 antihistamines, bronchodilators, and corticosteroids for biphasic reactions). Given the increased risk in mast cell disorders, patients should receive education on early recognition of symptoms and indications for epinephrine. Most clinicians prescribe at least one epinephrine autoinjector; patients with prior severe reactions or recurrent severe reactions should have multiple autoinjectors and individualized emergency action plans.

Preventive strategies include trigger avoidance (where triggers are known), venom immunotherapy for patients with venom-induced anaphylaxis (venom immunotherapy is highly effective and recommended, though close collaboration with an allergist experienced in mast cell disorders is important), and premedication strategies for unavoidable exposures (e.g., pre-procedure H1/H2 blockade and steroids for contrast media or anesthesia as directed by anesthesia/allergy consultation). For patients with confirmed clonal mast cell disease, prophylactic antibiotics are not indicated specifically for mastocytosis but general preventive care (vaccinations, standard infection prevention) applies.

Chronic symptomatic management centers on mediator-blocking therapies. H1 antihistamines (both H1 non-sedating for daytime control and sedating at night when needed) and H2 blockers reduce cutaneous and gastrointestinal symptoms. Leukotriene receptor antagonists (e.g., montelukast) and inhibitors of mast cell mediator release (cromolyn sodium for GI symptoms) can be helpful. Oral corticosteroids are effective for severe flare control but their long-term use is limited by toxicity. Bisphosphonates are indicated for osteoporosis; calcium and vitamin D supplementation as appropriate. For advanced or progressive SM with organ damage, cytoreductive therapies to lower mast cell burden are indicated. Agents include interferon-alpha and cladribine (2-chlorodeoxyadenosine); more recently, targeted KIT inhibitors with activity against D816V-mutant KIT—midostaurin and avapritinib—have demonstrated efficacy in advanced SM and are approved in many jurisdictions. Choice of systemic therapy depends on disease subtype, mutation profile, associated hematologic neoplasms, symptoms and comorbidities. Enrollment in clinical trials is appropriate when available.

Long-term management also addresses psychosocial aspects, education, and coordination among specialties (allergy/immunology, hematology/oncology, dermatology, gastroenterology, endocrinology, and primary care). Patients benefit from written action plans, training in autoinjector use, and counseling about medications and vaccinations with potential to trigger reactions. Pre-procedural planning (perioperative and anesthesia considerations) and careful medication reconciliation to avoid agents with high histamine-release potential (e.g., certain opiates, vancomycin rapid infusion) reduce risk.

Finally, genetic counseling and family assessment are sometimes relevant, particularly in familial clustering or pediatric-onset disease, although most adult SM cases are sporadic. Prognosis depends heavily on subtype—indolent SM often has near-normal life expectancy with symptom control, while aggressive SM, SM-AHN and mast cell leukemia have markedly worse outcomes and require specialist-directed cytoreductive treatment.

# Summary

Mast cell disorders encompass a spectrum from localized cutaneous disease to systemic clonal mast cell proliferation and disorders of inappropriate mediator release. Accurate classification—cutaneous mastocytosis, systemic mastocytosis with WHO-mandated major and minor criteria, and mast cell activation syndromes—guides diagnosis and management. KIT D816V and other mutations are central to pathogenesis, diagnosis and increasingly to targeted therapy. Bone marrow biopsy with careful histology and immunophenotyping (notably CD25/CD2 aberrant expression) is central to identifying systemic disease. Mast cell disorders carry significant morbidity both from chronic mediator effects and from risks of severe anaphylaxis; management combines acute preparedness (epinephrine, emergency planning), chronic mediator blockade, organ-specific management (bisphosphonates for bone disease, hepatic monitoring), and, for advanced disease, cytoreductive or targeted molecular therapy. Close multidisciplinary care, patient education and individualized monitoring strategies are essential to optimize outcomes.

Introduction

Drug hypersensitivity reactions encompass a wide spectrum of immune-mediated and non–immune-mediated adverse events related to medications. Correct classification and diagnosis are critical: mislabeling a patient as “allergic” can lead to use of less effective or more toxic antibiotics, increased healthcare costs, and risk of antimicrobial resistance, while failure to recognize a true immunologic drug allergy can lead to life‑threatening rechallenge. This chapter provides a practical, clinically oriented overview of the principles and methodologies used to diagnose both immediate (typically IgE-mediated) and delayed (T cell–mediated and other) drug hypersensitivity. Emphasis is placed on skin testing techniques, in vitro serologic assays, drug provocation testing (DPT), approaches to common problems such as beta‑lactam allergy, and when to defer or refer to an allergy specialist. Throughout, we relate pathophysiology to the timing and pattern of clinical findings to guide decision making and patient safety.

# Types of drug hypersensitivity (immediate vs delayed) and mechanisms

Drug hypersensitivity reactions are commonly categorized by timing and underlying immunopathology. Clinically useful divisions separate immediate reactions—those occurring within minutes to a few hours of drug exposure—from delayed reactions that appear hours to weeks later. Understanding mechanism helps determine which diagnostic tests are most informative and what interventions are safe.

Immediate hypersensitivity
Immediate reactions usually occur within minutes to 1–6 hours of drug administration and classically include urticaria, angioedema, bronchospasm, hypotension, and anaphylaxis. Many immediate reactions are mediated by drug‑specific IgE bound to high‑affinity FcεRI receptors on mast cells and basophils. Cross‑linking of these IgE molecules by the offending drug or a drug‑carrier complex triggers rapid degranulation and release of histamine, tryptase, prostaglandins, and leukotrienes.

Several nuances are important. Some immediate reactions are non‑IgE, nonimmunologic histamine release (“anaphylactoid”) due to direct mast cell activation (e.g., radiocontrast media, opiates, vancomycin‑related “red man” reaction) or complement activation (e.g., certain lipid formulations). These mimic IgE‑mediated anaphylaxis clinically but are unlikely to show specific IgE or positive skin tests.

Laboratory correlates include transiently elevated serum tryptase measured within 1–4 hours of event; values >11.4 ng/mL are commonly used as a threshold for concern, and a rise of at least 20% over baseline plus 2 ng/mL is often used to support mast cell activation. Serum-specific IgE testing and in vivo skin testing may identify drug-specific IgE when available.

Delayed hypersensitivity
Delayed reactions appear more than 1 hour after exposure, often days to weeks later. They are predominantly T cell–mediated and present as maculopapular exanthems, fixed drug eruptions, drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms (DRESS), Stevens–Johnson syndrome (SJS)/toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN), and various organ-specific syndromes (drug‑induced liver injury, interstitial nephritis). The underlying mechanisms generally involve drug or drug‑metabolite acting as a hapten to form peptides presented by HLA molecules, or direct pharmacologic interaction of the drug with immune receptors (the p–i concept), activating drug‑specific CD4+ or CD8+ T cells that drive inflammation and tissue damage.

Genetic risk factors have strong roles for many severe delayed reactions; HLA‑allele associations (e.g., HLA‑B*57:01 and abacavir hypersensitivity; HLA‑B*15:02 and carbamazepine SJS/TEN in some Asian populations) can be screened to prevent reactions. Patch testing and late intradermal readings can sometimes detect T cell–mediated sensitization, but sensitivity varies by syndrome and drug. Routine serologic IgE assays are generally not useful for delayed reactions.

Temporal patterns and phenotypes guide testing strategy. Immediate, anaphylaxis‑type reactions warrant evaluation for drug‑specific IgE and consideration of skin testing; delayed exanthems often require different approaches, including history‑driven avoidance, possible patch testing, and cautious graded challenge under specialist supervision when appropriate.

# Skin prick testing and intradermal testing: methodology, interpretation and safety considerations

Skin testing is a cornerstone for diagnosing IgE‑mediated drug allergy when standardized reagents are available (e.g., penicillins). There are two principal techniques: skin prick testing (SPT) and intradermal testing (IDT). SPT is preferred as the initial test because it is less sensitive but more specific and safer; IDT is more sensitive but carries greater risk of systemic reaction and false positives from irritant concentrations.

Methodology
Skin prick testing involves placing a drop of test solution on the volar forearm (or back) and introducing the allergen into the epidermis by a superficial prick. A positive control (histamine, typically 10 mg/mL or 1 mg/mL depending on local practice) and negative control (saline or diluent) must always be included. Reactions are read at 15–20 minutes; a wheal diameter of ≥3 mm more than the negative control is commonly considered positive. SPT solutions for many drugs are not commercially standardized; for penicillin, benzylpenicilloyl polylysine (penicilloyl‑major determinant, PPL) and minor determinant mix are used where available, plus the native drug (e.g., amoxicillin).

Intradermal testing involves injecting a small volume (usually 0.02–0.05 mL) of diluted drug solution intradermally to produce a small bleb. IDT is read at 20 minutes for immediate hypersensitivity; an increase in wheal diameter of ≥3 mm over the initial bleb size (and larger than the negative control) is considered positive. For delayed hypersensitivity, IDT sites can be read at 24–48–72 hours for induration and erythema reflecting T cell responses.

Test concentrations
Appropriate, non‑irritant concentrations are essential to avoid false positives. For penicillins, validated concentrations exist for PPL and minor determinant mix. For other drugs, recommended non‑irritant concentrations have been published, but availability varies. As an example, intradermal testing concentrations that are likely non‑irritant for common antibiotics are often in the range of 1:1000 to 1:10 dilution of the therapeutic solution, but empirical use without validated data risks false‑positive irritant results or false negatives if overly diluted. For many drugs (e.g., cephalosporins, macrolides, quinolones), both SPT and IDT concentrations and predictive values are less well standardized.

Interpretation
A positive SPT or IDT supports IgE‑mediated sensitization and increases the pretest probability that the drug caused the immediate hypersensitivity. A negative test reduces probability but does not eliminate risk—negative predictive value depends on the test sensitivity for the specific drug and on prevalence in the tested population. For penicillin, combined SPT and IDT using PPL and minor determinant mix historically have high negative predictive value (>95%) for immediate reactions; therefore, a negative test paired with negative specific IgE generally permits a graded oral challenge in many settings. For other drugs, negative skin testing is less reassuring.

False positives can occur from irritant effects at high concentrations or from dermographism. False negatives can result when testing is performed too long after the index reaction (IgE levels wane over time—sensitivity decreases with increasing interval), if drug metabolites (rather than parent drug) are the antigenic target, or if the test lacks sensitivity for that drug.

Safety considerations
Skin testing carries risk of systemic allergic reactions and, rarely, anaphylaxis. IDT is higher risk than SPT. Always have emergency resuscitation equipment and staff trained in anaphylaxis management available. Begin with SPT and proceed to IDT only when needed and when benefits outweigh risks. For patients with recent severe anaphylaxis or unstable cardiovascular/respiratory status, defer skin testing until stabilized and consider alternative evaluation strategies or specialist referral. For patients on antihistamines, hold H1 antihistamines for at least 5–7 half‑lives (commonly 48–72 hours; for longer‑acting agents, longer) to avoid false negatives; topical and systemic steroids have less clear impact on immediate skin testing but may blunt reactions when used in high doses.

Documentation should include lot numbers/concentrations of reagents, sites and sizes of wheals/erythema, time of readings, and patient consent after counseling about risks. For delayed readings, ensure patients can be followed or return for later assessment.

# Serologic testing (specific IgE) for drugs and other allergens: utility and limitations

In vitro serologic assays measuring drug‑specific IgE (e.g., ImmunoCAP, RAST) are available for a limited number of drugs (notably penicillins, some cephalosporins, and a few others). Additionally, component‑resolved diagnostics and basophil activation tests (BAT) expand the in vitro toolkit. Each modality has strengths and limitations that inform clinical use.

Specific IgE assays
Serologic specific IgE testing is non‑invasive and safer than in vivo testing in patients with high‑risk histories. For penicillin, validated specific IgE assays exist; a positive result indicates circulating drug‑specific IgE and supports the diagnosis of IgE‑mediated hypersensitivity. However, sensitivity is imperfect; a negative test does not reliably exclude allergy, particularly as specific IgE levels fall over time. For penicillin, combined skin testing and specific IgE testing provide better negative predictive value than either alone.

Performance characteristics vary widely by drug and assay. Reported sensitivities for drug-specific IgE range from low to moderate (often 30–70%) and specificities from moderate to high. Positive predictive value depends on the pretest probability. Because specific IgE assays for many drugs are unavailable, their absence does not imply lack of IgE‑mediated mechanism.

Basophil activation test (BAT)
BAT measures basophil activation markers (e.g., CD63, CD203c) by flow cytometry after in vitro exposure to the suspect drug. BAT can be useful when skin testing is negative or not feasible, especially for immediate reactions and some non‑standard drugs. Sensitivity and specificity vary by agent and laboratory; BAT is technically demanding, not widely available, and requires fresh samples. BAT may be particularly helpful for drugs that cause non‑IgE but IgE‑independent basophil activation or where metabolites are implicated if appropriate conditions are used.

Other serologic tests and biomarkers
Tryptase measurement during or soon after suspected anaphylaxis can document mast cell degranulation; reference range is assay‑dependent, but many labs use <11.4 ng/mL as an upper limit of normal. A rise from baseline (collected after recovery) of at least 20% + 2 ng/mL supports mast cell activation. For delayed reactions, specific lymphocyte transformation tests (LTT) and ex vivo cytokine assays can sometimes identify drug‑reactive T cells; however, LTT sensitivity is variable and requires specialized labs.

Limitations and clinical application
In vitro tests rarely stand alone. A positive specific IgE or BAT increases posttest probability sufficiently to avoid provocation in many settings, but false positives exist. A negative in vitro test does not reliably exclude immediate hypersensitivity—especially for agents without well‑validated assays. Timing matters: assays are most sensitive when performed relatively near the time of reaction; IgE levels may decline over months to years. Pretest probability informed by history guides test choice—high‑risk recent anaphylaxis may warrant avoidance or specialist evaluation even if tests are negative.

In sum, serologic testing is a valuable adjunct to skin testing and clinical history, particularly when in vivo testing is contraindicated or when reagents for skin testing are unavailable. Availability, turnaround time, and test performance characteristics should inform ordering and interpretation.

# Drug challenge/provocation testing: indications, contraindications and safety precautions

Drug provocation testing (DPT), also called graded challenge or drug challenge, is the definitive diagnostic tool to determine whether a patient tolerates a drug. It involves administering the suspect drug under controlled conditions and observing for reaction. DPT is used to confirm or exclude hypersensitivity when other testing is inconclusive or unavailable and when the risk of conservative management (i.e., avoiding the drug) would harm the patient. Because it deliberately exposes the patient, careful selection, planning, and safety measures are essential.

Indications
DPT is indicated when the clinical history is ambiguous or suggests low probability, skin testing and serology are negative or unavailable, and the drug has significant therapeutic importance (e.g., first‑line antibiotic) or alternatives are less desirable. It is appropriate to confirm tolerance in patients with remote histories of benign childhood rashes or nonspecific reactions. For patients labeled penicillin‑allergic with a low‑risk history and negative skin tests, an oral challenge is often performed to delabel.

Contraindications
DPT is contraindicated in patients with prior severe cutaneous adverse reactions (SCARs) such as SJS/TEN, DRESS, acute generalized exanthematous pustulosis (AGEP) with systemic involvement, or severe organ‑specific drug reactions (e.g., drug‑induced liver injury, interstitial nephritis) attributable to the drug in question. DPT should not be performed in patients with recent severe anaphylaxis with unstable control, pregnant patients (unless benefits outweigh risks and under specialist care), or in settings without immediate resuscitation and intensive monitoring facilities. For reactions where cross‑reactivity is likely and testing reagents for alternative agents are lacking, perform DPT only under specialist supervision.

Protocol and safety precautions
A DPT protocol is individualized to the drug, route of administration, and clinical context, but general principles apply. The challenge is performed in a monitored clinical environment with immediate access to resuscitation equipment, medications for anaphylaxis (epinephrine, oxygen, antihistamines, corticosteroids), and personnel trained in emergency response. Baseline assessment includes vital signs, review of comorbidities (cardiovascular, pulmonary), pregnancy test when applicable, and withholding medications that could mask or amplify reactions (e.g., beta‑blockers increase risk/severity of anaphylaxis and blunt epinephrine response; consider temporary adjustment in consultation with the prescribing physician).

Graded dosing typically starts with a very small fraction of the therapeutic dose (e.g., 1/100 to 1/10) and escalates at 30–60 minute intervals to the full therapeutic dose, with observation periods tailored to the suspected reaction pattern. For immediate reaction evaluation, serial doses with observation over several hours after the final dose are common. For delayed reactions, prolonged observation or ambulatory follow‑up for days is required; patch tests or delayed intradermal tests may be attempted before DPT for T cell–mediated patterns.

Stopping rules must be predefined: any objective signs of reaction (urticaria, respiratory symptoms, hypotension, gastrointestinal symptoms, significant erythema or mucosal involvement) warrant immediate cessation and treatment. Access to epinephrine and trained staff is mandatory. After a negative DPT, patients should be observed for a standard period (often 2 hours for immediate reactions) and educated about the residual, though low, risk of later reactions.

Decision points
Deciding whether to perform a DPT balances the pretest probability of allergy, severity of prior reactions, alternative therapy risks, and facility capability. In low‑risk histories (e.g., isolated non‑urticarial maculopapular rash in childhood, unclear timing), many centers favor DPT without prior skin testing to reduce unnecessary labeling. In high‑risk histories for IgE‑mediated reactions, skin testing and serology may precede DPT to reduce risk. For drugs with high lethality if the allergy is true (e.g., suspected penicillin‑mediated anaphylaxis), specialist oversight and staged approaches are warranted.

# Approach to antibiotic allergy (beta-lactam testing algorithms, cross-reactivity concepts)

Antibiotic allergy, and specifically penicillin allergy, is one of the most common and clinically consequential labels. The approach combines careful history, targeted skin and serologic testing when available, and selective DPT to remove inaccurate labels and allow use of first‑line agents.

Clinical assessment
Begin with a structured history: the implicated antibiotic, timing of reaction after exposure, symptom type (urticaria, angioedema, respiratory compromise, anaphylaxis, maculopapular rash), timing of the original event relative to present (memory fades and IgE wanes), number of exposures since the reaction, and presence of comorbidities (e.g., severe asthma). Classify the reaction as immediate (within 1–6 hours) or delayed. Many patients labeled “penicillin‑allergic” report distant mild rashes in childhood; these often do not represent persistent IgE‑mediated allergy.

Penicillin testing algorithm
For patients with histories suggesting immediate hypersensitivity to penicillins or recent reactions, perform penicillin skin testing where available using major (benzylpenicilloyl) and minor determinant reagents plus testing with the implicated penicillin (e.g., amoxicillin). A positive skin test, or positive specific IgE to penicillin, suggests avoidance of penicillins and related aminopenicillins. A negative skin test substantially lowers the probability of immediate allergy; many centers then perform an oral challenge (single‑dose or graded) to confirm tolerance. For patients with low‑risk histories (remote, non‑urticarial rash), some protocols proceed directly to oral challenge without prior skin testing.

Cross‑reactivity concepts
Understanding chemical structure explains cross‑reactivity patterns. Historically, high rates of cross‑reactivity between penicillins and cephalosporins were reported, but modern data show much lower risk, largely related to shared side‑chain (R group) structures rather than the beta‑lactam core itself. Cross‑reactivity risk between penicillins and later‑generation cephalosporins is small (<1%) when side chains differ; risk is higher when side chains are identical or similar (e.g., ampicillin/cephalexin, amoxicillin/cefadroxil share similar aminobenzyl side chains). Carbapenems carry low cross‑reactivity with penicillins (historical estimates up to 10% have been revised down to <1%–3% in many studies), and aztreonam has very low cross‑reactivity except when sharing side‑chain similarity with a specific cephalosporin (e.g., aztreonam and ceftazidime share an identical side chain).

Testing alternatives
If penicillin skin testing is negative but a cephalosporin is needed, consider the following: for low‑risk patients, administration of the cephalosporin may proceed without skin testing or with a single‑dose challenge. For high‑risk patients or when side chain similarity raises concern, perform cephalosporin skin testing if validated reagents exist, or do a graded challenge under supervision. If severe initial reaction occurred (anaphylaxis or SCAR), avoid related agents and refer to allergy specialist for testing considerations and alternatives.

Delabeling strategies
Because most patients labeled penicillin‑allergic are not truly allergic, structured algorithms for delabeling often use risk stratification: low‑risk histories proceed to direct oral challenge; intermediate risk undergo skin testing followed by challenge if negative; high‑risk patients are referred. Many institutions have implemented stewardship programs incorporating penicillin skin testing and DPT to reduce inappropriate use of alternative broad‑spectrum antibiotics.

Practical considerations
Availability of penicillin determinants and expertise varies. When determinants are unavailable, testing with the native penicillins (amoxicillin or benzylpenicillin) may be used with caveats. Documentation of testing results and clear communication to patients and primary clinicians are key: after a negative test and successful challenge, the allergy label should be removed from electronic records and the patient given a written statement of tolerance.

# When to defer testing and referral pathways to allergy specialists

Not every suspected drug hypersensitivity requires immediate testing, and there are clear situations where testing should be deferred or patients referred to an allergy specialist. Decisions should prioritize patient safety and diagnostic yield.

When to defer testing
Defer skin testing or DPT if the patient is within the acute phase of the reaction and unstable, if they have active severe cutaneous adverse reactions (SJS/TEN, DRESS, AGEP), or if they have significant comorbidities that would increase risk (cardiovascular instability, uncontrolled asthma). For immediate hypersensitivity, allow a washout period after the acute event before skin testing in some instances because IgE levels may be temporarily depleted (although waiting too long may reduce sensitivity as IgE wanes); typical practice is to perform testing at least 4–6 weeks after recovery from the acute reaction but within a timeframe where testing remains informative (often within months to a few years). For DPT, defer until the reaction has resolved and the patient is clinically well; for severe prior reactions, DPT is usually contraindicated.

Referral criteria
Refer to an allergy specialist when the history suggests high risk (anaphylaxis, SJS/TEN, DRESS), when testing reagents or expertise is not available locally, when the drug is essential and alternatives are limited, or when interpretation of tests is uncertain. Specialists are necessary for complex evaluations—e.g., suspected multiple drug hypersensitivities, reactions involving multiple organ systems, need for desensitization protocols, or when genetic testing (HLA typing) and interpretation is required.

Allergy specialist capabilities
Allergists/immunologists provide comprehensive assessment including detailed history, targeted skin and in vitro testing (including BAT, LTT if available), supervised DPTs, and desensitization when needed for patients who require a drug to which they are sensitized (e.g., for chemotherapy, transplant prophylaxis, or life‑threatening infections). Specialists can coordinate genetic screening (e.g., HLA‑B*57:01 before abacavir) and advise on cross‑reactivity and alternative prescribing. They also manage complex cases of chronic urticaria or multiple drug reactions where nonimmediate mechanisms are suspected.

System pathways and documentation
Establish local referral pathways so that primary teams know when and how to refer. For common scenarios such as inpatient penicillin delabeling, many centers have rapid access clinics or consult services to expedite testing and DPT. Clear documentation of test results, rationale for deferral, and follow‑up plans is essential. When testing is deferred, provide patients and clinicians with interim management advice and an action plan in case of inadvertent re‑exposure.

Conclusion

Accurate diagnosis of drug hypersensitivity requires integration of pathophysiology, timing and phenotype of reactions, and the strengths and limitations of available diagnostic tools. Immediate, IgE‑mediated reactions are best evaluated with history, skin testing, specific IgE, and—when safe—drug challenge. Delayed, T cell–mediated reactions demand careful risk stratification, use of patch testing and delayed intradermal readings in selected situations, and avoidance of provocation when severe cutaneous or systemic reactions have occurred. Penicillin allergy algorithms focusing on risk stratification, skin testing, and targeted challenges can safely delabel many patients, improving antibiotic stewardship. Drug provocation testing is the diagnostic gold standard but carries risk and must be used judiciously in appropriate settings with resuscitation capabilities. When testing is contraindicated or complex, timely referral to an allergy specialist is necessary. Clear documentation and communication ensure that testing results change future prescribing and improve patient safety.

Introduction

Systemic diseases often present with multisystem signs and symptoms that require coordinated diagnostic imaging and organ-specific tests to determine extent of involvement, identify complications, and exclude alternative causes. In disorders associated with mast cell activation and proliferation, such as mast cell activation syndrome (MCAS) and systemic mastocytosis, the clinical picture can be protean: cutaneous flushing, anaphylaxis, gastrointestinal symptoms, hepatosplenomegaly, cytopenias, and bone pain. Imaging and targeted organ assessment guide diagnostic classification, risk stratification, and management decisions. They also help exclude mimics such as infectious, metabolic, infiltrative, neoplastic, and autoimmune conditions. This chapter reviews the practical role and interpretation of focused imaging and organ-specific investigations in the evaluation of suspected systemic disease with emphasis on findings relevant to mast cell disorders, while highlighting general principles that apply across systemic illnesses.

# Ultrasound assessment (hepatomegaly, splenomegaly) and its role in systemic disease evaluation

Ultrasound is a first-line, noninvasive imaging modality for bedside assessment of abdominal organs. Its portability, lack of ionizing radiation, cost-effectiveness, and ability to provide dynamic real-time information make it well suited for initial evaluation of suspected hepatomegaly or splenomegaly. In systemic diseases associated with mast cell proliferation, enlarged liver or spleen may reflect organ infiltration, congestion, portal hypertension, or reactive hyperplasia. Sonographic detection of organomegaly can trigger further cross-sectional imaging or directed biopsy and informs staging criteria in disorders such as systemic mastocytosis.

Hepatomegaly on ultrasound is assessed by organ span and contour. Normal adult liver span in the midclavicular line ranges approximately from 13 to 15.5 cm, though values vary by body habitus and technique; values above this range raise suspicion for hepatomegaly. Ultrasound also assesses hepatic echotexture: increased echogenicity suggests steatosis, heterogeneous echotexture may indicate fibrosis, infiltration, or focal lesions, and nodularity raises concern for cirrhosis. Doppler evaluation can assess portal and hepatic venous flow to detect portal hypertension or vascular complications. In mast cell disorders, focal hepatic lesions are less common than diffuse infiltration, but an enlarged, heterogeneous liver with imaging or clinical signs of portal hypertension requires evaluation for portal flow abnormalities, chronic liver disease, or infiltrative processes.

Splenomegaly is measured by maximal craniocaudal length. A normal adult spleen is typically ≤12–13 cm in length; splenomegaly is generally defined as length >13 cm, though mild enlargement may be reported at somewhat lower thresholds depending on population. Ultrasound characterizes spleen size, focal lesions, and the presence of infarcts or capsular irregularity. Diffuse splenomegaly suggests systemic processes such as hemolysis, portal hypertension, infection, hematologic malignancy, or infiltration. In mast cell disease, splenomegaly can reflect infiltration by mast cells or reactive hyperplasia; significant splenomegaly with cytopenias suggests hypersplenism and may influence management.

Ultrasound also identifies ancillary findings relevant to systemic disease: ascites, biliary dilatation, renal size and echotexture, lymphadenopathy in accessible regions, and vascular thrombosis. In the work-up of a patient with suspected systemic mast cell disorder, ultrasound may be the initial investigation to confirm organomegaly and guide the need for more detailed cross-sectional imaging or biopsy. However, ultrasound is operator dependent and limited in obese patients or for deep structures; it cannot provide the same soft tissue characterization, vascular mapping, or bone marrow assessment as CT, MRI, or specialized nuclear imaging.

# Cross-sectional imaging (CT, MRI) indications for organomegaly or complications

Cross-sectional imaging with computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) offers higher-resolution anatomical detail and broader field of view compared with ultrasound, enabling comprehensive assessment of organ enlargement, focal lesions, vascular complications, and nodal or bone involvement. Indications for CT or MRI in suspected systemic disease include confirmation and quantification of organomegaly detected on ultrasound, characterization of suspicious focal lesions, evaluation of complications (for example, portal vein thrombosis, biliary obstruction, or focal hepatic infarction), staging of malignancy or infiltrative processes, and guidance for percutaneous biopsy.

CT is widely available and rapid, providing excellent spatial resolution for the liver, spleen, lymph nodes, lungs, and bones. Contrast-enhanced CT is valuable for detecting focal lesions, vascular patency, and abnormal enhancement patterns. In systemic mast cell disease, CT may reveal hepatosplenomegaly, lymphadenopathy, and osseous lesions. Typical osseous findings include osteosclerotic or osteolytic lesions and diffuse bone marrow changes; CT is sensitive for cortical bone changes and pathological fractures. CT pulmonary angiography is indicated if pulmonary embolism is suspected, which can be a complication of systemic inflammatory and prothrombotic states.

MRI offers superior soft tissue contrast and multiplanar capabilities without ionizing radiation. It is particularly useful for liver characterization (using sequences such as T1, T2, diffusion-weighted imaging, and hepatobiliary contrast agents), bone marrow evaluation, and cardiac imaging when CT is inadequate or contraindicated. MRI of the liver can distinguish fatty infiltration from fibrosis, identify focal nodules, and assess biliary anatomy. Whole-body MRI can detect bone marrow infiltration and is increasingly used in hematologic and infiltrative conditions to map disease extent. In mast cell disease, MRI may detect marrow-replacing lesions, focal bone lesions, and degrees of marrow involvement that are occult on CT.

Selecting CT versus MRI depends on the clinical question, patient factors (renal function, allergies, hemodynamic stability), and need for temporal speed. For suspected acute complications—such as suspected intra-abdominal hemorrhage, bowel ischemia, or pulmonary complications—contrast-enhanced CT is often preferred for its speed and availability. For characterization of indeterminate lesions, assessment of marrow, or when ionizing radiation is a concern (young patients, need for serial imaging), MRI is preferred.

Interpretation of organomegaly on cross-sectional imaging should be integrated with laboratory data and clinical context. For the liver and spleen, objective volumetric measurements or comparison to normative size charts improve reproducibility. Detection of focal hepatic lesions should prompt consideration of differential diagnoses including benign cysts, hemangiomas, adenomas, metastatic disease, granulomatous disease, and focal mast cell infiltrates (rare); contrast enhancement patterns and MRI sequences help differentiate these entities. Vascular assessment is critical: portal vein thrombosis or hepatic vein outflow obstruction changes management and prognosis. Similarly, identification of splenic infarcts, subcapsular hematoma, or splenic rupture in the setting of systemic disease has immediate clinical implications.

# Cardiac investigations in anaphylaxis (ECG changes, troponin considerations, myocardial dysfunction)

Anaphylaxis can produce acute cardiovascular complications ranging from vasodilation and distributive shock to arrhythmias, myocardial ischemia, and nonischemic myocardial dysfunction. Cardiac evaluation is essential in severe or atypical presentations and in patients with persistent chest pain, hemodynamic instability, or abnormal biomarkers. The principal investigations include 12-lead electrocardiography (ECG), measurement of cardiac biomarkers such as troponin, and echocardiography when myocardial dysfunction is suspected.

ECG during or after anaphylaxis may demonstrate changes attributable to myocardial ischemia, arrhythmia, or stress-related cardiomyopathy. Ischemic ECG changes can include ST-segment depression or elevation, T-wave inversion, or new Q waves. Arrhythmias observed include supraventricular tachycardia, atrial fibrillation, and ventricular arrhythmias. In anaphylaxis, myocardial ischemia can result from hypotension with supply-demand mismatch, coronary vasospasm mediated by vasoactive mediators (histamine, leukotrienes, prostaglandins), or plaque rupture precipitated by adrenergic surges. ECG interpretation must therefore be correlated with symptoms, hemodynamics, and biomarker trends.

Cardiac troponins are sensitive markers of myocardial injury. Contemporary assays report values with high precision; typical reference ranges for high-sensitivity troponin I or T have assay-specific 99th percentile cutoffs (for example, high-sensitivity troponin T 99th percentile approximately 14 ng/L in many assays), and values above this threshold suggest myocardial injury. In anaphylaxis, troponin may be transiently elevated due to demand ischemia, direct mediator-induced myocardial injury, or type 1 myocardial infarction. Serial troponin measurements at presentation and 3–6 hours later help distinguish acute myocardial injury. Interpretation requires integration with ECG and echocardiography. Modest troponin rises without ischemic ECG changes and with rapid resolution of symptoms may reflect reversible injury; larger rises with persistent ECG changes or wall motion abnormalities indicate myocardial infarction or myocarditis-like injury and warrant cardiology input.

Echocardiography is a key modality to assess myocardial function, wall motion, pericardial effusion, and dynamic obstruction. Point-of-care or transthoracic echocardiography can be performed at the bedside in unstable patients to evaluate for global hypokinesis, regional wall motion abnormalities, right ventricular strain (suggesting pulmonary embolism), or decreased ejection fraction consistent with stress cardiomyopathy. Cases of Kounis syndrome describe allergic acute coronary syndromes in which mast cell mediator release triggers coronary vasospasm, plaque erosion, or thrombosis; echocardiography in Kounis syndrome may show regional wall motion abnormalities corresponding to ischemia. In severe anaphylaxis, fluid resuscitation and vasopressors may confound cardiac assessment; thus, repeated imaging and biomarker measurements are often necessary.

Clinical decision points include obtaining an ECG on presentation in all patients with moderate to severe anaphylaxis, measuring troponin if chest pain, ECG changes, hypotension, or prolonged symptoms are present, and performing echocardiography when there is hemodynamic instability or suspicion of myocardial dysfunction. Positive findings should prompt cardiology consultation, consideration of coronary angiography if type 1 myocardial infarction is suspected, and close hemodynamic monitoring. Recognition that troponin elevation in anaphylaxis can be transient and noncoronary is important, but one must not dismiss significant troponin rises without evaluation for acute coronary pathology.

# Pulmonary imaging for airway/aspiration complications

Pulmonary complications in systemic diseases and in the context of anaphylaxis include bronchospasm, laryngeal edema, aspiration pneumonitis, pulmonary edema, and less commonly pulmonary embolism. Imaging helps assess airway patency, detect aspiration, evaluate for pulmonary infiltrates, and identify complications that change management. The choice of modality—chest radiograph, computed tomography, or airway-focused imaging—depends on clinical presentation and timing.

For acute airway compromise in anaphylaxis, clinical assessment and direct visualization (laryngoscopy) supersede radiographic imaging because management is time-sensitive. Nevertheless, chest radiography (posterior-anterior and lateral views) is commonly used after initial stabilization to evaluate for pulmonary edema, aspirated material, or other acute changes. Findings of aspiration on radiography are often subtle early and more readily apparent after several hours; typical patterns include patchy airspace consolidation in dependent areas—posterior upper lobes when supine or superior segments of lower lobes when upright.

Computed tomography of the chest provides greater sensitivity and specificity for parenchymal abnormalities, small airways disease, and detection of aspiration-related complications such as lobar consolidation, necrotizing pneumonia, or abscess formation. CT is indicated for persistent or worsening respiratory symptoms despite initial therapy, suspicion for complications such as pulmonary embolism (CT pulmonary angiography), or to evaluate for alternative diagnoses like interstitial lung disease or malignancy. In the context of mast cell diseases, CT may demonstrate airway wall thickening consistent with bronchial hyperreactivity, or multifocal consolidations suggesting aspiration or infectious processes.

Bronchoscopy is useful when there is concern for significant aspiration with retained material, unexplained hypoxemia, suspected airway injury, or when sampling for microbiology is required. In the acute setting, bronchoscopic removal of foreign or aspirated material can be therapeutic. For evaluation of suspected laryngeal edema, flexible nasendoscopy provides direct assessment and guides airway management.

Pulmonary imaging must be interpreted in the context of the timing of symptoms. Aspiration pneumonitis may not be radiographically evident immediately after the event; chest radiographs often become abnormal within 6–12 hours. In contrast, noncardiogenic pulmonary edema from increased vascular permeability can produce bilateral interstitial and alveolar infiltrates relatively rapidly. Decision points include obtaining a chest radiograph in any patient with respiratory symptoms after anaphylaxis or suspected aspiration, escalating to chest CT or bronchoscopy when there is clinical deterioration, persistent hypoxemia, or suspicion of complications. Recognition that imaging findings may lag behind clinical events is crucial to avoid false reassurance from an initially normal radiograph.

# Use of bone marrow biopsy and interpretation alongside imaging findings

Bone marrow biopsy is a cornerstone diagnostic test when systemic hematologic, infiltrative, or proliferative disorders are suspected. In mast cell disorders, bone marrow examination is mandatory for diagnosing systemic mastocytosis, as it allows morphological assessment, immunophenotyping, and molecular testing. Indications for bone marrow biopsy include unexplained cytopenias or cytoses, suspected hematologic malignancy, persistent organomegaly with hematologic abnormalities, and when imaging suggests marrow involvement.

A typical bone marrow procedure includes aspiration and trephine biopsy. Aspirate provides cellular detail and allows flow cytometry, cytogenetics, and molecular studies. Trephine biopsy preserves marrow architecture and is essential to detect focal aggregates of abnormal cells that can be missed on aspirate. In systemic mastocytosis, diagnostic criteria incorporate bone marrow findings: multifocal dense infiltrates of mast cells in bone marrow or extracutaneous organs, and confirmation by mast cell morphology and immunophenotype, together with relevant molecular markers such as KIT D816V mutation and elevated serum tryptase. Flow cytometry may demonstrate aberrant mast cell expression of CD25 and/or CD2 in addition to CD117. Standard laboratory references state a baseline serum tryptase >20 ng/mL often supports systemic disease but is not definitive alone.

Bone marrow interpretation must be integrated with imaging. Cross-sectional and nuclear imaging may raise suspicion for marrow involvement when bone lesions are present or when whole-body MRI shows marrow signal abnormalities. Imaging that reveals osteosclerotic or osteolytic lesions should prompt concordant targeted marrow evaluation and sometimes image-guided biopsy of focal lesions. Conversely, normal imaging does not exclude marrow disease: early or patchy marrow involvement may be occult on imaging but detected histologically. Histopathology should report mast cell burden, architecture, presence of fibrosis, and additional hematologic neoplastic components.

Molecular and cytogenetic studies performed on marrow aspirate refine diagnosis and prognosis. Detection of the KIT D816V mutation is common in systemic mastocytosis and informs targeted therapy options. Additional mutations (for example, in SRSF2, ASXL1, or RUNX1) are associated with advanced disease and worse prognosis. Bone marrow cellularity and peripheral cytopenias guide assessment for marrow failure or hypersplenism. When marrow biopsy reveals concurrent clonal myeloid neoplasms, this defines an advanced variant of systemic mastocytosis with distinct management and prognostic implications.

Clinical decision points include obtaining bone marrow biopsy when peripheral blood counts, organomegaly, and elevated baseline tryptase suggest systemic disease; performing both aspirate and trephine biopsy; ordering flow cytometry for immunophenotyping, molecular testing for KIT and other myeloid mutations, and cytogenetics where indicated; and integrating marrow results with imaging and laboratory data to establish disease classification and plan management. Communication between clinicians, radiologists, and pathologists is critical to ensure focal imaging findings are correlated with biopsy sites and histopathologic interpretation.

# Limitations and timelines for imaging in the acute vs elective setting

Appropriate timing of imaging is essential and must balance urgency, diagnostic yield, patient stability, and safety. In the acute setting—such as severe anaphylaxis, hemodynamic instability, acute respiratory failure, or suspected acute intra-abdominal catastrophe—rapid bedside and emergent imaging modalities are prioritized. Point-of-care ultrasound and portable chest radiography provide immediate, actionable information. Contrast-enhanced CT is often the modality of choice for acute abdominal pain, suspected hemorrhage, vascular complications, or pulmonary embolism if the patient is stable enough for transfer and contrast administration. MRI is usually less practical emergently due to longer acquisition times, limited availability, and contraindications such as unstable patients or those with implanted devices.

Imaging limitations in the acute phase include reduced sensitivity for certain pathologies immediately post-insult. For example, ultrasound and chest radiography may initially be normal after aspiration or early infection; CT may detect early changes but can still miss microscopic disease. Similarly, bone marrow involvement or hepatic fibrosis may not be appreciable on imaging performed too early in the disease course. Contrast administration in acutely ill patients requires caution: renal function should be considered to avoid contrast-induced nephropathy (creatinine and estimated glomerular filtration rate; eGFR <30 mL/min/1.73 m^2 increases risk), and in hypotensive patients, delayed renal clearance alters contrast safety profiles.

In elective settings, imaging can be optimized for diagnostic yield. Elective ultrasound can be performed by experienced sonographers with repeat views and Doppler assessment; CT and MRI protocols can be tailored with appropriate intravenous and oral contrast, multiphase liver protocols, and targeted sequences for marrow or cardiac evaluation. Whole-body imaging strategies—such as whole-body MRI or PET-CT—may be appropriate when mapping systemic disease burden is necessary for staging or treatment planning. However, elective imaging should consider cumulative radiation exposure, particularly for young patients or those requiring serial assessments; MRI or ultrasound may be preferred to reduce radiation.

Timelines for follow-up imaging should be individualized. In acute complications, repeat imaging is indicated when clinical status changes or to document resolution; for example, chest radiograph or CT may be repeated 24–72 hours after initial aspiration if symptoms persist or worsen. For organomegaly detected on ultrasound, more comprehensive cross-sectional imaging can be scheduled electively to further characterize lesions and guide biopsy. When monitoring known disease, standardized interval imaging—guided by disease activity, treatment response, and risk of progression—is advised. For instance, in indolent systemic mastocytosis without progressive symptoms, imaging may be performed only if new organ dysfunction arises; in advanced disease, more frequent assessments may be warranted.

Understanding the technical and clinical limitations of each modality helps set expectations and avoid misinterpretation. Operator dependency affects ultrasound; motion artifact and partial volume effects can degrade CT or MRI quality; metallic implants and patient factors (claustrophobia, inability to lie flat) limit MRI. Communication between the referring clinician and imaging specialists is essential to select the right test at the right time, tailor protocols to the clinical question, and plan any required follow-up or biopsy.

Conclusion

Focused imaging and organ-specific tests are integral to the evaluation of suspected systemic disease. Ultrasound provides a rapid initial assessment of hepatomegaly and splenomegaly but must be supplemented by CT or MRI for comprehensive characterization, vascular assessment, and detection of complications. Cardiac investigations in anaphylaxis require timely ECGs, consideration of troponin kinetics, and echocardiography to identify myocardial dysfunction or ischemia. Pulmonary imaging detects aspiration and airway-related complications and guides escalation to CT or bronchoscopy when necessary. Bone marrow biopsy remains diagnostic in hematologic and infiltrative disorders and must be interpreted alongside imaging and molecular studies. Finally, clinicians must balance the urgency and limitations of acute imaging against the enhanced diagnostic yield of elective, protocol-driven studies. Integrating clinical findings, laboratory data, and appropriate imaging in a timely fashion ensures accurate diagnosis, appropriate staging, and effective management planning for patients with systemic disease.

Introduction

Clonal mast cell disorders encompass a spectrum from cutaneous disease to systemic mastocytosis (SM) and mast cell leukemia. Laboratory evaluation integrates marrow histology, cytology, immunophenotyping and molecular testing to demonstrate clonality, define disease category and provide prognostic information that guides management. This chapter provides a practical, clinically oriented review of the techniques and key findings on bone marrow aspirate and trephine biopsy, the immunohistochemical and flow cytometric markers used to identify neoplastic mast cells, the molecular diagnostics centered on KIT mutations and other recurrent genomic alterations, and how these data are synthesized against World Health Organization (WHO) diagnostic criteria. The chapter also addresses reporting standards, interpretation pitfalls when sampling is incomplete, and the implications of laboratory results for treatment selection and referral to specialist centers.

# Bone marrow aspirate and trephine biopsy: technique and key histologic findings

Bone marrow examination in suspected clonal mast cell disease should include both aspirate (cytology) and trephine (core) biopsy with touch preparations. Mast cell neoplasms often produce a “dry tap” or poor aspirate yield because of marrow fibrosis or compact mast cell aggregates; therefore, a trephine biopsy is essential. Proper technique includes obtaining adequate core length (ideally ≥1.5–2 cm) and multiple sections for decalcification and histology. Touch imprints or crush smears from the biopsy provide cytologic detail when the aspirate is limited.

Key histologic features to evaluate on trephine biopsy include the presence, distribution and architecture of mast cell infiltrates. The WHO major criterion for systemic mastocytosis is multifocal, dense aggregates of mast cells (defined as at least 15 mast cells per cluster) in bone marrow or other extracutaneous organs. These aggregates are often perivascular, peri-trabecular or interstitial. Mast cells in indolent SM are frequently round or slightly ovoid with abundant metachromatic granules, whereas in more aggressive variants they may be spindle-shaped, atypical, or show hypogranulation.

On aspirate smears and touch preps, count and morphologic assessment of mast cells is important. A minor diagnostic criterion is the presence of atypical mast cells with an immature or spindle-shaped morphology constituting >25% of mast cells on cytology. Because mast cells can degranulate during aspiration, evaluation of multiple fields and correlation with trephine morphology is necessary. Special stains—such as Giemsa or toluidine blue—highlight metachromatic granules and facilitate mast cell identification. Mast cell tryptase immunostaining further confirms mast cell lineage and can make subtle infiltrates more conspicuous.

Additional marrow features that should be recorded include cellularity, fibrosis (reticulin or trichrome stain), and evidence of associated hematologic neoplasms (e.g., myelodysplasia, chronic myelomonocytic leukemia). Marrow fibrosis is common and may contribute to aspirate failure; grading reticulin fibrosis (e.g., WHO/European consensus scale) provides prognostic information. Documentation of sampling adequacy (core length, number of sections) and ancillary studies performed is necessary for correct interpretation.

# Immunohistochemistry and flow cytometry markers (CD117, CD25, CD2) on mast cells

Immunophenotyping by immunohistochemistry (IHC) and flow cytometry is central to distinguishing neoplastic mast cells from reactive mast cell hyperplasia. The most routinely used markers are CD117 (c-KIT), mast cell tryptase, CD25 and CD2. On IHC, tryptase is a sensitive and specific marker of mast cell lineage; CD117 is also expressed on mast cells, but is not specific because other hematopoietic cells (e.g., progenitors) and some non-hematopoietic cells also express c-KIT.

Aberrant expression of CD25 and/or CD2 on mast cells is regarded as evidence of clonality in adults. In practice, CD25 is more sensitive and specific than CD2 for neoplastic mast cells. Neoplastic mast cells typically show strong membranous and/or cytoplasmic CD25 staining on IHC; CD2 expression is variable. The WHO minor diagnostic criteria include expression of CD2 and/or CD25 on bone marrow mast cells. Some laboratories also assess CD30, which may be expressed in advanced SM and can have diagnostic and potentially therapeutic implications.

Flow cytometry enables high-sensitivity detection (often to 0.01–0.1% depending on panel and cell numbers) of aberrant mast cell immunophenotype when sample cellularity permits. Typical gating strategy identifies high side-scatter, low-to-intermediate CD45 events and uses CD117 and tryptase where available; surface CD117 (c-KIT) is usually bright. The hallmark aberrant pattern is coexpression of CD25 on CD117+ mast cells. CD2 can be tested but is less sensitive. Flow cytometry has the advantage of quantifying the percentage of mast cells among total nucleated cells and detecting small neoplastic populations, but interpretation requires careful gating because basophils and other cells can express overlapping signals. Mast cells may be lost during processing, particularly if the aspirate is hemodilute or if mast cell aggregates are tightly adherent to stroma; therefore, correlation with trephine biopsy results is necessary.

When reporting, laboratories should state the antibodies used, the thresholds for positivity (e.g., any membrane staining above background for IHC; percentage of cells for flow cytometry), and whether expression is considered aberrant relative to normal mast cell controls. Because CD117 is not discriminatory for clonality, the emphasis should be on demonstration of CD25 and/or CD2 on mast cells.

# Molecular testing for KIT mutations and other relevant genomic alterations

The canonical molecular lesion in adult systemic mastocytosis is an activating mutation in KIT, most commonly the D816V substitution in exon 17. Detection of KIT D816V is a WHO minor diagnostic criterion. Testing should be performed on bone marrow as the primary specimen; peripheral blood testing can be used as an initial screen but has lower sensitivity when allele burden is low.

Available methods for detecting KIT D816V include allele-specific PCR (AS-PCR), quantitative allele-specific PCR, droplet digital PCR (ddPCR), and next-generation sequencing (NGS). Sensitivity varies: conventional Sanger sequencing has limited sensitivity (roughly 10–20% mutant allele fraction), making it insufficient to rule out D816V. Targeted AS-PCR and ddPCR can detect mutant allele fractions down to approximately 0.01–0.1% (method-dependent), and are preferred when available. NGS panels typically achieve limits of detection around 1–5% but offer the advantage of profiling multiple genes simultaneously to detect additional somatic mutations.

Testing strategy: if peripheral blood shows a robust D816V allele by sensitive PCR, marrow biopsy can still be performed but molecular confirmation is already present. If peripheral blood is negative by a method whose sensitivity is adequate, a bone marrow specimen should be tested because marrow often has a higher allele burden. Laboratories must report the assay used and its analytic sensitivity and quantitate mutant allele burden where possible (e.g., variant allele fraction or copies per microliter). Serial measurement of allele burden can be used to monitor response to targeted therapy in advanced disease, but interpretation must account for assay variability.

Although D816V in exon 17 dominates adult SM, other KIT mutations (for example in exons 8, 9, 11 or 13) occur, particularly in children or in rare adult cases, and influence treatment choices because some kinase inhibitors (e.g., imatinib) are ineffective against D816V but may be active against other KIT mutants. Broad NGS panels that include KIT exons beyond 17 are therefore useful. In advanced SM, additional somatic mutations in genes implicated in myeloid neoplasms—classically SRSF2, ASXL1, and RUNX1 (the “S/A/R” panel)—are associated with worse prognosis. Comprehensive myeloid NGS panels will detect mutations in genes such as TET2, DNMT3A, NRAS, CBL, EZH2, and others; the presence and number of non-KIT high-risk mutations inform prognosis and may impact eligibility for clinical trials or consideration of allogeneic stem cell transplantation.

When reporting molecular results, specify the technique, the regions analyzed, the limit of detection, and the allele fraction. Clinically relevant decision points include: (1) detection of KIT D816V supports a minor WHO criterion; (2) absence of D816V by a low-sensitivity method does not exclude the mutation—state the assay limitations; (3) detection of alternative KIT mutations or high-risk additional mutations should trigger specialist input regarding targeted therapy options and prognosis.

# Correlation of laboratory and histologic findings with WHO diagnostic criteria

The current WHO diagnostic framework for systemic mastocytosis uses one major and four minor criteria. The major criterion is multifocal dense infiltrates of mast cells (≥15 per aggregate) in extracutaneous organs, typically bone marrow. The four minor criteria are: (1) >25% of mast cells in marrow aspirate smears are spindle-shaped or atypical, or of these cells are atypical in biopsy sections; (2) detection of KIT point mutation at codon 816 in marrow or another extracutaneous organ; (3) mast cells in marrow or blood expressing CD2 and/or CD25 in addition to normal mast cell markers; (4) serum total tryptase persistently >20 ng/mL (unless there is an associated clonal myeloid disorder that elevates tryptase independently).

Diagnosis of systemic mastocytosis requires either the major criterion plus one minor criterion, or at least three minor criteria. Therefore, concordant findings across morphology, immunophenotype and molecular testing are often necessary to establish the diagnosis. For example, a patient with dense mast cell aggregates on trephine plus aberrant CD25 expression or a confirmed KIT D816V fulfills the WHO criteria. In cases where the marrow biopsy shows only an increase in scattered mast cells without clusters, but molecular testing reveals KIT D816V and flow cytometry shows CD25 expression, three minor criteria may still be met.

In addition to establishing SM, laboratory data contribute to subclassification (indolent SM, smoldering SM, SM with an associated hematologic neoplasm, aggressive SM, mast cell leukemia). Criteria for these categories incorporate clinical signs of organ damage (so-called “C” findings), blood counts, percentage of atypical cells, and marrow blast count. Histologic features such as diffuse marrow replacement, high mast cell burden, and accompanying dysplasia should prompt consideration of more aggressive categories or associated hematologic neoplasm.

Tryptase measurement is part of the diagnostic algorithm; normal range for serum total tryptase in most laboratories is approximately 0–11 ng/mL, but the WHO suggests a threshold of >20 ng/mL as a minor criterion. Be aware that tryptase rises acutely with anaphylaxis and that chronic elevations can occur in clonal myeloid disease independent of mastocytosis; thus, results must be interpreted in clinical context.

# Reporting standards and interpretation in the context of incomplete sampling

Standardized reporting ensures that clinicians can integrate laboratory data into diagnostic and management pathways. Reports should clearly describe specimen source(s), adequacy (e.g., core length, cellularity), stains performed, and key morphologic findings: presence/absence of mast cell aggregates, percentage of atypical mast cells (if quantified), and fibrosis grade. Immunohistochemical and flow cytometric results must describe markers tested, pattern and distribution of staining, and whether expression is considered aberrant. Molecular reports must state genes/exons analyzed, method, analytic sensitivity, detected variants with nomenclature, and allele fraction.

Incomplete sampling is a common challenge in mast cell disease: dry taps are frequent and small or fragmented cores can make it hard to identify focal aggregates. In such contexts, reports should explicitly state limitations (e.g., “aspirate inadequate for cytology; trephine core 8 mm, fragmented”) and avoid overcalling absence of disease. A negative KIT result on peripheral blood or a low-sensitivity assay must be reported with the assay’s limit of detection and a recommendation to test marrow if clinical suspicion remains high. Similarly, absence of aberrant CD25 by flow cytometry in a hemodilute aspirate or in the setting of very low mast cell numbers is not definitive. When mast cell aggregates are focal, immunohistochemical stains (tryptase, CD117) may accentuate them: absence of visible aggregates on routine H&E does not exclude multifocal disease.

Reports should also integrate data against WHO criteria: explicitly state whether major and/or minor criteria are fulfilled and which findings support this conclusion. If the data are insufficient to confirm or exclude systemic mastocytosis, recommend specific next steps (e.g., repeat biopsy, bone marrow molecular testing by a more sensitive assay, referral to a mast cell center). Where additional high-risk myeloid mutations are detected, include commentary on prognostic implications, while noting that final risk stratification requires clinical correlation.

# Implications for treatment choices and specialist referral

Laboratory findings directly influence treatment selection and the need for specialist referral. Detection of KIT D816V not only fulfills a minor diagnostic criterion but also determines likely responsiveness to tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs). Imatinib has activity against some KIT mutants but is ineffective against D816V; therefore, confirmation of D816V usually precludes imatinib as a targeted therapy. Newer agents—midostaurin and avapritinib—have demonstrated efficacy in advanced SM with D816V and are approved in many jurisdictions; molecular status guides eligibility and expected benefit. Quantitative allele burden may be used to monitor response to these agents in advanced disease, but clinical endpoints remain paramount.

The presence of additional high-risk mutations (for example, SRSF2, ASXL1, RUNX1) confers a poorer prognosis and can prompt discussion of more aggressive approaches, including consideration of allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation in eligible patients. Laboratory identification of an associated hematologic neoplasm (e.g., chronic myelomonocytic leukemia or other myeloid neoplasm) changes the diagnostic category to SM with an associated hematologic neoplasm and alters treatment priority towards management of the myeloid disorder.

For patients whose laboratory evaluation is equivocal (e.g., inadequate samples, borderline tryptase, discordant molecular and immunophenotypic results), referral to a center experienced in mast cell disorders is recommended. Expert centers offer comprehensive testing (sensitive quantitative assays for KIT D816V such as ddPCR), integrated clinicopathologic interpretation, and access to clinical trials. They also provide multidisciplinary assessment for symptom management (antihistamines, leukotriene modifiers, mast cell stabilizers), management of anaphylaxis risk, and decisions around targeted therapy or transplantation.

Finally, laboratory results have immediate implications for peri-procedural care. Patients with confirmed or suspected systemic mastocytosis have increased risk of anaphylaxis; thus, documenting the diagnosis in the chart and communicating risks to anesthesia, surgical teams and emergency services is critical.

Conclusion

Accurate diagnosis and management of clonal mast cell disease require coordinated histopathology, immunophenotyping and molecular diagnostics. Adequate bone marrow sampling with both aspirate and trephine is essential, with IHC for tryptase and CD117 and assessment of aberrant CD25/CD2 expression. Molecular testing focused on KIT D816V with appropriately sensitive assays—and broader NGS when indicated—complements morphology and immunophenotype to establish clonality and guide therapy. Standardized reporting that states specimen adequacy, assay sensitivity, and how findings relate to WHO diagnostic criteria allows clinicians to make informed decisions about treatment and specialist referral.

Introduction

Anaphylaxis is a rapid, life-threatening systemic hypersensitivity reaction characterized by airway compromise, circulatory collapse, and multisystem involvement due to the sudden release of mediators (histamine, tryptase, leukotrienes, prostaglandins, platelet-activating factor) from mast cells and basophils. Most episodes respond to intramuscular epinephrine, aggressive intravascular volume resuscitation, and supportive care in the emergency department. However, a subset of patients progress to severe or refractory anaphylaxis that requires intensive care unit (ICU)–level monitoring and interventions, including continuous vasopressor infusions, advanced hemodynamic monitoring, airway management, and prolonged observation. This chapter describes the definitions, pathophysiology, recognition, advanced pharmacologic strategies, hemodynamic goals, monitoring tools, approaches to recurrent or relapsing anaphylaxis, and multidisciplinary coordination necessary for safe and effective ICU management of refractory anaphylaxis.

# Definition and recognition of refractory anaphylaxis requiring ICU-level care

Refractory anaphylaxis lacks a universally accepted single numeric definition, but it is commonly defined clinically as persistent or progressive cardiovascular collapse, respiratory compromise, or multisystem organ dysfunction despite initial standard therapy. Standard therapy typically comprises immediate intramuscular epinephrine (0.3–0.5 mg for adults IM into the mid-anterolateral thigh), high-flow oxygen, aggressive crystalloid boluses, airway support as needed, and adjunctive therapies (antihistamines, corticosteroids). Anaphylaxis becomes “severe” when there is shock (hypotension or poor perfusion), severe bronchospasm causing hypoxemia or respiratory failure, or rapidly progressive airway edema. It becomes “refractory” when hypotension and/or hypoxemia persist or worsen after repeated intramuscular or bolus intravenous epinephrine doses and adequate fluid resuscitation, necessitating continuous intravenous catecholamine infusion or other ICU-level interventions.

Recognizing refractory anaphylaxis early is essential. Key clinical features raising concern include hypotension that fails to respond to one or more boluses of epinephrine and 30–50 mL/kg of crystalloids (or continued need for epinephrine boluses), progressive tachycardia with poor peripheral perfusion, delayed capillary refill, cool mottled extremities, rising serum lactate and metabolic acidosis, ongoing or worsening bronchospasm despite inhaled bronchodilators and systemic epinephrine, stridor or rapidly progressive upper airway edema, altered mental status from poor cerebral perfusion, and recurrent biphasic reactions after initial stabilization. Other concerning features include arrhythmias, myocardial ischemia, or lactic acidosis (e.g., lactate >2 mmol/L suggesting significant tissue hypoperfusion). Patients exhibiting these patterns require escalation to ICU-level care for continuous infusions, invasive monitoring, controlled airway management (often fiberoptic or video laryngoscopic intubation with skilled airway team), and consideration of extracorporeal support when conventional therapies fail.

# Indications for continuous intravenous epinephrine infusion: initiation, titration and monitoring

Indications

Continuous intravenous (IV) epinephrine infusion is indicated when hemodynamic instability persists despite initial resuscitation with IM/IV epinephrine boluses and fluid resuscitation, or when repeated bolus dosing is impractical because of ongoing shock or recurrent life‑threatening symptoms. Specific clinical scenarios include persistent systolic blood pressure <90 mmHg (or mean arterial pressure (MAP) <65 mmHg) despite two or more epinephrine boluses and 30 mL/kg crystalloid, signs of end-organ hypoperfusion (oliguria: urine output <0.5 mL/kg/h, rising lactate), severe bronchospasm with hypoxemia requiring ventilator support, or recurrent severe reactions where ongoing mediator release or continued exposure is suspected.

Initiation

Prior to starting infusion, secure vascular access appropriate for vasopressor administration: a large-bore peripheral IV may be used for short-term infusions, but a central venous catheter is preferred in ongoing high-dose vasopressor requirements to reduce the risk of extravasation injury. Establish continuous cardiac monitoring, frequent noninvasive or invasive blood pressure monitoring (arterial line if vasopressor requirement is expected to be substantial or prolonged), pulse oximetry, and urine output measurement. Ensure adequate crystalloid resuscitation has begun.

Initial infusion dosing is individualized. Common adult starting regimens include 0.02–0.05 mcg/kg/min (approx. 1–5 mcg/min for an average adult) with rapid titration upward in 0.02–0.1 mcg/kg/min increments (or 1–5 mcg/min increments) every 2–5 minutes guided by response. Many centers use a pragmatic rate range of 0.05–0.5 mcg/kg/min, and some patients require up to 1 mcg/kg/min in extreme cases. For clarity, approximate fixed-dose adult infusion ranges are frequently expressed as 2–10 mcg/min (low dose) to 10–20 mcg/min (moderate) and higher for severe refractory shock. Pediatric dosing is weight-based and typically begins at 0.01–0.1 mcg/kg/min with similar titration principles.

Titration and goals

Titrate to clinically important end points: restoration of adequate MAP (target MAP ≥65 mmHg unless higher individualized target is required for known chronic hypertension), restoration of tissue perfusion (improving mental status, urine output >0.5 mL/kg/h, decreasing lactate), resolution of severe bronchospasm or upper-airway edema, and weaning of supplemental oxygen/ventilator support when appropriate. Perform frequent assessments (every few minutes during titration) and adjust infusion according to hemodynamics and side effects.

Monitoring and safety

Continuous telemetry is required to detect arrhythmias induced by catecholamines. Serial 12‑lead ECGs and cardiac enzyme testing should be considered if ischemia is suspected. Monitor for tachyarrhythmias, myocardial ischemia, severe hypertension, and splanchnic or peripheral ischemia. Regularly reassess arterial blood gases, lactate, electrolytes (potassium, magnesium), and glucose (epinephrine can induce hyperglycemia). Avoid abrupt cessation of infusion—taper slowly as hemodynamics stabilize to prevent rebound hypotension.

Extravasation risk is important with peripheral infusions. Use a central venous catheter when infusion rates exceed safe peripheral thresholds (institution-specific, but often >5–10 mcg/min) or when the expected duration is prolonged. If extravasation occurs, local infiltration with phentolamine and warm compresses may mitigate tissue injury.

# Second-line vasopressors and adjunctive drugs (norepinephrine, vasopressin, glucagon) in special populations

Rationale

Epinephrine remains the primary vasoactive agent for anaphylaxis because of its combined alpha- and beta-adrenergic effects (vasoconstriction to raise systemic vascular resistance and bronchodilation and inotropy to improve cardiac output). However, in refractory cases or when epinephrine is constrained by comorbidities (e.g., beta-blocker therapy) or side effects (tachyarrhythmia, myocardial ischemia), adjunctive vasopressors or second-line drugs are necessary.

Norepinephrine

Norepinephrine is a potent vasoconstrictor with strong alpha-1 activity and modest beta-1 inotropy. It is favored when distributive shock predominates and when pure vasoconstriction is required to maintain perfusion pressure. In severe anaphylaxis, where profound vasodilation and increased vascular permeability reduce preload and systemic vascular resistance, norepinephrine can restore MAP with less chronotropic effect than epinephrine. Typical adult dosing begins at 0.01–0.1 mcg/kg/min (approx. 2–20 mcg/min) and titrates to effect; however, doses may be higher in refractory shock. Norepinephrine is commonly used alone or combined with epinephrine, with close attention to heart rate and signs of ischemia.

Vasopressin

Vasopressin acts on V1 receptors to induce vasoconstriction via a mechanism independent of adrenergic receptors and is useful as an adjunct when catecholamine resistance develops or when large catecholamine doses are needed. It is particularly considered in patients on chronic ACE inhibitors or with profound vasoplegia. Common practice is either a low-dose fixed infusion (e.g., 0.03 units/min) as an adjunct or higher doses tailored to response, although higher doses increase the risk of splanchnic and digital ischemia. Vasopressin does not have direct bronchodilator effects, so it is not a substitute for epinephrine when bronchospasm is prominent. Potential adverse effects include ischemia, hyponatremia when prolonged, and cardiac ischemia.

Glucagon

Glucagon is the key adjunct in patients taking beta-adrenergic blockers or calcium channel blockers in whom typical adrenergic agents may have reduced efficacy. Glucagon increases intracellular cyclic AMP through G-protein–coupled receptors independent of beta-adrenergic pathways, producing inotropic and chronotropic effects and increasing systemic vascular resistance. Glucagon dosing commonly begins with an IV bolus of 50–150 mcg/kg (max 5 mg) given slowly over 1–2 minutes, followed by an infusion of 1–5 mg/hour titrated to hemodynamic response. Antiemetic therapy should accompany glucagon use because nausea and vomiting are frequent. Hypokalemia and hyperglycemia are potential metabolic effects. Efficacy is variable and usually temporizing while catecholamine therapy and other supportive measures continue.

Other adjuncts

Methylene blue has been reported in refractory vasoplegic states due to its inhibition of nitric oxide–mediated vasodilation, and some centers have used it in refractory anaphylactic vasoplegia, but evidence is limited and risks (serotonin syndrome in patients on SSRIs, hemolysis in G6PD deficiency) exist. Inhaled bronchodilators, nebulized epinephrine, and ketamine infusion may be adjuncts for severe bronchospasm. Intravenous antihistamines (H1 and H2) and systemic corticosteroids (e.g., methylprednisolone 1–2 mg/kg) are commonly given; steroids may not affect the acute phase but are used to reduce protracted or biphasic reactions.

Choice and sequencing

In practice, epinephrine infusion is the initial vasopressor in refractory anaphylaxis because of its combined alpha/beta activity and effects on bronchospasm. If the patient remains hypotensive despite escalating epinephrine or develops intolerable side effects, norepinephrine and vasopressin are appropriate adjuncts. In beta-blocked patients, glucagon should be given early if epinephrine response is blunted. All additions should be guided by hemodynamic targets and organ perfusion markers, with attention to side-effect profiles and interactions.

# Hemodynamic monitoring (invasive and non-invasive) and goals of resuscitation

Goals of resuscitation

The primary goals are restoration and maintenance of adequate perfusion to vital organs (brain, heart, kidneys) and resolution of hypoxemia. Numeric targets commonly used in ICU resuscitation include MAP ≥65 mmHg (higher targets may be used for chronic hypertension), urine output ≥0.5 mL/kg/h, decreasing serum lactate toward normal (<2 mmol/L) and normalization of mental status and skin perfusion. Oxygenation goals include SpO2 ≥92% (or individualized based on comorbidities) and PaO2 as clinically appropriate.

Non-invasive monitoring

Continuous monitoring of heart rate, rhythm, SpO2, noninvasive blood pressure (NIBP), respiratory rate, and mental status are the minimal requirements. Point-of-care testing including bedside lactate, arterial blood gases, and repeated measurements of hemoglobin and electrolytes are useful. Bedside transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) is highly valuable for rapid assessment of cardiac function (left and right ventricular function), intravascular volume status (inferior vena cava variability, though limited in mechanically ventilated patients), and pericardial effusion. Lung ultrasound can assess pulmonary interstitial edema and pleural fluid. These techniques aid in distinguishing hypovolemia, cardiogenic shock, obstructive shock, and distributive shock.

Invasive monitoring

When vasopressor support is anticipated or when hemodynamics are unstable, invasive monitoring becomes crucial. An arterial catheter allows beat-to-beat blood pressure monitoring and accurate blood sampling. Central venous access enables administration of vasopressors and measurement of central venous pressure (CVP) and central venous oxygen saturation (ScvO2). Although absolute CVP values are poor predictors of volume responsiveness, trends may help. ScvO2 (normal ~70–75%) gives insight into adequacy of oxygen delivery vs consumption; low ScvO2 suggests inadequate perfusion or cardiac output.

Advanced hemodynamic monitoring modalities include pulse contour analysis (e.g., PiCCO), esophageal Doppler, bioimpedance, and pulmonary artery catheterization. Pulmonary artery catheter (Swan-Ganz) can measure pulmonary artery pressures, mixed venous oxygen saturation, cardiac output, and cardiac filling pressures, which is informative in complex cases with potential myocardial involvement, severe pulmonary hypertension, or when differentiating cardiogenic shock from distributive shock is ambiguous. In refractory anaphylactic shock, where high-dose vasopressors or inotropes are required or when myocardial depression is suspected, these tools can guide fluid vs inotropic therapy and vasopressor selection.

Dynamic measures of preload responsiveness (passive leg raise, stroke volume variation in mechanically ventilated patients) should be used rather than static measures. Monitor urine output hourly and repeat serum lactate and arterial blood gases every 1–4 hours depending on acuity.

Integration for decision-making

A structured approach integrates clinical exam, serial lactates, urine output, bedside TTE, arterial line pressures, and response to fluid challenges or vasopressor adjustments. For example, a patient with low MAP, elevated lactate, poor urine output, low ScvO2, preserved left ventricular ejection fraction on TTE, and a collapsible IVC likely benefits from further fluid resuscitation. Conversely, depressed ejection fraction and elevated filling pressures suggest inotropic support rather than more fluids. Clear goals for weaning vasopressors should be established and pursued as perfusion normalizes.

# Management of multiple relapses: strategies, risks and benefits of preemptive infusion vs watchful waiting

Biphasic and protracted anaphylaxis

Anaphylaxis can relapse after initial resolution, manifesting as a biphasic reaction (return of symptoms hours after apparent recovery) or protracted anaphylaxis (symptoms that persist for many hours to days). Biphasic reactions occur in an estimated 1–20% of cases and usually present within 1–8 hours but can occur up to 72 hours later. Risk factors for biphasic or protracted reactions include delayed or insufficient initial epinephrine, severe initial reaction, need for multiple epinephrine doses, and peanut or tree nut allergy in some series. The unpredictability of timing and severity makes disposition decisions complex.

Watchful waiting approach

Shorter observation periods (4–6 hours) may be appropriate for patients with mild reactions that resolved with a single dose of epinephrine and lack risk factors. “Watchful waiting” in the ED or step-down setting includes serial assessments of vital signs, symptoms, and readiness for discharge with observation lasting based on local protocols and patient risk profile. Watchful waiting avoids unnecessary ICU admissions and vasopressor exposure but carries risk of failing to recognize a biphasic or delayed relapse, especially in high-risk patients.

Preemptive infusion approach

For patients with severe initial anaphylaxis, ongoing mediator release, or repeated relapses, some clinicians elect to start a preemptive low-dose continuous epinephrine (or other vasopressor) infusion after apparent stabilization to reduce the risk of relapse and to provide a safety net. Potential benefits include:

- Prevention of recurrent hypotension and bronchospasm by maintaining alpha- and beta-adrenergic support while mediators clear.
- Reduced need for repeated bolus dosing with its attendant hemodynamic swings and risk of underdosing.
- Controlled weaning under continuous monitoring.

Risks and downsides include:

- Catecholamine-induced adverse effects: tachyarrhythmias, myocardial ischemia, hyperglycemia, lactic acidosis, peripheral and splanchnic ischemia.
- Need for ICU-level monitoring and central access, increasing resource use and potential procedural complications.
- Potential for masking evolving clinical deterioration if infusions blunt warning signs.

Decision framework

A thoughtful individualized approach is needed. Indications favoring preemptive infusion include:

- Requirement for multiple epinephrine boluses to achieve stabilization.
- Ongoing or recurrent hypotension or bronchospasm despite recent therapy.
- Presence of risk factors for protracted or biphasic reaction (severe initial reaction, delayed presentation, ingestion of drugs that prolong mediator activity, such as certain opioids or ACE inhibitors).
- Concomitant beta-blocker therapy where epinephrine responsiveness may be blunted.

If preemptive infusion is used, select the lowest effective dose and define explicit weaning criteria based on stable MAP, urine output, lactate clearance, and absence of symptoms for a defined period (often 6–24 hours). Frequent reassessment and an attempt to wean as soon as clinically safe limit exposure. In all cases, ensure clear documentation and communication when choosing a preemptive strategy.

# Multidisciplinary care coordination (allergy, hematology, ICU) and escalation policies

Team-based care

Refractory anaphylaxis requires multidisciplinary coordination before, during, and after the ICU stay. The core team typically includes critical care physicians and nurses, allergy/immunology specialists, respiratory therapists, pharmacy, and—with specific indications—cardiology, hematology, and surgery or interventional teams for extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) or other invasive support. Clear roles, consistent communication, and use of checklists or protocols improve safety and outcomes.

Allergy/immunology

Allergy specialists provide expertise in identifying likely triggers, planning diagnostic testing (serum tryptase levels, specific IgE testing, skin testing when safe), and advising on prevention and long-term management. Baseline serum tryptase (and, if possible, an acute tryptase within 1–3 hours of reaction onset) helps confirm mast-cell–mediated anaphylaxis. Allergy teams advise on risk stratification for biphasic reactions and suitability for discharge versus prolonged observation. They also counsel on avoidance strategies and emergency plans (e.g., prescription of epinephrine auto-injectors, education on when and how to use them).

Hematology

In selected cases, hematology consultation is warranted: unusual presentations may reflect systemic mastocytosis or clonal mast-cell disorders that predispose to severe or recurrent episodes. Elevated baseline tryptase (>11.4 ng/mL in many labs) may prompt evaluation for mast cell activation syndrome or systemic mastocytosis; hematology helps determine the need for bone marrow biopsy or genetic testing (e.g., KIT D816V mutation) and long-term management.

Critical care and escalation policies

The ICU team must establish escalation policies and thresholds for additional interventions. Policies should define:

- Criteria for ICU admission from the ED (e.g., refractory hypotension, need for continuous vasopressors, ventilatory failure, recurrent severe reactions).
- Standardized orders for continuous infusions, monitoring frequency, and laboratory surveillance (e.g., lactate every 2–4 hours, hourly urine output).
- Protocols for airway management and involvement of anesthesia/ENT and ECMO teams when airway edema or severe cardiopulmonary collapse is anticipated.
- Guidelines for weaning vasopressors and criteria for transfer out of ICU.
- Procedures for obtaining and handling serum tryptase (timing of acute and baseline specimens) and for safe scheduling of skin testing once the patient is stabilized.

Pharmacy and medication safety

Pharmacy involvement is essential to ensure correct preparation and dosing of concentrated vasoactive medications, to prevent medication errors, and to provide guidance on interactions (for example, interactions of beta-blockers, MAO inhibitors, or tricyclic antidepressants with vasoactive agents). Standardized infusion concentrations and infusion pump programming, double checks for high-risk drugs, and protocols for extravasation management should be in place.

Documentation, patient education, and follow-up

Care transitions should include documentation of suspected triggers, acute tryptase values if obtained, doses of interventions used, and recommendations for follow-up. Before discharge from the ICU or hospital, patients should receive counseling on avoidance strategies, prescriptions for epinephrine auto-injectors, and urgent outpatient follow-up with allergy/immunology. For recurrent events or unexplained severe anaphylaxis, a coordinated outpatient plan with allergy and possibly hematology is crucial.

Conclusion

Management of refractory and severe anaphylaxis in the ICU requires early recognition of treatment failure, timely initiation and careful titration of continuous epinephrine infusions, judicious use of second-line vasopressors and adjunctive agents, and targeted hemodynamic monitoring to guide therapy. Decisions about preemptive versus watchful approaches to relapsing anaphylaxis require individualized risk assessment and clear criteria for escalation and de-escalation. Multidisciplinary collaboration among critical care, allergy/immunology, hematology, pharmacy, and procedural teams, combined with institution-specific protocols, optimizes patient safety and outcomes in these high-risk, dynamic clinical situations.

Introduction

Acute presentations with hypotension, flushing, and urticaria raise immediate concern for anaphylaxis, a potentially life‑threatening, IgE‑mediated or non‑IgE mast cell–driven reaction that requires rapid recognition and treatment. However, a variety of non‑allergic and alternative allergic mimics can produce overlapping features, and accurate differentiation in the acute setting influences immediate management (e.g., need for epinephrine, antibiotics, source control, volume resuscitation, or tumor‑directed therapy) and subsequent diagnostic testing. This chapter reviews the major conditions that can mimic anaphylaxis—septic and other distributive shock states, vasovagal syncope and orthostatic hypotension, vasoactive neuroendocrine tumor syndromes (including carcinoid syndrome and pheochromocytoma/paraganglioma), mast cell activation disorders versus chronic urticaria and dermatologic mimics, and drug‑induced non‑IgE mediated reactions. For each entity we emphasize pathophysiology, key historical and examination clues, appropriate immediate investigations (with normal reference ranges where relevant), and clinical decision points that help distinguish these disorders from anaphylaxis in the acute setting.

# Septic and distributive shock: distinguishing features (infectious source, inflammatory markers, fever)

Pathophysiology and clinical overlap
Distributive shock is characterized by a primary decrease in systemic vascular resistance due to vasodilation and endothelial dysfunction, often accompanied by relative intravascular hypovolemia and maldistribution of blood flow. The prototypical example is septic shock, a result of a dysregulated host response to infection with widespread inflammation, vasoplegia, increased vascular permeability, and myocardial depression in some patients. Because distributive shock produces vasodilation and occasionally cutaneous flushing, hypotension and skin findings can resemble anaphylaxis. Both conditions may show tachycardia and signs of end‑organ hypoperfusion. However, the underlying triggers and adjunctive features differ and guide management: sepsis requires source control and antimicrobials, while anaphylaxis requires immediate epinephrine.

Clinical features and history
Key historical clues favoring septic/distributive shock include evidence of infection or a high risk for infection (recent invasive procedures, indwelling catheters, immunosuppression, documented focal infectious symptoms such as cough, dysuria, abdominal pain, or skin/soft tissue infection). Fever or hypothermia (<36.0°C) is common in sepsis; temperature abnormalities are part of sepsis criteria. The onset may be more insidious than the immediate seconds‑to‑minutes onset typical of IgE‑mediated anaphylaxis after allergen exposure, but rapid fulminant sepsis is possible.

Examination commonly reveals warm, well‑perfused extremities early in distributive shock with bounding pulses and wide pulse pressure due to vasodilation. In contrast, anaphylaxis often produces urticaria, angioedema, and bronchospasm. Signs such as productive cough with purulent sputum, focal consolidation on lung exam, localized erythema and fluctuance, or peritoneal signs point toward an infectious source. Mucosal edema and stridor are more characteristic of anaphylaxis.

Laboratory and bedside investigations
Initial labs useful to distinguish sepsis include complete blood count, metabolic panel, lactate, blood cultures, urinalysis and culture, and site‑directed cultures or imaging. Serum lactate is a practical marker of tissue hypoperfusion and severity; elevated lactate (>2 mmol/L) suggests hypoperfusion and is incorporated into sepsis guidelines. Normal lactate is roughly 0.5–2.2 mmol/L, though laboratory ranges vary. Inflammatory markers such as C‑reactive protein (CRP) and procalcitonin can support the diagnosis of bacterial infection—procalcitonin elevations (commonly >0.5 ng/mL suggest bacterial sepsis) have greater specificity than CRP but are not definitive on their own. White blood cell count may be high, normal, or low; neutrophilia with left shift is common. Blood cultures should be obtained before antibiotics when possible.

Hemodynamics and monitoring
Septic shock may require aggressive fluid resuscitation, vasopressors (norepinephrine is first‑line), and identification/treatment of the source. Invasive hemodynamic monitoring is sometimes required in complex cases. The presence of warm, flushed skin with hypotension and a clear infectious nidus should direct early empiric antimicrobial therapy. Empiric doses and timing follow institutional sepsis protocols (commonly, broad‑spectrum antibiotics within 1 hour for septic shock). In contrast, while epinephrine is the immediate life‑saving therapy in anaphylaxis, its routine use in sepsis—unless there is refractory hypotension—is not supported; norepinephrine is preferred for septic vasoplegia.

Decision points for the acute clinician
- If there is a known allergen exposure within minutes and rapid development of urticaria, angioedema, bronchospasm, or hypotension, treat as anaphylaxis with intramuscular epinephrine immediately and reassess.  
- If fever, leukocytosis or leukopenia, elevated lactate, suspicion for infection, or an indwelling device/procedure is present and the time course is more gradual, prioritize sepsis evaluation and treatment (blood cultures, source control, antibiotics, fluids, vasopressors).  
- Mixed presentations can occur (e.g., septic patients with mast cell activation), so reassessment after initial resuscitation and targeted investigations are essential.

# Vasovagal syncope and orthostatic hypotension

Pathophysiology and clinical presentation
Vasovagal syncope (neurocardiogenic syncope) is mediated by an autonomic reflex resulting in sudden vagally mediated bradycardia and/or sympathetic withdrawal causing peripheral vasodilation, leading to transient cerebral hypoperfusion and syncope. Typical triggers include emotional distress, pain, prolonged standing, heat exposure, or phlebotomy. Orthostatic hypotension arises from failure to maintain blood pressure upon standing due to impaired autonomic reflexes, hypovolemia, medications, or volume shifts. Both conditions can produce transient hypotension and flushing due to peripheral vasodilation, and premonitory symptoms such as lightheadedness.

Distinguishing features from anaphylaxis
The key differences are timing, associated symptoms, and presence or absence of allergic signs. Vasovagal syncope is commonly preceded by prodromal symptoms—nausea, diaphoresis, pallor, visual changes, a sense of warmth, and lightheadedness—followed by brief loss of consciousness with rapid recovery. In vasovagal events, skin is often pale and diaphoretic rather than pruritic or urticarial. Orthostatic hypotension causes presyncope or syncope on standing; the hypotension is position‑dependent and improves on recumbency. Anaphylaxis characteristically includes mucocutaneous signs such as urticaria, pruritus, angioedema, wheezing, or stridor and usually follows allergen exposure with rapid onset.

History and targeted physical exam
A careful history of triggers (standing, emotional stress, pain, micturition, medication changes such as antihypertensives or diuretics) is central. Ask about recent vasodilatory agents (nitrates, alpha‑blockers), antihypertensive therapy, antidepressants, or anticholinergics. Physical exam should assess orthostatic vitals: measure blood pressure and heart rate supine, immediately on standing, and at 3 minutes. Orthostatic hypotension is defined as a fall in systolic blood pressure of at least 20 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure of at least 10 mmHg within 3 minutes of standing. Conversely, reflex syncope may be accompanied by transient bradycardia.

Investigations and acute management considerations
For patients with suspected vasovagal syncope and a clear prodrome, immediate management is supportive: place supine, elevate legs, and allow recovery. For orthostatic hypotension, lay the patient flat and address precipitating causes (fluid resuscitation if volume depleted, stop offending medications). Continuous cardiac monitoring is prudent if arrhythmia is suspected. Distinguishing from anaphylaxis is important because epinephrine may be unnecessary in vasovagal events and could produce tachyarrhythmias; however, if history is unclear and anaphylaxis remains possible, treatment with intramuscular epinephrine is appropriate given its life‑saving role when needed.

Clinical pearls
- Skin pallor and diaphoresis argue for vasovagal syncope; urticaria, pruritus, and respiratory compromise argue for anaphylaxis.  
- Orthostatic vitals are a quick bedside test with defined thresholds (ΔSBP ≥ 20 mmHg or ΔDBP ≥ 10 mmHg within 3 minutes).  
- Consider ECG and troponin if cardiac syncope or myocardial ischemia is a concern.

# Carcinoid syndrome and vasoactive tumor syndromes (clinical clues, biochemical testing)

Pathophysiology and clinical manifestations
Carcinoid syndrome results from systemic release of serotonin and other vasoactive mediators (kinins, tachykinins, prostaglandins) by metastatic neuroendocrine tumors (NETs), most commonly small intestinal carcinoids that have bypassed hepatic metabolism via liver metastases. These mediators produce episodic flushing, diarrhea, bronchospasm, and, over time, right‑sided valvular heart disease. Because symptoms can include profound flushing, hypotension, and wheeze, carcinoid syndrome can mimic anaphylaxis; however, the typical course, triggers, and associated features differ.

Clinical clues distinguishing carcinoid syndrome
Flushing in carcinoid syndrome often has characteristic features: it may be prolonged (minutes to hours), episodic, and associated with diarrhea and abdominal cramping. Flushing is frequently triggered by alcohol, emotional stress, certain foods (cheeses, bananas, chocolate), or medications (e.g., histamine‑releasing drugs). The flushing may be erythematous rather than urticarial and without pruritus; mucosal edema and urticaria are not typical. Bronchospasm may occur but is usually not associated with angioedema.

Biochemical testing and typical values
The most useful initial test when carcinoid syndrome is suspected is measurement of 24‑hour urinary 5‑hydroxyindoleacetic acid (5‑HIAA), the primary metabolite of serotonin. Normal urinary 5‑HIAA is approximately 2–8 mg/24 hours (laboratory ranges vary). Elevated 5‑HIAA supports the diagnosis but can be falsely elevated by serotonin‑rich foods (bananas, walnuts, pineapple), medications (acetaminophen, guaifenesin), and drugs that alter serotonin metabolism; patients should avoid these foods/drugs for several days before testing when feasible. Plasma chromogranin A is another marker of neuroendocrine tumors but is less specific (elevated in proton pump inhibitor use, renal insufficiency). For localized tumor detection, cross‑sectional imaging with CT or MRI and functional imaging (68Ga‑DOTATATE PET/CT or somatostatin receptor scintigraphy) are used.

Acute and chronic management considerations
In the acute setting, symptomatic management includes supportive fluid resuscitation and, for bronchospasm, bronchodilators. Octreotide, a somatostatin analogue, is effective acutely to reduce mediator release and control flushing and diarrhea; subcutaneous or intravenous formulations are used emergently. Awareness is critical during procedures (e.g., tumor manipulation, anesthesia) because tumors can release large mediator surges causing carcinoid crisis—prophylactic perioperative octreotide is often administered.

Decision points and pitfalls
- Consider carcinoid syndrome when flushing is episodic, prolonged, associated with diarrhea or right‑sided heart disease, and not accompanied by urticaria or angioedema.  
- In the acute unstable patient, treat life‑threatening features (airway, breathing, circulation) and consider octreotide if carcinoid crisis is suspected.  
- For diagnostic confirmation, obtain 24‑hour urinary 5‑HIAA (with dietary restrictions) and perform imaging once the patient is stabilized.

# Pheochromocytoma/paraganglioma presentations with flushing and hemodynamic instability

Pathophysiology and clinical spectrum
Pheochromocytomas (adrenal medullary catecholamine–secreting tumors) and paragangliomas (extra‑adrenal chromaffin tumors) produce episodic or sustained release of catecholamines (norepinephrine, epinephrine, dopamine) leading to hypertension, tachycardia, pallor or flushing, diaphoresis, and headache. Unlike carcinoid tumors, these tumors classically cause paroxysmal hypertension, but they can also present with hypotension or cardiovascular collapse due to catecholamine‑induced cardiomyopathy, arrhythmias, or sudden vasodilation after catecholamine surge and receptor desensitization. Because flushing and hemodynamic instability can mimic anaphylaxis, it is crucial to recognize the clinical pattern and biochemical testing.

Clinical clues and distinguishing features
The classic triad—episodic headache, sweating, and palpitations—combined with labile blood pressure suggests catecholamine excess. Flushing in pheochromocytoma is often accompanied by severe hypertension rather than the vasodilatory hypotension of anaphylaxis; however, some patients manifest episodic hypotension or shock, especially if catecholaminergic myocardial dysfunction or arrhythmias develop. Triggers include surgery, anesthesia, certain drugs (e.g., beta‑blockers given without prior alpha blockade, metoclopramide, opioids), or physical stress.

Biochemical testing and reference ranges
Diagnostic testing measures catecholamine metabolites: plasma free metanephrines (normetanephrine and metanephrine) or 24‑hour urinary fractionated metanephrines and catecholamines. Plasma free metanephrines have high sensitivity. Typical reference ranges depend on the assay, but elevated plasma normetanephrine or metanephrine beyond the laboratory upper limit suggests catecholamine excess. For 24‑hour urine, normal values are generally: total metanephrines < 1.2 mg/24 hr and normetanephrine < 0.9 mg/24 hr (laboratory variability applies). Interpretation must consider medications (tricyclic antidepressants, acetaminophen, labetalol) and physiologic stress, which can raise levels. Imaging with CT/MRI of the abdomen/pelvis and functional imaging (123I‑MIBG or 68Ga‑DOTATATE PET/CT) is used for localization after biochemical confirmation.

Acute management and perioperative considerations
In an acute catecholamine crisis, management centers on cardiovascular stabilization: control blood pressure and heart rate carefully, avoid precipitants, and manage arrhythmias and acute heart failure. Alpha‑adrenergic blockade (e.g., phenoxybenzamine or short‑acting agents like phentolamine) is necessary before beta‑blockade to prevent unopposed alpha stimulation. In emergencies with severe hypertension, short‑acting vasodilators and alpha blockers are used; if hypotension occurs due to catecholamine depletion or cardiogenic shock, vasopressors and inotropes may be required under careful monitoring. In suspected pheochromocytoma, avoid medications that provoke catecholamine release or interfere with catecholamine metabolism without specialist input.

Decision points and red flags
- Consider pheochromocytoma/paraganglioma in patients with paroxysmal hypertension, headache, palpitations, diaphoresis, or labile blood pressure; when such a patient presents with flushing and hemodynamic instability, include catecholamine excess in the differential.  
- Obtain biochemical testing (plasma free metanephrines or 24‑hour urinary metanephrines) after initial stabilization; interpret results in context of medications and stress.  
- Avoid empiric beta‑blockade in a hypertensive patient without prior alpha blockade if pheochromocytoma is possible.

# Acute mast cell activation vs chronic urticaria and other dermatologic conditions

Pathophysiology of mast cell activation
Mast cells are tissue resident immune cells that release preformed mediators (histamine, tryptase, heparin), newly synthesized mediators (leukotrienes, prostaglandins), and cytokines when activated. Acute mast cell activation underlies anaphylaxis and other mast cell activation syndromes. Acute systemic mast cell activation presents with urticaria (wheals), angioedema, flushing, pruritus, bronchospasm, gastrointestinal cramping, and hypotension. Chronic urticaria, including chronic spontaneous urticaria (CSU), involves recurrent pruritic wheals lasting for months to years and is typically not accompanied by hypotension or systemic anaphylactic features.

Clinical differentiation
Urticaria in acute systemic mast cell activation is often sudden in onset, pruritic, transient (individual lesions last <24 hours), and may be associated with systemic symptoms such as hypotension, respiratory compromise, or gastrointestinal symptoms. Angioedema involves deeper dermal/subcutaneous tissues and affects eyelids, lips, or tongue. In contrast, chronic urticaria produces recurrent wheals persisting for >6 weeks and is usually not life‑threatening; systemic signs such as hypotension are absent. Dermographism (urticaria induced by stroking the skin) may be present in chronic urticaria or physical urticarias.

Laboratory testing and interpretation
Serum tryptase is a helpful test in acute mast cell activation. Baseline tryptase is typically <11.4 ng/mL in adults (lab ranges vary). In anaphylaxis, an acute rise in tryptase compared with baseline supports mast cell degranulation. The practical diagnostic criterion often used is an increase in tryptase that exceeds 1.2 × baseline + 2 ng/mL (i.e., acute tryptase > 1.2 × baseline tryptase + 2 ng/mL). Because baseline tryptase is usually not known, an elevated level drawn during the reaction and a convalescent sample drawn at least 24–48 hours later allow comparison. Note that tryptase peaks approximately 1–2 hours after symptom onset and returns to baseline by 12–24 hours in many cases; thus, timeliness of sampling matters. Complement levels (C4) may help differentiate hereditary or acquired angioedema due to C1 inhibitor deficiency versus mast cell‑mediated angioedema—C4 is low in C1 inhibitor deficiency. Histamine levels rise quickly and fall fast; they are not practical in most clinical settings.

Dermatologic mimics
Several non‑mast cell dermatologic conditions can simulate urticaria or flushing. Erythroderma, viral exanthems, fixed drug eruptions, and vasculitic rashes may have different lesion morphology: vasculitis often produces palpable purpura and lesions that persist >24 hours with central petechiae; fixed drug eruptions recur at same location and often leave post‑inflammatory hyperpigmentation. Flushing without pruritic wheals suggests vascular causes or neuroendocrine syndromes rather than mast cell activation. Careful inspection of lesion characteristics (duration of individual lesions, blanching wheals vs petechial/purpuric lesions, presence of mucosal lesions) helps distinguish these entities.

Chronic urticaria management and when to suspect systemic disease
Chronic spontaneous urticaria typically fluctuates over weeks to months; it can have significant quality‑of‑life impact but rarely causes hypotension. Investigations in chronic urticaria focus on identifying triggers (autoimmune etiologies, thyroid disease, infections) but routine extensive allergy testing is often low yield. When systemic symptoms such as hypotension, bronchospasm, or gastrointestinal compromise are present, consider systemic mast cell activation or anaphylaxis and pursue emergent treatment and diagnostic tests (e.g., serial tryptase).

Decision points for acute care
- In a patient with urticaria plus hypotension, bronchospasm, or angioedema affecting the airway, treat for anaphylaxis immediately (intramuscular epinephrine) and obtain serum tryptase as soon as feasible.  
- If lesions are chronic, pruritic without systemic features, and history is compatible with chronic spontaneous urticaria, acute anaphylaxis is less likely.  
- For angioedema without urticaria, evaluate for bradykinin‑mediated causes (C1 inhibitor deficiency or ACE‑inhibitor induced) since these will not respond to antihistamines or epinephrine.

# Drug‑induced reactions unrelated to IgE (anaphylactoid/non‑IgE mediated) and their recognition

Mechanisms and terminology
Non‑IgE mediated reactions, historically termed “anaphylactoid,” result from direct mast cell or basophil activation, complement activation, or other mediator release without prior sensitization and IgE crosslinking. They can be clinically indistinguishable from IgE‑mediated anaphylaxis, featuring hypotension, urticaria, flushing, bronchospasm, and angioedema. Common culprits include radiographic contrast media, certain antibiotics (e.g., vancomycin causing “red man syndrome”), opioids, neuromuscular blocking agents, and plasma expanders (e.g., dextran).

Clinical recognition and distinguishing features
Timing is often immediate or within minutes of drug exposure. A key clue favoring non‑IgE mediated reaction is occurrence on the first exposure to a drug or with continuous infusion where symptoms are rate‑dependent (e.g., vancomycin infusion causing flushing and hypotension that resolves by slowing infusion). There may be absence of prior allergy history. Some drug reactions are dose or infusion rate dependent: for example, rapid vancomycin infusion leads to histamine release causing erythema and pruritus of the upper body and hypotension (“red man syndrome”), which improves with slowing the infusion and antihistamines rather than IgE testing.

Investigations and acute management
Management in the acute phase is determined by severity and is similar to anaphylaxis (airway management, intramuscular epinephrine for severe systemic reactions). Antihistamines (H1 and H2 blockers) and corticosteroids are commonly used adjuncts. For suspected contrast‑induced reactions, premedication protocols (steroids and antihistamines) reduce but do not eliminate risk. Drug skin testing and specific IgE testing may be available for some agents (e.g., beta‑lactam antibiotics) to distinguish IgE‑mediated allergy from non‑IgE reactions, though skin testing has limitations and must be interpreted with specialist input.

Patterns and examples
- Radiocontrast media can cause immediate flushing, hypotension, bronchospasm via complement activation and direct mast cell activation; premedication with corticosteroids and antihistamines is used in high‑risk patients.  
- Opioids (morphine, meperidine) and vancomycin cause direct mast cell histamine release; vancomycin infusion reactions are infusion‑rate dependent and often respond to slowing the infusion and antihistamines.  
- Physical factors such as temperature, exercise, or pressure can provoke non‑IgE mediated mast cell activation in conditions like cholinergic urticaria or cold urticaria.  
- Complement activation related to hemodialysis membranes, protamine (used in cardiopulmonary bypass), and certain intravenous agents can cause distributive features via anaphylatoxin generation.

Distinguishing IgE vs non‑IgE mechanisms after stabilization
Once the patient is stabilized, allergy consultation can help determine whether skin testing, graded drug challenges, or desensitization are appropriate. For severe reactions where an IgE mechanism is suspected (e.g., penicillin anaphylaxis), skin testing can guide future drug use. For reactions likely to be non‑IgE mediated and infusion‑related, alternative agents, slower infusion rates, premedication, or desensitization protocols may be considered.

Clinical decision points
- Treat life‑threatening reactions with epinephrine regardless of suspected mechanism.  
- If reaction occurred on first exposure or in a dose‑dependent fashion (e.g., rapid infusion), consider non‑IgE mediated mechanisms and adjust future administration accordingly.  
- Document the suspected mechanism in the medical record and arrange specialist allergy testing later when appropriate.

Conclusion

In the acute setting, hypotension, flushing, and urticaria demand rapid assessment and stabilization but also careful differential diagnosis because management strategies diverge. Anaphylaxis requires immediate intramuscular epinephrine, airway protection, and supportive measures. However, septic/distributive shock, vasovagal syncope, orthostatic hypotension, vasoactive tumor syndromes (carcinoid and catecholamine‑secreting tumors), mast cell activation disorders, chronic urticaria, and non‑IgE drug reactions can present with overlapping features. Distinguishing among these conditions rests on a synthesis of timing and triggers, associated symptoms (respiratory compromise, diarrhea, focal infectious signs), skin lesion characteristics (pruritic urticarial wheals versus prolonged erythema or purpura), hemodynamic patterns (paroxysmal hypertension vs vasodilation), and targeted laboratory testing (serum tryptase, lactate, blood cultures, urinary 5‑HIAA, plasma metanephrines). When uncertainty exists and anaphylaxis is plausible, immediate treatment with epinephrine is warranted because delay can be fatal. Subsequent directed investigations and specialist consultations (infectious disease, allergy/immunology, endocrinology, or dermatology) should follow once the patient is stabilized to identify the underlying cause and guide long‑term management.

Introduction

Anaphylaxis is an acute, potentially life-threatening systemic hypersensitivity reaction that requires prompt recognition, immediate treatment, and thoughtful planning for post-event observation and follow-up. From a public health and emergency medicine perspective, the frequency of anaphylaxis and the risk of recurrence after apparent clinical resolution are central determinants of observation policy. Observation policies aim to detect recurrent or biphasic reactions, ensure stabilization of organ systems, permit patient education and delivery of secondary prevention (epinephrine autoinjector, individualized action plan), and determine the need for allergy referral. At the same time, prolonged hospitalization or extended emergency-department (ED) observation increases resource use, costs, and patient burden. This chapter synthesizes population-level epidemiology of anaphylaxis and biphasic reactions, critical factors that influence estimates, and guideline-based, risk-stratified approaches to observation, disposition, and follow-up that clinicians can apply in routine practice.

# Incidence and common triggers of anaphylaxis in different populations

Epidemiology

Reported incidence of anaphylaxis varies widely according to study methodology, case definitions, data sources (ED visits, hospital admissions, population registries), and geographic region. Contemporary population-based estimates typically fall in the range of approximately 30–100 episodes per 100,000 person-years, with many high-quality studies clustering around 40–60 per 100,000 person-years. ED visit rates for anaphylaxis have generally increased over recent decades in numerous countries, although whether this reflects a true increase in incidence versus improved recognition and coding practices is debated.

Age and sex patterns

Age-specific incidence shows bimodal tendencies in many cohorts: young children (commonly food-triggered) and adults in the middle-aged bracket (often drug- or insect-venom–triggered) show peaks. In pediatric populations, food is the predominant trigger; young infants and toddlers frequently present after accidental or undiagnosed exposures. In adults, medications (antibiotics, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, anaesthetic agents) and insect stings become more common contributors. Sex distribution varies by age: food-allergic anaphylaxis is often more common in boys in childhood but tends to be more frequent among adult females in some series.

Common triggers by population and setting

- Foods: The leading cause of anaphylaxis in children and an important cause in young adults. Common culprits are peanuts, tree nuts (walnut, almond, cashew), shellfish, fish, milk, egg, and sesame. Food-induced reactions often occur rapidly after ingestion and frequently present with cutaneous and respiratory symptoms.

- Medications: A major cause in adults and hospitalized patients. Beta-lactam antibiotics, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, radiocontrast media, muscle relaxants and monoclonal antibodies are prominent triggers. Medication reactions may present with predominant cardiovascular collapse or bronchospasm.

- Insect venom: Hymenoptera (bees, wasps, hornets, yellow jackets) produce systemic reactions often seen in adults and associated with severe cardiovascular compromise.

- Latex and environmental exposures: Less common in many populations but important in certain health-care and occupational settings.

- Idiopathic or exercise-induced: A fraction of anaphylaxis cases (idiopathic anaphylaxis) have no identified trigger after evaluation. Exercise-associated anaphylaxis can be cofactor-dependent (often food-dependent).

Contextual and social determinants

Geographic differences in diet, prescribing practices, prevalence of venomous insects, and access to health care shape observed incidence patterns. Socioeconomic factors influence recognition and presentation: differences in access to primary care and allergists affect rates of ED presentation versus outpatient management.

# Reported incidence and timing of biphasic reactions and factors affecting estimates

Definition and clinical significance

A biphasic anaphylactic reaction is recurrence of symptoms after initial clinical resolution without re-exposure to the trigger. Biphasic reactions can range from mild cutaneous symptoms to severe airway or cardiovascular compromise and are the primary rationale for observation after resolution. Understanding their incidence, timing, and risk factors underpins evidence-based observation durations.

Reported incidence and sources of variability

Estimates of biphasic reaction incidence vary markedly across studies, from less than 1% to more than 20%. Many contemporary ED- and hospital-based cohorts report rates in the 2–8% range when using strict clinical criteria; older or heterogeneous datasets report higher figures. Sources of variability include:

- Case definition heterogeneity: Some studies include mild, transient urticaria as biphasic events, while others count only clinically significant recurrence requiring intervention (e.g., epinephrine, IV fluids, or airway support).

- Duration of follow-up: Short observation periods or incomplete post-discharge follow-up underestimate biphasic events that present later. Conversely, studies with long surveillance windows may capture unrelated recurrent symptoms.

- Treatment differences: Use of systemic corticosteroids and timing/doses of epinephrine at initial presentation may modify biphasic risk, complicating comparisons across cohorts with different practice patterns.

- Selection bias: Studies limited to admitted patients may reflect more severe initial reactions and higher biphasic rates.

Timing of biphasic reactions

The time from initial symptom resolution to recurrence is variable. Reported time windows span from 1 hour up to 72 hours, but most clinically relevant reviews indicate that the majority of biphasic reactions occur within 1–8 hours of initial resolution, with a substantial proportion (commonly cited as 60–80%) occurring within 4–10 hours. Severe biphasic reactions are more likely to occur earlier within that window. Rare late recurrences beyond 24–48 hours occur but are uncommon.

Risk factors associated with biphasic reactions

Multiple features correlate with increased biphasic risk, though the predictive power of individual factors is modest:

- Severity of initial reaction: Patients with hypotension, respiratory failure, or need for airway intervention have higher biphasic rates.

- Delayed administration of epinephrine: Longer intervals between symptom onset and first epinephrine dose are linked with higher risk.

- Need for multiple doses of epinephrine or intensive resuscitation: Requiring repeated intramuscular or intravenous epinephrine indicates more severe or refractory physiology and correlates with recurrence.

- Persistent or recurrent symptoms during initial observation period: Ongoing symptoms prior to documented resolution increase probability of relapse.

- Unknown or difficult-to-avoid allergen: Idiopathic anaphylaxis or reactions with unidentified triggers sometimes have higher recurrence.

- Comorbidities: Asthma (particularly poorly controlled), cardiovascular disease, beta-blocker use, and older age are associated with a greater likelihood of severe or recurrent reactions.

- Lack of early corticosteroid therapy: Evidence is inconsistent about steroids preventing biphasic reactions; observational associations exist but are not causative.

Because none of these factors precisely predict biphasic reactions, guidelines emphasize combined clinical judgment and risk stratification rather than rigid rules.

# Risk-stratified observation recommendations from major organizations and rationale

Principles guiding recommendations

Observation recommendations balance three priorities: patient safety (detection of recurrence), efficient use of health resources, and patient-centered care (minimizing unnecessary hospitalization). Guideline bodies use available evidence—predominantly observational studies, not randomized controlled trials—to recommend observation durations keyed to initial severity, response to therapy, comorbidities, and social circumstances.

Representative recommendations (summary of prevailing positions)

- World Allergy Organization (WAO): WAO’s position statements recommend observation tailored to severity and risk factors, commonly suggesting a minimum observation window (often around 4–6 hours) for mild-to-moderate reactions that resolve promptly after treatment. For severe reactions (hypotension, respiratory compromise, need for multiple epinephrine doses), extended observation or admission (often 12–24 hours or longer) is advised.

- Resuscitation Councils and national guidelines (e.g., Resuscitation Council UK, Australasian Resuscitation Council): These organizations commonly endorse a baseline observation period of 6 hours for patients whose symptoms have resolved with a single epinephrine dose and who have no other risk factors. If any of the following are present—severe initial reaction, requirement for multiple epinephrine doses, ongoing symptoms, significant comorbidity (e.g., poorly controlled asthma), beta-blocker therapy, delayed presentation or epinephrine administration—then longer observation (12–24 hours) or inpatient admission is recommended.

- Allergy/immunology societies (e.g., American Academy of Allergy, Asthma & Immunology; European Academy of Allergy and Clinical Immunology): These bodies emphasize early intramuscular epinephrine as best practice, the uncertain preventive role of corticosteroids, and advocate risk-adapted observation with a low threshold for admission in severe cases.

Common rationales across organizations

- The highest risk of clinically significant biphasic reactions occurs within the first several hours after initial resolution; thus, a window of several hours of observation is important.

- The absolute rate of biphasic reactions is low, particularly after mild, rapidly resolving episodes, so extended universal admission is unnecessary and resource-inefficient.

- Patients with high-risk features have a materially increased probability of recurrence and worse outcomes if recurrence occurs; therefore, more prolonged observation is justified.

- Patient education and rapid access to epinephrine on discharge are essential components of a safe outpatient strategy.

# Practical observation periods and criteria for admission vs discharge

Risk stratification approach

Practical application uses a graded risk-assessment to decide observation duration.

Low-risk patients (candidate for short observation and discharge if stable)
- Features: Mild-to-moderate anaphylaxis limited to cutaneous and mild gastrointestinal symptoms or bronchospasm that responds promptly to a single dose of intramuscular epinephrine and supportive care; vital signs stable; no hypotension; no need for repeated epinephrine; comorbid conditions controlled; reliable follow-up and social supports.
- Suggested observation period: commonly 4–6 hours after last dose of epinephrine and after complete symptom resolution. Some guidelines suggest 6–8 hours as a conservative minimum.

Intermediate-risk patients (consider extended observation or short admission)
- Features: More severe initial symptoms but stabilized; need for more than one dose of epinephrine but now stable; transient hypotension corrected; moderate persistent asthma or cardiovascular comorbidity; delayed administration of epinephrine; incomplete access to reliable emergency care or social support.
- Suggested observation period: frequently 12–24 hours, with clinician discretion based on trend of symptoms and comorbidities.

High-risk patients (admit or observe in monitored setting)
- Features: Initial cardiovascular collapse or respiratory failure; persistent hypotension requiring ongoing vasopressor support; recurrent or persistent symptoms despite therapy; need for intravenous epinephrine; ongoing airway compromise or requirement for supplemental oxygen/high-flow support; high-risk comorbidities (unstable cardiac disease, severe asthma, beta-blocker or ACE inhibitor use limiting epinephrine response); uncertain follow-up or inability to access emergency care.
- Suggested disposition: hospital admission in a monitored bed (step-down or ICU as indicated) for at least 24 hours with readiness to treat recurrent events.

Specific clinical criteria applied before discharge

Before discharge, ensure all of the following are met:

1. Complete resolution of signs and symptoms without ongoing need for pharmacologic support (e.g., no need for ongoing bronchodilators, vasopressors, or oxygen beyond baseline).

2. A defined observation interval elapsed after the last dose of epinephrine—commonly 4–6 hours for low-risk cases, extended up to 24 hours for higher-risk cases depending on guidelines and clinical assessment.

3. No recurrent symptoms during the observation period.

4. Vitals stable and within acceptable ranges for age: for adults, systolic blood pressure preferably >90 mmHg and no orthostatic hypotension; for children, systolic blood pressure exceeding age-appropriate thresholds (commonly cited pediatric hypotension cutoff: systolic BP < 70 + 2 × age in years for infants/young children).

5. Ability to tolerate oral intake and ambulate as appropriate; airway symptoms resolved and respiratory status stable on room air or baseline oxygen.

6. Adequate social support and ability to seek and obtain emergency care rapidly if recurrence occurs.

7. Provision of education and prescription(s) (see follow-up section) prior to discharge.

Documented discharge instructions should include explicit warnings about biphasic reactions, signs warranting immediate return (respiratory distress, lightheadedness, recurrent hives with cardiovascular symptoms), and instructions for using an epinephrine autoinjector.

Use of corticosteroids and antihistamines in observation decisions

- Antihistamines (H1 blockers) ameliorate cutaneous and pruritic symptoms but do not prevent anaphylaxis progression; they are adjunctive.

- Systemic corticosteroids are often administered with the intent of reducing biphasic risk, but high-quality evidence demonstrating prevention of biphasic reactions is lacking. Some guidelines recommend steroids as adjunct therapy, especially for patients with severe reactions, but clinicians should not rely on steroids to replace adequate observation periods.

# Strategies to balance patient safety with healthcare resource utilization

Triage and resource stewardship

A tiered, risk-based approach conserves resources while maintaining safety. Key strategies include:

- Standardized risk assessment tools and local protocols: Implementing institution-specific algorithms that map clinical findings to observation duration reduces variability, improves throughput, and ensures high-risk patients receive appropriate monitoring.

- Short-stay observation units: For patients at intermediate or lower risk who need several hours of observation, placement in an ED observation unit rather than an inpatient bed reduces costs and preserves hospital capacity.

- Rapid response to deterioration: Equip observation areas with staff trained in recognizing and managing anaphylaxis recurrence and with ready access to epinephrine and resuscitation equipment.

- Shared decision-making: Engage patients (or caregivers) in discussion of risks and benefits, particularly when evidence is uncertain (e.g., borderline cases). Document the discussion and the rationale for chosen disposition.

- Use of telemedicine and early outpatient follow-up: For selected low-risk patients discharged after adequate observation, arrange early virtual check-ins within 24–48 hours to reassess symptom recurrence and adherence to instructions.

- Education to reduce future ED utilization: Deliver targeted education (recognition of early signs, epinephrine administration, avoidance strategies), which can reduce future emergency encounters and improve quality of life.

Operational considerations

- Ensure rapid access to epinephrine autoinjectors at discharge, or confirm that patients have and can use their existing device.

- Provide written individualized emergency action plans in the patient’s preferred language and at an appropriate literacy level.

- Standardize documentation and coding for anaphylaxis to improve quality metrics and enable institutional data collection for continuous improvement.

Cost-effectiveness considerations

Although some rare biphasic reactions are severe, the low absolute incidence in low-risk patients supports selective observation rather than universal prolonged admission. Analyses generally favor observation periods guided by severity: brief observation for low-risk patients and admission for those at higher risk. Institutions should balance medicolegal risk, local outcome data, and patient preferences when defining their protocols.

# Follow-up pathways: allergy referral, patient education and provision of emergency medications

Allergy/immunology referral and diagnostic testing

Every patient who experienced anaphylaxis should be referred for specialist allergy evaluation unless a clear, unavoidable iatrogenic cause is identified and already being managed. The purposes of referral include identification of the trigger, counseling on avoidance strategies, education regarding emergency treatment, and planning for immunotherapy when appropriate (e.g., venom immunotherapy).

Timing and modality of testing

- Skin testing and specific IgE testing should generally occur after a suitable interval from the acute event (commonly recommended wait of 4–6 weeks) to reduce the risk of false-negative testing due to transient immune suppression post-event. Timing can vary by agent and clinical scenario.

- Drug allergy evaluation often requires an allergist-supervised protocol, which may include skin testing, serum specific IgE where available, and graded drug challenges. For certain agents (e.g., radiocontrast, common antibiotics), there are evidence-based testing pathways.

- Venom allergy evaluation (specific IgE and skin testing) is typically indicated for systemic reactions to stings and guides consideration of venom immunotherapy, which markedly reduces the risk of future systemic reactions.

Patient education and action plans

Education is a cornerstone of post-anaphylaxis care. Effective education covers:

- Recognition of early signs and symptoms of anaphylaxis and biphasic reactions: respiratory difficulty, throat tightness, sudden lightheadedness/syncope, progression of skin symptoms with systemic features.

- Immediate use of intramuscular epinephrine: instruct patients and caregivers that epinephrine is the first-line treatment and should be administered promptly; if symptoms persist or recur after 5–15 minutes, repeat dosing is indicated.

- How and when to seek emergency care after epinephrine administration: call emergency services immediately after administering epinephrine even if symptoms improve.

- Avoidance strategies tailored to the identified or suspected trigger (reading labels, asking about ingredients in restaurants, medication reconciliation).

- An individualized anaphylaxis emergency action plan written and reviewed in the ED and reinforced at follow-up.

Provision and prescribing of emergency medications

- Epinephrine autoinjector dosing and prescriptions: ensure patients leave with an epinephrine autoinjector (or multiple devices where indicated) and instructions on its use. Common autoinjector doses:

  - 0.15 mg for children weighing approximately 15–30 kg.

  - 0.3 mg for children/adults weighing approximately 30–60 kg.

  - 0.5 mg autoinjectors are available in some markets for larger adults; dosing should be individualized.

  Clinicians should adhere to local formulary options and consider device availability when prescribing. Patients requiring multiple autoinjectors (e.g., due to distance from medical care, severe prior reactions, or large body size) should be supplied accordingly.

- Adjunctive medications: Prescribe oral H1 antihistamines and, when appropriate, short courses of systemic corticosteroids for adjunctive control of symptoms (e.g., prednisone 0.5–1 mg/kg for short courses), recognizing that steroids have unproven efficacy in preventing biphasic reactions. Inhaled beta-2 agonist prescriptions are appropriate for patients with reactive airways disease.

Logistics and access

- Ensure patients have prescriptions filled or provided prior to discharge; where possible, provide an autoinjector in the ED if access or affordability would otherwise delay possession.

- Provide training and demonstration of autoinjector use (ideally using a trainer device) and written/visual materials.

- Arrange allergist referral and provide a clear timeline: many centers aim for evaluation within 1–6 weeks depending on local capacity; urgent referral is required for severe or unclear cases.

- Discuss workplace, school, or travel implications with documentation (e.g., medical alert identification, school action plans).

Conclusion

Observation after anaphylaxis must be individualized according to the severity of the index event, response to treatment, comorbidities, and social context. Epidemiologic data indicate that while biphasic reactions can occur, the absolute risk in low-risk patients is modest and frontloading of observation resources for high-risk presentations is reasonable. Clinicians should apply standardized, evidence-informed protocols that specify minimum observation intervals, clear criteria for admission, and mandatory discharge components (education, epinephrine autoinjector prescription, and allergy referral). Combining risk stratification with patient-centered education and timely specialist follow-up achieves the dual aims of patient safety and judicious use of health-care resources.

Introduction

Long-term management and non-acute care after an episode of anaphylaxis are essential to reduce the risk of recurrence, to prepare patients and caregivers to manage future reactions, and to investigate underlying causes such as IgE‑mediated allergy or systemic mast cell disorders. Effective outpatient care combines appropriate prescription and training in epinephrine auto‑injector (EAI) use, clear written action plans, targeted avoidance and medication review, timely specialist referrals, robust documentation and communication with primary care and other stakeholders, and thoughtful attention to ethical and medicolegal responsibilities. This chapter presents practical, evidence‑based guidance for clinicians responsible for follow‑up after anaphylaxis, emphasizing pathophysiologic rationale, clinical decision points, and how to translate laboratory and diagnostic workup into safe, patient‑centered management plans.

# Indications for prescribing epinephrine auto-injectors and choosing appropriate dose/device

Epinephrine is the first‑line treatment for anaphylaxis because its combined alpha and beta adrenergic effects counteract the principal pathophysiological processes: vasodilation and increased vascular permeability (alpha‑mediated vasoconstriction), bronchospasm and mucosal edema (beta‑2 bronchodilation), and myocardial depression and hypotension support (beta‑1 effects). Therefore, patients at risk of anaphylaxis should have ready access to an EAI.

Indications for prescribing an EAI include any of the following scenarios:
- A documented anaphylactic reaction (cutaneous signs plus respiratory compromise, hypotension, or involvement of other organ systems).
- Known IgE‑mediated allergy to an agent (food, venom, medication) with prior systemic reaction of sufficient severity to cause anaphylaxis, or in which future exposure is likely and potentially severe.
- Patients with mast cell activation syndromes or systemic mastocytosis who have had systemic allergic reactions or are at risk of severe reactions (venom allergy is especially high‑risk).
- In selected cases with high‑risk features (e.g., previous severe asthma, cardiovascular disease, inability to avoid the trigger, history of biphasic reactions) even if prior systemic reaction was moderate.

Device selection and dosing are driven by body weight and practical considerations:
- Typical EAI doses commonly available are 0.15 mg, 0.3 mg, and in some countries 0.5 mg. A practical approach is:
  - 0.15 mg device for children weighing approximately 15–30 kg (33–66 lb).
  - 0.3 mg device for those ≥30 kg (≥66 lb).
  - Consider a 0.5 mg device for adults with body mass that may render subcutaneous administration less effective or for those who have responded inadequately to 0.3 mg in the past. Some national guidelines recommend 0.5 mg for adults >50 kg who are obese; local device availability varies.
- Repeat dosing: If symptoms persist or recur, a second EAI dose may be administered after 5–15 minutes (many guidelines suggest 5 minutes as the earliest interval) while arranging emergency medical care. Recurrent or prolonged symptoms may necessitate repeated doses or intravenous epinephrine in hospital.
- Device choice should also account for ease of use, needle length, cost and availability, and patient factors such as dexterity and visual impairment. Demonstrating how different brands click and how to remove safety caps improves user confidence.
- Prescribe at least two EAIs for most patients who need them (one may fail or be inaccessible during a reaction), and advise replacement before expiry and after any use. Check local formulary and insurance constraints that may limit dispensing.

# Patient education and demonstration of auto-injector use; written anaphylaxis action plans

Education is an intervention as important as the prescription itself. Correct and timely epinephrine use reduces morbidity and mortality from anaphylaxis. Education should be practical, repeated, and accompanied by hands‑on demonstration and return demonstration.

Key teaching points and skills to cover:
- Recognizing early signs and symptoms of anaphylaxis: combinations of cutaneous signs (urticaria, angioedema), respiratory symptoms (wheeze, stridor, throat tightness), gastrointestinal symptoms (vomiting, abdominal pain), hypotension or collapse. Teach that respiratory and cardiovascular features can occur without cutaneous signs.
- When to use the EAI: any signs of airway, breathing, or circulatory compromise, or rapidly progressive symptoms after exposure to a known trigger. Teach that in cases of doubt in a patient with known severe allergy, early use is preferred to delay.
- Step-by‑step technique with the chosen device: remove safety cap, place against anterolateral thigh (can be through clothing if necessary), hold for the recommended time (usually 3 seconds for many devices, up to 10 seconds for others—emphasize device‑specific instructions), then massage the site for 10 seconds. Demonstrate on a trainer and require the patient or caregiver to perform a return demonstration.
- Immediate post‑injection actions: call emergency services, lie the patient flat with legs elevated if tolerated (unless vomiting or respiratory distress); if unconscious and breathing is present place in a recovery position. If symptoms persist after 5–15 minutes, a second EAI may be given.
- Storage and maintenance: keep at room temperature, avoid extreme heat or cold, check expiry dates monthly, and store in an accessible place. Explain that lights in the viewing window and discoloration of solution are not reliable indicators of potency—adhere to expiry dates.
- Address barriers: needle phobia, cost, stigma, or unwillingness to carry devices. Offer solutions such as smaller carrying cases, alternatives for cost (patient assistance programs), and practice with trainers to reduce fear.
- Special contexts: school, workplace, travel. Provide written instructions for caregivers, teachers, or occupational health staff, and discuss emergency contacts and anticipatory planning for meals, field trips, or sports.

Written anaphylaxis action plans
A concise, individualized written anaphylaxis action plan reinforces verbal education and aids third parties during an event. Effective plans include:
- Patient identifiers and emergency contact numbers.
- Clear recognition cues for anaphylaxis and explicit instructions for when to use the EAI.
- Stepwise instructions after epinephrine use (call emergency services, positioning, second dose timing).
- Documentation of known triggers, cross‑reactive substances to avoid, and any baseline medications (such as beta‑blockers) that may affect resuscitation.
- Location of spare EAIs and instructions for school or workplace staff.
- Plan for follow‑up: referral appointments and when to seek allergy/haematology review.

Provide the plan in plain language and, if needed, in the patient’s preferred language. Electronic versions can be useful for portability.

# Medication review and avoidance strategies for implicated triggers (drug labeling, alternatives)

After an anaphylactic reaction, a careful medication review is crucial to prevent inadvertent re‑exposure to suspected pharmaceuticals and to identify safe alternatives. This requires understanding mechanisms of drug hypersensitivity, cross‑reactivity patterns, and the implications of drug allergy labels.

Medication review process:
- Create a chronological medication exposure list around the time of reaction including prescription drugs, over‑the‑counter medications, topical agents, herbal remedies, anesthetic agents, contrast media, and biologics or vaccines.
- Distinguish between immunologic (IgE‑mediated) reactions and non‑immunologic mechanisms (e.g., direct mast cell activation by opiates, radiocontrast media, vancomycin causing “red man” syndrome). Document clinical features (timing, reproducibility) that suggest IgE mediation.
- For antibiotic allergies, especially penicillin and cephalosporins, assess the reaction history; many reported penicillin allergies are not true IgE‑mediated and can be cleared with skin testing and/or graded challenge. Cross‑reactivity between penicillins and cephalosporins is lower than historically believed; side‑chain similarity is more important than beta‑lactam ring alone.

Drug labeling and communication:
- Provide patients with a clear, specific allergy list for their medical record and for carry documentation (medical alert bracelet, wallet card). Avoid nonspecific labels like “sulfa” without documentation.
- Alert primary care, pharmacists, and any future prescribers (including dental, surgical, and anesthetic teams).
- For medications essential to care (e.g., beta‑lactam antibiotics in sepsis), consider referral for specialist testing (skin testing, specific IgE assays, graded challenge) or supervised desensitization when no safe alternative exists.

Avoidance strategies for non‑drug triggers:
- Food allergy: provide tailored avoidance counseling with clear lists of foods and cross‑contamination risks. Discuss food labeling laws in the patient’s country, reading ingredient lists and recognizing precautionary allergen labeling (“may contain”) versus mandated allergen declarations. Emphasize the need for vigilance with restaurants and shared cooking environments. For food allergies where oral immunotherapy is considered, refer to allergy specialists.
- Insect venom: for patients with systemic reactions to stings, advise sting avoidance measures (protective clothing, nest removal by professionals) and rapid access to EAIs. Referral for venom immunotherapy is indicated for those with systemic reactions and elevated risk for future exposures.
- Occupational triggers: for work‑related antigen exposure (e.g., laboratory animals, food production), coordinate with occupational health for risk assessment, workplace modifications, and possible reassignment if necessary.

Alternatives and cross‑reactivity:
- Provide specific alternative medications where appropriate (e.g., macrolide antibiotics when beta‑lactam allergy prohibits penicillins and cephalosporins, after risk assessment). Understand cross‑reactivity patterns for drug classes and counsel patients accordingly.
- When doubt exists regarding the safety of a needed medication, consider supervised graded challenge or desensitization protocols performed by allergy specialists.

# Referral to allergy and haematology/mast cell disorder specialists and timelines

Timely specialist referral optimizes diagnostic clarification and long‑term risk reduction. The urgency and pathway depend on the clinical picture.

When to refer to allergy/immunology:
- All patients who have had anaphylaxis should be referred for allergy assessment to determine the trigger and to guide avoidance and immunotherapy considerations. The optimal timeframe is within 1–6 weeks of the event, allowing acute‑phase mediators to normalize but not delaying beyond a few weeks if practical. If the reaction was recent, ensure that tryptase and acute blood sampling (see below) were performed appropriately.
- For suspected IgE‑mediated reactions, the allergist will perform skin testing, serum specific IgE testing, or supervised oral food challenges as indicated. For venom anaphylaxis, testing for venom‑specific IgE guides the need for venom immunotherapy, which can be disease‑modifying.
- For drug allergies, skin testing protocols exist for certain agents (notably penicillin). For other drugs, graded oral or intravenous challenges or desensitization protocols may be required under specialist supervision.

When to refer to haematology/mast cell specialists:
- Consider referral to haematology or a specialist in mast cell disorders when:
  - Baseline serum tryptase is elevated (see below) or when there is a personal or family history suggestive of systemic mastocytosis (e.g., recurrent unexplained anaphylaxis, cutaneous mastocytosis lesions, flushing, GI symptoms).
  - Anaphylaxis occurs in the absence of an identifiable trigger, particularly when recurrent or associated with hypotension and syncope.
  - There are features suggestive of clonal mast cell disease: persistently elevated baseline tryptase (>11.4 ng/mL or local laboratory upper limit) and signs of bone marrow or systemic involvement.
- Timelines for mast cell assessment: measure baseline serum tryptase at least 24–48 hours after the acute event, once the patient is clinically stable. If baseline tryptase is elevated (>11.4 ng/mL), or if clinical suspicion is high, coordinate specialist evaluation for further testing, which may include repeat tryptase, KIT D816V mutation testing, and bone marrow biopsy as indicated.

Diagnostic testing and interpretation:
- Acute tryptase: draw within 1–4 hours of symptom onset if possible to help document mast cell activation; an elevated acute tryptase that exceeds baseline by $>20\% + 2$ ng/mL supports mast cell degranulation associated with anaphylaxis.
- Baseline tryptase: the commonly used laboratory upper limit is approximately 11.4 ng/mL (reference ranges vary by laboratory). Persistent elevation warrants specialist assessment.
- For systemic mastocytosis, diagnostic criteria include bone marrow mast cell aggregates, atypical mast cell morphology, KIT mutation, and additional minor criteria—specialist involvement is essential to interpret these results and to manage long‑term risks.

# Documentation, communication with primary care and occupational/safety considerations

High‑quality documentation and proactive communication are central to safe follow‑up. The initial discharge summary and any specialist letters should be clear, specific, and distributed to all relevant parties.

Documentation elements to include:
- A clear narrative of the event (without forensic detail): suspected trigger(s), clinical manifestations, treatments given (including timing and dose of epinephrine), and immediate outcome.
- Explicit allergy labeling: list confirmed and suspected allergens, reaction types, and the level of diagnostic certainty (confirmed by testing vs. suspected). Avoid vague terms and document plans for future testing or challenges.
- Prescriptions issued: the number, dose, and brand of EAIs, expiry dates, and instructions provided for use and storage.
- Education provided: summary of topics reviewed, demonstration of EAI use, provision of written action plan, and who received training (patient, caregiver, school personnel).
- Follow‑up plan: referral appointments (specialist, primary care), recommended timing, and pending investigations (tryptase, specific IgE, skin tests).

Communication pathways:
- Provide a copy of discharge and action plan to the patient and send an expedited electronic or faxed summary to the primary care provider, school health services, and, if applicable, occupational health or the patient’s employer.
- For children, ensure that school or daycare has a copy of the action plan, access to EAIs, and staff trained to use them. Document the individual at the school responsible for medication administration and their training status.
- For healthcare workers or others at occupational risk, coordinate with occupational health to assess fitness for duty, help modify duties to reduce exposure, and implement workplace accommodations or personal protective measures. Discuss the need for workplace access to EAIs and emergency protocols.

Safety considerations for special environments:
- Travel: provide additional EAIs when crossing borders may limit access, advise carrying medical documentation and translated action plans, and counsel on temperature extremes.
- Sports and exercise: discuss timing of exercise relative to food ingestion in food‑dependent exercise‑induced anaphylaxis and advise on precautions, including preemptive EAI carriage during athletic events.
- Air travel: floating policies vary; advise patients to carry EAIs in carry‑on luggage and to be aware of compression or temperature effects; medications in some countries may need documentation for security.

# Ethical and medicolegal responsibilities in counselling about risks of re-exposure and risk mitigation

Counselling patients about future risk, avoidance, and the responsibilities of clinicians involves ethical principles (autonomy, beneficence, nonmaleficence, justice) and medicolegal obligations.

Informed consent and shared decision making:
- Provide balanced, understandable information about the risks of re‑exposure, the protective but not absolute nature of avoidance strategies, the role of epinephrine as emergency therapy rather than prevention, and the benefits and risks of diagnostic tests (skin testing, challenge) or immunotherapies.
- Respect patient autonomy while ensuring they understand potential consequences; document the discussion and the patient’s decisions. For patients who decline recommended testing or EAIs, document informed refusal and continue to provide education and safety advice.

Duty to warn and disclosure:
- Clinicians have an obligation to warn patients of risks associated with re‑exposure and to document warnings given. In some circumstances there is a duty to warn third parties (e.g., schools, employers) when the patient consents or when a serious, preventable public health risk exists. Always follow local laws and institutional policies.
- For healthcare professionals with drug allergies that affect practice (e.g., allergy to a commonly used anesthetic), document occupational implications and liaise with occupational health to determine safe work arrangements.

Standards of care and prescribing responsibilities:
- Prescribe EAIs for patients meeting indications; failure to prescribe an EAI when indicated may raise medicolegal concerns. Conversely, overprescribing without proper education is also suboptimal care.
- Provide training and documentation of teaching; if patients demonstrate inability to use the device despite instruction, consider alternative strategies and document counseling provided.

Privacy, documentation, and reporting:
- Maintain confidentiality in line with privacy legislation when communicating with third parties. Obtain consent before sharing detailed medical information with schools or employers, except where legal obligations to disclose exist.
- Report severe anaphylactic reactions to local surveillance or pharmacovigilance systems if triggered by drugs or biologics, particularly if there may be broader public health implications (e.g., vaccine‑related anaphylaxis). This fulfills ethical obligations to contribute to population safety.

Legal considerations for specific populations:
- When caring for minors, ensure parents or guardians receive full education; assent from older children should be sought. Document delegated responsibilities for administration of EAIs in schools; many jurisdictions have laws allowing school staff to administer EAIs even without a prescription under certain conditions—clinicians should be familiar with local statutes.
- For patients with mast cell disorders, discuss implications for insurance, employment, and driving if recurrent syncope or severe cardiovascular instability occurred; liaise with social services or occupational health as appropriate.

Conclusion

Effective long‑term management after anaphylaxis combines appropriate prescription and selection of epinephrine auto‑injectors, hands‑on education and written action plans, careful medication review and avoidance strategies, timely referral to allergy and mast cell specialists, comprehensive documentation and communication, and clear counselling about risks and responsibilities. Clinicians should adopt an individualized, evidence‑based approach that empowers patients to recognize and treat anaphylaxis promptly, reduces the probability of re‑exposure, and ensures coordinated follow‑up for definitive diagnosis and risk mitigation. Regular reassessment of the patient’s knowledge, device access, and evolving clinical status is essential to maintain safety over time.
