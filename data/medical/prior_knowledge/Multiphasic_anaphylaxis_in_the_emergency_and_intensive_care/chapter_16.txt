Introduction

Long-term management and non-acute care after an episode of anaphylaxis are essential to reduce the risk of recurrence, to prepare patients and caregivers to manage future reactions, and to investigate underlying causes such as IgE‑mediated allergy or systemic mast cell disorders. Effective outpatient care combines appropriate prescription and training in epinephrine auto‑injector (EAI) use, clear written action plans, targeted avoidance and medication review, timely specialist referrals, robust documentation and communication with primary care and other stakeholders, and thoughtful attention to ethical and medicolegal responsibilities. This chapter presents practical, evidence‑based guidance for clinicians responsible for follow‑up after anaphylaxis, emphasizing pathophysiologic rationale, clinical decision points, and how to translate laboratory and diagnostic workup into safe, patient‑centered management plans.

# Indications for prescribing epinephrine auto-injectors and choosing appropriate dose/device

Epinephrine is the first‑line treatment for anaphylaxis because its combined alpha and beta adrenergic effects counteract the principal pathophysiological processes: vasodilation and increased vascular permeability (alpha‑mediated vasoconstriction), bronchospasm and mucosal edema (beta‑2 bronchodilation), and myocardial depression and hypotension support (beta‑1 effects). Therefore, patients at risk of anaphylaxis should have ready access to an EAI.

Indications for prescribing an EAI include any of the following scenarios:
- A documented anaphylactic reaction (cutaneous signs plus respiratory compromise, hypotension, or involvement of other organ systems).
- Known IgE‑mediated allergy to an agent (food, venom, medication) with prior systemic reaction of sufficient severity to cause anaphylaxis, or in which future exposure is likely and potentially severe.
- Patients with mast cell activation syndromes or systemic mastocytosis who have had systemic allergic reactions or are at risk of severe reactions (venom allergy is especially high‑risk).
- In selected cases with high‑risk features (e.g., previous severe asthma, cardiovascular disease, inability to avoid the trigger, history of biphasic reactions) even if prior systemic reaction was moderate.

Device selection and dosing are driven by body weight and practical considerations:
- Typical EAI doses commonly available are 0.15 mg, 0.3 mg, and in some countries 0.5 mg. A practical approach is:
  - 0.15 mg device for children weighing approximately 15–30 kg (33–66 lb).
  - 0.3 mg device for those ≥30 kg (≥66 lb).
  - Consider a 0.5 mg device for adults with body mass that may render subcutaneous administration less effective or for those who have responded inadequately to 0.3 mg in the past. Some national guidelines recommend 0.5 mg for adults >50 kg who are obese; local device availability varies.
- Repeat dosing: If symptoms persist or recur, a second EAI dose may be administered after 5–15 minutes (many guidelines suggest 5 minutes as the earliest interval) while arranging emergency medical care. Recurrent or prolonged symptoms may necessitate repeated doses or intravenous epinephrine in hospital.
- Device choice should also account for ease of use, needle length, cost and availability, and patient factors such as dexterity and visual impairment. Demonstrating how different brands click and how to remove safety caps improves user confidence.
- Prescribe at least two EAIs for most patients who need them (one may fail or be inaccessible during a reaction), and advise replacement before expiry and after any use. Check local formulary and insurance constraints that may limit dispensing.

# Patient education and demonstration of auto-injector use; written anaphylaxis action plans

Education is an intervention as important as the prescription itself. Correct and timely epinephrine use reduces morbidity and mortality from anaphylaxis. Education should be practical, repeated, and accompanied by hands‑on demonstration and return demonstration.

Key teaching points and skills to cover:
- Recognizing early signs and symptoms of anaphylaxis: combinations of cutaneous signs (urticaria, angioedema), respiratory symptoms (wheeze, stridor, throat tightness), gastrointestinal symptoms (vomiting, abdominal pain), hypotension or collapse. Teach that respiratory and cardiovascular features can occur without cutaneous signs.
- When to use the EAI: any signs of airway, breathing, or circulatory compromise, or rapidly progressive symptoms after exposure to a known trigger. Teach that in cases of doubt in a patient with known severe allergy, early use is preferred to delay.
- Step-by‑step technique with the chosen device: remove safety cap, place against anterolateral thigh (can be through clothing if necessary), hold for the recommended time (usually 3 seconds for many devices, up to 10 seconds for others—emphasize device‑specific instructions), then massage the site for 10 seconds. Demonstrate on a trainer and require the patient or caregiver to perform a return demonstration.
- Immediate post‑injection actions: call emergency services, lie the patient flat with legs elevated if tolerated (unless vomiting or respiratory distress); if unconscious and breathing is present place in a recovery position. If symptoms persist after 5–15 minutes, a second EAI may be given.
- Storage and maintenance: keep at room temperature, avoid extreme heat or cold, check expiry dates monthly, and store in an accessible place. Explain that lights in the viewing window and discoloration of solution are not reliable indicators of potency—adhere to expiry dates.
- Address barriers: needle phobia, cost, stigma, or unwillingness to carry devices. Offer solutions such as smaller carrying cases, alternatives for cost (patient assistance programs), and practice with trainers to reduce fear.
- Special contexts: school, workplace, travel. Provide written instructions for caregivers, teachers, or occupational health staff, and discuss emergency contacts and anticipatory planning for meals, field trips, or sports.

Written anaphylaxis action plans
A concise, individualized written anaphylaxis action plan reinforces verbal education and aids third parties during an event. Effective plans include:
- Patient identifiers and emergency contact numbers.
- Clear recognition cues for anaphylaxis and explicit instructions for when to use the EAI.
- Stepwise instructions after epinephrine use (call emergency services, positioning, second dose timing).
- Documentation of known triggers, cross‑reactive substances to avoid, and any baseline medications (such as beta‑blockers) that may affect resuscitation.
- Location of spare EAIs and instructions for school or workplace staff.
- Plan for follow‑up: referral appointments and when to seek allergy/haematology review.

Provide the plan in plain language and, if needed, in the patient’s preferred language. Electronic versions can be useful for portability.

# Medication review and avoidance strategies for implicated triggers (drug labeling, alternatives)

After an anaphylactic reaction, a careful medication review is crucial to prevent inadvertent re‑exposure to suspected pharmaceuticals and to identify safe alternatives. This requires understanding mechanisms of drug hypersensitivity, cross‑reactivity patterns, and the implications of drug allergy labels.

Medication review process:
- Create a chronological medication exposure list around the time of reaction including prescription drugs, over‑the‑counter medications, topical agents, herbal remedies, anesthetic agents, contrast media, and biologics or vaccines.
- Distinguish between immunologic (IgE‑mediated) reactions and non‑immunologic mechanisms (e.g., direct mast cell activation by opiates, radiocontrast media, vancomycin causing “red man” syndrome). Document clinical features (timing, reproducibility) that suggest IgE mediation.
- For antibiotic allergies, especially penicillin and cephalosporins, assess the reaction history; many reported penicillin allergies are not true IgE‑mediated and can be cleared with skin testing and/or graded challenge. Cross‑reactivity between penicillins and cephalosporins is lower than historically believed; side‑chain similarity is more important than beta‑lactam ring alone.

Drug labeling and communication:
- Provide patients with a clear, specific allergy list for their medical record and for carry documentation (medical alert bracelet, wallet card). Avoid nonspecific labels like “sulfa” without documentation.
- Alert primary care, pharmacists, and any future prescribers (including dental, surgical, and anesthetic teams).
- For medications essential to care (e.g., beta‑lactam antibiotics in sepsis), consider referral for specialist testing (skin testing, specific IgE assays, graded challenge) or supervised desensitization when no safe alternative exists.

Avoidance strategies for non‑drug triggers:
- Food allergy: provide tailored avoidance counseling with clear lists of foods and cross‑contamination risks. Discuss food labeling laws in the patient’s country, reading ingredient lists and recognizing precautionary allergen labeling (“may contain”) versus mandated allergen declarations. Emphasize the need for vigilance with restaurants and shared cooking environments. For food allergies where oral immunotherapy is considered, refer to allergy specialists.
- Insect venom: for patients with systemic reactions to stings, advise sting avoidance measures (protective clothing, nest removal by professionals) and rapid access to EAIs. Referral for venom immunotherapy is indicated for those with systemic reactions and elevated risk for future exposures.
- Occupational triggers: for work‑related antigen exposure (e.g., laboratory animals, food production), coordinate with occupational health for risk assessment, workplace modifications, and possible reassignment if necessary.

Alternatives and cross‑reactivity:
- Provide specific alternative medications where appropriate (e.g., macrolide antibiotics when beta‑lactam allergy prohibits penicillins and cephalosporins, after risk assessment). Understand cross‑reactivity patterns for drug classes and counsel patients accordingly.
- When doubt exists regarding the safety of a needed medication, consider supervised graded challenge or desensitization protocols performed by allergy specialists.

# Referral to allergy and haematology/mast cell disorder specialists and timelines

Timely specialist referral optimizes diagnostic clarification and long‑term risk reduction. The urgency and pathway depend on the clinical picture.

When to refer to allergy/immunology:
- All patients who have had anaphylaxis should be referred for allergy assessment to determine the trigger and to guide avoidance and immunotherapy considerations. The optimal timeframe is within 1–6 weeks of the event, allowing acute‑phase mediators to normalize but not delaying beyond a few weeks if practical. If the reaction was recent, ensure that tryptase and acute blood sampling (see below) were performed appropriately.
- For suspected IgE‑mediated reactions, the allergist will perform skin testing, serum specific IgE testing, or supervised oral food challenges as indicated. For venom anaphylaxis, testing for venom‑specific IgE guides the need for venom immunotherapy, which can be disease‑modifying.
- For drug allergies, skin testing protocols exist for certain agents (notably penicillin). For other drugs, graded oral or intravenous challenges or desensitization protocols may be required under specialist supervision.

When to refer to haematology/mast cell specialists:
- Consider referral to haematology or a specialist in mast cell disorders when:
  - Baseline serum tryptase is elevated (see below) or when there is a personal or family history suggestive of systemic mastocytosis (e.g., recurrent unexplained anaphylaxis, cutaneous mastocytosis lesions, flushing, GI symptoms).
  - Anaphylaxis occurs in the absence of an identifiable trigger, particularly when recurrent or associated with hypotension and syncope.
  - There are features suggestive of clonal mast cell disease: persistently elevated baseline tryptase (>11.4 ng/mL or local laboratory upper limit) and signs of bone marrow or systemic involvement.
- Timelines for mast cell assessment: measure baseline serum tryptase at least 24–48 hours after the acute event, once the patient is clinically stable. If baseline tryptase is elevated (>11.4 ng/mL), or if clinical suspicion is high, coordinate specialist evaluation for further testing, which may include repeat tryptase, KIT D816V mutation testing, and bone marrow biopsy as indicated.

Diagnostic testing and interpretation:
- Acute tryptase: draw within 1–4 hours of symptom onset if possible to help document mast cell activation; an elevated acute tryptase that exceeds baseline by $>20\% + 2$ ng/mL supports mast cell degranulation associated with anaphylaxis.
- Baseline tryptase: the commonly used laboratory upper limit is approximately 11.4 ng/mL (reference ranges vary by laboratory). Persistent elevation warrants specialist assessment.
- For systemic mastocytosis, diagnostic criteria include bone marrow mast cell aggregates, atypical mast cell morphology, KIT mutation, and additional minor criteria—specialist involvement is essential to interpret these results and to manage long‑term risks.

# Documentation, communication with primary care and occupational/safety considerations

High‑quality documentation and proactive communication are central to safe follow‑up. The initial discharge summary and any specialist letters should be clear, specific, and distributed to all relevant parties.

Documentation elements to include:
- A clear narrative of the event (without forensic detail): suspected trigger(s), clinical manifestations, treatments given (including timing and dose of epinephrine), and immediate outcome.
- Explicit allergy labeling: list confirmed and suspected allergens, reaction types, and the level of diagnostic certainty (confirmed by testing vs. suspected). Avoid vague terms and document plans for future testing or challenges.
- Prescriptions issued: the number, dose, and brand of EAIs, expiry dates, and instructions provided for use and storage.
- Education provided: summary of topics reviewed, demonstration of EAI use, provision of written action plan, and who received training (patient, caregiver, school personnel).
- Follow‑up plan: referral appointments (specialist, primary care), recommended timing, and pending investigations (tryptase, specific IgE, skin tests).

Communication pathways:
- Provide a copy of discharge and action plan to the patient and send an expedited electronic or faxed summary to the primary care provider, school health services, and, if applicable, occupational health or the patient’s employer.
- For children, ensure that school or daycare has a copy of the action plan, access to EAIs, and staff trained to use them. Document the individual at the school responsible for medication administration and their training status.
- For healthcare workers or others at occupational risk, coordinate with occupational health to assess fitness for duty, help modify duties to reduce exposure, and implement workplace accommodations or personal protective measures. Discuss the need for workplace access to EAIs and emergency protocols.

Safety considerations for special environments:
- Travel: provide additional EAIs when crossing borders may limit access, advise carrying medical documentation and translated action plans, and counsel on temperature extremes.
- Sports and exercise: discuss timing of exercise relative to food ingestion in food‑dependent exercise‑induced anaphylaxis and advise on precautions, including preemptive EAI carriage during athletic events.
- Air travel: floating policies vary; advise patients to carry EAIs in carry‑on luggage and to be aware of compression or temperature effects; medications in some countries may need documentation for security.

# Ethical and medicolegal responsibilities in counselling about risks of re-exposure and risk mitigation

Counselling patients about future risk, avoidance, and the responsibilities of clinicians involves ethical principles (autonomy, beneficence, nonmaleficence, justice) and medicolegal obligations.

Informed consent and shared decision making:
- Provide balanced, understandable information about the risks of re‑exposure, the protective but not absolute nature of avoidance strategies, the role of epinephrine as emergency therapy rather than prevention, and the benefits and risks of diagnostic tests (skin testing, challenge) or immunotherapies.
- Respect patient autonomy while ensuring they understand potential consequences; document the discussion and the patient’s decisions. For patients who decline recommended testing or EAIs, document informed refusal and continue to provide education and safety advice.

Duty to warn and disclosure:
- Clinicians have an obligation to warn patients of risks associated with re‑exposure and to document warnings given. In some circumstances there is a duty to warn third parties (e.g., schools, employers) when the patient consents or when a serious, preventable public health risk exists. Always follow local laws and institutional policies.
- For healthcare professionals with drug allergies that affect practice (e.g., allergy to a commonly used anesthetic), document occupational implications and liaise with occupational health to determine safe work arrangements.

Standards of care and prescribing responsibilities:
- Prescribe EAIs for patients meeting indications; failure to prescribe an EAI when indicated may raise medicolegal concerns. Conversely, overprescribing without proper education is also suboptimal care.
- Provide training and documentation of teaching; if patients demonstrate inability to use the device despite instruction, consider alternative strategies and document counseling provided.

Privacy, documentation, and reporting:
- Maintain confidentiality in line with privacy legislation when communicating with third parties. Obtain consent before sharing detailed medical information with schools or employers, except where legal obligations to disclose exist.
- Report severe anaphylactic reactions to local surveillance or pharmacovigilance systems if triggered by drugs or biologics, particularly if there may be broader public health implications (e.g., vaccine‑related anaphylaxis). This fulfills ethical obligations to contribute to population safety.

Legal considerations for specific populations:
- When caring for minors, ensure parents or guardians receive full education; assent from older children should be sought. Document delegated responsibilities for administration of EAIs in schools; many jurisdictions have laws allowing school staff to administer EAIs even without a prescription under certain conditions—clinicians should be familiar with local statutes.
- For patients with mast cell disorders, discuss implications for insurance, employment, and driving if recurrent syncope or severe cardiovascular instability occurred; liaise with social services or occupational health as appropriate.

Conclusion

Effective long‑term management after anaphylaxis combines appropriate prescription and selection of epinephrine auto‑injectors, hands‑on education and written action plans, careful medication review and avoidance strategies, timely referral to allergy and mast cell specialists, comprehensive documentation and communication, and clear counselling about risks and responsibilities. Clinicians should adopt an individualized, evidence‑based approach that empowers patients to recognize and treat anaphylaxis promptly, reduces the probability of re‑exposure, and ensures coordinated follow‑up for definitive diagnosis and risk mitigation. Regular reassessment of the patient’s knowledge, device access, and evolving clinical status is essential to maintain safety over time.
