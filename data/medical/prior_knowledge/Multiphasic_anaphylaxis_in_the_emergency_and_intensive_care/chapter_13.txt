Introduction

Anaphylaxis is a rapid, life-threatening systemic hypersensitivity reaction characterized by airway compromise, circulatory collapse, and multisystem involvement due to the sudden release of mediators (histamine, tryptase, leukotrienes, prostaglandins, platelet-activating factor) from mast cells and basophils. Most episodes respond to intramuscular epinephrine, aggressive intravascular volume resuscitation, and supportive care in the emergency department. However, a subset of patients progress to severe or refractory anaphylaxis that requires intensive care unit (ICU)–level monitoring and interventions, including continuous vasopressor infusions, advanced hemodynamic monitoring, airway management, and prolonged observation. This chapter describes the definitions, pathophysiology, recognition, advanced pharmacologic strategies, hemodynamic goals, monitoring tools, approaches to recurrent or relapsing anaphylaxis, and multidisciplinary coordination necessary for safe and effective ICU management of refractory anaphylaxis.

# Definition and recognition of refractory anaphylaxis requiring ICU-level care

Refractory anaphylaxis lacks a universally accepted single numeric definition, but it is commonly defined clinically as persistent or progressive cardiovascular collapse, respiratory compromise, or multisystem organ dysfunction despite initial standard therapy. Standard therapy typically comprises immediate intramuscular epinephrine (0.3–0.5 mg for adults IM into the mid-anterolateral thigh), high-flow oxygen, aggressive crystalloid boluses, airway support as needed, and adjunctive therapies (antihistamines, corticosteroids). Anaphylaxis becomes “severe” when there is shock (hypotension or poor perfusion), severe bronchospasm causing hypoxemia or respiratory failure, or rapidly progressive airway edema. It becomes “refractory” when hypotension and/or hypoxemia persist or worsen after repeated intramuscular or bolus intravenous epinephrine doses and adequate fluid resuscitation, necessitating continuous intravenous catecholamine infusion or other ICU-level interventions.

Recognizing refractory anaphylaxis early is essential. Key clinical features raising concern include hypotension that fails to respond to one or more boluses of epinephrine and 30–50 mL/kg of crystalloids (or continued need for epinephrine boluses), progressive tachycardia with poor peripheral perfusion, delayed capillary refill, cool mottled extremities, rising serum lactate and metabolic acidosis, ongoing or worsening bronchospasm despite inhaled bronchodilators and systemic epinephrine, stridor or rapidly progressive upper airway edema, altered mental status from poor cerebral perfusion, and recurrent biphasic reactions after initial stabilization. Other concerning features include arrhythmias, myocardial ischemia, or lactic acidosis (e.g., lactate >2 mmol/L suggesting significant tissue hypoperfusion). Patients exhibiting these patterns require escalation to ICU-level care for continuous infusions, invasive monitoring, controlled airway management (often fiberoptic or video laryngoscopic intubation with skilled airway team), and consideration of extracorporeal support when conventional therapies fail.

# Indications for continuous intravenous epinephrine infusion: initiation, titration and monitoring

Indications

Continuous intravenous (IV) epinephrine infusion is indicated when hemodynamic instability persists despite initial resuscitation with IM/IV epinephrine boluses and fluid resuscitation, or when repeated bolus dosing is impractical because of ongoing shock or recurrent life‑threatening symptoms. Specific clinical scenarios include persistent systolic blood pressure <90 mmHg (or mean arterial pressure (MAP) <65 mmHg) despite two or more epinephrine boluses and 30 mL/kg crystalloid, signs of end-organ hypoperfusion (oliguria: urine output <0.5 mL/kg/h, rising lactate), severe bronchospasm with hypoxemia requiring ventilator support, or recurrent severe reactions where ongoing mediator release or continued exposure is suspected.

Initiation

Prior to starting infusion, secure vascular access appropriate for vasopressor administration: a large-bore peripheral IV may be used for short-term infusions, but a central venous catheter is preferred in ongoing high-dose vasopressor requirements to reduce the risk of extravasation injury. Establish continuous cardiac monitoring, frequent noninvasive or invasive blood pressure monitoring (arterial line if vasopressor requirement is expected to be substantial or prolonged), pulse oximetry, and urine output measurement. Ensure adequate crystalloid resuscitation has begun.

Initial infusion dosing is individualized. Common adult starting regimens include 0.02–0.05 mcg/kg/min (approx. 1–5 mcg/min for an average adult) with rapid titration upward in 0.02–0.1 mcg/kg/min increments (or 1–5 mcg/min increments) every 2–5 minutes guided by response. Many centers use a pragmatic rate range of 0.05–0.5 mcg/kg/min, and some patients require up to 1 mcg/kg/min in extreme cases. For clarity, approximate fixed-dose adult infusion ranges are frequently expressed as 2–10 mcg/min (low dose) to 10–20 mcg/min (moderate) and higher for severe refractory shock. Pediatric dosing is weight-based and typically begins at 0.01–0.1 mcg/kg/min with similar titration principles.

Titration and goals

Titrate to clinically important end points: restoration of adequate MAP (target MAP ≥65 mmHg unless higher individualized target is required for known chronic hypertension), restoration of tissue perfusion (improving mental status, urine output >0.5 mL/kg/h, decreasing lactate), resolution of severe bronchospasm or upper-airway edema, and weaning of supplemental oxygen/ventilator support when appropriate. Perform frequent assessments (every few minutes during titration) and adjust infusion according to hemodynamics and side effects.

Monitoring and safety

Continuous telemetry is required to detect arrhythmias induced by catecholamines. Serial 12‑lead ECGs and cardiac enzyme testing should be considered if ischemia is suspected. Monitor for tachyarrhythmias, myocardial ischemia, severe hypertension, and splanchnic or peripheral ischemia. Regularly reassess arterial blood gases, lactate, electrolytes (potassium, magnesium), and glucose (epinephrine can induce hyperglycemia). Avoid abrupt cessation of infusion—taper slowly as hemodynamics stabilize to prevent rebound hypotension.

Extravasation risk is important with peripheral infusions. Use a central venous catheter when infusion rates exceed safe peripheral thresholds (institution-specific, but often >5–10 mcg/min) or when the expected duration is prolonged. If extravasation occurs, local infiltration with phentolamine and warm compresses may mitigate tissue injury.

# Second-line vasopressors and adjunctive drugs (norepinephrine, vasopressin, glucagon) in special populations

Rationale

Epinephrine remains the primary vasoactive agent for anaphylaxis because of its combined alpha- and beta-adrenergic effects (vasoconstriction to raise systemic vascular resistance and bronchodilation and inotropy to improve cardiac output). However, in refractory cases or when epinephrine is constrained by comorbidities (e.g., beta-blocker therapy) or side effects (tachyarrhythmia, myocardial ischemia), adjunctive vasopressors or second-line drugs are necessary.

Norepinephrine

Norepinephrine is a potent vasoconstrictor with strong alpha-1 activity and modest beta-1 inotropy. It is favored when distributive shock predominates and when pure vasoconstriction is required to maintain perfusion pressure. In severe anaphylaxis, where profound vasodilation and increased vascular permeability reduce preload and systemic vascular resistance, norepinephrine can restore MAP with less chronotropic effect than epinephrine. Typical adult dosing begins at 0.01–0.1 mcg/kg/min (approx. 2–20 mcg/min) and titrates to effect; however, doses may be higher in refractory shock. Norepinephrine is commonly used alone or combined with epinephrine, with close attention to heart rate and signs of ischemia.

Vasopressin

Vasopressin acts on V1 receptors to induce vasoconstriction via a mechanism independent of adrenergic receptors and is useful as an adjunct when catecholamine resistance develops or when large catecholamine doses are needed. It is particularly considered in patients on chronic ACE inhibitors or with profound vasoplegia. Common practice is either a low-dose fixed infusion (e.g., 0.03 units/min) as an adjunct or higher doses tailored to response, although higher doses increase the risk of splanchnic and digital ischemia. Vasopressin does not have direct bronchodilator effects, so it is not a substitute for epinephrine when bronchospasm is prominent. Potential adverse effects include ischemia, hyponatremia when prolonged, and cardiac ischemia.

Glucagon

Glucagon is the key adjunct in patients taking beta-adrenergic blockers or calcium channel blockers in whom typical adrenergic agents may have reduced efficacy. Glucagon increases intracellular cyclic AMP through G-protein–coupled receptors independent of beta-adrenergic pathways, producing inotropic and chronotropic effects and increasing systemic vascular resistance. Glucagon dosing commonly begins with an IV bolus of 50–150 mcg/kg (max 5 mg) given slowly over 1–2 minutes, followed by an infusion of 1–5 mg/hour titrated to hemodynamic response. Antiemetic therapy should accompany glucagon use because nausea and vomiting are frequent. Hypokalemia and hyperglycemia are potential metabolic effects. Efficacy is variable and usually temporizing while catecholamine therapy and other supportive measures continue.

Other adjuncts

Methylene blue has been reported in refractory vasoplegic states due to its inhibition of nitric oxide–mediated vasodilation, and some centers have used it in refractory anaphylactic vasoplegia, but evidence is limited and risks (serotonin syndrome in patients on SSRIs, hemolysis in G6PD deficiency) exist. Inhaled bronchodilators, nebulized epinephrine, and ketamine infusion may be adjuncts for severe bronchospasm. Intravenous antihistamines (H1 and H2) and systemic corticosteroids (e.g., methylprednisolone 1–2 mg/kg) are commonly given; steroids may not affect the acute phase but are used to reduce protracted or biphasic reactions.

Choice and sequencing

In practice, epinephrine infusion is the initial vasopressor in refractory anaphylaxis because of its combined alpha/beta activity and effects on bronchospasm. If the patient remains hypotensive despite escalating epinephrine or develops intolerable side effects, norepinephrine and vasopressin are appropriate adjuncts. In beta-blocked patients, glucagon should be given early if epinephrine response is blunted. All additions should be guided by hemodynamic targets and organ perfusion markers, with attention to side-effect profiles and interactions.

# Hemodynamic monitoring (invasive and non-invasive) and goals of resuscitation

Goals of resuscitation

The primary goals are restoration and maintenance of adequate perfusion to vital organs (brain, heart, kidneys) and resolution of hypoxemia. Numeric targets commonly used in ICU resuscitation include MAP ≥65 mmHg (higher targets may be used for chronic hypertension), urine output ≥0.5 mL/kg/h, decreasing serum lactate toward normal (<2 mmol/L) and normalization of mental status and skin perfusion. Oxygenation goals include SpO2 ≥92% (or individualized based on comorbidities) and PaO2 as clinically appropriate.

Non-invasive monitoring

Continuous monitoring of heart rate, rhythm, SpO2, noninvasive blood pressure (NIBP), respiratory rate, and mental status are the minimal requirements. Point-of-care testing including bedside lactate, arterial blood gases, and repeated measurements of hemoglobin and electrolytes are useful. Bedside transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) is highly valuable for rapid assessment of cardiac function (left and right ventricular function), intravascular volume status (inferior vena cava variability, though limited in mechanically ventilated patients), and pericardial effusion. Lung ultrasound can assess pulmonary interstitial edema and pleural fluid. These techniques aid in distinguishing hypovolemia, cardiogenic shock, obstructive shock, and distributive shock.

Invasive monitoring

When vasopressor support is anticipated or when hemodynamics are unstable, invasive monitoring becomes crucial. An arterial catheter allows beat-to-beat blood pressure monitoring and accurate blood sampling. Central venous access enables administration of vasopressors and measurement of central venous pressure (CVP) and central venous oxygen saturation (ScvO2). Although absolute CVP values are poor predictors of volume responsiveness, trends may help. ScvO2 (normal ~70–75%) gives insight into adequacy of oxygen delivery vs consumption; low ScvO2 suggests inadequate perfusion or cardiac output.

Advanced hemodynamic monitoring modalities include pulse contour analysis (e.g., PiCCO), esophageal Doppler, bioimpedance, and pulmonary artery catheterization. Pulmonary artery catheter (Swan-Ganz) can measure pulmonary artery pressures, mixed venous oxygen saturation, cardiac output, and cardiac filling pressures, which is informative in complex cases with potential myocardial involvement, severe pulmonary hypertension, or when differentiating cardiogenic shock from distributive shock is ambiguous. In refractory anaphylactic shock, where high-dose vasopressors or inotropes are required or when myocardial depression is suspected, these tools can guide fluid vs inotropic therapy and vasopressor selection.

Dynamic measures of preload responsiveness (passive leg raise, stroke volume variation in mechanically ventilated patients) should be used rather than static measures. Monitor urine output hourly and repeat serum lactate and arterial blood gases every 1–4 hours depending on acuity.

Integration for decision-making

A structured approach integrates clinical exam, serial lactates, urine output, bedside TTE, arterial line pressures, and response to fluid challenges or vasopressor adjustments. For example, a patient with low MAP, elevated lactate, poor urine output, low ScvO2, preserved left ventricular ejection fraction on TTE, and a collapsible IVC likely benefits from further fluid resuscitation. Conversely, depressed ejection fraction and elevated filling pressures suggest inotropic support rather than more fluids. Clear goals for weaning vasopressors should be established and pursued as perfusion normalizes.

# Management of multiple relapses: strategies, risks and benefits of preemptive infusion vs watchful waiting

Biphasic and protracted anaphylaxis

Anaphylaxis can relapse after initial resolution, manifesting as a biphasic reaction (return of symptoms hours after apparent recovery) or protracted anaphylaxis (symptoms that persist for many hours to days). Biphasic reactions occur in an estimated 1–20% of cases and usually present within 1–8 hours but can occur up to 72 hours later. Risk factors for biphasic or protracted reactions include delayed or insufficient initial epinephrine, severe initial reaction, need for multiple epinephrine doses, and peanut or tree nut allergy in some series. The unpredictability of timing and severity makes disposition decisions complex.

Watchful waiting approach

Shorter observation periods (4–6 hours) may be appropriate for patients with mild reactions that resolved with a single dose of epinephrine and lack risk factors. “Watchful waiting” in the ED or step-down setting includes serial assessments of vital signs, symptoms, and readiness for discharge with observation lasting based on local protocols and patient risk profile. Watchful waiting avoids unnecessary ICU admissions and vasopressor exposure but carries risk of failing to recognize a biphasic or delayed relapse, especially in high-risk patients.

Preemptive infusion approach

For patients with severe initial anaphylaxis, ongoing mediator release, or repeated relapses, some clinicians elect to start a preemptive low-dose continuous epinephrine (or other vasopressor) infusion after apparent stabilization to reduce the risk of relapse and to provide a safety net. Potential benefits include:

- Prevention of recurrent hypotension and bronchospasm by maintaining alpha- and beta-adrenergic support while mediators clear.
- Reduced need for repeated bolus dosing with its attendant hemodynamic swings and risk of underdosing.
- Controlled weaning under continuous monitoring.

Risks and downsides include:

- Catecholamine-induced adverse effects: tachyarrhythmias, myocardial ischemia, hyperglycemia, lactic acidosis, peripheral and splanchnic ischemia.
- Need for ICU-level monitoring and central access, increasing resource use and potential procedural complications.
- Potential for masking evolving clinical deterioration if infusions blunt warning signs.

Decision framework

A thoughtful individualized approach is needed. Indications favoring preemptive infusion include:

- Requirement for multiple epinephrine boluses to achieve stabilization.
- Ongoing or recurrent hypotension or bronchospasm despite recent therapy.
- Presence of risk factors for protracted or biphasic reaction (severe initial reaction, delayed presentation, ingestion of drugs that prolong mediator activity, such as certain opioids or ACE inhibitors).
- Concomitant beta-blocker therapy where epinephrine responsiveness may be blunted.

If preemptive infusion is used, select the lowest effective dose and define explicit weaning criteria based on stable MAP, urine output, lactate clearance, and absence of symptoms for a defined period (often 6–24 hours). Frequent reassessment and an attempt to wean as soon as clinically safe limit exposure. In all cases, ensure clear documentation and communication when choosing a preemptive strategy.

# Multidisciplinary care coordination (allergy, hematology, ICU) and escalation policies

Team-based care

Refractory anaphylaxis requires multidisciplinary coordination before, during, and after the ICU stay. The core team typically includes critical care physicians and nurses, allergy/immunology specialists, respiratory therapists, pharmacy, and—with specific indications—cardiology, hematology, and surgery or interventional teams for extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) or other invasive support. Clear roles, consistent communication, and use of checklists or protocols improve safety and outcomes.

Allergy/immunology

Allergy specialists provide expertise in identifying likely triggers, planning diagnostic testing (serum tryptase levels, specific IgE testing, skin testing when safe), and advising on prevention and long-term management. Baseline serum tryptase (and, if possible, an acute tryptase within 1–3 hours of reaction onset) helps confirm mast-cell–mediated anaphylaxis. Allergy teams advise on risk stratification for biphasic reactions and suitability for discharge versus prolonged observation. They also counsel on avoidance strategies and emergency plans (e.g., prescription of epinephrine auto-injectors, education on when and how to use them).

Hematology

In selected cases, hematology consultation is warranted: unusual presentations may reflect systemic mastocytosis or clonal mast-cell disorders that predispose to severe or recurrent episodes. Elevated baseline tryptase (>11.4 ng/mL in many labs) may prompt evaluation for mast cell activation syndrome or systemic mastocytosis; hematology helps determine the need for bone marrow biopsy or genetic testing (e.g., KIT D816V mutation) and long-term management.

Critical care and escalation policies

The ICU team must establish escalation policies and thresholds for additional interventions. Policies should define:

- Criteria for ICU admission from the ED (e.g., refractory hypotension, need for continuous vasopressors, ventilatory failure, recurrent severe reactions).
- Standardized orders for continuous infusions, monitoring frequency, and laboratory surveillance (e.g., lactate every 2–4 hours, hourly urine output).
- Protocols for airway management and involvement of anesthesia/ENT and ECMO teams when airway edema or severe cardiopulmonary collapse is anticipated.
- Guidelines for weaning vasopressors and criteria for transfer out of ICU.
- Procedures for obtaining and handling serum tryptase (timing of acute and baseline specimens) and for safe scheduling of skin testing once the patient is stabilized.

Pharmacy and medication safety

Pharmacy involvement is essential to ensure correct preparation and dosing of concentrated vasoactive medications, to prevent medication errors, and to provide guidance on interactions (for example, interactions of beta-blockers, MAO inhibitors, or tricyclic antidepressants with vasoactive agents). Standardized infusion concentrations and infusion pump programming, double checks for high-risk drugs, and protocols for extravasation management should be in place.

Documentation, patient education, and follow-up

Care transitions should include documentation of suspected triggers, acute tryptase values if obtained, doses of interventions used, and recommendations for follow-up. Before discharge from the ICU or hospital, patients should receive counseling on avoidance strategies, prescriptions for epinephrine auto-injectors, and urgent outpatient follow-up with allergy/immunology. For recurrent events or unexplained severe anaphylaxis, a coordinated outpatient plan with allergy and possibly hematology is crucial.

Conclusion

Management of refractory and severe anaphylaxis in the ICU requires early recognition of treatment failure, timely initiation and careful titration of continuous epinephrine infusions, judicious use of second-line vasopressors and adjunctive agents, and targeted hemodynamic monitoring to guide therapy. Decisions about preemptive versus watchful approaches to relapsing anaphylaxis require individualized risk assessment and clear criteria for escalation and de-escalation. Multidisciplinary collaboration among critical care, allergy/immunology, hematology, pharmacy, and procedural teams, combined with institution-specific protocols, optimizes patient safety and outcomes in these high-risk, dynamic clinical situations.
