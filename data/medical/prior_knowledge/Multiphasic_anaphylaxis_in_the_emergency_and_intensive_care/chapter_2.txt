Introduction

Mast cells are tissue-resident immune effector cells that occupy key sentinel positions at the interface between the host and the external environment. They are best known for their central role in IgE-mediated immediate hypersensitivity and anaphylaxis, but their biology extends widely into host defense, tissue remodeling, and chronic inflammatory disease. Understanding mast cell types, development, the contents of their secretory granules and the profile of mediators they generate de novo is essential for interpreting the systemic physiology of mediator release, for recognizing and diagnosing mast cell activation syndromes, and for rational pharmacologic intervention. This chapter integrates the cellular and molecular basis of mast cell function with the clinical effects of their mediators, the regulation of activation thresholds, the laboratory detection of mediator release, and the principal pharmacologic targets used to block mediator actions.

# Mast cell types, distribution and lifecycle

Mast cells arise from hematopoietic progenitors in the bone marrow, circulate in an immature form, and complete differentiation in peripheral tissues. Unlike circulating granulocytes, mature mast cells are tissue-resident and do not recirculate. Key growth and differentiation signals include stem cell factor (SCF, the ligand for the receptor tyrosine kinase KIT) and several cytokines (notably IL-3, IL-4, IL-9, IL-33). KIT signaling is central to mast cell survival, proliferation, maturation and chemotaxis; genetic or pharmacologic perturbations of KIT profoundly alter mast cell numbers and function.

In humans, mast cells are classically divided by their protease content and location into two major phenotypes: tryptase-only mast cells (often designated MCT) and tryptase-plus-chymase mast cells (MCTC). MCT cells are more common in mucosal sites such as the gastrointestinal tract, lung mucosa and are often associated with allergic inflammation. MCTC cells predominate in connective tissues including skin and perivascular regions and contain tryptase, chymase, carboxypeptidase A and serine proteases plus abundant heparin in their granules. This protease profile influences how mast cells contribute to tissue-specific responses—e.g., chymase can activate matrix remodeling and modulate angiotensin II metabolism, while tryptase activates protease-activated receptors (PARs) on endothelium and nerve endings.

Anatomic distribution places mast cells at strategic sites: around small blood vessels and lymphatics, in the dermis, at mucosal surfaces (airways, gut), and adjacent to nerves. This makes their mediators especially effective at altering vascular tone and permeability, modulating smooth muscle, and engaging sensory neurons. Mast cells are long-lived compared with many leukocytes, with lifespan dependent on tissue cues; their numbers are dynamically regulated by local proliferation, recruitment of progenitors, and survival signals (SCF, IL-4, IL-9). In disease states such as systemic mastocytosis clonal expansion driven by activating KIT mutations (commonly KIT D816V) leads to markedly increased mast cell burden and constitutive activation.

# Preformed mediators (histamine, tryptase, heparin) and de novo mediators (leukotrienes, prostaglandins, cytokines)

Mast cell activation induces a biphasic secretory program. Immediately upon activation (seconds to minutes) there is exocytosis of cytoplasmic granules releasing preformed mediators. Over minutes to hours, enzymes and synthetic pathways generate new lipid mediators and cytokines that sustain and shape the inflammatory response.

Preformed mediators

- Histamine: Stored in dense granules complexed with proteoglycans (heparin, chondroitin sulfate). Release leads to rapid effects on vascular and bronchial smooth muscle via histamine receptors (H1, H2, H3, H4). Histamine is a prototypic mediator of pruritus, flushing, vasodilation, and increased microvascular permeability.

- Tryptase: A serine protease abundant in human mast cell granules, especially in MCT and MCTC subtypes. Tryptase exists as tetramers stabilized by interactions with proteoglycans and is relatively resistant to proteolysis and inactivation. Tryptase can activate protease-activated receptor-2 (PAR-2) on endothelial and nerve cells, cleave extracellular matrix components, modulate coagulation factors, and serve as a clinically useful biomarker of mast cell activation because of its relative specificity for mast cells.

- Heparin and other proteoglycans: Anionic polymers that stabilize granule proteases and have vasoactive and anticoagulant properties following release. Heparin contributes to the granule architecture and binds positively charged mediators.

Other preformed mediators include small quantities of serotonin (in rodents more than in humans), and stored cytokines such as TNF-alpha in some contexts.

De novo mediators

- Leukotrienes: Synthesized from arachidonic acid via 5-lipoxygenase (5-LO) into leukotriene C4 (LTC4), which is rapidly metabolized to LTD4 and LTE4. These cysteinyl leukotrienes are potent bronchoconstrictors, increase vascular permeability, and enhance mucus secretion. They are central to the sustained phase of bronchospasm and edema in allergic responses.

- Prostaglandins: Particularly prostaglandin D2 (PGD2) produced by cyclooxygenase (COX) and PGD synthase in mast cells. PGD2 causes bronchoconstriction, vasodilation, and recruits Th2 cells and eosinophils (via CRTH2 receptor), contributing to late-phase allergic inflammation.

- Platelet-activating factor (PAF): A phospholipid mediator generated rapidly that induces bronchoconstriction, increases vascular permeability, and can produce hypotension. High PAF levels correlate with more severe anaphylaxis in some studies.

- Cytokines and chemokines: Activated mast cells transcribe and secrete a wide range of cytokines (IL-4, IL-5, IL-6, IL-13, TNF-alpha, GM-CSF) and chemokines (CCL2/MCP-1, CCL3/MIP-1alpha). These mediators take hours to be produced and underlie cell recruitment (eosinophils, basophils, neutrophils), shifts in adaptive immunity toward Th2 responses, and chronic tissue changes.

- Nitric oxide (NO): Mast cells can induce endothelial NO production indirectly; mast cell-derived mediators can also modulate NO pathways contributing to vasodilation.

The pattern of mediator release (which mediators, quantities, and timing) differs by stimulus and mast cell subtype. IgE cross-linking typically causes rapid degranulation with histamine and tryptase release followed by lipid mediator synthesis; certain toll-like receptor (TLR) stimuli can favor cytokine production with limited degranulation.

# Effects of mediators on vascular tone, permeability, bronchial smooth muscle and the nervous system

The systemic physiologic manifestations of mast cell activation derive from the combined actions of their mediators on the vasculature, airways, and peripheral nervous system.

Vascular tone and permeability

Histamine acts on vascular endothelium and smooth muscle via H1 and H2 receptors. H1 receptor activation on endothelium increases intracellular calcium and induces endothelial cell contraction, creating interendothelial gaps that permit extravasation of plasma proteins and fluid — the basis for urticaria and angioedema. H1-mediated endothelial activation also promotes expression of adhesion molecules and chemokine release. H2 receptors on vascular smooth muscle cause vasodilation via cAMP increases, contributing to flushing and hypotension. The net effect of massive histamine release is a fall in systemic vascular resistance with increased vascular leak, third-spacing of intravascular volume, and intravascular hypovolemia.

Tryptase contributes to vascular effects by activating PAR-2 and remodeling the extracellular matrix; it also can enhance vascular permeability indirectly by generating kinins or modifying complement components. Heparin released from granules exerts anticoagulant effects locally and systemically, which can contribute to bleeding tendencies in some massive mast cell activation scenarios.

Leukotrienes (LTC4/D4/E4) and PGD2 potentiate vascular permeability and recruit leukocytes; PAF increases capillary leak and can produce hypotension. The combined action of these mediators during systemic anaphylaxis leads to rapid edema (including laryngeal edema) and distributive shock.

Bronchial smooth muscle

Cysteinyl leukotrienes are among the most potent bronchoconstrictors produced by inflammatory cells, acting on CysLT1 and CysLT2 receptors on airway smooth muscle and contributing to bronchospasm, increased mucus secretion, and airway hyperresponsiveness. Histamine, acting via H1 receptors on airway smooth muscle, produces immediate bronchoconstriction, though its effect is generally shorter-lived than leukotrienes. PGD2 also causes bronchoconstriction and is a chemoattractant for Th2 cells and eosinophils, promoting sustained airway inflammation. Nitric oxide and PAF can exacerbate lower airway dysfunction in severe reactions.

Nervous system

Mast cell mediators directly and indirectly interact with the peripheral nervous system. Histamine and tryptase stimulate sensory C-fibers and trigger neurogenic inflammation, which results in itching, burning, and reflex bronchoconstriction. Tryptase activation of PAR-2 on neurons increases nociceptive signaling and can potentiate pain and pruritus. Cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-alpha sensitize nociceptors and modulate central neuronal circuits, contributing to systemic symptoms such as malaise. Mast cells are also situated adjacent to nerves and can participate in bidirectional communication: neuronal mediators (substance P, CGRP) can trigger mast cell degranulation, and mast cell mediators modulate neuronal activity.

Cardiovascular collapse during severe anaphylaxis can result from profound vasodilation and increased vascular permeability producing hypovolemia, direct negative inotropic effects of mediators, and arrhythmogenic influences (e.g., histamine-induced coronary vasospasm). Pulmonary vasoconstriction from mediator effects can further impair oxygenation and right ventricular function.

# Regulation of mast cell activation and thresholds for degranulation

Mast cell activation is regulated at multiple levels: receptor engagement and signal strength, intracellular signaling cascades, cross-talk with other cells and mediators, and the balance of activating versus inhibitory receptors.

Receptors and primary activation pathways

The high-affinity IgE receptor, FcεRI, is the canonical pathway for classical allergic activation. Cross-linking of IgE bound to FcεRI by multivalent antigen induces phosphorylation of the receptor ITAMs by Lyn kinase, recruitment and activation of Syk kinase, and initiation of downstream signaling through LAT/SLP-76 leading to phospholipase C gamma (PLCγ) activation, inositol trisphosphate (IP3)-mediated calcium release, and protein kinase C activation. Calcium mobilization is essential for granule exocytosis. PI3K signaling contributes to cytoskeletal rearrangements and sustained mediator production.

Non-IgE triggers include complement split products (C3a, C5a) acting on their receptors, direct activation by drugs (e.g., radiocontrast, some antibiotics and opioids) via mechanisms that may involve MRGPRX2 (Mas-related G-protein–coupled receptor X2), physical stimuli (pressure), and pathogen-associated molecular patterns (TLRs) that can favor cytokine production without full degranulation.

Threshold modulation

The propensity of a mast cell to degranulate in response to a given stimulus depends on several modulatory influences:

- IgE load: The density of surface-bound IgE enhances sensitivity to antigen. High levels of specific IgE lower activation thresholds.

- Cytokine milieu: IL-4 and IL-33 can “prime” mast cells, increasing FcεRI expression and augmenting mediator synthesis. IL-10 and TGF-beta provide inhibitory signals.

- Co-receptors: Engagement of inhibitory receptors such as FcγRIIb (via IgG immune complexes) recruits phosphatases (SHP-1/2) that attenuate FcεRI signaling. Siglecs and other ITIM-bearing receptors similarly dampen activation.

- Intracellular signaling regulators: Proteins that modulate Syk, BTK (Bruton's tyrosine kinase), PI3K, and downstream calcium signaling set the amplitude of response. Pharmacologic inhibitors of Syk or BTK reduce degranulation and mediator production.

- Medications and comorbid conditions: Beta-blockers reduce the compensatory adrenergic response during anaphylaxis and may worsen outcomes. ACE inhibitors can augment bradykinin-mediated angioedema by blocking bradykinin degradation, potentially lowering the threshold for severe edema in mast cell–mediated or bradykinin-mediated processes. Chronic corticosteroids and anti-IgE therapy (omalizumab) can raise activation thresholds by lowering IgE levels and reducing FcεRI expression.

- Receptor desensitization and endocytosis: Following stimulation, receptor internalization and negative feedback mechanisms reduce further responses; repeated low-level stimulation can lead to altered responsiveness.

Collectively, the balance of activating and inhibitory influences determines whether a given stimulus produces limited local symptoms, a late-phase inflammatory response, or fulminant systemic mediator release as in anaphylaxis.

# Laboratory detection of mediator release (tryptase, histamine, metabolites)

Accurate laboratory documentation of mast cell activation is important for diagnosing anaphylaxis and mast cell activation syndromes, for distinguishing immunologic from non-immunologic mechanisms, and for following patients with mastocytosis. Several mediators are clinically useful biomarkers; each has specific kinetics, specimen requirements and interpretive caveats.

Tryptase

Tryptase is the most widely used biomarker of mast cell activation because it is relatively specific to mast cells and stable compared with free histamine. Baseline (total) tryptase primarily reflects mast cell burden; elevated baseline tryptase may indicate increased mast cell numbers as in systemic mastocytosis. Typical reference ranges vary by lab, but a commonly used upper limit of normal is approximately 11.4 ng/mL. For suspected anaphylaxis, an increase in serum total tryptase drawn during or shortly after the event supports mast cell activation. The diagnostic threshold commonly used is an acute tryptase that exceeds baseline by the formula:

acute tryptase ≥ (1.2 × baseline tryptase) + 2 ng/mL.

Because tryptase peaks later than histamine—usually within 1–2 hours after symptom onset, and remains elevated for up to 6–8 hours—optimal sampling for diagnosis is: an acute specimen drawn as soon as possible within 1–3 hours after symptom onset (ideally within 30–120 minutes) and a baseline specimen ≥24 hours after resolution of symptoms (or at least 48 hours later) to establish baseline. Interpretation must consider that not all anaphylactic events produce large tryptase rises (e.g., many food-induced anaphylaxis episodes show small or absent tryptase elevations), and that baseline tryptase can be elevated for reasons other than mastocytosis (familial hypertryptasemia due to increased TPSAB1 copy number, renal impairment).

Histamine and metabolites

Plasma histamine rises very rapidly after degranulation but has a short half-life (minutes) and is labile, making it difficult to measure reliably in routine clinical practice. Proper specimen handling (immediate chilling, use of appropriate anticoagulants and rapid assay) is critical. Because of these challenges, measurement of stable histamine metabolites is often preferred.

Urinary N-methylhistamine (and other methylated metabolites) offers a more stable integrated measure of histamine release over hours. Elevated urinary N-methylhistamine in a timed or 24-hour urine collection supports mast cell activation; reference ranges depend on the assay and are usually normalized to creatinine. Similarly, 24-hour urinary prostaglandin D2 metabolite (11-beta-PGF2alpha) and leukotriene metabolites (urinary LTE4) can reflect mast cell mediator synthesis. Urinary LTE4 may be elevated in aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease and in severe anaphylaxis involving leukotriene production.

Other markers

Plasma or urinary levels of PAF and PAF acetylhydrolase have been investigated; low PAF acetylhydrolase activity has been associated in some series with severe anaphylaxis, but routine clinical assays are not widely available.

Laboratory sampling considerations

- Timing: Choose analytes with appropriate kinetics. Histamine requires immediate sampling (within minutes) and rapid processing. Tryptase has a wider window (1–6 hours). Urinary metabolites reflect integrated release over longer periods.

- Baseline comparisons: Interpretation often relies on comparison to a reliable baseline measurement obtained after recovery.

- Preanalytical handling: Many mediators are labile; follow lab instructions for collection tubes, temperature, and timing.

- Specificity: Elevated markers indicate mast cell activation but must be interpreted in context. Tryptase elevation is more specific for mast cell degranulation, whereas histamine can derive from other cells (e.g., basophils) and metabolites can be influenced by other conditions.

# Pharmacologic targets to block mediator actions

Therapeutic interventions in mast cell–mediated disease and anaphylaxis address three conceptual targets: immediate reversal of life-threatening physiologic derangements, blockade of mediator effects, and reduction of future activation or mediator production.

Immediate, life-saving therapy

- Epinephrine: The first-line treatment for systemic anaphylaxis. Epinephrine acts on α1-adrenergic receptors to counteract vasodilation and increase vascular tone, on β1 receptors to support cardiac output, and on β2 receptors to induce bronchodilation and reduce mediator release from mast cells. Intramuscular epinephrine (0.3–0.5 mg of 1:1000 solution in adults) into the anterolateral thigh is recommended urgently. Repeat dosing every 5–10 minutes may be necessary. Intravenous epinephrine is reserved for refractory or peri-arrest situations and requires careful titration.

Blocking mediator actions

- Antihistamines: H1 receptor antagonists (e.g., cetirizine, loratadine, diphenhydramine) blunt histamine-mediated pruritus, urticaria and some bronchoconstriction; they do not reverse airway obstruction or hypotension in severe anaphylaxis and are adjunctive therapies. H2 receptor antagonists (e.g., ranitidine, famotidine) can provide additional control of vasodilation and gastric acid-related symptoms when combined with H1 blockers, particularly for cutaneous and gastrointestinal manifestations.

- Bronchodilators: Inhaled short-acting β2-agonists (albuterol/salbutamol) provide symptomatic relief of bronchospasm. Systemic beta-agonists are not substitutes for epinephrine in anaphylaxis because they lack α-adrenergic vasoconstrictive effects.

- Leukotriene pathway inhibitors: Leukotriene receptor antagonists (e.g., montelukast acting at CysLT1 receptors) reduce leukotriene-mediated bronchoconstriction and are useful in chronic management of asthma and aspirin-sensitive disease. 5-LO inhibitors (e.g., zileuton) block leukotriene synthesis but have more limited clinical use due to monitoring requirements.

- Prostaglandin/COX modulation: Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) inhibit COX and reduce prostaglandin production, but their use is nuanced: in aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease, aspirin/NSAIDs can paradoxically worsen symptoms by shunting arachidonic acid toward leukotriene production. Selective prostaglandin receptor antagonists or CRTH2 antagonists are investigational.

- Corticosteroids: Systemic corticosteroids (e.g., prednisone) are used to prevent or reduce protracted or biphasic reactions via anti-inflammatory and transcriptional suppression of cytokine production. They have delayed onset and are not effective for acute reversal of airway compromise or hypotension. Typical practice in emergency management of anaphylaxis involves administration of corticosteroids (e.g., methylprednisolone) after stabilization to reduce risk of late-phase reactions.

- Antagonists of PAF: No widely available, proven clinical PAF antagonists exist for routine practice, though the pathway is of research interest because of associations between PAF and anaphylaxis severity.

Mast cell stabilization and receptor blockade

- Cromolyn sodium and other mast cell stabilizers: Cromolyn reduces mast cell degranulation by interfering with chloride channels and calcium influx; clinically it is used mainly for prophylaxis in allergic rhinitis and some forms of mast cell activation but has limited efficacy in acute settings.

- Anti-IgE therapy: Omalizumab, a monoclonal antibody that binds circulating IgE, reduces free IgE and downregulates FcεRI expression on mast cells and basophils; it is effective in allergic asthma and chronic spontaneous urticaria and can reduce the frequency of severe reactions in selected patients. It raises activation thresholds over weeks to months.

- Kinase inhibitors and targeted agents: Because KIT signaling is central to mast cell survival and proliferation, KIT inhibitors (imatinib, midostaurin, avapritinib) are used in neoplastic mast cell disorders (systemic mastocytosis) with specific mutation profiles. Small molecule inhibitors of signaling kinases (Syk inhibitors, BTK inhibitors) can reduce FcεRI-dependent activation and are under investigation for allergic and mast cell–driven diseases. Caution is warranted: some kinase inhibitors have off-target effects that can alter mediator release.

Supportive and adjunctive therapies

- Intravenous fluids and vasopressors: Aggressive intravascular volume resuscitation is often required because of increased vascular permeability and relative hypovolemia in anaphylaxis. Norepinephrine may be needed for refractory distributive shock.

- Anticoagulation considerations: Heparin released from mast cells can have anticoagulant effects; monitoring and management of coagulopathy depend on clinical context.

Special considerations and emerging therapies

- MRGPRX2 antagonists: Since some non-IgE drugs produce mast cell degranulation via MRGPRX2, antagonists to this receptor are a potential approach to prevent pseudoallergic reactions.

- Cytokine-targeted biologics: Agents that neutralize cytokines involved in mast cell priming or Th2 inflammation (anti-IL-5, anti-IL-4Rα) are effective in certain eosinophilic and allergic disorders and may indirectly reduce mast cell–driven pathology.

- Gene and molecular approaches: For hereditary or clonal mast cell disorders, therapies aimed at underlying molecular drivers (e.g., KIT D816V inhibitors) are increasingly available and tailored based on mutation testing.

Clinical decision points

- In suspected anaphylaxis: administer intramuscular epinephrine without delay; adjunctive therapies (oxygen, intravenous fluids, H1/H2 antihistamines, inhaled bronchodilators, corticosteroids) should not replace epinephrine.

- For recurrent mast cell activation without anaphylaxis: combine trigger avoidance, H1/H2 antihistamines, leukotriene receptor antagonists, and consider omalizumab or mast cell–directed agents if symptoms persist.

- In patients with elevated baseline tryptase or suspected mastocytosis: evaluate for systemic mast cell disease and consider hematology referral and KIT mutation testing, as treatment options differ.

Conclusion

Mast cells are multifunctional effector cells whose rapid release of preformed mediators and synthesis of potent lipid mediators and cytokines produce the clinical picture of immediate hypersensitivity and contribute to chronic allergic disease. Understanding mast cell subtypes, mediator profiles, anatomic localization, and the molecular regulation of activation is essential for interpreting clinical presentations from urticaria to life-threatening anaphylaxis, selecting appropriate laboratory tests (with their timing and limitations), and choosing targeted pharmacologic strategies. The expanding therapeutic armamentarium — from traditional antihistamines and epinephrine to monoclonal antibodies and kinase inhibitors — reflects growing insight into the signaling pathways that control mast cell biology.
