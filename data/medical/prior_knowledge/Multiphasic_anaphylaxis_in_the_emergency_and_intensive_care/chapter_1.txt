Introduction

Immediate hypersensitivity (Type I hypersensitivity) is an evolutionarily ancient immune program that protects against parasites but can cause pathologic responses to otherwise innocuous antigens (allergens). Clinically, Type I reactions manifest rapidly after allergen exposure and range from localized urticaria and allergic rhinitis to life‑threatening systemic anaphylaxis. Understanding the cellular players, antibody classes, receptor signalling, downstream mediators and amplifying pathways is essential to interpret clinical findings, choose diagnostic tests, and apply rational therapy in allergic disease. This chapter reviews the classification of hypersensitivity reactions with emphasis on Type I mechanisms, details the processes of sensitization and IgE biology, explicates mast cell and basophil FcεRI signalling and degranulation, describes complement and other amplifying inflammatory pathways, summarizes the key cytokines and chemokines that shape allergic inflammation, and links major mediators (histamine, tryptase, prostaglandins, leukotrienes, platelet‑activating factor) to their clinical effects.

# Classification of hypersensitivity reactions (Gell and Coombs) with emphasis on immediate (Type I) reactions

The Gell and Coombs classification organizes immune‑mediated tissue injury into four mechanistic types. This schema remains clinically useful for mapping immune mechanisms to presentations, although many clinical syndromes involve mixed mechanisms.

- Type I (Immediate, IgE‑mediated): Antigen‑specific IgE bound to high‑affinity Fc receptors on mast cells and basophils triggers rapid mediator release following allergen cross‑linking. Time course is minutes to hours. Examples: anaphylaxis, allergic asthma exacerbation, allergic rhinitis, urticaria, angioedema (some forms).
- Type II (Antibody‑mediated cytotoxicity): IgG or IgM antibodies directed against cell surface or matrix antigens lead to complement activation, opsonization, or ADCC. Examples: autoimmune hemolytic anemia, transfusion reactions, some drug‑induced cytopenias.
- Type III (Immune complex): Deposition of antigen–antibody complexes activates complement and neutrophil‑mediated inflammation (immune complex vasculitis). Examples: serum sickness, certain forms of glomerulonephritis.
- Type IV (Delayed‑type, T‑cell mediated): Antigen‑specific T lymphocytes and macrophages effect tissue injury over 48–72 hours. Subtypes include classical Th1 DTH and Th17/neutrophil‑driven responses; contact dermatitis and tuberculin reactions are classic examples.

Type I responses are distinguished by their dependence on IgE, tissue mast cells and blood basophils, and the very rapid onset owing to preformed mediators in granules as well as newly synthesized lipid mediators. Clinically, the rapidity of onset guides initial management (epinephrine for anaphylaxis) and the diagnostic approach (measurement of serum tryptase within hours, skin testing and specific IgE assays later).

# Role of IgE, antigen presentation and sensitisation

Sensitization describes the induction of allergen‑specific IgE and its loading onto effector cells before clinical reactions occur. The major steps include allergen uptake, antigen presentation, T helper differentiation toward a Th2 phenotype, B cell class switching to IgE, and binding of IgE to FcεRI on mast cells and basophils.

Allergen uptake and presentation: Epithelial barriers (skin, airway, gut) and resident antigen‑presenting cells (APCs)—dendritic cells, Langerhans cells—capture environmental proteins. The context of exposure (epithelial cytokines, protease activity of the allergen, co‑exposures such as adjuvants) shapes the immune outcome. APCs process proteins and present peptide fragments in the context of MHC class II to naive CD4+ T cells in secondary lymphoid organs.

T cell polarization to Th2: Several signals favor Th2 differentiation: IL‑4 is central in driving the Th2 program; epithelial‑derived cytokines such as thymic stromal lymphopoietin (TSLP), IL‑25 and IL‑33 promote dendritic cell conditioning that biases toward Th2 responses. Genetic predisposition, age, microbial exposures (the hygiene hypothesis), and the nature of the allergen influence this polarization.

B cell switching to IgE: Under the influence of Th2 cytokines—principally IL‑4 and IL‑13—B cells undergo class switch recombination to IgE. Interactions with CD40L on Th cells are required. The resulting plasma cells secrete allergen‑specific IgE into the circulation.

IgE binding and sensitization: Secreted IgE binds with very high affinity (Kd ~10^-10 M) to FcεRI on mast cells and basophils, effectively coating these cells with allergen‑specific receptors. FcεRI expression is upregulated by the presence of IgE, creating a feed‑forward amplification—higher serum IgE increases receptor density and cellular sensitivity. Tissue mast cells often remain long‑lived and can carry IgE for weeks to months.

Serologic testing and normal ranges: Total serum IgE has wide interindividual and age‑dependent variability; approximate “normal” adult ranges often quoted are <100–150 IU/mL, but values must be interpreted in clinical context. Allergen‑specific IgE tests (ImmunoCAP and others) report quantitative values and classes that correlate imperfectly with clinical reactivity; positive specific IgE indicates sensitization but not necessarily clinical allergy. Skin prick testing provides a functional in vivo readout of immediate hypersensitivity and is often more sensitive than serum tests, but both have roles depending on medication use and skin conditions.

# Mast cell and basophil activation: FcεRI cross‑linking and downstream signalling

The immediate effector response in Type I hypersensitivity is driven by degranulation of mast cells and basophils after cross‑linking of FcεRI by multivalent allergen. FcεRI is a tetrameric (αβγ2) receptor on mast cells and basophils (and trimeric αγ2 forms on other cells) with a high‑affinity IgE‑binding α chain and signaling subunits containing immunoreceptor tyrosine‑based activation motifs (ITAMs).

Receptor cross‑linking and early phosphorylation events: When allergen simultaneously binds two or more IgE molecules fixed to FcεRI, receptors are brought into close proximity and ITAMs are phosphorylated by Src family kinases—primarily Lyn. Phosphorylated ITAMs recruit Syk tyrosine kinase, a critical proximal signaling enzyme. Syk amplification is required for downstream propagation of the activation signal.

Signalosomes and adapter proteins: Activated Syk phosphorylates scaffolding and adapter proteins such as LAT (linker for activation of T cells) and SLP‑76, organizing multiprotein complexes that recruit enzymes including phospholipase Cγ (PLCγ) and phosphoinositide 3‑kinase (PI3K). These complexes coordinate calcium mobilization, actin cytoskeleton remodeling, and vesicle trafficking.

Second messenger cascades: PLCγ hydrolyzes phosphatidylinositol 4,5‑bisphosphate to generate diacylglycerol (DAG) and inositol 1,4,5‑trisphosphate (IP3). IP3 drives release of calcium from endoplasmic reticulum stores, followed by store‑operated calcium entry through channels such as Orai1, producing sustained intracellular calcium elevation. Calcium and DAG together activate protein kinase C (PKC) isoforms and other calcium‑dependent enzymes that orchestrate exocytosis of preformed granule contents.

Degranulation: The rise in intracellular calcium and cytoskeletal changes promote fusion of secretory granules with the plasma membrane, releasing preformed mediators such as histamine, proteases (including tryptase and chymase), and heparin within minutes. Degranulation is a regulated, energy‑dependent process mediated by SNARE proteins and other fusion machinery.

De novo synthesis of lipid mediators and cytokines: Parallel to rapid degranulation, activation of PLA2 (phospholipase A2) liberates arachidonic acid for conversion into prostaglandins (via cyclooxygenases) and leukotrienes (via 5‑lipoxygenase). Gene transcription pathways (MAPK, NF‑κB, and AP‑1) induce synthesis and release of cytokines and chemokines over hours.

Regulatory receptors and negative signals: FcεRI signaling is modulated by co‑receptors and inhibitory pathways. FcγRIIb (an inhibitory IgG Fc receptor containing ITIM motifs) delivers negative signals when co‑aggregated, reducing degranulation. Other receptors—such as Siglecs, CD200R, and inhibitory cytokine receptors—can attenuate responses. Pharmacologic agents (e.g., omalizumab) reduce free IgE, downregulate FcεRI expression, and blunt cellular responsiveness.

Differences between mast cells and basophils: Mast cells are tissue resident, long‑lived, and heterogeneous (mucosal vs connective tissue types) with differential mediator content and receptor expression. Basophils circulate and are recruited to tissues; they also express FcεRI and can release histamine, LTC4, and Th2‑polarizing cytokines (notably IL‑4) rapidly upon activation. Both cell types contribute to immediate and late phase allergic responses.

# Complement and other amplifying inflammatory pathways

Complement can amplify Type I hypersensitivity though it is not typically the primary initiating mechanism. Key complement fragments—C3a and C5a, termed anaphylatoxins—bind to their G protein–coupled receptors on mast cells, basophils, neutrophils and other immune cells to enhance mediator release and leukocyte recruitment.

Anaphylatoxins as co‑activators: C3a and C5a can induce direct degranulation of mast cells and basophils or synergize with FcεRI signaling to lower the threshold for activation. For example, C5a is a potent chemoattractant and activator of neutrophils and a contributor to vasodilation and increased microvascular permeability in inflammatory foci.

Alternative complement activation: Immune complex formation (Type III mechanisms) or pathogen‑triggered complement activation can generate anaphylatoxins that potentiate allergic inflammation. Complement activation products also upregulate adhesion molecules on endothelium, promoting leukocyte extravasation into allergen‑exposed tissues.

Coagulation and kinin systems: Other proteolytic cascades (coagulation cascade, kallikrein‑kinin system) intersect with allergic pathways. Bradykinin increases vascular permeability and pain; it contributes to angioedema in hereditary or ACE inhibitor–associated contexts. Platelet‑activating factor (PAF) is generated by multiple cell types and contributes both to bronchospasm and vascular collapse in severe anaphylaxis.

Innate immune amplifiers: Pattern recognition receptors, alarmins (TSLP, IL‑33), and epithelial cell–derived chemotactic factors amplify type 2 inflammation and skew the microenvironment toward allergic responses. These innate signals can sustain and perpetuate the response beyond the immediate phase.

# Cytokines and chemokines in allergic inflammation

A Th2‑skewed cytokine milieu defines the adaptive arm of allergic immunity; innate cytokines from barrier cells further condition the response. The principal cytokines and their effects are:

- IL‑4: Drives naive CD4+ T cell differentiation into Th2 cells and mediates B cell class switching to IgE. IL‑4 promotes expression of vascular cell adhesion molecules and contributes to mucus production.
- IL‑13: Shares many functions with IL‑4 (via overlapping receptor components) and is particularly important for airway hyperresponsiveness, goblet cell metaplasia, mucus hypersecretion, and tissue remodeling.
- IL‑5: Key eosinophil growth, differentiation, activation and survival factor. IL‑5–mediated eosinophilia contributes to tissue damage in asthma, eosinophilic esophagitis, and other eosinophilic disorders.
- IL‑3 and GM‑CSF: Promote growth and activation of basophils, eosinophils, and mast cells.
- TNF‑α: Produced by mast cells and other cells; increases endothelial activation and sustains inflammation.
- IL‑9: Supports mast cell proliferation and mucosal mast cell expansion.
- TSLP, IL‑25, IL‑33: Epithelial‑derived “alarmins” that activate type 2 innate lymphoid cells (ILC2s) and condition dendritic cells to drive Th2 polarization. They are central to the bridge between barrier dysfunction and type 2 immunity.

Chemokines direct cellular recruitment and positioning. Eotaxins (CCL11, CCL24, CCL26) selectively recruit eosinophils via CCR3. CCL17 (TARC) and CCL22 recruit Th2 cells via CCR4. CXCL8 (IL‑8) attracts neutrophils in mixed inflammatory patterns.

Late‑phase response and chronicity: The immediate degranulation phase is followed hours later by a late phase driven by newly synthesized cytokines and chemokines, leading to cellular infiltration (eosinophils, basophils, Th2 lymphocytes) and sustained tissue inflammation. Chronic exposure produces remodeling: subepithelial fibrosis, smooth muscle hypertrophy/hyperplasia, and persistent mucus production, particularly relevant in chronic asthma.

Therapeutic targeting: Biological therapies exploit these cytokine pathways—anti‑IL‑5 (mepolizumab, reslizumab), anti‑IL‑5Rα (benralizumab) for eosinophilic disease; anti‑IL‑4Rα (dupilumab) blocks IL‑4/IL‑13 signaling and reduces IgE production and mucus; anti‑TSLP and anti‑IL‑33 therapies are under evaluation. Understanding which cytokines predominate in a patient’s phenotype guides therapy selection.

# Clinical correlates of mediator effects (histamine, tryptase, prostaglandins, leukotrienes, platelet‑activating factor)

The clinical manifestations of immediate hypersensitivity reflect the cumulative effects of preformed granule mediators and newly synthesized lipid mediators and cytokines.

Histamine: A small, rapidly acting amine stored in mast cell and basophil granules. Histamine acts through at least four G protein–coupled receptors:

- H1 receptor: Mediates bronchoconstriction, increased vascular permeability, endothelial contraction (wheal and flare), pruritus via nerve endings, and vasodilation. H1 antagonists (antihistamines) are effective for urticaria, allergic rhinitis and itch but provide limited protection in systemic anaphylaxis.
- H2 receptor: Involved in gastric acid secretion and some vasodilatory effects. H2 blockers historically used adjunctively in anaphylaxis.
- H3 and H4 receptors: Modulate neuronal transmission and immune cell chemotaxis; H4 is implicated in eosinophil and mast cell recruitment and pruritus.

Clinically, histamine is responsible for the immediate wheal and flare seen on skin testing, nasal congestion, pruritus, and bronchospasm to a degree. Antihistamines mitigate many cutaneous symptoms but do not reverse airway obstruction or hypotension in severe anaphylaxis—epinephrine is required.

Tryptase and proteases: Tryptase (a tetrameric serine protease) is one of the most specific markers of mast cell activation. Baseline serum tryptase varies by individual; a commonly used upper limit is approximately 11.4 ng/mL, though reference ranges vary by assay and population. In systemic anaphylaxis, tryptase typically rises within 1–3 hours of symptom onset and peaks around 1–6 hours; a suggested criterion for significant mast cell activation is an increase of acute tryptase over baseline by at least 20% + 2 ng/mL (i.e., acute ≥ baseline × 1.2 + 2 ng/mL). Elevated tryptase supports, but does not definitively prove, mast cell degranulation as the cause of acute events, since tryptase can be normal in food‑triggered anaphylaxis and other contexts. Tryptase and other proteases (chymase) have local tissue effects—matrix remodeling, activation of protease‑activated receptors, and promotion of inflammation.

Prostaglandins: Mast cells produce prostaglandin D2 (PGD2) via cyclooxygenase pathway—PGD2 is a bronchoconstrictor, vasodilator and chemoattractant for Th2 cells and eosinophils. PGD2 metabolites correlate with mast cell activation and are implicated in airway narrowing in asthma. Cyclooxygenase inhibitors (NSAIDs) can alter prostaglandin balance and, paradoxically, worsen certain types of urticaria or trigger aspirin‑exacerbated respiratory disease by shunting arachidonic acid to leukotriene pathways.

Leukotrienes: Cysteinyl leukotrienes (LTC4, LTD4, LTE4) are powerful and slow‑acting bronchoconstrictors, increase vascular permeability and promote mucus secretion. They are synthesized via 5‑lipoxygenase rapidly after activation and contribute to prolonged bronchospasm that often responds to leukotriene receptor antagonists (montelukast) or 5‑lipoxygenase inhibitors. The relative persistence and potency of leukotrienes make them major mediators in asthma exacerbations and some systemic allergic responses.

Platelet‑activating factor (PAF): PAF is a potent phospholipid mediator produced by mast cells, basophils, neutrophils, platelets and endothelial cells. PAF induces bronchoconstriction, vasodilation, increased vascular permeability and platelet aggregation. High PAF levels correlate with severity of anaphylaxis; PAF acetylhydrolase (the enzyme that degrades PAF) deficiency or low activity has been associated with more severe anaphylaxis in some studies. PAF is also implicated in cardiovascular collapse during severe reactions.

Temporal phases and organ effects: The immediate phase (minutes) is dominated by histamine and preformed mediators causing urticaria, angioedema, bronchospasm and hypotension. The late phase (hours) involves leukotrienes, prostaglandins, cytokines and recruited inflammatory cells causing sustained airway inflammation, tissue swelling and sometimes biphasic anaphylactic phenomena. Organ‑specific effects include:

- Skin: Urticaria (wheals) from localized vasodilation and increased permeability; pruritus from histamine and neuropeptides. Angioedema reflects deeper dermal/subcutaneous vascular leakage.
- Respiratory tract: Upper airway edema and bronchospasm produce stridor, wheeze and airflow obstruction. Mucus hypersecretion is driven by IL‑13 and leukotrienes.
- Cardiovascular: Vasodilation, increased vascular permeability and reduced venous return lead to hypotension and shock. Myocardial depressant effects of mediators and mediator‑induced arrhythmias can occur.
- Gastrointestinal: Smooth muscle contraction causes cramping, vomiting and diarrhea.

Diagnostic implications and laboratory use: Serum tryptase measurement is helpful when obtained appropriately (ideally within 1–3 hours of symptom onset, with a paired baseline sample). Measurement of allergen‑specific IgE and skin testing (prick or intradermal) are useful for identifying sensitization to suspected allergens once the patient is stable. Pulmonary function testing with bronchoprovocation may reveal airway hyperresponsiveness in asthma. Biomarkers like fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO) reflect eosinophilic airway inflammation driven by IL‑13.

Conclusion

Type I hypersensitivity is a multi‑step process that begins with sensitization and IgE production and culminates in rapid effector responses mediated by mast cells and basophils. FcεRI signalling, downstream calcium and kinase cascades, and the generation of preformed and newly synthesized mediators produce the characteristic clinical spectrum—from cutaneous manifestations to life‑threatening anaphylaxis. Complement and other innate pathways amplify responses while cytokines and chemokines orchestrate late‑phase inflammation and chronic remodeling. Recognizing mediator‑specific effects guides acute management (epinephrine for anaphylaxis; antihistamines and inhaled bronchodilators for milder symptoms) and informs targeted chronic therapies (anti‑IgE, anti‑IL‑5, anti‑IL‑4/13, leukotriene modifiers). A mechanistic understanding aids appropriate diagnostic testing, risk stratification and personalized therapeutic strategies in allergic disease.
