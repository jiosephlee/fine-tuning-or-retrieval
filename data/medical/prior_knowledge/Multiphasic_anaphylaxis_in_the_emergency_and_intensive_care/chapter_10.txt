Introduction

Drug hypersensitivity reactions encompass a wide spectrum of immune-mediated and non–immune-mediated adverse events related to medications. Correct classification and diagnosis are critical: mislabeling a patient as “allergic” can lead to use of less effective or more toxic antibiotics, increased healthcare costs, and risk of antimicrobial resistance, while failure to recognize a true immunologic drug allergy can lead to life‑threatening rechallenge. This chapter provides a practical, clinically oriented overview of the principles and methodologies used to diagnose both immediate (typically IgE-mediated) and delayed (T cell–mediated and other) drug hypersensitivity. Emphasis is placed on skin testing techniques, in vitro serologic assays, drug provocation testing (DPT), approaches to common problems such as beta‑lactam allergy, and when to defer or refer to an allergy specialist. Throughout, we relate pathophysiology to the timing and pattern of clinical findings to guide decision making and patient safety.

# Types of drug hypersensitivity (immediate vs delayed) and mechanisms

Drug hypersensitivity reactions are commonly categorized by timing and underlying immunopathology. Clinically useful divisions separate immediate reactions—those occurring within minutes to a few hours of drug exposure—from delayed reactions that appear hours to weeks later. Understanding mechanism helps determine which diagnostic tests are most informative and what interventions are safe.

Immediate hypersensitivity
Immediate reactions usually occur within minutes to 1–6 hours of drug administration and classically include urticaria, angioedema, bronchospasm, hypotension, and anaphylaxis. Many immediate reactions are mediated by drug‑specific IgE bound to high‑affinity FcεRI receptors on mast cells and basophils. Cross‑linking of these IgE molecules by the offending drug or a drug‑carrier complex triggers rapid degranulation and release of histamine, tryptase, prostaglandins, and leukotrienes.

Several nuances are important. Some immediate reactions are non‑IgE, nonimmunologic histamine release (“anaphylactoid”) due to direct mast cell activation (e.g., radiocontrast media, opiates, vancomycin‑related “red man” reaction) or complement activation (e.g., certain lipid formulations). These mimic IgE‑mediated anaphylaxis clinically but are unlikely to show specific IgE or positive skin tests.

Laboratory correlates include transiently elevated serum tryptase measured within 1–4 hours of event; values >11.4 ng/mL are commonly used as a threshold for concern, and a rise of at least 20% over baseline plus 2 ng/mL is often used to support mast cell activation. Serum-specific IgE testing and in vivo skin testing may identify drug-specific IgE when available.

Delayed hypersensitivity
Delayed reactions appear more than 1 hour after exposure, often days to weeks later. They are predominantly T cell–mediated and present as maculopapular exanthems, fixed drug eruptions, drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms (DRESS), Stevens–Johnson syndrome (SJS)/toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN), and various organ-specific syndromes (drug‑induced liver injury, interstitial nephritis). The underlying mechanisms generally involve drug or drug‑metabolite acting as a hapten to form peptides presented by HLA molecules, or direct pharmacologic interaction of the drug with immune receptors (the p–i concept), activating drug‑specific CD4+ or CD8+ T cells that drive inflammation and tissue damage.

Genetic risk factors have strong roles for many severe delayed reactions; HLA‑allele associations (e.g., HLA‑B*57:01 and abacavir hypersensitivity; HLA‑B*15:02 and carbamazepine SJS/TEN in some Asian populations) can be screened to prevent reactions. Patch testing and late intradermal readings can sometimes detect T cell–mediated sensitization, but sensitivity varies by syndrome and drug. Routine serologic IgE assays are generally not useful for delayed reactions.

Temporal patterns and phenotypes guide testing strategy. Immediate, anaphylaxis‑type reactions warrant evaluation for drug‑specific IgE and consideration of skin testing; delayed exanthems often require different approaches, including history‑driven avoidance, possible patch testing, and cautious graded challenge under specialist supervision when appropriate.

# Skin prick testing and intradermal testing: methodology, interpretation and safety considerations

Skin testing is a cornerstone for diagnosing IgE‑mediated drug allergy when standardized reagents are available (e.g., penicillins). There are two principal techniques: skin prick testing (SPT) and intradermal testing (IDT). SPT is preferred as the initial test because it is less sensitive but more specific and safer; IDT is more sensitive but carries greater risk of systemic reaction and false positives from irritant concentrations.

Methodology
Skin prick testing involves placing a drop of test solution on the volar forearm (or back) and introducing the allergen into the epidermis by a superficial prick. A positive control (histamine, typically 10 mg/mL or 1 mg/mL depending on local practice) and negative control (saline or diluent) must always be included. Reactions are read at 15–20 minutes; a wheal diameter of ≥3 mm more than the negative control is commonly considered positive. SPT solutions for many drugs are not commercially standardized; for penicillin, benzylpenicilloyl polylysine (penicilloyl‑major determinant, PPL) and minor determinant mix are used where available, plus the native drug (e.g., amoxicillin).

Intradermal testing involves injecting a small volume (usually 0.02–0.05 mL) of diluted drug solution intradermally to produce a small bleb. IDT is read at 20 minutes for immediate hypersensitivity; an increase in wheal diameter of ≥3 mm over the initial bleb size (and larger than the negative control) is considered positive. For delayed hypersensitivity, IDT sites can be read at 24–48–72 hours for induration and erythema reflecting T cell responses.

Test concentrations
Appropriate, non‑irritant concentrations are essential to avoid false positives. For penicillins, validated concentrations exist for PPL and minor determinant mix. For other drugs, recommended non‑irritant concentrations have been published, but availability varies. As an example, intradermal testing concentrations that are likely non‑irritant for common antibiotics are often in the range of 1:1000 to 1:10 dilution of the therapeutic solution, but empirical use without validated data risks false‑positive irritant results or false negatives if overly diluted. For many drugs (e.g., cephalosporins, macrolides, quinolones), both SPT and IDT concentrations and predictive values are less well standardized.

Interpretation
A positive SPT or IDT supports IgE‑mediated sensitization and increases the pretest probability that the drug caused the immediate hypersensitivity. A negative test reduces probability but does not eliminate risk—negative predictive value depends on the test sensitivity for the specific drug and on prevalence in the tested population. For penicillin, combined SPT and IDT using PPL and minor determinant mix historically have high negative predictive value (>95%) for immediate reactions; therefore, a negative test paired with negative specific IgE generally permits a graded oral challenge in many settings. For other drugs, negative skin testing is less reassuring.

False positives can occur from irritant effects at high concentrations or from dermographism. False negatives can result when testing is performed too long after the index reaction (IgE levels wane over time—sensitivity decreases with increasing interval), if drug metabolites (rather than parent drug) are the antigenic target, or if the test lacks sensitivity for that drug.

Safety considerations
Skin testing carries risk of systemic allergic reactions and, rarely, anaphylaxis. IDT is higher risk than SPT. Always have emergency resuscitation equipment and staff trained in anaphylaxis management available. Begin with SPT and proceed to IDT only when needed and when benefits outweigh risks. For patients with recent severe anaphylaxis or unstable cardiovascular/respiratory status, defer skin testing until stabilized and consider alternative evaluation strategies or specialist referral. For patients on antihistamines, hold H1 antihistamines for at least 5–7 half‑lives (commonly 48–72 hours; for longer‑acting agents, longer) to avoid false negatives; topical and systemic steroids have less clear impact on immediate skin testing but may blunt reactions when used in high doses.

Documentation should include lot numbers/concentrations of reagents, sites and sizes of wheals/erythema, time of readings, and patient consent after counseling about risks. For delayed readings, ensure patients can be followed or return for later assessment.

# Serologic testing (specific IgE) for drugs and other allergens: utility and limitations

In vitro serologic assays measuring drug‑specific IgE (e.g., ImmunoCAP, RAST) are available for a limited number of drugs (notably penicillins, some cephalosporins, and a few others). Additionally, component‑resolved diagnostics and basophil activation tests (BAT) expand the in vitro toolkit. Each modality has strengths and limitations that inform clinical use.

Specific IgE assays
Serologic specific IgE testing is non‑invasive and safer than in vivo testing in patients with high‑risk histories. For penicillin, validated specific IgE assays exist; a positive result indicates circulating drug‑specific IgE and supports the diagnosis of IgE‑mediated hypersensitivity. However, sensitivity is imperfect; a negative test does not reliably exclude allergy, particularly as specific IgE levels fall over time. For penicillin, combined skin testing and specific IgE testing provide better negative predictive value than either alone.

Performance characteristics vary widely by drug and assay. Reported sensitivities for drug-specific IgE range from low to moderate (often 30–70%) and specificities from moderate to high. Positive predictive value depends on the pretest probability. Because specific IgE assays for many drugs are unavailable, their absence does not imply lack of IgE‑mediated mechanism.

Basophil activation test (BAT)
BAT measures basophil activation markers (e.g., CD63, CD203c) by flow cytometry after in vitro exposure to the suspect drug. BAT can be useful when skin testing is negative or not feasible, especially for immediate reactions and some non‑standard drugs. Sensitivity and specificity vary by agent and laboratory; BAT is technically demanding, not widely available, and requires fresh samples. BAT may be particularly helpful for drugs that cause non‑IgE but IgE‑independent basophil activation or where metabolites are implicated if appropriate conditions are used.

Other serologic tests and biomarkers
Tryptase measurement during or soon after suspected anaphylaxis can document mast cell degranulation; reference range is assay‑dependent, but many labs use <11.4 ng/mL as an upper limit of normal. A rise from baseline (collected after recovery) of at least 20% + 2 ng/mL supports mast cell activation. For delayed reactions, specific lymphocyte transformation tests (LTT) and ex vivo cytokine assays can sometimes identify drug‑reactive T cells; however, LTT sensitivity is variable and requires specialized labs.

Limitations and clinical application
In vitro tests rarely stand alone. A positive specific IgE or BAT increases posttest probability sufficiently to avoid provocation in many settings, but false positives exist. A negative in vitro test does not reliably exclude immediate hypersensitivity—especially for agents without well‑validated assays. Timing matters: assays are most sensitive when performed relatively near the time of reaction; IgE levels may decline over months to years. Pretest probability informed by history guides test choice—high‑risk recent anaphylaxis may warrant avoidance or specialist evaluation even if tests are negative.

In sum, serologic testing is a valuable adjunct to skin testing and clinical history, particularly when in vivo testing is contraindicated or when reagents for skin testing are unavailable. Availability, turnaround time, and test performance characteristics should inform ordering and interpretation.

# Drug challenge/provocation testing: indications, contraindications and safety precautions

Drug provocation testing (DPT), also called graded challenge or drug challenge, is the definitive diagnostic tool to determine whether a patient tolerates a drug. It involves administering the suspect drug under controlled conditions and observing for reaction. DPT is used to confirm or exclude hypersensitivity when other testing is inconclusive or unavailable and when the risk of conservative management (i.e., avoiding the drug) would harm the patient. Because it deliberately exposes the patient, careful selection, planning, and safety measures are essential.

Indications
DPT is indicated when the clinical history is ambiguous or suggests low probability, skin testing and serology are negative or unavailable, and the drug has significant therapeutic importance (e.g., first‑line antibiotic) or alternatives are less desirable. It is appropriate to confirm tolerance in patients with remote histories of benign childhood rashes or nonspecific reactions. For patients labeled penicillin‑allergic with a low‑risk history and negative skin tests, an oral challenge is often performed to delabel.

Contraindications
DPT is contraindicated in patients with prior severe cutaneous adverse reactions (SCARs) such as SJS/TEN, DRESS, acute generalized exanthematous pustulosis (AGEP) with systemic involvement, or severe organ‑specific drug reactions (e.g., drug‑induced liver injury, interstitial nephritis) attributable to the drug in question. DPT should not be performed in patients with recent severe anaphylaxis with unstable control, pregnant patients (unless benefits outweigh risks and under specialist care), or in settings without immediate resuscitation and intensive monitoring facilities. For reactions where cross‑reactivity is likely and testing reagents for alternative agents are lacking, perform DPT only under specialist supervision.

Protocol and safety precautions
A DPT protocol is individualized to the drug, route of administration, and clinical context, but general principles apply. The challenge is performed in a monitored clinical environment with immediate access to resuscitation equipment, medications for anaphylaxis (epinephrine, oxygen, antihistamines, corticosteroids), and personnel trained in emergency response. Baseline assessment includes vital signs, review of comorbidities (cardiovascular, pulmonary), pregnancy test when applicable, and withholding medications that could mask or amplify reactions (e.g., beta‑blockers increase risk/severity of anaphylaxis and blunt epinephrine response; consider temporary adjustment in consultation with the prescribing physician).

Graded dosing typically starts with a very small fraction of the therapeutic dose (e.g., 1/100 to 1/10) and escalates at 30–60 minute intervals to the full therapeutic dose, with observation periods tailored to the suspected reaction pattern. For immediate reaction evaluation, serial doses with observation over several hours after the final dose are common. For delayed reactions, prolonged observation or ambulatory follow‑up for days is required; patch tests or delayed intradermal tests may be attempted before DPT for T cell–mediated patterns.

Stopping rules must be predefined: any objective signs of reaction (urticaria, respiratory symptoms, hypotension, gastrointestinal symptoms, significant erythema or mucosal involvement) warrant immediate cessation and treatment. Access to epinephrine and trained staff is mandatory. After a negative DPT, patients should be observed for a standard period (often 2 hours for immediate reactions) and educated about the residual, though low, risk of later reactions.

Decision points
Deciding whether to perform a DPT balances the pretest probability of allergy, severity of prior reactions, alternative therapy risks, and facility capability. In low‑risk histories (e.g., isolated non‑urticarial maculopapular rash in childhood, unclear timing), many centers favor DPT without prior skin testing to reduce unnecessary labeling. In high‑risk histories for IgE‑mediated reactions, skin testing and serology may precede DPT to reduce risk. For drugs with high lethality if the allergy is true (e.g., suspected penicillin‑mediated anaphylaxis), specialist oversight and staged approaches are warranted.

# Approach to antibiotic allergy (beta-lactam testing algorithms, cross-reactivity concepts)

Antibiotic allergy, and specifically penicillin allergy, is one of the most common and clinically consequential labels. The approach combines careful history, targeted skin and serologic testing when available, and selective DPT to remove inaccurate labels and allow use of first‑line agents.

Clinical assessment
Begin with a structured history: the implicated antibiotic, timing of reaction after exposure, symptom type (urticaria, angioedema, respiratory compromise, anaphylaxis, maculopapular rash), timing of the original event relative to present (memory fades and IgE wanes), number of exposures since the reaction, and presence of comorbidities (e.g., severe asthma). Classify the reaction as immediate (within 1–6 hours) or delayed. Many patients labeled “penicillin‑allergic” report distant mild rashes in childhood; these often do not represent persistent IgE‑mediated allergy.

Penicillin testing algorithm
For patients with histories suggesting immediate hypersensitivity to penicillins or recent reactions, perform penicillin skin testing where available using major (benzylpenicilloyl) and minor determinant reagents plus testing with the implicated penicillin (e.g., amoxicillin). A positive skin test, or positive specific IgE to penicillin, suggests avoidance of penicillins and related aminopenicillins. A negative skin test substantially lowers the probability of immediate allergy; many centers then perform an oral challenge (single‑dose or graded) to confirm tolerance. For patients with low‑risk histories (remote, non‑urticarial rash), some protocols proceed directly to oral challenge without prior skin testing.

Cross‑reactivity concepts
Understanding chemical structure explains cross‑reactivity patterns. Historically, high rates of cross‑reactivity between penicillins and cephalosporins were reported, but modern data show much lower risk, largely related to shared side‑chain (R group) structures rather than the beta‑lactam core itself. Cross‑reactivity risk between penicillins and later‑generation cephalosporins is small (<1%) when side chains differ; risk is higher when side chains are identical or similar (e.g., ampicillin/cephalexin, amoxicillin/cefadroxil share similar aminobenzyl side chains). Carbapenems carry low cross‑reactivity with penicillins (historical estimates up to 10% have been revised down to <1%–3% in many studies), and aztreonam has very low cross‑reactivity except when sharing side‑chain similarity with a specific cephalosporin (e.g., aztreonam and ceftazidime share an identical side chain).

Testing alternatives
If penicillin skin testing is negative but a cephalosporin is needed, consider the following: for low‑risk patients, administration of the cephalosporin may proceed without skin testing or with a single‑dose challenge. For high‑risk patients or when side chain similarity raises concern, perform cephalosporin skin testing if validated reagents exist, or do a graded challenge under supervision. If severe initial reaction occurred (anaphylaxis or SCAR), avoid related agents and refer to allergy specialist for testing considerations and alternatives.

Delabeling strategies
Because most patients labeled penicillin‑allergic are not truly allergic, structured algorithms for delabeling often use risk stratification: low‑risk histories proceed to direct oral challenge; intermediate risk undergo skin testing followed by challenge if negative; high‑risk patients are referred. Many institutions have implemented stewardship programs incorporating penicillin skin testing and DPT to reduce inappropriate use of alternative broad‑spectrum antibiotics.

Practical considerations
Availability of penicillin determinants and expertise varies. When determinants are unavailable, testing with the native penicillins (amoxicillin or benzylpenicillin) may be used with caveats. Documentation of testing results and clear communication to patients and primary clinicians are key: after a negative test and successful challenge, the allergy label should be removed from electronic records and the patient given a written statement of tolerance.

# When to defer testing and referral pathways to allergy specialists

Not every suspected drug hypersensitivity requires immediate testing, and there are clear situations where testing should be deferred or patients referred to an allergy specialist. Decisions should prioritize patient safety and diagnostic yield.

When to defer testing
Defer skin testing or DPT if the patient is within the acute phase of the reaction and unstable, if they have active severe cutaneous adverse reactions (SJS/TEN, DRESS, AGEP), or if they have significant comorbidities that would increase risk (cardiovascular instability, uncontrolled asthma). For immediate hypersensitivity, allow a washout period after the acute event before skin testing in some instances because IgE levels may be temporarily depleted (although waiting too long may reduce sensitivity as IgE wanes); typical practice is to perform testing at least 4–6 weeks after recovery from the acute reaction but within a timeframe where testing remains informative (often within months to a few years). For DPT, defer until the reaction has resolved and the patient is clinically well; for severe prior reactions, DPT is usually contraindicated.

Referral criteria
Refer to an allergy specialist when the history suggests high risk (anaphylaxis, SJS/TEN, DRESS), when testing reagents or expertise is not available locally, when the drug is essential and alternatives are limited, or when interpretation of tests is uncertain. Specialists are necessary for complex evaluations—e.g., suspected multiple drug hypersensitivities, reactions involving multiple organ systems, need for desensitization protocols, or when genetic testing (HLA typing) and interpretation is required.

Allergy specialist capabilities
Allergists/immunologists provide comprehensive assessment including detailed history, targeted skin and in vitro testing (including BAT, LTT if available), supervised DPTs, and desensitization when needed for patients who require a drug to which they are sensitized (e.g., for chemotherapy, transplant prophylaxis, or life‑threatening infections). Specialists can coordinate genetic screening (e.g., HLA‑B*57:01 before abacavir) and advise on cross‑reactivity and alternative prescribing. They also manage complex cases of chronic urticaria or multiple drug reactions where nonimmediate mechanisms are suspected.

System pathways and documentation
Establish local referral pathways so that primary teams know when and how to refer. For common scenarios such as inpatient penicillin delabeling, many centers have rapid access clinics or consult services to expedite testing and DPT. Clear documentation of test results, rationale for deferral, and follow‑up plans is essential. When testing is deferred, provide patients and clinicians with interim management advice and an action plan in case of inadvertent re‑exposure.

Conclusion

Accurate diagnosis of drug hypersensitivity requires integration of pathophysiology, timing and phenotype of reactions, and the strengths and limitations of available diagnostic tools. Immediate, IgE‑mediated reactions are best evaluated with history, skin testing, specific IgE, and—when safe—drug challenge. Delayed, T cell–mediated reactions demand careful risk stratification, use of patch testing and delayed intradermal readings in selected situations, and avoidance of provocation when severe cutaneous or systemic reactions have occurred. Penicillin allergy algorithms focusing on risk stratification, skin testing, and targeted challenges can safely delabel many patients, improving antibiotic stewardship. Drug provocation testing is the diagnostic gold standard but carries risk and must be used judiciously in appropriate settings with resuscitation capabilities. When testing is contraindicated or complex, timely referral to an allergy specialist is necessary. Clear documentation and communication ensure that testing results change future prescribing and improve patient safety.
