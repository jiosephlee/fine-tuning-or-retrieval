Introduction

Anaphylaxis is an acute, potentially life‑threatening systemic hypersensitivity reaction that evolves rapidly and requires prompt recognition and treatment. For clinicians in the emergency department, hospital wards, outpatient clinics, or prehospital settings, the ability to identify both typical and atypical presentations, rapidly assess severity, institute early resuscitation, and make triage decisions is essential to preventing morbidity and mortality. This chapter reviews the diagnostic criteria and organ‑system manifestations of anaphylaxis, describes common and atypical clinical features, explores severity grading and triage principles, outlines immediate monitoring and documentation priorities, and lists the major alternative diagnoses to consider. The emphasis is on translating pathophysiology into bedside clinical reasoning so that early life‑saving decisions (particularly administration of intramuscular epinephrine and airway management) are made without delay.

# Diagnostic criteria for anaphylaxis

Diagnosis of anaphylaxis is primarily clinical. Several internationally accepted definitions and criteria exist; the most widely used is the 2005 NIAID/FAAN clinical criteria that were designed to maximize sensitivity for early identification. According to those criteria, anaphylaxis is highly likely when any one of the following three clinical scenarios occurs minutes to hours after exposure to a likely allergen:

1. Acute onset (minutes to several hours) of skin, mucosal, or both skin and mucosal tissue involvement (e.g., generalized hives, pruritus, flushing, swollen lips, tongue, uvula) AND at least one of the following:
   - Respiratory compromise: dyspnea, wheeze, bronchospasm, stridor, hypoxemia.
   - Reduced blood pressure (BP) or associated symptoms of end‑organ dysfunction: hypotonia (collapse), syncope, incontinence.

2. Two or more of the following that occur rapidly after exposure to a likely allergen:
   - Involvement of the skin or mucosa,
   - Respiratory compromise,
   - Reduced blood pressure or associated symptoms,
   - Persistent gastrointestinal symptoms (crampy abdominal pain, vomiting).

3. Reduced blood pressure after exposure to a known allergen for that patient:
   - Infants and children: low systolic blood pressure (age‑specific) or a greater than 30% decrease from baseline.
   - Adults: systolic blood pressure (SBP) less than 90 mm Hg or greater than a 30% decrease from that person’s baseline.

Key elements embedded in these criteria: the multisystem nature of the reaction (skin plus respiratory, circulatory, or GI symptoms), the rapid timing after exposure, and the recognition that severe cardiovascular collapse may occur with minimal cutaneous signs. Clinicians should not require urticaria to be present to make the diagnosis.

Organ‑system features to elicit in the diagnostic assessment

- Airway: sensation of throat tightness, dysphagia, stridor, progressive hoarseness, tongue or oropharyngeal swelling. These findings reflect upper‑airway mucosal and submucosal edema and may progress rapidly to obstruction.

- Breathing: cough, chest tightness, shortness of breath, wheeze, bronchospasm, hypoxemia, or respiratory arrest. Lower airway involvement results from bronchoconstriction, mucosal edema, and mucus production.

- Circulation: dizziness, presyncope, syncope, hypotension, tachycardia, shock, pallor, collapse. Cardiovascular effects include vasodilation, increased vascular permeability with intravascular volume loss, and direct effects on the heart (arrhythmias, myocardial ischemia) mediated by mediators such as histamine, leukotrienes, and platelet‑activating factor.

- Skin and mucosa: generalized urticaria (hives), angioedema, flushing, pruritus. Skin findings are common but may be absent in severe cases.

- Gastrointestinal: abdominal pain, cramping, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea. These symptoms are caused by smooth muscle contraction and increased vascular permeability in the gut.

- Neurologic: agitation, confusion, altered mental status, loss of consciousness from hypotension or hypoxia, and occasionally seizures.

Time course: Symptoms typically develop within minutes but can present after several hours (e.g., delayed food‑dependent reactions). Biphasic reactions (recurrence of symptoms without re‑exposure) can occur hours after apparent resolution; the reported risk varies, commonly cited as 1–20%.

# Common presenting symptoms and signs

Understanding the common presenting features and their pathophysiology helps anticipate progression and prioritize interventions.

Urticaria and pruritus
Urticaria (hives) and widespread pruritus are among the most frequent signs. Urticaria results from dermal microvascular hyperpermeability induced by histamine, prostaglandins, and other mediators released from activated mast cells and basophils. Urticarial lesions are typically raised, erythematous, and intensely itchy; they often migrate. Presence of urticaria supports a diagnosis of an immediate hypersensitivity reaction but is not required.

Angioedema
Angioedema is deeper dermal and submucosal swelling due to fluid extravasation. It can affect the face, lips, eyelids, and, critically, the upper airway including the tongue, floor of mouth, and larynx. Rapidly progressive angioedema and any signs of upper‑airway compromise (stridor, voice change, drooling) should prompt urgent airway evaluation and early consideration of airway control.

Hypotension and circulatory collapse
Hypotension and shock in anaphylaxis arise from systemic vasodilation, increased vascular permeability leading to third‑spacing of intravascular volume, and sometimes direct myocardial depression. Clinically this manifests as lightheadedness, syncope, tachycardia (compensatory), weak pulses, cool clammy skin, and decreased urine output. For adults, an absolute threshold commonly used is SBP < 90 mm Hg, or a drop >30% from baseline; for children, age‑specific hypotension thresholds apply (for example, infants: SBP < 70 mm Hg + [2 × age]; toddlers/children use published pediatric tables).

Bronchospasm and respiratory compromise
Lower airway involvement produces bronchospasm, wheeze, increased work of breathing, hypoxemia, and respiratory failure in severe cases. Wheeze may not always be audible and absence of wheeze does not exclude severe bronchospasm—silent chest in a cyanotic, hypoventilating patient indicates critical obstruction. Tachypnea, accessory muscle use, and oxygen desaturation are important clues.

Gastrointestinal symptoms
GI manifestations such as severe crampy abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea occur commonly and reflect smooth muscle contraction and vasodilation in the GI tract. In some cases, GI symptoms may predominate (particularly with food‑induced anaphylaxis), which can mislead the clinician away from the correct diagnosis.

Neurologic features
Neurologic symptoms are generally secondary to hypoxia or hypotension: confusion, agitation, syncope, and seizures. These signs should alert clinicians to severe systemic compromise even when other signs are subtle.

Putting the features together: while cutaneous signs are common and helpful, anaphylaxis can present without skin involvement and may present as sudden circulatory collapse, severe bronchospasm, or predominantly GI complaints. Therefore, a high index of suspicion is required based on exposure history and rapid evolution of symptoms.

# Atypical presentations and pitfalls in recognition

Atypical presentations can delay diagnosis and treatment. Awareness of these pitfalls reduces missed or under‑treated anaphylaxis.

Absence of skin signs
As many as 10–20% of severe anaphylactic reactions may lack cutaneous manifestations. Relying on the presence of hives will miss some fulminant cases where hypotension or respiratory arrest occur first. Always consider anaphylaxis in any rapid multisystem reaction after known or suspected exposure, even without urticaria.

Predominant GI presentation
Food‑triggered anaphylaxis, particularly in adolescents and young adults, may present with severe abdominal pain, vomiting, and diarrhea before other signs develop. This may be misattributed to gastroenteritis or toxin ingestion. A key clue is the temporal relation to ingestion and accompanying pruritus, wheeze, or lightheadedness.

Delayed presentations
Reactions may be delayed by several hours in certain contexts (e.g., alpha‑gal allergy related to red meat, or cofactor‑dependent food anaphylaxis such as exercise‑induced anaphylaxis). Medications or biologics may cause delayed serum sickness‑type reactions, which are immunologically distinct from anaphylaxis and present later.

Confounding by sedation or concurrent illness
Elderly patients or those who are intoxicated, sedated, or have chronic neurologic disease may not report symptoms; hypotension or altered mental status may be interpreted as primary cardiac or neurologic events. Similarly, patients with underlying asthma may have wheeze from baseline disease; look for rapid change from usual symptoms or associated features like angioedema or hypotension.

Anaphylaxis in the perioperative setting and with anesthesia
Anaphylaxis can occur under general anesthesia when the patient is unable to report symptoms. The first signs may be hypotension, bronchospasm evidenced by increased peak inspiratory pressures or difficult ventilation, and tachycardia. Cutaneous signs may be occult due to drapes. Diagnosis requires high suspicion and clear communication among the anesthetic and surgical teams.

Overlapping signs with other causes of shock or respiratory distress
Acute pulmonary edema, severe asthma exacerbation, septic shock, vasovagal syncope, myocardial infarction, and pulmonary embolism can mimic aspects of anaphylaxis. Differentiation requires attention to timing, exposure history, presence or absence of cutaneous/mucosal findings, and responses to treatment (e.g., rapid improvement after epinephrine supports anaphylaxis).

Medication‑related confounders
Beta‑blocker therapy can blunt tachycardia and response to epinephrine, sometimes making hypotension refractory; concomitant ACE inhibitor or ARB may exacerbate angioedema. Chronic urticaria or mast cell disorders may cause baseline skin changes complicating recognition of new hives.

# Severity assessment scales and triage decisions

Grading severity guides urgency of treatment, disposition, and monitoring. Several severity grading systems exist; two commonly referenced frameworks are the World Allergy Organization (WAO) severity grading and the more concise emergency oriented categorizations (mild, moderate, severe).

WAO severity grading (simplified)
- Grade 1 (Mild): Cutaneous/mucosal signs only (urticaria, flushing, swollen lips/eyes).
- Grade 2 (Moderate): Mild respiratory, cardiovascular, or GI signs (wheeze, mild hypotension with symptoms, vomiting).
- Grade 3 (Severe): Severe respiratory compromise (laryngeal edema, bronchospasm), marked hypotension, loss of consciousness.
- Grade 4 (Cardiac/respiratory arrest): Arrest.

Emergency triage categories (practical)
- Severe/Life‑threatening: Any of the following—airway compromise (stridor, severe oropharyngeal swelling), severe bronchospasm with hypoxia (SpO2 < 90% on room air or increasing respiratory effort), hypotension (SBP < 90 mm Hg or ≥ 30% drop from baseline), collapse, loss of consciousness. These patients require immediate intramuscular epinephrine, airway assessment and likely airway intervention, high flow oxygen, intravenous access, fluid resuscitation, and continuous monitoring. Triage priority: immediate resuscitation bay/critical care.
- Moderate: Multiple organ systems involved (e.g., significant urticaria + wheeze + vomiting) without hypotension or respiratory failure. These patients need prompt epinephrine, observation, and may need escalation if not improving.
- Mild: Limited cutaneous/mucosal signs only without other system involvement. These patients still require assessment, epinephrine if progression occurs, and observation; many will be observed for several hours due to risk of progression.

Clinical decision points for triage and disposition
- Epinephrine: Administer intramuscular epinephrine without delay for any patient meeting diagnostic criteria or with high clinical suspicion of anaphylaxis. Do not wait for objective hypotension or allergy testing.
- Airway: Early involvement of anesthesia/ENT/airway teams is indicated for progressive oropharyngeal swelling, voice change, drooling, stridor, or rapidly worsening respiratory distress. Consider early intubation before severe edema makes airway control impossible.
- Refractory hypotension: If hypotension persists after IM epinephrine and fluids, escalate to intravenous epinephrine infusion and ICU care. Beta‑blocker–treated patients may need glucagon.
- Observation period: Due to risk of biphasic reactions, observe patients after resolution of symptoms. Common practice is 4–6 hours for mild-to-moderate reactions and 12–24 hours for severe reactions; institutional policies vary. Consider longer observation for those with risk factors for biphasic reactions (previous severe anaphylaxis, need for multiple epinephrine doses, delayed epinephrine administration, protracted or refractory symptoms).
- Disposition: Admission to monitored setting (e.g., observation unit, step‑down, ICU) is indicated for severe reactions, those requiring multiple epinephrine doses, persistent symptoms, or significant comorbidity (cardiopulmonary disease, pregnancy). Patients with mild reactions who are well after observation and who can be reliably educated may be discharged with epinephrine auto‑injector prescriptions and specialist follow‑up.

# Initial monitoring requirements and documentation

Monitoring in the initial phase serves two purposes: detect deterioration early and document response to therapy. Monitoring requirements differ with severity.

Vital signs and frequency
- Continuous monitoring for patients with suspected moderate to severe anaphylaxis.
- Obtain baseline vital signs immediately: heart rate, blood pressure, respiratory rate, temperature, oxygen saturation, and level of consciousness.
- Reassess vital signs frequently after treatment (every 5–15 minutes initially) until stable. Document trends, not just single values.

Pulse oximetry and oxygen therapy
- Apply pulse oximetry immediately. Target peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2) typically ≥ 94% in adults; higher targets in pregnancy where possible.
- Administer high‑flow oxygen (e.g., nonrebreather at 10–15 L/min) to any patient with respiratory distress, hypoxemia, or cardiovascular compromise.

Cardiac monitoring
- Continuous cardiac monitoring (ECG) is indicated for moderate to severe reactions, hypotension, arrhythmia risk (known heart disease), or patients receiving intravenous epinephrine.
- Obtain a 12‑lead ECG if chest pain, arrhythmia, or ischemia is suspected; anaphylaxis can coexist with myocardial ischemia or Takotsubo cardiomyopathy.

Intravenous access and fluids
- Establish at least one large‑bore IV for patients with significant symptoms or hypotension. Give isotonic crystalloid fluid boluses (e.g., 500–1000 mL in adults, repeat as needed) to counteract relative hypovolemia due to vasodilation and capillary leak.
- For children, use weight‑based boluses (e.g., 20 mL/kg isotonic crystalloid).

Monitoring for biphasic reactions
- Continue observations for an appropriate interval (commonly 4–24 hours depending on severity). Patients should be advised about delayed reactions.

Documentation: essential items to record
Accurate, contemporaneous documentation improves clinical continuity and medicolegal clarity. Record the following:
- Time of symptom onset and relationship to potential exposures (food, drug, insect sting, contrast, physical exertion).
- Initial signs and symptoms by organ system and their timing.
- Baseline vital signs and subsequent values with timestamped reassessments.
- Treatments given: doses, routes, and times (particularly epinephrine—dose and route are critical).
- Response to each therapy (improvement, no change, deterioration).
- Allergies and past history of anaphylaxis, baseline medications (e.g., beta‑blockers), and comorbidities (asthma, cardiovascular disease).
- Airway assessment findings and interventions (e.g., supplemental oxygen, intubation).
- Disposition decision and rationale, observation plan, prescriptions (e.g., epinephrine auto‑injector), and specialist follow‑up arrangements.

Laboratory testing and imaging
Initial diagnosis is clinical; however, certain tests may be useful:
- Serum tryptase: peaked within 1–2 hours after onset and can help confirm mast cell activation retrospectively. Obtain the first sample as soon as possible (ideally within 1–4 hours), and a baseline sample at least 24 hours later or during convalescence to compare. A significant elevation is considered when acute tryptase > baseline + 2 µg/L or greater than laboratory reference range (normal tryptase generally < 11.4 µg/L but lab‑specific).
- Complete blood count, electrolytes, blood glucose, arterial blood gas if respiratory compromise or shock present.
- Chest X‑ray if aspiration, pulmonary edema, or other pulmonary pathology is suspected.
Note that normal tryptase does not exclude anaphylaxis, particularly in food‑triggered reactions.

# Initial differential diagnostic considerations

Many acute illnesses can present with features overlapping anaphylaxis. Early differentiation guides therapy: epinephrine is key for anaphylaxis and will often help differentiate clinically by response. Important alternative diagnoses include:

Vasovagal (neurally mediated) syncope
Often preceded by prodrome (pallor, diaphoresis, nausea), with bradycardia or transient hypotension. Cutaneous flushing and urticaria are absent. Recovery is usually rapid when supine; however, vasovagal episodes rarely cause bronchospasm or angioedema. A history of prolonged standing or emotional distress may suggest vasovagal etiology.

Acute severe asthma exacerbation
Presents with wheeze, dyspnea, and hypoxemia. In asthma the onset may not be temporally linked to allergen ingestion or injection; absence of skin/mucosal signs or hypotension favors asthma. That said, coexistence is possible. Bronchodilators and systemic corticosteroids are mainstays for asthma; epinephrine can help bronchospasm when anaphylaxis is suspected.

Septic shock
May present with hypotension, fever, and altered mental status. Septic patients typically do not have rapid onset after an exposure and usually have signs of infection, elevated white blood cell counts, or other infectious sources. Response to fluid resuscitation and pressors, as well as laboratory markers (lactate), help distinguish septic shock.

Cardiogenic shock and acute coronary syndromes
Acute myocardial infarction, arrhythmias, or cardiogenic shock may present with hypotension, chest pain, and syncope. Distinguishing features include ECG changes, elevated cardiac enzymes, known ischemic heart disease, and lack of urticaria/angioedema. Anaphylaxis can rarely cause myocardial ischemia or arrhythmia (Kounis syndrome), complicating the picture.

Pulmonary embolism
Acute dyspnea, chest pain, tachycardia, and hypoxemia may mimic anaphylaxis. Pulmonary embolism typically lacks cutaneous features and has risk factors (recent surgery, immobilization). D‑dimer and imaging (CT pulmonary angiography) are diagnostic tools after stabilization.

Angioedema due to bradykinin (ACE inhibitors, hereditary angioedema)
Produces facial and oropharyngeal swelling without urticaria or pruritus and usually without bronchospasm or hypotension. Onset can be slower and not related to allergen exposure; ACE inhibitor–mediated angioedema often has gradual onset over hours and lacks response to epinephrine and antihistamines. Hereditary angioedema typically has recurrent episodes and family history; complement testing (C4, C1 esterase inhibitor) can aid diagnosis.

Anxiety/panic attacks
May produce dyspnea, tachycardia, chest discomfort, and paresthesias. Cutaneous wheals, hypotension, angioedema, and GI symptoms are not typical. Panic attacks usually do not progress to hypotension or respiratory failure.

Mast cell disorders and systemic mastocytosis
Patients with mast cell activation syndromes may have recurrent anaphylaxis‑like episodes, often with very high baseline tryptase. A history of recurrent idiopathic episodes and skin findings like telangiectasia or urticaria pigmentosa can offer clues.

Medication side effects and toxic exposures
Vasodilatory drug reactions (e.g., nitrates), overdoses (opioids causing respiratory depression and hypotension), and histamine‑releasing drugs (vancomycin “red man syndrome”) can mimic parts of anaphylaxis. Red man syndrome is typically linked to rapid vancomycin infusion and causes flushing and pruritus but not hypotension or systemic involvement unless severe.

Key approach to differential diagnosis
- Use the exposure and timing history: a clear temporal relationship to an identifiable trigger increases likelihood of anaphylaxis.
- Consider the presence or absence of skin/mucosal symptoms, pattern of respiratory compromise (upper‑airway vs lower‑airway), degree and rapidity of hypotension, and response to a trial of intramuscular epinephrine.
- When in doubt and anaphylaxis is plausible, administer epinephrine and observe the response—delay in epinephrine is associated with worse outcomes.

Conclusion

Rapid clinical recognition of anaphylaxis depends on awareness of typical and atypical presentations across organ systems, use of validated clinical criteria, early severity grading, immediate monitoring, and consideration of alternative diagnoses. The priority is early intramuscular epinephrine administration and stabilization of the airway, breathing, and circulation. Thorough documentation and appropriate observation or escalation of care reduce the risk of preventable deterioration and enable accurate follow‑up, allergist referral, and secondary prevention strategies.
