Introduction

Anaphylaxis is a life-threatening, systemic hypersensitivity reaction characterized by rapid onset airway compromise, bronchospasm, and circulatory collapse. Management hinges on prompt epinephrine administration and simultaneous supportive measures to secure the airway, maintain oxygenation, and restore tissue perfusion. In the critically ill patient who either fails initial therapy or presents with severe airway or hemodynamic compromise, decisive critical-care interventions — early recognition of impending airway obstruction, appropriate airway techniques for angioedema, graduated oxygen-delivery escalation, judicious fluid resuscitation, and titrated vasoactive support — are essential to prevent irreversible hypoxemia and end-organ injury. This chapter synthesizes pathophysiology with practical clinical decision-making for airway and circulatory support in anaphylactic shock, with emphasis on when and how to escalate interventions and how to monitor response.

# Assessment for impending airway obstruction and indications for early intubation

The airway in anaphylaxis can deteriorate rapidly because of laryngeal edema, angioedema of the soft tissues, and mucosal swelling compounded by bronchospasm. Clinical assessment must therefore be dynamic and proactive rather than reactive. Key features that indicate impending airway obstruction include progressive stridor (especially inspiratory stridor), hoarseness or muffled voice, drooling or difficulty handling secretions, progressive dysphagia, visible swelling of the face, lips, tongue, or oropharynx, and any signs of respiratory distress such as use of accessory muscles, tachypnea with hypoxia, or paradoxical breathing. Acute change in voice quality, failure to phonate, or increasing difficulty with bag-mask ventilation are particularly ominous. Consciousness level is also crucial: declining mental status, inability to cooperate, or fatigue from respiratory effort may necessitate airway control. Objective measures that inform the decision include oxygen saturation (target SpO2 ≥ 94% in most patients), arterial blood gas with rising carbon dioxide and falling pH, and escalating oxygen requirements despite supplemental oxygen.

Decision points for early intubation must balance the risk of a difficult airway against the risk of sudden complete obstruction. Indications for urgent or early intubation include: evidence of progressive upper-airway edema (stridor, oropharyngeal swelling), persistent hypoxemia (SpO2 < 90% despite high-flow oxygen), respiratory failure with hypercapnia or respiratory fatigue, inability to protect the airway due to altered mental status, and the need for prolonged mechanical ventilation for severe bronchospasm or hemodynamic instability. In angioedema, once edema is manifest and progressive, intubation should generally be performed before a "can't intubate, can't oxygenate" situation evolves, because conventional endotracheal intubation may become impossible as edema progresses.

Clinical reasoning requires anticipation: if the history indicates early facial or oropharyngeal involvement (e.g., recent exposure to a known allergen with rapid lip or tongue swelling) or there is a trend of worsening symptoms, prepare for definitive airway early. Preparation includes assembling the most experienced airway operator, ensuring full monitoring, pre-oxygenation strategies, availability of advanced airway equipment (videolaryngoscope, flexible fiberoptic scope), and planning for surgical airway backup.

# Techniques for airway management in angioedema (awake intubation, surgical airway backup)

Angioedema poses unique challenges: the anatomy may be distorted, the mucosa friable and edematous, and bag-mask ventilation may be ineffective. The safe approach emphasizes maintaining spontaneous ventilation when possible and using techniques that minimize further trauma or loss of airway patency.

Awake intubation is often the preferred strategy when time and patient cooperation permit. Awake techniques preserve spontaneous ventilation and allow assessment of airway patency during the procedure. For awake intubation in angioedema, topical anesthesia of the upper airway (e.g., lidocaine nebulization, topical sprays, or transtracheal block) and cautious sedation with agents that preserve respiratory drive (low-dose ketamine, dexmedetomidine titration) are options. Flexible fiberoptic bronchoscopy through the nasal or oral route is a commonly used awake technique; it provides direct visualization and can negotiate around swollen tissues. Videolaryngoscopy may also be effective in awake or lightly sedated patients and can improve glottic visualization compared with direct laryngoscopy, but it can be limited if supraglottic edema obscures views.

When performing awake intubation, meticulous preparation is essential: pre-oxygenation, positioning, suction available for copious secretions, and a stepwise plan that anticipates failure. The operator should use smaller-diameter endotracheal tubes if necessary to pass a swollen glottic inlet, and consider a tube exchanger or bougie to facilitate placement. Avoid excessive manipulation that can exacerbate bleeding or edema. If pharmacologic measures are being administered (epinephrine, antihistamines, steroids), these should proceed concurrently, but should not delay securing the airway.

Surgical airway backup must be planned for every difficult-angioedema airway. If intubation fails or is impossible and oxygenation cannot be maintained by mask ventilation or supraglottic devices, cricothyroidotomy is the recommended emergent approach in adults. The time-critical nature of "can't intubate, can't oxygenate" scenarios demands that equipment for emergency cricothyroidotomy (scalpel, bougie, cuffed tracheostomy or tracheal cannula kits) be immediately available and that the team be prepared to perform it without delay. In some settings where neck anatomy permits and the situation is not immediately catastrophic, awake tracheostomy in the operating room is an option for anticipated extremely difficult airways; however, in acute anaphylaxis with rapid deterioration, cricothyroidotomy remains the faster, life-saving intervention.

Throughout, the airway strategy must be integrated with ongoing resuscitation: high-flow oxygen, epinephrine administration, and hemodynamic support. After successful airway control, reassess ventilation and oxygenation, and secure the tube definitively. Be aware that angioedema may continue to progress after intubation, so endotracheal tube size and position should be chosen to allow for potential edema-related narrowing, and readiness for surgical airway conversion should remain.

# Oxygen delivery methods and escalation (nasal cannula, non-rebreather, non-invasive ventilation, mechanical ventilation)

Oxygen therapy in anaphylaxis follows a graded escalation based on severity of hypoxemia and respiratory compromise. The first step is always high-concentration oxygen to maintain adequate oxygen delivery to tissues. Initial options and clinical reasoning for escalation are as follows.

Low-flow devices such as nasal cannula (typically 1–6 L/min) provide fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2) increments up to approximately 44% depending on flow and patient inspiratory flow. Nasal cannula may be adequate for patients with mild hypoxemia or as a bridge while preparing higher-level support. However, in many anaphylactic patients with airway edema or bronchospasm, higher FiO2 is required.

A non-rebreather mask (NRB) with reservoir at 10–15 L/min can deliver an FiO2 approaching 0.60–0.90 when fitted snugly and the patient has moderate inspiratory flow, and should be used for patients with more significant hypoxemia or early respiratory distress. It is a rapid, noninvasive method to increase oxygenation while decisions about advanced airway are made.

High-flow nasal cannula (HFNC) systems can deliver warmed, humidified oxygen at flows up to 60 L/min with adjustable FiO2 up to near 1.0. HFNC offers advantages in selected patients: it can provide some degree of positive airway pressure, reduce work of breathing, and improve oxygenation without the need for an interface that may be poorly tolerated. HFNC may be useful in patients with significant hypoxemia or bronchospasm who are cooperative and do not have imminent airway compromise. However, HFNC should not delay definitive airway management when there is progressive upper-airway obstruction.

Non-invasive ventilation (NIV) such as bilevel positive airway pressure (BiPAP) or continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) can support ventilation and reduce work of breathing in selected patients with hypercapnic respiratory failure or severe bronchospasm. Positive pressure can help stent airways open and improve alveolar ventilation. However, NIV is contraindicated when there is risk of loss of airway protective reflexes, copious secretions, or progressive upper-airway obstruction from angioedema. In acute angioedema with upper-airway swelling, NIV risks aspiration and mask leak; thus avoidance is advised when airway compromise is present or suspected.

When noninvasive measures fail or are contraindicated, endotracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation are required. Mechanical ventilation strategy should be individualized. In the setting of bronchospasm, allow prolonged expiratory time to prevent air trapping and auto-PEEP (e.g., set inspiratory:expiratory ratio of 1:3 or greater), use lower respiratory rates, and consider higher inspiratory flow rates to shorten inspiratory time. Tidal volume recommendations for adults generally aim for lung-protective ventilation: 6–8 mL/kg predicted body weight, but severe bronchospasm may necessitate lower effective tidal volumes to avoid breath stacking. In severe cases, permissive hypercapnia may be acceptable to avoid dynamic hyperinflation, provided pH remains tolerable and hemodynamics are stable. Monitor for barotrauma and ventilator-associated complications. In cases of profound hypoxemia with refractory bronchospasm, adjuncts such as inhaled bronchodilators via inline nebulizer, intravenous magnesium sulfate, and neuromuscular blockade to facilitate ventilation may be used as clinically indicated.

Throughout escalation, continuous pulse oximetry, capnography if available (end-tidal CO2), and frequent clinical reassessment are essential. Use objective thresholds to guide escalation: SpO2 < 90% on NRB or rising PaCO2/respiratory acidosis on arterial blood gas are clear signals to escalate to invasive ventilation.

# Guidelines for initial fluid resuscitation volumes and rationale (crystalloids vs colloids)

Anaphylactic shock is characterized by vasodilation, increased vascular permeability, and relative intravascular volume depletion due to fluid extravasation into the interstitium. Rapid intravascular volume restoration is a cornerstone of resuscitation because severe hypotension may be refractory to vasopressors if the intravascular volume is low. Fluid therapy must be prompt, substantial, and guided by response.

Guideline-recommended initial fluid management typically begins with rapid crystalloid boluses. A commonly used initial bolus is 20 mL/kg of isotonic crystalloid (e.g., normal saline or balanced crystalloids) given rapidly, and in adults consideration of larger initial boluses (e.g., 1–2 liters) depending on severity and body habitus. If hypotension persists or there are signs of ongoing hypoperfusion, repeat boluses up to a cumulative fluid volume may be needed; many protocols suggest up to 30 mL/kg in distributive shock as an early resuscitation target. The rationale centers on restoring preload to support cardiac output in the setting of systemic vasodilation and third-spacing due to increased capillary permeability.

Crystalloids are preferred as first-line because they are readily available, cost-effective, and have strong evidence as initial resuscitation fluid in most forms of shock. Balanced crystalloids (e.g., lactated Ringer’s or Plasma-Lyte) may be favored over normal saline in some settings to avoid hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis when large volumes are anticipated, though evidence specific to anaphylactic shock is limited.

Colloids (e.g., albumin) have theoretic appeal because they remain intravascular for longer and might better restore oncotic pressure; however, they are not routinely recommended for initial resuscitation of anaphylactic shock. There is limited evidence of mortality benefit, higher cost, potential for allergic reactions, and in some cases concern for exacerbating pulmonary edema. Colloids may be considered if very large volumes of crystalloids are required and hypoalbuminemia or ongoing capillary leak is suspected, but this is an individualized decision.

Clinical reasoning must account for comorbidities: patients with heart failure or renal impairment are at higher risk of fluid overload; in these patients, more cautious titration of fluid with earlier use of vasopressors and invasive hemodynamic monitoring is appropriate. Clear endpoints for fluid resuscitation include improvements in blood pressure (e.g., mean arterial pressure ≥ 65 mmHg), normalization or downward trend in lactate, improved mental status, urine output ≥ 0.5 mL/kg/hr, and improved skin perfusion. Aim for rapid boluses with reassessment after each bolus rather than fixed large-volume infusions without response assessment.

# Use of vasopressors and inotropes beyond epinephrine

Epinephrine is the primary pharmacologic treatment for anaphylaxis because it has alpha-adrenergic vasoconstrictive effects to counteract vasodilation and capillary leak, beta-1 inotropic and chronotropic effects to support cardiac output, and beta-2 bronchodilatory effects. Intramuscular epinephrine (0.3–0.5 mg of 1:1000 or 0.3–0.5 mL of 1 mg/mL) into the lateral thigh is first-line for most pre-hospital and initial hospital care. Intravenous epinephrine is reserved for patients with severe anaphylaxis or cardiovascular collapse and should be titrated carefully due to risk of arrhythmia or myocardial ischemia.

When hypotension persists despite adequate epinephrine (and adequate intravascular volume), additional vasoactive agents should be considered. Norepinephrine is the vasopressor of choice for persistent hypotension in distributive shock states because of its strong alpha-1 vasoconstrictive activity and modest beta-1 support, which increases systemic vascular resistance and improves coronary perfusion without excessive tachycardia. Typical starting doses are in the range of 0.05–0.1 mcg/kg/min and titrated upward to achieve MAP ≥ 65 mmHg; adult dosing is often expressed in mcg/min with titration to effect.

Vasopressin (typically 0.03 units/min infusion) can be a useful adjunct in catecholamine-refractory distributive shock to restore vascular tone via non-adrenergic pathways. It is particularly useful when high-dose catecholamines provoke adverse effects or tachyarrhythmias. Phenylephrine, a pure alpha-agonist, can raise blood pressure but may reduce cardiac output because of reflex bradycardia and increased afterload; its use is mainly in select situations where tachyarrhythmia precludes other agents.

Inotropic support with agents such as dobutamine may be necessary when myocardial dysfunction or severe pump failure contributes to hypotension despite restored afterload. Dobutamine increases cardiac contractility and can improve cardiac output but may cause vasodilation and hypotension at lower doses; it should be used with or after vasopressor support to avoid worsening hypotension. Milrinone (a phosphodiesterase inhibitor) is another inotrope with vasodilatory effects and may be used in refractory cardiogenic components but is less commonly used acutely in anaphylaxis.

Selection and sequencing of vasopressors requires clinical judgment. In the collapsed patient with severe hypotension despite epinephrine and fluids, initiate norepinephrine early while ensuring adequate intravascular volume. If cardiac output is low and hypotension persists, add inotrope support. Continue to reassess for reversible causes such as ongoing hemorrhage, tamponade, or tension pneumothorax. Be vigilant for adverse effects: arrhythmias, ischemia, and excessive vasoconstriction that may worsen tissue perfusion.

# Monitoring hemodynamic response and tissue perfusion in shock

Monitoring guides resuscitation and helps determine adequacy of intervention. Initial monitoring includes continuous electrocardiography, pulse oximetry, noninvasive blood pressure, and frequent clinical assessments (mental status, skin perfusion, capillary refill). Beyond these, more advanced and objective measures are indicated in severe or refractory anaphylactic shock.

Arterial blood pressure monitoring via an indwelling arterial catheter provides beat-to-beat hemodynamic data, which is particularly valuable when titrating vasoactive infusions and when noninvasive blood pressure measurements are unreliable. Target mean arterial pressure (MAP) is generally ≥ 65 mmHg in most adults, with individualized targets for patients with chronic hypertension or other comorbidities.

Laboratory markers include serum lactate, which reflects tissue hypoperfusion when elevated; normal lactate is typically < 2 mmol/L. Serial lactate measurements can track response to resuscitation: declining lactate suggests improving perfusion. Arterial blood gases and mixed/central venous oxygen saturation provide additional insights into oxygen delivery and utilization. Central venous oxygen saturation (ScvO2) monitoring — with a target generally ≥ 70% in many protocols — can indicate whether oxygen delivery is sufficient for metabolic demands, although interpretation requires context regarding hemoglobin, cardiac output, and oxygen extraction.

Urine output is a simple, practical perfusion marker; a target of ≥ 0.5 mL/kg/hr in adults is commonly used. Mental status and skin perfusion (warm vs cool extremities, capillary refill time) also provide rapid bedside information about perfusion. Capillary refill < 2 seconds is often considered reassuring, though it is operator-dependent and less reliable in some patient populations.

Assessment of fluid responsiveness is central to guiding further fluid administration. Static measures such as central venous pressure (CVP) are poor predictors of responsiveness. Dynamic measures and bedside functional tests are preferred: pulse pressure variation and stroke volume variation during controlled mechanical ventilation (with tidal volumes ≥ 8 mL/kg) are predictive of fluid responsiveness, as are passive leg raise (PLR) tests that transiently increase venous return and can be coupled with stroke volume or cardiac output monitoring to observe response. Point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) is invaluable: evaluation of left ventricular function, inferior vena cava (IVC) collapsibility, and direct assessment of volume status and cardiac output guides resuscitation and differentiates distributive from cardiogenic causes. An IVC that is small and collapsible suggests low preload, but interpretation must consider spontaneous versus mechanical ventilation and intra-abdominal pressures.

For the sickest patients, comprehensive hemodynamic monitoring with pulmonary artery catheterization or advanced pulse-contour-based cardiac output monitoring can be used to measure cardiac output, pulmonary artery pressures, and mixed venous oxygen saturation to tailor therapy. These invasive monitors are not required for most patients but may inform management in refractory shock or when complex cardiac dysfunction is suspected.

Finally, reassessment frequency should be high: after each intervention (bolus fluid, vasopressor titration, mechanical ventilation changes), re-evaluate clinical and objective markers. Persistent hypotension, rising lactate, decreasing urine output, or worsening mental status despite appropriate therapy indicate need for escalation or re-evaluation for alternative etiologies.

Conclusion

Airway compromise and circulatory collapse in anaphylaxis demand rapid, coordinated critical-care interventions. Early recognition of impending airway obstruction and proactive airway management, including awake techniques and preparedness for surgical airway, can be lifesaving. Oxygen delivery should be escalated promptly according to clinical need, and mechanical ventilation tailored to the pathophysiology of bronchospasm or pulmonary edema. Aggressive initial crystalloid boluses (e.g., 20 mL/kg, with consideration up to 30 mL/kg) restore preload, while vasopressors such as norepinephrine and adjuncts (vasopressin, inotropes) address refractory vasodilatory shock. Continuous, multimodal monitoring of hemodynamics and tissue perfusion — including arterial pressure, lactate, urine output, ScvO2, and bedside ultrasound — guides resuscitation and reduces the risk of both under- and over-treatment. Integrating pathophysiology with practical airway and circulatory strategies enables timely, effective management of severe anaphylactic shock.
