Introduction

Anaphylaxis is an acute, potentially life-threatening systemic hypersensitivity reaction characterized by rapid onset and involvement of multiple organ systems. While many episodes resolve after prompt recognition and treatment, clinicians must also anticipate atypical temporal patterns of recurrence. These include biphasic anaphylaxis, multiphasic anaphylaxis, and refractory anaphylaxis. Each pattern carries distinct clinical implications for monitoring, resource use, and risk communication with patients and caregivers. This chapter defines these patterns operationally, reviews the range of reported incidence rates and methodological causes for variability, examines suggested predictors and risk factors, describes typical time-course patterns and windows for recurrence, outlines limitations of the existing evidence base, and draws practical implications for observation strategies and allocation of clinical resources.

# Operational definitions: biphasic vs multiphasic vs refractory anaphylaxis

Clear, operational definitions are essential for consistency in research and clinical practice. Although consensus statements exist (e.g., from professional allergy and immunology societies), definitions used in the literature vary, which contributes to heterogeneity in reported incidence and study conclusions. Below are definitions commonly applied in contemporary clinical and research contexts.

Biphasic anaphylaxis
Biphasic anaphylaxis refers to the recurrence of symptoms consistent with anaphylaxis after an initial episode has resolved without further exposure to the inciting allergen. By convention, a biphasic event fulfills three principal criteria: (1) there is an initial anaphylactic reaction that meets standard diagnostic criteria; (2) the initial event shows documented clinical improvement or resolution with therapy; and (3) a second phase of symptoms occurs after a symptom-free period, without re-exposure to the trigger. The second phase typically mirrors the first in organ systems involved (e.g., cardiovascular compromise, bronchospasm, cutaneous signs) but may be less or more severe. Definitions vary regarding the minimum symptom-free interval required to call a recurrence "biphasic" (common choices are ≥1 hour or ≥4 hours), and whether the second phase must require specific therapy (e.g., additional epinephrine) to qualify. Many studies require that the recurrence be clinically significant—prompting re-treatment or physician assessment—rather than minor, self-limited symptoms.

Multiphasic anaphylaxis
Multiphasic anaphylaxis denotes recurrence of anaphylactic symptoms on more than two discrete occasions (i.e., three or more phases), again without re-exposure to the trigger and with intervening symptom-free intervals. Multiphasic patterns are less commonly reported and can present as intermittent relapses over hours to days. The clinical importance of distinguishing multiphasic from biphasic patterns lies in recognizing that relapses may be unpredictable in timing and severity, sometimes necessitating prolonged or repeated therapy and extended monitoring.

Refractory anaphylaxis
Refractory anaphylaxis describes anaphylaxis that is unresponsive to initial standard therapy, typically defined as persistent or progressive signs of systemic anaphylaxis despite adequate doses of intramuscular (IM) epinephrine and other supportive measures. Operationally, refractory cases are often characterized by the requirement for repeated doses of epinephrine (e.g., more than three IM doses or continuous intravenous infusion) and ongoing hemodynamic or respiratory compromise that persists despite appropriate management. Definitions vary: some authors define refractoriness as failure to respond to two or more doses of epinephrine; others emphasize the need for continuous infusion or escalation to advanced cardiovascular support. Refractory anaphylaxis should prompt consideration of alternative or coexisting diagnoses (e.g., vasodilatory shock of another etiology, acute cardiogenic events, severe asthma exacerbation) and of adjunctive therapies and escalation to critical care.

Clinical diagnosis of anaphylaxis
Because biphasic, multiphasic, and refractory forms are temporal variations of anaphylaxis, the baseline diagnostic criteria for anaphylaxis apply. Commonly used clinical criteria include acute onset of illness with skin and/or mucosal involvement plus either respiratory compromise or reduced blood pressure; or two or more of the following after exposure to a likely allergen: skin/mucosal symptoms, respiratory compromise, reduced blood pressure or associated symptoms, and persistent gastrointestinal symptoms. For hypotension, age-appropriate thresholds apply (e.g., systolic blood pressure <90 mmHg in adults or a >30% decrease from baseline). Laboratory markers (e.g., serum tryptase) can support diagnosis but do not substitute for clinical criteria; tryptase is often elevated in anaphylaxis but a normal level does not exclude it.

# Reported incidence ranges and methodological reasons for variability

Reported incidence rates for biphasic anaphylaxis vary widely across studies, ranging from near 0% up to ~20% or higher depending on patient population and study methodology. Multiphasic anaphylaxis is less frequently quantified, and refractory anaphylaxis is uncommon but disproportionately represented in severe anaphylaxis cohorts. Several methodological factors account for this variability.

Heterogeneity in operational definitions
As noted, differing definitions of what constitutes a biphasic episode (minimum symptom-free interval, requirement for objective re-treatment) produce divergent estimates. Studies requiring a longer symptom-free interval or re-treatment with epinephrine typically report lower incidence, while those that include minor recurrent symptoms report higher rates.

Study population and setting
Incidence varies by setting: emergency department and hospital-based cohorts (which capture more severe reactions) report higher biphasic rates than outpatient immunology clinic series. Pediatric populations may have different patterns compared with adults. Anaphylaxis secondary to medications or intravenous exposures (e.g., radiocontrast, perioperative agents) tends to show higher rates of biphasic reactions than food-induced anaphylaxis in some series.

Ascertainment and follow-up duration
Short observation windows will miss late recurrences. Studies that limit observation to the emergency department stay often underestimate incidence relative to those with inpatient observation for 24 hours or structured post-discharge follow-up. Conversely, studies relying on patient self-report without objective verification may overestimate minor recurrences.

Treatment variability
Differences in acute management influence recurrence incidence. Prompt, adequate epinephrine administration and use of intravenous corticosteroids or longer antihistamine courses may correlate with lower recurrence rates in some reports. Conversely, underuse of epinephrine in initial management is associated with higher recurrence in several observational analyses.

Retrospective versus prospective design
Retrospective chart reviews are subject to incomplete documentation and under-detection of recurrences, particularly if patients sought care elsewhere. Prospective studies with predefined monitoring protocols yield more reliable estimates but are fewer and often limited by sample size.

Definition of clinical significance
Some studies count any recurrent symptom as biphasic anaphylaxis; others restrict to recurrences requiring further epinephrine, systemic corticosteroids, or hospitalization. The clinical implication is important: counting trivial or subjective recurrences inflates incidence but may not reflect outcomes that matter for resource allocation.

Diagnostic uncertainty and comorbidities
Overlap with other clinical entities (e.g., vasovagal syncope, asthma exacerbation, urticaria flares) can result in misclassification. Studies using rigorous diagnostic criteria and objective physiologic endpoints (e.g., hypotension, hypoxia, laryngeal edema confirmed on exam) will produce different incidence figures compared with looser clinical capture methods.

Given these methodological variations, clinicians should interpret reported incidence ranges with caution and consider the context—patient population, setting, and definitions—when applying literature findings to clinical practice.

# Suggested predictors and risk factors (delay in epinephrine, severe initial reaction, multiple doses, patient factors)

Several candidate predictors and risk factors for biphasic, multiphasic, and refractory anaphylaxis have been proposed and studied. Most evidence arises from observational cohorts and case series; therefore, associations may not be causal. Nevertheless, these factors are useful for clinical risk stratification.

Delay in epinephrine administration
Delayed administration of intramuscular epinephrine during the initial event is consistently associated with worse outcomes and a higher likelihood of prolonged or recurrent anaphylaxis. Multiple retrospective series show that patients who received epinephrine late or not at all were more likely to experience biphasic reactions and to require additional interventions. The pathophysiologic rationale is that timely epinephrine halts mediator release and counteracts life-threatening physiologic derangements (vasodilation, increased vascular permeability, bronchial smooth muscle constriction). Delays may allow a more intense or sustained immune cascade, increasing the risk of relapse.

Severity of initial reaction
Greater severity at presentation—manifested by hypotension, respiratory compromise (stridor, wheeze, hypoxemia), altered mental status, or requirement for multiple epinephrine doses—is associated with higher risk of biphasic and refractory patterns. Severe initial reactions likely reflect larger mediator burden, greater tissue involvement, or coexisting factors (e.g., beta-blocker use) that exacerbate hemodynamic instability. Several studies report that patients requiring >1 dose of epinephrine or vasopressors initially are more likely to have recurrence.

Requirement for multiple epinephrine doses during the initial episode
The need for repeated epinephrine during the first hour correlates with persistence of mediators or pharmacologic tachyphylaxis and predicts refractory courses. Patients receiving multiple doses are more likely to need ICU-level care and to have biphasic or multiphasic recurrences. This association supports closer observation in such patients.

Coexisting cardiopulmonary disease and medications
Comorbidities such as cardiovascular disease (ischemic heart disease, heart failure), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease or severe asthma, and older age are linked to more severe or refractory presentations. Certain medications influence response: beta-blockers may blunt response to epinephrine and are associated with refractory hypotension; angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors may predispose to angioedema or more pronounced hypotension in some settings. Concurrent use of selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors and other agents has been investigated but with inconsistent findings.

Atopic history and baseline mast cell disorders
A history of severe allergic disease (e.g., previous anaphylaxis) may predict recurrence, although the relationship is complex. Underlying mast cell disorders or elevated baseline tryptase (systemic mastocytosis, monoclonal mast cell activation syndrome) increase the risk of severe and recurrent anaphylaxis. Baseline tryptase >11.4 ng/mL is commonly used as a threshold suggesting mast cell disorders, but value interpretation requires clinical context and repeat testing. These patients are more prone to biphasic or protracted courses.

Type of trigger
Some triggers are more commonly associated with biphasic or severe reactions. For example, drug-induced and insect-sting–induced anaphylaxis may have higher rates of protracted or biphasic courses compared with food-induced anaphylaxis in certain series. IV exposures, perioperative agents, and radiocontrast reactions may produce more severe hemodynamic compromise and higher recurrence risk.

Laboratory markers
Post-event elevated serum tryptase (measured within 1–4 hours of onset and compared with baseline) suggests significant mast cell activation and has been associated with increased severity; however, data linking tryptase to biphasic recurrence are mixed. No single laboratory marker reliably predicts biphasic anaphylaxis in routine practice.

Demographic factors
Evidence about sex, race, and age is mixed. Some studies suggest older age correlates with increased risk of biphasic or refractory reactions, possibly via comorbidity burden and medication use. Pediatric populations have unique considerations—children may present with predominant respiratory symptoms and variable recurrence patterns.

In summary, delay in initial epinephrine, severity of the first reaction (including requirement for multiple epinephrine doses), certain triggers (drugs, intravenous exposures), comorbid cardiopulmonary disease, medications that blunt response to epinephrine (e.g., beta-blockers), and baseline mast cell disorders are among the strongest candidate predictors of biphasic, multiphasic, and refractory anaphylaxis. These risk factors should guide clinical decision-making regarding observation intensity and disposition.

# Time course patterns and typical windows for recurrence (hours to days)

Understanding temporal patterns of recurrence is crucial for setting observation durations and discharge instructions. Recurrence windows vary across individuals and settings, but several general patterns and typical timeframes have been consistently reported.

Immediate and early recurrence
Recurrent or persistent symptoms may appear within minutes to a few hours of the initial event. This early phase often reflects incomplete response to therapy or ongoing exposure/absorption of the allergen. In this context, recurrence typically manifests within the first 1–4 hours. Many studies consider a biphasic event to occur when recurrence happens after a minimum symptom-free interval of 1–4 hours.

Most common recurrence window
Multiple observational studies report that the majority of biphasic recurrences occur within 1–12 hours of symptom resolution, with a clustering between 4 and 10 hours in many cohorts. Consequently, several clinical guidelines recommend observation for at least 4–6 hours for patients with risk factors and up to 24 hours for higher-risk cases.

Late recurrence
Late biphasic or multiphasic recurrences occurring beyond 24 hours are uncommon but documented. Some case reports describe relapses within 24–72 hours or even several days later, especially in patients with protracted courses or delayed absorption from depot-type exposures (e.g., intradermal drug depots) or ongoing antigen exposure. Such late recurrences tend to be less frequent but can be clinically significant.

Protracted anaphylaxis versus biphasic
It is important to distinguish between biphasic recurrence and protracted anaphylaxis. Protracted anaphylaxis refers to an unremitting episode lasting many hours to days without a symptom-free interval. The clinical management involves continued therapy and often inpatient or ICU-level care. This differs from true biphasic or multiphasic patterns, which feature discrete symptom-free intervals.

Factors influencing timing
The timing of recurrence is influenced by trigger type (e.g., foods versus intravenous medications), amount and route of exposure, patient comorbidities, and adequacy of initial therapy (especially epinephrine). For example, reactions to ingested food allergens may have relatively rapid onset and resolution, whereas drug-induced reactions with ongoing release or slow redistribution can cause later relapses.

Clinical implication
Because most recurrences occur within the first 4–12 hours, observation periods within this window capture most biphasic events, especially in low-to-moderate risk patients. However, given the tails of the distribution and the presence of late recurrences in some higher-risk individuals, tailored observation durations and clear discharge instructions are essential.

# Evidence limitations and influence on clinical observation policies

The evidence base for biphasic, multiphasic, and refractory anaphylaxis has important limitations that affect clinical policy and practice.

Observational and retrospective data predominance
Most of the literature consists of retrospective chart reviews and small prospective cohorts. Randomized controlled trials comparing observation durations or specific interventions to prevent recurrence are lacking. Observational studies are subject to biases: selection bias (sicker patients are more likely to be observed and therefore have recurrences detected), information bias (incomplete documentation), and confounding (e.g., more aggressive treatment in sicker patients).

Heterogeneous definitions
As previously discussed, lack of standardized definitions across studies complicates meta-analysis and guideline development. Without consistent criteria for biphasic events (symptom-free interval, severity threshold), pooling data yields uncertain conclusions.

Limited power for rare events
True refractory anaphylaxis and multiphasic courses are relatively uncommon; small studies and heterogeneity reduce ability to identify reliable predictors or to quantify absolute risks accurately. Many studies report associations with wide confidence intervals, limiting precision.

Variable follow-up
Differences in follow-up methods (inpatient observation vs. telephone follow-up vs. chart review) influence detection. Post-discharge events that do not return to the index institution are often missed in retrospective series.

Lack of standardized treatment protocols
Divergence in acute management (timing and dosing of epinephrine, adjunctive therapies like corticosteroids) makes it difficult to isolate the effect of initial treatment on recurrence risk. This heterogeneity also complicates assessment of whether adjunctive therapies (e.g., corticosteroids) reduce biphasic risk—evidence is inconclusive.

Confounding by indication
Patients receiving more aggressive treatment (including hospitalization and longer observation) are inherently more likely to have severe disease and thus more likely to have recurrence; simply observing higher recurrence in hospitalized groups does not mean hospitalization increases risk.

Implications for observation policies
Given these limitations, guideline development has balanced the low absolute incidence of clinically significant biphasic events in many settings against the resource implications of prolonged observation for all patients. Many professional statements recommend risk-stratified observation: shorter ED observation (e.g., 4–6 hours) for low-risk patients who respond promptly to epinephrine and longer observation (12–24 hours or hospitalization) for higher-risk patients (severe initial reaction, delayed epinephrine, need for multiple epinephrine doses, refractory physiology, comorbidities). Policies also emphasize the need for clear discharge instructions regarding signs of recurrence, availability of epinephrine autoinjectors, and prompt access to care.

Because the evidence is imperfect, institutions should develop local pathways that consider resource constraints, local epidemiology, and acceptability of risk. Prospective data collection and standardized definitions are needed to refine observation recommendations further.

# Implications for monitoring strategies and resource allocation

Translating the available evidence into operational clinical pathways requires balancing patient safety with efficient use of healthcare resources. Monitoring strategies should be tailored based on initial severity, risk factors for recurrence, and local capabilities.

Risk-stratified observation
A tiered approach is practical. Low-risk patients—those with mild cutaneous symptoms only, prompt complete resolution after a single IM epinephrine dose, no respiratory or cardiovascular compromise, no significant comorbidities, and reliable access to follow-up—may be observed for a shorter interval (commonly 4–6 hours) before discharge with education and a prescription for an epinephrine autoinjector. Higher-risk patients—those with hypotension, respiratory compromise, need for repeated epinephrine doses, delayed initial epinephrine administration, suspicion for underlying mast cell disorder, or significant comorbidities—should be observed for longer (12–24 hours) or admitted for inpatient care. Refractory cases require ICU-level monitoring and access to advanced hemodynamic and airway support.

Monitoring modalities and thresholds
While continuous cardiorespiratory monitoring is not necessary for all patients, it is indicated for those with severe initial reactions or ongoing symptoms. Pulse oximetry, frequent vital sign checks (e.g., every 15–30 minutes initially), and observation for airway compromise are standard. For patients with hypotension requiring vasopressors or fluid resuscitation, invasive monitoring and critical care admission may be warranted.

Medication availability and staff training
Facilities should ensure immediate availability of epinephrine in appropriate formulations (IM autoinjector equivalents or pre-filled syringes), and staff must be trained in recognizing anaphylaxis and delivering timely epinephrine. Protocols for escalation (repeat epinephrine dosing, IV epinephrine infusion, adjunctive therapies, airway management) should be in place, particularly in settings where severe reactions are more likely (perioperative suites, radiology with contrast administration, inpatient infusion centers).

Resource allocation considerations
Widespread 24-hour observation for all anaphylaxis patients would strain resources and might not meaningfully reduce poor outcomes for low-risk patients given the low incidence of clinically significant late biphasic events. Conversely, inadequate observation of high-risk patients could result in preventable morbidity or mortality. Institutions must therefore allocate resources to identify and manage high-risk patients, invest in staff education, and provide systems for reliable outpatient follow-up and access to epinephrine autoinjectors.

Discharge planning and patient education
Regardless of observation length, discharge planning must emphasize recognition of recurrent symptoms, instructions for epinephrine administration, provision of an epinephrine autoinjector, and follow-up with an allergist/immunologist. Patients should be counseled on when to return for care (e.g., new or worsening respiratory symptoms, hypotension, persistent gastrointestinal symptoms) and given written action plans.

Quality improvement and data collection
To refine local practice, institutions should collect prospective data using standardized definitions, monitor timing and frequency of recurrences, and audit adherence to epinephrine administration guidelines. Such data can inform iterative adjustment of observation protocols and resource allocation.

Summary

Biphasic, multiphasic, and refractory anaphylaxis are important temporal variations of anaphylaxis with distinct implications for monitoring and management. Definitions vary across studies, contributing to a wide range of reported incidences. Delay in epinephrine, severity of the initial reaction, need for multiple epinephrine doses, certain triggers, comorbid conditions, and underlying mast cell disorders are among the most consistent risk factors identified. Most recurrences occur within the first 4–12 hours, but late relapses can occur. Evidence limitations—heterogeneous definitions, predominantly observational data, and variable follow-up—necessitate risk-stratified observation strategies that balance patient safety with efficient resource use. Clear protocols, staff training, availability of epinephrine, and robust discharge education are essential components of high-quality anaphylaxis care. Further prospective, standardized research is needed to refine prediction of recurrence and to optimize observation and resource allocation policies.
