Introduction

Anaphylaxis and other mast cell activation syndromes (MCAS) are clinical diagnoses defined by an acutely evolving constellation of mucocutaneous, respiratory, cardiovascular, and/or gastrointestinal signs and symptoms caused by rapid mediator release from mast cells (and basophils). Laboratory testing does not replace a clinical diagnosis but can provide objective evidence of mast cell activation, help distinguish anaphylaxis from mimics, and guide further investigation for underlying disorders such as systemic mastocytosis or hereditary alpha-tryptasemia. Understanding the biology and kinetics of mast cell mediators, the optimal timing of specimen collection, how to interpret results in the context of an individual patient, and the practical constraints of the acute care environment is essential for clinicians who manage these patients. This chapter reviews the major laboratory tests used in suspected anaphylaxis and mast cell activation, the biologic rationale for each, recommended sampling schedules, interpretation including quantitative decision rules, and limitations that affect clinical use.

# Tryptase biology: sources, kinetics after mast cell activation and baseline considerations

Tryptase is a serine protease stored in mast cell secretory granules and is released predominantly by mast cells during degranulation. It exists in several isoforms; the two most clinically relevant are alpha- and beta-tryptase. Beta-tryptase is predominantly the enzymatically active form released during acute degranulation; alpha-tryptase tends to be constitutively secreted and contributes to baseline concentrations. Basophils contain little to no tryptase, so circulating tryptase is considered a relatively specific marker for mast cell activation.

Kinetics. After an episode of mast cell degranulation, serum tryptase typically begins to rise within minutes, is often measurable within 15–30 minutes, and reaches a peak usually between 1 and 4 hours after symptom onset. In many patients, tryptase concentrations fall toward baseline over 12–24 hours, although the exact time course varies with the severity of activation, the patient’s baseline level, and renal clearance. Because tryptase is measured in serum, sample processing (allowing clotting, then centrifugation) and timing matter. The peak is commonly observed between 1–2 hours for many cases of anaphylaxis but can be later; some protocols capture an early sample (within 1–2 hours) and a second sample at 2–4 hours if feasible.

Baseline tryptase. Individuals have a personal baseline serum tryptase, reflecting constitutive secretion and total body mast cell burden. In the general population, many laboratories use an upper limit of normal of approximately 11.4 ng/mL for total tryptase measured by common commercial immunoassays (units vary by assay and laboratory; values are reported in ng/mL). Persistently elevated baseline tryptase (commonly defined as >20 ng/mL) raises concern for systemic mastocytosis or clonal mast cell disorders and should prompt specialist referral and further workup. Mild elevations above the population reference range but substantially below 20 ng/mL can reflect hereditary alpha-tryptasemia (an inherited copy-number variation in TPSAB1) or other conditions; therefore, interpretation requires knowledge of the baseline.

Physiologic and pathologic modifiers. Baseline and stimulated tryptase are influenced by a number of factors: renal insufficiency can slow clearance and elevate baseline concentrations; myeloid or mast-cell lineage hematologic disorders can produce persistently elevated levels; recent severe allergic or inflammatory events can transiently raise baseline. Medications do not substantially interfere with most immunoassays for tryptase, but pre-analytical factors (time to centrifugation, storage temperature) matter.

# Recommended sampling schedule for tryptase

Because tryptase kinetics are time-dependent, a planned sampling schedule improves the chance of documenting mast cell activation. The recommended approach in suspected anaphylaxis is to obtain at least two samples: an acute (event) sample and a baseline sample obtained after full clinical resolution.

Acute-phase sampling. Obtain the first serum tryptase as soon as possible after symptom onset and ideally within 1–2 hours. A useful target is within 1 hour of the peak of the clinical reaction, but practically within the first 1–2 hours is acceptable. If only one acute sample is feasible in the clinical setting, take it as early as possible; if two acute samples are possible, obtain the first within 1–2 hours of onset and a second at 2–4 hours to capture the expected peak and the initial decline. In many centers, a single acute sample transiently elevated compared with baseline is accepted for confirmation of mast cell activation, but multiple acute samples increase certainty.

Baseline (convalescent) sampling. A baseline serum tryptase should be obtained at least 24 hours after complete resolution of symptoms and cardiovascular stability, and ideally several days after the episode. Many guidelines recommend waiting at least 24–48 hours and preferably 48–72 hours post-resolution to ensure return to true baseline and avoid detecting a residual post-event elevation. Because baseline values are used for the decision rule (see next section), an accurate baseline is crucial; if baseline is elevated, repeat testing or hematology/immunology referral is indicated.

Practical considerations. In the acute emergency department (ED) setting, competing priorities (airway, breathing, circulation) and logistical factors (phlebotomy availability, lab turnaround) can delay sampling. Clinicians should document the exact time of symptom onset (or the earliest reliable time), the time(s) of sample collection, and whether the patient received treatments (epinephrine, fluids, steroids, antihistamines) before sampling. Tryptase remains a useful test even when sampling is delayed, but the sensitivity for detecting an elevation diminishes as time from symptom onset increases—particularly beyond 4–6 hours.

# Interpretation of elevated tryptase: relative vs absolute increases and thresholds suggestive of mast cell activation or systemic mastocytosis

Absolute thresholds. Many laboratories use a reference upper limit of normal for total tryptase around 11.4 ng/mL (assay-dependent). An isolated acute tryptase above that upper reference limit supports mast cell activation if correlated with compatible clinical findings. For concern about systemic mastocytosis, commonly used thresholds include a persistent baseline tryptase ≥20 ng/mL as one major minor criterion that warrants further hematologic workup (bone marrow biopsy, KIT mutation testing) in the appropriate clinical context.

Relative (delta) criteria. Because individuals have different baseline tryptase levels, relying solely on an absolute cutoff can miss clinically important activations in patients with low baseline values. A commonly used quantitative decision rule for significant mast cell activation is an increase of acute tryptase over baseline by at least $2 + 1.2 \times \text{baseline}$ ng/mL. Explicitly, an acute tryptase is considered significantly elevated if:

acute tryptase − baseline tryptase > $2 + 1.2 \times \text{baseline}$

This rule increases sensitivity for detecting activation across a range of baselines. For example, a patient with baseline tryptase of 5 ng/mL would meet the criterion if the acute tryptase exceeds $2 + 1.2 \times 5 = 8$ ng/mL (i.e., an acute level >13 ng/mL); a patient with baseline 2 ng/mL would reach significance with acute level >$2 + 1.2 \times 2 = 4.4$ ng/mL (i.e., >6.4 ng/mL). Using the delta criterion reduces false negatives in patients with elevated baseline tryptase and avoids overcalling trivial increases in patients with very low baseline values.

Clinical context matters. Tryptase elevation alone is not diagnostic of anaphylaxis in an asymptomatic patient. Interpretation requires integration with the clinical picture: presence of rapidly developing cutaneous signs, respiratory compromise, hypotension, or gastrointestinal symptoms supports that an elevated tryptase reflects clinically relevant mast cell activation. Conversely, in patients without suggestive symptoms, an isolated marginal tryptase elevation warrants repeat baseline testing and consideration of alternative etiologies.

Thresholds suggestive of systemic mastocytosis and hereditary conditions. Persistent baseline tryptase ≥20 ng/mL is a red flag for clonal mast cell disease (systemic mastocytosis); very high values substantially above this threshold are uncommon in isolated anaphylaxis and indicate the need for hematologic referral. Mild but persistent elevations (e.g., baseline 11–20 ng/mL) may reflect hereditary alpha-tryptasemia or other causes and should prompt specialist evaluation. Genetic testing for TPSAB1 copy-number variation may be considered in patients with reproducibly elevated baseline tryptase and appropriate symptoms.

# Other laboratory tests: histamine/metabolites, complete blood count, lactate, arterial blood gas, electrolytes

Histamine and metabolites. Histamine is another classic mediator released with mast cell (and basophil) activation. Plasma histamine rises very rapidly but has a very short half-life (on the order of minutes), making it difficult to capture in peripheral blood unless drawn promptly and handled properly (collected on ice, processed immediately). Because of these pre-analytic challenges, plasma histamine assays have limited practical utility in many routine ED settings.

Urinary and plasma metabolites of histamine and prostaglandin D2 are more stable and useful for retrospective confirmation. Urine concentrations of N-methylhistamine (also called 1-methylhistamine or methylhistamine) collected in a timed urine (often 24-hour) or spot urine (normalized to creatinine) can remain elevated longer than plasma histamine and are often used in research or outpatient evaluation of episodic mast cell activation. Similarly, urinary 11β-PGF2α is a stable urinary metabolite of prostaglandin D2 and may rise after mast cell activation. These tests are assay- and laboratory-dependent, have variable reference ranges, and are generally sent in the outpatient setting or for research rather than for immediate ED decision-making.

Complete blood count (CBC). CBC performed during and after an anaphylactic episode can contribute to clinical assessment. Leukocytosis, often with neutrophilia and relative lymphopenia, is common in acute physiologic stress. Eosinophil counts may be suppressed during the acute phase due to corticosterone-mediated demargination and do not reliably diagnose anaphylaxis. Hemoconcentration (elevated hemoglobin/hematocrit) can indicate significant plasma extravasation and intravascular volume loss. Platelet counts are usually unaffected acutely but may be helpful in evaluating disseminated intravascular coagulation in severe cases.

Lactate and arterial/venous blood gas. Serum lactate is a marker of tissue hypoperfusion and anaerobic metabolism and can be useful in assessing the severity of circulatory compromise during anaphylaxis. Normal venous lactate is typically <2 mmol/L; higher values suggest systemic hypoperfusion. Arterial blood gas (ABG) or venous blood gas can quantify hypoxemia, acid–base disturbances, or hypercapnia in respiratory compromise. Oxygenation status (PaO2 on ABG) and lactate trends can guide resuscitation and disposition decisions.

Electrolytes and renal function. Basic metabolic panel: sodium 135–145 mmol/L, potassium 3.5–5.0 mmol/L, chloride, bicarbonate, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine. Severe anaphylaxis with hypotension and hypoperfusion can cause acute kidney injury and electrolyte abnormalities; hypocalcemia may also be observed in severe cases. Monitoring electrolytes is pragmatic for fluid and vasopressor management. Elevated creatinine may also affect tryptase clearance and complicate interpretation.

Other tests. Cardiac troponin can rise transiently in severe hypotension or in Kounis syndrome (allergic coronary syndrome); consider obtaining if chest pain or ECG abnormalities occur. Coagulation studies may be useful if hemorrhagic or severe systemic inflammatory complications are suspected.

# Limitations: timing, stability, assay variability and differential diagnoses causing elevated tryptase

Timing. The single greatest practical limitation in using biochemical markers of mast cell activation is timing. Tryptase has optimal sensitivity when sampled within the first 1–4 hours after symptom onset; plasma histamine has an even narrower window. Delays in obtaining acute samples decrease sensitivity and increase the risk of false-negative results. Conversely, baseline samples too soon after resolution may be confounded by residual elevation.

Stability and pre-analytic handling. Tryptase is measured in serum; blood should be allowed to clot and then centrifuged according to laboratory protocols. Samples should be stored and transported per manufacturer recommendations (many assays tolerate refrigerated transport but prolonged delays or repeated freeze–thaw cycles can affect results). Plasma histamine requires immediate cooling and rapid processing, often beyond the capacity of many EDs. Urine metabolite tests require correct collection timing and creatinine normalization.

Assay variability. Different commercial assays and laboratory methods have different calibrators and reference ranges. Laboratories commonly report total tryptase measured by immunoassay, but assays differ in analytic sensitivity and specificity; therefore, absolute cutoffs (e.g., 11.4 ng/mL) should be interpreted in light of the assay-specific reference interval reported by the performing laboratory. When comparing acute and baseline tryptase values, use the same assay and ideally the same laboratory to avoid inter-assay variability.

Differential diagnoses causing elevated tryptase. Elevated tryptase is not pathognomonic for anaphylaxis. Causes of raised tryptase include systemic mastocytosis and other clonal mast cell disorders, hereditary alpha-tryptasemia, end-stage renal disease (reduced clearance), severe myeloid malignancies, myelodysplastic syndromes, severe systemic inflammation, and, rarely, other hematologic malignancies that release tryptase or tryptase-like proteins. Technical factors and assay cross-reactivity may also produce spurious elevations. A persistently elevated baseline requires a different diagnostic pathway than a transient post-event rise.

Sensitivity and specificity. Tryptase elevation has good specificity for mast cell activation when the delta criterion is applied and when correlated with compatible clinical signs, but sensitivity is incomplete. Many cases of clinically diagnosed anaphylaxis—particularly those dominated by isolated mucocutaneous or bronchospastic symptoms without profound hypotension—may not produce a measurable rise in serum tryptase. Therefore, a normal tryptase does not exclude anaphylaxis.

# Reporting and documentation for later specialist review

Accurate documentation and clear reporting are essential so later reviewers (allergy/immunology or hematology) can interpret laboratory results in context. Important elements to document in the medical record and on the laboratory requisition include: the exact time of symptom onset (or best estimate), the time of each blood draw, treatments given prior to sampling (dose and time of epinephrine, antihistamines, corticosteroids, vasopressors, fluids), the clinical features present at the time of sampling (hypotension, hypoxemia, respiratory distress, urticaria, angioedema, GI symptoms), and whether the sample is intended as an acute/event sample or a baseline/convalescent sample.

When sending tryptase, indicate on the requisition whether the sample is an “acute” or “baseline” specimen and provide the time of symptom onset. If multiple samples are sent, label them clearly (e.g., “acute 1 hr,” “acute 3 hr,” “baseline >24 hr”), and ensure they are run by the same laboratory and assay. Photocopying or including a brief clinical summary with the sample can help the laboratory prioritize handling and assist specialists interpreting results later.

Follow-up planning. Clinicians should communicate with the patient and arrange outpatient follow-up to obtain a true baseline tryptase (≥24–48 hours after full resolution). If the baseline is elevated or the clinical history is suggestive of an underlying mast cell disorder, arrange timely referral to allergy/immunology and/or hematology for further evaluation, which may include repeat baseline tryptase, KIT mutation testing, bone marrow biopsy, or genetic testing for hereditary alpha-tryptasemia.

Conclusion

Laboratory testing—chiefly serum tryptase—can provide objective support for a diagnosis of mast cell activation in suspected anaphylaxis and can reveal underlying chronic mast cell disorders when baseline levels are elevated. Optimal use requires an appreciation of mast cell mediator biology (sources, isoforms, and kinetics), a planned sampling strategy (acute sample ideally within 1–2 hours, peak usually 1–4 hours, and baseline ≥24–48 hours after resolution), and application of both absolute and relative interpretation rules (including the $2 + 1.2 \times \text{baseline}$ criterion). Other tests (histamine/metabolites, CBC, lactate, blood gases, electrolytes) provide complementary information but have practical limitations. Awareness of assay variability, pre-analytic requirements, and alternative causes of tryptase elevation is necessary to avoid misinterpretation. Clear documentation of timing, clinical context, and treatments is crucial so specialist reviewers can accurately interpret results and recommend further evaluation when indicated.
