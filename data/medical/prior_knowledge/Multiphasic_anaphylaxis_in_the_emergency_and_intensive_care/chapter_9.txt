# Introduction

Mast cells are tissue-resident hematopoietic cells derived from the myeloid lineage that play key roles in innate and adaptive immunity, host defense against parasites, and modulation of allergic inflammation. They contain preformed mediators (histamine, proteases such as tryptase and chymase), lipid mediators (prostaglandins, leukotrienes), and a wide range of cytokines and chemokines that are released on activation. Disorders of mast cells encompass a spectrum from isolated cutaneous disease to systemic clonal proliferation and disorders of inappropriate mast cell mediator release. These disorders are clinically important because they produce a wide range of symptoms—cutaneous, gastrointestinal, cardiovascular, neurologic, and skeletal—and because they predispose to recurrent and sometimes life-threatening anaphylaxis.

This chapter provides a clinical-pathophysiologic overview of mast cell disorders with emphasis on classification, diagnostic criteria for systemic mastocytosis (SM), the molecular underpinnings (particularly KIT mutations), typical organ involvement and manifestations, bone marrow histopathology, and implications for anaphylaxis risk, monitoring and long-term management. The goal is to give a foundation for understanding how these entities present, how they are investigated, and how the findings influence acute and chronic treatment decisions.

# Classification of mast cell disorders (cutaneous mastocytosis, systemic mastocytosis, mast cell activation syndrome)

Mast cell disorders are categorized by the distribution of mast cell involvement and by whether they represent a clonal neoplastic process. Broadly, they include cutaneous mastocytosis (CM), systemic mastocytosis (SM) and mast cell activation syndrome (MCAS), with further sub-classifications within systemic disease that reflect disease burden and prognosis.

Cutaneous mastocytosis is the most common form in children and can also occur in adults. In children it typically presents as urticaria pigmentosa (maculopapular cutaneous mastocytosis), solitary mastocytoma, or diffuse cutaneous mastocytosis. Pediatric disease is often limited to the skin and frequently regresses spontaneously during adolescence. In adults, cutaneous findings are more frequently associated with systemic involvement; therefore adult-onset cutaneous manifestations raise concern for systemic disease.

Systemic mastocytosis is a clonal mast cell disorder in which mast cells infiltrate extracutaneous organs (bone marrow is most commonly involved). The World Health Organization (WHO) classifies SM into several categories reflecting clinical behavior and associated hematologic disease: indolent systemic mastocytosis (ISM), smoldering SM (SSM), aggressive SM (ASM), systemic mastocytosis with an associated hematologic neoplasm (SM-AHN), and mast cell leukemia (MCL). Indolent SM is characterized primarily by mediator-related symptoms and limited organ dysfunction; aggressive forms exhibit organ damage attributable to mast cell infiltration (cytopenias, malabsorption, hepatic dysfunction, large-volume splenomegaly, portal hypertension). SM-AHN includes additional clonal hematologic neoplasms (for example chronic myelomonocytic leukemia, myelodysplastic syndromes).

Mast cell activation syndrome is a heterogeneous group of disorders defined by episodic symptoms attributable to mast cell mediator release without necessarily demonstrating clonality or mast cell proliferation. MCAS is typically subdivided into primary (clonal, e.g., KIT-mutant mast cells but without meeting full criteria for SM), secondary (reactive, e.g., mast cell activation driven by allergy, autoimmune disease, or other underlying trigger), and idiopathic (no identifiable clonal marker or trigger). Diagnostic rigor is important because the term MCAS has been applied variably; true MCAS requires objective evidence of mast cell mediator release and clinically significant response to mast cell–directed therapy (see section on diagnostic criteria below).

Understanding the classification is clinically important: pediatric cutaneous disease has different prognosis and management than adult-onset cutaneous lesions, and the presence or absence of clonality (KIT mutation, aberrant CD25 expression) directs both prognostication and consideration of targeted therapy. Distinguishing MCAS from SM has implications for monitoring for progressive organ involvement and for long-term therapies that suppress mast cell burden.

# WHO diagnostic criteria for systemic mastocytosis: major and minor criteria overview

Diagnosis of systemic mastocytosis follows WHO criteria that combine morphologic, immunophenotypic, biochemical, and molecular findings into a framework of one major and four minor criteria. The diagnosis of SM requires either the major criterion plus one minor criterion, or at least three minor criteria.

The major criterion is defined by histologic documentation of multifocal dense infiltrates of mast cells in bone marrow or other extracutaneous organs. Specifically, these are collections or aggregates of mast cells composed of at least 15 mast cells per aggregate as appreciated in hematoxylin-eosin stained sections or through mast cell tryptase immunostaining.

The four minor criteria are: (1) more than 25% of mast cells in the infiltrate are spindle-shaped or show atypical morphology on histology, or there is the presence of >25% atypical mast cells in marrow smears; (2) detection of an activating point mutation at codon 816 of KIT (most commonly D816V) in bone marrow or another extracutaneous organ; (3) mast cells in bone marrow or blood expressing aberrant surface markers not found on normal mast cells, typically CD25 and/or CD2 in addition to KIT (CD117); and (4) a persistently elevated baseline serum tryptase level >20 ng/mL (measured on samples drawn during clinical stability, not during acute mast cell activation).

Thus, a single focal bone marrow mast cell aggregation plus one of these minor criteria establishes systemic disease; alternatively, three minor criteria in the absence of the major criterion suffice. It is important to recognize the timing and pre-analytic constraints of some tests: baseline serum tryptase should be measured outside of acute events (because tryptase rises transiently during anaphylaxis), and molecular testing requires sufficient sensitivity to detect the often low allele burden of KIT D816V in marrow or peripheral blood. These criteria integrate morphology, immunophenotype, genetics and biochemical evidence of mast cell activation and have been repeatedly validated in clinical practice.

# Role of KIT mutations (eg, D816V) and other molecular markers

KIT, a receptor tyrosine kinase encoded by the KIT proto-oncogene (CD117), is a key regulator of mast cell growth, differentiation, survival and activation. Stem cell factor (SCF) is the ligand for KIT. Activating mutations in KIT lead to constitutive kinase activity, independent of SCF, and thereby drive clonal expansion of mast cells in many cases of systemic mastocytosis.

The most common and clinically relevant mutation in adult SM is the D816V substitution in exon 17 of KIT. This mutation confers constitutive activation and is present in the majority of adult SM cases, including most indolent SM and almost all advanced SM cases. Detection of KIT D816V serves both as a diagnostic minor criterion and as a target for therapy, though it also has therapeutic implications because many early generation tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) do not inhibit KIT D816V effectively.

Molecular testing for KIT D816V requires sensitive methods because the mutant allele may be present at a low variant allele fraction, particularly in indolent disease. Techniques include allele-specific PCR, quantitative PCR, digital droplet PCR, and next-generation sequencing panels with deep coverage. Detection in bone marrow aspirate or enriched mast cell fractions increases sensitivity; peripheral blood testing with highly sensitive assays can sometimes detect the mutation and serve as a less invasive screening tool.

Beyond D816V, other KIT mutations may occur, especially in pediatric cutaneous mastocytosis or in rare adult cases, and non-D816V mutations can influence responsiveness to kinase inhibitors. Additionally, other molecular markers may be present in SM-AHN or advanced SM, and concurrent somatic mutations in genes associated with myeloid neoplasms (for example SRSF2, ASXL1, RUNX1) are associated with more aggressive disease and poorer prognosis. These additional mutations are often sought with broad myeloid next-generation sequencing panels when SM is suspected or when an associated hematologic neoplasm is present, as they inform prognosis and therapeutic choices.

Immunophenotypic markers include CD117 (KIT) which is expressed on normal mast cells, but aberrant expression of CD25 and/or CD2 on mast cells is a minor criterion for SM. CD30 expression has been described in some cases of advanced SM and may have diagnostic or prognostic relevance. Flow cytometry and immunohistochemical stains are complementary in detecting aberrant antigen expression.

# Typical organ involvement (skin, bone marrow, liver, spleen, GI tract) and clinical manifestations

Mast cell disorders produce a spectrum of organ-specific signs and symptoms that reflect both mediator-related effects and tissue infiltration. Clinical manifestations can be grouped by primary organ systems.

Skin: Cutaneous involvement may be the first clue to a mast cell disorder. Urticaria pigmentosa (maculopapular cutaneous mastocytosis) presents as hyperpigmented macules or papules that often urticate (Darier sign) after stroking. Solitary mastocytomas are localized nodules often seen in children. Diffuse cutaneous mastocytosis causes widespread erythema, blistering, and increased risk of systemic mediator release. Pruritus, flushing, and dermatographism are common mediator-related symptoms. In adults, the presence of cutaneous lesions commonly coexists with systemic disease.

Bone marrow and hematopoietic system: The bone marrow is the most commonly involved extracutaneous organ. Bone marrow infiltration may be patchy and is the focus for histopathologic diagnosis. Symptoms related to marrow involvement include cytopenias (anemia, leukopenia, thrombocytopenia) when infiltration is extensive and mast cell burden is high. Bone involvement includes osteopenia and osteoporosis, particularly in adults with SM; pathogenic mechanisms include mast cell-derived mediators (e.g., histamine and cytokines) and local osteoclast activation. Patients may suffer pathologic fractures and bone pain. Bone mineral density testing (DEXA) is recommended in adults with SM; values are interpreted using standard T-scores with osteoporosis defined as T-score ≤ -2.5. Vertebral compression fractures are an important clinical consequence.

Liver and spleen: Mast cell infiltration can produce hepatomegaly, portal fibrosis, cholestatic liver chemistry abnormalities, and ultimately portal hypertension in aggressive disease. Splenomegaly (sometimes massive) is common in advanced SM and may be accompanied by hypersplenism leading to cytopenias. Liver function tests (AST, ALT, alkaline phosphatase, bilirubin) and imaging may reveal organomegaly or structural damage; progressive hepatic dysfunction is a marker of aggressive disease.

Gastrointestinal tract: GI symptoms are among the most frequent mediator-related complaints and include abdominal pain, cramping, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, and malabsorption leading to weight loss and nutrient deficiencies. Symptoms can be episodic and related to mast cell mediator release or chronic owing to mucosal infiltration and damage. Chronic diarrhea and malabsorption may require nutritional assessment and management.

Cardiovascular and anaphylaxis: Mast cell mediators (histamine, platelet-activating factor, prostaglandin D2, leukotrienes) produce vasodilation and increased vascular permeability, which underlie flushing, hypotension and anaphylaxis. Patients with SM and some forms of MCAS are at increased risk for severe, recurrent anaphylaxis, often with atypical triggers (e.g., insect venoms, medications) and sometimes with no identifiable trigger. Baseline tryptase tends to be higher in clonal mast cell disorders, and an elevated baseline tryptase is associated with greater anaphylaxis risk, though normal tryptase does not exclude risk.

Other organ systems: Neuropsychiatric symptoms (anxiety, cognitive fog), musculoskeletal pain, and constitutional symptoms (fatigue, weight loss) are commonly reported and are often multifactorial—mediator effects, chronic inflammation, sleep disturbance, and comorbid conditions contribute. Pulmonary involvement is uncommon but may present as bronchospasm. Renal and neurologic complications are rare but can occur in advanced disease.

Clinically, distinguishing mediator-driven symptoms from organ dysfunction due to mast cell infiltration is important because it guides urgency and type of therapy: antihistamines and mediator-directed therapies can control many symptoms, while organ damage from infiltrative disease may require cytoreductive therapy.

# Bone marrow biopsy and histopathologic findings (mast cell aggregates, CD25/CD2 expression, morphology)

Bone marrow biopsy with aspiration and immunohistochemistry is central to diagnosing systemic mastocytosis. Because mast cell infiltration can be focal and patchy, adequate sampling and careful histologic examination are necessary. The bone marrow evaluation includes morphology on H&E stains, special stains for mast cell tryptase and/or Giemsa, immunohistochemistry for CD117 (KIT), and flow cytometry to characterize aberrant antigen expression.

Histopathologically, the major diagnostic feature is multifocal dense infiltrates (aggregates) of mast cells in bone marrow or other extracutaneous organs. The WHO definition specifies aggregates of 15 or more mast cells per cluster. Morphologically, neoplastic mast cells are often spindle-shaped with elongated cytoplasmic processes and atypical nuclear contours; in smoldering and aggressive disease, increased numbers of atypical mast cells can be seen in marrow smears. In indolent disease, more rounded and mature-appearing mast cells may predominate, but abnormal clustering remains a key clue.

Immunophenotyping is crucial because neoplastic mast cells commonly express aberrant antigens not seen on normal mast cells. Normal mast cells are CD117 (KIT) positive and typically negative for CD25 and CD2. In SM, mast cells frequently express CD25 and/or CD2; detection of CD25 expression on mast cells in bone marrow is a minor WHO diagnostic criterion. Flow cytometry may also reveal expression of CD30 in some cases, particularly advanced disease. The profile CD117+/CD25+ (and sometimes CD2+) on mast cells is highly suggestive of clonality.

Mast cell tryptase immunostaining helps visualize both individual mast cells and their aggregates; tryptase is a useful stain because it highlights mast cells even when morphology is subtle. Quantification of mast cell burden and description of the pattern of infiltration (focal aggregates versus diffuse infiltration) are part of the pathology report and are used in the SM diagnostic algorithm and in classification (e.g., criteria for B- and C-findings that define smoldering or aggressive disease).

Because marrow infiltration can be patchy, a negative marrow biopsy does not completely exclude systemic disease especially if other minor criteria such as a KIT D816V mutation or elevated baseline tryptase are present; repeat sampling or testing of other extracutaneous sites may be needed in selected cases. Aspirates can also be useful for cytogenetics and molecular testing to identify concurrent myeloid neoplasms.

# Implications of mast cell disorders for anaphylaxis risk, monitoring and long-term management

Mast cell disorders carry an elevated risk of anaphylaxis compared with the general population, and this risk is particularly pronounced among patients with clonal mast cell disease and/or elevated baseline serum tryptase. Clinical management therefore has two parallel aims: prevention and prompt treatment of acute mast cell–mediator events (notably anaphylaxis), and control of chronic symptoms and organ damage through mediator suppression or reduction of mast cell burden when indicated.

Risk of anaphylaxis: Patients with SM (especially those with KIT D816V or other clonal markers) and some types of MCAS have recurrent severe anaphylaxis that may be triggered by hymenoptera venom (notably bee and wasp stings), medications (opioids, radiocontrast, neuromuscular blockers), and less commonly foods. In many cases of SM-associated anaphylaxis, no clear trigger is identified. Baseline serum tryptase correlates imperfectly with anaphylaxis risk, but extremely elevated baseline tryptase concentrations (for example, >20 ng/mL) raise concern for underlying clonal mast cell disease and heightened risk. For diagnosing anaphylaxis specifically related to mast cell activation, a pragmatic biochemical criterion is an acute rise in serum tryptase above baseline by at least 20% of baseline plus 2 ng/mL (i.e., acute tryptase > baseline × 1.2 + 2 ng/mL) measured within 3–4 hours of symptom onset.

Monitoring: Patients with diagnosed SM or established MCAS require a tailored monitoring strategy. Baseline assessments often include serum tryptase, complete blood count with differential (to assess cytopenias suggesting marrow involvement), liver function tests, alkaline phosphatase, bilirubin, coagulation profile if clinically indicated, and imaging (ultrasound or cross-sectional imaging) for organomegaly. Bone mineral density testing (DEXA) is indicated in adults because of the risk of osteoporosis. Periodic reassessment of blood counts, liver chemistry and tryptase (frequency individualized by disease subtype and therapy) is appropriate to detect progression to smoldering or aggressive disease. In SM-AHN or advanced SM, more intensive monitoring and hematologic evaluation are required.

Acute management: For anaphylactic episodes, standard emergency management applies—immediate intramuscular epinephrine is first-line, along with airway, breathing and circulation support, oxygen, intravenous fluids, and adjunctive therapies (H1 and H2 antihistamines, bronchodilators, and corticosteroids for biphasic reactions). Given the increased risk in mast cell disorders, patients should receive education on early recognition of symptoms and indications for epinephrine. Most clinicians prescribe at least one epinephrine autoinjector; patients with prior severe reactions or recurrent severe reactions should have multiple autoinjectors and individualized emergency action plans.

Preventive strategies include trigger avoidance (where triggers are known), venom immunotherapy for patients with venom-induced anaphylaxis (venom immunotherapy is highly effective and recommended, though close collaboration with an allergist experienced in mast cell disorders is important), and premedication strategies for unavoidable exposures (e.g., pre-procedure H1/H2 blockade and steroids for contrast media or anesthesia as directed by anesthesia/allergy consultation). For patients with confirmed clonal mast cell disease, prophylactic antibiotics are not indicated specifically for mastocytosis but general preventive care (vaccinations, standard infection prevention) applies.

Chronic symptomatic management centers on mediator-blocking therapies. H1 antihistamines (both H1 non-sedating for daytime control and sedating at night when needed) and H2 blockers reduce cutaneous and gastrointestinal symptoms. Leukotriene receptor antagonists (e.g., montelukast) and inhibitors of mast cell mediator release (cromolyn sodium for GI symptoms) can be helpful. Oral corticosteroids are effective for severe flare control but their long-term use is limited by toxicity. Bisphosphonates are indicated for osteoporosis; calcium and vitamin D supplementation as appropriate. For advanced or progressive SM with organ damage, cytoreductive therapies to lower mast cell burden are indicated. Agents include interferon-alpha and cladribine (2-chlorodeoxyadenosine); more recently, targeted KIT inhibitors with activity against D816V-mutant KIT—midostaurin and avapritinib—have demonstrated efficacy in advanced SM and are approved in many jurisdictions. Choice of systemic therapy depends on disease subtype, mutation profile, associated hematologic neoplasms, symptoms and comorbidities. Enrollment in clinical trials is appropriate when available.

Long-term management also addresses psychosocial aspects, education, and coordination among specialties (allergy/immunology, hematology/oncology, dermatology, gastroenterology, endocrinology, and primary care). Patients benefit from written action plans, training in autoinjector use, and counseling about medications and vaccinations with potential to trigger reactions. Pre-procedural planning (perioperative and anesthesia considerations) and careful medication reconciliation to avoid agents with high histamine-release potential (e.g., certain opiates, vancomycin rapid infusion) reduce risk.

Finally, genetic counseling and family assessment are sometimes relevant, particularly in familial clustering or pediatric-onset disease, although most adult SM cases are sporadic. Prognosis depends heavily on subtype—indolent SM often has near-normal life expectancy with symptom control, while aggressive SM, SM-AHN and mast cell leukemia have markedly worse outcomes and require specialist-directed cytoreductive treatment.

# Summary

Mast cell disorders encompass a spectrum from localized cutaneous disease to systemic clonal mast cell proliferation and disorders of inappropriate mediator release. Accurate classification—cutaneous mastocytosis, systemic mastocytosis with WHO-mandated major and minor criteria, and mast cell activation syndromes—guides diagnosis and management. KIT D816V and other mutations are central to pathogenesis, diagnosis and increasingly to targeted therapy. Bone marrow biopsy with careful histology and immunophenotyping (notably CD25/CD2 aberrant expression) is central to identifying systemic disease. Mast cell disorders carry significant morbidity both from chronic mediator effects and from risks of severe anaphylaxis; management combines acute preparedness (epinephrine, emergency planning), chronic mediator blockade, organ-specific management (bisphosphonates for bone disease, hepatic monitoring), and, for advanced disease, cytoreductive or targeted molecular therapy. Close multidisciplinary care, patient education and individualized monitoring strategies are essential to optimize outcomes.
