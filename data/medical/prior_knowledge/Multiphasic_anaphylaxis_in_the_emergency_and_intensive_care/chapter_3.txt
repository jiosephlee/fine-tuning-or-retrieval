Introduction

Anaphylaxis is an acute, systemic hypersensitivity reaction characterized by rapid mediator release from activated mast cells and basophils. The ensuing pathophysiologic cascade simultaneously targets the cardiovascular and respiratory systems and can rapidly progress to life‑threatening hypotension, airway compromise, and hypoxaemia. Understanding how normal cardiopulmonary physiology is perturbed by vasoactive and bronchoconstrictive mediators is essential for clinical recognition, triage, and rational intervention. This chapter reviews the normal cardiovascular and respiratory functions most relevant to shock and hypoxaemia, then details how mediators produce vasodilation, increased vascular permeability, bronchoconstriction and upper airway edema. It explains the consequences for tissue perfusion and oxygen delivery, describes compensatory mechanisms and why they fail in severe reactions, and contrasts the pathophysiology of single‑phase, biphasic, multiphasic and refractory anaphylaxis to inform monitoring and treatment decisions.

# Normal cardiovascular and respiratory physiology relevant to shock and hypoxaemia

Cardiovascular physiology in the context of shock centers on the determinants of systemic oxygen delivery (DO2), which is the product of cardiac output (CO) and arterial oxygen content (CaO2). Arterial oxygen content is given by the equation
CaO2 = (SaO2 × Hb × 1.34) + (0.0031 × PaO2),
where SaO2 is arterial oxygen saturation, Hb is hemoglobin concentration (g/dL), and PaO2 is arterial oxygen tension (mmHg). Under normal conditions, CaO2 is maintained by normal hemoglobin (adult male 13.5–17.5 g/dL; adult female 12.0–15.5 g/dL), near‑maximal oxygen saturation (SaO2 ≥ 95%), and adequate PaO2 (normal 80–100 mmHg). Cardiac output, the other major determinant of DO2, is the product of stroke volume and heart rate and depends on preload, afterload and contractility. Systolic blood pressure in adults is normally 90–140 mmHg; mean arterial pressure (MAP) of at least 65 mmHg is commonly targeted to preserve organ perfusion.

Microcirculatory flow is not a simple linear function of macrocirculatory variables. Capillary recruitment, arteriolar tone, endothelial integrity and plasma oncotic pressure govern effective tissue perfusion and oxygen extraction. Normal systemic vascular resistance (SVR) maintains perfusion pressure and organ blood flow distribution; reflex sympathetic activation can rapidly augment SVR and heart rate during perturbations.

Respiratory physiology relevant to hypoxaemia begins with ventilation, gas exchange and matching of ventilation (V) to perfusion (Q). Alveolar gas exchange efficiency depends on adequate alveolar ventilation (PaCO2 normal 35–45 mmHg) and preserved diffusion surface and capillary flow. Hypoxaemia may result from hypoventilation, diffusion limitation, shunt (perfusion without ventilation), low inspired oxygen, or V/Q mismatch. In acute bronchospasm and airway edema, V/Q mismatch and shunt predominate as airways and alveoli become under‑ventilated relative to perfusion, producing hypoxaemia and, if severe, hypercapnia.

Normal compensatory responses to mild reductions in DO2 include tachycardia, increased contractility (β1‑adrenergic), peripheral vasoconstriction (α‑adrenergic), increased respiratory rate, and mobilization of intravascular volume through constriction of venous capacitance vessels. These mechanisms can maintain perfusion for a time but are limited by myocardial oxygen supply, catecholamine reserve, intravascular volume and ongoing pathologic processes.

# Vasodilation, increased vascular permeability and distributive shock mechanics

Anaphylaxis produces a profound distributive shock that stems from two interrelated vascular phenomena: marked vasodilation and increased microvascular permeability. Mast cells and basophils activated by IgE cross‑linking, direct complement activation, or other mechanisms release a complex mixture of mediators—histamine, platelet‑activating factor (PAF), prostaglandin D2 (PGD2), leukotrienes (LTC4, LTD4, LTE4), nitric oxide (NO) via inducible nitric oxide synthase stimulation, and various cytokines and chemokines. These mediators act on vascular smooth muscle and endothelium to reduce systemic vascular resistance and to allow rapid extravasation of plasma.

Histamine causes arteriolar and venular smooth muscle relaxation through endothelial H1 receptor stimulation and NO release; this produces a fall in SVR and pooling of blood in the venous capacitance beds, reducing venous return (preload) and cardiac output. PAF contributes to vasodilation and also induces platelet aggregation and further inflammatory mediator generation. Leukotrienes, while primarily bronchoconstrictive, can contribute to increased vascular permeability, and prostaglandins have variable vascular effects that often augment vasodilation. Collectively, these factors can drop MAP precipitously.

Increased vascular permeability occurs through several mechanisms: endothelial cell contraction and retraction producing intercellular gaps; disruption of intercellular junctions including cadherin and tight junction complexes; and glycocalyx shedding. The result is a rapid shift of plasma fluid and proteins into the interstitium and extravascular compartments. Clinically, this causes hemoconcentration, decreased intravascular volume and worsening hypotension despite peripheral edema. The combination of vasodilation and plasma loss creates the classic distributive shock of anaphylaxis—low SVR with circulating volume deficit and inadequate tissue perfusion.

Microcirculatory dysfunction also develops: endothelial activation promotes leukocyte and platelet adhesion, capillary plugging, and heterogeneous blood flow, which further impairs oxygen extraction despite sometimes-preserved or even increased bulk cardiac output. This maldistribution of flow, with shunting and no‑flow areas within capillary beds, results in tissue hypoxia disproportionate to measured systemic hemodynamics.

# Bronchoconstriction and upper airway edema mechanisms

Bronchoconstriction in anaphylaxis is a multifactorial process driven by direct smooth muscle constrictors, neural reflexes, and inflammatory cell recruitment. Key mediators include histamine, leukotrienes (particularly LTC4, LTD4 and LTE4), prostaglandin D2 and PAF. Histamine acts on H1 receptors on bronchial smooth muscle to increase intracellular calcium and cause constriction; leukotrienes are more potent and longer‑lasting bronchoconstrictors than histamine, and their action on CysLT receptors produces sustained bronchospasm and increased bronchial hyperresponsiveness. PAF can directly induce bronchial smooth muscle contraction and enhance the response to other mediators.

Neural mechanisms amplify bronchoconstriction: airway sensory nerves (C‑fibers) activated by mediators can trigger vagally mediated bronchoconstrictor reflexes and mucus secretion. Bronchial mucosal edema—resulting from the same increased permeability that affects systemic capillaries—narrows airway lumens, increases resistance, and reduces airway compliance. Excessive mucus production further occludes airways. In the small airways, even modest reductions in radius produce large increases in resistance according to Poiseuille’s relation, so that diffuse bronchospasm and edema can rapidly produce severe airflow limitation and ventilatory failure.

Upper airway edema (laryngeal and pharyngeal) arises primarily from increased permeability of the submucosal microvasculature and resultant fluid extravasation into loose connective tissue spaces. The larynx and pharynx are particularly susceptible because loose submucosal tissue allows rapid swelling that can compromise the glottic aperture. Histamine, bradykinin (sometimes generated via complement and contact system activation), and leukotrienes mediate vasodilation and endothelial gap formation locally. Angioedema in some contexts may be mediated by bradykinin rather than histamine—this is a mechanistic distinction relevant to treatment choices in nonallergic angioedema but is less central to IgE‑mediated anaphylaxis where histamine predominates. Regardless of mediator, upper airway edema is dangerous because it can obstruct both spontaneous ventilation and the ability to intubate.

From a physiologic standpoint, bronchospasm increases airway resistance and work of breathing, leading to tachypnea, dynamic hyperinflation and, in severe cases, respiratory muscle fatigue and hypoventilation with rising PaCO2. Upper airway obstruction preferentially compromises oxygenation because it limits airflow into the alveoli even if perfusion is maintained, producing a large shunt fraction and rapid desaturation.

# Effects on tissue perfusion and oxygen delivery

The combination of reduced intravascular volume, decreased venous return, and arteriolar dilation diminishes cardiac preload and can reduce cardiac output. When cardiac output falls, DO2 declines according to DO2 = CO × CaO2. Even when cardiac output is preserved or increased (as in distributive states with high-output shock), the maldistribution of flow and microcirculatory shunting can impair effective oxygen delivery at the tissue level. Endothelial activation impairs autoregulation and promotes capillary leak and interstitial edema, which increases diffusion distances for oxygen and impairs oxygen extraction.

Hypoxaemia from airway obstruction and bronchospasm reduces CaO2 by decreasing SaO2 and PaO2; hypoxaemia compounds the effects of hypotension by reducing oxygen content available for tissues. Anemia (low Hb) or decreased hemoglobin saturation will multiplicatively reduce DO2. Tissue hypoxia stimulates anaerobic metabolism and lactate production; an elevated serum lactate (normal <2 mmol/L) is a marker of global or regional tissue hypoperfusion.

The myocardium is vulnerable to both supply and demand imbalance in anaphylaxis. Hypotension and coronary vasospasm (mediated by histamine and other vasoconstrictive mediators in coronary vessels) can precipitate myocardial ischemia. In addition, tachycardia and increased contractile demand increase myocardial oxygen consumption; together these can precipitate arrhythmias or pump failure. Clinically significant reductions in cerebral perfusion pressure can lead to syncope, confusion or loss of consciousness.

Microvascular stasis, leukocyte plugging and platelet aggregation further compromise oxygen extraction despite restoration of systemic blood pressure. Thus, achieving normalization of MAP alone does not guarantee effective tissue oxygenation—adequate intravascular volume, correction of hypoxaemia and reversal of mediator effects are all necessary for recovery.

# Compensatory responses and failure modes in severe anaphylaxis

The body launches rapid compensatory responses to hypotension and hypoxaemia: sympathetic activation increases heart rate and systemic vasoconstriction (except where local mediators oppose alpha‑adrenergic tone), and renin–angiotensin–aldosterone and vasopressin systems are engaged to conserve intravascular volume. Increased respiratory drive attempts to correct hypoxaemia. However, mediators released in anaphylaxis can blunt or overwhelm these compensations.

Failure modes include refractory hypotension, where vasopressors and fluids are ineffective because widespread endothelial failure, ongoing mediator release and severe capillary leak prevent restoration of effective intravascular volume and SVR. Myocardial depression can occur from direct actions of mediators (e.g., PAF has negative inotropic effects) and from ischemia secondary to hypotension or coronary vasospasm, leading to low‑output states that are not responsive to vasoconstriction alone. Patients on beta‑blockers may fail to mount an adequate tachycardic or inotropic response and may be particularly prone to refractory shock; prostaglandin and PAF effects are not countered by beta‑agonism in these patients.

Airway failure is another major failure mode: progressive laryngeal edema can obstruct airflow, and severe bronchospasm can produce near‑complete ventilatory obstruction. Respiratory muscle fatigue and hypercapnic respiratory failure may ensue; hypoxaemia rapidly worsens if mechanical ventilation cannot be established.

Arrhythmias and cardiac arrest may arise from severe hypoxaemia, ischemia, or direct effects of mediators or medications. Coronary vasospasm or thrombosis (Kounis syndrome) can precipitate myocardial infarction with attendant pump failure. Disseminated intravascular coagulation and multiorgan dysfunction can follow severe, prolonged anaphylaxis, reflecting systemic endothelial injury and widespread inflammation.

Clinical decision points arise from understanding these failure modes: early intramuscular epinephrine to reverse mediator effects and support perfusion; aggressive intravascular volume replacement to correct capillary leak–induced hypovolaemia; early airway assessment and low threshold for securing the airway when progressive stridor, hoarseness, or rapidly declining oxygenation are present; consideration of intravenous vasopressors (epinephrine, norepinephrine) and adjuncts such as glucagon when β‑blockade is present; and monitoring for myocardial ischemia and arrhythmia.

# Pathophysiologic distinctions between single‑phase, biphasic, multiphasic and refractory courses

Anaphylaxis is heterogeneous in temporal pattern. A single‑phase anaphylactic reaction is the most common presentation: rapid onset of symptoms after exposure, with resolution after treatment and no recurrence. Biphasic anaphylaxis describes a clinical course in which symptoms recur after apparent resolution, usually within 1–72 hours, most commonly within 8–10 hours. Multiphasic reactions involve two or more recurrences over a protracted period. Refractory anaphylaxis refers to severe anaphylaxis that does not respond adequately to initial standard therapy (intramuscular epinephrine, supplemental oxygen, airway support and crystalloid resuscitation) and requires escalating interventions including repeated intravenous epinephrine, vasopressors, and often intensive care.

The mechanisms underlying biphasic and multiphasic reactions are incompletely elucidated. Proposed explanations include persistent or delayed absorption of the allergen (e.g., from depot preparations or ingested food), ongoing de novo synthesis of mediators (particularly leukotrienes and cytokines) after initial mast cell degranulation, secondary waves of inflammatory cell activation and recruitment, and incomplete initial treatment allowing residual mediator activity. Some evidence implicates continued antigen exposure or ongoing antigen release from tissue stores as a driver of delayed recurrences. The degree of initial severity appears to correlate with risk of biphasic reactions, as does delayed administration of epinephrine.

Refractory anaphylaxis often reflects either a massive mediator load or patient‑specific modifiers that blunt therapeutic responses. Contributing factors include delayed or inadequate epinephrine dosing, concomitant use of beta‑blockers (which reduce epinephrine efficacy), ACE inhibitors (which may worsen angioedema by increasing bradykinin), underlying mast cell disorders such as systemic mastocytosis, or biphasic mediator release with ongoing production. Pathophysiologically, refractory responses may represent sustained vasodilatory mediator generation, severe capillary leak resulting in refractory intravascular depletion, direct myocardial depression, or a combination of these. The presence of comorbid cardiac disease or advanced age worsens the reserve to tolerate hypotension and hypoxaemia.

From a clinical standpoint, recognition of these distinctions guides monitoring and disposition: patients who had severe initial hypotension, delayed epinephrine, persistent symptoms, or need for multiple epinephrine doses have higher risk for biphasic or multiphasic courses and often warrant prolonged observation (commonly 4–24 hours or longer depending on risk factors). Refractory cases require ICU‑level care, invasive hemodynamic monitoring, and consideration of alternative pharmacologic strategies (e.g., glucagon in beta‑blocked patients, continuous infusion of epinephrine or other vasopressors, and advanced airway/ventilatory support).

Therapeutic rationale must be grounded in the pathophysiology: epinephrine is the first‑line therapy because of its combined alpha‑adrenergic vasoconstrictive effect (restores SVR and reduces mucosal edema), beta‑1 effects (increase heart rate and contractility to augment CO) and beta‑2 effects (bronchodilation and decreased mediator release from mast cells). Intramuscular administration into the mid‑anterolateral thigh (adult dose 0.3–0.5 mg of 1:1000 solution) provides rapid and reliable absorption for most patients; intravenous bolus epinephrine (1:10,000 concentration) is reserved for severe hypotension or cardiac arrest because of the risk of arrhythmia and hypertension. Large‑volume isotonic crystalloid resuscitation (e.g., 1–2 L in adults initially, guided by response; in children typically 20 mL/kg boluses) is necessary to counter capillary leak and venous pooling. Supplemental oxygen and bronchodilators (inhaled β2 agonists) address hypoxaemia and bronchospasm, but neither replaces epinephrine for systemic mediator reversal. Antihistamines and corticosteroids are adjuncts: H1 and H2 blockers may blunt cutaneous and some cardiovascular effects of histamine but have delayed onset and do not reverse airway obstruction or hypotension. Corticosteroids may reduce the risk of protracted or biphasic reactions by suppressing later inflammatory mediator synthesis, but their immediate effect is limited, and they should not replace epinephrine.

Summary

Anaphylaxis disrupts normal cardiopulmonary physiology through mediator‑driven vasodilation, capillary leak, bronchospasm and airway edema. These processes reduce effective intravascular volume, alter microcirculatory flow, impair oxygen delivery and can overwhelm compensatory mechanisms, leading to refractory shock, respiratory failure, and cardiac complications. Timely therapeutic interventions are directed at reversing mediator effects (primarily with epinephrine), restoring intravascular volume and oxygenation, and anticipating complications based on the likely temporal pattern of the reaction. A mechanistic understanding of these processes informs rational choices about medication selection, routes of administration, fluid resuscitation targets and monitoring intensity.
