Introduction

Clonal mast cell disorders encompass a spectrum from cutaneous disease to systemic mastocytosis (SM) and mast cell leukemia. Laboratory evaluation integrates marrow histology, cytology, immunophenotyping and molecular testing to demonstrate clonality, define disease category and provide prognostic information that guides management. This chapter provides a practical, clinically oriented review of the techniques and key findings on bone marrow aspirate and trephine biopsy, the immunohistochemical and flow cytometric markers used to identify neoplastic mast cells, the molecular diagnostics centered on KIT mutations and other recurrent genomic alterations, and how these data are synthesized against World Health Organization (WHO) diagnostic criteria. The chapter also addresses reporting standards, interpretation pitfalls when sampling is incomplete, and the implications of laboratory results for treatment selection and referral to specialist centers.

# Bone marrow aspirate and trephine biopsy: technique and key histologic findings

Bone marrow examination in suspected clonal mast cell disease should include both aspirate (cytology) and trephine (core) biopsy with touch preparations. Mast cell neoplasms often produce a “dry tap” or poor aspirate yield because of marrow fibrosis or compact mast cell aggregates; therefore, a trephine biopsy is essential. Proper technique includes obtaining adequate core length (ideally ≥1.5–2 cm) and multiple sections for decalcification and histology. Touch imprints or crush smears from the biopsy provide cytologic detail when the aspirate is limited.

Key histologic features to evaluate on trephine biopsy include the presence, distribution and architecture of mast cell infiltrates. The WHO major criterion for systemic mastocytosis is multifocal, dense aggregates of mast cells (defined as at least 15 mast cells per cluster) in bone marrow or other extracutaneous organs. These aggregates are often perivascular, peri-trabecular or interstitial. Mast cells in indolent SM are frequently round or slightly ovoid with abundant metachromatic granules, whereas in more aggressive variants they may be spindle-shaped, atypical, or show hypogranulation.

On aspirate smears and touch preps, count and morphologic assessment of mast cells is important. A minor diagnostic criterion is the presence of atypical mast cells with an immature or spindle-shaped morphology constituting >25% of mast cells on cytology. Because mast cells can degranulate during aspiration, evaluation of multiple fields and correlation with trephine morphology is necessary. Special stains—such as Giemsa or toluidine blue—highlight metachromatic granules and facilitate mast cell identification. Mast cell tryptase immunostaining further confirms mast cell lineage and can make subtle infiltrates more conspicuous.

Additional marrow features that should be recorded include cellularity, fibrosis (reticulin or trichrome stain), and evidence of associated hematologic neoplasms (e.g., myelodysplasia, chronic myelomonocytic leukemia). Marrow fibrosis is common and may contribute to aspirate failure; grading reticulin fibrosis (e.g., WHO/European consensus scale) provides prognostic information. Documentation of sampling adequacy (core length, number of sections) and ancillary studies performed is necessary for correct interpretation.

# Immunohistochemistry and flow cytometry markers (CD117, CD25, CD2) on mast cells

Immunophenotyping by immunohistochemistry (IHC) and flow cytometry is central to distinguishing neoplastic mast cells from reactive mast cell hyperplasia. The most routinely used markers are CD117 (c-KIT), mast cell tryptase, CD25 and CD2. On IHC, tryptase is a sensitive and specific marker of mast cell lineage; CD117 is also expressed on mast cells, but is not specific because other hematopoietic cells (e.g., progenitors) and some non-hematopoietic cells also express c-KIT.

Aberrant expression of CD25 and/or CD2 on mast cells is regarded as evidence of clonality in adults. In practice, CD25 is more sensitive and specific than CD2 for neoplastic mast cells. Neoplastic mast cells typically show strong membranous and/or cytoplasmic CD25 staining on IHC; CD2 expression is variable. The WHO minor diagnostic criteria include expression of CD2 and/or CD25 on bone marrow mast cells. Some laboratories also assess CD30, which may be expressed in advanced SM and can have diagnostic and potentially therapeutic implications.

Flow cytometry enables high-sensitivity detection (often to 0.01–0.1% depending on panel and cell numbers) of aberrant mast cell immunophenotype when sample cellularity permits. Typical gating strategy identifies high side-scatter, low-to-intermediate CD45 events and uses CD117 and tryptase where available; surface CD117 (c-KIT) is usually bright. The hallmark aberrant pattern is coexpression of CD25 on CD117+ mast cells. CD2 can be tested but is less sensitive. Flow cytometry has the advantage of quantifying the percentage of mast cells among total nucleated cells and detecting small neoplastic populations, but interpretation requires careful gating because basophils and other cells can express overlapping signals. Mast cells may be lost during processing, particularly if the aspirate is hemodilute or if mast cell aggregates are tightly adherent to stroma; therefore, correlation with trephine biopsy results is necessary.

When reporting, laboratories should state the antibodies used, the thresholds for positivity (e.g., any membrane staining above background for IHC; percentage of cells for flow cytometry), and whether expression is considered aberrant relative to normal mast cell controls. Because CD117 is not discriminatory for clonality, the emphasis should be on demonstration of CD25 and/or CD2 on mast cells.

# Molecular testing for KIT mutations and other relevant genomic alterations

The canonical molecular lesion in adult systemic mastocytosis is an activating mutation in KIT, most commonly the D816V substitution in exon 17. Detection of KIT D816V is a WHO minor diagnostic criterion. Testing should be performed on bone marrow as the primary specimen; peripheral blood testing can be used as an initial screen but has lower sensitivity when allele burden is low.

Available methods for detecting KIT D816V include allele-specific PCR (AS-PCR), quantitative allele-specific PCR, droplet digital PCR (ddPCR), and next-generation sequencing (NGS). Sensitivity varies: conventional Sanger sequencing has limited sensitivity (roughly 10–20% mutant allele fraction), making it insufficient to rule out D816V. Targeted AS-PCR and ddPCR can detect mutant allele fractions down to approximately 0.01–0.1% (method-dependent), and are preferred when available. NGS panels typically achieve limits of detection around 1–5% but offer the advantage of profiling multiple genes simultaneously to detect additional somatic mutations.

Testing strategy: if peripheral blood shows a robust D816V allele by sensitive PCR, marrow biopsy can still be performed but molecular confirmation is already present. If peripheral blood is negative by a method whose sensitivity is adequate, a bone marrow specimen should be tested because marrow often has a higher allele burden. Laboratories must report the assay used and its analytic sensitivity and quantitate mutant allele burden where possible (e.g., variant allele fraction or copies per microliter). Serial measurement of allele burden can be used to monitor response to targeted therapy in advanced disease, but interpretation must account for assay variability.

Although D816V in exon 17 dominates adult SM, other KIT mutations (for example in exons 8, 9, 11 or 13) occur, particularly in children or in rare adult cases, and influence treatment choices because some kinase inhibitors (e.g., imatinib) are ineffective against D816V but may be active against other KIT mutants. Broad NGS panels that include KIT exons beyond 17 are therefore useful. In advanced SM, additional somatic mutations in genes implicated in myeloid neoplasms—classically SRSF2, ASXL1, and RUNX1 (the “S/A/R” panel)—are associated with worse prognosis. Comprehensive myeloid NGS panels will detect mutations in genes such as TET2, DNMT3A, NRAS, CBL, EZH2, and others; the presence and number of non-KIT high-risk mutations inform prognosis and may impact eligibility for clinical trials or consideration of allogeneic stem cell transplantation.

When reporting molecular results, specify the technique, the regions analyzed, the limit of detection, and the allele fraction. Clinically relevant decision points include: (1) detection of KIT D816V supports a minor WHO criterion; (2) absence of D816V by a low-sensitivity method does not exclude the mutation—state the assay limitations; (3) detection of alternative KIT mutations or high-risk additional mutations should trigger specialist input regarding targeted therapy options and prognosis.

# Correlation of laboratory and histologic findings with WHO diagnostic criteria

The current WHO diagnostic framework for systemic mastocytosis uses one major and four minor criteria. The major criterion is multifocal dense infiltrates of mast cells (≥15 per aggregate) in extracutaneous organs, typically bone marrow. The four minor criteria are: (1) >25% of mast cells in marrow aspirate smears are spindle-shaped or atypical, or of these cells are atypical in biopsy sections; (2) detection of KIT point mutation at codon 816 in marrow or another extracutaneous organ; (3) mast cells in marrow or blood expressing CD2 and/or CD25 in addition to normal mast cell markers; (4) serum total tryptase persistently >20 ng/mL (unless there is an associated clonal myeloid disorder that elevates tryptase independently).

Diagnosis of systemic mastocytosis requires either the major criterion plus one minor criterion, or at least three minor criteria. Therefore, concordant findings across morphology, immunophenotype and molecular testing are often necessary to establish the diagnosis. For example, a patient with dense mast cell aggregates on trephine plus aberrant CD25 expression or a confirmed KIT D816V fulfills the WHO criteria. In cases where the marrow biopsy shows only an increase in scattered mast cells without clusters, but molecular testing reveals KIT D816V and flow cytometry shows CD25 expression, three minor criteria may still be met.

In addition to establishing SM, laboratory data contribute to subclassification (indolent SM, smoldering SM, SM with an associated hematologic neoplasm, aggressive SM, mast cell leukemia). Criteria for these categories incorporate clinical signs of organ damage (so-called “C” findings), blood counts, percentage of atypical cells, and marrow blast count. Histologic features such as diffuse marrow replacement, high mast cell burden, and accompanying dysplasia should prompt consideration of more aggressive categories or associated hematologic neoplasm.

Tryptase measurement is part of the diagnostic algorithm; normal range for serum total tryptase in most laboratories is approximately 0–11 ng/mL, but the WHO suggests a threshold of >20 ng/mL as a minor criterion. Be aware that tryptase rises acutely with anaphylaxis and that chronic elevations can occur in clonal myeloid disease independent of mastocytosis; thus, results must be interpreted in clinical context.

# Reporting standards and interpretation in the context of incomplete sampling

Standardized reporting ensures that clinicians can integrate laboratory data into diagnostic and management pathways. Reports should clearly describe specimen source(s), adequacy (e.g., core length, cellularity), stains performed, and key morphologic findings: presence/absence of mast cell aggregates, percentage of atypical mast cells (if quantified), and fibrosis grade. Immunohistochemical and flow cytometric results must describe markers tested, pattern and distribution of staining, and whether expression is considered aberrant. Molecular reports must state genes/exons analyzed, method, analytic sensitivity, detected variants with nomenclature, and allele fraction.

Incomplete sampling is a common challenge in mast cell disease: dry taps are frequent and small or fragmented cores can make it hard to identify focal aggregates. In such contexts, reports should explicitly state limitations (e.g., “aspirate inadequate for cytology; trephine core 8 mm, fragmented”) and avoid overcalling absence of disease. A negative KIT result on peripheral blood or a low-sensitivity assay must be reported with the assay’s limit of detection and a recommendation to test marrow if clinical suspicion remains high. Similarly, absence of aberrant CD25 by flow cytometry in a hemodilute aspirate or in the setting of very low mast cell numbers is not definitive. When mast cell aggregates are focal, immunohistochemical stains (tryptase, CD117) may accentuate them: absence of visible aggregates on routine H&E does not exclude multifocal disease.

Reports should also integrate data against WHO criteria: explicitly state whether major and/or minor criteria are fulfilled and which findings support this conclusion. If the data are insufficient to confirm or exclude systemic mastocytosis, recommend specific next steps (e.g., repeat biopsy, bone marrow molecular testing by a more sensitive assay, referral to a mast cell center). Where additional high-risk myeloid mutations are detected, include commentary on prognostic implications, while noting that final risk stratification requires clinical correlation.

# Implications for treatment choices and specialist referral

Laboratory findings directly influence treatment selection and the need for specialist referral. Detection of KIT D816V not only fulfills a minor diagnostic criterion but also determines likely responsiveness to tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs). Imatinib has activity against some KIT mutants but is ineffective against D816V; therefore, confirmation of D816V usually precludes imatinib as a targeted therapy. Newer agents—midostaurin and avapritinib—have demonstrated efficacy in advanced SM with D816V and are approved in many jurisdictions; molecular status guides eligibility and expected benefit. Quantitative allele burden may be used to monitor response to these agents in advanced disease, but clinical endpoints remain paramount.

The presence of additional high-risk mutations (for example, SRSF2, ASXL1, RUNX1) confers a poorer prognosis and can prompt discussion of more aggressive approaches, including consideration of allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation in eligible patients. Laboratory identification of an associated hematologic neoplasm (e.g., chronic myelomonocytic leukemia or other myeloid neoplasm) changes the diagnostic category to SM with an associated hematologic neoplasm and alters treatment priority towards management of the myeloid disorder.

For patients whose laboratory evaluation is equivocal (e.g., inadequate samples, borderline tryptase, discordant molecular and immunophenotypic results), referral to a center experienced in mast cell disorders is recommended. Expert centers offer comprehensive testing (sensitive quantitative assays for KIT D816V such as ddPCR), integrated clinicopathologic interpretation, and access to clinical trials. They also provide multidisciplinary assessment for symptom management (antihistamines, leukotriene modifiers, mast cell stabilizers), management of anaphylaxis risk, and decisions around targeted therapy or transplantation.

Finally, laboratory results have immediate implications for peri-procedural care. Patients with confirmed or suspected systemic mastocytosis have increased risk of anaphylaxis; thus, documenting the diagnosis in the chart and communicating risks to anesthesia, surgical teams and emergency services is critical.

Conclusion

Accurate diagnosis and management of clonal mast cell disease require coordinated histopathology, immunophenotyping and molecular diagnostics. Adequate bone marrow sampling with both aspirate and trephine is essential, with IHC for tryptase and CD117 and assessment of aberrant CD25/CD2 expression. Molecular testing focused on KIT D816V with appropriately sensitive assays—and broader NGS when indicated—complements morphology and immunophenotype to establish clonality and guide therapy. Standardized reporting that states specimen adequacy, assay sensitivity, and how findings relate to WHO diagnostic criteria allows clinicians to make informed decisions about treatment and specialist referral.
