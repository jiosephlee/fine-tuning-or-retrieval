Introduction

Systemic diseases often present with multisystem signs and symptoms that require coordinated diagnostic imaging and organ-specific tests to determine extent of involvement, identify complications, and exclude alternative causes. In disorders associated with mast cell activation and proliferation, such as mast cell activation syndrome (MCAS) and systemic mastocytosis, the clinical picture can be protean: cutaneous flushing, anaphylaxis, gastrointestinal symptoms, hepatosplenomegaly, cytopenias, and bone pain. Imaging and targeted organ assessment guide diagnostic classification, risk stratification, and management decisions. They also help exclude mimics such as infectious, metabolic, infiltrative, neoplastic, and autoimmune conditions. This chapter reviews the practical role and interpretation of focused imaging and organ-specific investigations in the evaluation of suspected systemic disease with emphasis on findings relevant to mast cell disorders, while highlighting general principles that apply across systemic illnesses.

# Ultrasound assessment (hepatomegaly, splenomegaly) and its role in systemic disease evaluation

Ultrasound is a first-line, noninvasive imaging modality for bedside assessment of abdominal organs. Its portability, lack of ionizing radiation, cost-effectiveness, and ability to provide dynamic real-time information make it well suited for initial evaluation of suspected hepatomegaly or splenomegaly. In systemic diseases associated with mast cell proliferation, enlarged liver or spleen may reflect organ infiltration, congestion, portal hypertension, or reactive hyperplasia. Sonographic detection of organomegaly can trigger further cross-sectional imaging or directed biopsy and informs staging criteria in disorders such as systemic mastocytosis.

Hepatomegaly on ultrasound is assessed by organ span and contour. Normal adult liver span in the midclavicular line ranges approximately from 13 to 15.5 cm, though values vary by body habitus and technique; values above this range raise suspicion for hepatomegaly. Ultrasound also assesses hepatic echotexture: increased echogenicity suggests steatosis, heterogeneous echotexture may indicate fibrosis, infiltration, or focal lesions, and nodularity raises concern for cirrhosis. Doppler evaluation can assess portal and hepatic venous flow to detect portal hypertension or vascular complications. In mast cell disorders, focal hepatic lesions are less common than diffuse infiltration, but an enlarged, heterogeneous liver with imaging or clinical signs of portal hypertension requires evaluation for portal flow abnormalities, chronic liver disease, or infiltrative processes.

Splenomegaly is measured by maximal craniocaudal length. A normal adult spleen is typically ≤12–13 cm in length; splenomegaly is generally defined as length >13 cm, though mild enlargement may be reported at somewhat lower thresholds depending on population. Ultrasound characterizes spleen size, focal lesions, and the presence of infarcts or capsular irregularity. Diffuse splenomegaly suggests systemic processes such as hemolysis, portal hypertension, infection, hematologic malignancy, or infiltration. In mast cell disease, splenomegaly can reflect infiltration by mast cells or reactive hyperplasia; significant splenomegaly with cytopenias suggests hypersplenism and may influence management.

Ultrasound also identifies ancillary findings relevant to systemic disease: ascites, biliary dilatation, renal size and echotexture, lymphadenopathy in accessible regions, and vascular thrombosis. In the work-up of a patient with suspected systemic mast cell disorder, ultrasound may be the initial investigation to confirm organomegaly and guide the need for more detailed cross-sectional imaging or biopsy. However, ultrasound is operator dependent and limited in obese patients or for deep structures; it cannot provide the same soft tissue characterization, vascular mapping, or bone marrow assessment as CT, MRI, or specialized nuclear imaging.

# Cross-sectional imaging (CT, MRI) indications for organomegaly or complications

Cross-sectional imaging with computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) offers higher-resolution anatomical detail and broader field of view compared with ultrasound, enabling comprehensive assessment of organ enlargement, focal lesions, vascular complications, and nodal or bone involvement. Indications for CT or MRI in suspected systemic disease include confirmation and quantification of organomegaly detected on ultrasound, characterization of suspicious focal lesions, evaluation of complications (for example, portal vein thrombosis, biliary obstruction, or focal hepatic infarction), staging of malignancy or infiltrative processes, and guidance for percutaneous biopsy.

CT is widely available and rapid, providing excellent spatial resolution for the liver, spleen, lymph nodes, lungs, and bones. Contrast-enhanced CT is valuable for detecting focal lesions, vascular patency, and abnormal enhancement patterns. In systemic mast cell disease, CT may reveal hepatosplenomegaly, lymphadenopathy, and osseous lesions. Typical osseous findings include osteosclerotic or osteolytic lesions and diffuse bone marrow changes; CT is sensitive for cortical bone changes and pathological fractures. CT pulmonary angiography is indicated if pulmonary embolism is suspected, which can be a complication of systemic inflammatory and prothrombotic states.

MRI offers superior soft tissue contrast and multiplanar capabilities without ionizing radiation. It is particularly useful for liver characterization (using sequences such as T1, T2, diffusion-weighted imaging, and hepatobiliary contrast agents), bone marrow evaluation, and cardiac imaging when CT is inadequate or contraindicated. MRI of the liver can distinguish fatty infiltration from fibrosis, identify focal nodules, and assess biliary anatomy. Whole-body MRI can detect bone marrow infiltration and is increasingly used in hematologic and infiltrative conditions to map disease extent. In mast cell disease, MRI may detect marrow-replacing lesions, focal bone lesions, and degrees of marrow involvement that are occult on CT.

Selecting CT versus MRI depends on the clinical question, patient factors (renal function, allergies, hemodynamic stability), and need for temporal speed. For suspected acute complications—such as suspected intra-abdominal hemorrhage, bowel ischemia, or pulmonary complications—contrast-enhanced CT is often preferred for its speed and availability. For characterization of indeterminate lesions, assessment of marrow, or when ionizing radiation is a concern (young patients, need for serial imaging), MRI is preferred.

Interpretation of organomegaly on cross-sectional imaging should be integrated with laboratory data and clinical context. For the liver and spleen, objective volumetric measurements or comparison to normative size charts improve reproducibility. Detection of focal hepatic lesions should prompt consideration of differential diagnoses including benign cysts, hemangiomas, adenomas, metastatic disease, granulomatous disease, and focal mast cell infiltrates (rare); contrast enhancement patterns and MRI sequences help differentiate these entities. Vascular assessment is critical: portal vein thrombosis or hepatic vein outflow obstruction changes management and prognosis. Similarly, identification of splenic infarcts, subcapsular hematoma, or splenic rupture in the setting of systemic disease has immediate clinical implications.

# Cardiac investigations in anaphylaxis (ECG changes, troponin considerations, myocardial dysfunction)

Anaphylaxis can produce acute cardiovascular complications ranging from vasodilation and distributive shock to arrhythmias, myocardial ischemia, and nonischemic myocardial dysfunction. Cardiac evaluation is essential in severe or atypical presentations and in patients with persistent chest pain, hemodynamic instability, or abnormal biomarkers. The principal investigations include 12-lead electrocardiography (ECG), measurement of cardiac biomarkers such as troponin, and echocardiography when myocardial dysfunction is suspected.

ECG during or after anaphylaxis may demonstrate changes attributable to myocardial ischemia, arrhythmia, or stress-related cardiomyopathy. Ischemic ECG changes can include ST-segment depression or elevation, T-wave inversion, or new Q waves. Arrhythmias observed include supraventricular tachycardia, atrial fibrillation, and ventricular arrhythmias. In anaphylaxis, myocardial ischemia can result from hypotension with supply-demand mismatch, coronary vasospasm mediated by vasoactive mediators (histamine, leukotrienes, prostaglandins), or plaque rupture precipitated by adrenergic surges. ECG interpretation must therefore be correlated with symptoms, hemodynamics, and biomarker trends.

Cardiac troponins are sensitive markers of myocardial injury. Contemporary assays report values with high precision; typical reference ranges for high-sensitivity troponin I or T have assay-specific 99th percentile cutoffs (for example, high-sensitivity troponin T 99th percentile approximately 14 ng/L in many assays), and values above this threshold suggest myocardial injury. In anaphylaxis, troponin may be transiently elevated due to demand ischemia, direct mediator-induced myocardial injury, or type 1 myocardial infarction. Serial troponin measurements at presentation and 3–6 hours later help distinguish acute myocardial injury. Interpretation requires integration with ECG and echocardiography. Modest troponin rises without ischemic ECG changes and with rapid resolution of symptoms may reflect reversible injury; larger rises with persistent ECG changes or wall motion abnormalities indicate myocardial infarction or myocarditis-like injury and warrant cardiology input.

Echocardiography is a key modality to assess myocardial function, wall motion, pericardial effusion, and dynamic obstruction. Point-of-care or transthoracic echocardiography can be performed at the bedside in unstable patients to evaluate for global hypokinesis, regional wall motion abnormalities, right ventricular strain (suggesting pulmonary embolism), or decreased ejection fraction consistent with stress cardiomyopathy. Cases of Kounis syndrome describe allergic acute coronary syndromes in which mast cell mediator release triggers coronary vasospasm, plaque erosion, or thrombosis; echocardiography in Kounis syndrome may show regional wall motion abnormalities corresponding to ischemia. In severe anaphylaxis, fluid resuscitation and vasopressors may confound cardiac assessment; thus, repeated imaging and biomarker measurements are often necessary.

Clinical decision points include obtaining an ECG on presentation in all patients with moderate to severe anaphylaxis, measuring troponin if chest pain, ECG changes, hypotension, or prolonged symptoms are present, and performing echocardiography when there is hemodynamic instability or suspicion of myocardial dysfunction. Positive findings should prompt cardiology consultation, consideration of coronary angiography if type 1 myocardial infarction is suspected, and close hemodynamic monitoring. Recognition that troponin elevation in anaphylaxis can be transient and noncoronary is important, but one must not dismiss significant troponin rises without evaluation for acute coronary pathology.

# Pulmonary imaging for airway/aspiration complications

Pulmonary complications in systemic diseases and in the context of anaphylaxis include bronchospasm, laryngeal edema, aspiration pneumonitis, pulmonary edema, and less commonly pulmonary embolism. Imaging helps assess airway patency, detect aspiration, evaluate for pulmonary infiltrates, and identify complications that change management. The choice of modality—chest radiograph, computed tomography, or airway-focused imaging—depends on clinical presentation and timing.

For acute airway compromise in anaphylaxis, clinical assessment and direct visualization (laryngoscopy) supersede radiographic imaging because management is time-sensitive. Nevertheless, chest radiography (posterior-anterior and lateral views) is commonly used after initial stabilization to evaluate for pulmonary edema, aspirated material, or other acute changes. Findings of aspiration on radiography are often subtle early and more readily apparent after several hours; typical patterns include patchy airspace consolidation in dependent areas—posterior upper lobes when supine or superior segments of lower lobes when upright.

Computed tomography of the chest provides greater sensitivity and specificity for parenchymal abnormalities, small airways disease, and detection of aspiration-related complications such as lobar consolidation, necrotizing pneumonia, or abscess formation. CT is indicated for persistent or worsening respiratory symptoms despite initial therapy, suspicion for complications such as pulmonary embolism (CT pulmonary angiography), or to evaluate for alternative diagnoses like interstitial lung disease or malignancy. In the context of mast cell diseases, CT may demonstrate airway wall thickening consistent with bronchial hyperreactivity, or multifocal consolidations suggesting aspiration or infectious processes.

Bronchoscopy is useful when there is concern for significant aspiration with retained material, unexplained hypoxemia, suspected airway injury, or when sampling for microbiology is required. In the acute setting, bronchoscopic removal of foreign or aspirated material can be therapeutic. For evaluation of suspected laryngeal edema, flexible nasendoscopy provides direct assessment and guides airway management.

Pulmonary imaging must be interpreted in the context of the timing of symptoms. Aspiration pneumonitis may not be radiographically evident immediately after the event; chest radiographs often become abnormal within 6–12 hours. In contrast, noncardiogenic pulmonary edema from increased vascular permeability can produce bilateral interstitial and alveolar infiltrates relatively rapidly. Decision points include obtaining a chest radiograph in any patient with respiratory symptoms after anaphylaxis or suspected aspiration, escalating to chest CT or bronchoscopy when there is clinical deterioration, persistent hypoxemia, or suspicion of complications. Recognition that imaging findings may lag behind clinical events is crucial to avoid false reassurance from an initially normal radiograph.

# Use of bone marrow biopsy and interpretation alongside imaging findings

Bone marrow biopsy is a cornerstone diagnostic test when systemic hematologic, infiltrative, or proliferative disorders are suspected. In mast cell disorders, bone marrow examination is mandatory for diagnosing systemic mastocytosis, as it allows morphological assessment, immunophenotyping, and molecular testing. Indications for bone marrow biopsy include unexplained cytopenias or cytoses, suspected hematologic malignancy, persistent organomegaly with hematologic abnormalities, and when imaging suggests marrow involvement.

A typical bone marrow procedure includes aspiration and trephine biopsy. Aspirate provides cellular detail and allows flow cytometry, cytogenetics, and molecular studies. Trephine biopsy preserves marrow architecture and is essential to detect focal aggregates of abnormal cells that can be missed on aspirate. In systemic mastocytosis, diagnostic criteria incorporate bone marrow findings: multifocal dense infiltrates of mast cells in bone marrow or extracutaneous organs, and confirmation by mast cell morphology and immunophenotype, together with relevant molecular markers such as KIT D816V mutation and elevated serum tryptase. Flow cytometry may demonstrate aberrant mast cell expression of CD25 and/or CD2 in addition to CD117. Standard laboratory references state a baseline serum tryptase >20 ng/mL often supports systemic disease but is not definitive alone.

Bone marrow interpretation must be integrated with imaging. Cross-sectional and nuclear imaging may raise suspicion for marrow involvement when bone lesions are present or when whole-body MRI shows marrow signal abnormalities. Imaging that reveals osteosclerotic or osteolytic lesions should prompt concordant targeted marrow evaluation and sometimes image-guided biopsy of focal lesions. Conversely, normal imaging does not exclude marrow disease: early or patchy marrow involvement may be occult on imaging but detected histologically. Histopathology should report mast cell burden, architecture, presence of fibrosis, and additional hematologic neoplastic components.

Molecular and cytogenetic studies performed on marrow aspirate refine diagnosis and prognosis. Detection of the KIT D816V mutation is common in systemic mastocytosis and informs targeted therapy options. Additional mutations (for example, in SRSF2, ASXL1, or RUNX1) are associated with advanced disease and worse prognosis. Bone marrow cellularity and peripheral cytopenias guide assessment for marrow failure or hypersplenism. When marrow biopsy reveals concurrent clonal myeloid neoplasms, this defines an advanced variant of systemic mastocytosis with distinct management and prognostic implications.

Clinical decision points include obtaining bone marrow biopsy when peripheral blood counts, organomegaly, and elevated baseline tryptase suggest systemic disease; performing both aspirate and trephine biopsy; ordering flow cytometry for immunophenotyping, molecular testing for KIT and other myeloid mutations, and cytogenetics where indicated; and integrating marrow results with imaging and laboratory data to establish disease classification and plan management. Communication between clinicians, radiologists, and pathologists is critical to ensure focal imaging findings are correlated with biopsy sites and histopathologic interpretation.

# Limitations and timelines for imaging in the acute vs elective setting

Appropriate timing of imaging is essential and must balance urgency, diagnostic yield, patient stability, and safety. In the acute setting—such as severe anaphylaxis, hemodynamic instability, acute respiratory failure, or suspected acute intra-abdominal catastrophe—rapid bedside and emergent imaging modalities are prioritized. Point-of-care ultrasound and portable chest radiography provide immediate, actionable information. Contrast-enhanced CT is often the modality of choice for acute abdominal pain, suspected hemorrhage, vascular complications, or pulmonary embolism if the patient is stable enough for transfer and contrast administration. MRI is usually less practical emergently due to longer acquisition times, limited availability, and contraindications such as unstable patients or those with implanted devices.

Imaging limitations in the acute phase include reduced sensitivity for certain pathologies immediately post-insult. For example, ultrasound and chest radiography may initially be normal after aspiration or early infection; CT may detect early changes but can still miss microscopic disease. Similarly, bone marrow involvement or hepatic fibrosis may not be appreciable on imaging performed too early in the disease course. Contrast administration in acutely ill patients requires caution: renal function should be considered to avoid contrast-induced nephropathy (creatinine and estimated glomerular filtration rate; eGFR <30 mL/min/1.73 m^2 increases risk), and in hypotensive patients, delayed renal clearance alters contrast safety profiles.

In elective settings, imaging can be optimized for diagnostic yield. Elective ultrasound can be performed by experienced sonographers with repeat views and Doppler assessment; CT and MRI protocols can be tailored with appropriate intravenous and oral contrast, multiphase liver protocols, and targeted sequences for marrow or cardiac evaluation. Whole-body imaging strategies—such as whole-body MRI or PET-CT—may be appropriate when mapping systemic disease burden is necessary for staging or treatment planning. However, elective imaging should consider cumulative radiation exposure, particularly for young patients or those requiring serial assessments; MRI or ultrasound may be preferred to reduce radiation.

Timelines for follow-up imaging should be individualized. In acute complications, repeat imaging is indicated when clinical status changes or to document resolution; for example, chest radiograph or CT may be repeated 24–72 hours after initial aspiration if symptoms persist or worsen. For organomegaly detected on ultrasound, more comprehensive cross-sectional imaging can be scheduled electively to further characterize lesions and guide biopsy. When monitoring known disease, standardized interval imaging—guided by disease activity, treatment response, and risk of progression—is advised. For instance, in indolent systemic mastocytosis without progressive symptoms, imaging may be performed only if new organ dysfunction arises; in advanced disease, more frequent assessments may be warranted.

Understanding the technical and clinical limitations of each modality helps set expectations and avoid misinterpretation. Operator dependency affects ultrasound; motion artifact and partial volume effects can degrade CT or MRI quality; metallic implants and patient factors (claustrophobia, inability to lie flat) limit MRI. Communication between the referring clinician and imaging specialists is essential to select the right test at the right time, tailor protocols to the clinical question, and plan any required follow-up or biopsy.

Conclusion

Focused imaging and organ-specific tests are integral to the evaluation of suspected systemic disease. Ultrasound provides a rapid initial assessment of hepatomegaly and splenomegaly but must be supplemented by CT or MRI for comprehensive characterization, vascular assessment, and detection of complications. Cardiac investigations in anaphylaxis require timely ECGs, consideration of troponin kinetics, and echocardiography to identify myocardial dysfunction or ischemia. Pulmonary imaging detects aspiration and airway-related complications and guides escalation to CT or bronchoscopy when necessary. Bone marrow biopsy remains diagnostic in hematologic and infiltrative disorders and must be interpreted alongside imaging and molecular studies. Finally, clinicians must balance the urgency and limitations of acute imaging against the enhanced diagnostic yield of elective, protocol-driven studies. Integrating clinical findings, laboratory data, and appropriate imaging in a timely fashion ensures accurate diagnosis, appropriate staging, and effective management planning for patients with systemic disease.
