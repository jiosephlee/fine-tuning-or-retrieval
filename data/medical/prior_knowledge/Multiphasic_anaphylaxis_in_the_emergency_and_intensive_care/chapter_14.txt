Introduction

Acute presentations with hypotension, flushing, and urticaria raise immediate concern for anaphylaxis, a potentially life‑threatening, IgE‑mediated or non‑IgE mast cell–driven reaction that requires rapid recognition and treatment. However, a variety of non‑allergic and alternative allergic mimics can produce overlapping features, and accurate differentiation in the acute setting influences immediate management (e.g., need for epinephrine, antibiotics, source control, volume resuscitation, or tumor‑directed therapy) and subsequent diagnostic testing. This chapter reviews the major conditions that can mimic anaphylaxis—septic and other distributive shock states, vasovagal syncope and orthostatic hypotension, vasoactive neuroendocrine tumor syndromes (including carcinoid syndrome and pheochromocytoma/paraganglioma), mast cell activation disorders versus chronic urticaria and dermatologic mimics, and drug‑induced non‑IgE mediated reactions. For each entity we emphasize pathophysiology, key historical and examination clues, appropriate immediate investigations (with normal reference ranges where relevant), and clinical decision points that help distinguish these disorders from anaphylaxis in the acute setting.

# Septic and distributive shock: distinguishing features (infectious source, inflammatory markers, fever)

Pathophysiology and clinical overlap
Distributive shock is characterized by a primary decrease in systemic vascular resistance due to vasodilation and endothelial dysfunction, often accompanied by relative intravascular hypovolemia and maldistribution of blood flow. The prototypical example is septic shock, a result of a dysregulated host response to infection with widespread inflammation, vasoplegia, increased vascular permeability, and myocardial depression in some patients. Because distributive shock produces vasodilation and occasionally cutaneous flushing, hypotension and skin findings can resemble anaphylaxis. Both conditions may show tachycardia and signs of end‑organ hypoperfusion. However, the underlying triggers and adjunctive features differ and guide management: sepsis requires source control and antimicrobials, while anaphylaxis requires immediate epinephrine.

Clinical features and history
Key historical clues favoring septic/distributive shock include evidence of infection or a high risk for infection (recent invasive procedures, indwelling catheters, immunosuppression, documented focal infectious symptoms such as cough, dysuria, abdominal pain, or skin/soft tissue infection). Fever or hypothermia (<36.0°C) is common in sepsis; temperature abnormalities are part of sepsis criteria. The onset may be more insidious than the immediate seconds‑to‑minutes onset typical of IgE‑mediated anaphylaxis after allergen exposure, but rapid fulminant sepsis is possible.

Examination commonly reveals warm, well‑perfused extremities early in distributive shock with bounding pulses and wide pulse pressure due to vasodilation. In contrast, anaphylaxis often produces urticaria, angioedema, and bronchospasm. Signs such as productive cough with purulent sputum, focal consolidation on lung exam, localized erythema and fluctuance, or peritoneal signs point toward an infectious source. Mucosal edema and stridor are more characteristic of anaphylaxis.

Laboratory and bedside investigations
Initial labs useful to distinguish sepsis include complete blood count, metabolic panel, lactate, blood cultures, urinalysis and culture, and site‑directed cultures or imaging. Serum lactate is a practical marker of tissue hypoperfusion and severity; elevated lactate (>2 mmol/L) suggests hypoperfusion and is incorporated into sepsis guidelines. Normal lactate is roughly 0.5–2.2 mmol/L, though laboratory ranges vary. Inflammatory markers such as C‑reactive protein (CRP) and procalcitonin can support the diagnosis of bacterial infection—procalcitonin elevations (commonly >0.5 ng/mL suggest bacterial sepsis) have greater specificity than CRP but are not definitive on their own. White blood cell count may be high, normal, or low; neutrophilia with left shift is common. Blood cultures should be obtained before antibiotics when possible.

Hemodynamics and monitoring
Septic shock may require aggressive fluid resuscitation, vasopressors (norepinephrine is first‑line), and identification/treatment of the source. Invasive hemodynamic monitoring is sometimes required in complex cases. The presence of warm, flushed skin with hypotension and a clear infectious nidus should direct early empiric antimicrobial therapy. Empiric doses and timing follow institutional sepsis protocols (commonly, broad‑spectrum antibiotics within 1 hour for septic shock). In contrast, while epinephrine is the immediate life‑saving therapy in anaphylaxis, its routine use in sepsis—unless there is refractory hypotension—is not supported; norepinephrine is preferred for septic vasoplegia.

Decision points for the acute clinician
- If there is a known allergen exposure within minutes and rapid development of urticaria, angioedema, bronchospasm, or hypotension, treat as anaphylaxis with intramuscular epinephrine immediately and reassess.  
- If fever, leukocytosis or leukopenia, elevated lactate, suspicion for infection, or an indwelling device/procedure is present and the time course is more gradual, prioritize sepsis evaluation and treatment (blood cultures, source control, antibiotics, fluids, vasopressors).  
- Mixed presentations can occur (e.g., septic patients with mast cell activation), so reassessment after initial resuscitation and targeted investigations are essential.

# Vasovagal syncope and orthostatic hypotension

Pathophysiology and clinical presentation
Vasovagal syncope (neurocardiogenic syncope) is mediated by an autonomic reflex resulting in sudden vagally mediated bradycardia and/or sympathetic withdrawal causing peripheral vasodilation, leading to transient cerebral hypoperfusion and syncope. Typical triggers include emotional distress, pain, prolonged standing, heat exposure, or phlebotomy. Orthostatic hypotension arises from failure to maintain blood pressure upon standing due to impaired autonomic reflexes, hypovolemia, medications, or volume shifts. Both conditions can produce transient hypotension and flushing due to peripheral vasodilation, and premonitory symptoms such as lightheadedness.

Distinguishing features from anaphylaxis
The key differences are timing, associated symptoms, and presence or absence of allergic signs. Vasovagal syncope is commonly preceded by prodromal symptoms—nausea, diaphoresis, pallor, visual changes, a sense of warmth, and lightheadedness—followed by brief loss of consciousness with rapid recovery. In vasovagal events, skin is often pale and diaphoretic rather than pruritic or urticarial. Orthostatic hypotension causes presyncope or syncope on standing; the hypotension is position‑dependent and improves on recumbency. Anaphylaxis characteristically includes mucocutaneous signs such as urticaria, pruritus, angioedema, wheezing, or stridor and usually follows allergen exposure with rapid onset.

History and targeted physical exam
A careful history of triggers (standing, emotional stress, pain, micturition, medication changes such as antihypertensives or diuretics) is central. Ask about recent vasodilatory agents (nitrates, alpha‑blockers), antihypertensive therapy, antidepressants, or anticholinergics. Physical exam should assess orthostatic vitals: measure blood pressure and heart rate supine, immediately on standing, and at 3 minutes. Orthostatic hypotension is defined as a fall in systolic blood pressure of at least 20 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure of at least 10 mmHg within 3 minutes of standing. Conversely, reflex syncope may be accompanied by transient bradycardia.

Investigations and acute management considerations
For patients with suspected vasovagal syncope and a clear prodrome, immediate management is supportive: place supine, elevate legs, and allow recovery. For orthostatic hypotension, lay the patient flat and address precipitating causes (fluid resuscitation if volume depleted, stop offending medications). Continuous cardiac monitoring is prudent if arrhythmia is suspected. Distinguishing from anaphylaxis is important because epinephrine may be unnecessary in vasovagal events and could produce tachyarrhythmias; however, if history is unclear and anaphylaxis remains possible, treatment with intramuscular epinephrine is appropriate given its life‑saving role when needed.

Clinical pearls
- Skin pallor and diaphoresis argue for vasovagal syncope; urticaria, pruritus, and respiratory compromise argue for anaphylaxis.  
- Orthostatic vitals are a quick bedside test with defined thresholds (ΔSBP ≥ 20 mmHg or ΔDBP ≥ 10 mmHg within 3 minutes).  
- Consider ECG and troponin if cardiac syncope or myocardial ischemia is a concern.

# Carcinoid syndrome and vasoactive tumor syndromes (clinical clues, biochemical testing)

Pathophysiology and clinical manifestations
Carcinoid syndrome results from systemic release of serotonin and other vasoactive mediators (kinins, tachykinins, prostaglandins) by metastatic neuroendocrine tumors (NETs), most commonly small intestinal carcinoids that have bypassed hepatic metabolism via liver metastases. These mediators produce episodic flushing, diarrhea, bronchospasm, and, over time, right‑sided valvular heart disease. Because symptoms can include profound flushing, hypotension, and wheeze, carcinoid syndrome can mimic anaphylaxis; however, the typical course, triggers, and associated features differ.

Clinical clues distinguishing carcinoid syndrome
Flushing in carcinoid syndrome often has characteristic features: it may be prolonged (minutes to hours), episodic, and associated with diarrhea and abdominal cramping. Flushing is frequently triggered by alcohol, emotional stress, certain foods (cheeses, bananas, chocolate), or medications (e.g., histamine‑releasing drugs). The flushing may be erythematous rather than urticarial and without pruritus; mucosal edema and urticaria are not typical. Bronchospasm may occur but is usually not associated with angioedema.

Biochemical testing and typical values
The most useful initial test when carcinoid syndrome is suspected is measurement of 24‑hour urinary 5‑hydroxyindoleacetic acid (5‑HIAA), the primary metabolite of serotonin. Normal urinary 5‑HIAA is approximately 2–8 mg/24 hours (laboratory ranges vary). Elevated 5‑HIAA supports the diagnosis but can be falsely elevated by serotonin‑rich foods (bananas, walnuts, pineapple), medications (acetaminophen, guaifenesin), and drugs that alter serotonin metabolism; patients should avoid these foods/drugs for several days before testing when feasible. Plasma chromogranin A is another marker of neuroendocrine tumors but is less specific (elevated in proton pump inhibitor use, renal insufficiency). For localized tumor detection, cross‑sectional imaging with CT or MRI and functional imaging (68Ga‑DOTATATE PET/CT or somatostatin receptor scintigraphy) are used.

Acute and chronic management considerations
In the acute setting, symptomatic management includes supportive fluid resuscitation and, for bronchospasm, bronchodilators. Octreotide, a somatostatin analogue, is effective acutely to reduce mediator release and control flushing and diarrhea; subcutaneous or intravenous formulations are used emergently. Awareness is critical during procedures (e.g., tumor manipulation, anesthesia) because tumors can release large mediator surges causing carcinoid crisis—prophylactic perioperative octreotide is often administered.

Decision points and pitfalls
- Consider carcinoid syndrome when flushing is episodic, prolonged, associated with diarrhea or right‑sided heart disease, and not accompanied by urticaria or angioedema.  
- In the acute unstable patient, treat life‑threatening features (airway, breathing, circulation) and consider octreotide if carcinoid crisis is suspected.  
- For diagnostic confirmation, obtain 24‑hour urinary 5‑HIAA (with dietary restrictions) and perform imaging once the patient is stabilized.

# Pheochromocytoma/paraganglioma presentations with flushing and hemodynamic instability

Pathophysiology and clinical spectrum
Pheochromocytomas (adrenal medullary catecholamine–secreting tumors) and paragangliomas (extra‑adrenal chromaffin tumors) produce episodic or sustained release of catecholamines (norepinephrine, epinephrine, dopamine) leading to hypertension, tachycardia, pallor or flushing, diaphoresis, and headache. Unlike carcinoid tumors, these tumors classically cause paroxysmal hypertension, but they can also present with hypotension or cardiovascular collapse due to catecholamine‑induced cardiomyopathy, arrhythmias, or sudden vasodilation after catecholamine surge and receptor desensitization. Because flushing and hemodynamic instability can mimic anaphylaxis, it is crucial to recognize the clinical pattern and biochemical testing.

Clinical clues and distinguishing features
The classic triad—episodic headache, sweating, and palpitations—combined with labile blood pressure suggests catecholamine excess. Flushing in pheochromocytoma is often accompanied by severe hypertension rather than the vasodilatory hypotension of anaphylaxis; however, some patients manifest episodic hypotension or shock, especially if catecholaminergic myocardial dysfunction or arrhythmias develop. Triggers include surgery, anesthesia, certain drugs (e.g., beta‑blockers given without prior alpha blockade, metoclopramide, opioids), or physical stress.

Biochemical testing and reference ranges
Diagnostic testing measures catecholamine metabolites: plasma free metanephrines (normetanephrine and metanephrine) or 24‑hour urinary fractionated metanephrines and catecholamines. Plasma free metanephrines have high sensitivity. Typical reference ranges depend on the assay, but elevated plasma normetanephrine or metanephrine beyond the laboratory upper limit suggests catecholamine excess. For 24‑hour urine, normal values are generally: total metanephrines < 1.2 mg/24 hr and normetanephrine < 0.9 mg/24 hr (laboratory variability applies). Interpretation must consider medications (tricyclic antidepressants, acetaminophen, labetalol) and physiologic stress, which can raise levels. Imaging with CT/MRI of the abdomen/pelvis and functional imaging (123I‑MIBG or 68Ga‑DOTATATE PET/CT) is used for localization after biochemical confirmation.

Acute management and perioperative considerations
In an acute catecholamine crisis, management centers on cardiovascular stabilization: control blood pressure and heart rate carefully, avoid precipitants, and manage arrhythmias and acute heart failure. Alpha‑adrenergic blockade (e.g., phenoxybenzamine or short‑acting agents like phentolamine) is necessary before beta‑blockade to prevent unopposed alpha stimulation. In emergencies with severe hypertension, short‑acting vasodilators and alpha blockers are used; if hypotension occurs due to catecholamine depletion or cardiogenic shock, vasopressors and inotropes may be required under careful monitoring. In suspected pheochromocytoma, avoid medications that provoke catecholamine release or interfere with catecholamine metabolism without specialist input.

Decision points and red flags
- Consider pheochromocytoma/paraganglioma in patients with paroxysmal hypertension, headache, palpitations, diaphoresis, or labile blood pressure; when such a patient presents with flushing and hemodynamic instability, include catecholamine excess in the differential.  
- Obtain biochemical testing (plasma free metanephrines or 24‑hour urinary metanephrines) after initial stabilization; interpret results in context of medications and stress.  
- Avoid empiric beta‑blockade in a hypertensive patient without prior alpha blockade if pheochromocytoma is possible.

# Acute mast cell activation vs chronic urticaria and other dermatologic conditions

Pathophysiology of mast cell activation
Mast cells are tissue resident immune cells that release preformed mediators (histamine, tryptase, heparin), newly synthesized mediators (leukotrienes, prostaglandins), and cytokines when activated. Acute mast cell activation underlies anaphylaxis and other mast cell activation syndromes. Acute systemic mast cell activation presents with urticaria (wheals), angioedema, flushing, pruritus, bronchospasm, gastrointestinal cramping, and hypotension. Chronic urticaria, including chronic spontaneous urticaria (CSU), involves recurrent pruritic wheals lasting for months to years and is typically not accompanied by hypotension or systemic anaphylactic features.

Clinical differentiation
Urticaria in acute systemic mast cell activation is often sudden in onset, pruritic, transient (individual lesions last <24 hours), and may be associated with systemic symptoms such as hypotension, respiratory compromise, or gastrointestinal symptoms. Angioedema involves deeper dermal/subcutaneous tissues and affects eyelids, lips, or tongue. In contrast, chronic urticaria produces recurrent wheals persisting for >6 weeks and is usually not life‑threatening; systemic signs such as hypotension are absent. Dermographism (urticaria induced by stroking the skin) may be present in chronic urticaria or physical urticarias.

Laboratory testing and interpretation
Serum tryptase is a helpful test in acute mast cell activation. Baseline tryptase is typically <11.4 ng/mL in adults (lab ranges vary). In anaphylaxis, an acute rise in tryptase compared with baseline supports mast cell degranulation. The practical diagnostic criterion often used is an increase in tryptase that exceeds 1.2 × baseline + 2 ng/mL (i.e., acute tryptase > 1.2 × baseline tryptase + 2 ng/mL). Because baseline tryptase is usually not known, an elevated level drawn during the reaction and a convalescent sample drawn at least 24–48 hours later allow comparison. Note that tryptase peaks approximately 1–2 hours after symptom onset and returns to baseline by 12–24 hours in many cases; thus, timeliness of sampling matters. Complement levels (C4) may help differentiate hereditary or acquired angioedema due to C1 inhibitor deficiency versus mast cell‑mediated angioedema—C4 is low in C1 inhibitor deficiency. Histamine levels rise quickly and fall fast; they are not practical in most clinical settings.

Dermatologic mimics
Several non‑mast cell dermatologic conditions can simulate urticaria or flushing. Erythroderma, viral exanthems, fixed drug eruptions, and vasculitic rashes may have different lesion morphology: vasculitis often produces palpable purpura and lesions that persist >24 hours with central petechiae; fixed drug eruptions recur at same location and often leave post‑inflammatory hyperpigmentation. Flushing without pruritic wheals suggests vascular causes or neuroendocrine syndromes rather than mast cell activation. Careful inspection of lesion characteristics (duration of individual lesions, blanching wheals vs petechial/purpuric lesions, presence of mucosal lesions) helps distinguish these entities.

Chronic urticaria management and when to suspect systemic disease
Chronic spontaneous urticaria typically fluctuates over weeks to months; it can have significant quality‑of‑life impact but rarely causes hypotension. Investigations in chronic urticaria focus on identifying triggers (autoimmune etiologies, thyroid disease, infections) but routine extensive allergy testing is often low yield. When systemic symptoms such as hypotension, bronchospasm, or gastrointestinal compromise are present, consider systemic mast cell activation or anaphylaxis and pursue emergent treatment and diagnostic tests (e.g., serial tryptase).

Decision points for acute care
- In a patient with urticaria plus hypotension, bronchospasm, or angioedema affecting the airway, treat for anaphylaxis immediately (intramuscular epinephrine) and obtain serum tryptase as soon as feasible.  
- If lesions are chronic, pruritic without systemic features, and history is compatible with chronic spontaneous urticaria, acute anaphylaxis is less likely.  
- For angioedema without urticaria, evaluate for bradykinin‑mediated causes (C1 inhibitor deficiency or ACE‑inhibitor induced) since these will not respond to antihistamines or epinephrine.

# Drug‑induced reactions unrelated to IgE (anaphylactoid/non‑IgE mediated) and their recognition

Mechanisms and terminology
Non‑IgE mediated reactions, historically termed “anaphylactoid,” result from direct mast cell or basophil activation, complement activation, or other mediator release without prior sensitization and IgE crosslinking. They can be clinically indistinguishable from IgE‑mediated anaphylaxis, featuring hypotension, urticaria, flushing, bronchospasm, and angioedema. Common culprits include radiographic contrast media, certain antibiotics (e.g., vancomycin causing “red man syndrome”), opioids, neuromuscular blocking agents, and plasma expanders (e.g., dextran).

Clinical recognition and distinguishing features
Timing is often immediate or within minutes of drug exposure. A key clue favoring non‑IgE mediated reaction is occurrence on the first exposure to a drug or with continuous infusion where symptoms are rate‑dependent (e.g., vancomycin infusion causing flushing and hypotension that resolves by slowing infusion). There may be absence of prior allergy history. Some drug reactions are dose or infusion rate dependent: for example, rapid vancomycin infusion leads to histamine release causing erythema and pruritus of the upper body and hypotension (“red man syndrome”), which improves with slowing the infusion and antihistamines rather than IgE testing.

Investigations and acute management
Management in the acute phase is determined by severity and is similar to anaphylaxis (airway management, intramuscular epinephrine for severe systemic reactions). Antihistamines (H1 and H2 blockers) and corticosteroids are commonly used adjuncts. For suspected contrast‑induced reactions, premedication protocols (steroids and antihistamines) reduce but do not eliminate risk. Drug skin testing and specific IgE testing may be available for some agents (e.g., beta‑lactam antibiotics) to distinguish IgE‑mediated allergy from non‑IgE reactions, though skin testing has limitations and must be interpreted with specialist input.

Patterns and examples
- Radiocontrast media can cause immediate flushing, hypotension, bronchospasm via complement activation and direct mast cell activation; premedication with corticosteroids and antihistamines is used in high‑risk patients.  
- Opioids (morphine, meperidine) and vancomycin cause direct mast cell histamine release; vancomycin infusion reactions are infusion‑rate dependent and often respond to slowing the infusion and antihistamines.  
- Physical factors such as temperature, exercise, or pressure can provoke non‑IgE mediated mast cell activation in conditions like cholinergic urticaria or cold urticaria.  
- Complement activation related to hemodialysis membranes, protamine (used in cardiopulmonary bypass), and certain intravenous agents can cause distributive features via anaphylatoxin generation.

Distinguishing IgE vs non‑IgE mechanisms after stabilization
Once the patient is stabilized, allergy consultation can help determine whether skin testing, graded drug challenges, or desensitization are appropriate. For severe reactions where an IgE mechanism is suspected (e.g., penicillin anaphylaxis), skin testing can guide future drug use. For reactions likely to be non‑IgE mediated and infusion‑related, alternative agents, slower infusion rates, premedication, or desensitization protocols may be considered.

Clinical decision points
- Treat life‑threatening reactions with epinephrine regardless of suspected mechanism.  
- If reaction occurred on first exposure or in a dose‑dependent fashion (e.g., rapid infusion), consider non‑IgE mediated mechanisms and adjust future administration accordingly.  
- Document the suspected mechanism in the medical record and arrange specialist allergy testing later when appropriate.

Conclusion

In the acute setting, hypotension, flushing, and urticaria demand rapid assessment and stabilization but also careful differential diagnosis because management strategies diverge. Anaphylaxis requires immediate intramuscular epinephrine, airway protection, and supportive measures. However, septic/distributive shock, vasovagal syncope, orthostatic hypotension, vasoactive tumor syndromes (carcinoid and catecholamine‑secreting tumors), mast cell activation disorders, chronic urticaria, and non‑IgE drug reactions can present with overlapping features. Distinguishing among these conditions rests on a synthesis of timing and triggers, associated symptoms (respiratory compromise, diarrhea, focal infectious signs), skin lesion characteristics (pruritic urticarial wheals versus prolonged erythema or purpura), hemodynamic patterns (paroxysmal hypertension vs vasodilation), and targeted laboratory testing (serum tryptase, lactate, blood cultures, urinary 5‑HIAA, plasma metanephrines). When uncertainty exists and anaphylaxis is plausible, immediate treatment with epinephrine is warranted because delay can be fatal. Subsequent directed investigations and specialist consultations (infectious disease, allergy/immunology, endocrinology, or dermatology) should follow once the patient is stabilized to identify the underlying cause and guide long‑term management.
