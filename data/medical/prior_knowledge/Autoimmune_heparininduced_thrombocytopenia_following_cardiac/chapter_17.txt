Chapter 17: Antithrombin Physiology and Replacement Therapy

Introduction

Antithrombin (AT, formerly antithrombin III) is a central physiological regulator of the coagulation cascade. It neutralizes several activated serine proteases, most notably thrombin (factor IIa) and factor Xa, and thus serves as a critical endogenous anticoagulant. Clinical recognition of AT deficiency and its management is important in a variety of settings: congenital thrombophilia, acquired thrombosis associated with consumptive coagulopathy or liver disease, heparin resistance during anticoagulant therapy, and supportive therapy during high-risk perioperative or obstetric periods. This chapter reviews the molecular structure and synthesis of antithrombin, the laboratory assays used to measure AT and how to interpret them, the pathophysiologic consequences of AT deficiency with special attention to how AT levels alter the efficacy of indirect anticoagulants, and the indications, dosing, monitoring, and safety considerations for antithrombin concentrate replacement. The final section focuses on the pharmacologic interactions between AT and two commonly used indirect anticoagulants—heparin and fondaparinux—and the clinical implications of those interactions.

# Antithrombin structure, synthesis and physiological function

Antithrombin is a member of the serine protease inhibitor (serpin) superfamily. The mature molecule is a glycoprotein of approximately 58 kDa produced primarily in the liver and secreted into plasma. Human antithrombin contains a reactive center loop that acts as a pseudo-substrate for target proteases; target proteases cleave the reactive loop and become irreversibly trapped in a covalent complex with AT, thereby inactivating the enzyme. Post-translational modifications, particularly N-linked glycosylation, influence AT’s plasma concentration, half-life, and functional properties. A clinically important isoform is the beta-AT variant that has decreased glycosylation and higher affinity for heparin-binding; this variant can modulate clinical phenotype in some contexts.

Physiologically, AT inhibits multiple activated coagulation factors, most potently thrombin (factor IIa) and factor Xa, but also factor IXa, XIa, and XIIa to lesser degrees. AT baseline activity in healthy adults is typically expressed as percent of normal or as IU/mL; typical reference ranges for AT activity are approximately 80–120% (often 0.80–1.20 IU/mL) though specific laboratory ranges vary. AT’s inhibitory reactions occur slowly in plasma under basal conditions; the anticoagulant potency of AT is dramatically accelerated by glycosaminoglycans such as heparin and heparan sulfate. Heparin binds to AT via a specific pentasaccharide sequence and induces a conformational change in AT that increases the rate of factor Xa inhibition by several hundred-fold; for thrombin inhibition, a longer heparin chain is required to simultaneously bind AT and thrombin and serve as a template for the interaction.

Beyond neutralizing coagulation proteases, AT has additional biologic actions, including anti-inflammatory and endothelial-protective effects mediated in part by interactions with endothelial cell surface glycosaminoglycans. These non-anticoagulant properties are of interest in critical-care settings, but the dominant clinical relevance is AT’s role in regulating coagulation and the dependence of many anticoagulants on AT to exert their effects.

# Laboratory measurement and interpretation of antithrombin levels

AT measurement is commonly performed by either functional (activity) assays or antigen assays. Functional assays assess the ability of patient plasma to inhibit a target protease (usually factor Xa or thrombin) in the presence of excess heparin or without heparin, depending on the methodology; chromogenic assays that measure residual enzyme activity after incubation with patient plasma are widely used. Antigen assays quantify the concentration of AT protein by immunologic methods (e.g., immunoturbidimetry, nephelometry). The distinction matters: a low functional activity with normal antigen suggests a qualitative (type II) AT defect, whereas parallel reductions in antigen and activity suggest a quantitative (type I) deficiency.

Reference ranges vary among laboratories; typical AT activity reference range is ~80–120%. Many assays express results in percent of normal or IU/mL; always report and interpret in the context of the laboratory’s reference interval. For neonates, AT levels are physiologically lower and gradually increase to adult values over months; corrected reference intervals for age should be used. Acute-phase changes, heparin therapy, sample handling (e.g., contamination with heparin), and presence of direct thrombin inhibitors can influence assay results—aware interpretation is required.

Indications to measure AT include: unexplained or recurrent venous thromboembolism (particularly in young patients or family history suggesting inherited thrombophilia); heparin resistance (see below); low antithrombin suspected in critical illnesses (sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), liver failure, nephrotic syndrome with urinary AT loss); and when planning management for patients with known congenital AT deficiency during pregnancy or surgery. When low AT activity is found, repeated testing off heparin and after recovery from acute illness is often required to distinguish congenital from transient acquired deficiency. Genetic testing of SERPINC1 (the AT gene) can confirm congenital deficiency when clinically indicated.

Clinical laboratories may also report AT as percent of pooled normal plasma. Key diagnostic points: type I congenital AT deficiency demonstrates proportionally reduced antigen and activity (e.g., both 40–60% of normal), while type II defects have normal antigen with reduced activity and are further subclassified by the location of the functional defect (reactive site, heparin-binding site, pleiotropic). Because AT assays may be influenced by anticoagulant drugs, interpretation must account for concurrent therapy. For example, heparin present in a sample can artifactually raise activity in some assay formats; conversely, direct factor Xa or IIa inhibitors can interfere variably with chromogenic substrates.

# Consequences of low antithrombin on anticoagulant efficacy (especially indirect agents)

Antithrombin deficiency predisposes to thrombosis by allowing unopposed activity of thrombin and factor Xa and decreased regulation of the coagulation cascade. Clinically, patients with congenital AT deficiency have a markedly increased lifetime risk of venous thromboembolism (VTE); heterozygous type I or type II defects are the typical forms encountered. Homozygous or compound heterozygous deficiency is rare and often incompatible with life. Acquired AT deficiency—seen in sepsis with consumptive coagulopathy, DIC, nephrotic syndrome (due to urinary losses), or severe liver disease (decreased synthesis)—similarly increases thrombotic risk.

A critical and clinically actionable consequence of low AT is reduced efficacy of indirect anticoagulants that rely on AT as a cofactor. Unfractionated heparin (UFH) binds to AT and potentiates its inhibitory activity against thrombin and factor Xa. Low plasma AT levels limit the amount of functional AT-heparin complex that can form; therefore, in AT deficiency heparin’s anticoagulant effect is blunted. The phenomenon of heparin resistance—failure to achieve expected prolongation of activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) or inadequate anti-Xa response despite escalating heparin dosing—can reflect insufficient AT. Similarly, low AT reduces the activity of low molecular weight heparins (LMWH) which predominantly potentiate AT-mediated inhibition of factor Xa, and of fondaparinux, a synthetic pentasaccharide that exerts anti-Xa activity exclusively via AT. Thus, in AT deficiency these therapies may be quantitatively and qualitatively less effective.

This pharmacologic dependency has practical consequences. For example, patients with severe AT deficiency may present with VTE despite therapeutic doses of LMWH or UFH. In the setting of continuous anticoagulation (e.g., cardiopulmonary bypass, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO), or heparin infusion for VTE), AT depletion can produce heparin resistance necessitating large heparin doses or alternative strategies. Clinicians need to distinguish causes of inadequate anticoagulation: inadequate dosing, increased heparin clearance, elevated levels of heparin-binding proteins, hypercoagulable state, and AT deficiency. Measuring AT activity and anti-Xa levels can guide whether supplementing AT is likely to restore anticoagulant responsiveness.

Of note, direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) and direct thrombin inhibitors (e.g., dabigatran) exert anticoagulant effects independently of AT. Therefore, AT deficiency does not directly impair their mechanism of action. This pharmacologic distinction is useful in both acute management and when planning long-term anticoagulation strategies for patients with AT deficiency.

# Indications, dosing and monitoring of antithrombin concentrate

Indications

Antithrombin concentrate (plasma-derived AT replacement) is used to correct clinically significant AT deficiency in specific scenarios. Indications include: 1) patients with congenital AT deficiency who have active thrombosis in whom AT repletion is clinically indicated; 2) patients with congenital AT deficiency undergoing high-risk surgery or invasive procedures (including cesarean section) where perioperative prophylaxis against thrombosis is required; 3) correction of AT deficiency-associated heparin resistance to restore heparin responsiveness in therapeutic anticoagulation or extracorporeal circuits (e.g., ECMO, cardiopulmonary bypass); and 4) selected critically ill patients with severe acquired AT deficiency in the context of coagulopathy where clinicians judge that AT replacement will aid management. The strength of evidence for some indications (e.g., routine use in sepsis-associated DIC) is limited and practice varies; institutional protocols and specialist consultation (hematology) are recommended.

Dosing

Commercial AT concentrates are human plasma–derived products; recombinant AT products also exist for neonatal congenital deficiency in some jurisdictions. Dosing strategies aim to raise the patient’s AT activity to a target level sufficient to restore anticoagulant efficacy. Common clinical targets are AT activity between 80% and 120% of normal, though lower thresholds (e.g., >60–70%) are sometimes considered adequate to achieve heparin responsiveness depending on clinical context.

A widely used dosing formula for AT concentrate is:

Dose (IU) = \frac{(Target\;AT\;% - Measured\;AT\;% ) \times Weight\;(kg)}{1.4}

This formula is derived from pharmacokinetic estimates of AT distribution and the expected rise in percent activity per IU/kg. For example, to raise AT activity from 40% to a target of 100% in a 70 kg patient:

Dose = \frac{(100 - 40) \times 70}{1.4} = \frac{60 \times 70}{1.4} \approx 3000\;IU

Alternatively, dosing regimens provided by manufacturers or institutional protocols often recommend an initial bolus of approximately 50–60 IU/kg to achieve a rapid rise, followed by maintenance doses (e.g., 25 IU/kg every 24 hours) to sustain activity, with adjustments guided by measured AT levels. Another practical approach used in some centers is to give an initial bolus of 1,500–3,000 IU depending on body weight and baseline AT, followed by daily dosing for several days as needed.

Because individual responses vary, weight, baseline AT activity, target level, and product-specific potency must be used to calculate dose. For neonates and children, dosing is weight-based; neonates also have naturally lower baseline AT, which influences targets and interpretation. In emergencies with severe deficiency and heparin resistance, higher initial boluses may be required to rapidly restore heparin responsiveness.

Monitoring

After AT concentrate administration, measure AT activity to confirm that target levels have been achieved. Timing of measurement typically is within 30–60 minutes after bolus dosing for peak level assessment, and before subsequent maintenance doses to guide further therapy. Concomitant monitoring of anticoagulant effect is essential: with UFH, monitor aPTT and anti-Xa activity; with LMWH or fondaparinux, assess anti-Xa levels (using calibrators appropriate for the agent). For extracorporeal circuits and cardiopulmonary bypass, activated clotting time (ACT) may be monitored alongside AT activity.

Clinical decision points include reassessing if heparin resistance persists despite AT correction—other causes of resistance should be sought. Duration of AT replacement is individualized and determined by the clinical scenario: for perioperative prophylaxis, replacement may be given perioperatively and in the immediate postoperative period until thrombotic risk subsides; for treatment of heparin resistance, replacement is continued until heparin responsiveness is restored and the underlying cause is addressed.

Safety and practical considerations

AT concentrate is generally well tolerated. Because it is plasma-derived, product safety with respect to viral transmission has been optimized with donor screening and viral inactivation steps, but theoretical residual risk remains. Thrombosis is a potential paradoxical risk—rapid correction of AT in certain prothrombotic states could, in theory, have complex effects, although the principal clinical concern is ensuring that anticoagulation is adequate when AT levels are corrected. Hypersensitivity reactions, including anaphylaxis, are possible. Because AT replacement can interact with concurrent anticoagulants, careful monitoring is needed to avoid over- or under-anticoagulation.

Practically, AT concentrate should be administered with hematology consultation whenever possible. Fresh frozen plasma (FFP) contains AT and may be used if concentrate is unavailable, but FFP carries risks of volume overload and transfusion reactions and requires much larger volumes to achieve meaningful AT increases.

# Interaction with fondaparinux and heparin efficacy

Mechanistic basis

Understanding the molecular interactions among AT, heparin, LMWH, and fondaparinux clarifies clinical behavior. UFH is a heterogeneous mixture of glycosaminoglycan chains. A specific pentasaccharide sequence on heparin binds to AT and induces a conformational change in AT that enhances inhibition of factor Xa; for efficient thrombin inhibition, UFH must be long enough (≥ approximately 18 saccharide units) to bridge AT and thrombin simultaneously, forming a ternary complex. LMWHs are shorter chains that preferentially enhance AT-mediated factor Xa inhibition; some still inhibit thrombin to a limited extent depending on chain length. Fondaparinux is a synthetic pentasaccharide that faithfully reproduces the AT-binding pentasaccharide segment; it has essentially no direct action on thrombin and exerts its anticoagulant effect exclusively by potentiating AT inhibition of factor Xa.

Clinical implications

Because both UFH and fondaparinux depend on AT to exert their anticoagulant effects, low AT levels blunt their activity. The dependence is most absolute for fondaparinux: without sufficient AT, fondaparinux cannot inhibit factor Xa. LMWH, while also AT-dependent, retains relatively greater anti-Xa activity because of its pharmacokinetic properties, but the effect is still diminished in AT deficiency. UFH’s capacity to accelerate inhibition of thrombin and factor Xa similarly relies on AT; however, in some situations high-dose UFH may partially compensate because of high plasma concentrations and other interactions, though often at the cost of bleeding risk and without reliable restoration of therapeutic anticoagulation if AT is severely depleted.

Clinically, the consequence is heparin resistance or therapeutic failure with fondaparinux/LMWH when AT is low. In a patient with suspected heparin resistance—defined variably but often as a requirement for large heparin doses (e.g., >35,000 IU/day) to reach aPTT goal, or failure to achieve therapeutic anti-Xa despite escalating heparin—the clinician should measure AT activity. If AT activity is low and the clinical setting warrants restoration of anticoagulation (e.g., ongoing thrombosis, requirement for extracorporeal support), AT concentrate administration is likely to improve responses to heparin and fondaparinux by supplying the required cofactor. Conversely, if AT is normal, alternative explanations for resistance should be pursued (e.g., high levels of heparin-binding proteins, increased heparin clearance, or laboratory assay interference).

Fondaparinux has practical constraints: because it exclusively requires AT, it is particularly susceptible to therapeutic failure in low AT states. In patients with documented congenital AT deficiency, fondaparinux may be ineffective unless AT replacement is provided. When using fondaparinux or LMWH in a patient with recent or ongoing consumption of AT (e.g., severe sepsis, DIC), clinicians should anticipate possible attenuation of effect and prefer strategies that do not rely solely on AT if appropriate (e.g., direct factor Xa inhibitors or parenteral direct thrombin inhibitors), or consider AT replacement where clinically indicated.

Summary of clinical decision points

- Measure AT activity when anticoagulation with heparin, LMWH, or fondaparinux is unexpectedly inadequate, or when there is high thrombotic risk with clinical suspicion for AT deficiency.

- If AT activity is significantly reduced (e.g., well below the lower limit of normal, often <60–70%), and restoration of indirect anticoagulant efficacy is needed, consider AT concentrate to reach a target AT activity (commonly 80–120%), using weight-based dosing and post-dose measurement to confirm response.

- If AT activity is normal, look for other causes of heparin resistance and consider alternative anticoagulants that do not require AT (e.g., direct thrombin inhibitors or DOACs) when appropriate.

- In patients with congenital AT deficiency, individualized perioperative and peripartum plans should be made, often combining AT replacement with anticoagulant therapy to minimize thrombotic risk.

Conclusion

Antithrombin is a pivotal endogenous anticoagulant whose deficiency—congenital or acquired—has major implications for thrombosis risk and for the pharmacologic efficacy of heparin-based anticoagulants and fondaparinux. Accurate laboratory assessment of AT activity and antigen, an understanding of the distinctions among anticoagulant mechanisms, and judicious use of AT concentrate are essential competencies for clinicians managing patients with thrombosis, heparin resistance, or high-risk periprocedural states. When AT replacement is indicated, dosing should be individualized using standardized formulas or manufacturer guidance, with prompt post-administration monitoring to ensure therapeutic targets are achieved while avoiding complications.
