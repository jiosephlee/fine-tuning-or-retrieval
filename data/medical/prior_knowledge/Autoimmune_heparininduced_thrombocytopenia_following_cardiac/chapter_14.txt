Introduction

Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) is pooled polyclonal IgG derived from thousands of donors and is widely used in immune-mediated hematologic disorders. Its use in thrombocytopenias—most commonly immune thrombocytopenia (ITP) and the rarer, more pathophysiologically specific autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (aHIT)—is based on several immunomodulatory mechanisms that can rapidly blunt antibody-mediated platelet clearance and activation. For clinicians managing patients with immune-mediated platelet disorders, understanding how IVIG works, how to dose it, what responses to expect, and what risks it carries is essential for rational use. This chapter reviews the mechanisms of action of IVIG relevant to platelet disorders, standard dosing and rationale for repeat administration, expected time-course of platelet and laboratory responses, major adverse effects (with emphasis on thrombotic and renal complications), comparative efficacy in ITP versus aHIT and predictors of response, and the ways IVIG can interfere with diagnostic laboratory testing and how to interpret results in that context.

# Mechanisms of action: Fc receptor blockade, anti-idiotype antibodies, modulation of complement and cytokines

IVIG exerts a multiplicity of effects on the immune system; no single mechanism explains all clinical benefit. Three mechanistic themes are most relevant to immune-mediated thrombocytopenia.

Fc receptor blockade and modulation of Fc gamma receptors. A principal and rapid mechanism by which IVIG increases platelet counts in antibody-mediated thrombocytopenias is competitive blockade of Fc gamma receptors (FcγRs) on phagocytic cells, primarily splenic macrophages. Platelet opsonization by IgG autoantibodies leads to FcγR-mediated phagocytosis. High concentrations of exogenous IgG saturate FcγRs and mitigate binding and internalization of autoantibody-coated platelets, reducing peripheral platelet destruction. In addition to simple saturation, IVIG can modulate the expression and activation state of FcγRs: it downregulates activating FcγRIIa and FcγRIII and upregulates the inhibitory FcγRIIb on effector cells. This shift in the activating/inhibitory balance further diminishes antibody-mediated clearance and cellular activation.

Anti-idiotype antibodies and neutralization. IVIG contains a repertoire of anti-idiotype antibodies that can bind to pathogenic autoantibodies. In classic ITP, where the dominant antibodies are directed against platelet glycoproteins (e.g., GPIIb/IIIa, GPIb/IX), anti-idiotypic IgG in IVIG may bind and neutralize these autoantibodies, reducing their capacity to opsonize platelets. In aHIT—an autoimmune HIT variant characterized by high-titer, platelet-activating anti-PF4 antibodies—there is biologic plausibility that IVIG might include anti-idiotypes to anti-PF4 antibodies, though the mechanism most consistently operative in aHIT appears to be FcγR blockade rather than direct antibody neutralization.

Modulation of complement and inflammatory pathways. IVIG contains natural antibodies and complement regulatory proteins that can inhibit complement activation. Complement contributes to platelet opsonization and activation in some immune cytopenias; by scavenging activated complement components and inhibiting formation of membrane attack complexes, IVIG reduces complement-mediated platelet injury. IVIG also modulates cytokine networks: it can neutralize proinflammatory cytokines, affect dendritic cell and T-cell function, and induce expansion/function of regulatory T cells. These slower immunomodulatory effects may help prevent recurrence and sustain platelet recovery after the immediate Fc receptor blockade effect wanes.

These mechanisms act on different time scales. Fc receptor blockade is rapid and explains the brisk platelet count rises often seen within 24–72 hours. Anti-idiotype neutralization and modulation of cellular immunity evolve over days to weeks and may determine durability of response.

# Standard dosing regimens and repeat dosing rationale

Two principal dosing regimens are used clinically and supported by controlled trials in ITP: a high single-day regimen of 1 g/kg IV daily for 1–2 consecutive days (commonly 1 g/kg × 2 days) and a longer regimen of 0.4 g/kg IV daily for 5 consecutive days. Both regimens deliver a total dose of approximately 2 g/kg, although clinical use frequently employs the shorter 1 g/kg × 2 schedule for convenience and similar short-term efficacy. Dosing is based on actual body weight in most adult patients. For pediatric patients dosages are scaled by weight in a similar fashion.

Repeat dosing is considered when the initial response is incomplete or when there is relapse after an initial improvement. In ITP, repeat courses of IVIG may be given if platelet counts fall again and bleeding risk emerges, although chronic reliance on IVIG is limited by cost, supply, and cumulative adverse effects. In the context of aHIT, repeat dosing is sometimes needed because pathogenic anti-PF4 antibodies can persist and continue to activate platelets. The rationale for repeat dosing rests on the transient nature of FcγR saturation: infused IgG has a half-life of approximately 21–30 days, but the functional high serum concentrations that produce Fc receptor blockade decline over days to a few weeks. If pathogenic antibody levels remain high or platelet destruction/activation recurs, retreatment can re-establish FcγR blockade and raise platelet counts. Clinicians must balance repeat dosing against increasing thrombotic and renal risk and consider alternative or adjunctive therapies when repeated IVIG is required.

Infusion rates and preparation choice matter. Slower infusion rates decrease infusion reactions. In patients at increased renal risk, formulations without sucrose are preferred because sucrose-containing IVIG preparations are associated with osmotic nephrosis and acute kidney injury.

# Expected time-course of platelet response and markers of efficacy

Time course in antibody-mediated thrombocytopenia is predictable but variable. In ITP, IVIG generally produces a rapid platelet rise: many patients show an increase within 24–48 hours, with a peak typically within 2–7 days. A clinically meaningful response is commonly defined as achieving a platelet count >30 × 10^9/L or a doubling of the baseline platelet count in the context of bleeding risk mitigation. Durable remission after a single course is less common; responses may last weeks to months in some patients but often wane as infused IgG concentrations fall.

In aHIT, time to platelet recovery may be similar or slightly delayed compared with ITP, depending on the intensity of ongoing platelet activation and whether exposure to heparin or other triggers persists. A desirable treatment effect in HIT is restoration of a rising platelet count within 24–72 hours and cessation of new thrombotic events. Because HIT is a prothrombotic disorder in which platelets are consumed and activated rather than solely cleared, platelet count normalization may lag behind clinical stabilization in some cases.

Quantitative markers of efficacy include absolute platelet counts and the slope of platelet recovery. Normal platelet count ranges are approximately 150–450 × 10^9/L. In patients with severe thrombocytopenia, reaching a safe hemostatic threshold depends on context: for minor bleeding or invasive procedures a platelet count of at least 50 × 10^9/L is often targeted; for life-threatening bleeding or neurosurgical procedures, higher thresholds apply. Clinically, a rise to >50 × 10^9/L within 48–72 hours is considered an excellent response. In addition to platelet counts, clinicians monitor clinical endpoints: cessation of bleeding, avoidance of new thrombosis (particularly in HIT), and stabilization of hemoglobin.

Laboratory markers beyond platelet counts have limited direct utility but can be adjunctive. For HIT, functional platelet activation assays (e.g., serotonin release assay or heparin-induced platelet activation assays) indicate ongoing platelet activation by anti-PF4 antibodies. After IVIG, these functional assays can become negative due to Fc receptor blockade even if anti-PF4 antibodies persist, so clinicians should interpret changes in functional assay results with the knowledge of recent IVIG exposure. Conversely, anti-PF4 ELISA optical density values may persist despite clinical improvement.

# Risks and adverse effects of high-dose IVIG (thrombotic risk, renal dysfunction)

IVIG is generally well tolerated, but high-dose therapy carries important, sometimes severe risks that must be weighed before use.

Thrombotic events. High-dose IVIG increases the risk of both venous and arterial thromboses, including deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, myocardial infarction, and ischemic stroke. Mechanisms include increased serum viscosity due to transient high IgG concentrations, procoagulant contaminants in some preparations, platelet activation, and increased levels of prothrombotic factors such as fibrinogen in susceptible individuals. Risk factors for IVIG-associated thrombosis include advanced age, prior thromboembolic disease, cardiovascular risk factors, immobility, dehydration, high baseline hematocrit or hyperviscosity states (e.g., monoclonal gammopathy), and concurrent prothrombotic conditions (such as active systemic inflammation, antiphospholipid antibody syndrome, or HIT itself). When giving IVIG to patients with active thrombosis or high thrombotic risk, the clinician must balance the potential benefit of rapidly increasing platelet counts against the potential of worsening thrombosis; in many cases, continued anticoagulation (appropriate for HIT management) is necessary and should be carefully coordinated with IVIG administration, since anticoagulation and IVIG together may raise bleeding vs thrombosis balancing issues.

Renal dysfunction and acute kidney injury. IVIG-associated renal impairment is a recognized complication, most notably with formulations that use sucrose as a stabilizer. Osmotic nephrosis—proximal tubular cell injury due to accumulation of sugar molecules—can cause acute kidney injury (AKI). Risk factors include pre-existing renal insufficiency, older age, diabetes, use of nephrotoxic drugs, and dehydration. To mitigate risk, use non-sucrose preparations, ensure adequate hydration before and during infusion, avoid rapid infusion rates, and monitor renal function (serum creatinine, urine output) closely. In patients on dialysis or with severe CKD, IVIG may still be used but with caution, smaller doses, and avoidance of high infusion rates.

Other adverse effects. Acute infusion reactions (headache, chills, fever, myalgias), aseptic meningitis (more common in high-dose IVIG), hemolytic anemia due to passive transfer of blood group antibodies (particularly in IVIG lots with anti-A or anti-B), neutropenia, and rare severe allergic reactions (anaphylaxis; more likely in IgA-deficient patients with anti-IgA antibodies) are all possible. Hemolysis risk is higher in non-O blood groups and with high cumulative doses. Monitor hemoglobin and signs of hemolysis in susceptible patients. Pre-medication with acetaminophen and antihistamines and slowing infusion rates can reduce mild infusion reactions.

Clinical decision points to minimize harm include screening for thrombosis risk, using non-sucrose products in renal risk, hydrating patients, avoiding rapid infusion, and close monitoring of renal function and for thrombotic signs. In patients with very high thrombotic risk, alternative therapies or co-management with hematology and nephrology is advisable.

# Relative efficacy in ITP versus aHIT and factors predicting response

Comparative efficacy. IVIG is highly effective in producing rapid platelet count increases in acute ITP and is a mainstay for patients with significant bleeding or needing urgent platelet elevation (e.g., before procedures). The mechanism—blocking FcγR-mediated platelet phagocytosis—matches the dominant pathophysiology in many cases of primary ITP, explaining consistent and often dramatic responses.

In autoimmune HIT (aHIT), the pathophysiology differs: patients have high-titer, platelet-activating IgG anti-platelet factor 4 (PF4) antibodies that form immune complexes with PF4 (with or without heparin) and engage FcγRIIa on platelets and monocytes, driving platelet activation and thrombosis. IVIG can blunt this FcγR-mediated activation through Fc receptor blockade, and clinical reports show benefit in many aHIT cases, particularly in restoring platelet counts and halting further thrombosis. However, IVIG efficacy in aHIT is more variable than in ITP because ongoing immune complex-mediated platelet activation can overwhelm Fc blockade, and because antibody levels and platelet activation potency in aHIT may be very high. Thus, while IVIG is a useful adjunct in aHIT—especially for severe, refractory thrombocytopenia or when rapid platelet recovery is required before procedures—it is not universally effective.

Predictors of response. Factors that predict a favorable response to IVIG include lower antibody burden and less intense platelet activation, earlier administration relative to onset, preserved spleen function (for ITP), and absence of persistent triggers that sustain antibody production. In ITP, a history of prior IVIG responsiveness predicts future benefit. In aHIT, predictors of a good response include lower anti-PF4 ELISA optical density (OD) values and weaker reactivity on functional platelet activation assays; conversely, very high ELISA OD values and strongly positive functional assays (e.g., high serotonin release) suggest a higher pathogenic antibody load and may predict a blunted or transient response. Renal impairment and concurrent prothrombotic conditions may also influence the risk-benefit ratio but are not direct predictors of immunologic efficacy.

Duration of response differs: ITP patients often have transient responses that can be sustained with additional immunomodulatory therapy (steroids, thrombopoietin receptor agonists, rituximab). In aHIT the key clinical goal is to control platelet activation while pathogenic antibodies wane and to prevent or treat thrombosis with non-heparin anticoagulation; IVIG may rapidly bridge patients but repeated dosing or additional immunosuppression may be needed if antibodies persist.

# Interaction of IVIG with laboratory assays and interpretation

IVIG interferes with several laboratory assays used in the diagnosis and monitoring of immune-mediated thrombocytopenias, often complicating interpretation in the immediate post-infusion period.

Effects on functional platelet activation assays. IVIG’s Fc receptor blockade can render functional assays that depend on platelet FcγR-mediated activation false-negative. For example, the serotonin release assay (SRA) and other platelet activation tests will often be suppressed after IVIG administration despite persistence of pathogenic antibodies. This is an important mechanistic artifact: a negative functional assay after IVIG does not necessarily mean the pathogenic antibody has disappeared; rather, the assay’s pathway for detecting activation has been silenced by saturating FcγRs. Clinicians must note timing of IVIG when interpreting functional assays and, if possible, delay functional testing for several half-lives of the infused IgG or interpret results in clinical context.

Effects on immunoassays for antibodies. IVIG can lead to transient positivity in some serologic tests because of passive transfer of antibodies from donors. Conversely, IVIG may alter optical density readings in ELISAs for anti-PF4 antibodies: some patients may have persistent high OD values despite clinical improvement, whereas others may show declines in OD unrelated to true disappearance of autoantibodies because of assay interference. Laboratories may report anti-PF4 ELISA OD values; ELISA results should be integrated with clinical probability scores (e.g., 4Ts score for HIT) and functional assays, bearing in mind IVIG’s potential to blunt functional assay positivity.

Effects on coagulation and hemolysis testing. IVIG can cause hemolysis via passive anti-A/B antibodies that result in positive direct antiglobulin (Coombs) tests and decreased hemoglobin. Hemolytic markers (LDH, bilirubin, haptoglobin) should be interpreted with this possibility in mind. IVIG may also slightly alter measured levels of immunoglobulins, complement components, and certain serologic tests, potentially causing transient false positives for infectious serologies or autoantibodies. Passive transfer is time-limited and depends on the half-life of the infused IgG.

Practical laboratory guidance. When possible, draw baseline diagnostic samples before IVIG administration. If IVIG has already been given, document timing precisely so laboratory specialists can interpret assays correctly. If a functional assay is negative after IVIG but clinical suspicion remains high, either repeat testing after an interval that allows IVIG effects to wane or rely on clinical gestalt and other laboratory information (ELISA OD, clinical course) for management decisions. Communicate with the laboratory about assay limitations—many reference labs annotate reports with cautionary language when recent IVIG exposure is known.

Conclusion and clinical decision points

IVIG is a powerful and mechanistically multifaceted therapy for immune-mediated thrombocytopenias. Its rapid action via Fc receptor blockade makes it particularly valuable when quick platelet elevation is needed, such as active bleeding, urgent procedures, or severe thrombocytopenia in ITP and as an adjunctive therapy in aHIT to blunt platelet activation while definitive measures (discontinuation of heparin, initiation of non-heparin anticoagulation, and immunologic recovery) take effect. Standard dosing regimens are 1 g/kg IV daily for 1–2 days or 0.4 g/kg IV daily for 5 days, with repeat dosing guided by clinical response and bleeding/thrombosis risk.

Risks—most importantly thrombosis and renal dysfunction—require careful patient selection, screening for predisposing conditions, use of non-sucrose formulations in renal risk, adequate hydration, and slow infusion rates. Expect platelet rises within 24–72 hours in many patients; durable remission is less predictable and differs between ITP and aHIT. IVIG interferes with functional and immunologic assays; baseline testing before infusion is ideal, and interpretation of post-IVIG assays must account for these effects.

In practice, IVIG is an essential tool in the hematologist’s armamentarium for immune-mediated platelet disorders, but its use should be individualized, balancing the need for rapid platelet recovery against the potential for significant adverse events and the limitations it imposes on diagnostic testing.
