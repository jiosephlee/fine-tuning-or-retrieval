Introduction

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) has evolved from an alternative for inoperable patients to a mainstream therapy for severe aortic stenosis across surgical risk profiles. Central to the success and safety of TAVI is device design: the frame architecture, leaflet position, sealing mechanisms, radial force characteristics, and delivery mechanics all determine how a valve performs hemodynamically, how it interacts with patient anatomy (especially calcified annuli and the coronary ostia), and what complications are likely. Understanding device classifications and the engineering choices behind them allows clinicians to match patient anatomy and procedural goals to the implant best suited for each case. This chapter reviews the major classes of transcatheter valves, examines the elements of frame and leaflet design, describes device-specific features that influence coronary access and implantation strategy, and links these design features to clinical consequences such as paravalvular leak, gradients, conduction disturbances and coronary obstruction.

# Device classifications: self-expanding, balloon-expandable, mechanically expandable

TAVI devices can be grouped by how the final valve frame is deployed and how radial force is generated and maintained.

Self-expanding devices
- Mechanism and materials: Self-expanding valves are typically made from shape-memory alloys such as nitinol. Nitinol frames are constrained during delivery and then released; they expand to a predetermined shape at body temperature. Expansion is gradual and results in persistent outward radial force.
- Deployment characteristics: Deployment is often gradual, allowing repositioning or recapture with some systems. The expansion is driven by the material’s stored energy rather than an external inflation. Because expansion is progressive and adaptive, self-expanding valves may conform to irregular annular shapes and accommodate some degree of under-sizing without exerting abrupt excessive force.
- Clinical implications: The chronic outward radial force may help seat the valve and reduce immediate paravalvular regurgitation over time, but persistent expansion against the conduction system can increase rates of conduction abnormalities and permanent pacemaker implantation. Examples include the Medtronic CoreValve and Evolut family, and other nitinol-based systems such as Portico (though it has its own deployment nuances) and ACURATE neo (which uses self-expanding elements but with specific design differences).

Balloon-expandable devices
- Mechanism and materials: Balloon-expandable frames are commonly made of cobalt-chromium or stainless steel alloys that are plastically deformed by balloon inflation during deployment, achieving the final diameter by permanent expansion.
- Deployment characteristics: Deployment is rapid and controlled by inflating a balloon to a specific volume/pressure. It generally produces high immediate radial force and a well-defined final shape. Repositioning is limited or impossible after full deployment.
- Clinical implications: High immediate radial force achieves strong anchoring, good circularization of the annulus and typically low residual paravalvular leak when sizing is appropriate. However, abrupt expansion can transmit high forces to the annulus and root, increasing risk of annular rupture, particularly in heavily calcified or small annuli or when aggressive oversizing is used. Balloon-expandable devices include the Edwards Sapien family and other systems employing balloon deployment.

Mechanically expandable devices
- Mechanism and materials: Mechanically expanding valves use a mechanical stent that can be expanded in a controlled fashion by a screw, slider, or other mechanism rather than by balloon inflation or shape-memory recoil. They allow precise, sometimes stepwise, adjustment of diameter and often permit full retrieval prior to final release.
- Deployment characteristics: The controlled mechanical expansion confers predictable apposition and often allows resheathing and repositioning until a final lock is engaged.
- Clinical implications: The ability to precisely control expansion can reduce risk of annular injury compared to balloon expansion and allow better optimization of final position. Mechanically expandable devices have varied market penetration; examples included the now-discontinued Boston Scientific Lotus valve, which illustrated both the benefits (precise deployment, low paravalvular leak) and real-world challenges (device availability/production issues). Use of mechanically expandable platforms may be considered when precise positioning and retrieval are priorities.

Choosing among classes involves weighing trade-offs: balloon-expandable valves for predictable circularization and lower post-implant gradients in many settings; self-expanding valves for conformability and potentially better outcomes in very large annuli or when the annulus is highly elliptical; mechanically expandable valves for stepwise, retrievable deployment when immediate precise control is required.

# Valve frame architecture, skirt/ sealing mechanisms and leaflet positions (supra- vs intra-annular)

Frame architecture
- Purpose and varieties: The frame is the structural scaffold that anchors the valve, transmits radial force to the annulus, and determines height and cell geometry. Frames vary by height (short vs tall), cell size (open vs dense lattice), and frame profile (tubular, flared inflow/outflow, hourglass). A short, low-profile frame may reduce interference with the coronary ostia and conduction system, whereas a tall frame may provide more anchoring surface in the LVOT or ascending aorta and can better accommodate eccentric or large annuli.
- Materials and fatigue: Frames are engineered from alloys chosen for strength, fatigue resistance, and compatibility with crimping for delivery. Frame fatigue and long-term structural integrity influence durability and rates of late deformation.

Skirt and sealing mechanisms
- Rationale: Paravalvular leak (PVL) was a major early complication of TAVI. Skirts and sealing mechanisms are engineered to reduce PVL by filling gaps between the stent frame and annular surface, especially in the presence of calcific irregularities.
- Designs: Skirts range from simple fabric cuffs (commonly polyethylene terephthalate, PET) positioned at the inflow portion of the frame, to more elaborate adaptive seals composed of compressible foam or pericardial wraps that expand to fill irregular spaces. Some valves incorporate external pericardial tissue or sealing rings integrated into the frame. The height and placement of the skirt determine where sealing occurs: an effective inflow skirt mitigates PVL by sealing at the annular plane or just below it; outflow skirts may be used to decrease regurgitation toward the ascending aorta.
- Clinical effects: A well-designed skirt reduces the degree of PVL and improves hemodynamics. However, very thick or external skirts may impede coronary access, and skirts that extend higher may increase risk of sinus obstruction or complicate future coronary interventions.

Leaflet position: supra-annular versus intra-annular
- Definitions: Intra-annular leaflets are mounted within the native annulus plane, such that the leaflet coaptation point lies at or below the anatomic annulus. Supra-annular leaflets are mounted on the stent frame above the native annulus so the effective orifice area (EOA) of the prosthetic valve is positioned above the native annulus.
- Hemodynamic consequences: Supra-annular valves typically achieve larger EOAs and lower transvalvular gradients for a given nominal valve size, because the leaflet opening occurs in a larger diameter region above the potentially constrained annulus. This is especially advantageous in small annuli or patients at risk for prosthesis–patient mismatch (PPM). Intra-annular valves may have slightly higher gradients when annular dimension limits the prosthetic orifice.
- Durability and structural considerations: Leaflet mounting influences leaflet stresses and flow profiles. Supra-annular leaflets are subject to different loading patterns and may impart distinct shear stresses compared with intra-annular designs. Long-term durability data are accruing and depend on material, leaflet treatment, and hemodynamic environment.

Integration of frame, skirt, and leaflet placement is not independent: a tall frame with a high skirt and supra-annular leaflets produces different hemodynamic and anatomic interactions than a short frame with an intra-annular leaflet and low skirt. Device selection should consider these interacting design elements in relation to patient anatomy, risk of PVL, need for coronary access and expected gradients.

# Examples of device-specific features affecting coronary access and frame height

Coronary access after TAVI is an increasingly important consideration, especially as TAVI is used in younger patients who may require future coronary angiography or interventions. Device-specific features—frame height, cell design and commissural alignment strategy—determine how readily catheters can engage coronary ostia post-implant.

Representative devices and features
- Balloon-expandable Edwards Sapien family: These devices have relatively short frames and an intra-annular leaflet position. The lower frame height and open cell design above the skirt generally facilitate coronary access. The skirt is relatively low and circumferential; however, valve stent posts can still partially cover coronary ostia depending on implant depth and native cusp calcification.
- Medtronic CoreValve / Evolut family: These are tall, self-expanding nitinol frames with supra-annular leaflet position. The frame extends well into the ascending aorta and may sit above the coronary ostia depending on implant depth and valve size. The cell architecture varies, and outer cells may obstruct direct vertical access, requiring catheterization through smaller cells. These valves frequently present more challenges to coronary access, particularly in patients with low coronary heights or narrow sinus of Valsalva.
- Boston Scientific ACURATE neo: This self-expanding system is designed with a specific “top-down” deployment and has design features to facilitate commissural alignment and coronary access, including large open cells in the upper frame.
- Portico (St. Jude/Abbott) and Navitor: These valves have unique frame and leaflet positions intended to balance radial force and ease of access; cell size and skirt design are aspects to consider.

Anatomic and implant-related determinants
- Coronary height and sinus anatomy: Independent of device, coronary ostial height measured from the annulus and dimensions of the sinus of Valsalva are primary determinants of coronary access post-TAVI. Low coronary heights (<10–12 mm) and small sinuses complicate access, especially with tall frames.
- Frame alignment and rotation: If the prosthesis’ commissural posts land in front of a coronary ostium, catheter engagement may be blocked. Some manufacturers and operators use marker-based alignment strategies during implantation to optimize commissural alignment and thus improve future coronary access.
- Cell size and strut configuration: Open cell designs with large cells near the level of the coronary ostia are more permissive for catheters to traverse the frame. Dense cell designs or small upper cells reduce maneuverability and require specialized catheter techniques.

Practical considerations for coronary interventions post-TAVI
- Pre-procedural CT planning must include assessment of coronary height and sinus dimensions and consideration of device frame height relative to those parameters.
- When future coronary access is anticipated (e.g., younger patients, known coronary disease), favoring valves with low frame height, large upper cells and predictable commissural alignment may preserve access.
- During implantation, aiming for commissural alignment (rotational positioning) and optimal depth can reduce the probability of post-implant coronary obstruction or difficult catheter engagement.

# Radial force, conformability to anatomy and interaction with calcified annuli

Radial force
- Definitions: Radial force describes the outward pressure exerted by the valve frame on the annulus and adjacent tissues. For balloon-expandable devices radial force is delivered quickly and results from plastic deformation; for self-expanding devices radial force is applied progressively and maintained as chronic outward force.
- Clinical implications: Adequate radial force is necessary to secure the prosthesis and to oppose regurgitant flow that causes PVL. Excessive radial force, particularly abrupt or focal, can induce annular injury or rupture, root disruption, or coronary obstruction. Conversely, insufficient radial force may increase the risk of migration or significant PVL.

Conformability and eccentric annuli
- Conformability refers to how well a device adapts to the shape of an irregular or elliptical annulus. A highly conformable device can accommodate eccentric anatomy and uneven calcification, achieving apposition without focal pressure peaks.
- Self-expanding nitinol frames often excel at conforming to eccentric annuli because expansion is gradual and the material can redistribute forces. Balloon-expandable frames achieve circularization at the cost of potentially high focal stress where rigid calcific nodules prevent expansion.
- In small or highly calcified annuli, conformability reduces the need for aggressive oversizing and may lower rates of PVL and annular injury.

Interaction with calcified annuli
- Calcific distribution and burden: The pattern of calcification (nodular vs diffuse, location on cusps or LVOT) affects seating and sealing. Focal bulky nodules can prevent full apposition leading to PVL or displacement, and can focalize stress causing annular rupture with rigid frames.
- Clinical trade-offs: Balloon-expandable valves may forcibly displace calcium to create a circular orifice—this can be effective but risks localized trauma and annular rupture, particularly in small annuli, heavy LVOT calcification, or bicuspid valves with bulky raphe. Self-expanding valves may deform around nodules, reducing rupture risk but possibly leaving PVL channels if calcific nodules prevent adequate sealing.
- Adjunctive maneuvers: Pre-dilation (balloon aortic valvuloplasty) or post-dilation are strategies to modify the annulus or improve apposition; however, both carry risks: pre-dilation can embolize calcium, and post-dilation with high-pressure balloons can cause annular injury. The decision to pre- or post-dilate should integrate device choice, calcific burden, and expected radial force.

Device-specific performance in calcified anatomy
- Some devices incorporate adaptive sealing skirts or conformable frame geometries designed to accommodate calcification and reduce PVL without high expansion forces.
- Operators must balance the desire to eliminate PVL with the risk of annular injury: in severely calcified or small annuli, selecting devices with lower peak radial stress or the ability to recapture and reposition may be safer.

# Clinical implications of frame height, cell design and commissural alignment

Frame height
- Effects on conduction system: Taller frames extend deeper into the left ventricular outflow tract and may impinge on the membranous septum and the proximal conduction tissues, increasing rates of new left bundle branch block (LBBB) and need for permanent pacemaker implantation. For example, self-expanding tall frames are associated with higher pacemaker rates compared with shorter balloon-expandable frames. Implant depth is an important modifiable factor: shallower implantation reduces conduction disturbance risk but may increase valve embolization risk or PVL.
- Coronary obstruction and access: Tall frames may overlie coronary ostia. In patients with low coronary heights or small sinuses, tall frames elevate the risk of coronary obstruction, especially if native leaflets are bulky and get displaced upwards.
- Future interventions: A tall frame that extends into the ascending aorta may complicate coronary catheterization or valve-in-valve procedures. Conversely, a taller frame provides a larger landing zone for valve-in-valve scenarios in some designs.

Cell design
- Open vs closed cells: Open cell designs with larger diamond-shaped apertures allow easier access for coronary catheters to pass through the frame. Closed cell or dense lattice designs can hinder catheter manipulation and increase the procedural difficulty of coronary interventions.
- Radial stiffness and cell geometry: Smaller cell size can increase radial stiffness and scaffolding but at the expense of access. Cell geometry also affects how a future transcatheter heart valve or guidewire can be advanced during valve-in-valve procedures.
- Thrombosis risk and flow patterns: Dense frames may alter local flow vortices and influence leaflet washout; while not the dominant factor, cell geometry affects hemodynamic micro-environment and may contribute to leaflet thrombosis risk.

Commissural alignment
- Concept and importance: Native aortic valve commissures and prosthetic commissural posts should ideally be aligned so that prosthetic posts do not overlay coronary ostia. Commissural malalignment can place posts in front of coronaries and make future access or coronary interventions difficult or impossible.
- Techniques: Many operators now aim for commissural alignment using rotational orientation markers on delivery systems and pre-procedural CT to orient the valve prior to release. Some valve platforms have specific design features to facilitate alignment.
- Clinical impact: Proper commissural alignment improves the likelihood of straightforward coronary access, may reduce risk of coronary obstruction, and facilitates future interventions such as valve-in-valve procedures. Malalignment has been associated with more challenging coronary intubation in registry and bench studies.

Decision-making based on design features
- Patient-specific matching: Older patients without significant coronary disease and with low likelihood of future coronary interventions may tolerate devices that prioritize minimal PVL and optimal gradients even if coronary access is more challenging. Younger patients or those with multivessel coronary disease may benefit from valves with low frames, open cells and a commitment to commissural alignment.
- Conduction system risk mitigation: In patients with pre-existing conduction disease or those at high risk for pacemaker dependency, selecting valves and implantation depths associated with lower conduction disturbance rates is critical—often favoring balloon-expandable or shorter-frame options and shallow implantation.
- Valve-in-valve future planning: If valve-in-valve is anticipated, frame height and leaflet position influence feasibility. Supra-annular leaflets may offer better effective orifice areas after valve-in-valve but may also alter coronary flow dynamics. Some strategies evolve around bioprosthetic valve leaflet orientation to preserve coronary access.

Conclusion

Device design profoundly influences the immediate procedural strategy and long-term outcomes after TAVI. Understanding the distinctions among self-expanding, balloon-expandable, and mechanically expandable platforms; how frame architecture, skirts and leaflet position interact with patient anatomy; and how radial force, conformability and commissural alignment dictate both benefit and risk allows clinicians to individualize device selection. Thoughtful pre-procedural imaging and anticipation of future needs (such as coronary access or valve-in-valve therapy) together with awareness of device-specific behaviors in calcified annuli, conduction system proximity and sinus anatomy are essential for optimal TAVI planning and long-term care.
