Introduction

Aortic valve disease imposes a progressive burden on the left ventricle and systemic circulation that culminates in significant morbidity and mortality if untreated. Two clinical contexts dominate: native degenerative (calcific) aortic stenosis (AS) and dysfunction of implanted aortic bioprosthetic valves. Although one is a native disease of aging valve tissue and the other a consequence of implanting xenograft or homograft tissue, they share common biological processes—chronic inflammation, lipid infiltration, active osteogenic signaling, and mechanical stress—that ultimately produce leaflet stiffening, calcification, fibrosis, tearing, and functional obstruction or incompetence. Understanding the pathophysiology that leads to native AS and structural valve deterioration (SVD) in bioprostheses is essential for clinical reasoning around diagnosis, monitoring, prevention, and management. This chapter reviews the mechanisms of degenerative aortic stenosis, the structural pathways of bioprosthetic valve deterioration, how to differentiate thrombosis from structural degeneration, the impact of valve leaflet design on durability, the role of infection in accelerating valve destruction, and the clinical and hemodynamic manifestations of valve failure.

# Degenerative (calcific) aortic stenosis mechanisms

Degenerative, or calcific, aortic stenosis is not a passive “wear-and-tear” phenomenon but an active, regulated disease process that resembles atherosclerosis and ectopic bone formation. The earliest events include endothelial injury at the aortic side of the valve cusps, frequently in areas of altered shear stress. This endothelial perturbation allows infiltration of lipids, inflammatory cells (macrophages, T lymphocytes), and oxidized low-density lipoprotein (oxLDL) into the valvular interstitium.

Once within the leaflet, these components stimulate an inflammatory milieu. Valve interstitial cells (VICs), normally quiescent fibroblast-like cells, undergo phenotypic changes toward myofibroblast and osteoblast-like cells under the influence of cytokines (e.g., interleukin-1, tumor necrosis factor-alpha), transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β), and bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs). These osteogenic signals upregulate transcription factors such as Runx2, promoting deposition of a bone-like extracellular matrix and mineralization. Calcific nodules form within the leaflet matrix, around cellular debris and lipid cores; microcalcifications can coalesce into larger sheets that restrict cusp motion.

Mechanical stress and repetitive flexion also drive disease progression. Regions of high tensile and shear stress (the leaflet commissures and the tip regions) are predisposed to microinjury, increasing permeability and perpetuating the inflammatory and osteogenic cascade. Matrix metalloproteinases and other proteases remodel the extracellular matrix, enabling further calcification.

Systemic risk factors overlap with atherosclerotic disease: age, male sex, smoking, hyperlipidemia, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and chronic kidney disease (especially with disordered mineral metabolism) increase the risk and accelerate progression. However, the efficacy of systemic lipid-lowering therapy for halting established calcific AS has been disappointing in randomized trials, indicating that once osteogenic processes and calcification are established, they are poorly reversible.

Hemodynamically, progressive leaflet stiffening and reduced opening area cause increased transvalvular gradients and impulse load on the left ventricle. The left ventricle adapts with concentric hypertrophy to preserve systolic wall stress and maintain forward flow, but chronic pressure overload leads to increased myocardial oxygen consumption, diastolic dysfunction, myocardial fibrosis, and eventually systolic dysfunction if untreated.

# Structural valve deterioration (SVD): leaflet calcification, fibrosis, tearing and pannus

Structural valve deterioration (SVD) of bioprosthetic valves refers to intrinsic changes of the prosthetic tissue that compromise function, distinguishable from patient-prosthesis mismatch, paravalvular leak, or endocarditis. Bioprosthetic valves are most commonly made from porcine aortic valves or bovine pericardium subjected to chemical fixation (typically glutaraldehyde), which stabilizes collagen and reduces immunogenicity but leaves the tissue metabolically inert and prone to long-term degeneration.

The major morphologic pathways of SVD include leaflet calcification, progressive fibrosis and stiffening, mechanical tearing, and pannus formation. Leaflet calcification mirrors native calcific AS in many respects: passive deposition of calcium salts onto damaged collagen matrices and active cellular processes when residual viable cells or host inflammatory elements promote mineralization. Calcification predominantly affects regions of high mechanical stress and hinge points, translating clinically into restricted leaflet excursion and stenosis.

Fibrosis of the leaflet tissue can occur as a response to chronic mechanical stress, immune-mediated reactions to residual antigens in the xenograft, or low-grade inflammation. Fibrotic thickening reduces leaflet compliance, causing stenosis or contributing to coaptation failure in the case of irregular leaflet geometry. Mechanical tearing is often precipitated by chronic cyclical loading, localized calcific nodules producing stress concentration, or mismatch between the prosthesis and native annulus; tearing more commonly produces abrupt new regurgitation if it affects coaptation edges.

Pannus is an organized fibrous tissue ingrowth from the sewing ring or host annular tissue onto the prosthetic leaflets or hinge mechanisms. Unlike calcific deposits, pannus is composed of host fibrovascular tissue and tends to develop more slowly; it may restrict leaflet motion (producing stenosis) or interfere with leaflet coaptation. Risk factors for pannus include neo-intimal proliferation related to surgical trauma, persistent mechanical irritation at the sewing ring interface, and in some cases, an exaggerated fibroproliferative host response. Pannus is often dense and may be relatively insensitive to anticoagulation or anti-inflammatory measures.

Over time, these structural changes can produce isolated stenosis (reduced effective orifice area), isolated regurgitation (leaflet tear, loss of coaptation), or mixed lesions. The timeline of SVD is variable: older-generation bioprostheses frequently demonstrate clinically relevant SVD within 10–15 years, whereas contemporary treatments, improved tissue processing, and anti-calcification treatments have extended durability in many series. Nonetheless, younger patient age, higher metabolic activity, abnormal hemodynamic stress (e.g., prosthesis–patient mismatch, high cardiac output states), and immunologic factors accelerate deterioration.

# Bioprosthetic valve thrombosis vs structural degeneration

Distinguishing bioprosthetic valve thrombosis (BPVT) from structural degeneration is critical because their management differs: BPVT often responds to anticoagulation, while SVD may require reintervention. Thrombosis can produce both obstructive (reduced leaflet motion causing stenosis) and non-obstructive (reduced leaflet motion without significant gradient change) presentations. BPVT often occurs relatively early after implantation but can also present years later, particularly in the context of subtherapeutic anticoagulation, atrial fibrillation, low cardiac output states, or hypercoagulable conditions.

Pathophysiologically, thrombus formation on a bioprosthetic leaflet usually begins at regions of low flow or flow separation (e.g., near sewing ring, around stent posts) and can extend to the leaflet surface, forming mobile thrombi or layered thrombotic deposits. Antiplatelet therapy alone may inadequately prevent valve thrombosis in high-risk contexts. In contrast to SVD, thrombotic obstruction is primarily composed of platelets and fibrin and lacks the calcific or fibrotic architecture of structural deterioration.

Diagnostic differentiation relies on imaging. Transthoracic or transesophageal echocardiography can detect reduced leaflet motion and elevated transvalvular gradients; however, computing tomography (four-dimensional cardiac CT) is particularly useful for detecting hypoattenuated leaflet thickening (HALT) and reduced leaflet motion (RELM), both imaging hallmarks of subclinical or clinical BPVT. HALT appears as low-attenuation masses on the aortic-side of leaflets; RELM is quantified by measuring leaflet excursion compared with expected opening. A thrombotic process may show abrupt change in gradients over weeks to months and may reverse with therapeutic anticoagulation (typically warfarin with INR target 2–3, or direct oral anticoagulant in some contexts), with reduction of HALT on follow-up imaging and improvement in leaflet motion.

In contrast, SVD shows progressive calcification and thickening on imaging, often with nodular high-attenuation areas on CT consistent with calcium, and these changes do not regress with anticoagulation. Echocardiography may show increased echogenicity, restricted motion, or flail/tearing. Time course helps: thrombosis tends to have a more acute or subacute rise in gradients, whereas SVD typically evolves more slowly over years. Clinical context—recent valve implantation, new hypercoagulable state, discontinuation of anticoagulation—raises suspicion for BPVT.

Clinically important decision points include: when a patient with a bioprosthetic valve develops new gradients or symptoms, consider baseline imaging with echo; if leaflet thickening or reduced motion is present, obtain cardiac CT to assess HALT/RELM; if imaging suggests thrombus and clinical picture supports it, a trial of anticoagulation with close follow-up imaging is reasonable before committing to invasive therapy. If there is evidence of calcific change, leaflet tear, or pannus causing dysfunction, structural degeneration is likely and reintervention should be considered based on symptom status and severity.

# Impact of leaflet design (supra- vs intra-annular) on durability

Prosthetic valve design influences hemodynamics and potentially long-term durability. Two key positional concepts for circular stented bioprosthetic valves are supra-annular and intra-annular leaflet mounting. Intra-annular valves position the effective orifice largely within the native annulus; supra-annular valves seat the leaflets slightly above the annular plane, allowing placement of a larger effective orifice for a given annulus size.

Hemodynamically, supra-annular designs often confer a larger effective orifice area (EOA) and lower transvalvular gradients, which reduces residual stenosis and relieves LV afterload more effectively. Lower gradients reduce cyclic mechanical stress on the valve leaflets for a given cardiac output, which theoretically could benefit durability by decreasing the mechanical drivers of SVD. In addition, a larger EOA reduces the risk of prosthesis–patient mismatch (PPM), which itself is a contributor to higher stress and earlier deterioration.

However, leaflet design also affects leaflet curvature, stress distribution, and regions prone to flow stagnation—factors that influence both thrombogenicity and mechanical wear. Leaflets constructed from pericardium and mounted in a stent frame incur stress concentrations at commissures and attachment points. Stentless valves or valves with different anti-calcification treatments alter how forces are transmitted to the tissue. Supra-annular placement may expose leaflets to different flow patterns and shear stress in the aortic root that could influence pannus formation or local mechanical fatigue. Valve-in-valve transcatheter procedures further complicate these considerations because the initial valve frame and leaflet position determine the geometry and hemodynamic reserve for subsequent implants.

Therefore, while supra-annular designs frequently yield better immediate hemodynamics and may reduce mechanical loading that contributes to SVD, durability is multifactorial and depends on tissue treatment, host factors (age, comorbidities), implant technique, and hemodynamic environment. Clinical decision-making must balance immediate hemodynamic benefit against surgical considerations and long-term reintervention strategy, particularly in younger patients where longevity of the prosthesis is paramount.

# Role of infection (prosthetic valve endocarditis) in accelerated degeneration

Prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) is an infection of prosthetic material—including bioprosthetic leaflets, sewing rings, and adjacent tissue—that can rapidly and catastrophically accelerate valve dysfunction. The pathogenesis involves microbial colonization of prosthetic surfaces, formation of vegetations, and release of proteolytic enzymes and inflammatory mediators. Microorganisms commonly implicated include Staphylococcus aureus, coagulase-negative staphylococci, streptococci, and enterococci, but a broad range of organisms can cause PVE, including fungi in certain settings.

PVE can damage prosthetic valves through several mechanisms. Vegetations composed of organisms, fibrin, platelets, and inflammatory cells can physically impede leaflet motion, producing obstruction. Microbial proteases and the host immune response can degrade collagen and the structural proteins of the prosthetic tissue, leading to weakening, tearing, or perforation of leaflets and resultant regurgitation. Infection can also erode surrounding annular tissue, creating abscesses or fistulae that disrupt the anchoring of the valve and lead to paravalvular leaks and hemodynamic instability. Recurrent subclinical infection or low-grade colonization may promote chronic inflammation that accelerates pannus formation and fibrosis.

Clinically, PVE often presents with fever, systemic symptoms, new or changing murmurs, and signs of embolic phenomena. Diagnosis depends on the modified Duke criteria, which incorporate blood cultures and echocardiographic findings. For prosthetic valves, transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) is more sensitive than transthoracic imaging for detecting vegetations, perivalvular abscesses, valve dehiscence, or new regurgitation. Laboratory support includes multiple sets of blood cultures; typical inflammatory markers (elevated ESR, CRP) may be present but are nonspecific. ESR and CRP are often elevated in endocarditis, and persistently positive blood cultures or bacteremia with organisms known to seed prostheses should prompt evaluation for PVE.

PVE is a major clinical accelerator of prosthetic failure: it may necessitate urgent surgical or transcatheter re-replacement if there is uncontrolled infection, heart failure due to valve dysfunction, large vegetations at high risk of embolism, or prosthetic dehiscence. Even when infection is successfully treated, residual structural damage and fibrotic changes in the prosthetic tissue often render the valve dysfunctional over the medium term. Prevention strategies include perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis and adherence to endocarditis prophylaxis recommendations for high-risk procedures, but once an infection establishes on a prosthetic valve, eradication is challenging.

# Clinical and hemodynamic manifestations of valve failure (stenosis, regurgitation, mixed)

Valve failure from either native AS or SVD in bioprostheses manifests in characteristic clinical and hemodynamic patterns that guide diagnosis and management. The primary phenotypes are stenosis, regurgitation (insufficiency), or a mixed lesion.

Stenosis: In stenotic lesions, progressive narrowing of the effective orifice raises transvalvular gradients and imposes a pressure overload on the left ventricle. Patients may be asymptomatic for long periods due to compensatory concentric hypertrophy, after which symptoms develop: exertional dyspnea, angina (due to demand–supply mismatch in hypertrophied myocardium), syncope (often exertional, due to fixed cardiac output), and heart failure. Hemodynamically, echocardiography quantifies stenosis severity using peak aortic jet velocity, mean transvalvular gradient, and calculated aortic valve area (AVA) by the continuity equation. Common thresholds for native AS severity are: severe AS defined by AVA ≤ 1.0 cm² (or indexed AVA ≤ 0.6 cm²/m²), peak aortic jet velocity ≥ 4.0 m/s, or mean gradient ≥ 40 mmHg. But in prosthetic valves and in low-flow states, interpretation must consider flow dependence: low-flow, low-gradient severe AS may have AVA ≤ 1.0 cm² with mean gradient < 40 mmHg due to reduced stroke volume.

Regurgitation: Aortic regurgitation results from failure of leaflet coaptation because of tears, perforation, dehiscence, or retraction. Acute severe regurgitation (e.g., caused by leaflet tear or infective destruction) leads to a sudden volume load on the left ventricle; because there is no time for compensatory dilation, patients develop pulmonary edema, hypotension, and cardiogenic shock. Chronic regurgitation leads to eccentric left ventricular remodeling with volume overload: increased end-diastolic volume, progressive dilation, and eventual systolic dysfunction. Clinical signs include wide pulse pressure (in chronic disease), bounding pulses, and a high-pitched, decrescendo diastolic murmur best heard at the left sternal border. Echocardiographic assessment grades regurgitation by jet size, vena contracta, regurgitant volume and fraction, and impact on chamber size. Quantitative cutoffs commonly used: severe AR regurgitant volume ≥ 60 mL/beat, regurgitant fraction ≥ 50%, and effective regurgitant orifice area ≥ 0.3 cm².

Mixed lesions: Mixed aortic valve disease features both significant stenosis and regurgitation, as can occur with advanced SVD where calcification restricts opening while tears or incomplete coaptation cause backflow. Hemodynamic assessment becomes more complex because forward stroke volume and regurgitant fraction interact; clinical management prioritizes the dominant lesion and symptom burden while recognizing that mixed lesions often carry worse prognosis than isolated valve disease.

In prosthetic valves, hemodynamic assessment additionally must account for baseline prosthesis characteristics: normal prosthetic gradients are often higher than native valves, and normal leaflet motion may be limited compared with native cusps. Serial comparison with prior studies is important to detect meaningful change. Key clinical decision points include intervening when patients develop symptoms attributable to valve dysfunction, exhibit evidence of ventricular decompensation (left ventricular ejection fraction decline below normal thresholds), or demonstrate severe hemodynamic derangement. For native AS, the classic indications for aortic valve replacement include symptomatic severe AS, or asymptomatic severe AS with left ventricular ejection fraction < 50%, or with very severe hemodynamics or abnormal exercise testing. For prosthetic SVD, reintervention is indicated for severe prosthetic stenosis or regurgitation with symptoms or evidence of ventricular dysfunction, and modality (redo surgical replacement vs transcatheter valve-in-valve) depends on patient factors, anatomy, and institutional expertise.

Summary

Degenerative calcific aortic stenosis and bioprosthetic valve degeneration stem from overlapping biological and mechanical processes—endothelial injury, inflammation, osteogenic signaling, fibrotic remodeling, mechanical fatigue, and, when present, infection. Recognizing the distinctions between structural deterioration and potentially reversible processes such as thrombosis or infection informs diagnostic imaging strategies and therapeutic choices. Valve design influences hemodynamic performance and may modulate mechanical stress and longevity, but host factors and tissue treatment remain central determinants of durability. Clinically, valve failure presents as stenosis, regurgitation, or often a combination; timely identification of the severity and mechanism of dysfunction is essential to guide appropriate intervention and to optimize patient outcomes.
