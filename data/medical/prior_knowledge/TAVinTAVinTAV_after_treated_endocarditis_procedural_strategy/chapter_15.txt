Introduction

Coronary artery disease (CAD) and aortic stenosis frequently coexist due to shared risk factors and the ageing of the population. Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) has become an established therapy for severe aortic stenosis across a broad range of surgical risk profiles. For patients who have concomitant coronary disease, management must balance the safety and efficacy of revascularization with the technical and anatomic implications of prosthetic aortic valves for current and future coronary access. The interplay between percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) and TAVI spans preprocedural planning, procedural sequencing, prosthesis selection, intra-procedural techniques to preserve coronary access, and strategies to evaluate and treat ischemic symptoms after valve-in-valve (ViV) or redo TAVI procedures. This chapter integrates the pathophysiology, imaging assessment, device-related considerations, and clinical decision-making required to optimize outcomes in patients who require both valve replacement and coronary revascularization.

# Timing of PCI relative to TAVI (staged vs concurrent strategies)

Deciding when to perform PCI in patients who will undergo TAVI requires an individualized approach that weighs the urgency and anatomical complexity of coronary lesions, the symptomatic burden attributable to ischemia versus aortic stenosis, the risks of combined procedures, and the potential impact of antiplatelet therapy on periprocedural bleeding. The primary strategies are staged PCI before TAVI, concomitant (ad hoc) PCI at the time of TAVI, and staged PCI after TAVI. Each strategy has advantages and drawbacks.

Staged PCI before TAVI is often chosen when there are critical proximal coronary lesions (for example, left main stenosis or severe proximal left anterior descending disease) that are likely to cause hemodynamic instability during TAVI or when ischemia is clearly responsible for symptoms. Revascularizing significant lesions beforehand can reduce periprocedural myocardial infarction (MI) risk and improve hemodynamic reserve, which is especially relevant in balloon-expandable or self-expanding valve deployment where rapid hemodynamic changes may occur. However, performing PCI before TAVI introduces a delay in definitive valve therapy and exposes the patient to cumulative vascular and bleeding risks. Dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) after PCI can increase bleeding risk during TAVI if TAVI is performed while DAPT is in force; conversely, stopping DAPT early to proceed to TAVI increases stent thrombosis risk. For most contemporary bare metal or drug-eluting stents (DES), guidelines recommend at least 1 month of DAPT for high bleeding risk patients and 6 months for standard risk after elective PCI with DES, though shorter durations (1–3 months) are increasingly considered for high bleeding risk patients. Thus, the urgency of TAVI and the acceptable timing of DAPT interruption must be reconciled.

Concurrent PCI and TAVI reduces the number of vascular access episodes, shortens hospitalization, and allows for definitive management of both pathologies in a single session. This approach is attractive for patients with symptomatic CAD and severe aortic stenosis when the coronary lesions are amenable to PCI without prolonged procedural time or complex adjunctive techniques (for example, chronic total occlusion PCI or atherectomy). The primary disadvantage is that combined procedures increase contrast load, radiation exposure, and total anticoagulant/antiplatelet therapy complexity. Bleeding risk may be higher, especially in frail older patients. Clinically significant access site complications or periprocedural MI during a combined strategy can complicate the TAVI procedure.

Staged PCI after TAVI is chosen when coronary lesions are not critical, when symptoms are predominantly attributable to aortic stenosis, or when there is concern that valve anatomy may impede coronary access. TAVI modifies coronary hemodynamics and sometimes alleviates angina by improving left ventricular function and coronary perfusion. After TAVI, residual symptoms attributable to CAD can be reassessed; invasive physiologic testing with fractional flow reserve (FFR) or instantaneous wave-free ratio (iFR) may be more reliable once the valve gradient has been relieved. However, TAVI can make subsequent coronary access technically more difficult, particularly depending on valve design and commissural alignment. Therefore, preprocedural anticipation of coronary access difficulty should influence the decision toward earlier revascularization when lesions are anatomically significant.

Clinical decision-making balances ischemic risk and procedural safety. High-risk coronary anatomy (left main, proximal severe multivessel disease with left ventricular dysfunction, lesions supplying a large territory) generally favors revascularization before or at the time of TAVI. Isolated, stable, non-proximal lesions can be deferred and reassessed after TAVI. For patients with recent acute coronary syndromes, PCI should follow standard coronary care principles, with TAVI timing individualized according to hemodynamics and procedural risk.

# Impact of prosthesis design and commissural alignment on future coronary access

The design of a transcatheter heart valve (THV) has profound implications for coronary access after implantation. THV designs differ by frame material and geometry, leaflet position (intra-annular vs supra-annular), leaflet mounting (intra-frame vs external), stent height, cell size and shape, and radiopaque markers for commissural orientation. Self-expanding valves (e.g., nitinol frames) often have taller frames with denser cell structures compared with balloon-expandable valves (e.g., cobalt-chromium frames), which can affect the spatial relationship between the valve frame and coronary ostia. Valve-in-valve (ViV) procedures add further complexity because the leaflets and stent frame of the surgical bioprosthesis may be displaced outward, creating a barrier to coronary flow or access.

Commissural alignment refers to the rotational orientation of the THV relative to the native aortic valve commissures and the coronary ostia. Optimal commissural alignment positions the prosthetic commissures away from the coronary ostia, keeping the larger cells of the frame aligned with the coronaries and leaving clear pathways for catheter engagement. Conversely, commissural misalignment can position a prosthetic commissural post directly in front of a coronary ostium, making selective engagement challenging or impossible and increasing the risk of coronary obstruction during ViV procedures.

Prosthesis cell size and the location of sealing skirts or polyethylene terephthalate (PET) cuffs may block catheter passage. For example, some self-expanding supra-annular valves have closed-cell designs at certain levels, constraining the ability to pass catheters through the frame. Supra-annular leaflet位置 may also extend above the sinotubular junction, interfering with coronary ostia. The height of the stent relative to the sinuses of Valsalva and the sinotubular junction determines how much of the valve overlies the coronary ostia; low sinuses and high prosthesis stent positions elevate the risk of coronary occlusion and compromise access.

Radiopaque markers and fluoroscopic commissural alignment techniques can help during implantation, but even with best efforts, rotational variability and patient-specific anatomy make perfect alignment difficult. The importance clinically is that prosthesis choice should consider the need for future coronary access. For younger patients or those with significant coronary disease likely to require future interventions, selecting a valve with a favorable frame design, larger cell size at the coronary level, and predictable commissural orientation can preserve future PCI feasibility.

# Techniques to preserve or restore coronary access after valve implantation

Multiple procedural techniques are used to preserve coronary blood flow at the time of TAVI and to maintain or restore transthoracic coronary access for future interventions. These can be divided into preventive strategies applied during initial implantation and remedial techniques used when coronary flow or access is compromised.

Preventive strategies include careful valve sizing and implantation depth, commissural alignment techniques, and the use of prophylactic coronary wiring or balloon protection in high-risk cases. Valve sizing and implantation depth aim to avoid excessive supra-annular displacement of native leaflets that could obstruct coronary ostia. Implanting the valve as low as safely possible can reduce the degree of leaflet displacement into the sinuses. Commissural alignment involves pre-procedural identification of fluoroscopic markers on the THV and controlled rotational deployment so that the commissural posts avoid the coronary ostia. Some operators use orientation-control devices or manufacturers’ recommended cusp-overlap views to improve reliability of alignment.

When pre-procedural imaging indicates a high risk of obstruction (for example, low-lying coronary ostia, shallow sinuses, or bulky leaflets), techniques to actively protect the coronaries can be employed. One commonly used method is wiring the coronary artery with a guidewire and leaving a balloon or undeployed stent ready to be inflated or deployed rapidly (the "chimney" or "snorkel" technique) if obstruction occurs. The chimney stent creates a channel from the aorta into the coronary ostium, preserving flow even if the THV or displaced leaflets impede the native ostium. However, chimney stents have long-term issues including risk of stent deformation, thrombosis, and interference with future valve-in-valve procedures.

For ViV cases or when native leaflets are likely to be displaced outward, the BASILICA (Bioprosthetic or Native Aortic Scallop Intentional Laceration to prevent Iatrogenic Coronary Artery obstruction) technique can be used. BASILICA involves transcatheter electrosurgical laceration of the aortic leaflet that would otherwise obstruct a coronary ostium. By splitting the leaflet, the tissue can splay open during valve deployment, creating a path for coronary flow and preserving access. BASILICA is technically demanding and requires coordination between imaging, electrophysiology-style electrosurgery tools, and interventional skills, but has been demonstrated to reduce coronary obstruction risk in selected high-risk patients.

Post-implantation restorative techniques for coronary access include transcatheter retrieval or modification strategies when catheter engagement is blocked. Using high-support catheters, telescoping sheaths, or imaging guidance (rotational angiography, intravascular ultrasound [IVUS]) can sometimes allow crossing frame cells to reach the coronary ostium. In some cases, retrograde access via the radial or contralateral femoral approach with specific catheter shapes or use of mother-and-child catheter systems can facilitate engagement. If catastrophic coronary obstruction occurs and cannot be managed percutaneously, emergent surgical revascularization via coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) or urgent percutaneous coronary intervention with chimney stenting may be necessary.

Long-term preservation of coronary access can be optimized by pre-selecting THVs with designs more amenable to future PCI and by performing implantation techniques that minimize obstruction risk. Even with preventive measures, operators must be prepared for remedial interventions and plan for access strategies in the index procedure.

# Assessment of coronary anatomy in pre-TAVI CT

Multidetector computed tomography (CT) is a cornerstone of pre-TAVI assessment, providing high-resolution anatomic detail that informs prosthesis sizing, access planning, and coronary risk stratification. Dedicated pre-TAVI CT protocols include ECG-gated acquisitions during contrast administration to evaluate the aortic root, sinuses, sinotubular junction, coronary ostia heights, and the course of the coronary arteries. Assessment of coronary anatomy on pre-TAVI CT serves three purposes: estimating the risk of coronary obstruction at the time of TAVI or ViV, planning commissural alignment relative to the coronary ostia, and identifying significant coronary lesions or high-risk plaque characteristics that may prompt preprocedural or staged PCI.

Key measurements and anatomic features to report include coronary ostial heights (distance from the aortic annular plane to the coronary ostia), sinus of Valsalva dimensions (diameters and volumes), sinotubular junction height and diameter, leaflet morphology and calcification burden, and the spatial relationship between aortic valve leaflets and the coronary ostia. Coronary ostial heights less than approximately 10–12 mm are often considered low and increase the risk of obstruction, especially when sinuses are small. A small sinus of Valsalva (typically diameter <30 mm, though the threshold depends on valve and patient specifics) and a narrow sinotubular junction further elevate obstruction risk. The length and bulk of calcified native leaflets are also important: tall, bulky leaflets with heavy calcification are more likely to be displaced outward and obstruct the ostia.

Pre-TAVI CT can also visualize the coronary arteries for the presence of significant stenoses. Although CT angiography (CTA) may be limited by heavy coronary calcification in the elderly, it can identify proximal and mid-vessel lesions that are clinically significant. CTA may underestimate stenosis severity in heavily calcified vessels; therefore, invasive coronary angiography with physiologic assessment remains the standard when therapeutic decisions depend on lesion significance. Preprocedural CT also allows simulation of virtual transcatheter valve implantation, using software that models prosthesis frame position and predicts interactions with native anatomy. Virtual valve implantation can help anticipate whether leaflet displacement will reach the coronary ostia and can guide decisions such as performing BASILICA or prophylactic coronary protection.

When interpreting CT, the operator should consider dynamic factors: the aortic root dimensions change with cardiac cycle, and measurements at end-diastole versus systole can differ. Therefore, using an ECG-gated acquisition and reporting dimensions in standardized phases is important for reproducibility. Coronary dominance patterns and anomalous coronary origins should be noted because they affect risk: an anomalous or low-origin right coronary artery, for instance, may be particularly vulnerable.

# Management of ischemic symptoms after ViV and redo TAVI

Ischemic symptoms after valve-in-valve or redo TAVI present a complex diagnostic and therapeutic challenge. Pathophysiologically, ischemia may arise from progression or unrecognized CAD, prosthesis-related coronary obstruction or flow limitation, altered coronary hemodynamics after valve implantation, or technical issues impairing access and hence timely revascularization. The approach to evaluation and management must integrate clinical assessment, noninvasive testing, invasive coronary angiography, and consideration of prosthesis anatomy.

First, clinical assessment should characterize ischemic symptoms, their timing relative to the procedure, associated heart failure signs, and any biomarker elevation. Troponin elevations are common after TAVI and must be interpreted in context; a rising or markedly elevated troponin with new wall motion abnormalities suggests procedural MI. When patients present later with exertional angina or unexplained dyspnea, distinguishing ischemia from heart failure from prosthetic dysfunction is essential.

Noninvasive testing may include stress imaging (stress echocardiography, nuclear perfusion imaging, or stress cardiac MRI) to localize ischemia. However, the presence of prosthetic material and chronic changes can confound some modalities. In many cases, invasive coronary angiography remains indispensable to define coronary anatomy and to permit physiologic assessment. When angiography is planned, the operator should be prepared for potentially difficult coronary engagement due to the implanted valve(s), particularly after ViV or when a supra-annular THV with high stent frame overlies the sinuses. Preprocedural CT review, knowledge of the valve type and orientation, and use of tailored catheter shapes increase the likelihood of successful selective engagement.

If obstructive coronary disease is found and selective engagement is achievable, standard PCI techniques apply, with attention to contrast load and renal protection in older patients. Physiologic testing with FFR or iFR can guide revascularization decisions for intermediate lesions. If selective engagement is not possible due to prosthesis obstruction, alternative strategies include non-selective angiography through the valve cells to estimate coronary flow, use of coronary guidewires advanced across frame cells under imaging guidance, or use of mother-and-child catheters and extension devices to reach the ostium. In some cases, non-selective coronary injection combined with pressure wire assessment can provide functional information.

When ischemia results from prosthesis-related coronary obstruction (for example, after ViV when displaced surgical bioprosthetic leaflets occlude an ostium), urgent remedial strategies include chimney stenting, BASILICA-like leaflet laceration if feasible, and in extreme situations, emergent surgical revascularization. Chimney stenting can restore flow but may complicate future ViV procedures and carries risks of stent deformation or late thrombosis. If structural causes are not remediable percutaneously and symptoms are severe, surgical options including reoperation and CABG can be considered, taking into account patient comorbidity and operative risk.

Longer term, clinicians must plan for future access and surveillance. For patients with ViV, monitoring for progressive coronary compromise, valve degeneration, or in-stent restenosis is important. Antiplatelet and anticoagulation strategies should balance thrombotic and bleeding risks, particularly when coronary stents and prosthetic valves coexist. Decisions regarding duration of DAPT after PCI in patients with prosthetic valves should adhere to guideline recommendations while accounting for surgical risk, recent bleeding events, and the complexity of coronary stenting.

Conclusion

Managing CAD in patients undergoing structural aortic valve interventions demands an integrated strategy that begins with meticulous preprocedural imaging and planning and extends through device selection, procedural technique, and postprocedural surveillance. Timing of PCI relative to TAVI must be individualized based on coronary lesion severity, ischemic risk, and anatomical factors that affect future coronary access. Prosthesis design and commissural alignment have major implications for coronary engagement, and techniques such as BASILICA, coronary wiring, and chimney stenting play roles in preventing or treating coronary obstruction. Pre-TAVI CT provides essential measurements and simulation capabilities that guide risk stratification and procedural planning. When ischemic symptoms occur after ViV or redo TAVI, a systematic evaluation combining clinical assessment, noninvasive testing, and invasive angiography, informed by knowledge of valve anatomy, is necessary to determine the appropriate remedial approach. Anticipating future coronary needs when choosing valves and planning procedures remains a cornerstone of care for patients with both significant CAD and aortic valve disease.
