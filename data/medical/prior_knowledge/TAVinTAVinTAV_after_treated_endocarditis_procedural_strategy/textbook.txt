Introduction

A precise understanding of the anatomy of the aortic valve complex and aortic root is essential for clinicians involved in the diagnosis and management of aortic valve disease and for planning and performing valve interventions, including surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR) and transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR). The aortic valve is not an isolated structure; it is integrated with the left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT), the aortic root (including the sinuses of Valsalva and the sinotubular junction), and the proximal ascending aorta. The spatial relationships among the valve cusps and commissures, the coronary ostia, and the surrounding fibrous and muscular structures determine hemodynamics, vulnerability to disease processes (degeneration, calcification, infection), and procedural risks such as coronary obstruction or conduction disturbances during valve implantation. This chapter reviews the detailed anatomy of the aortic valve complex and aortic root, highlights normal dimensional ranges and diagnostic criteria relevant to imaging and intervention planning, and discusses anatomical variants that influence the approach to transcatheter and surgical procedures.

# Aortic valve cusps, commissures and annulus structure

The normal aortic valve is most commonly tricuspid, composed of three semilunar cusps — the right coronary cusp (RCC), left coronary cusp (LCC), and noncoronary cusp (NCC) — that coapt centrally during diastole. Each cusp attaches at its base to the aortic wall along a narrow fibrous ridge and at its free edge to adjacent cusps via commissures. The commissures are the points of apposition between cusps and correspond to the interleaflet fibrous triangles. Microscopically, cusps consist of three layers: an intimal layer (fibrosa at the aortic side), a spongiosa (middle), and a ventricularis (on the ventricular side). The fibrosa confers tensile strength, the spongiosa provides shock absorption, and the ventricularis supplies elastic recoil. The hinge line of each cusp curves into the root and marks the transition between the mobile leaflet and the relatively immobile aortic wall.

The aortic annulus is a complex three-dimensional structure rather than a simple ring. For imaging and procedural purposes, several anatomical and virtual definitions are used. The anatomical annulus is the basal attachment of the leaflets where they meet the ventricular myocardium and fibrous tissue, though this region is not planar. Interventional cardiology commonly uses the "virtual basal ring" defined on imaging as the plane passing through the nadirs of the three leaflet attachments; this plane is used for sizing transcatheter valves. The annulus is often elliptical rather than circular; therefore, reliance on a single diameter can be misleading. Cross-sectional area and perimeter measurements derived from computed tomography (CT) or three-dimensional transesophageal echocardiography (3D-TEE) more accurately represent the annular geometry. When approximating annular area as an ellipse, the area is given by $A=\pi a b$, where $a$ and $b$ are the semi-major and semi-minor axes.

Histologically and functionally, the annulus transitions circumferentially from muscular LVOT tissue (below the cusps) to fibrous continuity between the aortic and mitral valves (the aortomitral curtain) anteromedially. The annulus serves as the interface for suture placement during SAVR and as the anchoring zone (or seal) for transcatheter valves. Annular calcification commonly accumulates along the leaflet hinge lines and can be eccentric, affecting prosthesis seating and paravalvular leak risk.

# Aortic root components: sinuses of Valsalva, sinotubular junction, ascending aorta

The aortic root is a distinct segment of the proximal aorta extending from the annular plane to the sinotubular junction (STJ). Its principal components are the sinuses of Valsalva, the interleaflet sinuses where the cusps bulge outward; the STJ, the external transition between root and tubular ascending aorta; and the coronary ostia that typically arise from the sinuses. The sinuses have a critical role in normal valve function: they create flow vortices during systole that facilitate valve opening and, during early diastole, support gentle closure and coronary blood flow.

Each sinus is named for the coronary artery that arises from it (right and left) or for being noncoronary. The sinus dimensions are not uniform; the maximal sinus diameter is often greater than the annular diameter, and the height of the sinus from the annulus to the STJ (sinus height) contributes to the space available for coronary flow and for prosthetic leaflets after valve replacement. The sinuses are bounded externally by the aortic media and internally by the cuspal tissue.

The sinotubular junction is the level where the sinuses converge into the smooth tubular ascending aorta. Its diameter is functionally important because the STJ defines the geometric boundary that constrains the commissures and determines leaflet coaptation height. A dilated STJ can lead to central regurgitation due to commissural separation and leaflet tethering, whereas a narrow STJ can restrict leaflet motion. The ascending aorta immediately above the STJ is the region most often affected by aneurysmal dilation in conditions such as bicuspid aortopathy or connective tissue disorders.

For imaging and procedural planning, quantitative measurements are taken at several levels. Typical adult values (approximate and patient-dependent) include an annular mean diameter of roughly 20–30 mm, sinus diameters that may be 5–10 mm larger than the annulus in normal adults, and STJ diameters varying with age and body size. Precise thresholds for clinical decisions depend on indexed values and institutional practice; for example, concomitant aortic replacement during SAVR is often considered when ascending aortic diameter exceeds 45–55 mm depending on concomitant pathology and patient factors.

# Coronary ostia anatomy and variability (height, orientation, takeoff)

The coronary ostia almost always arise from the sinuses of Valsalva, with the right coronary artery (RCA) arising from the right coronary sinus and the left main coronary artery (LMCA) from the left coronary sinus. The noncoronary sinus lacks a coronary ostium in normal anatomy. The ostial takeoff height is measured from the virtual basal ring (annular plane) to the lowest point of the coronary ostium. Typical ostial heights vary but commonly range from approximately 8 mm to 15 mm. Considerations for procedural risk depend on ostial height: coronary obstruction after TAVR is more likely when the coronary ostium height is low (commonly cited risk when height <10–12 mm) and when sinus dimensions are small and cannot accommodate displaced native cuspal tissue or prosthetic frames.

Orientation and angulation of the ostia also vary. The ostia can be eccentrically located within the sinus (anterior, posterior, high, or low), and the takeoff may be tangential rather than perpendicular to the aortic wall. The coronary artery’s initial course relative to the STJ and ascending aorta also matters; short proximal coronary length between the ostium and the first major branch (e.g., short left main) leaves less margin for obstruction. Anomalous coronary origins (e.g., high takeoff above the STJ or origin from the opposite sinus) alter hemodynamics and procedural planning.

Imaging assessment with CT angiography is used to measure ostial height, sinus dimensions, and the spatial relation of the ostium to potential prosthetic frames. Specific procedural thresholds used in planning include ostial height and sinus width, because obstruction risk is a function of the space available for bulky native leaflets or calcific nodules to be displaced after valve implantation. A combined scenario of ostial height less than approximately 10–12 mm and sinus diameter <30–32 mm markedly increases the possibility of coronary obstruction in some prostheses, although device design and individual patient anatomy modify risk.

# Left ventricular outflow tract anatomy and dimensions

The LVOT is the region of the left ventricle leading to the aortic annulus and is anatomically and functionally distinct from the mitral apparatus and the aortic root. The LVOT comprises the muscular interventricular septum anteriorly and the fibrous continuity of the aortic-mitral curtain medially and posteriorly. Its shape tapers from the basal left ventricle to the annular plane and is frequently somewhat elliptical in cross section. The LVOT is the anchoring substrate for the ventricular side of valve sutures and, in TAVR, for some valves that expand below the annulus.

Normal LVOT dimensions are influenced by body size and sex. Typical LVOT diameter measured by echocardiography at the insertion of the aortic valve is about 18–22 mm in adult females and 20–26 mm in adult males, but these numbers vary. Measurement technique matters: 2D echocardiography often measures the anteroposterior dimension in a single plane, whereas 3D imaging and CT can reveal noncircular geometry. The LVOT area is frequently calculated using the circular assumption $A=\pi r^2$ in echocardiography, which overestimates or underestimates flow when the LVOT is elliptical; thus, 3D measurements are preferred when precise flow calculations and annular sizing are important.

The LVOT is closely related to the conduction system: the membranous septum lies near the posteroinferior commissure and gives rise to the atrioventricular conduction bundle. Procedures that impinge on the LVOT or extend into the membranous septum can create conduction abnormalities, including new bundle branch block or higher-degree atrioventricular block requiring permanent pacing. The risk is increased with deep valve implantation or with bulky calcification extending into the LVOT.

# Spatial relationships between native valve, prosthetic frames and coronaries

The implantation of a prosthetic valve — surgical or transcatheter — alters the native three-dimensional relationships within the aortic root. In SAVR, the native leaflets are usually excised, and a sewing ring is sutured to the annulus with direct visualization of coronary ostia preserved. In TAVR, the native leaflets are typically left in situ and are displaced outward between the prosthetic frame and the aortic wall. This displacement can create two major clinical concerns: coronary obstruction and sinus sequestration.

Coronary obstruction occurs when displaced native leaflets, bulky or protruding calcifications, or parts of the prosthetic frame cover the coronary ostium. The risk of obstruction depends on the ostial height, the sinus depth, the amount and distribution of cusp calcification, and the prosthesis design. Self-expanding and balloon-expandable devices differ in their frame height and cell geometry, which affects the pattern of coronary access and the likelihood of obstruction. When the prosthetic frame or displaced leaflets physically extend above the level of the coronary ostium—or when they seal the sinus against the aortic wall such that coronary flow cannot enter the sinus—myocardial ischemia and hemodynamic collapse may follow.

Sinus sequestration is a related concept whereby the prosthesis and displaced valve leaflets create a sealed chamber (the sinus) whose ostium to the aortic lumen is obstructed at the STJ level, effectively isolating the sinus and coronary ostia from normal perfusion pathways. This can occur when the STJ is narrow and the prosthetic frame or native leaflet touches the STJ circumferentially, creating a sealed pocket. This is of particular concern in valve-in-valve procedures (implanting a transcatheter valve within a surgical bioprosthesis) where the surgical valve frame may alter the relation of the sinuses and STJ.

Furthermore, prosthetic frames can interfere with future coronary access. High frame heights, dense strut patterns, and supra-annular valve positions may complicate catheter engagement of the coronary ostia for angiography or percutaneous coronary intervention. Pre-procedural CT helps anticipate these issues by mapping commissural alignment, leaflet bulk, and the relationship between the expected prosthesis landing zone and coronary ostia.

# Anatomical variants relevant to transcatheter interventions (calcification distribution, bicuspid anatomy)

Anatomical variants markedly influence the planning and outcomes of valve procedures. Two categories deserve special emphasis: calcification distribution and bicuspid aortic valve (BAV) anatomy.

Calcification distribution in the valve complex varies from focal nodular deposits at the cusp tips to extensive leaflet and annular calcification that extends into the LVOT and interleaflet triangles. Asymmetric, bulky calcification, for example concentrated at the commissures or on a single cusp, can cause eccentric valve orifice, unequal expansion of transcatheter frames, paravalvular leak, or valve malposition. Heavily calcified leaflets can also impinge on coronary ostia upon displacement. Annular or subannular calcification increases the risk of annular rupture during balloon expansion, especially with stiff, noncompliant calcific rings. CT characterization of calcium burden, density, and distribution is therefore a routine part of pre-TAVR assessment, and procedural strategies (pre-dilation, post-dilation, choice of prosthesis type and size) are tailored accordingly.

Bicuspid aortic valves are the most common congenital valvular anomaly and present particular challenges. BAVs are heterogeneous; the Sievers classification describes them by the number of raphes (fusion lines): Type 0 (no raphe, truly bicuspid), Type 1 (one raphe, most common), and Type 2 (two raphes, rare). The fused cusps and the presence of a raphe produce an eccentric, often elliptical annulus and asymmetric sinuses. BAVs are frequently associated with aortopathy — dilation of the ascending aorta — and with heavy, asymmetric calcification concentrated at the raphe. These features complicate valve sizing and expansion, increase the risk of paravalvular leak, and may predispose to device malalignment or embolization. In TAVR candidates with BAV, specific device-selection considerations, higher rates of pacemaker implantation, and potentially different procedural techniques (e.g., more liberal use of balloon pre- or post-dilation) are often necessary.

Other anatomical variants of clinical importance include high or anomalous coronary takeoff, small sinuses, horizontal aortas (aortic root angulation relative to the LVOT), and presence of prior surgical grafts or prostheses. Also relevant are variations in the membranous septum length (shorter membranous septum confers higher risk of conduction disturbance), and differences in commissural orientation which affect planned commissural alignment of transcatheter valves.

Conclusion and clinical decision points

A robust understanding of the aortic valve complex and aortic root anatomy — including the three-dimensional shapes and dimensions of the annulus and sinuses, the heights and orientations of the coronary ostia, LVOT geometry, and the implications of calcification and congenital variants — underpins safe and effective valve intervention. Key practical measurements used in procedural planning include the annular perimeter and area (derived from CT or 3D imaging), annular diameters in orthogonal planes, sinus diameters and heights, STJ diameter, and coronary ostial heights from the annular plane. When evaluating risk for coronary obstruction during TAVR, particular attention should be paid to coronary ostial height (<10–12 mm often raises concern), limited sinus width (commonly <30–32 mm), bulky leaflet calcification, and short distance from the annulus to the STJ. LVOT morphology and the proximity of calcification to the membranous septum should be considered with respect to conduction system injury risk.

Imaging modalities complement anatomic knowledge: high-resolution contrast-enhanced CT provides the most comprehensive reproducible measurements for annular sizing, sinus and STJ assessment, and coronary ostial mapping. Three-dimensional echocardiography is valuable intra-procedurally and when CT is contraindicated. Integration of detailed anatomic assessment with device-specific design features guides prosthesis selection, sizing, and procedural strategy to minimize complications and optimize outcomes.

Introduction

The aortic valve (AV) occupies a central role in left ventricular (LV) afterload and systemic circulation. Its function is to allow unimpeded ejection of blood during systole and to prevent regurgitation in diastole. The hemodynamic effects of the valve derive from the interplay between valve geometry, transvalvular flow, and the pressure–volume properties of the ventricle and arterial tree. Understanding the physics of flow through an orifice, how velocities relate to pressure gradients, and how we quantify valve function using Doppler echocardiography and invasive catheter techniques is essential for accurate diagnosis and appropriate clinical decision-making. This chapter reviews the principles of normal and abnormal aortic valve hemodynamics and explains the commonly used Doppler-echocardiographic and catheter-based parameters for quantifying valve function.

# Flow dynamics across the aortic valve and pressure–flow relationships

Flow through the aortic valve is characterized by acceleration of blood from the LV outflow tract (LVOT) to a higher velocity jet through the valve orifice into the ascending aorta. The relationship between flow (volume per time, $Q$), velocity ($v$), and cross-sectional area ($A$) follows the continuity principle: $Q = A \cdot v$. When the effective orifice area is reduced, maintaining the same stroke volume requires higher velocities across the valve. The pressure–flow relationship across a valve depends nonlinearly on velocity because kinetic energy increases with the square of velocity.

In terms of energetics, the LV converts contractile work into mechanical energy delivered to the blood as kinetic energy and pressure energy. Across a constriction (the valve), some of the LV pressure is dissipated as kinetic energy in the jet and as turbulent energy losses downstream. The conversion between pressure and velocity is described by the Bernoulli relationship, with subsequent loss terms. In simplified form used clinically, the pressure drop due to a localized high-velocity jet is approximated as proportional to the square of the jet velocity: $\Delta P \approx 4 v^2$ (using velocity in m/s and pressure in mmHg). This "simplified Bernoulli" equation is the basis for rapid estimation of instantaneous pressure gradients from Doppler velocities.

The pressure gradient across the valve therefore depends on both the flow rate and the valve opening. For a fixed effective orifice area (EOA), increases in flow produce a steep rise in peak velocity and in pressure drop because $\Delta P$ scales approximately with $v^2$ and $v = Q / A$. Conversely, for a fixed flow, a smaller EOA produces higher velocities and gradients. However, other physiologic factors modulate gradients: stroke volume, heart rate (which affects flow per beat), arterial impedance, systemic arterial compliance, and proximal velocities in the LVOT. The temporal pattern of flow during systole also matters—short, high-flow bursts generate different gradients compared with prolonged lower-flow ejections even when total stroke volume is similar.

Finally, pressure recovery must be recognized. Downstream of the valve jet, some kinetic energy is converted back into pressure as the jet decelerates within the aorta. The amount of pressure recovered depends on the relation between the vena contracta size (EOA) and the aortic cross-sectional area; it is greater when the aorta is small relative to the EOA. Because Doppler measures velocities at the vena contracta and yields the peak instantaneous gradient, while catheter measurements of pressure across the valve can be made further downstream after some pressure recovery has occurred, Doppler-derived gradients are typically higher than catheter-derived gradients in situations where significant pressure recovery exists.

# Transvalvular gradients and their determinants

Transvalvular pressure gradients are a central metric in assessing aortic stenosis (AS). Two clinically reported gradients are commonly used: peak instantaneous gradient (the maximal difference between LV and aortic instantaneous pressures during systole) and mean gradient (the time-averaged instantaneous gradient over the ejection period). Clinically, Doppler echocardiography reports peak velocity and computes instantaneous peak gradient using the simplified Bernoulli equation and calculates mean gradient by integrating the instantaneous gradient over systole (commonly from the velocity-time integral, VTI).

Determinants of transvalvular gradients include:

- Effective orifice area (EOA): Smaller EOA leads to higher velocity and hence higher gradients for a given flow.
- Transvalvular flow (stroke volume and ejection profile): Higher flow produces higher gradients even with constant EOA. Low-flow states reduce gradients and can mask severe stenosis.
- LV systolic function and contractility: Reduced contractile force may produce lower stroke volumes and velocities, yielding low gradients despite severe anatomical narrowing (low-flow, low-gradient AS).
- Heart rate and systolic ejection period: For a given cardiac output, tachycardia shortens systolic ejection time and can alter peak and mean gradient relationships.
- Systemic arterial pressure and vascular compliance: High arterial impedance increases LV systolic pressure; but gradient across the valve reflects pressure difference between LV and aorta—if aortic pressure is elevated, LV pressure must exceed it, and gradients may change with systemic load.
- Proximal velocities (LVOT velocity): The corrected Bernoulli equation accounts for proximal velocity: $\Delta P = 4 (v_{\text{AV}}^2 - v_{\text{LVOT}}^2)$. Usually $v_{\text{LVOT}}$ is small (<1.0 m/s) so the second term is often neglected.
- Pressure recovery: If a portion of kinetic energy is reconverted to pressure within the aorta, the net pressure loss between LV and more distal aortic measurement is less than the local instantaneous gradient measured at the vena contracta.

Severity thresholds commonly used in clinical practice for aortic stenosis are: peak aortic jet velocity $\ge 4.0$ m/s (corresponding to peak instantaneous gradient $\ge 64$ mmHg using $\Delta P = 4v^2$) and mean gradient $\ge 40$ mmHg as cutoffs suggesting severe AS. These must always be interpreted in the context of flow: high-gradient AS with preserved flow is straightforward, but low-flow situations require different considerations.

# Effective orifice area (EOA) and indexed EOA

The effective orifice area (EOA) represents the functional orifice through which blood flows; it is distinct from the anatomic geometric orifice area and is usually smaller due to flow contraction. EOA is the critical physiologic parameter because it governs the relationship between flow and velocity (and hence gradients). EOA is usually measured by echocardiography using the continuity equation (see next section). For native aortic valves, an EOA ≤ 1.0 cm^2 is typically taken as a criterion for severe AS. For valves with small body surface area (BSA), indexing the EOA to patient size improves assessment, because a given absolute area has different hemodynamic consequences in small vs large individuals.

Indexed EOA (EOA_i) is defined as:
EOA_i = EOA / BSA,
with BSA in m^2 and EOA in cm^2. Accepted thresholds for prosthetic valve sizing and for prosthesis–patient mismatch (see later) use indexed EOA. Typical cutoffs used in guideline and surgical literature for prosthetic valves are:
- Severe prosthesis–patient mismatch: EOA_i ≤ 0.65 cm^2/m^2 (for obese patients the threshold is often reduced to ≤ 0.60 cm^2/m^2).
- Moderate PPM: EOA_i 0.65–0.85 cm^2/m^2 (or 0.60–0.85 cm^2/m^2 in obese).

For native valve AS, an EOA_i ≤ 0.6 cm^2/m^2 often parallels the absolute criterion of EOA ≤ 1.0 cm^2 in average-sized adults. Normal aortic valve area is commonly > 2.0 cm^2, with a peak average EOA around 3 cm^2 in young healthy adults, though reference values vary with body size and age.

EOA is a physiologic parameter and is flow-dependent: it can increase with higher transvalvular flow because the leaflets or prosthetic leaflets can open more widely with greater stroke volume. Therefore, EOA measured under low-flow conditions may underestimate the functional opening seen during higher output states.

# Continuity equation and its application to AVA calculation

The continuity equation is founded on mass conservation: the volume of blood passing through the LVOT per beat equals the volume passing through the aortic valve during the same ejection period (neglecting regurgitant volumes). Clinically, this principle is used to calculate aortic valve area (AVA) from measured LVOT dimensions and Doppler-derived velocity-time integrals (VTIs).

The typical echocardiographic continuity equation is written as:
AVA = (CSA_{LVOT} \cdot VTI_{LVOT}) / VTI_{AV}
where CSA_{LVOT} is the cross-sectional area of the LVOT, calculated from the LVOT diameter (D_{LVOT}) as
CSA_{LVOT} = \pi \cdot (D_{LVOT} / 2)^2 = 0.7854 \cdot D_{LVOT}^2,
VTI_{LVOT} is the pulsed-wave Doppler velocity-time integral measured in the LVOT (sampled just proximal to the valve, typically in the apical 5-chamber or long-axis view), and VTI_{AV} is the continuous-wave Doppler VTI across the aortic valve jet (traced from all available acoustic windows to capture the highest velocity).

Key practical points and pitfalls:
- Accurate measurement of LVOT diameter is critical because CSA scales with the square of diameter; a 10% error in diameter produces ~21% error in area. LVOT diameter should be measured in mid-systole at the level of the annulus in the parasternal long-axis view, inner edge to inner edge.
- The LVOT is often not perfectly circular; using a single diameter assumes circular geometry and can introduce systematic error. CT or 3D echo can provide better LVOT area when needed.
- VTI_{LVOT} must be obtained with PW Doppler and the sample volume placed about 0.5–1.0 cm proximal to the leaflet insertion region (carefully avoiding contamination by high-velocity flow near the leaflets).
- VTI_{AV} requires excellent spectral envelope definition via continuous-wave Doppler, with proper Doppler alignment (angle error reduces measured velocity and causes underestimation of gradient and EOA).
- The continuity equation generally provides physiologic AVA (the EOA) and is preferred over anatomic planimetry when there is heavy calcification or when planimetry is technically difficult. Planimetry measures geometric orifice area (anatomic area) and may diverge from functional EOA.

Interpretation: For native valves, AVA ≤ 1.0 cm^2 indicates severe AS, 1.0–1.5 cm^2 is moderate, and >1.5 cm^2 is generally mild or normal, though interpretation must incorporate flow status and gradients. For prosthetic valves, the same equation applies but measurement of LVOT diameter may be more complicated depending on valve type and acoustic shadowing.

# Doppler velocity index (DVI) and interpretation

The Doppler velocity index (DVI), also called the dimensionless index, is an alternative to the continuity equation that avoids reliance on LVOT diameter measurement and thus is less sensitive to errors from LVOT sizing. DVI is defined as the ratio of velocities (or VTIs) proximal and distal to the valve. Two equivalent formulations are commonly used:

DVI = VTI_{LVOT} / VTI_{AV}

or, when using peak instantaneous velocities,

DVI = v_{LVOT} / v_{AV}.

Because the LVOT area cancels in the continuity equation, DVI directly reflects the ratio of flow velocity in the LVOT to the velocity across the valve and hence is a surrogate for valve narrowing.

Interpretation thresholds for DVI in native aortic stenosis are:
- DVI ≤ 0.25: consistent with severe AS.
- DVI 0.25–0.50: moderate AS.
- DVI > 0.50: mild or no significant AS.

DVI is particularly useful in prosthetic valves and in technically difficult studies where LVOT diameter measurement is unreliable. However, DVI is still flow-dependent: low-flow states will raise the LVOT-to-valve VTI ratio and can obscure severity assessment. DVI is insensitive to pressure recovery because both numerator and denominator are derived from velocity measurements at the same cardiac beat.

Methodologic considerations:
- Ensure the LVOT VTI is measured with PW Doppler and that the LVOT sampling site is appropriately located and aligned.
- For peak-DVI (using peak velocities) angle alignment and spectral quality are crucial.
- When using VTI-based DVI values, use the integrated VTI values traced over the full systolic envelope for both LVOT and AV.

DVI can complement AVA from continuity equation: if AVA by continuity and DVI provide concordant severity assessments, confidence is higher; discordant results warrant further evaluation for low-flow states, measurement error, or presence of prosthesis-related phenomena.

# Impact of prosthesis design and position on hemodynamics

Prosthetic valve hemodynamics vary substantially with valve design, size, and position relative to the annulus. The principal prosthesis categories are: stented surgical prostheses (bileaflet or bioprosthetic with sewing ring), stentless surgical prostheses, and transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) devices. Other design differences include intra-annular versus supra-annular leaflet position, stented frame geometry, external sewing rings, and leaflet thickness.

Key effects of prosthesis design on hemodynamics:

- Effective orifice area by prosthesis size: For any given labeled prosthesis size, stentless and supra-annular designs usually provide larger EOAs than intra-annular stented designs because leaflet orifice is positioned more upstream and available orifice is less constrained by the sewing ring or stent posts. TAVR devices with supra-annular leaflet function often demonstrate larger EOAs compared with similarly labeled surgical valve sizes.

- Gradient and EOA relation: For a given EOA, gradients follow the same pressure–flow relationship as native valves; however, some prostheses have characteristic higher residual gradients at rest. Small prostheses (e.g., 19–21 mm labels) often produce higher gradients in larger patients, predisposing to prosthesis–patient mismatch.

- Flow patterns and turbulence: The design of the prosthesis influences jet eccentricity and downstream flow turbulence. Constrictions and asymmetry can increase energy loss and affect pressure recovery. Certain stented valves can generate eccentric jets that impact gradients and Doppler alignment challenges.

- Positioning relative to the annulus: Supra-annular positioning typically yields a larger effective flow area for a given annular diameter, reducing gradients. Conversely, intra-annular valves reside within the annulus and may limit EOA relative to the geometric annulus.

- Patient anatomy–valve interplay: A small aortic root or ascending aorta can exacerbate pressure recovery when EOA is relatively large compared with the aortic area; conversely, a large aorta reduces pressure recovery. Similarly, prosthesis stent posts or skirt material may interact with surrounding calcification or asymmetric annular shape to alter opening dynamics.

- Prosthetic degeneration and stenosis: Over time, bioprosthetic valves may undergo structural valve degeneration (SVD) with leaflet calcification and reduced EOA, producing stenosis and increased gradients. Mechanical valves may develop pannus growth around sewing rings restricting leaflet motion.

Clinical implications: Selection of valve type and size must balance thromboembolic and durability considerations against hemodynamic performance. Recognizing typical gradient profiles and EOA expectations for each prosthesis type helps interpret postoperative Doppler findings and identify abnormal valve function or mismatch.

# Concepts of prosthesis–patient mismatch (PPM)

Prosthesis–patient mismatch (PPM) occurs when the EOA of the implanted prosthetic valve, after implantation, is too small in relation to the patient’s body size, resulting in abnormally high gradients for that patient. PPM is defined physiologically—by the indexed effective orifice area (EOA_i)—rather than by the labeled valve size alone, since two patients with the same prosthesis can have differing hemodynamic consequences based on BSA and hemodynamic demands.

Classification thresholds commonly used are:
- Severe PPM: EOA_i ≤ 0.65 cm^2/m^2 (or ≤ 0.60 cm^2/m^2 for obese patients).
- Moderate PPM: EOA_i 0.65–0.85 cm^2/m^2 (0.60–0.85 cm^2/m^2 in obese patients).
- No/minimal PPM: EOA_i > 0.85 cm^2/m^2.

Clinical significance: PPM can have important clinical consequences. Severe PPM is associated with higher residual gradients, less regression of LV hypertrophy after valve replacement, higher incidence of heart failure symptoms, and in some studies, worse long-term survival. The adverse effects are more pronounced in patients with reduced LV function or those with higher systemic demands.

Determinants and prevention:
- Patient BSA relative to annular size: Large-bodied patients with small annuli are at highest risk.
- Prosthesis selection: Using prostheses with supra-annular leaflet position or stentless designs tends to reduce PPM risk due to larger EOAs.
- Surgical strategies: Root enlargement procedures (e.g., Nicks, Manouguian) allow implantation of a larger prosthesis and reduce PPM risk. In TAVR vs surgical valve options, TAVR devices with supra-annular function may achieve better EOAs in small annuli.
- Predicting PPM: Preoperative echocardiographic or CT measurements of annulus and calculation of expected EOA_i for candidate prostheses based on manufacturer EOA charts help guide prosthesis choice. However, manufacturer EOA tables may not always predict in vivo EOA accurately; published in vivo series provide better estimates.

Diagnosis and post-operative assessment:
- Postoperative echocardiography measures EOA by continuity equation and indexes to BSA to determine PPM severity. Persistent elevated gradients should prompt evaluation for PPM versus prosthetic obstruction (panus, thrombus) or patient-prosthesis malposition.
- Energy loss coefficient (ELC) may be used to account for pressure recovery and provide a more physiologic assessment of valve performance. The ELC is calculated as:
ELC = (EOA \cdot A_a) / (A_a - EOA)
where $A_a$ is the cross-sectional area of the ascending aorta at the level where pressure recovery occurs. The ELC indexed to BSA can reduce the apparent severity of PPM in small aortas where pressure recovery is significant.

# Additional practical considerations: low-flow states, pressure recovery, catheter vs Doppler measurements, and Gorlin concept

Low-flow states and discrepant classifications:
One of the most challenging clinical scenarios in AV hemodynamics is when AVA and gradients are discordant—e.g., AVA ≤ 1.0 cm^2 but mean gradient < 40 mmHg. Such situations occur in low-flow, low-gradient severe AS and require differentiation between truly severe AS and pseudosevere AS (where the valve opening is limited by low flow rather than intrinsic severity). There are two major low-flow contexts: classical low-flow low-gradient (reduced LVEF, stroke volume index ≤ 35 mL/m^2) and paradoxical low-flow low-gradient (preserved LVEF but small stroke volume index due to concentric remodeling and small cavity). Dobutamine stress echocardiography can help distinguish flow-dependent (pseudosevere) from fixed severe stenosis by increasing flow and observing EOA and gradient behavior. AVA that increases substantially with higher flow suggests pseudosevere disease, whereas limited increase with rising gradient is consistent with true severe AS.

Pressure recovery:
As noted earlier, pressure recovery results in a partial reconversion of kinetic energy to pressure distal to the vena contracta. The consequence is that the maximal instantaneous gradient measured by Doppler (at the vena contracta) is higher than the net pressure loss measured further downstream (e.g., by catheter if measured in the ascending aorta at some distance). The magnitude of pressure recovery depends on the ratio of EOA to the aortic cross-sectional area and can be clinically significant in small aortas and in prosthetic valves with relatively large EOAs.

Catheter-based measurements and the Gorlin equation:
Invasive measurement of transvalvular pressure gradient is performed with simultaneous LV and aortic pressure recordings and provides direct pressure differences without reliance on Doppler. Historically, the Gorlin formula was used to calculate valve area from cardiac output and mean transvalvular pressure gradient measured during catheterization. The Gorlin equation conceptualizes valve orifice flow as analogous to flow through an orifice with an empirical coefficient that accounts for contraction and loss:
AVA_{Gorlin} = \frac{CO}{K \cdot HR \cdot SEP \cdot \sqrt{\Delta P_{mean}}}
where $CO$ is cardiac output (mL/s or L/min), $HR$ is heart rate, $SEP$ is systolic ejection period, $\Delta P_{mean}$ is mean transvalvular pressure gradient, and $K$ is an empirical constant (dependent on units and conversion factors). The Gorlin formula depends on accurate measurement of cardiac output and assumes steady conditions through the ejection period. Limitations include sensitivity to flow and the fact that the Gorlin constant was empirically derived, so different measurement techniques and hemodynamic states can produce variability. Consequently, the continuity equation by Doppler is now the standard noninvasive method in routine practice, and catheter-based AVA calculation is used selectively, with recognition of its limitations.

Summary and clinical decision points

- AV hemodynamics are governed by continuity (flow–area–velocity relationship) and by nonlinear pressure–velocity relationships (Bernoulli), with important modulation by flow rate, valve geometry, and vascular factors.
- Doppler echocardiography provides noninvasive estimation of instantaneous peak gradient ($\Delta P \approx 4 v^2$), mean gradient (from VTI integration), AVA by continuity equation, and DVI as a diameter-independent index.
- Severity criteria for native AS commonly include AVA ≤ 1.0 cm^2, mean gradient ≥ 40 mmHg, and peak velocity ≥ 4.0 m/s, but always interpret in the context of flow. Low-flow states require special diagnostic strategies (stress echo, careful measurement).
- Accurate measurement technique is critical: LVOT diameter measurement, precise PW and CW Doppler sampling and alignment, and careful tracing of VTIs are the foundation of reliable AV hemodynamic assessment.
- Prosthesis design and position (supra- vs intra-annular, stented vs stentless, TAVR vs surgical) significantly affect EOA and gradients; prosthesis–patient mismatch is diagnosed by indexed EOA and has important clinical consequences.
- Pressure recovery and the differences between Doppler-derived gradients and catheter pressures should be recognized, particularly in small aortas or after implantation of large relative-EOA prostheses.
- When Doppler and clinical findings are discordant, consider measurement error, low-flow states, PPM, valve degeneration, or obstructive processes such as pannus or thrombus; invasive hemodynamics and advanced imaging (CT, 3D echo) may be necessary for clarification.

Normal reference and commonly used thresholds (summary)
- Normal AVA: > 2.0 cm^2 (typical adult values vary by size and age).
- Severe AS: AVA ≤ 1.0 cm^2; mean gradient ≥ 40 mmHg; peak velocity ≥ 4.0 m/s.
- Indexed EOA severe PPM (prosthesis): EOA_i ≤ 0.65 cm^2/m^2 (≤ 0.60 in obese).
- Stroke volume index used to define low-flow: ≤ 35 mL/m^2.
- DVI thresholds: ≤ 0.25 (severe), 0.25–0.50 (moderate), >0.50 (mild/normal).
- Simplified Bernoulli: $\Delta P \approx 4 v^2$ (mmHg for $v$ in m/s).

Mastering these principles enables the clinician to interpret Doppler and invasive findings correctly, discriminate true severe obstruction from flow-dependent phenomena, and make informed choices about timing and type of intervention for patients with aortic valve disease.

Introduction

Aortic valve disease imposes a progressive burden on the left ventricle and systemic circulation that culminates in significant morbidity and mortality if untreated. Two clinical contexts dominate: native degenerative (calcific) aortic stenosis (AS) and dysfunction of implanted aortic bioprosthetic valves. Although one is a native disease of aging valve tissue and the other a consequence of implanting xenograft or homograft tissue, they share common biological processes—chronic inflammation, lipid infiltration, active osteogenic signaling, and mechanical stress—that ultimately produce leaflet stiffening, calcification, fibrosis, tearing, and functional obstruction or incompetence. Understanding the pathophysiology that leads to native AS and structural valve deterioration (SVD) in bioprostheses is essential for clinical reasoning around diagnosis, monitoring, prevention, and management. This chapter reviews the mechanisms of degenerative aortic stenosis, the structural pathways of bioprosthetic valve deterioration, how to differentiate thrombosis from structural degeneration, the impact of valve leaflet design on durability, the role of infection in accelerating valve destruction, and the clinical and hemodynamic manifestations of valve failure.

# Degenerative (calcific) aortic stenosis mechanisms

Degenerative, or calcific, aortic stenosis is not a passive “wear-and-tear” phenomenon but an active, regulated disease process that resembles atherosclerosis and ectopic bone formation. The earliest events include endothelial injury at the aortic side of the valve cusps, frequently in areas of altered shear stress. This endothelial perturbation allows infiltration of lipids, inflammatory cells (macrophages, T lymphocytes), and oxidized low-density lipoprotein (oxLDL) into the valvular interstitium.

Once within the leaflet, these components stimulate an inflammatory milieu. Valve interstitial cells (VICs), normally quiescent fibroblast-like cells, undergo phenotypic changes toward myofibroblast and osteoblast-like cells under the influence of cytokines (e.g., interleukin-1, tumor necrosis factor-alpha), transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β), and bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs). These osteogenic signals upregulate transcription factors such as Runx2, promoting deposition of a bone-like extracellular matrix and mineralization. Calcific nodules form within the leaflet matrix, around cellular debris and lipid cores; microcalcifications can coalesce into larger sheets that restrict cusp motion.

Mechanical stress and repetitive flexion also drive disease progression. Regions of high tensile and shear stress (the leaflet commissures and the tip regions) are predisposed to microinjury, increasing permeability and perpetuating the inflammatory and osteogenic cascade. Matrix metalloproteinases and other proteases remodel the extracellular matrix, enabling further calcification.

Systemic risk factors overlap with atherosclerotic disease: age, male sex, smoking, hyperlipidemia, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and chronic kidney disease (especially with disordered mineral metabolism) increase the risk and accelerate progression. However, the efficacy of systemic lipid-lowering therapy for halting established calcific AS has been disappointing in randomized trials, indicating that once osteogenic processes and calcification are established, they are poorly reversible.

Hemodynamically, progressive leaflet stiffening and reduced opening area cause increased transvalvular gradients and impulse load on the left ventricle. The left ventricle adapts with concentric hypertrophy to preserve systolic wall stress and maintain forward flow, but chronic pressure overload leads to increased myocardial oxygen consumption, diastolic dysfunction, myocardial fibrosis, and eventually systolic dysfunction if untreated.

# Structural valve deterioration (SVD): leaflet calcification, fibrosis, tearing and pannus

Structural valve deterioration (SVD) of bioprosthetic valves refers to intrinsic changes of the prosthetic tissue that compromise function, distinguishable from patient-prosthesis mismatch, paravalvular leak, or endocarditis. Bioprosthetic valves are most commonly made from porcine aortic valves or bovine pericardium subjected to chemical fixation (typically glutaraldehyde), which stabilizes collagen and reduces immunogenicity but leaves the tissue metabolically inert and prone to long-term degeneration.

The major morphologic pathways of SVD include leaflet calcification, progressive fibrosis and stiffening, mechanical tearing, and pannus formation. Leaflet calcification mirrors native calcific AS in many respects: passive deposition of calcium salts onto damaged collagen matrices and active cellular processes when residual viable cells or host inflammatory elements promote mineralization. Calcification predominantly affects regions of high mechanical stress and hinge points, translating clinically into restricted leaflet excursion and stenosis.

Fibrosis of the leaflet tissue can occur as a response to chronic mechanical stress, immune-mediated reactions to residual antigens in the xenograft, or low-grade inflammation. Fibrotic thickening reduces leaflet compliance, causing stenosis or contributing to coaptation failure in the case of irregular leaflet geometry. Mechanical tearing is often precipitated by chronic cyclical loading, localized calcific nodules producing stress concentration, or mismatch between the prosthesis and native annulus; tearing more commonly produces abrupt new regurgitation if it affects coaptation edges.

Pannus is an organized fibrous tissue ingrowth from the sewing ring or host annular tissue onto the prosthetic leaflets or hinge mechanisms. Unlike calcific deposits, pannus is composed of host fibrovascular tissue and tends to develop more slowly; it may restrict leaflet motion (producing stenosis) or interfere with leaflet coaptation. Risk factors for pannus include neo-intimal proliferation related to surgical trauma, persistent mechanical irritation at the sewing ring interface, and in some cases, an exaggerated fibroproliferative host response. Pannus is often dense and may be relatively insensitive to anticoagulation or anti-inflammatory measures.

Over time, these structural changes can produce isolated stenosis (reduced effective orifice area), isolated regurgitation (leaflet tear, loss of coaptation), or mixed lesions. The timeline of SVD is variable: older-generation bioprostheses frequently demonstrate clinically relevant SVD within 10–15 years, whereas contemporary treatments, improved tissue processing, and anti-calcification treatments have extended durability in many series. Nonetheless, younger patient age, higher metabolic activity, abnormal hemodynamic stress (e.g., prosthesis–patient mismatch, high cardiac output states), and immunologic factors accelerate deterioration.

# Bioprosthetic valve thrombosis vs structural degeneration

Distinguishing bioprosthetic valve thrombosis (BPVT) from structural degeneration is critical because their management differs: BPVT often responds to anticoagulation, while SVD may require reintervention. Thrombosis can produce both obstructive (reduced leaflet motion causing stenosis) and non-obstructive (reduced leaflet motion without significant gradient change) presentations. BPVT often occurs relatively early after implantation but can also present years later, particularly in the context of subtherapeutic anticoagulation, atrial fibrillation, low cardiac output states, or hypercoagulable conditions.

Pathophysiologically, thrombus formation on a bioprosthetic leaflet usually begins at regions of low flow or flow separation (e.g., near sewing ring, around stent posts) and can extend to the leaflet surface, forming mobile thrombi or layered thrombotic deposits. Antiplatelet therapy alone may inadequately prevent valve thrombosis in high-risk contexts. In contrast to SVD, thrombotic obstruction is primarily composed of platelets and fibrin and lacks the calcific or fibrotic architecture of structural deterioration.

Diagnostic differentiation relies on imaging. Transthoracic or transesophageal echocardiography can detect reduced leaflet motion and elevated transvalvular gradients; however, computing tomography (four-dimensional cardiac CT) is particularly useful for detecting hypoattenuated leaflet thickening (HALT) and reduced leaflet motion (RELM), both imaging hallmarks of subclinical or clinical BPVT. HALT appears as low-attenuation masses on the aortic-side of leaflets; RELM is quantified by measuring leaflet excursion compared with expected opening. A thrombotic process may show abrupt change in gradients over weeks to months and may reverse with therapeutic anticoagulation (typically warfarin with INR target 2–3, or direct oral anticoagulant in some contexts), with reduction of HALT on follow-up imaging and improvement in leaflet motion.

In contrast, SVD shows progressive calcification and thickening on imaging, often with nodular high-attenuation areas on CT consistent with calcium, and these changes do not regress with anticoagulation. Echocardiography may show increased echogenicity, restricted motion, or flail/tearing. Time course helps: thrombosis tends to have a more acute or subacute rise in gradients, whereas SVD typically evolves more slowly over years. Clinical context—recent valve implantation, new hypercoagulable state, discontinuation of anticoagulation—raises suspicion for BPVT.

Clinically important decision points include: when a patient with a bioprosthetic valve develops new gradients or symptoms, consider baseline imaging with echo; if leaflet thickening or reduced motion is present, obtain cardiac CT to assess HALT/RELM; if imaging suggests thrombus and clinical picture supports it, a trial of anticoagulation with close follow-up imaging is reasonable before committing to invasive therapy. If there is evidence of calcific change, leaflet tear, or pannus causing dysfunction, structural degeneration is likely and reintervention should be considered based on symptom status and severity.

# Impact of leaflet design (supra- vs intra-annular) on durability

Prosthetic valve design influences hemodynamics and potentially long-term durability. Two key positional concepts for circular stented bioprosthetic valves are supra-annular and intra-annular leaflet mounting. Intra-annular valves position the effective orifice largely within the native annulus; supra-annular valves seat the leaflets slightly above the annular plane, allowing placement of a larger effective orifice for a given annulus size.

Hemodynamically, supra-annular designs often confer a larger effective orifice area (EOA) and lower transvalvular gradients, which reduces residual stenosis and relieves LV afterload more effectively. Lower gradients reduce cyclic mechanical stress on the valve leaflets for a given cardiac output, which theoretically could benefit durability by decreasing the mechanical drivers of SVD. In addition, a larger EOA reduces the risk of prosthesis–patient mismatch (PPM), which itself is a contributor to higher stress and earlier deterioration.

However, leaflet design also affects leaflet curvature, stress distribution, and regions prone to flow stagnation—factors that influence both thrombogenicity and mechanical wear. Leaflets constructed from pericardium and mounted in a stent frame incur stress concentrations at commissures and attachment points. Stentless valves or valves with different anti-calcification treatments alter how forces are transmitted to the tissue. Supra-annular placement may expose leaflets to different flow patterns and shear stress in the aortic root that could influence pannus formation or local mechanical fatigue. Valve-in-valve transcatheter procedures further complicate these considerations because the initial valve frame and leaflet position determine the geometry and hemodynamic reserve for subsequent implants.

Therefore, while supra-annular designs frequently yield better immediate hemodynamics and may reduce mechanical loading that contributes to SVD, durability is multifactorial and depends on tissue treatment, host factors (age, comorbidities), implant technique, and hemodynamic environment. Clinical decision-making must balance immediate hemodynamic benefit against surgical considerations and long-term reintervention strategy, particularly in younger patients where longevity of the prosthesis is paramount.

# Role of infection (prosthetic valve endocarditis) in accelerated degeneration

Prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) is an infection of prosthetic material—including bioprosthetic leaflets, sewing rings, and adjacent tissue—that can rapidly and catastrophically accelerate valve dysfunction. The pathogenesis involves microbial colonization of prosthetic surfaces, formation of vegetations, and release of proteolytic enzymes and inflammatory mediators. Microorganisms commonly implicated include Staphylococcus aureus, coagulase-negative staphylococci, streptococci, and enterococci, but a broad range of organisms can cause PVE, including fungi in certain settings.

PVE can damage prosthetic valves through several mechanisms. Vegetations composed of organisms, fibrin, platelets, and inflammatory cells can physically impede leaflet motion, producing obstruction. Microbial proteases and the host immune response can degrade collagen and the structural proteins of the prosthetic tissue, leading to weakening, tearing, or perforation of leaflets and resultant regurgitation. Infection can also erode surrounding annular tissue, creating abscesses or fistulae that disrupt the anchoring of the valve and lead to paravalvular leaks and hemodynamic instability. Recurrent subclinical infection or low-grade colonization may promote chronic inflammation that accelerates pannus formation and fibrosis.

Clinically, PVE often presents with fever, systemic symptoms, new or changing murmurs, and signs of embolic phenomena. Diagnosis depends on the modified Duke criteria, which incorporate blood cultures and echocardiographic findings. For prosthetic valves, transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) is more sensitive than transthoracic imaging for detecting vegetations, perivalvular abscesses, valve dehiscence, or new regurgitation. Laboratory support includes multiple sets of blood cultures; typical inflammatory markers (elevated ESR, CRP) may be present but are nonspecific. ESR and CRP are often elevated in endocarditis, and persistently positive blood cultures or bacteremia with organisms known to seed prostheses should prompt evaluation for PVE.

PVE is a major clinical accelerator of prosthetic failure: it may necessitate urgent surgical or transcatheter re-replacement if there is uncontrolled infection, heart failure due to valve dysfunction, large vegetations at high risk of embolism, or prosthetic dehiscence. Even when infection is successfully treated, residual structural damage and fibrotic changes in the prosthetic tissue often render the valve dysfunctional over the medium term. Prevention strategies include perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis and adherence to endocarditis prophylaxis recommendations for high-risk procedures, but once an infection establishes on a prosthetic valve, eradication is challenging.

# Clinical and hemodynamic manifestations of valve failure (stenosis, regurgitation, mixed)

Valve failure from either native AS or SVD in bioprostheses manifests in characteristic clinical and hemodynamic patterns that guide diagnosis and management. The primary phenotypes are stenosis, regurgitation (insufficiency), or a mixed lesion.

Stenosis: In stenotic lesions, progressive narrowing of the effective orifice raises transvalvular gradients and imposes a pressure overload on the left ventricle. Patients may be asymptomatic for long periods due to compensatory concentric hypertrophy, after which symptoms develop: exertional dyspnea, angina (due to demand–supply mismatch in hypertrophied myocardium), syncope (often exertional, due to fixed cardiac output), and heart failure. Hemodynamically, echocardiography quantifies stenosis severity using peak aortic jet velocity, mean transvalvular gradient, and calculated aortic valve area (AVA) by the continuity equation. Common thresholds for native AS severity are: severe AS defined by AVA ≤ 1.0 cm² (or indexed AVA ≤ 0.6 cm²/m²), peak aortic jet velocity ≥ 4.0 m/s, or mean gradient ≥ 40 mmHg. But in prosthetic valves and in low-flow states, interpretation must consider flow dependence: low-flow, low-gradient severe AS may have AVA ≤ 1.0 cm² with mean gradient < 40 mmHg due to reduced stroke volume.

Regurgitation: Aortic regurgitation results from failure of leaflet coaptation because of tears, perforation, dehiscence, or retraction. Acute severe regurgitation (e.g., caused by leaflet tear or infective destruction) leads to a sudden volume load on the left ventricle; because there is no time for compensatory dilation, patients develop pulmonary edema, hypotension, and cardiogenic shock. Chronic regurgitation leads to eccentric left ventricular remodeling with volume overload: increased end-diastolic volume, progressive dilation, and eventual systolic dysfunction. Clinical signs include wide pulse pressure (in chronic disease), bounding pulses, and a high-pitched, decrescendo diastolic murmur best heard at the left sternal border. Echocardiographic assessment grades regurgitation by jet size, vena contracta, regurgitant volume and fraction, and impact on chamber size. Quantitative cutoffs commonly used: severe AR regurgitant volume ≥ 60 mL/beat, regurgitant fraction ≥ 50%, and effective regurgitant orifice area ≥ 0.3 cm².

Mixed lesions: Mixed aortic valve disease features both significant stenosis and regurgitation, as can occur with advanced SVD where calcification restricts opening while tears or incomplete coaptation cause backflow. Hemodynamic assessment becomes more complex because forward stroke volume and regurgitant fraction interact; clinical management prioritizes the dominant lesion and symptom burden while recognizing that mixed lesions often carry worse prognosis than isolated valve disease.

In prosthetic valves, hemodynamic assessment additionally must account for baseline prosthesis characteristics: normal prosthetic gradients are often higher than native valves, and normal leaflet motion may be limited compared with native cusps. Serial comparison with prior studies is important to detect meaningful change. Key clinical decision points include intervening when patients develop symptoms attributable to valve dysfunction, exhibit evidence of ventricular decompensation (left ventricular ejection fraction decline below normal thresholds), or demonstrate severe hemodynamic derangement. For native AS, the classic indications for aortic valve replacement include symptomatic severe AS, or asymptomatic severe AS with left ventricular ejection fraction < 50%, or with very severe hemodynamics or abnormal exercise testing. For prosthetic SVD, reintervention is indicated for severe prosthetic stenosis or regurgitation with symptoms or evidence of ventricular dysfunction, and modality (redo surgical replacement vs transcatheter valve-in-valve) depends on patient factors, anatomy, and institutional expertise.

Summary

Degenerative calcific aortic stenosis and bioprosthetic valve degeneration stem from overlapping biological and mechanical processes—endothelial injury, inflammation, osteogenic signaling, fibrotic remodeling, mechanical fatigue, and, when present, infection. Recognizing the distinctions between structural deterioration and potentially reversible processes such as thrombosis or infection informs diagnostic imaging strategies and therapeutic choices. Valve design influences hemodynamic performance and may modulate mechanical stress and longevity, but host factors and tissue treatment remain central determinants of durability. Clinically, valve failure presents as stenosis, regurgitation, or often a combination; timely identification of the severity and mechanism of dysfunction is essential to guide appropriate intervention and to optimize patient outcomes.

# Introduction

Infective endocarditis (IE) is an infection of the endocardial surface of the heart, most commonly involving cardiac valves. It remains a high-morbidity and high-mortality condition that requires integration of microbiology, pathophysiology, diagnostic imaging, and coordinated medical and surgical management. Prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) — infection of a prosthetic valvular device — presents distinct diagnostic and therapeutic challenges because of biofilm formation, atypical presentations, and higher rates of complications and mortality compared with native valve endocarditis (NVE). This chapter provides a detailed, clinically oriented overview of the microbiology, mechanisms of vegetation formation, clinical manifestations, diagnostic approach, antimicrobial therapy (with emphasis on enterococcal disease), indications and timing for surgery in PVE, and principles of long-term management, relapse risk, and suppressive therapy.

# Microbiology: common pathogens and their clinical implications

The microbiology of IE is dominated by a relatively small group of organisms, with Staphylococcus, Streptococcus, and Enterococcus species accounting for the majority of cases. The distribution differs between native and prosthetic valve infections, early (within 60 days of valve implantation) and late PVE, and depending on healthcare exposure and patient comorbidities.

Staphylococcus spp.
- Staphylococcus aureus is now the single most common cause of acute IE in many settings. It produces aggressive valve destruction, systemic emboli, metastatic infection (e.g., vertebral osteomyelitis, pulmonary septic emboli in right-sided disease), and high early mortality. Both methicillin-susceptible S. aureus (MSSA) and methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) cause IE; MRSA is associated with worse outcomes and limits β-lactam therapy. S. aureus readily adheres to both native and prosthetic material; hematogenous seeding after bacteremia is common.
- Coagulase-negative staphylococci (CoNS), primarily Staphylococcus epidermidis, are important causes of PVE, particularly early PVE associated with perioperative contamination and later PVE associated with biofilm on prosthetic material. CoNS usually produce indolent infections but are adept at forming biofilm, contributing to antibiotic tolerance and relapse risk.

Streptococcus spp.
- Viridans group streptococci (e.g., Streptococcus sanguinis, S. mitis, S. oralis) are classic causes of subacute NVE, historically linked to dental sources. They often produce large vegetations and are generally susceptible to β-lactams; penicillin susceptibility guides therapy.
- Streptococcus gallolyticus (formerly S. bovis) bacteremia/IE is associated with colonic pathology, including neoplasia.
- Streptococci of the anginosus group and group B streptococci can also cause IE, and Streptococcus pneumoniae can cause rapidly progressive disease particularly in the setting of pneumonia with bacteremia.

Enterococcus spp.
- Enterococcus faecalis and E. faecium are common causes of IE, particularly in older adults, in those with genitourinary or gastrointestinal manipulations, and in nosocomial settings. Enterococci are intrinsically less virulent than S. aureus but more difficult to treat because of intrinsic and acquired resistance (e.g., to aminoglycosides, β-lactams, and vancomycin). E. faecalis is more antibiotic-susceptible than E. faecium; the choice of regimen depends on susceptibility testing (e.g., vancomycin-resistant enterococci, VRE).

Other pathogens
- HACEK organisms (Haemophilus spp., Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans, Cardiobacterium hominis, Eikenella corrodens, Kingella kingae) are fastidious Gram-negative rods and cause subacute IE, often requiring prolonged incubation of blood cultures and sometimes specialized media.
- Fungi (Candida spp., Aspergillus spp.) cause IE, particularly in severely immunocompromised patients, intravenous drug users, or prosthetic devices; fungal PVE often requires combined medical and surgical therapy.
- Culture-negative endocarditis may reflect prior antibiotics, fastidious organisms (e.g., Bartonella, Coxiella burnetii, Tropheryma whipplei), or noninfective etiologies.

Clinical implications
- Organism identity dictates urgency, likelihood of complications, choice of antibiotic(s), duration of therapy, need for combination therapy (e.g., synergistic aminoglycoside use), and surgical decision-making.
- S. aureus and fungal infections generally require urgent, often surgical, intervention due to aggressive tissue destruction.
- Enterococcal infections require prolonged, often combination regimens and have higher relapse rates; susceptibility testing is essential.
- The presence of prosthetic material raises concern for biofilm-associated tolerance and frequent need for valve explantation.

# Pathogenesis of endocardial infection and vegetation formation

Infective endocarditis arises from the confluence of endothelial injury, bacteremia, and the capacity of organisms to adhere and proliferate on endocardial surfaces. The classic sequence involves three interrelated processes: (1) formation of a sterile nonbacterial thrombotic endocarditis (NBTE) lesion, (2) seeding of that lesion during transient or persistent bacteremia, and (3) microbial proliferation with vegetation maturation.

Endothelial injury and NBTE
- Turbulent blood flow across valves (e.g., from preexisting valvular disease such as rheumatic mitral disease, calcific aortic stenosis, or congenital abnormalities) or direct damage to prosthetic material exposes subendothelial extracellular matrix and initiates platelet and fibrin deposition. These sterile thrombotic deposits — NBTE — provide a scaffold for bacterial attachment.
- In prosthetic valves, the sewing ring and nonendothelialized surfaces are inherently thrombogenic and facilitate early deposition; microscopically irregular surfaces of bioprosthetic or mechanical devices favor adherence.

Bacteremia and adherence
- Microorganisms enter the bloodstream from a variety of foci: skin and soft tissue (S. aureus), oral cavity (viridans streptococci), genitourinary or gastrointestinal tract (enterococci), vascular catheters, or intraoperative contamination (PVE). Transient bacteremia during dental work or mucosal manipulation can seed NBTE lesions.
- Adherence mechanisms vary by organism: microbial surface components recognizing adhesive matrix molecules (MSCRAMMs) in staphylococci and streptococci bind fibrinogen, fibronectin, and collagen exposed on damaged tissue. Enterococci use aggregation substance, adhesins, and pili for attachment.

Vegetation maturation and biofilm
- Once bacteria adhere, they stimulate ongoing deposition of platelets and fibrin, forming vegetations that are relatively avascular. Vegetation architecture provides immune evasion; neutrophil access and antibiotic penetration are limited, particularly deep within vegetations.
- Many organisms form biofilm — a multicellular community encased in extracellular polymeric substances — especially on prosthetic material. Biofilm confers high tolerance to antibiotics and host defenses, necessitating prolonged therapy and often surgical removal of infected prosthetic devices.

Local and systemic effects
- Vegetations can cause local tissue destruction through proteolytic enzyme release and direct extension, leading to valvular perforation, cusp rupture, annular abscess, fistula formation, and conduction system involvement (e.g., heart block from perivalvular abscess).
- Embolization of vegetative fragments causes infarction or seeding to distant sites: brain (stroke, mycotic aneurysm), spleen, kidney, lungs (in right-sided IE), and musculoskeletal system.
- Host inflammatory and immune complex phenomena (e.g., glomerulonephritis, Osler nodes, Roth spots) reflect both direct infection and immune-mediated injury.

Temporal considerations in PVE
- Early PVE (commonly defined as within 60 days of valve insertion) often reflects perioperative contamination with skin flora or intraoperative seeding and tends to present more acutely. Late PVE (>60 days) is more similar to NVE in microbiology and presentation but remains complicated by biofilm on prosthetic material.

# Clinical features and complications of IE and PVE

Clinical presentation
- IE has a wide clinical spectrum, ranging from acute, fulminant illness (e.g., S. aureus bacteremia with sudden fevers, sepsis, acute regurgitation) to indolent, subacute presentations (e.g., viridans streptococci with prolonged low-grade fever, malaise, weight loss).
- Classic peripheral stigmata (that may occur but are not universally present) include Janeway lesions (painless erythematous macules on palms/soles from septic emboli), Osler nodes (painful subcutaneous nodules typically on fingers and toes reflecting immune complexes), splinter hemorrhages, and Roth spots (retinal hemorrhages with white centers). These signs are less frequently seen in contemporary practice but are still diagnostically suggestive when present.
- Symptoms frequently include fever, night sweats, anorexia, weight loss, new or changing heart murmur, and signs of heart failure. Embolic phenomena produce focal symptoms depending on organ involvement (e.g., stroke, flank pain from renal infarct).

Complications specific to IE and PVE
- Valvular destruction and acute regurgitation: Rapid destruction, particularly with S. aureus or virulent organisms, can produce acute severe regurgitation and congestive heart failure requiring urgent surgery.
- Perivalvular extension: Abscess formation, pseudoaneurysm, fistulae, and dehiscence of prosthetic valves are more frequent in PVE and with highly invasive organisms. Periannular abscesses can cause conduction disturbances including high-grade atrioventricular block.
- Embolic events: Risk factors for embolism include large mobile vegetations (>10 mm), vegetations on the mitral valve, and S. aureus infection. Cerebral emboli may result in ischemic stroke, intracerebral hemorrhage (especially following anticoagulation or mycotic aneurysm rupture), or mycotic aneurysm with delayed hemorrhage.
- Sepsis and metastatic infection: Persistent bacteremia can seed other sites (vertebral osteomyelitis, septic arthritis, end-organ abscess) and cause septic shock.
- Infective complications with prosthetic material: PVE carries higher rates of prosthetic dehiscence, paravalvular leak, and need for reoperation. Biofilm formation predisposes to recurrent or relapsing infection despite apparently adequate therapy.

Prognostic factors
- Poor prognostic features include S. aureus infection, fungal etiology, prosthetic valve involvement (especially early PVE), presence of heart failure, perivalvular extension, persistent bacteremia, large vegetations with embolic events, and delayed diagnosis.
- Early recognition and targeted therapy, with timely surgical consultation, are essential to mitigate these complications.

# Diagnostic criteria: blood cultures, echocardiography (TTE/TEE) and ancillary imaging

Diagnosis of IE relies on integrating clinical, microbiological, and echocardiographic data. The modified Duke criteria offer a standardized framework that categorizes cases as definite, possible, or rejected based on major and minor criteria. Familiarity with these criteria and their limitations — particularly in PVE — is important.

Modified Duke major criteria (summary)
- Positive blood cultures for typical IE organisms from two separate blood cultures: examples include viridans streptococci, S. aureus, enterococci (in the absence of a primary focus), or persistently positive blood cultures (e.g., two or more positive cultures drawn >12 hours apart or all of three or the majority of four or more separate cultures).
- Evidence of endocardial involvement on echocardiography: vegetations, abscess, new partial dehiscence of a prosthetic valve, or new valvular regurgitation.

Minor criteria include predisposing heart condition or intravenous drug use, fever ≥38.0°C, vascular phenomena (arterial emboli, septic pulmonary infarcts, mycotic aneurysm), immunologic phenomena (Osler nodes, Roth spots, glomerulonephritis), microbiological evidence not meeting major criteria, and echocardiographic findings consistent but not meeting major criteria.

Blood cultures
- Obtain multiple sets of blood cultures prior to initiating antibiotics whenever possible. Recommended practice is at least three sets (each set being an aerobic and anaerobic bottle) drawn from separate venipuncture sites over the initial several hours, although two well-timed sets may be acceptable in certain situations.
- Typical incubation times are 5 days for most organisms, but HACEK organisms may require prolonged incubation up to 14 days or molecular diagnostics if cultures are negative.
- Persistently positive cultures despite appropriate therapy suggest uncontrolled infection, perivalvular extension, or an infected prosthetic device.
- Interpret culture results in clinical context: single positive cultures with common skin contaminants (e.g., coagulase-negative staphylococci) may represent contamination unless repeated or associated with clinical evidence of IE, particularly in prosthetic valve patients where CoNS can be pathogenic.

Echocardiography: TTE vs TEE
- Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) is noninvasive and is the initial imaging modality for suspected IE. Its sensitivity for vegetations ranges from 50% to 70% depending on body habitus, vegetation size, and valve of interest; specificity is high.
- Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) has greater sensitivity and resolution, exceeding 90% for detecting vegetations, perivalvular abscesses, prosthetic valve dehiscence, and small lesions. TEE is the preferred test for suspected PVE and for evaluation when TTE is nondiagnostic but clinical suspicion remains high.
- In patients with prosthetic valves, TEE is essential because acoustic shadowing and prosthetic material limit TTE sensitivity. TEE should be performed early in all suspected PVE cases.

Ancillary imaging
- Cardiac CT can complement echocardiography, providing detailed assessment of perivalvular anatomy, abscesses, pseudoaneurysms, and prosthetic dehiscence. It is useful for surgical planning.
- 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography combined with CT (FDG-PET/CT) has emerging utility in PVE and in prosthetic device infections. FDG-PET/CT can detect increased metabolic activity around prosthetic valves suggestive of infection and has been incorporated into modified diagnostic algorithms for prosthetic valve infection, particularly when echocardiography is equivocal. FDG-PET/CT improves sensitivity for PVE, especially late PVE, but false positives can occur in recent surgery or inflammatory states.
- Cardiac MRI is less commonly used but may help delineate complications in selected cases.
- Neuroimaging (CT or MRI brain) should be obtained when neurologic symptoms/signs occur; asymptomatic patients with high risk (e.g., large vegetations) may also be evaluated preoperatively due to potential cerebrovascular events.

Practical approach
- In suspected IE, obtain multiple blood cultures before starting antibiotics, perform TTE urgently, and proceed to TEE if TTE is nondiagnostic, if PVE is suspected, or if complications are suspected.
- If blood cultures are negative, consider prolonged incubation, serology and molecular testing for uncommon pathogens (Coxiella, Bartonella), and imaging such as FDG-PET/CT for prosthetic devices.
- Reassess frequently; a single negative TEE does not rule out IE if clinical suspicion remains high — repeat imaging within 7–10 days may be necessary.

# Medical treatment principles and antibiotic regimens for common organisms (eg, Enterococcus spp.)

Treatment principles
- Effective management requires organism-directed, bactericidal therapy delivered for an adequate duration to sterilize vegetations and address biofilm resistance. Therapy is typically intravenous and prolonged because vegetations are avascular and antibiotics penetrate poorly.
- Selection of antibiotics is informed by organism identity, susceptibility profile, presence of prosthetic material, and clinical factors (renal function, allergies). Where possible, combination therapy that achieves synergistic bactericidal activity is used — classically, a β-lactam plus an aminoglycoside for enterococci.
- Duration of therapy: typically 4–6 weeks for definitive endocarditis treatment, counting from the first day of effective therapy and based on valve type and organism. For PVE, durations are often at least 6 weeks. Shorter regimens (e.g., 2 weeks) are rarely appropriate except in highly selected right-sided uncomplicated S. aureus IE treated with specific regimens.

Enterococcal endocarditis
- Enterococci are challenging because of intrinsic tolerance to many bactericidal agents and increasing antimicrobial resistance. Treatment principles for enterococcal IE depend on species and susceptibility.

Enterococcus faecalis susceptible to ampicillin/penicillin and gentamicin
- Standard therapy: a cell-wall active agent (ampicillin 2 g IV every 4 hours or penicillin G continuous or frequent dosing) plus an aminoglycoside (gentamicin) for synergy. Typical regimen: ampicillin 2 g IV q4h plus gentamicin 3 mg/kg/day in divided doses, adjusted by renal function with therapeutic drug monitoring targeting gentamicin peak 3–4 mcg/mL and trough <1 mcg/mL when used for synergy.
- Duration: 4–6 weeks depending on native vs prosthetic valve and clinical response. Traditionally, for NVE: ampicillin + gentamicin for 4–6 weeks; for PVE, at least 6 weeks.

Ampicillin-susceptible enterococci where aminoglycoside use is contraindicated (e.g., renal impairment)
- Ampicillin plus ceftriaxone synergy: Ampicillin 2 g IV q4h combined with ceftriaxone 2 g IV every 12 hours (or continuous infusion approaches) produces synergistic killing for E. faecalis by dual targeting of penicillin-binding proteins. This regimen is an established aminoglycoside-sparing alternative for ampicillin-susceptible E. faecalis IE and is particularly useful in patients at high risk for aminoglycoside toxicity. Duration typically 6 weeks for PVE and 4–6 weeks for NVE depending on specifics.

Vancomycin for β-lactam allergy or resistant strains
- If isolates are penicillin-allergic or penicillin-resistant but vancomycin-susceptible, vancomycin 15–20 mg/kg IV q8–12h with trough monitoring (target trough often 15–20 mg/L for serious infections, though area under the curve AUC-based dosing is preferred in many centers) may be used, often combined with gentamicin for synergistic effect if susceptibility allows.

Vancomycin-resistant enterococci (VRE)
- VRE (commonly E. faecium with vanA/vanB genes) are difficult to treat. Options may include daptomycin (high-dose regimens, e.g., 8–12 mg/kg daily; often combined with a second agent for synergy such as ampicillin if partial susceptibility, ceftaroline, or gentamicin where appropriate), linezolid (IV or oral), or combinations guided by susceptibility testing and expert consultation. For serious infections like endocarditis, bactericidal agents (e.g., daptomycin) are generally preferred over solely bacteriostatic drugs (e.g., linezolid), though clinical context and susceptibilities drive choices. Consultation with infectious diseases specialists is critical.

Staphylococcal endocarditis
- MSSA NVE: oxacillin/nafcillin 2 g IV q4h or cefazolin if β-lactam allergy excludes penicillin anaphylaxis. Duration typically 6 weeks for complicated disease; 4 weeks may be acceptable for uncomplicated right-sided disease in selected patients.
- MRSA: vancomycin or daptomycin (high-dose) are mainstays. Daptomycin (8–10 mg/kg IV daily) is an alternative with rapid bactericidal activity; combination therapy is considered in complicated cases. Monitor vancomycin trough or ideally AUC-guided dosing.
- CoNS PVE: vancomycin is typical initial empiric therapy until susceptibilities are known; rifampin is added for prosthetic device infections if organisms are susceptible, given its biofilm activity (rifampin should not be used as monotherapy).

Streptococcal endocarditis
- Viridans streptococci with penicillin MIC ≤0.12 µg/mL: penicillin G or ceftriaxone monotherapy for 4 weeks may be adequate for NVE; addition of gentamicin can shorten duration to 2 weeks in selected uncomplicated cases. For PVE, durations are usually 6 weeks.
- For higher penicillin MICs or penicillin allergy, vancomycin may be used.

Fungal endocarditis
- Candida and Aspergillus endocarditis are managed with antifungal therapy plus early surgical removal of infected prosthetic material. Amphotericin B formulations, echinocandins (for Candida), or voriconazole (for Aspergillus) may be used depending on organism and susceptibility.

General antimicrobial considerations
- Empiric therapy should be started when blood culture results are pending in unstable patients or when delays in therapy would be harmful. Empiric regimens must cover likely pathogens given the clinical setting (e.g., S. aureus, CoNS, Gram-negatives in early PVE, and enterococci in healthcare-associated infections). Choices are refined once cultures and susceptibilities are available.
- Duration of therapy is counted from the first day of effective therapy; therapeutic drug monitoring and renal function adjustments are essential, particularly with aminoglycosides and vancomycin.
- For PVE, rifampin is often added when staphylococci are implicated and prosthesis is retained initially, because rifampin penetrates biofilm and is active against adherent organisms; but rifampin should never be used alone, and resistance can develop rapidly if used as monotherapy or in the absence of other active agents.

# Indications and timing for surgical intervention in PVE

Surgical management is a cornerstone of care in PVE. Decisions combine the presence of complications, the causative organism, response to antimicrobials, and the patient’s overall operative risk. Goals of surgery are to remove infected prosthetic material and vegetations, debride infected tissue (including abscesses and fistulae), repair or replace valves to restore hemodynamic integrity, and reduce embolic risk.

General indications for surgery in PVE
- Heart failure due to valve dysfunction: New or worsening congestive heart failure from valvular regurgitation or obstruction is a principal indication for urgent surgery. This includes acute severe regurgitation causing pulmonary edema or cardiogenic shock.
- Uncontrolled infection: Persistent bacteremia or fever beyond a predefined period (commonly >7 days) despite appropriate antimicrobial therapy suggests uncontrolled infection, perivalvular extension (abscess), or infected prosthetic material requiring surgery.
- Perivalvular extension: Development of abscess, pseudoaneurysm, fistula, or prosthetic dehiscence typically necessitates surgical intervention because these lesions rarely resolve with antibiotics alone and often lead to worsening hemodynamics or conduction disturbances.
- Large vegetations with embolic risk: Vegetations >10 mm (or >15 mm in some guidelines) with prior embolic events, or rapidly increasing vegetation size despite therapy, are considered for surgery to reduce risk of further embolization, particularly cerebral emboli.
- Fungal or highly resistant organisms: Fungal IE and infections with multidrug-resistant organisms (e.g., VRE where no bactericidal option exists) often require early valve replacement given poor response to medical therapy alone.
- Valve obstruction or prosthetic dehiscence: Hemodynamically significant obstruction or dehiscence mandates surgical correction.

Timing of surgery
- Emergency surgery: Indicated within hours when there is refractory cardiogenic shock, uncontrolled sepsis with valve perforation causing severe hemodynamic compromise, or severe prosthetic dehiscence with life-threatening regurgitation.
- Urgent surgery: Usually defined as within days (typically within 7 days) for cases with persistent sepsis, perivalvular abscess, or large vegetations with recent emboli.
- Elective (delayed) surgery: May be appropriate for patients who can be stabilized and for whom delaying surgery reduces perioperative risk (e.g., stabilization of organ dysfunction). However, unnecessary delay in PVE can worsen outcomes; the balance of infection control versus surgical risk must be individualized.

Special considerations for PVE
- Early PVE after valve implantation often involves intraoperative contamination or perioperative seeding and tends to present with severe infection and higher surgical urgency. Removal of the prosthetic device and extensive debridement is frequently necessary.
- In late PVE, tissue planes may be more fibrotic; surgical complexity and risk remain high. Recurrence risk is significant if the infected prosthesis is not removed.
- Choice of valve prosthesis (mechanical vs bioprosthetic, homograft, or valve repair where feasible) is individualized. Some centers favor homografts or cryopreserved aortic root replacement for extensive aortic root infection due to lower reinfection rates in selected series, but randomized data are lacking.
- Multidisciplinary decision-making in an “endocarditis team” (cardiology, cardiac surgery, infectious diseases, microbiology, neurology, and imaging specialists) improves outcomes and helps time interventions appropriately.

Neurologic events and surgery timing
- Patients with recent ischemic stroke may still require early surgery if indicated for cardiac failure or uncontrolled infection. The risk of hemorrhagic transformation increases if surgery requires full anticoagulation or cardiopulmonary bypass, and timing must balance neurological risk against cardiac deterioration. In cases of intracranial hemorrhage, surgery is usually delayed until stabilization.

# Long-term management, relapse risk and role of suppressive therapy

Post-treatment care and long-term follow-up
- After completion of intravenous therapy and any indicated surgery, patients require structured follow-up to monitor for relapse, assess valve function, manage complications (e.g., heart failure), and coordinate secondary prevention.
- Serial blood cultures are obtained during therapy to document clearance of bacteremia; repeat cultures are unnecessary after documented culture negativity unless clinical relapse occurs.
- Echocardiography (usually TTE) is typically repeated after completing therapy or earlier if clinical concern arises, to document resolution of vegetations or detect structural complications.

Relapse vs reinfection
- Relapse refers to infection with the same organism within 6 months of completing therapy and usually reflects inadequate initial therapy, undrained focus, persistent nidus of infection (e.g., retained infected prosthesis or intracardiac device), or antimicrobial resistance. Reinfection is infection with a new organism or with the same organism after a more prolonged interval and often reflects new exposure.
- Risk factors for relapse include PVE (especially if the prosthesis is retained), organisms with high propensity for biofilm and tolerance (enterococci, coagulase-negative staphylococci), incomplete surgical removal of infected material, and inadequate duration or dosing of antibiotics.

Suppressive therapy
- Long-term suppressive (chronic suppressive) antimicrobial therapy is considered in selected circumstances where definitive eradication is unlikely or surgical removal is impossible or prohibitively risky. Situations include inoperable prosthetic device infection, retained infected hardware (e.g., pacemaker leads), or end-stage surgical risk.
- Suppressive regimens are organism-specific, generally use oral agents with good bioavailability and activity against the pathogen, and are intended to suppress rather than eradicate infection. The decision to use suppressive therapy requires careful consideration of the risks (antimicrobial toxicity, resistance selection) and benefits (symptom control, prevention of systemic relapse).
- Examples: For staphylococcal PVE when surgery is not possible, oral agents (e.g., doxycycline or TMP-SMX) have been used in limited circumstances if the organism is susceptible; rifampin-based combinations are sometimes used for device-related infections but resistance emerges if rifampin is used alone. For VRE in non-operable settings, long-term oral linezolid has been used but is limited by hematologic toxicity and neuropathy.

Preventive measures and secondary prophylaxis
- Patients with prior IE or prosthetic valves are candidates for antibiotic prophylaxis prior to certain dental procedures per guideline recommendations, because of higher risk of IE from mucosal bacteremia. Current guidelines restrict prophylaxis to high-risk patients (prosthetic heart valves, prior IE, specific congenital heart disease, or cardiac transplant recipients with valvulopathy) and to high-risk procedures (dental procedures that manipulate gingival tissue or perforate oral mucosa).
- General preventive strategies include infection control for intravascular catheters, timely treatment of bacteremia, and cautious use of invasive procedures with appropriate peri-procedural measures in high-risk patients.

Monitoring for relapse
- Clinical vigilance for fever recurrence, new murmurs, embolic phenomena, or recurrent bacteremia is critical. Re-evaluation with blood cultures and echocardiography should be undertaken promptly for suspected relapse.
- In PVE patients with retained prosthetic material who did not undergo surgery, a lower threshold for imaging (e.g., FDG-PET/CT) and infectious disease consultation is warranted when symptoms recur.

Vaccination and lifestyle considerations
- Immunizations (influenza, pneumococcal) are recommended per general guidelines to reduce secondary infections that can predispose to bacteremia. Patients should receive counseling about skin and dental hygiene to minimize bacteremia risk.

Conclusion

Infective endocarditis and prosthetic valve endocarditis remain complex clinical problems necessitating integration of microbiologic diagnosis, imaging, and individualized therapeutic strategies. Understanding the microbiology — particularly the implications of staphylococci, streptococci, and enterococci — the pathogenesis of vegetation and biofilm formation, and the indications and timing of surgical intervention are central to management. Long-term outcomes depend on prompt recognition, appropriate organism-directed antimicrobial therapy (with attention to enterococcal treatment nuances), and early multidisciplinary surgical consultation when indicated. Relapse risk is highest in the setting of prosthetic material and biofilm-producing organisms, and suppressive therapy is reserved for carefully selected, often high-risk patients.

Introduction

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) has evolved from an alternative for inoperable patients to a mainstream therapy for severe aortic stenosis across surgical risk profiles. Central to the success and safety of TAVI is device design: the frame architecture, leaflet position, sealing mechanisms, radial force characteristics, and delivery mechanics all determine how a valve performs hemodynamically, how it interacts with patient anatomy (especially calcified annuli and the coronary ostia), and what complications are likely. Understanding device classifications and the engineering choices behind them allows clinicians to match patient anatomy and procedural goals to the implant best suited for each case. This chapter reviews the major classes of transcatheter valves, examines the elements of frame and leaflet design, describes device-specific features that influence coronary access and implantation strategy, and links these design features to clinical consequences such as paravalvular leak, gradients, conduction disturbances and coronary obstruction.

# Device classifications: self-expanding, balloon-expandable, mechanically expandable

TAVI devices can be grouped by how the final valve frame is deployed and how radial force is generated and maintained.

Self-expanding devices
- Mechanism and materials: Self-expanding valves are typically made from shape-memory alloys such as nitinol. Nitinol frames are constrained during delivery and then released; they expand to a predetermined shape at body temperature. Expansion is gradual and results in persistent outward radial force.
- Deployment characteristics: Deployment is often gradual, allowing repositioning or recapture with some systems. The expansion is driven by the material’s stored energy rather than an external inflation. Because expansion is progressive and adaptive, self-expanding valves may conform to irregular annular shapes and accommodate some degree of under-sizing without exerting abrupt excessive force.
- Clinical implications: The chronic outward radial force may help seat the valve and reduce immediate paravalvular regurgitation over time, but persistent expansion against the conduction system can increase rates of conduction abnormalities and permanent pacemaker implantation. Examples include the Medtronic CoreValve and Evolut family, and other nitinol-based systems such as Portico (though it has its own deployment nuances) and ACURATE neo (which uses self-expanding elements but with specific design differences).

Balloon-expandable devices
- Mechanism and materials: Balloon-expandable frames are commonly made of cobalt-chromium or stainless steel alloys that are plastically deformed by balloon inflation during deployment, achieving the final diameter by permanent expansion.
- Deployment characteristics: Deployment is rapid and controlled by inflating a balloon to a specific volume/pressure. It generally produces high immediate radial force and a well-defined final shape. Repositioning is limited or impossible after full deployment.
- Clinical implications: High immediate radial force achieves strong anchoring, good circularization of the annulus and typically low residual paravalvular leak when sizing is appropriate. However, abrupt expansion can transmit high forces to the annulus and root, increasing risk of annular rupture, particularly in heavily calcified or small annuli or when aggressive oversizing is used. Balloon-expandable devices include the Edwards Sapien family and other systems employing balloon deployment.

Mechanically expandable devices
- Mechanism and materials: Mechanically expanding valves use a mechanical stent that can be expanded in a controlled fashion by a screw, slider, or other mechanism rather than by balloon inflation or shape-memory recoil. They allow precise, sometimes stepwise, adjustment of diameter and often permit full retrieval prior to final release.
- Deployment characteristics: The controlled mechanical expansion confers predictable apposition and often allows resheathing and repositioning until a final lock is engaged.
- Clinical implications: The ability to precisely control expansion can reduce risk of annular injury compared to balloon expansion and allow better optimization of final position. Mechanically expandable devices have varied market penetration; examples included the now-discontinued Boston Scientific Lotus valve, which illustrated both the benefits (precise deployment, low paravalvular leak) and real-world challenges (device availability/production issues). Use of mechanically expandable platforms may be considered when precise positioning and retrieval are priorities.

Choosing among classes involves weighing trade-offs: balloon-expandable valves for predictable circularization and lower post-implant gradients in many settings; self-expanding valves for conformability and potentially better outcomes in very large annuli or when the annulus is highly elliptical; mechanically expandable valves for stepwise, retrievable deployment when immediate precise control is required.

# Valve frame architecture, skirt/ sealing mechanisms and leaflet positions (supra- vs intra-annular)

Frame architecture
- Purpose and varieties: The frame is the structural scaffold that anchors the valve, transmits radial force to the annulus, and determines height and cell geometry. Frames vary by height (short vs tall), cell size (open vs dense lattice), and frame profile (tubular, flared inflow/outflow, hourglass). A short, low-profile frame may reduce interference with the coronary ostia and conduction system, whereas a tall frame may provide more anchoring surface in the LVOT or ascending aorta and can better accommodate eccentric or large annuli.
- Materials and fatigue: Frames are engineered from alloys chosen for strength, fatigue resistance, and compatibility with crimping for delivery. Frame fatigue and long-term structural integrity influence durability and rates of late deformation.

Skirt and sealing mechanisms
- Rationale: Paravalvular leak (PVL) was a major early complication of TAVI. Skirts and sealing mechanisms are engineered to reduce PVL by filling gaps between the stent frame and annular surface, especially in the presence of calcific irregularities.
- Designs: Skirts range from simple fabric cuffs (commonly polyethylene terephthalate, PET) positioned at the inflow portion of the frame, to more elaborate adaptive seals composed of compressible foam or pericardial wraps that expand to fill irregular spaces. Some valves incorporate external pericardial tissue or sealing rings integrated into the frame. The height and placement of the skirt determine where sealing occurs: an effective inflow skirt mitigates PVL by sealing at the annular plane or just below it; outflow skirts may be used to decrease regurgitation toward the ascending aorta.
- Clinical effects: A well-designed skirt reduces the degree of PVL and improves hemodynamics. However, very thick or external skirts may impede coronary access, and skirts that extend higher may increase risk of sinus obstruction or complicate future coronary interventions.

Leaflet position: supra-annular versus intra-annular
- Definitions: Intra-annular leaflets are mounted within the native annulus plane, such that the leaflet coaptation point lies at or below the anatomic annulus. Supra-annular leaflets are mounted on the stent frame above the native annulus so the effective orifice area (EOA) of the prosthetic valve is positioned above the native annulus.
- Hemodynamic consequences: Supra-annular valves typically achieve larger EOAs and lower transvalvular gradients for a given nominal valve size, because the leaflet opening occurs in a larger diameter region above the potentially constrained annulus. This is especially advantageous in small annuli or patients at risk for prosthesis–patient mismatch (PPM). Intra-annular valves may have slightly higher gradients when annular dimension limits the prosthetic orifice.
- Durability and structural considerations: Leaflet mounting influences leaflet stresses and flow profiles. Supra-annular leaflets are subject to different loading patterns and may impart distinct shear stresses compared with intra-annular designs. Long-term durability data are accruing and depend on material, leaflet treatment, and hemodynamic environment.

Integration of frame, skirt, and leaflet placement is not independent: a tall frame with a high skirt and supra-annular leaflets produces different hemodynamic and anatomic interactions than a short frame with an intra-annular leaflet and low skirt. Device selection should consider these interacting design elements in relation to patient anatomy, risk of PVL, need for coronary access and expected gradients.

# Examples of device-specific features affecting coronary access and frame height

Coronary access after TAVI is an increasingly important consideration, especially as TAVI is used in younger patients who may require future coronary angiography or interventions. Device-specific features—frame height, cell design and commissural alignment strategy—determine how readily catheters can engage coronary ostia post-implant.

Representative devices and features
- Balloon-expandable Edwards Sapien family: These devices have relatively short frames and an intra-annular leaflet position. The lower frame height and open cell design above the skirt generally facilitate coronary access. The skirt is relatively low and circumferential; however, valve stent posts can still partially cover coronary ostia depending on implant depth and native cusp calcification.
- Medtronic CoreValve / Evolut family: These are tall, self-expanding nitinol frames with supra-annular leaflet position. The frame extends well into the ascending aorta and may sit above the coronary ostia depending on implant depth and valve size. The cell architecture varies, and outer cells may obstruct direct vertical access, requiring catheterization through smaller cells. These valves frequently present more challenges to coronary access, particularly in patients with low coronary heights or narrow sinus of Valsalva.
- Boston Scientific ACURATE neo: This self-expanding system is designed with a specific “top-down” deployment and has design features to facilitate commissural alignment and coronary access, including large open cells in the upper frame.
- Portico (St. Jude/Abbott) and Navitor: These valves have unique frame and leaflet positions intended to balance radial force and ease of access; cell size and skirt design are aspects to consider.

Anatomic and implant-related determinants
- Coronary height and sinus anatomy: Independent of device, coronary ostial height measured from the annulus and dimensions of the sinus of Valsalva are primary determinants of coronary access post-TAVI. Low coronary heights (<10–12 mm) and small sinuses complicate access, especially with tall frames.
- Frame alignment and rotation: If the prosthesis’ commissural posts land in front of a coronary ostium, catheter engagement may be blocked. Some manufacturers and operators use marker-based alignment strategies during implantation to optimize commissural alignment and thus improve future coronary access.
- Cell size and strut configuration: Open cell designs with large cells near the level of the coronary ostia are more permissive for catheters to traverse the frame. Dense cell designs or small upper cells reduce maneuverability and require specialized catheter techniques.

Practical considerations for coronary interventions post-TAVI
- Pre-procedural CT planning must include assessment of coronary height and sinus dimensions and consideration of device frame height relative to those parameters.
- When future coronary access is anticipated (e.g., younger patients, known coronary disease), favoring valves with low frame height, large upper cells and predictable commissural alignment may preserve access.
- During implantation, aiming for commissural alignment (rotational positioning) and optimal depth can reduce the probability of post-implant coronary obstruction or difficult catheter engagement.

# Radial force, conformability to anatomy and interaction with calcified annuli

Radial force
- Definitions: Radial force describes the outward pressure exerted by the valve frame on the annulus and adjacent tissues. For balloon-expandable devices radial force is delivered quickly and results from plastic deformation; for self-expanding devices radial force is applied progressively and maintained as chronic outward force.
- Clinical implications: Adequate radial force is necessary to secure the prosthesis and to oppose regurgitant flow that causes PVL. Excessive radial force, particularly abrupt or focal, can induce annular injury or rupture, root disruption, or coronary obstruction. Conversely, insufficient radial force may increase the risk of migration or significant PVL.

Conformability and eccentric annuli
- Conformability refers to how well a device adapts to the shape of an irregular or elliptical annulus. A highly conformable device can accommodate eccentric anatomy and uneven calcification, achieving apposition without focal pressure peaks.
- Self-expanding nitinol frames often excel at conforming to eccentric annuli because expansion is gradual and the material can redistribute forces. Balloon-expandable frames achieve circularization at the cost of potentially high focal stress where rigid calcific nodules prevent expansion.
- In small or highly calcified annuli, conformability reduces the need for aggressive oversizing and may lower rates of PVL and annular injury.

Interaction with calcified annuli
- Calcific distribution and burden: The pattern of calcification (nodular vs diffuse, location on cusps or LVOT) affects seating and sealing. Focal bulky nodules can prevent full apposition leading to PVL or displacement, and can focalize stress causing annular rupture with rigid frames.
- Clinical trade-offs: Balloon-expandable valves may forcibly displace calcium to create a circular orifice—this can be effective but risks localized trauma and annular rupture, particularly in small annuli, heavy LVOT calcification, or bicuspid valves with bulky raphe. Self-expanding valves may deform around nodules, reducing rupture risk but possibly leaving PVL channels if calcific nodules prevent adequate sealing.
- Adjunctive maneuvers: Pre-dilation (balloon aortic valvuloplasty) or post-dilation are strategies to modify the annulus or improve apposition; however, both carry risks: pre-dilation can embolize calcium, and post-dilation with high-pressure balloons can cause annular injury. The decision to pre- or post-dilate should integrate device choice, calcific burden, and expected radial force.

Device-specific performance in calcified anatomy
- Some devices incorporate adaptive sealing skirts or conformable frame geometries designed to accommodate calcification and reduce PVL without high expansion forces.
- Operators must balance the desire to eliminate PVL with the risk of annular injury: in severely calcified or small annuli, selecting devices with lower peak radial stress or the ability to recapture and reposition may be safer.

# Clinical implications of frame height, cell design and commissural alignment

Frame height
- Effects on conduction system: Taller frames extend deeper into the left ventricular outflow tract and may impinge on the membranous septum and the proximal conduction tissues, increasing rates of new left bundle branch block (LBBB) and need for permanent pacemaker implantation. For example, self-expanding tall frames are associated with higher pacemaker rates compared with shorter balloon-expandable frames. Implant depth is an important modifiable factor: shallower implantation reduces conduction disturbance risk but may increase valve embolization risk or PVL.
- Coronary obstruction and access: Tall frames may overlie coronary ostia. In patients with low coronary heights or small sinuses, tall frames elevate the risk of coronary obstruction, especially if native leaflets are bulky and get displaced upwards.
- Future interventions: A tall frame that extends into the ascending aorta may complicate coronary catheterization or valve-in-valve procedures. Conversely, a taller frame provides a larger landing zone for valve-in-valve scenarios in some designs.

Cell design
- Open vs closed cells: Open cell designs with larger diamond-shaped apertures allow easier access for coronary catheters to pass through the frame. Closed cell or dense lattice designs can hinder catheter manipulation and increase the procedural difficulty of coronary interventions.
- Radial stiffness and cell geometry: Smaller cell size can increase radial stiffness and scaffolding but at the expense of access. Cell geometry also affects how a future transcatheter heart valve or guidewire can be advanced during valve-in-valve procedures.
- Thrombosis risk and flow patterns: Dense frames may alter local flow vortices and influence leaflet washout; while not the dominant factor, cell geometry affects hemodynamic micro-environment and may contribute to leaflet thrombosis risk.

Commissural alignment
- Concept and importance: Native aortic valve commissures and prosthetic commissural posts should ideally be aligned so that prosthetic posts do not overlay coronary ostia. Commissural malalignment can place posts in front of coronaries and make future access or coronary interventions difficult or impossible.
- Techniques: Many operators now aim for commissural alignment using rotational orientation markers on delivery systems and pre-procedural CT to orient the valve prior to release. Some valve platforms have specific design features to facilitate alignment.
- Clinical impact: Proper commissural alignment improves the likelihood of straightforward coronary access, may reduce risk of coronary obstruction, and facilitates future interventions such as valve-in-valve procedures. Malalignment has been associated with more challenging coronary intubation in registry and bench studies.

Decision-making based on design features
- Patient-specific matching: Older patients without significant coronary disease and with low likelihood of future coronary interventions may tolerate devices that prioritize minimal PVL and optimal gradients even if coronary access is more challenging. Younger patients or those with multivessel coronary disease may benefit from valves with low frames, open cells and a commitment to commissural alignment.
- Conduction system risk mitigation: In patients with pre-existing conduction disease or those at high risk for pacemaker dependency, selecting valves and implantation depths associated with lower conduction disturbance rates is critical—often favoring balloon-expandable or shorter-frame options and shallow implantation.
- Valve-in-valve future planning: If valve-in-valve is anticipated, frame height and leaflet position influence feasibility. Supra-annular leaflets may offer better effective orifice areas after valve-in-valve but may also alter coronary flow dynamics. Some strategies evolve around bioprosthetic valve leaflet orientation to preserve coronary access.

Conclusion

Device design profoundly influences the immediate procedural strategy and long-term outcomes after TAVI. Understanding the distinctions among self-expanding, balloon-expandable, and mechanically expandable platforms; how frame architecture, skirts and leaflet position interact with patient anatomy; and how radial force, conformability and commissural alignment dictate both benefit and risk allows clinicians to individualize device selection. Thoughtful pre-procedural imaging and anticipation of future needs (such as coronary access or valve-in-valve therapy) together with awareness of device-specific behaviors in calcified annuli, conduction system proximity and sinus anatomy are essential for optimal TAVI planning and long-term care.

Introduction

Valve-in-valve (ViV) interventions—implantation of a transcatheter heart valve (THV) within a previously implanted bioprosthetic surgical or transcatheter valve—have become an essential option in structural heart disease practice. As the population of patients with prior surgical and transcatheter bioprostheses ages, clinicians increasingly face decisions about whether to pursue repeat transcatheter intervention or surgical re-replacement, and how to perform ViV safely and effectively. ViV procedures carry distinct anatomic and hemodynamic consequences because the new device is nested inside the frame and leaflets of the existing prosthesis. Optimal outcomes depend on careful patient selection, rigorous imaging-based planning, an understanding of valve dimensions and bioprosthesis design, and procedural strategies to mitigate risks such as coronary obstruction, high residual gradients and patient–prosthesis mismatch (PPM). This chapter reviews indications for ViV versus redo surgery, the anatomic and physiologic implications of nested valve frames, sizing strategies that use surgical internal diameter (S‑ID) and true internal diameter, ViV‑specific risks, and technical measures to maximize effective orifice area and preserve future coronary access.

# Indications for ViV versus surgical re-replacement

Choosing ViV versus surgical re-replacement requires individualized assessment of the patient’s surgical risk, life expectancy, valve pathology, anatomic feasibility, and goals of care. ViV is generally an attractive option in patients at elevated surgical risk who have structural degeneration of a bioprosthetic valve. Typical indications for ViV include symptomatic bioprosthetic aortic stenosis, mixed dysfunction with predominant stenosis or regurgitation, and bioprosthetic failure in patients with prohibitive surgical risk or frailty. Conversely, redo surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR) is favored when there are features less amenable to ViV or when long-term durability and potential anatomic correction are priorities.

Key decision elements:

- Surgical risk and comorbidity profile: Guidelines and practice often favor ViV in patients at high or prohibitive surgical risk (e.g., Society of Thoracic Surgeons Predicted Risk of Mortality [STS-PROM] substantially elevated or major frailty). In low-risk patients, redo SAVR may be preferred particularly if a durable solution with a larger prosthetic or root enlargement can be achieved.

- Nature of valve failure: Pure regurgitation (insufficient leaflet coaptation) is more complex for ViV because the underlying frame and leaflets of the surgical valve can impede optimal seating or sealing; however, ViV can be performed for predominant regurgitation in selected high-risk patients. Endocarditis is a general contraindication to transcatheter ViV if active infection persists.

- Patient age and life expectancy: Younger patients or those with longer projected survival should be counseled regarding the limited lifetime of sequential valve interventions and the cumulative effect of nested prostheses on effective orifice area. Surgical re-replacement with possible annular/root enlargement or mechanical prosthesis may provide more durable hemodynamics in younger patients.

- Anatomic considerations: Small surgical annulus, presence of extensive pannus or thrombus, malpositioned prior prosthesis, root dilation, significant paravalvular regurgitation requiring debridement, and proximity of coronary ostia to the valve leaflets may favor surgery.

- Feasibility and anticipated hemodynamic outcome: In small surgical valves where ViV is likely to produce high gradients or severe PPM, providers should consider alternatives—redo surgery with root enlargement, patient‑prosthesis mismatch mitigation strategies, or ViV with adjunctive techniques such as bioprosthetic valve fracture (BVF) if the valve type is susceptible.

Clinical decision-making is shared and multidisciplinary. A heart team approach—bringing together interventional cardiology, cardiothoracic surgery, imaging specialists, and anesthesiology—should weigh the relative risks and benefits and discuss long-term implications, including likelihood of future re-interventions.

# Anatomical and hemodynamic implications of nested valve frames

When a transcatheter valve is implanted inside a surgical bioprosthesis, the new stent frame and leaflets are constrained by the existing valve ring and leaflet geometry. This nested configuration has both geometric and flow-dynamic consequences that translate into procedural challenges and clinical outcomes.

Geometric constraints: The inner diameter available to the THV is limited by the true internal diameter (true ID) of the surgical prosthesis, which may be substantially smaller than the labeled valve size. The existing stent frame, sewing ring, and remnant leaflets occupy space and create a fixed cylindrical or non‑cylindrical constraint. For stented surgical valves, the frame may be rigid (e.g., metallic sewing ring) and resistant to radial expansion, whereas for flexible stentless valves or porcine valves the constraint differs. In valve designs with internally mounted leaflets (stented valves with internally placed leaflets), the leaflet thickness further reduces the effective lumen.

Leaflet dynamics and flow: The prosthetic leaflets of the surgical valve are displaced outward by the THV but remain present behind the new valve leaflets. These displaced leaflets can create turbulent flow patterns and add to transvalvular resistance. The nested leaflets can also lead to noncentral jet patterns and increased shear stress across the valve. In addition, the presence of two leaflet sets can increase the risk of leaflet-related thrombosis and altered leaflet motion is a recognized phenomenon after ViV.

Hemodynamic consequences: The primary hemodynamic concern after ViV is an elevated transvalvular gradient. Because the effective orifice area (EOA) is limited by the smaller true ID, and because the THV may not expand to its intended diameter, flow is reduced and gradients rise. This is particularly consequential in patients with small surgical valves (true ID ≤ 21 mm), where post‑ViV mean gradients can be substantial and clinically meaningful PPM can result. Elevated gradients can present immediately or evolve over time due to structural degeneration or pannus.

Coronary access and sinus interactions: The surgical valve frame and displaced leaflets may impinge on the sinus of Valsalva and coronary ostia. A transcatheter valve that expands within a small sinus can push surgical leaflets toward the coronary ostia, resulting in potential obstruction. Also, the stent frames of both prostheses and their relative orientation can impede future coronary catheterization, complicating revascularization.

Valve durability and leaflet stress: The geometry of nested valves may subject THV leaflets to abnormal stress and altered closing dynamics, with potential implications for leaflet fatigue and mid-term durability. Although long-term data are evolving, the altered leaflet motion and increased hemodynamic stress should be part of the informed consent discussion.

# Sizing strategies using surgical internal diameter (S‑ID) and true internal diameter

Accurate sizing for ViV depends on understanding the surgical valve’s labeled size, surgical internal diameter (S‑ID), and true internal diameter (true ID). Labeled prosthetic sizes are not standardized across manufacturers; the nominal label often reflects external sewing ring diameter rather than the lumen available for valve implantation. Using the correct internal diameter guides appropriate THV size selection and helps predict the risk of high residual gradients.

Definitions and practical measurements:
- Labeled size: Manufacturer’s nominal size of the surgical valve (e.g., 21 mm), often corresponding to sewing ring or outer frame dimensions.
- Surgical internal diameter (S‑ID): Manufacturer-published inner diameter measured between the stent posts or internal frame; commonly used for ViV sizing charts and represents the internal lumen as intended at implantation.
- True internal diameter (true ID): The practical internal dimension available at the time of re‑intervention. It may be less than the S‑ID due to leaflet thickness, pannus, calcification, or tissue ingrowth. True ID can be estimated by imaging (CT) or inferred from manufacturer data adjusting for leaflet mounting (internally versus externally mounted leaflets).

Imaging-based sizing:
- Multidetector computed tomography (MDCT) is the cornerstone for precise measurement of the annulus and the internal dimensions of the surgical prosthesis. CT allows measurement of the actual lumen, quantification of leaflet thickness/pannus, calcification, sinus dimensions, and distance to coronary ostia.
- Fluoroscopy and echocardiography (TTE/TEE) supplement CT when CT is not feasible or to document functional severity.

Reference data and ViV sizing charts:
- ViV sizing charts (e.g., VIVID registry and manufacturer-provided tables) use S‑ID as the basis for recommending THV model and size. These charts also indicate which surgical valves are amenable to bioprosthetic valve fracture (BVF).
- Practitioners must recognize that S‑ID is an estimate and that true ID may be smaller. For example, a labeled 23 mm valve may have an S‑ID of 21 mm and a true ID of 19–20 mm once leaflets are considered.

Sizing strategy principles:
- Use the largest THV that the true ID safely accommodates to maximize EOA, keeping in mind radial strength of the THV and resistance imposed by the surgical frame.
- For small true IDs, consider adjunctive techniques (BVF when feasible) to increase the internal diameter prior to THV deployment or select THVs with supra‑annular leaflet position that provide larger EOAs for a given inflow diameter.
- Avoid undersizing a balloon-expandable THV such that it under-expands and results in paravalvular leak, but also avoid oversizing that risks annular injury.

Documentation and planning:
- Create a written plan with measured S‑ID and CT-derived true ID, THV type and size selected, anticipated need for BVF, and coronary protection strategies if VTC (virtual transcatheter valve to coronary) distance is borderline. Anticipate potential need for post‑dilatation and select balloon types accordingly.

# Risks specific to ViV: coronary obstruction, elevated gradients, PPM

ViV carries several risks that are distinct or amplified relative to primary TAVR, including coronary obstruction, elevated residual gradients potentially causing PPM, and challenges with coronary access.

Coronary obstruction:
- Mechanism: Coronary obstruction in ViV primarily results from displacement of the failed surgical valve's leaflets outward toward the coronary ostia when the THV is expanded. The pre-existing bioprosthetic leaflets are not removed; when the THV pushes them outward they can act as a barrier, especially if the native coronary ostia lie low relative to the annulus or the sinus of Valsalva is small.
- Risk factors: Low coronary height (usually defined as <10–12 mm from the annular plane to the ostium), narrow sinus of Valsalva dimensions (sinus diameter <30–32 mm for the left sinus is often cited), internally mounted leaflets (which are positioned closer to the coronary ostia), externally mounted leaflets with tall leaflets, externally mounted leaflets that extend beyond the frame, and certain surgical valve models known for a low leaflet profile. Additionally, small true ID and high implantation depth of the THV can increase risk.
- Assessment tools: CT helps calculate VTC (virtual transcatheter valve to coronary ostium distance), which estimates the post‑implant distance between the transcatheter valve frame and the coronary ostium; a VTC <4 mm is associated with high risk of obstruction. Angiographic coronary protection during the procedure (wiring and ready stent) reduces morbidity if obstruction occurs.
- Prevention and management: Preventive strategies include prophylactic coronary wiring with ready-to-deploy stent (chimney stenting), performing the BASILICA (bioprosthetic or native aortic scallop intentional laceration to prevent iatrogenic coronary artery obstruction) leaflet laceration to create a flow channel, selecting lower-profile THVs and implantation depths that minimize leaflet displacement toward coronaries, and in select cases choosing surgical re-replacement if obstruction risk is prohibitive.

Elevated gradients and PPM:
- Mechanism: Elevated gradients after ViV occur because of reduced EOA from the constrained true ID and double leaflet layers. Limited radial expansion of the THV stent frame in a rigid surgical ring can yield significant residual stenosis.
- Definitions: Patient–prosthesis mismatch (PPM) is defined by indexed effective orifice area (EOAi = EOA / body surface area). For prosthetic aortic valves, commonly used thresholds are: severe PPM EOAi ≤ 0.65 cm^2/m^2, moderate PPM EOAi 0.65–0.85 cm^2/m^2. These cutoffs correlate with exercise capacity and outcomes.
- Risk factors: Small surgical valve sizes (true ID ≤ 21 mm), older/smaller patients with lower body surface area, and use of intra-annular THVs that place effective leaflets within the constrained lumen tend to produce higher gradients.
- Clinical implications: Elevated gradients may cause persistent symptoms, reduced functional capacity, earlier re-intervention, and adverse prognosis in some populations.

Other specific risks:
- Coronary access difficulty after ViV due to frame overlap and leaflet orientation may complicate future coronary procedures.
- Embolization risk if the THV does not seat properly.
- Valve thrombosis risk might be altered by abnormal flow dynamics and double leaflet configurations; anticoagulation strategy post-procedure should follow institutional practice and consider individual thrombotic risk.
- Hemodynamic instability in the rare instance of acute coronary obstruction or periprocedural complications.

# Strategies to optimize effective orifice area (high implant, supra‑annular valves)

Because small effective orifice area is the principal hemodynamic challenge in ViV, operators have developed techniques and device selection strategies to maximize EOA and minimize gradients.

Device selection:
- Supra‑annular THVs: Valves with leaflet coaptation and functional orifice positioned above the annulus (supra‑annular) generally yield larger EOAs for a given annular/body size than intra‑annular valves. For small true IDs, choosing a supra‑annular THV (e.g., certain self-expanding designs) can improve post-procedural gradients.
- Valve radial force: Stronger radial force may achieve larger expansion within rigid surgical frames, but the capacity to expand can be limited by the surgical valve’s frame design. Balloon-expandable THVs can achieve precise placement and high radial expansion if the frame allows.

Implantation technique:
- High implant (supra-annular positioning): Implanting the THV slightly higher (more aortic) so that the functional leaflet orifice sits partially above the surgical sewing ring may result in better hemodynamics; this can minimize the degree to which the new leaflets are constrained by the prior valve’s ring. However, too high an implant increases the risk of coronary obstruction and malposition; evidence and practice suggest careful balancing, and detailed knowledge of the THV’s frame and leaflet height is essential.
- Post‑dilatation and optimization: Aggressive post‑dilatation with non‑compliant balloons after THV deployment may improve expansion within the surgical frame and reduce gradients, but carries risks (annular injury, coronary obstruction, valve embolization). Post‑dilatation is frequently combined with BVF when indicated.

Adjunctive mechanical techniques:
- Bioprosthetic valve fracture (BVF): Certain stented surgical valves are amenable to BVF—high-pressure inflation of an appropriately sized non‑compliant balloon within the surgical ring to fracture the rigid frame and increase the internal diameter. BVF can produce significant increases in EOA and reduce post‑ViV gradients. Important considerations include knowing which valve models are fractureable (some frames are resistant), timing (BVF can be performed before or after THV implantation—each approach has pros and cons), requirement for high-pressure balloon and its potential for annular injury, and the effect on coronary obstruction risk. BVF is usually contraindicated in stentless valves or those with brittle sewing rings that could cause unpredictable deformation.
- Bioprosthetic valve remodeling: Less commonly, methods to disrupt calcification or pannus (e.g., targeted debridement) are surgical maneuvers and not typical of ViV.

Clinical choice algorithm:
- For small true ID (<21 mm), consider a supra‑annular THV, plan for BVF if the surgical valve is fractureable, and consider high implant when anatomy allows. If BVF is not feasible and predicted gradients are prohibitive, surgical re-replacement with annular enlargement should be considered.
- When using a balloon-expandable THV in small valves, underexpansion will lead to high gradients; a strategy combining BVF then appropriately sized balloon-expandable THV yields the best EOA in compatible valves.

Post-procedural monitoring:
- Measure transvalvular gradients by TTE before discharge and serially thereafter. Expected mean gradient targets vary by valve size and type, but a mean gradient <20 mmHg is generally desirable after ViV; higher gradients warrant investigation and may reflect PPM or incomplete expansion.
- Assess EOAi and compare to body surface area to document PPM severity using the thresholds above.

# Planning for potential future coronary access in ViV contexts

Future coronary access after ViV is an important long-term consideration. Because the THV frame and displaced surgical leaflets can obstruct access paths to the coronary ostia, planning at the time of ViV to preserve or facilitate future coronary catheterization is crucial, particularly in patients with known coronary disease or younger patients with a higher likelihood of future coronary interventions.

Pre-procedural assessment:
- CT evaluation: Detailed MDCT should measure coronary ostial heights, sinus of Valsalva dimensions, sinotubular junction, and take careful note of the surgical valve frame structure and leaflet mounting. These measurements inform both the risk of coronary obstruction and the feasibility of selective coronary cannulation after ViV.
- Coronary anatomy and future need: Document current coronary artery disease, prior bypass grafts, and the likelihood of future revascularization. Patients with unprotected coronaries or significant disease may warrant strategies to ensure future access.

Techniques to preserve or enable coronary access:
- Commissural alignment: Rotational alignment of the THV at deployment so that its commissural posts avoid covering coronary ostia can facilitate subsequent catheter engagement. Commissural alignment depends on accurate positioning using fluoroscopic markers, device-specific orientation markers, and pre-procedural planning. Achieving consistent and reproducible commissural alignment can be technically challenging but is increasingly emphasized.
- Avoidance of supra‑high skirt coverage: Some THV designs have skirts or sealing components that can extend above the annulus and obstruct coronary ostia; selecting a THV with favorable skirt geometry or positioning to minimize skirt coverage of the coronary takeoff can preserve access.
- BASILICA to create a path: In patients at risk of coronary obstruction, the BASILICA procedure intentionally lacerates the leaflet to split it, creating space for coronary flow and also a channel that may facilitate future access.
- Chimney stenting and protected deployment: When coronary protection is undertaken (guidewire and stent parked in coronary), operators can achieve rescue access if needed, and the presence of a stent may maintain a channel—but this approach creates permanent metal scaffolds that alter future access patterns and have their own long-term implications.
- Documentation: Detailed procedural documentation, including rotational orientation markers, fluoroscopic images showing commissural alignment, final THV depth relative to annulus, and angiographic landmarks, should be recorded and shared with the patient and future treating teams. Uploading CT and procedural images to a shared system is ideal.

Post‑ViV catheterization considerations:
- Catheter selection: Guide catheter shapes that can navigate through THV frames and selectively engage ostia may differ from standard choices. Use of smaller or more tapered catheters, extension catheters, and supportive guidewires can facilitate access. Operators should be prepared for longer procedure times and potentially complex catheter manipulations.
- Imaging guidance: CT or intracoronary imaging may be helpful pre-procedure if access is anticipated to be challenging.
- Revascularization planning: In some scenarios, when future access to a particular coronary artery is likely to be difficult or risky, upfront coronary revascularization (e.g., PCI or CABG) at the time of ViV may be considered after heart team discussion.

Counseling and long-term planning:
- Inform patients that ViV may complicate future coronary procedures and that planned strategies were employed to minimize this risk. Encourage maintenance of copies of procedural imaging and reports.
- Coordinate follow-up between interventional cardiology and cardiothoracic surgery to reassess options should coronary access or further valve intervention be required.

Conclusion

Valve-in-valve and repeat transcatheter interventions are powerful tools in the management of failing bioprosthetic valves but require meticulous planning and technique. Understanding when ViV is appropriate versus surgical re-replacement, the anatomical and hemodynamic consequences of nested prostheses, accurate sizing using S‑ID and true ID, and the primary risks of coronary obstruction and elevated gradients guides patient selection. Procedural strategies—including supra‑annular valve selection, high implant techniques, bioprosthetic valve fracture when indicated, coronary protection strategies, and attention to commissural alignment—can optimize effective orifice area and preserve future coronary access. A multidisciplinary heart team approach, imaging-driven planning, and careful patient counseling are essential for maximizing both immediate procedural safety and long-term outcomes.

Introduction

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) has evolved from a therapy for high-risk surgical patients to a mainstream option for many patients with aortic stenosis and for valve-in-valve (ViV) procedures. Success and safety of transcatheter interventions depend heavily on imaging before, during, and after the procedure. Imaging guides patient selection, prosthesis sizing, vascular access planning, prevention of complications such as annular rupture and coronary obstruction, and surveillance of valve function. Multislice computed tomography (MSCT) and echocardiography (transthoracic, TTE, and transesophageal, TEE) are complementary: CT provides high-resolution, three-dimensional structural detail of the annulus, root, ascending aorta and peripheral vasculature, while echocardiography provides real-time assessment of valve hemodynamics, leaflet motion, and intracardiac complications (for example, endocarditis). Advanced postprocessing—3D reconstructions, virtual valve implantation, and computational simulation—further refines planning by predicting frame-host interactions and coronary access. This chapter details practical MSCT acquisition protocols for TAVI, the key measurements required, interpretation of prior surgical and transcatheter prostheses, the role of echocardiography, 3D modeling and simulation tools, and the limitations and artifacts clinicians must recognize.

# MSCT acquisition protocols for TAVI planning (ECG-gating, contrast timing)

Multislice CT is the cornerstone of anatomic TAVI planning because it offers isotropic, high-resolution volumetric imaging across the thorax and peripheral arteries. The acquisition must be tailored to the clinical question: an aortic root and annular study requires ECG-gating for motion-free imaging of the annulus and leaflets; peripheral vascular access assessment can often be performed without gating. Optimal protocols balance image quality, radiation dose, contrast volume, and temporal resolution.

ECG-gating: There are two principal ECG-gating strategies—prospective ECG-triggering and retrospective ECG-gating. Prospective ECG-triggering acquires data at a predefined phase(s) of the cardiac cycle, limiting radiation dose and providing excellent image quality when heart rate is stable and slow. Retrospective ECG-gating acquires continuous data across the cardiac cycle, enabling multiphase reconstructions to analyze annular dynamics and leaflet motion but at higher radiation dose. For native annular sizing, many centers perform retrospective gating with tube current modulation to permit full-phase evaluation of annular area and perimeter and to allow selection of the phase with the smallest (or largest, per institutional standards) annular dimensions for prosthesis sizing. Prospective gating at a single mid-systolic phase (usually 10–20% of the R-R interval or 30–40% depending on the system and heart rate) can be sufficient when lower radiation dose is desired and heart rate is controlled (ideally <70 bpm). It is important to standardize which phase is used for sizing in your institution because annular dimensions vary through the cycle: systolic annular area is typically larger than diastolic.

Heart rate control with beta-blockade is often used when planning peripheral access or coronary assessment but must be balanced against hemodynamic status; it is less essential for annular studies that use retrospective gating. Arrhythmias (atrial fibrillation, extrasystoles) complicate ECG-gated acquisitions, reduce image quality, and may require acquisition strategies adapted to irregular R-R intervals or the use of single-beat wide-volume scanners.

Contrast timing: Optimal contrast opacification of the aortic root, sinuses, coronary ostia and peripheral arteries is critical. A triphasic or biphasic injection protocol is common for comprehensive TAVI workup. For example, a typical triphasic injection may consist of: (1) an initial bolus of iodinated contrast (e.g., 60–80 mL of 350–370 mg I/mL) at 4–6 mL/s to opacify the arterial system; (2) a saline-contrast mixture or reduced-rate contrast to delineate the root while minimizing streak artifacts in the left ventricle; and (3) a saline chaser to clear contrast from the peripheral veins and improve vascular opacification. Bolus tracking is used to time acquisition: a region of interest placed in the ascending aorta triggers the scan when a threshold (commonly 150 HU) is reached. For simultaneous coronary assessment, timing and injection rate are optimized to achieve adequate coronary opacification while maintaining root contrast—this often entails higher injection rates (5–6 mL/s) and a larger volume.

Scan range and reconstruction: The acquisition should extend from the lower neck (to include the carotids if considering alternative access) through the femoral arteries to the level of the femoral heads to fully evaluate peripheral access. For patients with a prior prosthesis, the scan should also include the entire chest to evaluate ascending aorta geometry and any surgical grafts. Thin-slice isotropic acquisition (0.5–0.75 mm) allows multiplanar reformatting and 3D modeling. Reconstructions should include multiphase datasets of the root (if retrospective gating was used) and high-resolution arterial-phase datasets for the vasculature.

Radiation and contrast considerations: Tailor protocols to minimize radiation and contrast in patients with renal impairment. Use iterative reconstruction, reduced kVp in lean patients (e.g., 100 kVp rather than 120 kVp), and limit z-axis coverage to reduce dose. Consider hydration and nephroprotective measures when contrast volume exceeds thresholds relevant to renal function.

Quality control: Assess for motion artifact, inadequate contrast opacification, or metallic streaking. If artifacts preclude accurate measurement (e.g., severe beam-hardening from a mechanical prosthesis), alternative imaging or repeat acquisition with optimized technique may be needed.

# Key CT measurements: annulus dimensions, perimeter, area, leaflet length, sinus diameters, coronary heights

Accurate measurements from CT underlie prosthesis selection, sizing, and procedural risk assessment. Standardization of measurement definitions reduces complications related to oversizing (e.g., annular rupture) or undersizing (e.g., paravalvular leak). Measurements are typically obtained on multiplanar reformats with the short-axis plane aligned at the true annular plane—the virtual basal ring—defined by the nadirs of the aortic cusps.

Annulus dimensions, perimeter, and area: The aortic annulus for TAVI planning is not a circular, single-point structure but a three-dimensional virtual ring defined by the basal attachment points of the leaflets. The annular plane is identified by locating the three aortic cusp nadirs and formatting a perpendicular short-axis reformat. Measurements include the maximal and minimal diameters (in millimeters), the annular cross-sectional area (in mm^2), and the annular perimeter (in mm). Since annuli are often elliptical, reliance on the mean diameter alone can be misleading; most sizing charts reference annular area or perimeter ranges to match valve sizes. For example, an annular area of 400 mm^2 might correspond to a specific valve size per manufacturer sizing tables; always use the manufacturer’s recommended parameter (area, perimeter, or diameter) for sizing. Recognize that annular area and perimeter vary by cardiac phase: many centers use mid-systolic measurements for balloon-expandable valves to capture the largest annular dimensions, whereas others use diastolic measurements or those specified by device trials.

Leaflet length and calcification: Measurement of native leaflet length from the annular plane to leaflet tip, as well as the distribution and burden of calcification, is important. Long, bulky, or heavily calcified leaflets, particularly bulky commissural calcium that extends supra-annularly, increase the risk of coronary obstruction during native valve TAVI and may influence prosthesis selection and implantation depth. Quantify calcification by location (e.g., cusp, annulus, LVOT) and by qualitative burden; some centers use Agatston-like scoring or semi-quantitative grading, though reproducibility varies.

Sinus of Valsalva diameters and sinus height: The sinus diameters (measured as maximal sinus width perpendicular to the aortic wall) and the sinus height (distance from the annular plane to the sinotubular junction or to the highest sinus point) determine the space available for displaced leaflets and prosthesis skirt. Small sinuses relative to leaflets and prosthesis height predispose to coronary obstruction. Record maximal and minimal sinus diameters for each cusp, and measure the sinotubular junction diameter and height as they affect valve deployment and frame expansion.

Coronary ostial heights: Measure the vertical distance from the annular plane to the origin of the left and right coronary ostia. Coronary heights less than commonly cited thresholds (e.g., <10–12 mm) are associated with increased risk of ostial obstruction, especially when combined with low sinuses or bulky leaflets. Many operators use 10–12 mm as a concern threshold, but risk is cumulative: a coronary height of 12 mm may be safe in a large sinus, whereas 14–15 mm in very small sinuses or with bulky leaflets may still be risky. Record the coronary height for both coronaries and the relation of the ostia to leaflet calcification.

Ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) and aortic root geometry: Measure the LVOT area and dimensions immediately below the annulus, as well as any sub-annular calcification or septal hypertrophy that may affect prosthetic seating or risk of conduction abnormalities. Record the diameter and angulation of the ascending aorta because severe angulation may affect catheter trajectory and valve deployment.

Valve deployment depth considerations: Preprocedural CT allows planning of the intended implantation depth relative to the annulus and coronary ostia; measure the distance from the annulus to the proposed landing zone to ensure adequate leaflet coverage and to anticipate interactions with the conduction system.

# Valve-to-coronary (VTC) distance and concepts for ViV planning

Valve-to-coronary (VTC) distance is a critical derived metric when planning valve-in-valve (ViV) procedures in patients with a degenerated surgical bioprosthesis or prior transcatheter valve. VTC quantifies the minimal distance between the anticipated final position of the implanted transcatheter valve frame (or the displaced surgical leaflets) and the coronary ostia. A short VTC predicts a heightened risk of coronary obstruction.

Definition and measurement: VTC is measured on multiplanar CT reformats by virtually positioning the new transcatheter valve at the intended depth within the existing surgical valve frame and measuring the shortest distance from the anticipated outer surface of the new valve frame (or the displaced surgical leaflet plane) to the coronary ostium. This requires accurate depiction of the surgical valve geometry (true inner diameter, stent posts, leaflet attachment), the internal diameter of the prior prosthesis (labelled “true internal diameter” or measured on CT), and the expected expansion geometry of the new valve.

Risk thresholds: Multiple studies and expert consensus use VTC thresholds to stratify coronary obstruction risk. A VTC <4 mm is generally considered high risk for coronary obstruction; VTC between 4 and 6 mm is intermediate risk and may require additional protective strategies; VTC ≥6 mm is lower risk. These thresholds are not absolute; factors such as sinus diameter, leaflet length and material, coronary height, and whether the prior surgical valve leaflets are internally or externally mounted must be integrated. For example, externally mounted leaflets on surgical valves project outward and are more likely to obstruct coronaries when displaced by a transcatheter valve.

Complementary metrics—Valve-to-sinus (VTS) and sinotubular considerations: VTS, the distance from the anticipated prosthetic frame to the sinus wall, indicates whether there is room for leaflet displacement within the sinus. A small VTS suggests that displaced leaflets may be forced toward the coronary ostia. The sinotubular junction height and diameter relative to the prosthesis height also influence the likelihood of coronary obstruction.

Planning strategies for high VTC risk: In high-risk scenarios (VTC <4 mm, low coronary height, small sinuses, bulky or externally mounted surgical leaflets), procedural strategies include coronary protection with guidewires and undeployed stents (parked in the coronary artery ready for immediate deployment), use of BASILICA (bioprosthetic or native aortic scallop intentional laceration to prevent iatrogenic coronary obstruction) to split leaflets, selection of a valve with lower skirt or shorter frame, or surgical re-replacement when necessary. Preprocedural CT assessing VTC combined with simulation enables operators to decide among these strategies and to counsel patients about procedural risk.

# Assessment of prior prosthesis: internal lumen, frame geometry, leaflet position

Evaluation of a prior surgical or transcatheter aortic prosthesis is essential in ViV planning and in reinterventions. CT provides high-resolution detail of the prosthetic frame, sewing ring, internal lumen (true internal diameter), leaflet position and mode of failure, and spatial relationships to the annulus and coronaries.

True internal diameter and effective orifice: Manufacturing labels may not reflect the true internal lumen available for a new transcatheter valve because sewing rings, stent posts, and leaflet materials reduce internal diameter. CT allows measurement of the internal lumen at the level of anticipated valve anchoring; this measurement informs choice of transcatheter valve type and size. Small true internal diameters increase the risk of patient–prosthesis mismatch after ViV and may require consideration of techniques to expand the surgical ring (e.g., high-pressure balloon fracture—valve ring fracture—when applicable) or use of supra-annular, small-frame valves to maximize effective orifice area.

Frame geometry and integrity: For metallic stented bioprostheses or prior transcatheter frames, document the stent geometry including frame diameter, height, degree of expansion, and presence of fractures or deformation. For balloon-expandable surgical stentless valve remnants or previous transcatheter devices, note if frames encroach on coronaries, whether the frame has eccentric expansion, and whether frame struts may impede coronary access in the future.

Leaflet position and mode of failure: Distinguish between restrictive (stenotic) leaflet motion due to calcification or pannus and regurgitant lesions due to leaflet tears, detachment, or endocarditis. CT can visualize calcification and leaflet thickening but has limited temporal resolution compared with echo for small or mobile lesions. For bioprosthetic leaflets, note whether they are internally or externally mounted relative to the stent frame because externally mounted leaflets project outward toward the sinuses and coronaries after displacement. Location of leaflet tears, dehiscence of the sewing ring, and pannus undergrowth can inform likelihood of successful ViV anchoring and sealing.

Relationship to coronaries and surgical anatomy: Identify any coronary buttons or reimplantations from prior surgical procedures, the presence of surgical grafts, and the position of the surgical valve relative to the annular plane. Some surgical valves may be supra-annularly seated, altering the effective landing zone. Document presence of calcification at the sewing ring or leaflet insertion that may affect sealing and paravalvular leak risk.

Integration with fluoroscopic landmarks and fluoroscopy–CT fusion: Correlate CT imaging with fluoroscopic landmarks (e.g., stent posts, commissural markers) to predict optimal C-arm angles and to anticipate challenges in valve crossing or alignment. Some workplaces perform CT-to-fluoroscopy fusion to overlay CT-derived anatomy during the procedure.

# Role of TEE and TTE: structural assessment, vegetation detection, hemodynamic quantification

Echocardiography remains indispensable in TAVI evaluation and management. While CT offers high-resolution structural detail, echocardiography—both transthoracic (TTE) and transesophageal (TEE)—offers dynamic, hemodynamic, and tissue-characterization information that cannot be obtained by CT alone. Each modality has strengths and limitations, and they are often complementary.

Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE): TTE is the first-line modality for screening and follow-up. It quantifies the severity of aortic stenosis (AS) and regurgitation (AR), evaluates left ventricular (LV) size and function, detects concomitant valvular disease, and screens for vegetations or thrombus if image quality permits. Key TTE measurements include peak aortic velocity, mean transvalvular gradient, and effective orifice area (EOA) by continuity equation. Diagnostic criteria for severe native AS include mean gradient ≥40 mmHg and peak velocity ≥4.0 m/s; however, low-flow, low-gradient AS with reduced ejection fraction or paradoxical low-flow with preserved EF require careful interpretation using dobutamine stress echocardiography or multimodality correlation. TTE is essential for baseline LV assessment, monitoring for procedural complications (e.g., pericardial effusion/tamponade), and for serial assessment of prosthetic valve function post-TAVI (mean gradient, Doppler velocity index, presence of paravalvular leak).

Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE): TEE offers higher spatial resolution for the aortic valve, root, and left atrium, and is particularly useful for detecting vegetations, prosthetic leaflet motion abnormalities, perivalvular abscess, and small paravalvular leaks. TEE is more sensitive than TTE for detection of infective endocarditis of prosthetic valves and for identification of small, mobile vegetations and periannular extension. During TAVI, TEE can be used intraprocedurally to guide transseptal puncture for mitral procedures, confirm valve position and function, and identify immediate complications; however, many centers perform TAVI under conscious sedation with fluoroscopic guidance and transthoracic imaging only, reserving TEE for complex cases.

Hemodynamic quantification and prosthetic valve assessment: Echocardiography—especially Doppler—remains the gold standard for functional assessment of valve gradients, effective orifice area, and quantification of regurgitation. For prosthetic valves, elevated gradients should be interpreted in the context of valve type, size, and expected transvalvular velocities. For example, small transcatheter valves in smaller annuli will exhibit higher normal gradients; comparison to expected norms and serial trends is important. Paravalvular regurgitation is best assessed with color Doppler, vena contracta, and quantitative techniques when possible; 3D TEE enhances localization and quantification of paravalvular leaks. Distinguish between transvalvular and paravalvular regurgitation as the management differs.

Vegetation detection and infection: TEE is preferred when prosthetic valve endocarditis is suspected because of higher sensitivity for vegetations, abscesses, and leaflet perforations. Typical echocardiographic criteria for infective endocarditis include oscillating intracardiac mass consistent with vegetation, abscess, new dehiscence of prosthetic valve, or new valvular regurgitation. CT can complement TEE by identifying perivalvular extension, pseudoaneurysms, or vascular septic emboli.

Integration with CT: Use echocardiographic assessment to confirm hemodynamic severity suggested by CT morphology. For example, severe leaflet calcification on CT accompanied by high gradients on echo supports the diagnosis of severe AS. Conversely, discordance—e.g., severe calcification with low gradients—warrants further hemodynamic and flow evaluation and may influence candidacy for intervention.

# 3D reconstructions, virtual valve implantation and simulation tools

Three-dimensional reconstructions and virtual implantation tools bridge the gap between static anatomy and procedural dynamics. Postprocessing of CT datasets enables segmentation, surface rendering, and simulation of device deployment. These technologies help predict frame–host interactions, paravalvular leak, coronary obstruction risk, and future coronary access.

3D reconstructions: Segmentation of the aortic root, annulus, leaflets, valve frames, and coronary ostia produces 3D surface models that can be manipulated to visualize relationships not apparent on 2D multiplanar reformats. 3D printing allows production of physical models for tactile assessment and procedural rehearsal, particularly for complex anatomy or training. Virtual reality and augmented reality platforms increasingly allow team-based preprocedural walkthroughs.

Virtual valve implantation: Software tools allow the operator to place virtual transcatheter valve models into the CT-derived anatomy at varying sizes and implantation depths. The simulation calculates projected contact points, frame expansion, and potential impingement on coronary ostia. These predictive tools often provide derived metrics such as predicted VTC, residual neo-sinus volumes, and estimated residual gradients based on effective orifice area predictions. Some platforms model interaction with prior surgical leaflets to simulate leaflet displacement in ViV scenarios.

Computational fluid dynamics (CFD) and finite element analysis: Advanced simulation can extend to hemodynamic modeling—estimating flow patterns, shear stress, and potential thrombogenic areas post-implantation. Finite element modeling predicts tissue strain and stress on the annulus and surrounding structures, which may inform risk of annular rupture in heavily calcified or small annuli. CFD and structural mechanics simulations require significant computational resources and expertise but can be invaluable in anatomically challenging or high-risk cases.

Use cases and clinical integration: 3D and simulation tools are most helpful when standard measurements produce borderline or discordant recommendations—for example, when annular dimensions straddle two valve sizes, when coronary obstruction risk is intermediate, or when ViV sizing must account for unpredictable expansion constrained by previous frames. These tools guide selection of valve type (balloon-expandable vs. self-expanding), size, implantation depth, need for BASILICA, or the advisability of ring fracture. They can also plan fluoroscopic angles, identify commissural alignment strategies to preserve coronary access, and help educate patients and the multidisciplinary team.

Validation and limitations: Simulation accuracy depends on the fidelity of anatomical segmentation, device modeling, assumptions about tissue properties, and prediction of tissue displacement, particularly of rigid or calcified leaflets. Validation studies show reasonable predictive value for coronary obstruction and annular interactions but are not perfect. Use simulation outputs as adjuncts to, not replacements for, clinical judgment and multidisciplinary consensus.

# Limitations and artifacts in CT and echocardiography for prosthetic valves

Awareness of modality-specific limitations and artifacts is essential to avoid misinterpretation in TAVI planning.

CT limitations and artifacts:

- Beam-hardening and metal artifact: Metallic stent frames and surgical prosthesis components create beam-hardening and streak artifacts that can obscure adjacent anatomy such as cusp leaflets or coronary ostia. Techniques to reduce artifacts include higher kVp settings, iterative metal artifact reduction algorithms, and dual-energy CT with virtual monoenergetic reconstructions. However, even with these methods, some detail may remain obscured.

- Temporal resolution: CT temporal resolution is finite and may not capture rapid leaflet motion; motion artifact can blur leaflet edges and affect measurements, particularly in tachycardia or arrhythmia. Retrospective gating and multiphase reconstructions mitigate but do not eliminate this limitation.

- Spatial resolution for thin structures: While CT offers high spatial resolution, thin tissue structures (small vegetations, thin flaps, or early endocarditic lesions) may be below the detection threshold compared with TEE. CT is better for calcification and gross structural relationships than for small mobile masses.

- Contrast opacification issues: Poor contrast opacification due to timing errors, cardiac output variations, or venous access limitations can yield nondiagnostic studies. Dense contrast in the LV cavity can create streak artifacts; optimizing injection protocols and acquisition timing mitigates this.

- Radiation and contrast exposure: Repeated CTs contribute cumulative radiation and use iodinated contrast, limiting feasibility in patients with renal dysfunction.

Echocardiography limitations and artifacts:

- Acoustic shadowing and reverberation: Prosthetic frames and calcifications produce acoustic shadowing that obscures leaflets or underlying structures. TTE often has limited windows in obese patients or those with pulmonary disease; TEE can overcome some of these limitations at the cost of invasiveness.

- Limited field of view and operator dependence: Echocardiography is operator-dependent and has limited visualization of the ascending aorta and peripheral vasculature. Quantification of paravalvular regurgitation by color Doppler is semi-quantitative and affected by beam alignment, multiple regurgitant jets, and prosthetic artifacts.

- Doppler angle dependence: Accurate Doppler gradients require optimal insonation angle; suboptimal alignment can underestimate velocities. For prosthetic valves, elevated gradients must be compared to expected norms for the valve type and size.

- Prosthetic valve material effects: Mechanical prostheses generally produce pronounced acoustic shadowing, and some prosthetic materials cause signal dropout that reduces sensitivity for vegetations compared with TEE.

Combined-modality considerations: No single modality suffices for comprehensive TAVI planning. Use CT for annular sizing, root anatomy, coronary heights and vascular access; use echocardiography to quantify hemodynamic severity, evaluate LV function, assess for vegetations, and monitor periprocedural hemodynamic complications. Be mindful that discordant findings (e.g., severe CT calcification but low gradients on echo) require integration of flow status, valve dynamics, and clinical context.

Artifacts specific to ViV: In valve-in-valve scenarios, overlapping metal frames from prior prostheses and the planned transcatheter valve produce complex artifacts on CT and shadowing on echo. Frame overlap may make it difficult to measure true internal diameters and leaflet positions; leveraging manufacturer dimensions, surgical records, and multiplanar inspection improve accuracy.

Conclusion

Imaging for TAVI planning is a multidisciplinary, multimodality endeavor that requires attention to technical detail, robust measurements, and clinical reasoning. MSCT provides spatially detailed, three-dimensional information on annulus size, leaflet morphology, sinuses, and coronary relationships critical for valve sizing and risk stratification—particularly for coronary obstruction and access planning. TTE and TEE complement CT by providing dynamic hemodynamic assessment, detection of vegetations and perivalvular complications, and intra-procedural guidance when needed. 3D reconstructions and simulation tools add predictive capability for valve-host interactions and coronary risk but must be used within the clinical context and with awareness of their limitations. Understanding artifacts and modality-specific constraints allows accurate interpretation, safer procedural planning, and improved outcomes in transcatheter valve interventions.

Introduction

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) has evolved from an alternative for inoperable patients to a mainstream therapy for severe aortic stenosis across risk spectra. Safe, reproducible results hinge not only on device design but on precise procedural technique: selecting and obtaining vascular access, choosing appropriate sheaths and wires, managing rapid ventricular pacing, deciding when and how to perform balloon valvuloplasty, and deploying the chosen prosthesis with attention to depth and commissural orientation. This chapter synthesizes the procedural principles and tactics that underpin successful TAVI, linking anatomy and device mechanics to intra-procedural decision-making and complication avoidance. It is written for trainees who must understand the why and how of routinely encountered maneuvers so they can apply them adaptively in the cath lab.

# Vascular access options: transfemoral, transradial, alternative routes

Transfemoral access is the default access route for contemporary TAVI and is preferred whenever iliofemoral anatomy permits safe passage of the required sheath and delivery system. The transfemoral route benefits from a relatively straightforward trajectory to the aortic valve, ease of percutaneous closure, and minimal invasiveness compared with surgical approaches. Pre-procedural contrast-enhanced CT angiography is essential to evaluate vessel diameter, tortuosity, calcification, and the presence of iliofemoral disease or prior stents. A minimal luminal diameter requirement depends on the device and sheath; modern low-profile systems often require sheath outer diameters in the range of 14–16 Fr, though some devices and sheathless techniques reduce this requirement. Heavy circumferential calcification, a severe arterial tortuosity with a sharp bend (>60–90 degrees), or a vessel diameter smaller than the required sheath diameter are common reasons to consider alternative access.

Transradial access is used in TAVI centers primarily for ancillary arterial access — for example, contralateral femoral angiography or as the access site for a pigtail catheter in the aortic root. Transradial use for the main TAVI delivery is not standard because of vessel size limitations; a reach into the root for secondary angiography or a second arterial access site is however commonly performed via the radial approach. Radial access decreases the risk of access-site bleeding for adjunctive catheters and facilitates earlier mobilization.

Alternative non-transfemoral routes exist when femoral access is unfeasible. These include transaxillary/subclavian, transcarotid, transcaval, direct transaortic, and transapical approaches. Transaxillary/subclavian access uses a more favorable straight-line trajectory to the aortic valve and can be achieved percutaneously or via surgical cut-down. Vessels should be assessed for diameter (>6–7 mm desirable), calcification, and the presence of atherosclerotic plaque that could embolize. Transcarotid access is an option particularly when axillary anatomy is unsuitable; it requires careful neurologic assessment and often a surgical cut-down with cerebral protection strategy and neuro-monitoring. Transcaval access is a specialized technique in which the iliac vein is intentionally crossed into the adjacent abdominal aorta and a closure device is used to seal the aorto-caval tract at the end of the case. It is a valuable salvage or planned strategy when arterial access options are exhausted, but requires specific experience and appropriate CT anatomy (favorable aorto-caval apposition and absence of interposed tissue or large retroperitoneal structures). Transaortic and transapical access are more invasive surgical approaches, typically reserved for when no percutaneous option is viable; they require sternotomy or mini-thoracotomy and are used less frequently in contemporary practice.

Choosing the access route requires integration of CT angiography, consideration of comorbid conditions (vascular disease, prior CABG with internal mammary grafts), and the operator’s institutional experience. For any chosen route, detailed knowledge of complication profiles—vascular injury, bleeding, stroke risk (especially with carotid), and local infections—is essential to informed consent and procedural planning.

# Sheath selection and percutaneous closure devices

Sheath selection is determined by the device profile, the size and compliance of the access vessel, and whether the operator will use a dedicated introducer or a sheathless approach. Sheath outer diameter, rather than the nominal French size, defines the minimum arterial lumen required. Balloon-expandable devices traditionally required larger sheaths than newer generation devices; for example, older Sapien delivery systems required 18–24 Fr sheaths, whereas Sapien 3 Ultra transcatheter heart valves now commonly use 14–16 Fr expandable sheaths. Self-expanding devices such as Medtronic Evolut use an introducer system with an integrated sheath and may be deployed through 14–20 Fr equivalent systems depending on the valve size and whether a sheathless technique is used. Operators must also consider sheath flexibility and trackability through tortuous vessels and the presence of hydrophilic coatings to reduce friction and vascular trauma.

Percutaneous closure is standard for transfemoral access in most centers. Suture-mediated devices (e.g., Perclose ProGlide) are widely used. The “pre-close” technique involves placing two or more sutures before upsizing to the large-bore sheath; upon sheath removal these sutures are tightened to achieve hemostasis. Proper suture orientation, typically at the 10 o’clock and 2 o’clock positions when accessing the right common femoral artery (or mirrored for left), reduces the risk of residual bleeding. Collagen-based closures such as MANTA provide a single-device solution that deploys a biocompatible plug to seal the arterial puncture and are attractive for rapid hemostasis with immediate device removal. The MANTA device is available in different sizes for particular puncture diameters and is less dependent on operator suture technique but requires accurate measurement of the puncture depth and can leave an intraluminal or extraluminal component that may be associated with hematoma or pseudoaneurysm. Surgical cut-down remains a bail-out or primary strategy when percutaneous closure is contraindicated or artery quality is poor.

Common complications associated with percutaneous closure include bleeding, hematoma, pseudoaneurysm, arterial occlusion or dissection, and failure requiring conversion to open surgical repair. Failure rates for percutaneous closure vary with operator experience and patient factors; an experienced team using pre-close with Perclose devices typically achieves primary hemostasis rates >90%, but repeated attempts or heavy calcification increase failure risk. Meticulous ultrasound-guided puncture of the common femoral artery at the optimal anterior wall location (below the inguinal ligament and above the bifurcation) reduces complication rates. Intra-procedural imaging with fluoroscopic guidance using the femoral head as a landmark, and sometimes roadmapping with contrast, supports accurate arterial entry.

# Use of rapid ventricular pacing and its indications

Rapid ventricular pacing (RVP) transiently reduces cardiac output and systemic blood pressure by inducing ventricular tachycardia at high rates (commonly 160–220 beats per minute), thereby reducing cardiac motion and transvalvular flow. RVP is used principally to facilitate predictable balloon inflation during balloon aortic valvuloplasty and secure positioning and expansion of balloon-expandable transcatheter valves. By lowering ejection and stroke volume, RVP minimizes balloon displacement and reduces the force of blood flow against the balloon or valve during expansion, enabling controlled deployment.

RVP can be delivered via a temporary pacing lead positioned in the right ventricle through a venous access (typically femoral or jugular), or less commonly by rapid pacing through an LV support guidewire (if that wire can transmit sufficient current). The temporary transvenous RV lead remains the most reliable method, allowing stable capture and adjustable pacing output. Typical pacing rates range between 160 and 220 bpm; operators usually titrate the rate to achieve near–complete loss of arterial pressure pulsatility (systolic blood pressure typically falls below 50 mmHg), sufficient to immobilize the balloon/valve but minimize duration of profound hypotension. The typical RVP durations are brief—commonly 10–15 seconds per inflation—but multiple or prolonged episodes increase the risk of myocardial ischemia, arrhythmia, or hemodynamic collapse, especially in patients with limited left ventricular reserve.

Indications for RVP in TAVI include pre-dilation balloon aortic valvuloplasty to fracture calcific leaflets and facilitate valve passage, balloon inflation for balloon-expandable valve deployment, and post-dilation when further expansion is required to correct device under-expansion or paravalvular regurgitation. Some self-expanding valve deployments avoid RVP because self-expanding frames foreshorten and expand slowly; nonetheless, RVP can be used during critical phases such as rapid release of a recapturable frame in some cases or when controlled hemodynamics are desired. Contraindications to RVP include patients with unstable hemodynamics who cannot tolerate any drop in blood pressure, though in many such patients careful planning and short-duration RVP may still be possible with inotropic support and temporary pacing in place.

Complications of RVP include sustained ventricular arrhythmias, inability to achieve capture (sometimes due to poor lead contact), puncture-related venous complications, and ischemic myocardial injury if prolonged hypotension occurs. Operators should ensure immediate availability of defibrillation, consider backup ventricular pacing configurations, and limit the number and duration of RVP episodes. Hemodynamic monitoring of arterial pressure during pacing assures adequacy of suppression of pulsatility and guides timing of balloon inflation or valve release.

# Balloon valvuloplasty (pre- and post-dilation): indications and sizing

Balloon aortic valvuloplasty (BAV) has evolved from a stand-alone palliation to a strategic adjunct of TAVI. Two contexts are important: pre-dilation (balloon inflation before valve delivery) and post-dilation (balloon inflation after valve deployment).

Pre-dilation is performed to increase leaflet mobility, fracture or mobilize bulky calcific nodules, and facilitate passage and expansion of the transcatheter valve. Indications for routine pre-dilation have declined with improvements in delivery systems, but pre-dilation remains valuable in specific scenarios: severely calcified native valves, very tight stenosis with small valve area that may impede device crossing, heavily asymmetric calcification or bulky leaflet nodularity (e.g., in bicuspid valves), and when operator anticipates difficulty crossing or expanding the device. Pre-dilation can also serve as a hemodynamic test to assess the patient’s tolerance of temporary afterload reduction.

Post-dilation is used when the implanted valve exhibits under-expansion, high transvalvular gradients, or significant paravalvular regurgitation. Post-dilation can improve leaflet coaptation and reduce paravalvular leak by expanding the frame against the annulus and remodeling any incomplete apposition. However, post-dilation carries increased risk of annular injury or rupture, coronary obstruction (rare), cerebrovascular embolization, and device migration if the balloon forces shift the valve. The decision to post-dilate should weigh the severity of residual regurgitation or gradient against these risks.

Balloon sizing is based on accurate annular measurements obtained from ECG-gated CT. The procedural principle is that the balloon diameter should approximate the annular dimension but be selected conservatively relative to the prosthesis and annular calcification. For pre-dilation, operators often choose a balloon equal to or slightly smaller than the annulus diameter (e.g., 80–100% of the annular mean diameter) to fracture commissural calcification without excessive radial force. For example, with an annulus mean diameter of 23 mm, a pre-dilation balloon of 20–23 mm may be used. For post-dilation, balloon sizing often mirrors prosthesis size recommendations: operators may use the same nominal diameter as the target valve size or modestly oversize (but generally avoid aggressive oversizing) to optimize frame expansion. Precise recommendations vary by device; manufacturers’ instructions and institutional experience inform choices.

Careful preparation for valvuloplasty includes rapid pacing set-up, hemodynamic monitoring, and readiness to manage complications such as severe aortic regurgitation, ventricular arrhythmias, or annular rupture. Balloon inflation should be of short duration (typically 5–10 seconds), performed under rapid pacing as indicated, and with careful fluoroscopic and hemodynamic observation. Adjunctive cerebral protection strategies may be considered in patients at particularly high risk of embolic stroke from heavy calcific burden.

# Guidewire selection and positioning (eg, LV support wires)

Guidewire choice and positioning are critical to provide stable support for device delivery and to minimize the risk of ventricular injury. Guidewires used during TAVI differ by stiffness and tip shape. Commonly used LV support wires include the Safari, Safari2, Amplatz Super Stiff, Confida, and others specifically designed with a flexible curved tip and a stiff shaft. LV-support wires typically have a pre-shaped atraumatic curve at the tip to rest against the LV apex, providing coaxial alignment and stability during advancement and deployment of large devices.

The selection principle is to balance support and safety. A stiff shaft confers support for tracking the large-bore delivery system while a soft or pre-shaped atraumatic tip minimizes the risk of LV perforation. For example, the Safari wire has a nitinol curved atraumatic tip and a variable stiffness body to provide central support with less apex trauma. Amplatz Super Stiff wires have more stiffness and risk of perforation and are therefore used cautiously or with a floppy tip configuration. Some operators prefer placing an LV guidewire with a J-tip in the aortic valve cusp and positioning an extra-stiff shaft in the mid-LV cavity, while keeping a long soft polymer tip to reduce endocardial trauma.

Positioning the wire in the mid-to-apical LV cavity provides the best balance of support, but the wire should not abut the lateral LV wall or papillary muscles to avoid perforation. Continuous fluoroscopic guidance in orthogonal views helps confirm that the wire tip is in an acceptable midline apical location with gentle curves rather than a sharp, penetrating tip. When using an LV support wire as a rapid pacing conductor, operators must ensure the wire can reliably conduct pacing energy; otherwise, a separate RV pacing lead should be used.

Wire-related complications include LV perforation with tamponade, wire entrapment, and embolization. Careful manipulation, avoidance of forceful advancement, and using wires with appropriate tip softness reduce these risks. If using a stiff wire to support a self-expanding valve, consider using an extra-stiff wire only after careful crossing of the valve and confirmation of tip position, and ensure there is an immediate plan for pericardial drainage should perforation occur.

# Deployment techniques for self-expanding vs balloon-expandable valves

Deployment strategies differ between valve platforms because of differences in frame mechanics, foreshortening characteristics, and ability to recapture or reposition.

Balloon-expandable valves (e.g., Edwards Sapien family) are mounted on a balloon and require rapid inflation to expand the frame against the annulus. Deployment steps include precise positioning with fluoroscopic landmarks (cusp overlap and annular landing zone), securing rapid pacing to reduce ejection, and inflating the balloon to nominal volume for the selected valve size. Over- or underfilling the balloon can respectively oversize or underexpand the valve. Operators must measure the annulus via CT and choose the valve size accordingly; common implantation depths aim for 3–5 mm ventricular implantation for optimal conduction and paravalvular leak profile, but depth varies with anatomy and surgical preferences. Because balloon-expandable valves deploy rapidly and are not recapturable once fully expanded, accurate initial positioning is critical. Strategies to improve placement accuracy include using the cusp-overlap fluoroscopic view (to minimize parallax and better visualize the non-coronary cusp) and slow, controlled inflation during complete loss of pulsatility.

Self-expanding valves (e.g., Medtronic Evolut family) use a nitinol frame that expands gradually and often can be partially deployed, repositioned, or recaptured depending on the generation. Deployment typically begins with initial release of the frame to a point where the inflow portion is anchored, and then slow continued release allowing the valve to assume its final geometry. Implantation depth is targeted based on CT measurements and membranous septum height; for self-expanding valves, a slightly higher (aortic) implantation depth may reduce conduction disturbance risk but increases the risk of paravalvular regurgitation or valve embolization. Operators use controlled pull-back of the delivery catheter while maintaining tension to avoid inadvertent upward migration. For devices that allow repositioning, operators can recapture partially deployed frames and reposition prior to full release. Because self-expanding valves exert continuous outward radial force as they nitinol-heat-set to their full diameter, accurate initial centering and orientation are important.

Key deployment considerations common to both types include confirming coronary ostial distance to avoid obstruction, ensuring a coaxial trajectory to the annulus to avoid asymmetric expansion, monitoring for conduction disturbances, and having bailout strategies prepared (embolization management tools, valve-in-valve options, or emergency surgery availability). Imaging adjuncts — transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) or intracardiac echo in some centers — assist in fine-tuning positioning and assessing immediate valve function. Importantly, the choice between valve types often reflects patient anatomy (e.g., bicuspid valves, annular size), operator experience, and priorities such as the ability to recapture.

# Techniques for commissural alignment during deployment

Commissural alignment of the transcatheter valve seeks to align the prosthetic commissures with the native valve commissures to preserve coronary access, facilitate future coronary interventions, and potentially improve valve hemodynamics. Poor alignment can obscure coronary ostia behind frame struts and complicate future coronary cannulation.

Pre-procedural planning using ECG-gated CT is essential. The CT allows identification of the optimal fluoroscopic projection(s) (cusp-overlap view and a true en-face or short-axis view) and the native commissural locations relative to identifiable anatomical landmarks such as coronary ostia and the sinotubular junction. Different valve platforms have specific orientation markers on the delivery system or valve frame that can be used to achieve predictable commissural alignment. For example, some devices have an asymmetrical marker or radiopaque tab that corresponds to a commissural post. Operators can use the relationship between this marker in the catheter hub and a reference orientation during system insertion to rotate the valve to a pre-specified orientation.

Techniques used to achieve alignment include rotating the delivery system externally before advancing the valve across the iliofemoral system in a reproducible manner derived from CT-based simulation. Because the delivery catheter often rotates as it traverses tortuous vasculature, operators must anticipate and compensate for predictable rotations: e.g., when advancing through the iliofemoral axis, a known amount of torque may be transferred, so initial orientation is adjusted accordingly. Fluoroscopic checks in orthogonal views help refine the rotation as the valve approaches the annulus.

During deployment, maintaining slow, controlled release with continuous observation of frame rotation relative to native cusps facilitates final adjustments; for repositionable valves, partial recapture allows reorientation if alignment is suboptimal. Commissural alignment is intrinsically more straightforward with newer valve designs that incorporate alignment-friendly markers and delivery catheters engineered to preserve orientation. Nonetheless, in many cases perfect alignment is not achievable; the practical goal is to avoid placing commissural posts directly in front of coronary ostia and to orient frames to maximize future coronary access.

Clinical implications of commissural alignment extend beyond immediate procedure success. Patients with coronary artery disease or those likely to require future coronary access (younger patients, those with complex coronary disease) benefit most from deliberate alignment strategies. Finally, documenting orientation in the procedure note and implant imaging facilitates future coronary procedures by informing operators of frame orientation and expected strut locations.

Conclusion

Procedural success in TAVI depends on deliberate planning and meticulous execution of technical steps: selecting the optimal vascular access, matching sheath and closure strategies to vascular anatomy, using rapid pacing judiciously, deciding when to pre- or post-dilate, choosing and positioning supportive guidewires, and tailoring deployment techniques to the chosen prosthesis with attention to commissural alignment. Throughout, integration of high-quality pre-procedural CT, intra-procedural fluoroscopy and echocardiography, and a team-based approach to complications are essential. Mastery of these techniques, together with an understanding of the underlying device mechanics and patient-specific anatomy, enables safe expansion of TAVI indications and the maintenance of excellent short- and long-term outcomes.

Introduction

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) and valve‑in‑valve (ViV) procedures have transformed the management of aortic stenosis and failing surgical bioprostheses. Despite their overall safety, one of the most catastrophic and immediately life‑threatening complications is coronary obstruction, which can cause acute myocardial ischemia, hemodynamic collapse, and death if not anticipated and managed promptly. This chapter reviews the mechanisms by which coronary obstruction occurs in native and prosthetic valve settings, defines the anatomic predictors that increase risk, explains CT‑based preprocedural risk stratification metrics, and describes prophylactic and rescue strategies including coronary protection techniques, the chimney stent approach, leaflet laceration (BASILICA), and immediate management when coronary compromise occurs. The focus is on integrating pathophysiology, imaging interpretation, and procedural decision‑making to prevent and treat coronary obstruction during TAVI/ViV.

# Pathophysiology of coronary obstruction in native and prosthetic valve settings

Coronary obstruction during TAVI results when the ostium of a coronary artery is acutely occluded or its effective flow lumen is severely compromised at the time of valve deployment. Mechanisms differ between native valve TAVI and ViV procedures but share a common final pathway—mechanical impediment of coronary ostial flow producing acute ischemia.

In native aortic stenosis, obstruction typically arises when bulky native leaflets (often heavily calcified) are displaced after transcatheter heart valve (THV) deployment. A self‑ or balloon‑expanded THV displaces the diseased aortic leaflets outward toward the sinuses of Valsalva and the coronary ostia. If the displaced leaflet tissue, or a calcific nodule within it, lies close to or above the coronary ostium, it can cover or impinge on the ostium or create an external compression of the sinotubular junction (STJ) region that dynamically reduces the coronary inflow or causes ostial occlusion.

In ViV procedures, risk is often higher because the previously implanted surgical bioprosthesis provides a rigid frame and bioprosthetic leaflets that can be displaced in a predictable but potentially dangerous trajectory. Leaflets of surgical valves are typically mounted externally to the valve frame and, when a THV is implanted inside (ViV), the surgical leaflets are pushed outwards between the surgical frame and the aortic wall. This narrows the sinus and can position leaflet tissue directly across coronary ostia. Supra‑annular surgical valves (e.g., stented valves with tall leaflets) and stentless or stented valves with externally mounted leaflets confer increased risk because leaflet cusps are located higher relative to the coronary ostia.

Another mechanism is external compression of the coronary ostium by the THV frame itself, particularly with large‑diameter THVs or tall frames that extend above the STJ. In the presence of narrow sinuses or low ostial heights, the frame can physically contact or limit expansion of the sinus, compromising flow into the coronary sinus and ostium. Additionally, displaced leaflet tissue can be trapped between the THV frame and aortic wall, forming a flap that may impinge on the ostium intermittently or persistently, with dynamic effects during the cardiac cycle.

Hemodynamically, acute coronary occlusion abolishes antegrade flow into the downstream coronary circulation, producing myocardial ischemia according to the territory supplied and the degree/duration of occlusion. If the obstruction is complete and prolonged, it leads to arrhythmia, pump failure, and rapid hemodynamic collapse. Partial obstruction or severe reduction in coronary flow may manifest as ST changes, hemodynamic instability, or isolated biomarker elevation. Because TAVI patients often have limited coronary reserve and multiple comorbidities, even partial reductions in flow can have clinically significant consequences.

# Anatomic predictors: low coronary heights, small sinuses, narrow VTC

The risk of coronary obstruction is fundamentally anatomical. Key anatomic predictors include coronary ostial height above the annular plane, sinus of Valsalva (SOV) or sinus diameter, virtual transcatheter valve to coronary (VTC) distance, leaflet length and bulk, and the characteristics of prior surgical bioprostheses (in ViV).

Coronary height is measured from the basal annular plane to the coronary ostium. Lower coronary heights increase the chance that displaced native or prosthetic leaflets will reach the ostium. Historically, a coronary height ≤10–12 mm has been widely accepted as increased risk. Many centers use a cutoff of <10 mm as high risk and 10–12 mm as intermediate risk; risk increases as height decreases.

Sinus of Valsalva diameter: Small sinuses limit the space available for displaced leaflets and the THV frame. A small sinus (often cited as SOV diameter <30 mm) is associated with higher obstruction risk. If the sinus cannot accommodate the displaced tissue, leaflets are more likely to be forced directly toward the coronary ostia.

Virtual transcatheter valve to coronary (VTC) distance: This CT‑derived metric estimates the post‑implant distance between the anticipated outer surface of the THV skirt/leaflet complex and the coronary ostium. VTC integrates annular sizing, expected THV position, leaflet length, and coronary location to predict residual clearance. A small VTC indicates that the leaflets (or THV skirt) will be very close to or touching the coronary ostium after implantation. Cutoffs differ slightly in literature, but a VTC <4 mm is generally considered high risk in ViV procedures; some centers use <3–4 mm as the critical threshold. In native TAVI, a similar concept, the virtual transcatheter valve to coronary (virtual THV to coronaries) distance, is used with similar thresholds.

Leaflet length and bulk are particularly relevant in ViV. The length of the surgical bioprosthetic leaflets relative to coronary height may determine whether leaflet tissue will extend above the coronary ostium when displaced. Externally mounted leaflets and stented bioprostheses with tall leaflets (e.g., Mitroflow, Trifecta) are notorious for high ViV obstruction rates.

Sinotubular junction and ascending aortic dimensions: A narrow STJ or small ascending aorta restricts space for leaflet displacement and THV expansion, contributing to impingement at the coronary level. When STJ diameter is small (often <30–35 mm), the risk rises.

Other predictors: Dense leaflet calcification near the commissures or coronary cusps can create bulky nodules that, when displaced, are more likely to obstruct. Orientation of commissures and relation of surgical valve posts to coronary ostia can also affect risk.

Taken together, no single metric is perfectly predictive; risk is assessed by integrating coronary height, sinus dimensions, VTC, leaflet anatomy, and valve type.

# Preprocedural risk stratification using CT-based metrics

Computed tomography angiography (CTA) of the aortic root is the cornerstone of preprocedural risk assessment for coronary obstruction. Multidetector CT provides high‑resolution, gated images that permit precise measurement of annular anatomy, coronary heights, sinus dimensions, leaflet morphology, and the predicted interaction between an implanted THV and the coronary ostia.

Measurements performed on ECG‑gated contrast CT include:

- Annular plane delineation: define the basal annulus by the hinge points of the native leaflets or the sewing ring of the prosthetic valve.
- Coronary ostial height: perpendicular distance from the basal annular plane to the ostium of each coronary artery. Record left main and right coronary ostial heights separately. Standard thresholds: <10 mm high risk; 10–12 mm borderline.
- Sinus of Valsalva diameter: maximal orthogonal diameter measured at the mid‑sinus level. Diameter <30 mm often considered small.
- Sinotubular junction (STJ) diameter and height: small or low STJ increases risk.
- Leaflet length: measure the length of native or prosthetic leaflets from hinge to free edge; if leaflet length exceeds coronary height, the leaflet can reach the ostium.
- Coronary ostial orientation and takeoff angle: acute takeoff or ostia located near commissural posts elevate risk.
- Virtual transcatheter valve to coronary (VTC) distance: simulate the THV by reconstructing the intended THV frame and projecting its position relative to the coronary ostium. VTC may be calculated by creating a virtual cylinder representing the deployed THV and measuring the shortest distance from the outer aspect of this cylinder (and the expected displaced leaflet surface) to the coronary ostium. In ViV, use the inner diameter of the surgical valve and simulate THV expansion within that frame. A VTC <4 mm (or <3 mm in some series) carries high risk.

Software tools with dedicated TAVI planning modules facilitate these simulations and permit three‑dimensional virtual device implantation. The evaluation should be performed in both systolic and diastolic phases if possible, because leaflet position and coronary relations may shift dynamically. Some centers also use CT to simulate the potential position of displaced leaflets and to visualize whether the coronary ostium will be covered by such tissue.

Risk stratification is practical rather than binary. Patients are typically categorized as low, intermediate, or high risk of coronary obstruction based on integrated CT findings, valve type, and other clinical factors. For example, a patient with left main height 8 mm, SOV diameter 25 mm, and VTC 2–3 mm would be designated high risk and considered for preventative strategies. Conversely, a patient with 14 mm coronary height and broad sinuses would be low risk and likely proceed without prophylactic measures.

Importantly, CT assessment must be individualized for left and right coronaries since anatomy and risk often differ; left main obstruction carries greater hemodynamic consequences due to the larger myocardial territory supplied.

# Coronary protection techniques: guidewire protection, pre-positioned balloons/stents (chimney technique)

When preprocedural assessment identifies intermediate or high risk of obstruction, procedural coronary protection strategies can be employed. These can be prophylactic (intended to prevent obstruction during deployment) or elective (standby for immediate rescue). The principal approaches are (1) guidewire protection only, (2) protection with a pre‑positioned balloon, and (3) protection with a pre‑positioned stent (chimney technique) ready for immediate deployment.

Guidewire protection: This simplest form involves placing a coronary guidewire across the ostium into the distal coronary artery before THV deployment. The wire provides rapid access to the coronary artery if an obstruction occurs, facilitating immediate balloon angioplasty or stent delivery without the delay of wiring a collapsed ostium. Guidewire protection is low risk and is commonly used when CT indicates moderate risk. It does not itself prevent obstruction but expedites rescue.

Pre‑positioned balloon: An angioplasty balloon is advanced and positioned across the coronary ostium (sometimes partially in the proximal artery) over the guidewire and kept uninflated during THV deployment. If obstruction occurs, the balloon can be immediately inflated to attempt to restore lumen patency by displacing obstructing leaflet tissue outward. Balloon inflation can transiently open flow and facilitate subsequent stent delivery. Pre‑positioned ballooning can also be performed prophylactically during THV deployment in a protective “parking” position; however, balloon inflation at the time of THV deployment is usually a rescue maneuver.

Pre‑positioned stent (chimney technique): In this technique, a coronary guidewire is placed and a balloon‑mounted or balloon‑expandable coronary stent is advanced and positioned with its proximal portion protruding into the aortic root, just outside the coronary ostium. The stent is left undeployed while the THV is implanted. If obstruction occurs or is impending (visualized angiographically or by loss of flow), the stent is deployed and expanded, creating a channel between the coronary ostium and aortic lumen that runs alongside the THV frame—hence the “chimney” moniker. The chimney stent protrudes into the aorta beyond the THV skirt, preventing leaflet tissue from sealing the ostium. The technique can be employed prophylactically (deploy immediately after THV) or as salvage if obstruction appears.

Advantages of chimney stenting include immediate restoration of flow when obstruction occurs and potential avoidance of emergency surgery. Disadvantages include permanent placement of a stent that protrudes into the aorta (potential for thrombosis and challenges to future coronary access), risk of coronary dissection or malapposition, and potential for interaction between the stent and THV frame causing malexpansion or stent deformation. Long‑term outcomes of chimney stents after TAVI are not fully defined, and dual antiplatelet therapy is often employed postprocedure per operator preference and bleeding risk.

Choice of protection strategy depends on the perceived risk and the specific anatomy. For high‑risk ViV cases with very small VTC or extremely low coronary height, a chimney stent or BASILICA may be preferred. For moderate risk, wire or balloon protection with readiness to convert to chimney stenting is common.

# Leaflet laceration (BASILICA): principles, indications and technical steps

Principles: The Bioprosthetic or Native Aortic Scallop Intentional Laceration to prevent Iatrogenic Coronary Artery obstruction (BASILICA) is a preemptive transcatheter technique that lacerates the native or bioprosthetic leaflet that would otherwise be displaced over a coronary ostium during TAVI. By splitting the leaflet down its midline toward the surgical hinge, the two resulting flaps splay outward upon THV deployment and form a pair of halves that do not collectively obstruct the coronary ostium, thus preserving coronary flow. BASILICA converts a single large continuous leaflet flap that can seal the ostium into two smaller flaps that leave a channel for the coronary.

Indications: BASILICA is indicated for patients with high predicted risk of coronary obstruction where other strategies are insufficient or undesirable. Typical scenarios include ViV with externally mounted or tall leaflets and VTC predicted to be very small, native valve TAVI with low coronary height and bulky leaflets, or cases where chimney stenting is considered suboptimal due to concerns about future coronary access or stent thrombosis. BASILICA is most commonly applied prophylactically rather than as a rescue maneuver; performing it electively before THV deployment avoids hemodynamic deterioration.

Contraindications include inability to safely access the targeted leaflet, unfavorable leaflet anatomy (e.g., heavily scarred or calcified leaflet that resists laceration), severe coagulopathy, or lack of operator expertise/resources.

Technical steps:

1. Planning: Detailed CT planning identifies target leaflet(s) for laceration (usually the leaflet facing the threatened coronary ostium, often the left coronary cusp). Determine the optimal cusp, crossing and target points, and catheter/guidewire angles needed.

2. Vascular access: Obtain arterial and venous access per institutional practice. BASILICA is typically performed via transfemoral arterial access for delivery of THV components and transseptal or contralateral arterial access for snare retrieval, depending on operator technique. A coronary guiding catheter is positioned in the target coronary artery in many approaches to protect the coronary and to act as an external snare.

3. Leaflet traversal: Using a guiding catheter (e.g., an AL or multipurpose shape), an electrified guidewire assembly is directed to the target cusp and used to traverse the leaflet from the aortic side into the left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) or across the prosthetic leaflet. Traversal is performed under fluoroscopic and echocardiographic guidance. Electrified guidewires (e.g., Astato XS with insulation) are energized briefly to assist passage through leaflet tissue.

4. Creation of a snare loop: The traversing wire is captured in the LVOT or opposing arterial access with a snare or through a retrograde catheter, creating a rail across the leaflet. The wire is externalized so it forms a loop that passes through the leaflet.

5. Laceration: The midportion of the externalized wire loop is insulated except for a short exposed segment positioned along the leaflet midline. Controlled radiofrequency energy (or electrocautery) is delivered to the exposed segment while applying tension to the wire loop. The energized wire cuts the leaflet from the free edge toward the base, creating a longitudinal split. Continuous imaging confirms the laceration; echocardiography can visualize leaflet mobility.

6. Confirmation and THV deployment: After successful laceration, the THV is implanted. The lacerated leaflet halves splay apart during valve expansion, allowing coronary flow. Choice and timing of antithrombotic therapy and hemodynamic support follow institutional protocols.

Technical considerations: Accurate alignment of the traversal trajectory to the leaflet midline is critical to ensure an effective central laceration. Care must be taken to avoid cutting adjacent structures, such as the aortic wall, commissures, or conduction system. Adequate anticoagulation is maintained. Operators require training and experience; BASILICA is performed in centers with multidisciplinary TAVI teams.

Outcomes and limitations: Studies show that BASILICA reduces the incidence of coronary obstruction in high‑risk patients compared with historical controls and allows many patients to undergo TAVI who would otherwise be inoperable. However, it is not 100% protective—anatomic variation, incomplete laceration, or extensive calcification can limit effectiveness. Potential complications include leaflet avulsion, stroke from embolized tissue, perforation, or conduction disturbances. Long‑term data are still evolving.

# Rescue strategies and immediate management of coronary compromise

Despite careful planning, coronary obstruction may occur acutely during THV deployment. Rapid recognition and immediate action are essential to reduce myocardial injury and improve survival.

Recognition: Suspect coronary compromise if there is sudden hypotension, bradycardia or ventricular arrhythmia, ST‑segment elevation on ECG (especially in leads corresponding to involved territory), new regional wall motion abnormalities on echocardiography, or angiographic evidence of lost coronary flow. Intra‑procedural aortography and selective coronary angiography are definitive for diagnosis. Continuous hemodynamic and ECG monitoring during deployment facilitate early detection.

Initial immediate steps:

1. Maintain vascular access: Ensure guidewires and catheters are in place and ready. If a coronary guidewire or pre‑positioned balloon/stent is present, use it without delay. If not, rapid wiring of the coronary should be attempted immediately, but this can be difficult if the ostium is sealed.

2. Hemodynamic support: Begin or escalate hemodynamic support as needed. Options include volume resuscitation if appropriate, vasopressors, inotropes, and mechanical circulatory support. Rapid deployment of percutaneous devices such as intra‑aortic balloon pump (IABP), Impella, or venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA‑ECMO) may be lifesaving in profound cardiogenic shock or during prolonged resuscitation. Selection depends on institutional capability and patient condition.

3. Coronary reperfusion—preferred rescue strategies:

- If a pre‑positioned stent is available (chimney strategy), deploy the stent promptly to create a channel and restore flow. Post‑dilate as needed to optimize expansion.
- If a pre‑positioned balloon is in place, inflate the balloon to reestablish partial flow and facilitate stent delivery. Balloon inflation can temporarily open the ostium and may be lifesaving by restoring some perfusion.
- If only a guidewire is present, rapid balloon angioplasty followed by stent implantation is attempted. Use of aspiration thrombectomy is appropriate if thrombus is suspected but is rarely the primary maneuver in this setting.
- If no coronary access exists and ostial sealing prevents wiring, consider immediate snaring or externalization techniques to permit access. In extreme cases, emergency cardiac surgery (coronary bypass or surgical removal of the THV) may be required if percutaneous options fail and the patient remains salvageable.

4. Pharmacologic adjuncts: Administer antiplatelet and anticoagulant therapy as appropriate, balancing the need to prevent stent thrombosis with bleeding risk. Intracoronary vasodilators (nitroprusside, nitroglycerin) may be used if vasospasm contributes. Glycoprotein IIb/IIIa inhibitors can be considered if thrombotic occlusion is suspected, but caution is needed.

5. Imaging and confirmation: After reperfusion maneuvers, perform repeat angiography to confirm restoration of TIMI 3 flow when possible and echocardiography to assess ventricular function. Monitor biomarkers and ECG evolution.

Rescue when BASILICA was planned but fails: If BASILICA was attempted and incomplete laceration occurs with subsequent obstruction, proceed with chimney stenting or emergent wiring and stenting as above. Hemodynamic support is crucial because laceration attempts may prolong procedure time.

Postprocedural considerations: Patients who suffered coronary compromise require monitoring for myocardial injury (serial troponin, ECG, echo), management of arrhythmias, and optimization of heart failure therapy if myocardial dysfunction occurred. Antiplatelet therapy should be tailored to the stent strategy used and bleeding risk. Discuss long‑term implications: chimney stents may complicate future coronary access and influence antiplatelet duration decisions.

Prevention-first approach: Because rescue is complex and outcomes worsen with delay and greater myocardial injury, current best practice emphasizes prevention through CT‑guided risk stratification and the use of prophylactic strategies (BASILICA, pre‑positioned stent, or guidewire protection) in patients judged to be at elevated risk.

Conclusion

Coronary obstruction during TAVI and ViV is a rare but severe complication driven by the interaction of valve leaflets, THV frame, and patient anatomy. Preprocedural CT assessment—measuring coronary height, sinus dimensions, leaflet length, and calculating VTC—is essential to stratify risk. When significant risk is identified, prophylactic or standby coronary protection strategies (wire, balloon, chimney stent) and leaflet laceration (BASILICA) are effective tools to preserve coronary flow. Operators must be prepared with rapid rescue algorithms, including immediate coronary wiring, ballooning/stenting, and escalation to mechanical circulatory support or surgery when required. Multidisciplinary planning, meticulous imaging, and procedural readiness are key to preventing catastrophic outcomes.

Introduction

Valve-in-valve (ViV) transcatheter valve implantation has become an established option for patients with failing surgical bioprosthetic heart valves who are at elevated surgical risk. However, implanting a new transcatheter heart valve within a stenotic or small surgical bioprosthesis can result in patient–prosthesis mismatch, elevated transvalvular gradients, and suboptimal hemodynamics. Two interventional strategies have emerged to address these limitations: bioprosthetic valve fracture (BVF), which uses high-pressure balloon expansion to rupture a rigid sewing ring or stent frame of the surgical valve to enlarge the effective orifice area; and leaflet modification techniques such as intentional leaflet laceration (commonly referred to as BASILICA, or Bioprosthetic or Native Aortic Scallop Intentional Laceration to prevent Iatrogenic Coronary Artery obstruction), which split native or prosthetic leaflets to prevent coronary obstruction and to improve leaflet mobility. This chapter reviews the principles, mechanisms, procedural considerations, hemodynamic effects, and risks of BVF and leaflet modification, and summarizes the clinical evidence base, outcomes, and limitations for their use in repeat valve interventions.

# Principles of bioprosthetic valve fracture and applicable valve types

Bioprosthetic valve fracture is based on the mechanical principle that many surgical bioprosthetic valves have a rigid supporting structure—typically a polymeric or metallic stent frame, or a fibrous sewing ring—that constrains the internal diameter of the valve. By delivering a noncompliant high-pressure balloon within the surgical valve and inflating progressively to high pressures, the rigid frame or ring can be fractured, yielding an abrupt increase in internal diameter and effective orifice area. The desired outcome is to reduce transvalvular gradients and relieve patient–prosthesis mismatch when a transcatheter valve is implanted inside the surgical valve.

Not all surgical bioprostheses are amenable to BVF. The technique is most successful with valves that have readily fractureable components such as polyethylene terephthalate (Dacron)-reinforced sewing rings or certain polymer or thermoplastic stent structures. Examples of surgical valves that have been reported to fracture reliably include some models of the Carpentier-Edwards Perimount series and similar stented tissue valves with nonmetallic stent posts or sewing rings. Conversely, valves with fully metallic sewing rings or rigid metallic stent frames, such as certain older stented or stentless surgical valves with substantial metal reinforcement, often cannot be fractured. The material properties of the supporting frame (elastic modulus, yield strength, and fracture toughness) and its geometry dictate whether and how the structure will fail under radial load. When fracture occurs, it typically results in loss of structural integrity at one or more points around the ring, with an immediate and measurable increase in inner diameter.

Recognition of valve type prior to the procedure is critical. Pre-procedural multimodality imaging, including computed tomography and review of operative records or valve identification charts, is used to determine the valve model and the likelihood of successful BVF. The valve label, model number, and known manufacturer-specific behavior in response to high-pressure dilation should guide procedural planning. Operators must also consider the original labelled internal diameter of the surgical valve and the relation between that diameter and the intended transcatheter valve size, since BVF is most helpful when the predicted effective orifice without fracture would be insufficient or associated with high gradients.

# Balloon selection, inflation pressures and timing relative to valve deployment

Balloon selection and inflation strategy are central to BVF. The procedure employs noncompliant, high-pressure balloons engineered to resist longitudinal expansion while transmitting radial force to the target. Valves are sized using the labelled internal diameter of the surgical valve and measured internal diameters on computed tomography. Balloons are chosen to match or slightly exceed the true annular or valve internal diameter in order to apply sufficient radial stress to fracture the ring. Operators typically select balloon diameters that range from equal to the labelled internal diameter up to 1–2 mm larger, balancing the need for effective fracture against the risk of oversizing and annular injury.

Inflation pressures required to achieve fracture vary by valve type and balloon size but are substantially higher than pressures used for routine valvuloplasty. Reported fracture pressures commonly fall in the range of $12$–$24$ atmospheres (atm), with many fractures achieved between $12$ and $20$ atm. The choice of balloon brand and rated burst pressure is important: noncompliant balloons such as the Atlas Gold or True Dilatation balloons with rated burst pressures exceeding the target inflation pressure are typically used. The fracturing event is often accompanied by an audible “pop” and a sudden drop in inflation pressure or balloon waist release, and may be visible fluoroscopically as a change in the contour of the frame. Real-time hemodynamic monitoring will often demonstrate an immediate improvement in transvalvular gradient after fracture.

The timing of BVF relative to transcatheter valve deployment can follow one of two strategies: pre-deployment fracture (fracture before implanting the transcatheter valve) or post-deployment fracture (fracture after implanting the transcatheter valve within the surgical valve). Each approach has rationale and tradeoffs. Pre-deployment BVF allows the operator to maximally enlarge the surgical valve before transcatheter valve insertion, potentially enabling placement of a larger transcatheter valve and avoiding deformation of the transcatheter frame during fracture. However, fracturing an exposed ring may cause abrupt hemodynamic changes or embolization of material. Post-deployment BVF is frequently performed because the newly implanted transcatheter valve can contain displaced tissue and provide some protection against annular trauma and embolization; additionally, balloon fracture can further expand the transcatheter stent frame to optimize leaflet function and effective orifice area. In practice, many operators favor post-deployment BVF in ViV cases to directly expand the transcatheter frame and improve leaflet mobility, provided that the transcatheter valve is durable enough to withstand the high-pressure inflation and that the balloon position relative to the transcatheter valve commissures is carefully controlled.

Technical factors important for successful fracture include appropriate balloon centration within the valve, maintenance of coaxial alignment, and rapid ventricular pacing during inflation to minimize cardiac motion and reduce ejection. Rapid pacing at rates typically around $180$–$220$ beats per minute is used to reduce cardiac output transiently, thereby stabilizing balloon position and reducing the risk of balloon migration. The duration of inflation is brief—just long enough to confirm fracture and hemodynamic response. Contrast injection may be used to visualize the valve and surrounding structures during inflation, but excessive contrast should be avoided in patients with renal impairment.

# Hemodynamic effects and risks (annular injury, aortic root complications)

The primary hemodynamic effect of BVF is an increase in the internal diameter of the failing surgical prosthesis, which translates into greater effective orifice area (EOA) and lower transvalvular gradients. This improves forward flow and can reduce symptoms of heart failure attributable to prosthetic valve stenosis or patient–prosthesis mismatch. The magnitude of gradient reduction depends on the degree of enlargement achieved and on the interaction between the fractured ring and the transcatheter valve frame. Post-procedure echocardiography typically documents decreased peak and mean gradients, increased EOA, and improved transvalvular velocity profiles.

Despite these benefits, BVF introduces several potential risks related to disruption of prosthetic and native anatomical structures. The high radial forces required to fracture the prosthetic ring are transmitted to adjacent tissues. One major concern is annular injury, including dehiscence of the surgical annulus, disruption of sutures, or creation of perivalvular leaks. If the fracture propagates into adjacent tissues or if the balloon oversizes relative to the anatomical confines, tearing of the annulus or paravalvular tissue can occur, which may present acutely with significant regurgitation, hemodynamic instability, or requirement for urgent surgery.

Aortic root complications are another important consideration, particularly for aortic valve BVF. Fracture can produce jagged edges or outward deformation of the sewing ring that may impinge on the aortic root or coronary ostia. Although BVF is not typically associated with direct coronary obstruction, the risk increases if the fractured fragments or displaced leaflets obstruct coronary ostia. Aortic root injury, including dissection or rupture, is a catastrophic although uncommon complication. The risk is increased in patients with heavily calcified or fragile annular tissue, prior root enlargement procedures, or mixed prosthetic-native anatomy.

Embolic events are possible due to liberation of calcific or fibrous material when the valve structure fails. Thromboembolic protection devices have been considered in some centers, though their routine use in BVF has not been established by randomized data. Hemodynamic instability during the fracture event may occur, especially if rapid pacing is inadequate or if significant regurgitation is precipitated. There is also a theoretical risk that the transcatheter valve frame may be damaged by the high-pressure inflation; therefore, operators must ensure that the chosen transcatheter valve is compatible with post-deployment dilation.

Procedural planning should therefore include assessment of annular and root anatomy with CT, measurement of coronary heights, evaluation of calcification and tissue fragility, and contingency planning for possible surgical bailout. Intra-procedural transesophageal echocardiography and fluoroscopy are used to monitor for immediate complications. Recognizing early signs—new paravalvular leak, abrupt hemodynamic deterioration, or evidence of contained aortic root injury—allows for prompt management.

# Leaflet modification procedures (eg, intentional laceration) and indications

Leaflet modification refers to techniques intended to prevent or mitigate obstruction of coronary ostia and to improve leaflet opening after ViV procedures. The best-known method is intentional leaflet laceration of the native or bioprosthetic leaflets—BASILICA—which uses an electrified wire to split leaflets longitudinally before valve implantation. The rationale is that when a transcatheter valve is deployed inside a surgical bioprosthesis or native valve with bulky leaflets, the displaced leaflet tissue can be pushed outward and upward toward the coronary ostia, potentially occluding flow. By creating a controlled longitudinal split in the leaflet, the two resultant halves splay outward during transcatheter valve expansion, leaving a channel for coronary blood flow and reducing the risk of obstruction.

The BASILICA technique is performed under fluoroscopic and echocardiographic guidance. A guidewire is advanced and snared to create a rail across the aortic valve leaflet targeted for laceration. Electrified energy is applied to the guidewire to lacerate the leaflet from base to tip, creating a midline split. The procedure requires meticulous technique to ensure the laceration plane is centered and full-thickness, and to avoid unintended injury to adjacent structures such as the mitral valve or aortic wall. BASILICA can be applied to native valve leaflets, prosthetic surgical leaflets, and transcatheter leaflets in selected circumstances.

Indications for leaflet modification include high risk of coronary obstruction predicted by pre-procedural imaging—most commonly computed tomography measurements that show low coronary ostial height, small sino-tubular junction dimensions, narrow sinuses of Valsalva, or anticipated leaflet-to-coronary ostium distances below threshold values. Specific risk stratification often uses the virtual transcatheter valve-to-coronary (VTC) distance, where a VTC less than approximately $4$ mm is associated with high risk of obstruction. When the predicted risk is high, BASILICA or similar techniques may be indicated to prevent life-threatening coronary obstruction.

Leaflet modification may also improve hemodynamics by increasing leaflet mobility in ViV settings where immobile prosthetic leaflets could be pinned by the new valve, thereby reducing effective orifice area. In some ViV scenarios, combining BVF with leaflet laceration may be considered: BVF enlarges the ring, while leaflet laceration ensures that displaced leaflet tissue does not obstruct coronaries and allows better leaflet opening. The decision to perform leaflet modification requires assessment of the valve type, coronary anatomy, patient comorbidities, and operator expertise.

Risks of leaflet modification include perforation or laceration extending beyond the target leaflet, creation of severe regurgitation if leaflet competence is compromised unpredictably, embolization of leaflet material, and conduction disturbances. The BASILICA technique also adds procedural complexity, time, and radiation exposure, and may require transseptal or retrograde arterial access routes and specialized equipment.

# Clinical evidence, outcomes and limitations of BVF/BASILICA in repeat interventions

Clinical evidence for both BVF and BASILICA comes from registries, observational series, bench testing, and limited prospective studies rather than large randomized trials. For BVF, early multi-center registry data and single-center series have demonstrated substantial reductions in transvalvular gradients and improvements in effective orifice area in ViV procedures when fracture is successful. Patients with small surgical bioprostheses who underwent BVF showed lower residual gradients and less patient–prosthesis mismatch compared with historical controls undergoing ViV without BVF. Bench studies and imaging corroborate the mechanism by demonstrating abrupt increases in internal diameter after fracture.

Reported outcomes generally indicate high procedural success rates for valve models known to be fractureable, with acute gradient reduction and symptomatic improvement. However, the durability of the hemodynamic benefit over the long term is still under investigation, and some series report residual gradients in a subset of patients. Major complications such as annular rupture, coronary obstruction, and need for emergency surgery have been infrequent but not negligible, reflecting the importance of careful patient selection and operator experience.

For BASILICA, prospective registry data demonstrate feasibility in preventing coronary obstruction during high-risk transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) and ViV cases. Early multicenter experiences showed that BASILICA reduced the incidence of coronary obstruction compared with historical expectations based on pre-procedural CT risk stratification. Procedural success rates are high in experienced centers, and most reported complications are manageable. Follow-up data indicate acceptable short-term outcomes, but the impact of leaflet laceration on long-term valve function, thrombosis risk, and structural degeneration is not yet fully defined.

Limitations of the evidence include selection bias, institution-specific expertise, and relatively short follow-up durations. Many reports are from high-volume centers with considerable expertise in complex TAVR, which may not generalize to all settings. Device-specific factors further complicate evidence synthesis: fractureability is valve-model dependent, and BASILICA may be more challenging in certain anatomic configurations or with particular prosthetic leaflet materials. Comparative randomized data that quantify net clinical benefit—balancing improvements in gradients or prevention of coronary obstruction against the risk of procedural complications—are lacking.

Clinical decision-making should therefore be individualized, integrating detailed imaging-based risk assessment, knowledge of the surgical valve model, anticipation of hemodynamic benefit, and the institution and operator experience with these techniques. Key decision points include whether the surgical valve is fractureable, the predicted residual gradient without BVF, the coronary anatomy and calculated VTC or valve-to-sinotubular junction distances, the patient’s surgical risk, and the potential for adverse events. Multidisciplinary Heart Team discussion that includes cardiac surgeons, interventional cardiologists, imaging specialists, and anesthesiologists is recommended for complex ViV cases considering BVF and/or BASILICA.

Conclusion

Bioprosthetic valve fracture and intentional leaflet modification (BASILICA) have expanded the toolkit available for treating failed surgical bioprostheses and high-risk native valves in repeat transcatheter interventions. BVF can markedly improve valve hemodynamics by increasing internal diameter, while BASILICA reduces the risk of coronary obstruction and can enhance leaflet mobility. Both techniques require careful valve identification, detailed pre-procedural imaging, precise balloon and equipment selection, and skilled execution to balance benefits against risks such as annular injury, aortic root complications, embolization, and procedural complexity. Current clinical evidence supports their use in appropriately selected patients at experienced centers, but limitations in long-term data and randomized comparisons necessitate thoughtful, individualized application and ongoing study.

Introduction

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) has become a cornerstone therapy for calcific aortic stenosis across surgical risk spectrums. As TAVI volumes rise and life expectancy after implantation increases, complications—both periprocedural and long-term—are increasingly encountered. Recognition of these complications, understanding their mechanisms and risk factors, and timely management are essential to optimize outcomes and to plan for possible repeat valve procedures, including valve-in-valve interventions. This chapter reviews the major complications associated with TAVI and redo valve procedures, emphasizing pathophysiology, clinical presentation, diagnostic strategies, prevention, and treatment options.

# Vascular complications: dissection, perforation, pseudoaneurysm and hematoma

Vascular complications remain among the most common early adverse events after TAVI because most procedures use percutaneous arterial access (typically transfemoral). The pathophysiology is related to large-bore sheath insertion, calcified or tortuous iliofemoral vessels, balloon valvuloplasty, and device passage. Vessel injury can range from intimal tear (dissection) to full-thickness perforation with extravasation, or focal arterial wall disruption producing pseudoaneurysm; soft tissue bleeding produces hematoma.

Clinical presentation varies with severity. Small dissections may be asymptomatic or manifest as limb ischemia from flow limitation. Perforation typically presents with hypotension, expanding groin hematoma, or signs of retroperitoneal hemorrhage (flank pain, abdominal distension, declining hemoglobin). Pseudoaneurysms often present with a pulsatile mass, bruit, or persistent groin pain days after access. Hematomas can be localized or cause compartment syndrome.

Diagnosis relies on a combination of clinical exam and imaging. Duplex ultrasound is first-line for suspected pseudoaneurysm or hematoma at the access site and can demonstrate flow within a pseudoaneurysm sac (yin-yang flow) and the characteristic "to-and-fro" waveform in the neck. Computed tomography angiography (CTA) of the pelvis and abdomen is the best test for complex injuries, retroperitoneal bleeding, vessel perforation, or dissections extending proximally; it also informs endovascular repair planning. Digital subtraction angiography during the procedure is essential when intraprocedural bleeding is suspected.

Management is graded to the lesion and hemodynamic status. Small pseudoaneurysms may thrombose spontaneously; ultrasound-guided thrombin injection is highly effective for pseudoaneurysms with a narrow neck. Covered stent graft placement (endovascular repair) is commonly used for perforation or extensive dissections when anatomy permits and is preferable in hemodynamically stable patients to avoid open surgery. Open surgical repair is reserved for cases not amenable to endovascular therapy or with ongoing hemorrhage and instability. Local hematomas without expanding or compressive features are managed conservatively with monitoring, analgesia, and transfusion if needed, but expanding hematomas or those causing neurovascular compromise require urgent evacuation and possible vascular repair.

Prevention is critical: preprocedural CT angiography to plan access route and gauge vessel diameter, calcification, and tortuosity; using the smallest feasible sheath and lower-profile devices; intraprocedural ultrasound-guided vascular puncture; percutaneous closure devices deployed by experienced operators; and bailout strategies (e.g., access-site covered stent availability). For patients with hostile femoral anatomy, alternative access routes (transaxillary, transcarotid, transcaval, or surgical transapical/transaortic approaches) should be considered.

# Bleeding risks and access-site management

Bleeding after TAVI encompasses access-site bleeding, intraprocedural anticoagulant-related hemorrhage, and delayed bleeding from antithrombotic therapy. Patient factors – advanced age, frailty, chronic kidney disease, anemia, and concomitant antiplatelet or anticoagulant medications – increase bleeding risk. Procedural factors include large-bore vascular access, multiple arterial punctures, and the need for surgical cutdown.

The Valve Academic Research Consortium (VARC) definitions are commonly used to classify bleeding severity into minor, major, and life-threatening/disabling bleeding. Major bleeding is associated with significant hemoglobin drop, transfusion, or procedural intervention; life-threatening bleeding includes fatal bleeding, hemodynamic compromise requiring surgery, or bleeding in critical sites. Clinical decision-making balances ischemic and bleeding risks when prescribing periprocedural antithrombotic therapy. Typical practice has been dual antiplatelet therapy briefly post-TAVI in patients without an indication for anticoagulation, but trials (e.g., POPular TAVI) showed that single antiplatelet therapy reduces bleeding without increasing ischemic events in many patients. For patients with atrial fibrillation requiring oral anticoagulation, guideline-endorsed strategies favor oral anticoagulant alone over combination therapy with antiplatelet agents except in specific high-thrombotic scenarios, limiting bleeding risk.

Access-site management aims to minimize bleeding through meticulous technique. Ultrasound-guided arterial access reduces inadvertent punctures. Preclosure with suture-based percutaneous closure devices (e.g., ProGlide) is routine for transfemoral TAVI; operators must be experienced and prepared for closure device failure, which is a known mechanism of groin bleeding. Postprocedural monitoring includes frequent inspection of puncture sites, hemodynamic surveillance, serial hemoglobin checks, and early imaging with duplex ultrasound if bleeding or pseudoaneurysm is suspected. In cases of major bleeding, transfusion thresholds should be individualized; however, restrictive strategies (e.g., transfuse for hemoglobin <7–8 g/dL in stable patients) are often applied, with higher thresholds for symptomatic or high-risk patients. Reversal agents for anticoagulants (e.g., protamine for unfractionated heparin) should be used judiciously.

When percutaneous closure fails or there is ongoing bleeding, options include manual compression, deployment of covered stents for arterial injury, surgical repair, and embolization techniques for smaller branches. Multidisciplinary team readiness (interventional cardiology, vascular surgery, and interventional radiology) improves outcomes.

# Paravalvular leak: mechanisms, assessment and percutaneous closure options

Paravalvular leak (PVL) refers to regurgitation around the implanted transcatheter valve due to incomplete sealing between the prosthesis and native annulus. PVL is more common after TAVI than surgical replacement because TAVI relies on radial force and stent frame apposition within a calcified, irregular annulus rather than sutured annular closure. Mechanisms include severe or asymmetric annular calcification preventing full valve expansion, undersized prosthesis relative to annular dimensions, irregular or elliptical annulus geometry, and malpositioning or under-expansion of the frame. Postdilatation can improve apposition but risks annular injury or conduction disturbances.

Assessment of PVL requires multimodality imaging. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) is the first-line tool for screening and grading regurgitation; transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) provides higher sensitivity and is valuable intra-procedurally. Criteria for quantification include jet width, circumferential extent of regurgitation around the prosthesis, regurgitant volume and effective regurgitant orifice area, and Doppler-based measures. Because prosthetic stents create acoustic shadowing and multiple eccentric jets may occur, integrative assessment combining color Doppler, spectral Doppler (e.g., continuous-wave Doppler of LV outflow tract), and quantitative methods is necessary. A widely used qualitative grading uses none/trace, mild, moderate, and severe categories; moderate or greater PVL is associated with worse outcomes and heart failure symptoms.

CT angiography plays a role in preprocedural sizing and in assessing the mechanism in symptomatic patients by delineating valve frame deformation and annular calcification. Aortography with selective contrast injection can visualize the regurgitant jets and is often performed at the time of implantation.

Management starts with prevention: careful annular sizing with CT, selection of appropriate valve type and size, and attention to positioning. Balloon-expandable valves may offer more predictable expansion in heavily calcified annuli, while self-expanding valves may be better tolerated in certain anatomies. Intraprocedural techniques include careful deployment depth control and, if appropriate, postdilatation to improve seal. When significant PVL is identified post-implant and persists despite ballooning or is associated with symptoms, percutaneous closure is the preferred first-line therapy in many centers.

Percutaneous PVL closure involves transcatheter delivery of occluder devices into the paravalvular channels to reduce regurgitation. Devices employed include dedicated paravalvular leak plugs and off-label use of vascular plugs such as the Amplatzer Vascular Plug II and III. Choice of device depends on the size, shape, and number of jets; multiple small devices or larger plugs may be required. Access can be transfemoral, transseptal (for mitral PVL), or transapical depending on valve location. Procedural success requires careful crossing of the paravalvular defect under fluoroscopic and echocardiographic guidance, accurate device sizing (often trialled under fluoroscopy), and monitoring for interference with valve leaflets or coronary flow. Complications of closure include device embolization, interference with valve function leading to stenosis or new regurgitation, and residual leak. In some patients with severe PVL not amenable to percutaneous repair, repeat valve implantation (valve-in-valve or valve-in-ring) or surgical intervention may be considered.

# Valve malposition, embolization and coronary obstruction

Valve malposition and embolization are uncommon but potentially catastrophic procedural complications. Malposition occurs when the transcatheter valve is deployed too high (aortic side) or too low (ventricular side) relative to the annulus. Both can result in paravalvular leak, coronary obstruction risk, conduction disturbances, or left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) obstruction. Embolization is complete displacement of the valve from the intended landing zone into the aorta or into the left ventricle; embolized valves may occlude branch vessels, impede flow, or cause arrhythmias.

Mechanisms include inaccurate annular sizing, insufficient anchoring due to undersizing or inadequate calcification, rapid pacing failure during deployment, operator error, or hostile anatomy (e.g., low coronary height, shallow sinuses of Valsalva, severe annular angulation). Emergent management depends on direction of embolization and hemodynamics. A valve embolized into the aorta can sometimes be secured proximally with a second valve deployed in the annulus (valve-in-valve) and the embolized valve left in the aorta if not causing obstruction, sometimes sandwiched against the aortic walls or anchored with a stent. An embolized valve into the left ventricle is more complex and often requires surgical retrieval. Prevention hinges on meticulous preprocedural CT planning, intra-procedural imaging, careful deployment technique, and readiness for bailout strategies.

Coronary obstruction is a life-threatening complication that results from occlusion of a coronary ostium by displaced native leaflet tissue or by the prosthetic valve frame or leaflets, leading to myocardial ischemia, cardiogenic shock, and malignant arrhythmias. Risk factors include low coronary ostial height (<10–12 mm to the annulus), small sinus of Valsalva diameter (<30–35 mm depending on prosthesis and operator assessment), bulky calcified leaflets, and valve-in-valve procedures where the surgical bioprosthetic leaflets may be displaced towards the coronary ostia. Presentation is acute ST-elevation or ischemia during or shortly after deployment, hypotension, and arrhythmia.

Prevention is critical and includes meticulous CT assessment of coronary heights and sinus anatomy, consideration of valve choice and implantation depth, and preventive techniques when high risk is identified. The BASILICA (Bioprosthetic or Native Aortic Scallop Intentional Laceration to prevent Iatrogenic Coronary Artery obstruction) technique intentionally lacerates the native or bioprosthetic leaflet(s) projecting toward a coronary ostium to create a controlled split that allows coronary flow post-implant. Intra-procedural prophylactic coronary protection (wiring the coronary artery and having a stent ready for immediate deployment) is another strategy. For acute coronary obstruction, immediate coronary angiography is required. If the coronary can be crossed, ballooning and urgent stent deployment (chimney stenting) can restore flow. In refractory cases, cardiopulmonary support and surgical intervention may be necessary.

# Conduction disturbances, new bundle branch block and need for permanent pacing

Conduction disturbances are a common and clinically significant complication after TAVI because of the close anatomical relationship between the aortic valve complex and the cardiac conduction system (notably the atrioventricular node and the left bundle branch system, which runs subendocardially near the membranous septum). Compression, inflammation, or ischemic injury of the conduction tissue by the valve frame or by annular calcium can produce new atrioventricular block (AVB), new left bundle branch block (LBBB), or wider QRS complexes.

New LBBB is common after TAVI, with rates varying by valve type and implantation depth—self-expanding valves and deeper (more ventricular) implants are associated with higher LBBB incidence. New LBBB may be transient or persistent and can be associated with adverse outcomes, including eventual progression to high-degree AV block and reduced left ventricular synchrony and function. The clinical consequences can include heart failure exacerbation and increased mortality in some studies.

Permanent pacemaker implantation (PPI) remains the primary therapy for high-degree (second-degree Mobitz type II or third-degree) AV block and for symptomatic bradycardia unresponsive to temporary pacing. Indications for PPI after TAVI follow standard pacing guidelines but must be applied with knowledge of TAVI-specific dynamics: some conduction disturbances are transient, so many centers observe patients with continuous telemetry for 48–72 hours before committing to PPI unless there is persistent high-grade AV block. Predictors of PPI include baseline right bundle branch block (higher risk), deep valve implantation, greater valve oversizing, and use of self-expanding devices. Preprocedural ECGs, baseline conduction abnormalities, and continuous postprocedural telemetry inform decision-making.

Strategies to reduce conduction complications include precise implantation technique to minimize ventricular extension of the frame (i.e., higher implantation), selecting valve type based on patient anatomy and conduction risk, and avoiding aggressive oversizing. When temporary pacing is used during the procedure, a temporary transvenous pacemaker or active fixation temporary leads provide short-term support; leadless pacing systems are emerging options for certain patients. Left bundle branch area pacing and His-bundle pacing are investigational approaches for preserving physiologic conduction in select patients.

# Acute kidney injury, stroke and infection risks

Acute kidney injury (AKI)

AKI after TAVI is multifactorial: contrast-induced nephropathy from intraprocedural iodinated contrast, hemodynamic instability, embolic renal ischemia from atheromatous debris mobilized during vascular manipulation, and pre-existing chronic kidney disease (CKD) contribute. AKI is commonly classified using KDIGO criteria: an increase in serum creatinine by ≥0.3 mg/dL within 48 hours, or a 1.5× increase within 7 days, or urine output <0.5 mL/kg/h for 6 hours. Baseline serum creatinine, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), and urine output should be closely monitored. Normal creatinine ranges depend on age, sex, and muscle mass; roughly 0.6–1.2 mg/dL for adult males and 0.5–1.1 mg/dL for adult females are common reference ranges, but eGFR provides a better assessment.

Prevention includes adequate hydration (isotonic saline), minimizing contrast volume using procedural techniques and alternative imaging, avoiding nephrotoxic drugs, and careful periprocedural hemodynamic management. For patients with established CKD, the threshold for alternative access, staged procedures, or modified contrast strategies should be considered. If AKI occurs, management is supportive with fluid and electrolyte optimization, avoidance of further nephrotoxins, and renal replacement therapy if indicated.

Stroke

Ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke are feared complications. The pathophysiology of periprocedural stroke is primarily embolic, from dislodged aortic arch plaque, native valve or annular calcium, or thrombus formed on devices or wires. Clinical presentation may be immediate or delayed within days after the procedure. Stroke assessment uses standard clinical scales (e.g., National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale, NIHSS) and brain imaging (non-contrast CT to rule out hemorrhage, CTA for large-vessel occlusion) to guide therapy. Mechanical thrombectomy is indicated for large-vessel occlusions in appropriate candidates.

Cerebral embolic protection devices (filters or deflectors deployed in the brachiocephalic and left common carotid arteries) have been studied to reduce embolic load to the brain, showing reduction in procedure-associated particulate matter but variable impact on clinical stroke rates. Their use is considered in selected high-risk patients but is not universally mandated. Strategies to reduce stroke risk include careful manipulation within the aorta, minimizing wire and catheter exchanges, maintaining adequate anticoagulation during the procedure, and prompt identification and management of arrhythmias such as atrial fibrillation.

Infection risks

Infection after TAVI includes access-site cellulitis, bloodstream infection, and prosthetic valve endocarditis. Perioperative prophylactic antibiotics are routine, but mucosal or device-related infections can still occur. Endocarditis after TAVI behaves similarly to prosthetic valve endocarditis after surgical replacement, with staphylococci, enterococci, and oral streptococci common culprits. Fever, bacteremia, new or changing murmur, heart failure, and septic emboli may be clues. Diagnosis uses modified Duke criteria adapted for prosthetic valves, supported by blood cultures and imaging (TEE is more sensitive than TTE for prosthetic valve infection). Positron emission tomography (FDG-PET/CT) may assist in difficult cases.

Management requires prolonged targeted intravenous antibiotics and often involves a multidisciplinary heart team including cardiology, infectious diseases, and cardiac surgery. Indications for surgical explantation include uncontrolled infection, abscess formation, persistent bacteremia, prosthetic dysfunction with heart failure, and end-organ septic embolization. Prevention centers on sterile technique, prophylactic antibiotics, and attention to dental and cutaneous sources of bacteremia.

# Long-term complications: structural deterioration, leaflet thrombosis, endocarditis

Structural valve deterioration (SVD)

SVD refers to intrinsic changes in the bioprosthetic valve leading to stenosis or regurgitation over time due to leaflet calcification, tearing, or degeneration. The rate and pattern of SVD depend on valve material, patient factors, and hemodynamic stresses. Surgical bioprostheses have known long-term failure curves; data for transcatheter prostheses are accumulating but suggest comparable durability in the medium term (5–8 years), with longer-term data still emerging. Clinical surveillance with periodic echocardiography is essential; increases in mean transvalvular gradient, new or worsening regurgitation, or reduced effective orifice area suggest SVD. Reference hemodynamic values post-TAVI vary by prosthesis size, but mean gradients >20 mmHg or a rapid rise in gradients warrant evaluation. Symptomatic SVD may be managed with redo-TAVI (valve-in-valve) in many patients, with attention to patient–prosthesis mismatch and coronary obstruction risk.

Leaflet thrombosis

Bioprosthetic leaflet thrombosis presents as hypoattenuated leaflet thickening (HALT) with reduced leaflet motion (RELM) on 4-dimensional CT imaging, and can manifest as increased transvalvular gradients or as subclinical findings. The pathophysiology includes thrombus formation on low-flow areas of the valve, influenced by patient prothrombotic states, atrial fibrillation, left ventricular dysfunction, and inadequate antithrombotic therapy. Subclinical leaflet thrombosis is common in early imaging studies but its clinical significance varies; however, it has been associated with increased gradients and possible increased risk of stroke in some analyses.

Diagnosis is best made by ECG-gated multidetector CT, which shows hypoattenuated masses on leaflet surfaces and reduced excursion. Echocardiography may detect increased gradients and abnormal leaflet motion but is less sensitive than CT for HALT. Management typically involves anticoagulation: oral anticoagulant therapy (often warfarin or direct oral anticoagulants) can resolve HALT and reduce gradients in many patients. There is ongoing debate about routine anticoagulation after TAVI; current practice is individualized, favoring anticoagulation when leaflet thrombosis is documented or when there is another indication (e.g., atrial fibrillation).

Endocarditis

Prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) after TAVI is a severe complication with substantial morbidity and mortality. Presentation may be subtle, particularly in elderly patients, and can include fever, embolic phenomena, heart failure, or persistent bacteremia. Risk factors include procedural contamination, poor dental hygiene, and repeated vascular access manipulations. Diagnosis uses modified Duke criteria supplemented by advanced imaging because echocardiography may be limited by prosthetic artifacts. TEE is more sensitive than TTE, and FDG-PET/CT and CT angiography can help detect perivalvular extension, abscesses, or prosthetic dehiscence.

Management includes prolonged intravenous antibiotics guided by culture and sensitivity results and individualized consideration of surgical explantation. Surgical indications are similar to other PVE contexts: refractory infection, abscess, prosthetic dysfunction with heart failure, and uncontrolled sepsis. However, many TAVI patients are older or have prohibitive surgical risk, complicating decisions; outcomes depend on timely diagnosis and multidisciplinary care.

Long-term surveillance and prevention

Given the spectrum of late complications, structured follow-up after TAVI is essential. Baseline TTE before discharge establishes a reference for future comparisons. Routine clinical review and imaging at 30 days, 6–12 months, and annually thereafter are common. Any new symptoms—heart failure, syncope, fever, embolic phenomena, or angina—should prompt early evaluation. Prevention of long-term complications includes patient selection, meticulous procedural technique, optimization of antithrombotic strategies tailored to each patient’s ischemic and bleeding risks, dental care and infection prophylaxis when indicated, and patient education regarding signs and symptoms that require urgent attention.

Conclusion

Complications of TAVI span vascular injury and bleeding, paravalvular regurgitation, valve malposition and coronary obstruction, conduction system disease, organ-specific insults such as AKI and stroke, and long-term prosthetic failures including thrombosis and endocarditis. Prevention begins with comprehensive preprocedural imaging and planning, careful intra-procedural technique, and multidisciplinary readiness for bailout therapies. Early recognition through vigilant monitoring and targeted imaging enables timely interventions that range from percutaneous repair to surgical management. As TAVI extends to younger and lower-risk populations, attention to durability, strategies to minimize late complications, and refined approaches to repeat interventions will be increasingly important.

Introduction

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) has become a principal therapy for symptomatic severe aortic stenosis across a broad spectrum of surgical risk. Despite its less invasive nature, TAVI carries a nontrivial risk of injury to the cardiac conduction system because of the close anatomical relationship between the aortic valvar apparatus and the atrioventricular conduction pathways. Conduction disturbances after TAVI range from transient first-degree atrioventricular (AV) delay and new bundle branch block to persistent high-degree AV block requiring permanent pacemaker (PPM) implantation. Understanding the anatomic basis of these injuries, the clinical and procedural predictors for post‑TAVI conduction disease, device-related differences, strategies for intra-procedural monitoring and temporary pacing, and evidence-based indications and selection of pacing modes are essential for peri‑procedural management and for minimizing long‑term adverse consequences such as pacing‑induced cardiomyopathy.

# Anatomical basis for conduction disturbances after aortic valve interventions

The propensity for conduction injury during aortic valve interventions reflects close spatial relationships among the aortic annulus, the membranous septum, and components of the specialized conduction system — namely the AV node, the penetrating His bundle, and the proximal bundle branches. The AV node is located in the triangle of Koch at the base of the interatrial septum. The His bundle penetrates the central fibrous body and emerges at the crest of the interventricular septum near the base of the noncoronary cusp (NCC) and right coronary cusp (RCC). Thereafter, the His divides into the right bundle branch (RBB) and the left bundle branch (LBB); the LBB fan courses subendocardially along the left ventricular septum.

Key anatomic features that determine vulnerability include the length and position of the membranous septum and the distance from the annular plane to the His bundle. A short membranous septum or a His bundle that lies close to the inferior aspect of the aortic annulus increases the likelihood that valve frame expansion, prosthesis oversizing, or bulky calcification at the base of the leaflets will compress or stretch conduction tissue. The left bundle branch lies immediately beneath the noncoronary and right coronary cusps in many individuals, such that radial forces from a valve stent or adjacent hematoma can injure the LBB resulting in new left bundle branch block (LBBB).

Valve deployment depth and radial force are anatomically meaningful. A valve implanted deeper into the left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) approximates the stent frame to the His-Purkinje system; greater radial forces (as with some self-expanding devices) can produce sustained compression or ischemia of conduction tissue. In addition, extensive subannular or commissural calcification may directly impinge upon or transmit force to the conduction system during balloon dilatation or valve expansion. Understanding this three-dimensionally complex anatomy is central to procedural planning and to predicting which patients are at higher risk of conduction sequelae.

# Incidence and predictors of high-degree AV block and new pacemaker implantation

Incidence
Reported rates of new conduction abnormalities and PPM implantation after TAVI vary with patient population, device type, operator technique, and definitions used. Broadly, new-onset LBBB occurs in approximately 5% to 40% of patients depending on device and timing; high-degree AV block (second-degree type II AV block or complete AV block) leading to PPM implantation commonly occurs in about 5% to 30% of patients. Contemporary pooled analyses generally place overall permanent pacemaker rates in the range of roughly 8%–20%, with higher values associated with older self-expanding devices and lower values with balloon-expandable valves using modern techniques.

Predictors
Pre-procedural patient factors and baseline conduction abnormalities significantly influence the risk of post‑TAVI conduction disease:

- Baseline right bundle branch block (RBBB): This is consistently the strongest clinical predictor of subsequent high-degree AV block and PPM need. Because pre-existing RBB makes the conduction system dependent on the left-sided conduction fibers, any new injury to the LBB or distal His substantially raises the chance of complete AV block.

- Baseline first-degree AV block (PR interval >200 ms) and pre-existing LBBB: Both indicate existing conduction disease and increase risk for progression.

- Baseline QRS duration: Wider baseline QRS duration (e.g., >120 ms) is associated with higher risk.

- Advanced age and comorbidities: Older patients and those with greater burden of conduction system disease from ischemia, fibrosis, or prior surgery may be more susceptible.

Procedural and anatomic predictors include:

- Valve implantation depth: Deeper implantation into the LVOT — commonly quantified as distance below the noncoronary/right coronary cusp plane — correlates with increased PPM rates. Several series identify depths greater than approximately 6 mm below the annulus as higher risk; thresholds vary by device.

- Degree of valve oversizing: Excessive oversizing increases radial forces transmitted to the adjacent septum and conduction tissue.

- Pre-dilation and post-dilation: Balloon valvuloplasty or post‑dilatation can transiently or permanently injure conduction tissue, particularly with aggressive balloon sizing.

- Presence and distribution of calcification: Heavy and subannular calcification, especially when concentrated adjacent to the membranous septum, predisposes to conduction injury.

- Procedure-related factors: Valve type, operator technique, and need for multiple deployment attempts are relevant. Use of rapid ventricular pacing during deployment can transiently mask conduction abnormalities that manifest afterward.

Temporal course
Conduction disturbances may appear immediately during or after implantation, but some manifest hours to days later. Early transient conduction delays may recover within days to weeks; conversely, some patients experience delayed high-degree AV block beyond 24–48 hours, although most clinically significant events occur in the immediate post-procedural period.

# Device-specific rates and risk stratification

Device design and mechanical properties — particularly radial force, frame length, and interaction with the LVOT — influence conduction disturbance rates. Historically and in many series:

- Self-expanding nitinol frame valves (e.g., older-generation CoreValve/Evolut family) have been associated with higher rates of new LBBB and PPM requirement, ranging from roughly 10% to >30% in some registries. The long stent frame extending into the LVOT and continuous outward radial force contribute to this risk.

- Balloon-expandable valves (e.g., Sapien family) tend to have lower PPM rates, commonly reported in the range of about 3%–10%, although transcatheter valve iterations and implantation techniques affect these numbers.

- Mechanically expanding valves and newer-generation devices with repositionability or lower radial force aim to reduce conduction injury. Device-specific comparisons must account for patient selection and evolving operator experience.

Risk stratification combines baseline conduction status with procedural variables. Practical models used in clinical practice consider:

- Baseline ECG: presence of RBBB confers a high pre-test probability of post-TAVI high-degree AV block. Patients with RBBB plus first-degree AV block or prolonged HV intervals are at particularly high risk.

- Planned device and implantation strategy: If a device with historically higher conduction complication rates is selected, or if deep implantation is likely, the clinician anticipates increased risk.

- Imaging findings: Pre-procedural CT assessing membranous septum length, distribution of calcification, and annular anatomy helps predict the distance of the conduction system from the annulus and may guide valve choice and implantation depth to mitigate risk.

Risk stratification is dynamic and should inform intra‑procedural monitoring plans, decisions about pre-emptive temporary pacing, and post‑procedural observation.

# Intra-procedural monitoring and temporary pacing strategies

Continuous electrocardiographic monitoring during and immediately after TAVI is indispensable. Key elements include real-time 12‑lead ECG or multi‑lead monitoring, arterial pressure tracing, and, often, intracardiac electrograms when available. Rapid recognition of new conduction defects enables timely interventions.

Temporary pacing strategies
Temporary pacing serves two principal purposes: to support hemodynamics during deployment (rapid ventricular pacing for balloon-expandable valves) and to treat emergent bradyarrhythmias or high-degree AV block. Approaches include:

- Routine temporary transvenous pacing lead: Many centers place a temporary right ventricular pacing lead via the femoral or jugular vein before valve deployment. This provides immediate backup pacing for procedural asystole or post-deployment high‑degree AV block. The advantages are immediate availability and reliability; disadvantages include potential for vascular access complications and the need for careful lead stabilization during valve exchange.

- On-demand temporary pacing: Some operators avoid routine temporary transvenous leads, instead relying on external transcutaneous pacing and quick percutaneous venous access if pacing is required. This practice is riskier if high-degree AV block occurs unpredictably.

- Prophylactic temporary-permanent leads: An increasingly used strategy in high-risk patients is placement of a temporary-permanent active fixation lead connected to an externalized pacemaker generator. This provides more stable pacing over days while the conduction system may recover, reducing the need for urgent PPM implantation and allowing observation.

- Epicardial or coronary sinus approaches are not routinely used for acute backup.

Monitoring for conduction recovery and delayed block
Because conduction injury may evolve, continuous inpatient telemetry for at least 24–72 hours is recommended for most patients. Early recognition of progressive PR prolongation, new or worsening LBBB, or intermittent AV block allows timely PPM implantation if required. Continuous rhythm strips and frequent 12-lead ECGs (baseline, immediate post‑implant, daily until discharge, and with any symptoms) help detect deterioration.

Electrophysiological study (EPS)
Selected cases may benefit from EPS to localize the level of block (AV nodal vs infra-Hisian) and measure HV interval. Prolonged HV interval at baseline or after TAVI (e.g., HV >70 ms by some centers) suggests infra-Hisian disease and a higher likelihood of progression to complete heart block; such findings may sway the decision toward earlier PPM. EPS is most useful when the decision is equivocal — for example, persistent new LBBB with equivocal symptoms or intermittently prolonged PR but no sustained high-degree AV block.

Clinical decision points for temporary pacing:

- High-risk baseline (e.g., RBBB) undergoing implantation with a device/technique associated with higher conduction risk: strong consideration for pre-emptive temporary pacing with a stable lead.

- Emergent complete heart block or sustained symptomatic bradycardia: immediate temporary transvenous pacing and PPM implantation if block persists.

- New conduction abnormality without high-degree AV block: continue monitoring with low threshold for pacing if progression occurs.

# Indications for permanent pacemaker post-TAVI and pacing modes

Indications for PPM after TAVI are based on established pacing guidelines adapted to the TAVI context, clinical presentation, and the expected likelihood of conduction recovery. Key indications include:

Absolute indications
- Persistent high‑degree AV block: persistent second-degree Mobitz type II AV block or third-degree (complete) AV block causing symptoms or hemodynamic instability generally mandates PPM. If block persists beyond a brief period and is not expected to recover rapidly, implantation is indicated.

- Symptomatic bradycardia or pauses: recurrent symptomatic pauses, syncope attributable to bradyarrhythmia, or asystolic events.

- Alternating bundle branch block: alternating LBBB and RBBB indicates unstable infra‑Hisian conduction and is an accepted indication for PPM.

Relative/conditional indications
- Persistent new LBBB without high-degree AV block: isolated new LBBB per se is not an absolute indication. However, if accompanied by significant PR prolongation, syncope, or an abnormal invasive EPS (e.g., HV interval prolongation), PPM may be indicated.

- Intermittent high-degree AV block: episodes of transient complete AV block that recur or are prolonged suggest need for PPM.

Timing considerations
Because some conduction disturbances recover within days to weeks, many centers use an observation interval, typically 24–72 hours of continuous monitoring, before deciding on PPM for conduction changes that are not severe or symptomatic. In patients with persistent complete AV block immediately post-procedure, PPM implantation is not usually deferred. In borderline cases, a temporary-permanent lead allows safe observation while avoiding premature PPM placement.

Choice of pacing mode
Pacing mode selection should balance the immediate need for AV synchrony, minimization of right ventricular (RV) pacing burden, and long-term ventricular function preservation.

- Dual-chamber pacing (DDD) is commonly selected for patients with AV block when sinus node function is intact because it preserves AV synchrony and can reduce symptoms related to pacemaker syndrome. DDD pacing also allows algorithms to minimize unnecessary ventricular pacing (e.g., managed ventricular pacing).

- Single-chamber ventricular pacing (VVI) may be used when atrial lead implantation is contraindicated, in patients with permanent atrial fibrillation, or when quick implantation is required. VVI pacing is simple but can promote interventricular dyssynchrony and pacing-induced cardiomyopathy with high pacing burdens.

- His-bundle pacing (HBP) and left bundle branch area pacing (LBBAP): Physiologic conduction system pacing aims to maintain or restore synchronous ventricular activation by recruiting native His-Purkinje conduction. HBP or LBBAP may preserve ventricular function better than conventional RV apical pacing, particularly in patients expected to require high percentages of ventricular pacing or those with reduced LVEF. Technical considerations, operator expertise, and device compatibility influence feasibility. HBP may be limited by high pacing thresholds or lead instability, whereas LBBAP appears more stable and has emerged as an attractive alternative.

- Cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT): In patients with reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) (typically LVEF ≤ 35%) who develop high-degree AV block and are expected to be dependent on ventricular pacing, initial dual-site pacing with biventricular pacing (CRT) may be considered to reduce the risk of pacing-induced dyssynchrony. Decision to implant CRT rather than standard PPM should follow guideline-based criteria and individualized assessment.

Programming considerations
Programming to minimize ventricular pacing (e.g., adaptive AV delay algorithms) is especially important in patients with intact AV conduction who develop intermittent conduction abnormalities or new LBBB. When physiologic pacing is used, thresholds and sensing must be optimized to avoid unnecessary high output and premature battery depletion.

# Long-term implications of pacing on ventricular function and outcomes

While PPMs prevent bradycardia-related morbidity and sudden asystolic events, long-term chronic RV pacing may have adverse consequences, particularly when the percentage of ventricular pacing is high.

Pacing-induced cardiomyopathy
Chronic RV apical pacing produces ventricular activation patterns that mimic LBBB, causing electrical and mechanical dyssynchrony. Over time, this dyssynchrony can reduce LVEF and lead to heart failure in a subset of patients. Observational studies suggest that a high burden of RV pacing (commonly cited thresholds include >20%–40% ventricular pacing) is associated with an increased risk of developing pacing-induced cardiomyopathy, although thresholds vary among cohorts. Therefore, minimizing unnecessary RV pacing is a practical goal, particularly in patients with baseline reduced LVEF or those likely to be paced frequently (e.g., complete heart block and permanent dependence).

Impact on morbidity and mortality
PPM implantation after TAVI has been associated in some observational analyses with longer hospital stays and higher rates of rehospitalization for heart failure; long-term mortality data are mixed. The adverse impact is largely mediated by ventricular dyssynchrony and heart failure progression in those with high ventricular pacing burden. Conversely, appropriate pacing in patients who need it reduces syncope and sudden death risk. Net clinical impact depends on patient selection, pacing strategy, and device programming.

Strategies to mitigate long-term harm
- Minimize RV pacing: Use AV‑delay modulation and algorithms that favor intrinsic conduction when feasible.

- Physiologic pacing: HBP or LBBAP may preserve mechanical synchrony and reduce the risk of pacing-induced cardiomyopathy by using the native conduction system to achieve near-normal activation. Early data show promise, especially when high pacing burden is anticipated, but long-term outcome data are still evolving.

- Consider CRT in high-risk patients: In those with reduced LVEF who will be dependent on ventricular pacing, primary CRT may be preferable to conventional RV pacing.

- Surveillance: Routine follow-up with device interrogation and periodic echocardiography (e.g., at baseline, 3–6 months, and as clinically indicated) is recommended to monitor LVEF and detect early pacing-related decline. If LVEF falls significantly, consider upgrade to CRT or change of pacing strategy.

- Infection and lead complications: Long-term risks of PPM include lead dysfunction, pocket infection, and thromboembolic events. These risks should be weighed when deciding on PPM timing and the necessity of implantation, favoring observation when safe.

Recovery of conduction
A proportion of patients with early conduction disorders after TAVI experience recovery of AV conduction over days to weeks. If there is clinical equipoise and the patient is hemodynamically stable, temporary-permanent pacing and close monitoring allow time for recovery and may prevent unnecessary PPM implantation. Conversely, in patients with persistent or recurrent high-degree AV block, earlier definitive PPM avoids prolonged hospitalization and recurrent bradyarrhythmic events.

Conclusion

Managing conduction disturbances after TAVI requires a synthesis of anatomic knowledge, baseline conduction status, device- and procedure-related factors, and careful peri‑procedural monitoring. Risk stratification using ECG, imaging, and procedural planning informs the use of temporary pacing and the timing of PPM implantation. When a pacemaker is required, selection of pacing mode and strategies to minimize long-term ventricular dyssynchrony are critical to preserving cardiac function. An individualized approach that balances the risks of delayed PPM insertion against the potential harms of unnecessary chronic pacing optimizes patient outcomes.

Introduction

Infective endocarditis (IE) and prosthetic valve interventions such as transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) intersect with multiple pharmacologic domains: targeted antimicrobial therapy for causative pathogens, peri- and post-procedural antithrombotic strategies, peri‑procedural management of anticoagulants and antiplatelets, and optimization of guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT) for coexistent heart failure. This chapter reviews the pharmacology most relevant to management of IE—particularly commonly encountered organisms like Enterococcus spp.—principles that determine intravenous (IV) versus oral antibiotic courses for prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE), antithrombotic approaches after TAVI including when to use single versus dual therapy and how atrial fibrillation (AF) and recent percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) change strategy, practical management of antithrombotics around procedures with bleeding-risk considerations, heart failure pharmacotherapy in patients with valvular disease, and the current recommendations for antibiotic prophylaxis for patients with prosthetic valves undergoing dental and other procedures.

# Antimicrobial regimens for common IE pathogens (eg, Enterococcus spp.): beta‑lactams, aminoglycosides, combination therapy alternatives

Enterococci, most commonly Enterococcus faecalis and E. faecium, are frequent causes of IE, especially PVE and healthcare‑associated native valve endocarditis. Their intrinsic and acquired resistance patterns and their relative tolerance to bactericidal antibiotics make therapy more complex than for streptococci or staphylococci.

Enterococcus faecalis
E. faecalis remains typically susceptible to beta‑lactams such as ampicillin and penicillin G. The classical bactericidal regimen relies on synergism between a cell‑wall active agent and an aminoglycoside: ampicillin (or penicillin) plus gentamicin. Ampicillin alone is bacteriostatic against enterococci at achievable concentrations in many cases; adding gentamicin achieves synergistic bactericidal activity through enhanced penetration and ribosomal inhibition. A commonly used regimen for susceptible E. faecalis IE is ampicillin 2 g IV every 4 hours (or continuous infusion equivalent) plus gentamicin 3 mg/kg/day in divided doses (commonly 1 mg/kg every 8 hours), with dose adjustment and therapeutic drug monitoring targeted to minimize nephrotoxicity. The duration for native valve enterococcal IE is typically 4–6 weeks; for PVE the duration is most often 6 weeks.

However, since the 2000s, combination therapy using double beta‑lactam therapy (ampicillin plus ceftriaxone) has become a preferred alternative for E. faecalis strains that are susceptible to ampicillin but show high-level aminoglycoside resistance or in patients at high risk for aminoglycoside toxicity (older age, baseline renal dysfunction). The mechanism of synergy is complementary inhibition of penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs). Typical dosing is ampicillin 2 g IV every 4 hours plus ceftriaxone 2 g IV every 12 hours, for a total duration similar to aminoglycoside-containing regimens (4–6 weeks for native, 6 weeks for prosthetic). Advantages include avoidance of aminoglycoside nephrotoxicity and ototoxicity while achieving bactericidal activity.

Enterococcus faecium
E. faecium often carries resistance to ampicillin and many beta‑lactams; vancomycin resistance (VRE) is also common in some centers. For ampicillin‑resistant but vancomycin‑susceptible isolates, vancomycin IV dosed to trough goals historically has been used, though contemporary practice prefers AUC‑guided dosing when available. Target vancomycin troughs of 15–20 mg/L (AUC24/MIC targets dependent) were used but AUC monitoring is superior. For VRE or when vancomycin fails or is not an option, daptomycin or linezolid are mainstays. Daptomycin is bactericidal and often used at higher doses for endovascular infections (e.g., 8–10 mg/kg IV once daily, sometimes higher), though combination therapy (daptomycin plus a beta‑lactam such as ampicillin or ceftaroline) may enhance activity and reduce resistance emergence. Linezolid is bacteriostatic and penetrates well but is generally reserved for selected situations or when daptomycin cannot be used, and prolonged therapy risks bone marrow suppression and neuropathies.

Key practical points
- Always interpret susceptibility testing in the context of MICs and the clinical situation. High‑level aminoglycoside resistance (HLAR) in enterococci (gentamicin MICs very high) negates synergism and mandates avoiding gentamicin-containing regimens.
- For E. faecalis that are ampicillin‑susceptible, consider ampicillin plus ceftriaxone as first-line for many patients (particularly older or renal impairment) to minimize aminoglycoside toxicity.
- For E. faecium and VRE, consider daptomycin (high dose) +/- a beta‑lactam, or linezolid when appropriate; consultation with infectious diseases is essential.
- Duration: native valve IE typically 4–6 weeks; prosthetic valve IE is usually treated for at least 6 weeks, often longer based on response.

# Indications and duration of intravenous versus oral therapy in PVE

Traditionally, IE and particularly prosthetic valve endocarditis have been treated with prolonged courses of parenteral bactericidal antibiotics because of concerns about poor oral bioavailability, tissue penetration to biofilms on prosthetic material, and the need for reliably high serum and tissue concentrations. However, recent evidence has supported the selective use of partial oral step‑down therapy in carefully selected patients with left‑sided IE, though PVE poses a higher risk and requires particular caution.

Indications for IV therapy
Initial therapy for PVE is almost always IV. Indications for initial and continued IV therapy include hemodynamic instability, persistently positive blood cultures, uncontrolled sepsis, complications requiring urgent surgery (e.g., heart failure from valve destruction, perivalvular abscess), organisms with limited or unreliable oral agents (e.g., staphylococci, enterococci with resistant patterns), presence of prosthetic material with biofilm formation risk, and inability to tolerate oral intake or absorb medications. IV therapy allows high and predictable drug concentrations and flexibility to adjust regimens based on susceptibilities.

Transition to oral therapy: evidence and selection criteria
The POET trial (Partial Oral versus Intravenous Antibiotic Treatment of Endocarditis) randomized stable patients with left‑sided IE (both native and prosthetic) to complete IV therapy versus a switch to selected oral regimens after an initial period (at least 10 days) of IV therapy. The trial showed noninferiority for composite outcomes in a highly selected population meeting stringent stability criteria: clinical stability for >10 days, afebrile, negative repeat blood cultures, no signs of uncontrolled infection or complications, and availability of oral agents with proven efficacy and bioavailability for the causative pathogen. Importantly, only certain pathogens and drug combinations were used in POET (e.g., streptococci, sensitive enterococci, staphylococci with available oral options), and many centers interpret POET as support for selective step‑down rather than routine oral therapy for PVE.

Specific considerations in PVE
PVE is inherently higher risk than native valve IE because of biofilm formation and prosthetic material. Many guidelines still recommend prolonged IV therapy for PVE. If considering oral step‑down in PVE, the patient should meet strict criteria: clinical stability for an extended initial IV period (often 2 weeks or more per some centers), no paravalvular complications, negative blood cultures, and assurance that the oral regimen attains adequate bactericidal concentrations against the isolate. Oral regimens must have proven bioavailability and activity; for enterococci this can be challenging. The decision usually involves infectious diseases consultation and close outpatient follow-up with the ability to readmit if relapse is suspected.

Duration of therapy
Typical durations vary by organism and valve status:
- Native valve IE: generally 4–6 weeks, depending on organism and regimen.
- Prosthetic valve IE: generally at least 6 weeks of targeted parenteral therapy from the date of the first negative blood culture; some recommendations extend therapy duration based on complications or persistent infection.
When oral step‑down is used, the total duration (IV + oral) should be the same as recommended total therapy for that pathogen and valve condition. For example, a PVE course totaling 6 weeks may include an initial IV phase followed by oral therapy only in exceptional, well‑selected cases.

# Antiplatelet and anticoagulant strategies after TAVI (single vs dual therapy, indications based on AF/PCI)

Antithrombotic management following transcatheter aortic valve implantation balances prevention of early thrombotic complications (valve thrombosis, early coronary or cerebrovascular events) against bleeding risk in an often elderly, comorbid population. Practices have evolved from routine dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) toward more conservative regimens informed by randomized trials.

Antiplatelet therapy for patients without another indication for anticoagulation
Early experience favored DAPT (aspirin plus clopidogrel for 1–3 months) after TAVI, extrapolating from coronary stent practice. Subsequent randomized trials demonstrated that single antiplatelet therapy (SAPT) with aspirin alone reduces bleeding without increasing ischemic events compared with DAPT for most patients undergoing TAVI who do not have an indication for systemic anticoagulation. Current common practice and guideline recommendations support aspirin monotherapy (e.g., aspirin 75–100 mg daily) in patients without other indications for anticoagulation. Clopidogrel monotherapy is an option in some contexts if aspirin is contraindicated.

Anticoagulation for patients with indications (e.g., AF)
Patients with established indications for long‑term anticoagulation, most commonly AF, should receive therapeutic oral anticoagulation (OAC) rather than antiplatelet therapy alone after TAVI. Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) are frequently used in those with nonvalvular AF unless there are contraindications; for mechanical valves DOACs are contraindicated (but mechanical valves are not placed in TAVI). The choice between a vitamin K antagonist (VKA) and DOAC depends on patient factors and local practice; for bioprosthetic valves and TAVI, DOACs are often reasonable after the immediate post‑prosthetic period, but evidence is still evolving.

Recent randomized data (e.g., POPular-TAVI antiplatelet cohort) support withholding routine clopidogrel and using SAPT in patients not anticoagulated. For patients on OAC for another indication, adding antiplatelet therapy increases bleeding without clear ischemic benefit in many cases and is generally avoided unless there is a compelling reason (e.g., recent coronary stent).

When recent PCI or coronary stenting complicates the picture
In patients undergoing TAVI who also have recent PCI requiring antiplatelet therapy, a balance is required. If PCI with stent implantation is recent, guidelines for coronary stents (e.g., duration of DAPT) apply, but clinicians should tailor therapy to bleeding risk. Typical pragmatic approaches:
- If PCI was remote and patient requires systemic OAC for AF, OAC alone may suffice after an initial short period.
- If PCI was recent (particularly within 1–6 months) and dual therapy is indicated for stent protection, consider OAC plus a single antiplatelet (usually clopidogrel) for the minimal necessary duration. Assess stent type, time since PCI, and bleeding risk.
- Triple therapy (OAC + aspirin + P2Y12 inhibitor) should be avoided if possible because of very high bleeding risk; if absolutely required for a very short period (days to weeks) use the shortest effective duration and prefer clopidogrel as the P2Y12 agent.

Valve thrombosis prophylaxis and empiric short-term regimens
Some centers have used short courses (e.g., 30 days) of anticoagulation post-TAVI to reduce subclinical leaflet thrombosis, especially in patients deemed at higher thrombotic risk, but randomized data are mixed. Routine OAC purely to prevent subclinical leaflet thrombosis in patients without another indication is not universally recommended.

Practical decision points
- No OAC indication: aspirin monotherapy is generally preferred after TAVI.
- Chronic OAC indication (eg, AF): continue OAC; do not add antiplatelet unless required for recent PCI.
- Recent PCI: tailor therapy to stent timing/type and ischemic vs bleeding risks; favor OAC + single antiplatelet for the shortest necessary duration rather than prolonged triple therapy.
- Always individualize based on bleeding risk (HAS-BLED), ischemic risk, and patient comorbidities.

# Management of antithrombotic therapy around procedures and bleeding risk considerations

Peri-procedural management of antiplatelet and anticoagulant medications requires balancing thrombotic risk from interruption versus bleeding risk if therapy is continued. Key elements include the indication for antithrombotic therapy, the thrombotic risk of interruption, the bleeding risk of the scheduled procedure, pharmacokinetics of the agent, and reversibility/bridging options.

Assessing thrombotic and bleeding risks
Thrombotic risk drivers include mechanical valves, recent venous thromboembolism, recent stent (especially within 1–6 months), and high‑risk AF (CHA2DS2-VASc score). Bleeding risk is driven by the procedure type (major surgery vs minor procedure) and patient factors (age, renal disease, prior bleeding, concurrent antiplatelet drugs).

Anticoagulant interruption and bridging
For vitamin K antagonists (warfarin), reversal is achieved with vitamin K and prothrombin complex concentrates when needed; cessation prior to surgery is typically 5 days to allow INR normalization, with checking INR preoperatively. Bridging with heparin (therapeutic LMWH or IV unfractionated heparin) historically was used for patients at very high thrombotic risk during warfarin interruption. However, bridging confers increased bleeding risk and should be reserved for the highest risk scenarios (e.g., mechanical mitral valve, recent thromboembolus). For many AF patients, temporary interruption of warfarin without bridging is safe.

For DOACs, because of shorter half‑lives, interruption timing is shorter and bridging is usually not needed. General guidance:
- Low bleeding-risk procedures: hold DOAC for at least 24 hours prior.
- High bleeding-risk procedures or reduced renal function: hold for 48–72 hours or longer depending on renal function and agent half‑life.
Restart DOACs post‑procedure once hemostasis is secured—often 24 hours after low risk and 48–72 hours after high‑risk procedures.

Antiplatelet therapy around procedures
Aspirin is often continued for many procedures unless bleeding risk is high and aspirin would materially increase bleeding (e.g., neuraxial anesthesia, certain surgeries). For dual antiplatelet therapy after recent PCI, if interruption is necessary, avoid stopping P2Y12 inhibitors within the early post‑stent period; if bleeding risk mandates interruption, plan timing carefully and resume as soon as hemostasis is achieved. For neuraxial anesthesia or spinal procedures, P2Y12 inhibitors have specific washout periods (e.g., clopidogrel 7 days).

Reversal agents and emergency management
Reversal agents are relevant for life‑threatening bleeding:
- Warfarin: vitamin K, prothrombin complex concentrate (PCC).
- Dabigatran: idarucizumab (specific reversal).
- Factor Xa inhibitors (apixaban, rivaroxaban, edoxaban): andexanet alfa (where available) or PCC may be used in severe bleeding; availability and cost limit universal use.
- Antiplatelet agents: no specific reversal; platelet transfusion may be considered in life‑threatening hemorrhage.

Risk scores and clinical tools
Bleeding risk can be estimated using scores such as HAS-BLED for AF patients, though these are imperfect. Decisions should integrate scores with clinical judgment, considering factors such as prior bleeding, frailty, renal function, and concurrent antiplatelet therapy.

Specific considerations in TAVI patients
TAVI recipients are often elderly with multiple comorbidities and elevated bleeding risk. For elective procedures after TAVI, consider continuing aspirin where safe, and treat OAC interruption in AF patients per DOAC/warfarin guidance. In patients with recent TAVI and concomitant coronary stenting, coordinate cardiology and surgical teams about timing to minimize interruption of crucial antithrombotic therapy.

# Heart failure pharmacotherapy relevant to valve disease (ACE inhibitors/ARBs, beta‑blockers, MRAs, SGLT2 inhibitors)

Heart failure pharmacotherapy remains central to managing patients with valvular heart disease who have left ventricular dysfunction or symptoms, and many heart failure drugs have important interactions with hemodynamics of valvular lesions.

ACE inhibitors / ARBs / ARNI
Angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEi) and angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs) reduce afterload, attenuate maladaptive remodeling, and improve morbidity and mortality in heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF). In valve disease, these agents can be beneficial when LV dysfunction or heart failure is present. However, in severe aortic stenosis (AS), marked afterload reduction with ACEi in the presence of fixed obstruction requires caution because of the risk of hypotension; gradual titration and close monitoring is advised. In aortic regurgitation (AR) and functional MR, vasodilators may reduce regurgitant volume and improve symptoms pending definitive valve therapy.

Angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitor (ARNI; sacubitril/valsartan) is superior to ACEi in appropriate HFrEF patients and should be used per guidelines in patients who remain symptomatic despite optimal ACEi/ARB therapy or substituted after appropriate washout from ACEi. Initiation in patients with valvular disease follows the same principles as in nonvalvular HFrEF, with attention to blood pressure and renal function.

Beta-blockers
Beta-blockers (e.g., metoprolol succinate, bisoprolol, carvedilol) improve survival in HFrEF. In patients with valvular disease, beta-blocker use is indicated for coexistent heart failure with reduced EF. In AS, beta-blockers must be used cautiously in decompensated states because heart rate control can compromise cardiac output when the stroke volume is fixed. In MR, rate control may be beneficial, especially in tachyarrhythmias. Beta-blockers also affect blood pressure and may complicate perioperative hemodynamics; start low and titrate.

Mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs)
Spironolactone or eplerenone reduce morbidity and mortality in moderate to severe HFrEF in appropriate patients already on ACEi/ARB and beta-blocker therapy. They are important in managing congestion and counteracting aldosterone-mediated fibrosis. Monitor potassium (normal range approximately 3.5–5.0 mmol/L) and renal function; discontinue or lower dose if eGFR declines or potassium rises above safe thresholds.

SGLT2 inhibitors
Sodium–glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors (e.g., dapagliflozin 10 mg daily, empagliflozin 10 mg daily) have demonstrated benefit in reducing heart failure hospitalizations and cardiovascular mortality in HFrEF and in some patients with preserved EF. These agents are now recommended as foundational therapy in HFrEF irrespective of diabetes status. In valvular patients with heart failure, SGLT2 inhibitors can be introduced to improve outcomes and reduce hospitalizations. Monitor for volume depletion, renal function deterioration, and genitourinary infections.

Diuretics and symptomatic management
Loop diuretics (e.g., furosemide) control congestion and are titrated to symptom relief. Diuretics do not alter long-term mortality but are essential for symptomatic management. Monitor renal function and electrolytes.

Integration with valve disease management
- Optimize GDMT in patients with valvular heart disease and HFrEF, tailoring to hemodynamics. For example, use vasodilators judiciously in severe AS; they can benefit afterload-sensitive regurgitant lesions but worsen hypotension in advanced fixed stenosis.
- Pre‑ and post‑valve intervention therapy: GDMT should be optimized prior to valve intervention where feasible; after valve correction, myocardial remodeling and symptomatology may change and medications may need modification.
- Monitor blood pressure, renal function, and electrolytes closely in older patients and those with concomitant infections or procedures.

# Antibiotic prophylaxis principles for prosthetic valves and dental procedures

Antibiotic prophylaxis aims to prevent infective endocarditis caused by bacteremia related to certain procedures. Guidelines (e.g., American Heart Association, European Society of Cardiology) have converged to limit prophylaxis to patients at highest risk of adverse outcomes from IE.

Who should receive prophylaxis?
Prophylaxis is recommended for patients at highest risk of adverse outcomes from IE, including:
- Patients with prosthetic heart valves, including transcatheter prostheses and homografts.
- Patients with prior infective endocarditis.
- Certain congenital heart disease categories (e.g., unrepaired cyanotic CHD, repaired prosthetic material during first 6 months after procedure).
- Cardiac transplant recipients who develop valvulopathy.

Patients with prosthetic valves (including valve repair with prosthetic material and TAVI prostheses) fall into this high‑risk category and should receive prophylaxis for procedures that are likely to produce bacteremia involving oral flora.

Procedures that warrant prophylaxis
- Dental procedures that involve manipulation of the gingival tissue, periapical region of teeth, or perforation of oral mucosa (e.g., tooth extraction, periodontal procedures, dental implants). Routine anesthetic injections in noninfected tissue and dental radiographs do not require prophylaxis.
- Prophylaxis is not recommended for routine gastrointestinal or genitourinary procedures except in rare circumstances where an active infection is present.

Regimen and dosing
The standard recommended oral prophylactic regimen for dental procedures in patients able to take oral medication is amoxicillin 2 g orally given 30–60 minutes before procedure (adult dosing). For those allergic to beta‑lactams, alternatives include:
- Clindamycin 600 mg orally 30–60 minutes before procedure.
- Azithromycin or clarithromycin 500 mg orally 30–60 minutes before procedure (where clindamycin is not appropriate).
Parenteral options (e.g., ampicillin 2 g IV/IM) are used if oral administration is not feasible.

Practical points
- Single preprocedural dose is generally recommended; prolonged postprocedural courses are not indicated.
- Emphasize good oral hygiene and regular dental care; the cumulative risk of bacteremia from daily oral activities is greater than single dental procedures, and optimization of oral health reduces IE risk.
- Inform patients with prosthetic valves, including those with TAVI, that they qualify for prophylaxis for qualifying dental procedures and that they should communicate this to dental providers.

Conclusion

Pharmacologic management at the interface of infective endocarditis, prosthetic valves/TAVI, and heart failure requires integration of antimicrobial stewardship, individualized antithrombotic strategy, peri‑procedural risk assessment, and evidence‑based heart failure therapies. Key principles include organism‑directed antibiotic selection with attention to synergistic combinations (e.g., ampicillin plus gentamicin or ampicillin plus ceftriaxone for E. faecalis), preference for IV therapy in PVE with selective step‑down to oral agents only in carefully selected stable patients, SAPT (typically aspirin) after TAVI for patients without other indications for anticoagulation, continuation of OAC for AF with minimization of combined antiplatelet therapy unless recent PCI mandates it, cautious interruption and reversal strategies tailored to bleeding and thrombotic risk, optimization of ACEi/ARB/ARNI, beta‑blockers, MRAs, and SGLT2 inhibitors for heart failure in the setting of valve disease, and adherence to guideline‑based antibiotic prophylaxis for dental procedures in patients with prosthetic valves. Coordination among cardiology, cardiac surgery/interventional teams, infectious diseases, and primary care/dental providers is essential to implement these pharmacologic strategies safely and effectively.

Introduction

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) and valve-in-valve (ViV) procedures have transformed management of valvular heart disease. After implantation, accurate hemodynamic assessment and serial imaging are essential to determine prosthetic valve function, detect complications, and distinguish mechanical or biological failure modes that require different management strategies. This chapter reviews the principles of Doppler-based hemodynamic assessment, calculation and interpretation of effective orifice area (EOA), indexed EOA and Doppler velocity index (DVI) for prosthetic valves, criteria for patient–prosthesis mismatch (PPM) and its clinical significance, recommended echocardiographic surveillance, and the imaging features that distinguish structural valve deterioration, thrombosis, and paravalvular leak (PVL). The complementary roles of transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) and computed tomography (CT) in ambiguous cases are emphasized so that a clinician can integrate imaging findings with clinical context and decide on further management.

# Doppler assessment of transvalvular gradients and velocity profiles post-implantation

Doppler echocardiography is the cornerstone for noninvasive hemodynamic evaluation of prosthetic aortic valves. Continuous-wave Doppler (CWD) records the highest velocities through the valve and is used to derive peak velocities and transvalvular gradients. The maximal instantaneous gradient is calculated from the peak velocity (V) using the simplified Bernoulli equation: gradient = $4V^2$. The mean gradient is obtained by tracing the velocity curve over systole and integrating to provide the mean pressure drop across the valve. These measurements are flow-dependent, so interpretation must account for concomitant cardiac output, stroke volume, blood pressure, and heart rate.

Immediately after TAVI/ViV, Doppler velocities and gradients may differ from those expected for a given prosthesis because of altered flow state (e.g., anemia, sepsis, reduced left ventricular function), transient hyperdynamic circulation, or measurement errors (suboptimal acoustic windows, misalignment with jet). Accurate alignment of the CWD beam with the jet is essential; multiple acoustic windows (apical, right parasternal, suprasternal notch) should be interrogated to find the highest velocity. Spectral envelopes must be carefully analyzed: a smooth, well-defined envelope indicates laminar flow through an unobstructed orifice; a rounded, prolonged envelope may reflect impaired leaflet opening or elevated transvalvular resistance. Acceleration time (AT, time from onset of flow to peak velocity) can provide additional insight; an AT >100–120 ms suggests significant aortic stenosis physiology in native valves and, in prosthetic settings, prolonged AT should raise suspicion for obstruction.

Indexed interpretation is necessary because prosthetic valves generate higher baseline gradients than native valves of equivalent EOA due to prosthesis geometry and orifice position. Published valve-specific expected gradients and velocity ranges for each prosthesis model should be referenced. More important than an absolute gradient is change over time: a new increase in mean gradient >10–20 mmHg compared with a validated baseline should prompt investigation for thrombosis, pannus, degeneration, or PPM.

Key technical pitfalls include inaccurate measurement of the left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) diameter (which affects EOA calculation), failure to account for para-prosthetic or supra-annular flow convergence, and confounding by concomitant aortic regurgitation or subvalvular obstruction. Doppler recordings should be corroborated with 2D/3D morphology and TEE or CT as needed.

# Calculation and interpretation of EOA, indexed EOA and DVI in prosthetic valves

The effective orifice area (EOA) quantifies the functional opening through which blood flows and is calculated for prosthetic aortic valves using the continuity equation. The continuity equation for aortic prostheses is:

EOA = $\dfrac{CSA_{LVOT} \times VTI_{LVOT}}{VTI_{AV}}$,

where $CSA_{LVOT}$ is the cross-sectional area of the LVOT (calculated as $\pi \times \left(\dfrac{D_{LVOT}}{2}\right)^2$), $VTI_{LVOT}$ is the pulsed-wave Doppler velocity–time integral in the LVOT, and $VTI_{AV}$ is the CWD VTI through the prosthetic valve. Precision in each measurement is crucial. The LVOT diameter should be measured at the correct anatomic location, typically just below the prosthetic sewing ring in prosthetic valves; measurement error in diameter is squared in the CSA, so small errors can produce large EOA errors. For TAVI devices with supra-annular leaflets or stent frames, the LVOT measurement site and shape can complicate CSA estimation; in those situations, three-dimensional planimetry or CT measurements of LVOT area may improve accuracy.

Indexed EOA (iEOA) normalizes EOA to body surface area (BSA) to account for patient size:

iEOA = $\dfrac{EOA}{BSA}$.

This is essential for determining PPM and assessing whether a given EOA is adequate for the patient’s metabolic demands.

Doppler velocity index (DVI), also called the dimensionless index or velocity ratio, is an alternative when LVOT diameter measurement is unreliable. DVI is the ratio of velocity or VTI in the LVOT to the velocity or VTI through the valve:

DVI = $\dfrac{VTI_{LVOT}}{VTI_{AV}}$ (or equivalently peak velocity LVOT / peak velocity AV).

DVI is independent of LVOT area. For prosthetic aortic valves, a DVI less than approximately $0.25$–$0.35$ suggests significant obstruction; commonly used cutoffs are DVI < $0.25$ for severe stenosis and DVI between $0.25$ and $0.35$ for moderate disease, though prosthesis-specific norms should be consulted. DVI interpretation should be correlated with gradients and clinical picture.

When interpreting EOA and iEOA in prostheses, remember that normal EOA values differ by prosthesis type (supra-annular vs intra-annular) and size. Reference tables provided by manufacturers and published studies supply expected EOAs for model/size combinations. A substantially smaller measured EOA than expected for that prosthesis suggests either PPM (if valve leaflets are functioning normally), obstruction from thrombosis/pannus (if decreased leaflet mobility is seen), or measurement error.

# Criteria for patient–prosthesis mismatch and clinical significance

Patient–prosthesis mismatch (PPM) occurs when the prosthetic valve’s effective orifice area is too small relative to the patient’s body size, producing higher-than-expected transvalvular gradients despite normal prosthetic leaflet function. PPM is a hemodynamic diagnosis, distinct from structural obstruction. The widely accepted classification for aortic valve prostheses defines PPM using indexed EOA:

- No PPM: iEOA > $0.85\ \mathrm{cm}^2/\mathrm{m}^2$
- Moderate PPM: iEOA $0.65$–$0.85\ \mathrm{cm}^2/\mathrm{m}^2$
- Severe PPM: iEOA < $0.65\ \mathrm{cm}^2/\mathrm{m}^2$

For obese patients (BSA-derived indices may be misleading), adjusted thresholds are often applied; many experts suggest higher cutoffs (for example, defining severe PPM as iEOA < $0.6$–$0.7$ in patients with elevated BMI), and clinical judgment is required. Valve-specific expected EOAs must be referenced because supra-annular TAVI valves typically have larger EOAs for a given labeled size compared with intra-annular surgical valves.

The clinical significance of PPM is multifaceted. Moderate or severe PPM is associated with persistent elevation of transvalvular gradients, less regression of left ventricular hypertrophy, reduced improvement in symptoms and exercise capacity, and in some series worse long-term survival, particularly after surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR). After TAVI, the incidence and impact of PPM vary with valve type and implantation strategy; ViV procedures are particularly prone to PPM because the internal diameter of the failed surgical valve limits the new prosthesis’ EOA. Recognition of PPM has implications for procedural planning (e.g., choosing a valve with larger EOA, performing annular enlargement during SAVR, or considering bioprosthesis fracture techniques in ViV) and postoperative follow-up.

Clinically, distinguishing PPM from true obstruction is critical: PPM is a fixed mismatch in which valve leaflets open normally and no structural deterioration is present, whereas obstruction due to thrombosis, pannus, or structural degeneration will show abnormal leaflet motion and often a change from prior measurements. If elevated gradients are present but DVI and Doppler profiles suggest normal leaflet opening and imaging demonstrates normal mobility, calculate iEOA to evaluate for PPM. If iEOA indicates PPM, management is usually conservative and directed at symptoms, exercise tolerance, and consideration of re-intervention in severe, symptomatic cases.

# Echocardiographic surveillance schedule and key endpoints

Systematic surveillance after TAVI/ViV is necessary to detect early complications and to monitor long-term valve performance. A pragmatic schedule used in many centers and recommended by consensus documents includes a baseline transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) prior to discharge or within 30 days post-procedure, a follow-up at 30 days to 3 months if not already done, another at 1 year, and annually thereafter. Additional echocardiographic assessment is warranted whenever symptoms arise (dyspnea, heart failure signs, new murmur) or if there is a change in clinical status suggesting valve dysfunction.

Key echocardiographic endpoints to document at each visit include valve morphology (leaflet mobility, thickening or prolapse), Doppler hemodynamics (peak velocity, mean gradient), EOA and iEOA (or DVI), severity and mechanism of regurgitation (central vs paravalvular), left ventricular size and systolic function (including ejection fraction and diastolic parameters), right ventricular function and pulmonary pressures, and presence or progression of other valvular lesions. Record baseline values within the first 30 days to establish a reference; future changes are interpreted relative to this baseline.

Specific thresholds and trigger points for further investigation or intervention include a new mean gradient increase of more than $10$ mmHg from baseline, an absolute mean gradient above approximately $20$ mmHg for bioprosthetic valves (especially for valves implanted to treat aortic stenosis), or a DVI falling below expected values for the prosthesis. New or worsening regurgitation graded at least moderate (≥ grade 2–3/4) should prompt detailed evaluation for PVL or leaflet dysfunction. New reductions in leaflet excursion or evidence of thrombus on imaging, or detection of hypoattenuated leaflet thickening on CT in symptomatic patients or with gradient changes, should trigger consideration of anticoagulation or surgical/endovascular therapy. Serial progression of mean gradient or decreasing EOA over successive studies is suggestive of structural valve deterioration (SVD).

It is important to integrate clinical findings and laboratory data (e.g., biomarkers of hemolysis or inflammation) with imaging. Hemolysis may indicate significant regurgitation or PVL; elevated inflammatory markers are nonspecific but infections raise concern for endocarditis. Accurate documentation and standardized reporting (including regimen of image acquisition, measurement technique, and reference values) allow meaningful longitudinal comparison.

# Differentiating structural valve deterioration, thrombosis and paravalvular leak on imaging

Discerning the cause of prosthetic valve dysfunction requires synthesis of hemodynamics and imaging morphology. Structural valve deterioration (SVD), thrombosis, pannus formation, and paravalvular leaks each have characteristic features, although overlap occurs and multimodality imaging may be required.

Structural valve deterioration is a progressive, intrinsic change in the valve leaflets or supporting structure, most commonly in bioprosthetic valves. SVD manifests as leaflet calcification, fibrosis, tear, or disruption leading to stenosis, regurgitation, or a combination. Echocardiographic signs include increased leaflet echogenicity and thickness, restricted leaflet excursion on 2D imaging, increased transvalvular velocities and gradients, and reduced EOA. When regurgitation predominates, eccentric regurgitant jets or central regurgitation with abnormal coaptation can be seen. SVD typically evolves over months to years. In serial studies, SVD is suggested by a steady progression of gradients and worsening leaflet mobility over several follow-up exams.

Thrombosis of prosthetic leaflets can be subclinical or present with sudden hemodynamic changes. On echocardiography, thrombosis may appear as new leaflet thickening and reduced excursion, often more accentuated during systole than SVD depending on thrombus burden. Doppler shows an acute or subacute increase in transvalvular gradients. On CT, the hallmark is hypoattenuated leaflet thickening (HALT) and associated reduced leaflet motion (RELM); these findings are highly suggestive of thrombus when found in appropriate clinical context. Thrombus is often responsive to anticoagulation, so recognition is important: an increase in mean gradient of $>10$ mmHg with imaging evidence of reduced leaflet motion and HALT on CT favors thrombosis.

Pannus is organized fibrous ingrowth typically occurring on the sewing ring or around the valve frame and develops over months to years. Pannus produces fixed obstruction and may be difficult to distinguish from SVD on echocardiography. Pannus is usually denser and less mobile than thrombus and may have different CT attenuation characteristics; however, differentiation requires CT and occasionally fluoroscopic or intraoperative inspection.

Paravalvular leak (PVL) results from incomplete apposition of the prosthesis to the annulus or from annular/valve tissue defects. Echocardiographically, PVL is characterized by one or more eccentric jets originating outside the sewing ring rather than through the central flow of the valve. Color Doppler on TTE may underestimate PVL severity; TEE, particularly 3D TEE, improves localization and quantification. Key features include crescentic or focal jets adjacent to the valve frame, multiple jets around the circumference, and systolic/diastolic flow patterns depending on valve position and hemodynamics. Hemolysis and heart failure disproportionate to central regurgitation are clinical clues that suggest significant PVL. Quantification uses vena contracta width, regurgitant volume, and regurgitant fraction when feasible; regurgitant fraction >30%–40% typically indicates at least moderate-severe regurgitation.

Distinguishing features in summary: SVD is progressive structural change with calcification and restricted mobility developing over years; thrombosis tends to be subacute with HALT/RELM on CT and often reversible with anticoagulation; PVL is extracentral regurgitation with eccentric color Doppler jets and annular dehiscence or malapposition. Careful comparison with prior imaging is one of the most powerful tools for differentiation.

# Role of CT and TEE in ambiguous cases

When transthoracic echocardiography is inconclusive or when the mechanism of prosthetic dysfunction is uncertain, transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) and multi-detector computed tomography (CT) are the primary complementary modalities.

Transesophageal echocardiography, particularly 3D TEE, offers superior spatial resolution for valvular structures compared with TTE and is excellent for evaluating PVL. TEE allows precise localization of PVL jets relative to the sewing ring, characterization of the size and shape of defects, assessment of perivalvular abscesses or vegetations, and evaluation of leaflet motion. Intraoperative or procedural TEE is indispensable for guiding percutaneous PVL closure and for assessing valve seating and immediate complications. Limitations include semi-invasive nature, need for sedation or anesthesia in some cases, and artifact from prosthetic components, which may obscure visualization.

Cardiac CT provides excellent spatial resolution and is especially sensitive for leaflet morphology, stent-frame apposition, and tissue characterization. CT is the best modality to detect hypoattenuated leaflet thickening (HALT) and to assess reduced leaflet motion (quantitatively defined as RELM). CT can identify thrombus versus pannus based on attenuation values and enhancement patterns; thrombus commonly appears as low-attenuation material (<90–100 Hounsfield units) whereas pannus is denser and may have higher attenuation. CT also visualizes the prosthesis relative to the annulus and adjacent structures, detects prosthesis malapposition, and quantifies the circumferential extent of PVL by assessing gaps between prosthetic frame and annular tissue. CT is indispensable in TAVI planning and in evaluating suspected leaflet thrombosis, particularly when gradients rise or symptoms occur without clear TTE/TEE correlates.

The combined use of TEE and CT is often necessary. For example, when Doppler indicates increased gradients and TTE reveals reduced leaflet motion but cannot define the cause, CT demonstrating HALT strongly supports thrombosis and may prompt therapeutic anticoagulation. Conversely, a patient with significant regurgitation by TTE should undergo TEE to map PVL and CT to assess frame apposition and annular anatomy if percutaneous closure is contemplated.

In practice, the imaging pathway depends on the clinical scenario. For suspected thrombosis with hemodynamic compromise, CT (for HALT/RELM) plus TEE may be performed urgently. For suspected endocarditis or annular abscess, TEE is the first test. For pre-procedural planning of PVL closure, TEE and CT together provide complementary data on defect morphology, size, and spatial relationships.

Conclusion

Accurate hemodynamic assessment and structured echocardiographic surveillance after TAVI/ViV are essential to differentiate normal postoperative findings from PPM, thrombosis, SVD, or PVL. Doppler gradients, EOA, iEOA and DVI are central quantitative tools; their interpretation requires awareness of prosthesis-specific normal values and adjustment for flow state. PPM is a hemodynamic mismatch that must be distinguished from structural causes of obstruction, because management differs. A standardized imaging follow-up schedule with attention to changes from baseline permits early detection of clinically significant deterioration. When transthoracic echocardiography is ambiguous, TEE and CT provide complementary morphological and tissue-specific information indispensable for diagnosis and therapeutic planning. Integrating serial imaging with clinical and laboratory data allows rational, patient-centered decisions about medical therapy, anticoagulation, percutaneous interventions, or re-intervention.

Introduction

Coronary artery disease (CAD) and aortic stenosis frequently coexist due to shared risk factors and the ageing of the population. Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) has become an established therapy for severe aortic stenosis across a broad range of surgical risk profiles. For patients who have concomitant coronary disease, management must balance the safety and efficacy of revascularization with the technical and anatomic implications of prosthetic aortic valves for current and future coronary access. The interplay between percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) and TAVI spans preprocedural planning, procedural sequencing, prosthesis selection, intra-procedural techniques to preserve coronary access, and strategies to evaluate and treat ischemic symptoms after valve-in-valve (ViV) or redo TAVI procedures. This chapter integrates the pathophysiology, imaging assessment, device-related considerations, and clinical decision-making required to optimize outcomes in patients who require both valve replacement and coronary revascularization.

# Timing of PCI relative to TAVI (staged vs concurrent strategies)

Deciding when to perform PCI in patients who will undergo TAVI requires an individualized approach that weighs the urgency and anatomical complexity of coronary lesions, the symptomatic burden attributable to ischemia versus aortic stenosis, the risks of combined procedures, and the potential impact of antiplatelet therapy on periprocedural bleeding. The primary strategies are staged PCI before TAVI, concomitant (ad hoc) PCI at the time of TAVI, and staged PCI after TAVI. Each strategy has advantages and drawbacks.

Staged PCI before TAVI is often chosen when there are critical proximal coronary lesions (for example, left main stenosis or severe proximal left anterior descending disease) that are likely to cause hemodynamic instability during TAVI or when ischemia is clearly responsible for symptoms. Revascularizing significant lesions beforehand can reduce periprocedural myocardial infarction (MI) risk and improve hemodynamic reserve, which is especially relevant in balloon-expandable or self-expanding valve deployment where rapid hemodynamic changes may occur. However, performing PCI before TAVI introduces a delay in definitive valve therapy and exposes the patient to cumulative vascular and bleeding risks. Dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) after PCI can increase bleeding risk during TAVI if TAVI is performed while DAPT is in force; conversely, stopping DAPT early to proceed to TAVI increases stent thrombosis risk. For most contemporary bare metal or drug-eluting stents (DES), guidelines recommend at least 1 month of DAPT for high bleeding risk patients and 6 months for standard risk after elective PCI with DES, though shorter durations (1–3 months) are increasingly considered for high bleeding risk patients. Thus, the urgency of TAVI and the acceptable timing of DAPT interruption must be reconciled.

Concurrent PCI and TAVI reduces the number of vascular access episodes, shortens hospitalization, and allows for definitive management of both pathologies in a single session. This approach is attractive for patients with symptomatic CAD and severe aortic stenosis when the coronary lesions are amenable to PCI without prolonged procedural time or complex adjunctive techniques (for example, chronic total occlusion PCI or atherectomy). The primary disadvantage is that combined procedures increase contrast load, radiation exposure, and total anticoagulant/antiplatelet therapy complexity. Bleeding risk may be higher, especially in frail older patients. Clinically significant access site complications or periprocedural MI during a combined strategy can complicate the TAVI procedure.

Staged PCI after TAVI is chosen when coronary lesions are not critical, when symptoms are predominantly attributable to aortic stenosis, or when there is concern that valve anatomy may impede coronary access. TAVI modifies coronary hemodynamics and sometimes alleviates angina by improving left ventricular function and coronary perfusion. After TAVI, residual symptoms attributable to CAD can be reassessed; invasive physiologic testing with fractional flow reserve (FFR) or instantaneous wave-free ratio (iFR) may be more reliable once the valve gradient has been relieved. However, TAVI can make subsequent coronary access technically more difficult, particularly depending on valve design and commissural alignment. Therefore, preprocedural anticipation of coronary access difficulty should influence the decision toward earlier revascularization when lesions are anatomically significant.

Clinical decision-making balances ischemic risk and procedural safety. High-risk coronary anatomy (left main, proximal severe multivessel disease with left ventricular dysfunction, lesions supplying a large territory) generally favors revascularization before or at the time of TAVI. Isolated, stable, non-proximal lesions can be deferred and reassessed after TAVI. For patients with recent acute coronary syndromes, PCI should follow standard coronary care principles, with TAVI timing individualized according to hemodynamics and procedural risk.

# Impact of prosthesis design and commissural alignment on future coronary access

The design of a transcatheter heart valve (THV) has profound implications for coronary access after implantation. THV designs differ by frame material and geometry, leaflet position (intra-annular vs supra-annular), leaflet mounting (intra-frame vs external), stent height, cell size and shape, and radiopaque markers for commissural orientation. Self-expanding valves (e.g., nitinol frames) often have taller frames with denser cell structures compared with balloon-expandable valves (e.g., cobalt-chromium frames), which can affect the spatial relationship between the valve frame and coronary ostia. Valve-in-valve (ViV) procedures add further complexity because the leaflets and stent frame of the surgical bioprosthesis may be displaced outward, creating a barrier to coronary flow or access.

Commissural alignment refers to the rotational orientation of the THV relative to the native aortic valve commissures and the coronary ostia. Optimal commissural alignment positions the prosthetic commissures away from the coronary ostia, keeping the larger cells of the frame aligned with the coronaries and leaving clear pathways for catheter engagement. Conversely, commissural misalignment can position a prosthetic commissural post directly in front of a coronary ostium, making selective engagement challenging or impossible and increasing the risk of coronary obstruction during ViV procedures.

Prosthesis cell size and the location of sealing skirts or polyethylene terephthalate (PET) cuffs may block catheter passage. For example, some self-expanding supra-annular valves have closed-cell designs at certain levels, constraining the ability to pass catheters through the frame. Supra-annular leaflet位置 may also extend above the sinotubular junction, interfering with coronary ostia. The height of the stent relative to the sinuses of Valsalva and the sinotubular junction determines how much of the valve overlies the coronary ostia; low sinuses and high prosthesis stent positions elevate the risk of coronary occlusion and compromise access.

Radiopaque markers and fluoroscopic commissural alignment techniques can help during implantation, but even with best efforts, rotational variability and patient-specific anatomy make perfect alignment difficult. The importance clinically is that prosthesis choice should consider the need for future coronary access. For younger patients or those with significant coronary disease likely to require future interventions, selecting a valve with a favorable frame design, larger cell size at the coronary level, and predictable commissural orientation can preserve future PCI feasibility.

# Techniques to preserve or restore coronary access after valve implantation

Multiple procedural techniques are used to preserve coronary blood flow at the time of TAVI and to maintain or restore transthoracic coronary access for future interventions. These can be divided into preventive strategies applied during initial implantation and remedial techniques used when coronary flow or access is compromised.

Preventive strategies include careful valve sizing and implantation depth, commissural alignment techniques, and the use of prophylactic coronary wiring or balloon protection in high-risk cases. Valve sizing and implantation depth aim to avoid excessive supra-annular displacement of native leaflets that could obstruct coronary ostia. Implanting the valve as low as safely possible can reduce the degree of leaflet displacement into the sinuses. Commissural alignment involves pre-procedural identification of fluoroscopic markers on the THV and controlled rotational deployment so that the commissural posts avoid the coronary ostia. Some operators use orientation-control devices or manufacturers’ recommended cusp-overlap views to improve reliability of alignment.

When pre-procedural imaging indicates a high risk of obstruction (for example, low-lying coronary ostia, shallow sinuses, or bulky leaflets), techniques to actively protect the coronaries can be employed. One commonly used method is wiring the coronary artery with a guidewire and leaving a balloon or undeployed stent ready to be inflated or deployed rapidly (the "chimney" or "snorkel" technique) if obstruction occurs. The chimney stent creates a channel from the aorta into the coronary ostium, preserving flow even if the THV or displaced leaflets impede the native ostium. However, chimney stents have long-term issues including risk of stent deformation, thrombosis, and interference with future valve-in-valve procedures.

For ViV cases or when native leaflets are likely to be displaced outward, the BASILICA (Bioprosthetic or Native Aortic Scallop Intentional Laceration to prevent Iatrogenic Coronary Artery obstruction) technique can be used. BASILICA involves transcatheter electrosurgical laceration of the aortic leaflet that would otherwise obstruct a coronary ostium. By splitting the leaflet, the tissue can splay open during valve deployment, creating a path for coronary flow and preserving access. BASILICA is technically demanding and requires coordination between imaging, electrophysiology-style electrosurgery tools, and interventional skills, but has been demonstrated to reduce coronary obstruction risk in selected high-risk patients.

Post-implantation restorative techniques for coronary access include transcatheter retrieval or modification strategies when catheter engagement is blocked. Using high-support catheters, telescoping sheaths, or imaging guidance (rotational angiography, intravascular ultrasound [IVUS]) can sometimes allow crossing frame cells to reach the coronary ostium. In some cases, retrograde access via the radial or contralateral femoral approach with specific catheter shapes or use of mother-and-child catheter systems can facilitate engagement. If catastrophic coronary obstruction occurs and cannot be managed percutaneously, emergent surgical revascularization via coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) or urgent percutaneous coronary intervention with chimney stenting may be necessary.

Long-term preservation of coronary access can be optimized by pre-selecting THVs with designs more amenable to future PCI and by performing implantation techniques that minimize obstruction risk. Even with preventive measures, operators must be prepared for remedial interventions and plan for access strategies in the index procedure.

# Assessment of coronary anatomy in pre-TAVI CT

Multidetector computed tomography (CT) is a cornerstone of pre-TAVI assessment, providing high-resolution anatomic detail that informs prosthesis sizing, access planning, and coronary risk stratification. Dedicated pre-TAVI CT protocols include ECG-gated acquisitions during contrast administration to evaluate the aortic root, sinuses, sinotubular junction, coronary ostia heights, and the course of the coronary arteries. Assessment of coronary anatomy on pre-TAVI CT serves three purposes: estimating the risk of coronary obstruction at the time of TAVI or ViV, planning commissural alignment relative to the coronary ostia, and identifying significant coronary lesions or high-risk plaque characteristics that may prompt preprocedural or staged PCI.

Key measurements and anatomic features to report include coronary ostial heights (distance from the aortic annular plane to the coronary ostia), sinus of Valsalva dimensions (diameters and volumes), sinotubular junction height and diameter, leaflet morphology and calcification burden, and the spatial relationship between aortic valve leaflets and the coronary ostia. Coronary ostial heights less than approximately 10–12 mm are often considered low and increase the risk of obstruction, especially when sinuses are small. A small sinus of Valsalva (typically diameter <30 mm, though the threshold depends on valve and patient specifics) and a narrow sinotubular junction further elevate obstruction risk. The length and bulk of calcified native leaflets are also important: tall, bulky leaflets with heavy calcification are more likely to be displaced outward and obstruct the ostia.

Pre-TAVI CT can also visualize the coronary arteries for the presence of significant stenoses. Although CT angiography (CTA) may be limited by heavy coronary calcification in the elderly, it can identify proximal and mid-vessel lesions that are clinically significant. CTA may underestimate stenosis severity in heavily calcified vessels; therefore, invasive coronary angiography with physiologic assessment remains the standard when therapeutic decisions depend on lesion significance. Preprocedural CT also allows simulation of virtual transcatheter valve implantation, using software that models prosthesis frame position and predicts interactions with native anatomy. Virtual valve implantation can help anticipate whether leaflet displacement will reach the coronary ostia and can guide decisions such as performing BASILICA or prophylactic coronary protection.

When interpreting CT, the operator should consider dynamic factors: the aortic root dimensions change with cardiac cycle, and measurements at end-diastole versus systole can differ. Therefore, using an ECG-gated acquisition and reporting dimensions in standardized phases is important for reproducibility. Coronary dominance patterns and anomalous coronary origins should be noted because they affect risk: an anomalous or low-origin right coronary artery, for instance, may be particularly vulnerable.

# Management of ischemic symptoms after ViV and redo TAVI

Ischemic symptoms after valve-in-valve or redo TAVI present a complex diagnostic and therapeutic challenge. Pathophysiologically, ischemia may arise from progression or unrecognized CAD, prosthesis-related coronary obstruction or flow limitation, altered coronary hemodynamics after valve implantation, or technical issues impairing access and hence timely revascularization. The approach to evaluation and management must integrate clinical assessment, noninvasive testing, invasive coronary angiography, and consideration of prosthesis anatomy.

First, clinical assessment should characterize ischemic symptoms, their timing relative to the procedure, associated heart failure signs, and any biomarker elevation. Troponin elevations are common after TAVI and must be interpreted in context; a rising or markedly elevated troponin with new wall motion abnormalities suggests procedural MI. When patients present later with exertional angina or unexplained dyspnea, distinguishing ischemia from heart failure from prosthetic dysfunction is essential.

Noninvasive testing may include stress imaging (stress echocardiography, nuclear perfusion imaging, or stress cardiac MRI) to localize ischemia. However, the presence of prosthetic material and chronic changes can confound some modalities. In many cases, invasive coronary angiography remains indispensable to define coronary anatomy and to permit physiologic assessment. When angiography is planned, the operator should be prepared for potentially difficult coronary engagement due to the implanted valve(s), particularly after ViV or when a supra-annular THV with high stent frame overlies the sinuses. Preprocedural CT review, knowledge of the valve type and orientation, and use of tailored catheter shapes increase the likelihood of successful selective engagement.

If obstructive coronary disease is found and selective engagement is achievable, standard PCI techniques apply, with attention to contrast load and renal protection in older patients. Physiologic testing with FFR or iFR can guide revascularization decisions for intermediate lesions. If selective engagement is not possible due to prosthesis obstruction, alternative strategies include non-selective angiography through the valve cells to estimate coronary flow, use of coronary guidewires advanced across frame cells under imaging guidance, or use of mother-and-child catheters and extension devices to reach the ostium. In some cases, non-selective coronary injection combined with pressure wire assessment can provide functional information.

When ischemia results from prosthesis-related coronary obstruction (for example, after ViV when displaced surgical bioprosthetic leaflets occlude an ostium), urgent remedial strategies include chimney stenting, BASILICA-like leaflet laceration if feasible, and in extreme situations, emergent surgical revascularization. Chimney stenting can restore flow but may complicate future ViV procedures and carries risks of stent deformation or late thrombosis. If structural causes are not remediable percutaneously and symptoms are severe, surgical options including reoperation and CABG can be considered, taking into account patient comorbidity and operative risk.

Longer term, clinicians must plan for future access and surveillance. For patients with ViV, monitoring for progressive coronary compromise, valve degeneration, or in-stent restenosis is important. Antiplatelet and anticoagulation strategies should balance thrombotic and bleeding risks, particularly when coronary stents and prosthetic valves coexist. Decisions regarding duration of DAPT after PCI in patients with prosthetic valves should adhere to guideline recommendations while accounting for surgical risk, recent bleeding events, and the complexity of coronary stenting.

Conclusion

Managing CAD in patients undergoing structural aortic valve interventions demands an integrated strategy that begins with meticulous preprocedural imaging and planning and extends through device selection, procedural technique, and postprocedural surveillance. Timing of PCI relative to TAVI must be individualized based on coronary lesion severity, ischemic risk, and anatomical factors that affect future coronary access. Prosthesis design and commissural alignment have major implications for coronary engagement, and techniques such as BASILICA, coronary wiring, and chimney stenting play roles in preventing or treating coronary obstruction. Pre-TAVI CT provides essential measurements and simulation capabilities that guide risk stratification and procedural planning. When ischemic symptoms occur after ViV or redo TAVI, a systematic evaluation combining clinical assessment, noninvasive testing, and invasive angiography, informed by knowledge of valve anatomy, is necessary to determine the appropriate remedial approach. Anticipating future coronary needs when choosing valves and planning procedures remains a cornerstone of care for patients with both significant CAD and aortic valve disease.

Introduction

Deciding between surgical and transcatheter approaches for valvular heart disease and infective endocarditis requires integration of patient-specific risk, anatomic suitability, prosthesis type, microbiology, and expected procedural outcomes. The choice is not binary; it involves estimating short- and long-term mortality and morbidity, predicting technical feasibility, and balancing the risks of reintervention and recurrent infection with the benefits of less invasive approaches. This chapter reviews commonly used risk stratification tools, formal indications for surgery in native and prosthetic valve endocarditis, technical and anatomic challenges encountered when removing transcatheter prostheses or performing aortic root replacement, comparative outcomes of surgical versus transcatheter reintervention in high-risk cohorts, and special perioperative considerations in frail and comorbid patients. Emphasis is placed on pathophysiologic rationales for operative intervention and the clinical reasoning that guides therapy selection.

# Risk scoring systems: EuroSCORE II, STS score and their applications

Risk scoring models attempt to provide an evidence-based estimate of perioperative mortality and major morbidity to inform shared decision-making and procedural planning. Two widely used tools are the European System for Cardiac Operative Risk Evaluation II (EuroSCORE II) and the Society of Thoracic Surgeons (STS) Adult Cardiac Surgery Risk Model. Both are derived from large surgical registries and use clinical variables to estimate operative mortality; the STS models also predict specific morbidities such as stroke, renal failure, prolonged ventilation, and need for reoperation.

EuroSCORE II is calculated from variables including age, sex, renal impairment, extracardiac arteriopathy, poor mobility, previous cardiac surgery, chronic lung disease, active endocarditis, critical preoperative state, left ventricular function, pulmonary hypertension, urgency of surgery, weight of the intervention, and other factors. It outputs an estimated percentage probability of in-hospital or 30-day mortality. EuroSCORE II is convenient and widely used in European centers and for international comparisons. Its calibration can drift over time as populations and techniques evolve; thus local validation is desirable.

The STS risk score family includes distinct models for isolated aortic or mitral valve procedures, combined procedures, and for specific morbidity endpoints. Key inputs include age, sex, body surface area, chronic lung disease, renal function (creatinine or dialysis), neurologic disease, cardiogenic shock, urgency, frailty markers, left ventricular ejection fraction, prior cardiac operations, and concomitant coronary artery bypass grafting. The STS models give separate probabilities for operative mortality and major complications and are commonly used in North America and in guideline recommendations for decision-making regarding transcatheter therapies. The STS-PROM (Predicted Risk of Mortality) is often used to define low-, intermediate-, and high-risk strata; for example, an STS-PROM >8% has been used historically to define high surgical risk, though thresholds vary by indication and era.

Applications and limitations: Risk scores are most helpful as one component of a multi-disciplinary assessment rather than as absolute determinants. They are useful for informing consent, benchmarking institutional performance, and triaging patients for heart-team discussions. Limitations include imperfect calibration in certain subgroups (for example, patients undergoing reoperation, endocarditis, or complex root reconstruction), inability to capture nuanced anatomic variables (such as extent of annular destruction, presence of fistula, or size and location of vegetations), and inadequate incorporation of frailty and cognitive function in older adults. Newer risk paradigms integrate objective frailty measures, nutritional status, and quality-of-life projections. Clinicians should apply risk scores in the context of individual patient goals, expected longer-term outcomes, and the team’s technical experience with either surgical or transcatheter approaches.

# Indications for surgical valve replacement in native and prosthetic valve endocarditis

Indications for surgical intervention in infective endocarditis are driven by complications that threaten survival or are unlikely to be controlled by antibiotics alone. Guidelines from major societies (e.g., European Society of Cardiology, American Heart Association) provide criteria to identify patients who should be considered for early surgery. The major indications include heart failure due to valvular dysfunction, uncontrolled infection, and prevention of embolic events.

Heart failure: Severe valvular regurgitation or obstruction causing hemodynamic instability, cardiogenic shock, or persistent pulmonary edema is a surgical emergency. For native aortic or mitral valve endocarditis, acute severe regurgitation with signs of pulmonary edema or low cardiac output typically mandates urgent valve repair or replacement. Left-sided prosthetic valve dysfunction causing obstruction or severe regurgitation with heart failure also commonly requires operation. Decision-making includes assessment of left ventricular ejection fraction and other measures of hemodynamic compromise.

Uncontrolled infection: Indications for surgery include abscess formation (perivalvular extension), fistulae, large or expanding vegetations despite appropriate antibiotic therapy, and persistent bacteremia lasting more than 5–7 days with organisms known to be difficult to eradicate (e.g., fungi, multidrug-resistant organisms) or with evidence of persistent septic emboli. Perivalvular abscess, especially in the aortic root or annular area, portends a high risk of conduction disturbances, pseudoaneurysm, and sepsis; surgery is generally required to debride infected tissue and reconstruct the annulus/root.

Prevention of embolism: Vegetations larger than approximately 10 mm, especially those that are mobile, are associated with increased risk of systemic embolization. The presence of recurrent emboli despite antibiotics, large vegetation size (>10 mm) with additional risk features (mobility, prior embolus), or vegetations >15 mm in some guidelines are considered thresholds for surgical consideration to prevent further embolic events. The timing of surgery must weigh embolic risk against procedural risk in the setting of recent cerebral embolism; in ischemic stroke without hemorrhage, early surgery is often safe and beneficial, while in intracranial hemorrhage surgery may be delayed.

Native versus prosthetic: Prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) carries higher morbidity and mortality and a higher incidence of perivalvular complications compared with native valve endocarditis. Indications for surgical intervention are similar but the threshold for operation is often lower because of the greater risk of annular involvement, dehiscence, and difficulty in achieving sterilization of infected prosthetic material. Early PVE (occurring within 60 days of valve implantation) and late PVE may differ in microbiology and pathogenesis, but both may require explantation of infected prostheses in the presence of structural dysfunction, abscess, or persistent infection.

Microbiology and timing: Certain pathogens drive the urgency of surgical management. Candida and other fungal endocarditis and infections due to multidrug-resistant organisms have poor response to medical therapy alone; early operation is recommended when feasible. Staphylococcus aureus, particularly methicillin-resistant strains, and enterococcal infections also have a higher propensity for complications; the presence of persistent bacteremia or the failure of high-quality antibiotics to sterilize the blood is an indication for surgery.

Valve repair versus replacement: When possible, valve repair is preferred for mitral valve endocarditis because it preserves the subvalvular apparatus and has better long-term outcomes in selected patients. However, the extent of destruction, presence of annular abscess, or prosthetic involvement often mandates replacement. Choice of prosthesis in endocarditis is individualized, with attention to the patient’s age, comorbidities, and the potential need for future interventions.

Decision-making framework: The heart team evaluates hemodynamics, structural destruction on echocardiography (transesophageal echocardiography is central), extracardiac complications (cerebral emboli, mycotic aneurysms), microbiology and antibiotic response, and patient comorbidities including frailty. Timing is categorized as emergent (within 24 hours), urgent (within days), or elective (after a period of stabilization) based on the clinical scenario.

# Technical challenges of explanting transcatheter valves and root replacement scenarios

The increasing adoption of transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) has created new technical challenges when these valves fail, become infected, or require removal. Explantation of transcatheter valves and reconstruction of the aortic root often present technical complexity beyond standard redo valve operations due to endothelialization, stent frame endothelial ingrowth, valve-in-valve constructs, and interaction with annular or root anatomy.

Biologic behavior of transcatheter valves: Transcatheter valves consist of a stent frame (often nitinol or stainless steel/cobalt-chromium), leaflets typically made from bioprosthetic tissue, and a fabric skirt. Over time the stent frame apposes and integrates with the aortic annulus and adjacent tissue. Endothelialization and fibrous ingrowth anchor the frame firmly, making complete extraction difficult and potentially injurious to the annulus, coronary ostia, and surrounding structures. When a valve-in-valve (ViV) is present, multiple layers of stent and leaflets can further complicate removal.

Explant techniques: Explant begins with careful exposure and identification of the frame edges. Strategies include sharp dissection to free fibrous tissue, cutting or fragmenting the frame, and in some instances leaving portions of the frame in place while reconstructing the annulus. Extraction risks include annular rupture, aortic root disruption, damage to the mitral-aortic curtain, injury to the conduction system, and coronary ostial injury. Surgeons must be prepared for extended cardiopulmonary bypass times, complex reconstruction, and the possibility of root replacement.

Aortic root replacement scenarios: When infection, annular destruction, or intraoperative injury compromises the native aortic root, replacement of the aortic root is often required. Options include composite valve grafts (Bentall procedure), root replacement with a biologic homograft, or use of xenograft roots. Homografts are attractive in endocarditis because of their resistance to infection and the absence of sewing ring prosthetic material, but availability is limited and long-term durability varies. Composite mechanical or bioprosthetic grafts provide excellent hemodynamics and durability but entail implanting prosthetic material in an infected field, which can be mitigated by aggressive debridement and prolonged antibiotics.

Conduction and coronary considerations: Explantation near the membranous septum risks conduction tissue injury with consequent atrioventricular block requiring permanent pacing. Proximity of the transcatheter frame to coronary ostia complicates safe removal; in some cases exteriorization of the left coronary artery or coronary reimplantation may be necessary during root replacement. The presence of a prior surgical graft or conduit also affects approach and exposure.

Planning and intraoperative decision-making: Preoperative imaging, including gated CT angiography, is essential to assess frame position, leaflet calcification, coronary heights, and annular dimensions. In endocarditis, CT can help identify abscesses, pseudoaneurysms, and extension to adjacent structures. The heart team should anticipate the need for alternative strategies—such as root replacement, patch reconstruction of annular defects, or adjunctive coronary bypass—and have appropriate grafts, homografts, and tissue available. Intraoperative flexibility is critical because the extent of debridement and the degree of tissue destruction may only be fully appreciated after opening the aorta.

# Comparative outcomes of surgical vs transcatheter reintervention in high-risk patients

Comparative outcomes between repeat surgery and transcatheter reinterventions (such as valve-in-valve procedures) reflect a balance of short-term procedural risk and long-term durability and complications. Randomized data comparing surgery to transcatheter reintervention for failed TAVR or for infected transcatheter valves are limited, so observational registries and cohort studies guide practice.

Short-term outcomes: In high-risk or frail patients, transcatheter reinterventions (for example, valve-in-valve TAVR in failed bioprostheses) are associated with lower immediate procedural morbidity and mortality compared with redo open surgery. Shorter operative times, avoidance of sternotomy and cardiopulmonary bypass, and more rapid recovery often favor transcatheter approaches in patients with prohibitive surgical risk. For primary TAVR failure unrelated to infection (structural valve degeneration or paravalvular leak), valve-in-valve TAVR can be an excellent option when anatomic factors permit.

Long-term outcomes and durability: Surgical reoperation provides the advantage of complete removal of dysfunctional or infected prosthetic tissue, comprehensive debridement, and the ability to reconstruct damaged annular and root structures. In cases of infection, surgical explantation with debridement and reconstruction generally has superior eradication rates compared with conservative or purely percutaneous approaches. For noninfectious valve degeneration, the long-term durability of valve-in-valve TAVR may be limited by patient–prosthesis mismatch due to decreased effective orifice area, residual gradients, coronary obstruction risk, and leaflets’ hemodynamics. Repeated transcatheter valves can compound these issues.

Endocarditis-specific outcomes: For infective endocarditis involving transcatheter valves or prosthetic material, early surgical removal plus reconstruction is associated with lower rates of persistent infection and recurrent sepsis in many series. Percutaneous interventions or conservative management may be considered in selected patients with prohibitive surgical risk, but the risk of uncontrolled infection and perivalvular complications must be carefully considered. Observational data suggest higher recurrence of infection and worse long-term survival when infected prostheses are not explanted when feasible.

Risk-adjusted decision-making: Comparative outcome data emphasize individualized decisions. For a frail, comorbid patient with a failed transcatheter leaflets causing high gradients but no infection, valve-in-valve TAVR may provide symptom relief with acceptable short-term risk. In contrast, a patient with prosthetic valve endocarditis and root abscess is generally best treated with surgical explantation and reconstruction despite elevated operative risk because the likelihood of cure with medical therapy alone is low. The heart team must incorporate risk scores, frailty assessment, expected quality-of-life improvement, and the patient’s priorities. In many centers, hybrid strategies (for example, percutaneous bridging to definitive surgery or staged procedures) are employed in selected high-risk patients.

Quality metrics and institutional experience: Outcomes of complex redo operations and explantation procedures correlate strongly with institutional and operator experience. Centers with multidisciplinary programs that include cardiology, cardiac surgery, infectious disease, imaging, and geriatric or palliative care achieve better outcomes through coordinated care and appropriate patient selection. Referral to high-volume centers should be considered for complex reinterventions.

# Operative perioperative considerations in frail and comorbid patients

Frail and comorbid patients represent a growing proportion of those referred for valve interventions. Frailty, multimorbidity, and cognitive impairment are independent predictors of adverse outcomes after cardiac surgery and transcatheter procedures. Effective perioperative management requires objective assessment of frailty, optimization of reversible conditions, perioperative planning to reduce complications, and shared decision-making that aligns interventions with the patient’s goals.

Frailty assessment and prognostication: Frailty captures physiologic reserve and vulnerability to stressors. Tools include gait speed (e.g., 5-meter walk time), grip strength, the Clinical Frailty Scale, Timed Up and Go, and composite indices such as the frailty phenotype or frailty index. Objective measures correlate with increased mortality, prolonged hospitalization, and lower quality-of-life gains after valve procedures. Incorporating frailty into risk assessment improves prediction over scores based solely on comorbidity. Cognitive screening and assessment of social support are also essential for postoperative recovery planning.

Preoperative optimization: Attention to nutrition, anemia, glycemic control, and pulmonary function can mitigate risks. Correcting reversible anemia and optimizing iron stores reduces transfusion requirements and may improve outcomes. Prehabilitation programs that include physical therapy and nutritional support can enhance functional reserve. In patients with chronic kidney disease, reviewing medication nephrotoxicity risks and optimizing volume status preoperatively reduces postoperative dialysis risk. Multidisciplinary involvement of geriatrics, nutritionists, and rehabilitation services should be initiated early.

Anesthetic and intraoperative strategies: Minimally invasive or transcatheter approaches may reduce physiologic stress and facilitate faster recovery in frail patients; however, they can be limited by anatomic factors or infection. When surgery is chosen, careful anesthetic planning to reduce hemodynamic instability, minimize cardiopulmonary bypass duration, and employ myocardial protection strategies is critical. Use of cerebral oximetry, tight glucose control, and avoidance of excessive transfusion are components of best practice. Consideration of regional anesthesia adjuncts and multimodal analgesia can reduce opioid-related delirium risk.

Perioperative infection control and antibiotics: In endocarditis or when infection is suspected, perioperative antimicrobial therapy must be coordinated with infectious disease specialists. Timing and duration of preoperative antibiotics should be individualized; in some urgent cases surgery proceeds after hours of therapy when indicated for hemodynamic collapse. Postoperative antibiotic plans depend on operative findings, tissue cultures, and whether prosthetic material was implanted.

Postoperative care and discharge planning: Frail patients have higher rates of delirium, functional decline, and prolonged rehabilitation needs. Early mobilization, delirium prevention protocols (sleep hygiene, minimizing deliriogenic medications, reorientation), and aggressive physiotherapy are essential. Discharge planning should involve assessment of the home environment, availability of caregivers, and need for inpatient rehabilitation. Advance care planning and alignment of postoperative goals with patient preferences are part of high-quality perioperative care.

Palliative considerations: For some frail patients with limited life expectancy or high procedural risk and low expected symptomatic benefit, nonoperative management or palliative approaches may better align with the patient’s goals. Discussions should frame the expected trajectory with and without intervention, including the likelihood of symptom relief, procedural risks, rehabilitation needs, and impact on independence. Multidisciplinary conversations including cardiology, cardiac surgery, geriatrics, and palliative care enhance patient-centered decisions.

Conclusion

Surgical and transcatheter valve interventions each have roles determined by anatomic suitability, infection status, patient risk, and goals of care. Risk scores such as EuroSCORE II and STS provide useful quantitative estimates but must be integrated with frailty assessment and detailed imaging. Endocarditis—particularly with annular destruction or root abscess—often compels surgical debridement and reconstruction, whereas noninfectious structural valve degeneration may be amenable to transcatheter solutions in the appropriate anatomic context. Explantation of transcatheter valves and aortic root replacement are technically demanding and require meticulous preoperative planning and institutional expertise. Comparative outcomes favor less invasive approaches for short-term recovery in high-risk patients but favor surgical explantation when infection control or durable reconstruction are priorities. In frail and comorbid patients, individualized assessment, preoperative optimization, and multidisciplinary planning are essential to achieve the best possible outcomes that align with patient values.

Introduction

Bioprosthetic aortic valves are increasingly chosen for surgical and transcatheter aortic valve replacement because they avoid the need for long-term anticoagulation and have good hemodynamics and quality-of-life outcomes in the short and medium term. However, they are subject to structural valve degeneration (SVD) over years to decades, and modern management of patients with bioprosthetic aortic valves requires a lifetime perspective: selection of the initial prosthesis with future interventions in mind, a surveillance strategy that detects early hemodynamic changes and other complications, and an anticipatory plan to preserve options for reintervention. This chapter provides a practical, clinically oriented framework covering patient-centered planning, prosthesis selection features that influence future reinterventions, surveillance protocols and reintervention triggers, strategies to minimize prosthesis–patient mismatch over time, and how to counsel patients about trade-offs and potential procedural trajectories.

# Patient-centered planning: age, comorbidities and expected longevity

The choice of a bioprosthetic valve and the strategy for long-term management must begin with a personalized assessment of the patient's age, comorbid conditions, life expectancy, lifestyle preferences and willingness to accept reintervention risk. Age remains a dominant influence on valve durability and on the balance between a mechanical valve (which offers durability but requires lifelong anticoagulation) and a bioprosthesis (which typically avoids long-term anticoagulation but may require reintervention). Contemporary guidelines often recommend bioprostheses for older patients and mechanical valves for younger patients, but the age thresholds are shifting as transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) and valve-in-valve (ViV) options expand.

Expected longevity should be integrated with comorbidity burden and functional status. For a relatively healthy 70–75-year-old, anticipated life expectancy may exceed the time to clinically relevant SVD for many surgical bioprostheses, but the availability of viable percutaneous reinterventions (ViV TAVR) often tilts the balance toward a bioprosthesis. Conversely, a patient aged 50–60 years with a low surgical risk and long life expectancy might still be counseled toward mechanical valves if the priority is to minimize lifetime reinterventions, particularly when anticoagulation is acceptable and bleeding risk is low. Comorbid conditions that reduce life expectancy (e.g., advanced malignancy, frailty, end-stage organ disease) may favor a bioprosthesis even in younger patients. Conversely, conditions that increase bleeding risk (prior intracranial hemorrhage, severe thrombocytopenia, or labile anticoagulation) or make anticoagulation hazardous support the use of a bioprosthesis.

Other patient-level considerations include the patient’s preferences regarding anticoagulation, work or lifestyle constraints that make warfarin management impractical, pregnancy considerations in women of childbearing potential, and the patient’s acceptance of future reinterventions and their potential morbidity. Shared decision-making is essential: present lifetime trajectories—what a pathway with a mechanical valve means versus a bioprosthesis plus likely ViV procedures—frame the trade-offs in terms of quality-adjusted life expectancy, expected number and complexity of future interventions, and how future options might be preserved or compromised by the initial device choice.

# Choosing prosthesis type with future re-intervention in mind (frame height, commissural markers)

When planning an aortic bioprosthesis, surgeons and interventionalists must anticipate not only immediate hemodynamic performance but also how valve design affects future feasibility, safety and technical aspects of reintervention. Important design features include whether the valve is intra-annular or supra-annular, stented versus stentless or sutureless, frame height and cell geometry, the presence of radiopaque or polymerized commissural markers, leaflet mounting (inside or outside frame), and the internal diameter (true internal orifice area) of the implanted prosthesis.

Frame height and cell architecture influence coronary access and the risk of coronary obstruction during future transcatheter valve-in-valve procedures. Valves with tall external sewing rings or with leaflets mounted externally (leaflets that extend above the annulus when a new valve is implanted within them) can create a cylinder of tissue that, when displaced by a new transcatheter valve, may obstruct coronary ostia. Lower frame heights and designs that facilitate leaflet flaring or intentional leaflet modification reduce this risk. Commissural alignment markers—radiopaque markers or suturable landmarks that enable precise rotational orientation during implantation—are important for later transcatheter interventions, where accurate alignment can help keep transcatheter valve struts away from coronary ostia and maintain future coronary access.

The true effective orifice area (EOA) of an implanted valve is determined by the valve’s internal dimensions, which are often smaller than the labeled external or nominal size. For lifetime planning it is crucial to select a device with the largest possible internal diameter consistent with patient anatomy to minimize prosthesis–patient mismatch (PPM) and to enable a larger inner dimension for subsequent ViV implantation. When a small annulus is encountered, options include annular enlargement procedures at the time of surgery (e.g., Nicks, Manougian, Konno, or patch augmentation) to allow implantation of a larger surgical valve. Sutureless valves can offer larger EOAs in small annuli but have different profiles for subsequent ViV. Stentless or root replacement options (e.g., full root replacement or homograft) may provide larger EOA but carry other trade-offs in surgical complexity and future reinterventions.

Commissural alignment is increasingly recognized as critical for future coronary access. At the time of surgical AVR, orienting the prosthetic valve so that its commissures do not overlie coronary ostia and recording the orientation in the operative note facilitates future TAVR planning. For transcatheter prostheses, modern systems provide markers and techniques to orient the device to minimize overlap of frame posts with coronary ostia. Thoughtful selection of prosthesis design and careful intraoperative orientation are decisive steps in preserving coronary access and reducing the risk of coronary obstruction at reintervention.

# Surveillance protocols and triggers for reintervention

Lifelong surveillance aims to detect structural valve degeneration (SVD), prosthetic valve dysfunction, thrombosis or infective endocarditis early enough to plan reintervention electively rather than emergently. Surveillance encompasses clinical assessment, echocardiography, and—when indicated—multimodality imaging with computed tomography (CT) for valve morphology and coronary anatomy.

Baseline assessment should be performed before hospital discharge or within 30 days post-implantation and include transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) to document prosthetic gradients, effective orifice area (EOA), and baseline regurgitation. Later surveillance frequency depends on time since implantation and clinical risk factors for accelerated degeneration. Reasonable general recommendations are:

- Routine TTE at 3–12 months after implantation to establish early post-procedural hemodynamics and then at 12 months.
- If the valve is stable and the patient is asymptomatic, repeat TTE annually beginning at 5 years after implantation for most patients. For younger patients, those with small prostheses, or those with risk factors for accelerated SVD (renal failure, hyperparathyroidism, metabolic disease, mechanical trauma), consider earlier and more frequent imaging (e.g., annually starting at 3 years).
- If any change in symptoms, new murmur, or clinical concern arises, perform immediate echocardiography.

Standard echocardiographic parameters to follow include peak aortic jet velocity, mean transvalvular gradient, Doppler-derived EOA and indexed EOA (iEOA; EOA / body surface area), and assessment of regurgitation. Reference ranges and thresholds relevant to prosthetic valves include normal mean gradient after bioprosthetic AVR typically in the range of 10–20 mmHg depending on valve size and design; absolute thresholds must be interpreted in the context of valve type. Definitions of prosthetic valve dysfunction often rely on the staging system for SVD:

- Stage 0: no SVD—normal prosthetic function.
- Stage 1 (morphologic SVD): structural abnormalities on imaging (leaflet thickening, calcification) without significant hemodynamic change.
- Stage 2 (hemodynamic SVD, moderate): moderate stenosis or regurgitation (e.g., mean gradient increase from baseline but not severe).
- Stage 3 (hemodynamic SVD, severe): severe stenosis (e.g., mean gradient ≥40 mmHg or peak velocity ≥4.0 m/s for native valves; for prosthetic valves, severe prosthetic stenosis corresponds to a new mean gradient ≥40 mmHg or an increase in mean gradient ≥20 mmHg from baseline) or severe regurgitation with signs of chamber remodeling or symptoms.

Triggers for consideration of reintervention include symptomatic prosthetic valve dysfunction (heart failure, syncope, angina) attributable to the valve, progressive LV dysfunction (decline in LVEF), progressive left ventricular hypertrophy or dilatation due to pressure overload, or hemodynamic criteria of severe prosthetic stenosis or regurgitation. Specifically, reintervention should be considered when:

- New or worsening symptoms are clearly attributable to valve dysfunction and objective imaging confirms severe SVD.
- Hemodynamic criteria for severe obstruction are met: mean transvalvular gradient ≥40 mmHg or peak aortic velocity ≥4.0 m/s, or a significant increase in gradient compared with baseline (e.g., increase ≥20 mmHg). For prosthetic valve stenosis, absolute gradients must be interpreted in the context of prosthesis type and valve size, which influence baseline expected gradients.
- Severe prosthetic regurgitation with resultant volume overload and LV dilatation or decline in LVEF.
- Evidence of valve thrombosis unresponsive to medical therapy, persistent or recurrent infective endocarditis with prosthetic dysfunction, or recurrent embolic events despite appropriate therapy.

When findings are borderline or discordant, multimodality imaging helps: transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) affords better morphological assessment; cardiac CT can detect leaflet calcification, hypo-attenuated leaflet thickening (HALT), pannus versus thrombus, and valve geometry relevant to procedural planning. Coronary CT angiography (CTA) becomes essential when planning ViV TAVR to assess coronary ostial height, sinus of Valsalva dimensions and virtual transcatheter valve–to–coronary distance.

# Strategies to minimize prosthesis–patient mismatch over time

Prosthesis–patient mismatch (PPM) occurs when the prosthetic valve’s effective orifice area (EOA) is too small relative to the patient’s body size, resulting in higher gradients and suboptimal hemodynamics. PPM increases risk for persistent LV hypertrophy, heart failure symptoms, and may accelerate SVD. Indexed EOA (iEOA = EOA / body surface area) is used to quantify PPM. Commonly accepted thresholds for the aortic position are:

- No PPM: iEOA > 0.85 cm^2/m^2
- Moderate PPM: iEOA 0.65–0.85 cm^2/m^2
- Severe PPM: iEOA ≤ 0.65 cm^2/m^2

These thresholds may require adjustment with obesity (body surface area effect) and in female patients. Minimizing PPM at the index operation is the most effective strategy because PPM is difficult to remedy later and contributes to earlier symptomatic deterioration.

Preoperative planning should include accurate annular sizing and calculation of projected iEOA for candidate prostheses using manufacturer EOA tables and institutional performance data rather than labeled size alone. When projected iEOA for all available standard prostheses would result in moderate or severe PPM, surgical strategies include root enlargement (Nicks, Manougian, Konno procedures) to allow implantation of a larger prosthesis, choosing supra-annular or stentless valve designs that provide larger EOAs for a given annulus, or selecting sutureless valves that can permit larger internal diameters. For transcatheter approaches, choosing supra-annular valves with larger EOAs may be preferable in small annuli.

Planning for future ViV procedures also affects PPM management. ViV implantation inevitably reduces the final internal orifice area relative to the original surgical valve because the transcatheter valve frame and leaflets occupy space. Therefore, it is prudent at the time of index surgery to avoid implantation of prostheses with very small internal diameters, and when small annuli cannot be avoided, to consider annular enlargement or root enlargement to provide room for a larger future ViV valve. When ViV is anticipated, intentionally selecting surgical valves that can undergo balloon-expandable frame fracture (ring fracture) is another strategy to allow larger final diameters at reintervention; however, not all surgical valve sewing rings are suitable for fracture and the technique carries its own risks. Knowledge of the implanted valve brand/model and its inner diameter is essential for future ViV planning; thus, meticulous operative documentation and long-term documentation in the medical record (including valve make/model/serial number) are important.

Post-implantation strategies include appropriate blood pressure control and medical therapy to reduce LV afterload, which can improve effective hemodynamics and reduce the clinical impact of mild PPM. Serial echocardiography to track gradients and LV remodeling will identify early adverse effects of PPM and inform timing of reintervention when appropriate.

# Counseling patients about trade-offs and procedural trajectories

Counseling is a longitudinal process that should occur before the initial valve selection and be revisited at key decision points during follow-up. Patients must understand the lifetime implications of their initial valve choice and the probable sequence of future interventions, including probabilities and types of reinterventions, potential complications, and how choices now will constrain or enable future options.

Key elements to communicate include:

- Durability expectations: Bioprosthetic valves have finite durability with increasing rates of SVD over time. Depending on valve type and patient age, the median time to clinically significant SVD may range from 8–20+ years, but younger patients tend to experience more rapid degeneration.
- Reintervention pathways: Describe likely trajectories—for example, a patient receiving a surgical bioprosthesis might later be a candidate for transcatheter ViV TAVR, for redo surgical AVR, or for hybrid strategies. Explain the technical factors that influence feasibility of ViV (annulus size, coronary height, valve type and orientation), and that some valve choices at implantation can preserve or limit those options.
- Procedural risks and outcomes: Provide data-based estimates for procedural mortality, stroke, pacemaker requirement, and other complications for both initial and potential future procedures (surgery vs TAVR), emphasizing that reinterventions may carry higher risk than initial elective procedures, particularly if they occur emergently.
- Anticoagulation and lifestyle: For mechanical valves, discuss lifelong anticoagulation and its implications for bleeding risk, monitoring, and pregnancy. For bioprosthetic valves, discuss the typical limited period of anticoagulation (often 3 months of warfarin or 1–3 months of dual antiplatelet therapy depending on institution and indication), but also discuss the potential need for restarting anticoagulation if valve thrombosis develops.
- Coronary access and future coronary interventions: Explain that some valves or valve orientations may make future coronary angiography or percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) more difficult, and describe efforts taken to minimize this risk (commissural alignment, valve selection).
- Surveillance commitments: Emphasize the need for regular follow-up and imaging—early detection of valve dysfunction improves chances of elective, safer reinterventions. Provide a clear schedule for monitoring and describe symptoms that should prompt urgent evaluation (e.g., new dyspnea, syncope, chest pain, fever).
- Uncertainty and shared decision-making: Be explicit about uncertainties—individual variability in valve degeneration rates, evolving technologies (improvements in TAVR and ViV techniques), and the possibility that strategies considered standard today may change. Encourage patients to state their values and preferences (acceptance of anticoagulation, desire to avoid repeat surgeries, tolerance of procedural risk) and use decision aids where available.

Documenting the discussion and the patient’s preferences in the medical record is essential for future providers. If a patient elects a bioprosthesis with the plan for future ViV, ensure that the implanted valve’s brand, model and size are recorded and that the operative note documents commissural orientation and any annular enlargement performed. Similarly, provide the patient with written information about their valve and the recommended follow-up schedule.

Conclusion

Lifetime management of patients with bioprosthetic aortic valves requires foresight at the index operation, a structured surveillance program to detect early valve dysfunction, and strategies aimed at preserving future therapeutic options. Thoughtful prosthesis selection—accounting for frame design, commissural markers, internal diameter and the need for annular enlargement—combined with individualized surveillance and patient-centered counseling, maximizes the probability of safe, effective staged care over a patient’s lifespan. Regular reassessment of clinical status and imaging findings allows reinterventions to be timed electively, reducing risk and optimizing outcomes.

Introduction

Advances in cardiac computed tomography (CT) and three-dimensional (3D) image processing have transformed procedural planning for structural heart interventions. Where once operators relied primarily on two-dimensional imaging, angiography, and intra-procedural judgment, CT-based 3D modeling and virtual implantation now permit pre-procedural simulation of device–anatomy interaction. These tools can predict how prosthetic frames and skirts will sit relative to the aortic root, native leaflets, and coronary ostia; estimate the risk of coronary obstruction; and inform device selection and implant depth. A thorough appreciation of the steps in CT segmentation, the biophysical and geometric principles underlying virtual deployment, the metrics used to quantify valve-to-coronary relationships, and the limitations of current simulation approaches is essential for clinicians who interpret these models and translate them into procedural decisions.

# Workflow for CT segmentation and 3D model generation

CT-based 3D modeling begins with acquisition of high-quality cardiac CT angiography optimized for structural heart planning. This typically involves electrocardiographic (ECG)-gated contrast-enhanced imaging of the heart and aortic root, with isotropic or near-isotropic voxels and slice thickness usually in the range of 0.5–1.0 mm. Proper bolus timing to opacify the aortic root and coronary ostia is critical; inadequate contrast results in poor delineation of leaflet and leaflet/calcification borders. Multiphase acquisitions are often obtained to capture dynamic changes in annular size and leaflet position across the cardiac cycle; systolic or mid-diastolic phases may be selected depending on the structure of interest.

Segmentation is the process of delineating anatomic structures from the CT voxel data and converting them into 3D surface or volumetric meshes. Automated or semi-automated tools are used to segment the cardiac blood pool, aortic root, native leaflets, valve leaflets (in the case of bioprosthetic valves), coronary arteries to their ostia, and adjacent structures such as the mitral valve apparatus when relevant. Initial steps typically rely on Hounsfield unit (HU) thresholding to separate contrast-enhanced blood from soft tissue. Contrast-enhanced blood typically measures in the range of 150–400 HU depending on timing and scanner; thresholds are set to include the blood pool while excluding surrounding tissue. Thresholding is complemented by region-growing algorithms that expand from seed points within the aortic lumen and by edge-detection filters to refine the boundary.

Calcified leaflet nodules and degenerative bioprosthetic material present a segmentation challenge because calcifications may have much higher HU values and can produce blooming artifacts. Therefore, manual editing by an experienced operator is frequently required to ensure accurate leaflet geometry. The aortic annulus is defined by the nadirs of the three cusp insertions in the virtual ring plane; segmentation software often allows interactive annular plane fitting to extract annular perimeter, area, and derived diameters. Sinus of Valsalva contours, sinotubular junction, ascending aorta dimensions, and coronary ostial coordinates are derived from the segmented root.

Once segmentation is complete, surface meshes are generated to create watertight 3D models. The meshes may be smoothed and decimated to optimize file size while preserving essential geometric fidelity. For finite-element or computational fluid dynamics simulations, volumetric tetrahedral meshes may be created. Important quality-control steps include visual inspection in multiple planes to ensure anatomical landmarks are preserved, and comparison of derived measurements (annular area, perimeter, coronary heights) with standard 2D CT measurements for consistency.

# Virtual valve deployment simulation and prediction of neo-skirt and leaflet position

Virtual valve deployment reproduces how a specific prosthetic device would expand within a patient’s anatomy. There are two broad categories of virtual implantation: geometric placement and physics-based finite element simulation. Geometric approaches position a parametric device model into the 3D anatomy according to specified implantation depth and orientation and then compute intersections with surrounding tissues. Physics-based simulations model device expansion behavior (radial force, frame deformation), interaction with deformable host tissue, and contact forces—producing more physiologically realistic predictions, especially in heavily calcified anatomies.

A central output of virtual deployment is the neo-skirt geometry: the final configuration of native leaflets and prosthetic skirt material that forms the new effective barrier between the aortic root and the coronary ostia or distal aorta. In native aortic valve TAVR, native leaflets are displaced outward by the frame and become part of the neo-skirt; their bulk and residual position determine both paravalvular regurgitation risk and potential coronary obstruction. In valve-in-valve procedures, the degenerated surgical valve leaflets and stent frame also interact with the implanted transcatheter valve to produce a new skirt profile.

During simulation, the software computes the 3D position of leaflets after expansion, including their height above the annulus, radial position relative to the sinus walls, and whether portions of the leaflet are folded or pinned against the frame. The resulting neo-skirt silhouette in cross-section and in en face views is used to evaluate whether it overlaps the coronary ostia. Leaflet thickness, calcific nodules, and the presence of stent posts from prior prostheses influence how much leaflet tissue protrudes into the sinuses. For self-expanding frames that elongate or flare into the sinotubular junction, the neo-skirt may be pushed higher; balloon-expandable frames tend to have more fixed expansion geometry.

Predicting leaflet position also informs hemodynamic consequences. If leaflets are pinned close to the sinotubular junction without adequate space for coronary flow, there is potential for impaired coronary filling, particularly if the sinuses are shallow. Conversely, if the neo-skirt leaves a channel to the coronary ostium, coronary flow may be preserved even with low coronary heights. Advanced simulations may model the size and shape of residual flow channels and estimate cross-sectional area, which correlates with potential coronary perfusion; some groups further simulate contrast or flow to quantify obstruction risk.

# Quantification of valve-to-coronary distances and predicted obstruction

Objective, reproducible metrics are required to quantify the proximity of a planned prosthesis to coronary ostia and to predict coronary obstruction risk. Traditional CT measurements include coronary height (vertical distance from the annular plane to the coronary ostium) and sinus of Valsalva diameter. Coronary height is typically measured perpendicular to the fitted annular plane. General clinical thresholds used to stratify obstruction risk include coronary height less than approximately 10–12 mm and sinus of Valsalva diameter less than ~30–32 mm as markers of increased risk. These thresholds are imperfect and context-dependent because obstructive risk also depends on leaflet length, leaflet bulk, and the anticipated final position of displaced leaflets.

Virtual implantation enables calculation of the valve-to-coronary (VTC) distance, a metric that is often more predictive than coronary height alone. VTC is defined as the minimal distance between the projected outer surface of the implanted transcatheter valve (including displaced leaflets / neo-skirt) and the coronary ostium. Calculation involves performing the virtual deployment at the intended implant depth and then measuring the orthogonal distance from the ostium to the nearest point on the device or neo-skirt. A commonly cited threshold is VTC < 4 mm as high risk for coronary obstruction, with intermediate risk for VTC 4–6 mm and low risk for VTC > 6 mm. These numbers derive from cohort studies comparing predicted VTC with occurrence of coronary obstruction, but thresholds vary across centers and device types.

Another related metric is the valve-to-sinotubular junction (VTSTJ) distance and the predicted residual ostial orifice area when the neo-skirt overlaps the ostium. Simulation can compute the minimal cross-sectional area of the remaining coronary inlet after virtual implantation. In borderline cases, computed neo-skirt geometry may show a small residual slit-like flow channel even when coronary height is low — such information can influence decisions about prophylactic measures such as guide catheter placement or wire protection.

It is essential to recognize that these calculations take into account device-specific external dimensions and skirt heights. Different devices have different skirt lengths, frame heights, and configurations of cells or posts, all of which change the spatial relationship to the coronaries. Therefore, simulation should use manufacturer-provided 3D device models with accurate outer contours for the chosen device and size.

# Limitations of simulation and integration with fluoroscopic planning

Despite their power, CT-based simulations have limitations that influence their reliability. First, CT-derived anatomies represent a snapshot (or a set of snapshots) in time and may not capture dynamic motion or changes that occur during the cardiac cycle, with anesthesia, or under hemodynamic perturbations at the time of the procedure. Segmentation errors arising from limited image quality, motion artifacts, or heavy calcification can propagate through to inaccurate meshes. Calcific and fibrotic tissues do not deform uniformly; assumptions regarding tissue elasticity in physics-based models are approximations and may not reflect local heterogeneity. For example, asymmetric calcification can lead to eccentric expansion or frame deformation that deviates from simulation predictions.

Device behavior in vivo can differ from modeled behavior. Balloon-expandable and self-expanding frames exert distinct radial force patterns, and device foreshortening or frame flaring can be influenced by operator technique, deployment speed, and balloon inflation volumes. Models may not perfectly reproduce these operator-dependent factors. Moreover, the interaction between the prosthetic skirt, displaced leaflet tissue, and complex sinus geometry is influenced by friction, tissue tearing, and anchoring dynamics that are challenging to model precisely.

Integration with fluoroscopic planning helps bridge the gap between pre-procedural simulation and intra-procedural execution. CT-based planning can generate fluoroscopic projection angles (c-arm angulations) that produce a coplanar view of the aortic annulus and cusps for optimal visualization at implantation. Two commonly used projections are the three-cusp view and the "cusp-overlap" technique for left coronary cusp alignment; software can compute the optimal angulations that align anatomical landmarks in the fluoroscopic plane, reducing parallax and improving visualization of deployment depth relative to the annulus. During the procedure, fluoroscopy and transesophageal echocardiography provide real-time feedback about valve position, but they do not completely substitute for the anatomical insights offered by pre-procedural CT.

Because simulation cannot account for all variables, it should be used as an adjunct, not a substitute, for clinical judgment. When simulation predicts high-risk features—very low VTC, small sinuses, or a neo-skirt overlapping the coronary ostium—the operator can plan protective strategies (coronary wiring, positioning of undeployed stent for chimney technique, or consideration of leaflet laceration procedures). The decision to perform such adjunctive maneuvers will also depend on intra-procedural findings, fluoroscopic landmarks, and the patient’s clinical context.

# Examples of how modeling informs choice of valve type and implant depth

Modeling influences device selection and implantation strategy through specific, mechanistic insights. Consider a patient with low coronary heights but large sinuses: simulation may show that although coronary height is borderline, the neo-skirt avoids direct ostial coverage because the sinuses provide lateral space for leaflet displacement. In this scenario, a device with a shorter skirt and conservative implant depth may be favored. Conversely, if simulation shows that leaflet bulk will project directly over the coronary ostium, a device with a shorter skirt or a retrievable design that permits repositioning may be preferred.

Device type selection is also informed by predicted frame–anatomy interaction. For example, in a heavily calcified annulus with eccentric calcium, a balloon-expandable device may achieve more symmetric radial expansion and better sealing, but it may also press bulky native leaflets more directly toward the coronaries. A self-expanding device with a supra-annular leaflet position and taller frame may allow for higher implantation with a larger neo-sinotubular junction clearance, or it may increase the chance of coronary coverage depending on the specific anatomy. Virtual implantation allows the operator to test multiple device sizes and models digitally and observe how the neo-skirt and leaflet positions change with each choice.

Implant depth is another critical variable. A high implant (more atrial) tends to leave more of the prosthetic frame below the annulus and may reduce the risk that leaflets are displaced upward into the coronary ostia; however, it may increase the risk of paravalvular regurgitation or embolization in some anatomies. A deeper (ventricular) implant positions the skirt differently relative to the cusp nadirs and can reduce pacemaker risk by avoiding conduction tissue contact, but might increase the likelihood of leaflet tissue obstructing the coronary ostia. Virtual deployment can be performed iteratively at multiple depths to quantify VTC at each proposed depth, enabling a risk–benefit analysis: for instance, choosing an implant depth that provides adequate coronary clearance (VTC > 4–6 mm) while keeping device radial forces and conduction-system proximity within acceptable limits.

In valve-in-valve procedures, modeling is particularly informative because the prior surgical valve frame and leaflet mounting height govern the space available. Virtual implantation can predict whether the transcatheter valve expansion will trap bioprosthetic leaflets against the surgical frame and whether those trapped leaflets will obstruct coronaries. In such cases, simulations may guide preemptive use of the BASILICA (bioprosthetic or native aortic scallop intentional laceration to prevent iatrogenic coronary artery obstruction) procedure or planning for coronary protection with a guidewire and undeployed stent.

Conclusion

CT-based 3D modeling and virtual implantation are powerful tools that bring detailed anatomic and device-specific insights to procedural planning. A rigorous workflow—starting with high-quality ECG-gated CT, careful segmentation, and generation of accurate 3D meshes—and the use of both geometric and physics-based simulation approaches allow clinicians to predict neo-skirt formation, leaflet displacement, and valve-to-coronary relationships. Quantitative metrics such as coronary height, sinus dimensions, and especially valve-to-coronary distance (VTC) inform risk stratification for coronary obstruction and guide choices of valve type and implant depth. Awareness of limitations—image quality constraints, modeling assumptions, and intra-procedural variability—means these simulations should augment, not replace, clinical judgment and fluoroscopic planning. When integrated thoughtfully into pre-procedural strategy, 3D modeling improves the ability to anticipate complications and tailor the intervention to the patient’s unique anatomy.

Introduction

Management of complex valvular heart disease increasingly relies on combined technical expertise, nuanced risk–benefit appraisal, and patient-centered decision-making. The advent of transcatheter therapies, valve-in-valve and valve-in-ring procedures, and novel devices has expanded options for patients who previously were considered inoperable or high risk. However, these advances also introduce new ethical, legal, and clinical challenges. Clinicians must integrate an understanding of pathophysiology, procedural risk, long-term outcomes, and patient goals into decisions that frequently occur under uncertainty. This chapter reviews how multidisciplinary heart teams are structured and operate, provides frameworks for weighing risks and benefits in high-risk reinterventions, outlines best practices for documentation and informed consent—particularly when devices are used off-label—discusses palliative approaches and goals-of-care conversations in structural heart disease, and details the regulatory and institutional governance that should guide novel or complex procedures.

# Structure and roles of the heart team in valvular disease management

The heart team is a formalized, multidisciplinary group established to synthesize expertise from different specialties and to provide a balanced assessment of therapeutic options for patients with valvular disease. The team’s composition typically includes interventional cardiologists with structural expertise, cardiothoracic surgeons, cardiac imaging specialists (echocardiography, CT, MRI), anesthesiologists (often cardiac anesthesiologists), heart failure specialists or general cardiologists, geriatricians or frailty experts when appropriate, and allied professionals such as nurse coordinators, clinical pharmacists, and sometimes ethicists or palliative care clinicians. Each member contributes a particular lens: surgeons assess operative candidacy and technical feasibility; interventionalists bring knowledge of device options and catheter-based approaches; imagers provide anatomic and functional data essential for procedural planning; anesthesiologists evaluate periprocedural risk and airway/cardiopulmonary management; heart failure specialists consider medical optimization and long-term prognosis; and geriatricians/frailty experts offer insights into physiologic reserves and vulnerability to complications.

Operationally, the heart team functions through regular meetings (virtual or in-person) where cases are presented in a structured fashion. A comprehensive presentation includes baseline clinical status, comorbidities, frailty and functional status, objective imaging (e.g., echocardiography with quantitative measures, CT annular sizing or CT angiography for access evaluation), prior surgical history, and patient preferences. The group evaluates: diagnosis and severity confirmation; candidacy for medical therapy alone versus intervention; choice among surgical, transcatheter, or hybrid approaches; timing of intervention; and postprocedural care plans. Decision-making should be evidence-informed and transparent, with documentation of dissenting opinions and the rationale for a given recommendation.

Communication with the patient and family is a critical role of the heart team. A designated clinician—often the structural interventionalist, surgeon, or heart failure physician—should lead discussion with the patient, presenting the team’s consensus recommendation, explicating alternatives, and ensuring comprehension. The team must assess the patient’s capacity and enlist surrogate decision-makers when appropriate. Importantly, the team should be attentive to the possibility of bias: enthusiasm for novel interventions should be tempered by objective appraisal of outcomes data, and conflicts of interest (financial or academic) must be declared and managed. The heart team model aims to improve consistency, reduce individual bias, and align care with the best available evidence and patient values.

# Frameworks for weighing risks and benefits in high-risk reinterventions

High-risk reinterventions in valvular disease—such as redo surgical valve replacement, transcatheter valve-in-valve procedures, or off-label device placements—require a structured framework to weigh potential benefits against procedural and long-term risks. This framework integrates probabilistic risk assessment tools, frailty and geriatric assessments, disease-specific prognostic metrics, and careful consideration of patient-centric outcomes such as symptom relief, functional status, and quality of life.

Risk stratification begins with validated surgical mortality models. Commonly used scores for cardiac surgery include the Society of Thoracic Surgeons Predicted Risk of Mortality (STS-PROM) and the EuroSCORE II. These scores quantify perioperative mortality risk and can guide comparison between surgical and transcatheter approaches, but they have limitations—particularly in reoperative and highly complex anatomies where the models may under- or overestimate risk. For transcatheter procedures, procedural risk models are less well standardized; institutional and operator experience significantly affects outcomes. Therefore, risk estimation must be individualized, integrating quantitative scores with qualitative clinical judgment.

Frailty is a crucial determinant of outcome beyond chronological age. Measures such as gait speed, the Fried phenotype, the Short Physical Performance Battery, or cognitive assessment tools (e.g., Mini-Cog) predict morbidity, prolonged hospitalization, and mortality after valvular interventions. For example, a gait speed slower than 0.8 m/s or inability to rise from a chair without using upper extremity support predicts poorer outcomes. Incorporating frailty into decision-making may shift recommendations from aggressive reintervention toward less invasive options or palliative approaches when the likelihood of meaningful recovery is low.

Disease-specific criteria refine decision-making. For aortic stenosis, objective thresholds such as valve area <1.0 cm^2, mean gradient >40 mmHg, or peak velocity >4 m/s define severe disease in symptomatic patients; biochemical markers like elevated B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) suggest hemodynamic stress and worse prognosis. For mitral valve disease, quantitative echocardiographic parameters—effective regurgitant orifice area, regurgitant volume, and pulmonary pressures—help to gauge the severity and consequence of regurgitation. For prosthetic valve dysfunction, imaging (transesophageal echocardiography, fluoroscopy, CT) can establish the mechanism—thrombosis, pannus, structural degeneration, or endocarditis—and this mechanistic understanding directly informs treatment risk and expected benefit.

Benefit analysis should address both short-term endpoints (survival, procedural success, complication avoidance) and patient-centered outcomes (symptom relief, functional improvement, reduced rehospitalizations, and quality of life). Decision thresholds are inherently individual: a 30% procedural mortality risk may be acceptable to a patient facing progressive NYHA class IV symptoms with poor life expectancy without intervention, while the same risk may be unacceptable to a patient prioritized for independence and minimal procedural burden. Explicitly articulating the magnitude and uncertainty of benefits and harms, and linking them to the patient’s goals, is essential.

When evidence is limited—common in reinterventions or novel techniques—the heart team should apply decision analysis principles: estimate probabilities of outcomes based on best available data (published series, registries, institutional outcomes), consider the quality-adjusted life expectancy implications, and discuss the range of plausible outcomes with the patient. Sensitivity analyses in one’s mind (e.g., “if the chance of major complication is 10% vs 30%, would you still choose this?”) help reveal patient preferences under uncertainty. Finally, the team should ensure realistic plans for contingency management—how complications would be handled, escalation pathways, and limits on escalation consistent with the patient’s wishes.

# Documentation and informed consent for off-label device use

Informed consent for any interventional procedure must include disclosure of diagnosis, recommended intervention, nature and purpose of the proposed procedure, reasonable alternatives (including nonintervention), the material risks and benefits, and an assessment of patient understanding and voluntary agreement. When devices are used outside their labeled indications (off-label use), additional documentation and discussion are ethically and legally necessary because the risk–benefit profile for that specific indication is often less established.

Key elements to address in the consent conversation for off-label device use include: the fact that the device is being used in a manner not approved by the regulatory authority for that specific indication; the rationale for using it given the patient’s individual anatomy and clinical circumstances; the evidence base supporting this use (e.g., series, registries, prior case reports), including the strength and limitations of that evidence; alternative, approved therapeutic options and their risks and benefits; and foreseeable additional risks stemming from the off-label use. The clinician should explain potential unknowns, such as durability, device–host interactions in the off-label setting, and the lack of long-term data. If data are limited, it is important to quantify uncertainty and to state what outcomes will be tracked post-procedure.

Documentation must go beyond a perfunctory signature. The informed consent form should explicitly state the off-label nature of the device use and the reasons it is considered appropriate for this patient. The narrative note in the medical record should summarize the discussion, the alternatives presented, the patient’s questions and responses, the patient’s stated goals and values, the patient’s consent (or refusal), and the names of team members present. If a surrogate decision-maker provides consent, the note should record the legal basis for surrogate authority and the assessment of the patient’s capacity that necessitated surrogate involvement.

When off-label use involves substantial uncertainty or increased risk, institutional oversight may require additional steps: review and approval by a device or procedural oversight committee, involvement of the hospital ethics team, or formal documentation of a heart team recommendation endorsed by multiple members. This oversight guards against inappropriate experimentation and helps ensure that the use is driven by patient benefit rather than novelty or financial incentives.

Consent should address logistical issues unique to complex interventions: potential need for conversion from transcatheter to open surgery, the likelihood of prolonged intensive care or rehabilitation, need for blood transfusion, and the plan if the device fails. Patients should be informed about postprocedural monitoring, imaging follow-up, and any requirements for antithrombotic therapy. If the off-label use will contribute data to a registry, quality-improvement program, or research study, this should be disclosed; research consent requirements are distinct from therapeutic consent and may necessitate separate informed consent processes approved by an institutional review board (IRB).

Finally, informed consent is a process, not a single event. Revisit consent with new information, changes in condition, or if there is a delay between consent and procedure. Ensure that translators or cultural mediators are used when language or cultural differences might impair understanding. Capacity assessment should be documented using standardized phrasing when there is any doubt about the patient’s ability to decide.

# Palliative considerations and patient goals of care in structural heart disease

Structural heart disease often affects older individuals with multiple comorbidities and frailty. Interventions can improve survival and symptoms, but may also prolong suffering or reduce quality of life if not aligned with patient goals. Incorporating palliative care principles early—coordinated with interventional and surgical options—ensures that decisions reflect what matters most to the patient.

Palliative care in the context of valvular disease emphasizes symptom management (dyspnea, pain, fatigue), psychosocial support, clarification of goals, advance care planning, and care coordination. It is not synonymous with choosing no intervention; rather, it complements disease-directed care by aligning treatment choices with patient values. For example, if a patient values maximal longevity and is willing to accept substantial perioperative risk for potential life-extension, an aggressive reintervention may be appropriate. Conversely, if a patient prioritizes maintaining independence and avoiding prolonged hospitalization, less invasive or noninterventional approaches—or a focus on symptom-directed management—may better reflect their goals.

Goals-of-care conversations should be structured and recurrent. They begin with exploring the patient’s understanding of their illness and prognosis, eliciting values and priorities, and framing choices in terms of likely outcomes that matter to the patient (survival with significant functional dependence, symptom relief without survival benefit, risk of prolonged rehabilitation). Clinicians should provide realistic estimates of prognosis both with and without intervention, using evidence when available and acknowledging uncertainty. Documentation of these discussions, including the patient’s stated preferences and any advance directives or durable power of attorney for health care, is essential to guide care should the patient lose decision-making capacity.

When procedural risk is high and chances of meaningful recovery are uncertain, time-limited trials of intervention can be considered: an intervention is pursued with predefined goals and a prearranged plan for reassessment and limitation if goals are not met. For instance, a time-limited trial might state that the team will pursue the intervention but will not continue aggressive life-sustaining therapies if the patient remains ventilator-dependent with no neurologic recovery after a specified period. Such agreements should be documented and communicated to all treating teams and the patient’s surrogates.

Integration of palliative care specialists into the heart team improves communication, symptom management, and decision-making. They can assist with complex discussions when there is discordance between patient/family expectations and clinical prognosis. Importantly, palliative care involvement reduces the likelihood that patients undergo burdensome interventions unlikely to achieve their stated goals.

# Regulatory and institutional governance for novel or complex procedures

Procedures that are novel, technically complex, or involve off-label device use require oversight beyond individual clinician judgment. Regulatory frameworks and institutional governance mechanisms exist to protect patient safety, ensure ethical standards, and maintain public trust. Clinicians must be familiar with national regulatory pathways as well as their institution’s policies.

At the national level, device use is regulated by agencies such as the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA). Approved indications reflect the evidence reviewed at the time of clearance or approval. Use outside those indications is permitted under “practice of medicine” but becomes ethically fraught when it departs substantially from the evidence base or when the use is effectively investigational. When a procedure is genuinely investigational (intended to generate generalizable knowledge about safety and efficacy), it typically requires an investigational device exemption (IDE) or research protocol approval by an IRB, and informed consent that complies with research regulations.

There are regulatory pathways that address access to investigational devices outside premarket approval, including compassionate use (expanded access) and emergency use provisions. These require specific applications and documentation, and often mandate data submission and monitoring. Device manufacturers may have responsibility under national regulations for reporting adverse events and for monitoring outcomes in expanded access programs.

Institutional governance typically involves committees such as a device oversight committee, procedural quality and safety committee, or a multidisciplinary peer-review panel (often termed a “valve review board” or “structural procedures committee”). These bodies review proposed novel practices, evaluate operator and team competency, set proctoring requirements, define credentialing criteria, and ensure that programs collect outcomes data for quality assurance. Approval of a new program or a departure from standard practice often requires evidence of the team’s training, procedural volume, and access to contingency resources (e.g., cardiac surgery backup).

Credentialing and privileging are central institutional mechanisms. Hospitals set criteria for granting privileges to perform specific structural heart procedures, often requiring documentation of training, case logs, proctorship, and outcomes monitoring. For complex procedures, an initial period of proctoring by an experienced operator may be required. Institutions should also define minimum volume thresholds to ensure maintenance of competency, recognizing that volume–outcome relationships exist for many complex cardiac procedures.

Quality governance requires systematic outcome measurement. Participation in national registries (e.g., transcatheter valve registries) allows benchmarking against broader datasets and supports surveillance for rare complications. Institutional morbidity and mortality review processes should include structured review of adverse events and near misses, with systems-based recommendations for improvement. Transparency with patients and families in the event of complications is ethically mandated—open disclosure policies should be in place.

Finally, conflict-of-interest policies must govern relationships between clinicians and device manufacturers. Financial relationships, research sponsorship, and speaking or advisory roles can introduce bias. Institutions should require disclosure of such relationships and manage them through recusal from decision-making when appropriate, independent review of proposed uses, and transparent reporting to patients when a clinician’s relationship could influence recommendations.

Conclusion

Complex valvular interventions require more than technical skill; they demand integrated multidisciplinary assessment, careful risk–benefit analysis, rigorous informed consent—especially when devices are used off-label—and alignment with the patient’s goals of care. Institutional and regulatory frameworks serve to ensure patient safety and ethical practice as technologies evolve. Effective heart team functioning, transparent documentation, and early incorporation of palliative principles promote decisions that are clinically sound and ethically defensible.

Introduction

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI; also called transcatheter aortic valve replacement, TAVR) has shifted from an experimental therapy for patients deemed inoperable to a mainstream option for a broad spectrum of patients with severe aortic stenosis. Population-level experience now spans more than a decade and includes randomized trials, large registries, and administrative datasets. These data inform how TAVI is used across age and surgical-risk strata, the durability of transcatheter heart valves compared with surgical bioprostheses, the epidemiology and prognosis of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE), and outcomes when transcatheter valves undergo subsequent interventions such as valve-in-valve (ViV) procedures or repeat TAVI. Understanding these issues requires integrating device performance (hemodynamics and structural durability), infection risk and diagnostics, and the clinical decision-making framework that determines patient selection and long-term management. This chapter synthesizes current population-level knowledge to help the learner interpret clinical presentations and plan management in patients with prosthetic aortic valves.

# Trends in TAVI use across risk strata and age groups

The early era of TAVI was defined by use in patients at prohibitive or high surgical risk. Randomized trials comparing TAVI with surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR) initially enrolled predominantly older patients with multiple comorbidities. Over time, powered trials and registry analyses expanded enrollment to intermediate- and low-risk cohorts, and guidelines evolved to support TAVI in selected younger and lower-risk patients. At the population level, this progression has produced three major trends.

First, the absolute number of TAVI procedures has risen markedly in most regions. As device technology (lower-profile delivery sheaths, improved valve designs) and procedural techniques (percutaneous access, conscious sedation) matured, more centers adopted TAVI, and procedure volumes increased. Greater operator experience and lower periprocedural morbidity contributed to broader referral patterns, including patients who would previously have been directed to SAVR.

Second, the risk profile of patients undergoing TAVI has shifted downward. Contemporary registries show increasing proportions of intermediate- and low-surgical-risk patients receiving TAVI compared with earlier epochs. This shift partially reflects trial data demonstrating noninferiority or superiority of TAVI versus SAVR for certain endpoints in intermediate- and low-risk cohorts. However, the decision to offer TAVI is not based on surgical risk score alone. Age, life expectancy, anatomic suitability (aortic annulus size, valve morphology, coronary anatomy), frailty, comorbidities such as chronic kidney disease, and patient preferences all influence the heart team’s recommendation. As a result, many elderly patients still receive SAVR when additional indications (e.g., concomitant coronary bypass or complex aortic pathology) exist, while some younger patients with hostile chest surgery history or prohibitive surgical risk are preferentially offered TAVI.

Third, age has emerged as a central axis of decision-making. Because surgical bioprostheses and transcatheter valves undergo structural deterioration over time, life expectancy and the feasibility of future reinterventions guide device choice. In practice, many centers favor TAVI for patients older than the mid-70s, whereas SAVR remains commonly recommended for younger patients, particularly those likely to require durable valve function for decades. Nonetheless, the age cutoff is not fixed; it varies by country, institutional practice, and evolving evidence. The increasing use of TAVI in younger patients also raises questions about long-term valve durability and the practicality of future valve-in-valve or surgical re-replacement strategies.

In addition to age and risk, there is regional variability in TAVI adoption driven by reimbursement, local expertise, and institutional referral patterns. Finally, the expanding use of TAVI for aortic regurgitation, bicuspid aortic valve disease, and other nonstandard anatomies remains limited and is the subject of ongoing investigation; widespread adoption in these subgroups awaits more robust outcomes data.

# Durability data and rates of structural valve deterioration after TAVI versus surgical bioprostheses

Durability—often operationalized as freedom from structural valve deterioration (SVD) or the need for reintervention—is the principal long-term performance metric for bioprosthetic valves. Definitions are standardized by consensus documents from groups such as the Valve Academic Research Consortium (VARC) and the European Association of Percutaneous Cardiovascular Interventions (EAPCI). SVD broadly denotes intrinsic degeneration of the valve tissue or frame that results in stenosis and/or regurgitation, whereas non-structural dysfunction includes pannus, paravalvular leak, endocarditis, or thrombosis. Objective echocardiographic thresholds for SVD often include a new mean gradient exceeding a valve-specific increment or the emergence of new moderate or severe regurgitation attributable to leaflet or frame dysfunction.

Randomized trial data and long-term registry follow-up provide the principal durability evidence. For both transcatheter and surgical bioprosthetic valves, the risk of SVD is low in the first 5 years after implantation but rises with time thereafter. In several randomized trials and pooled analyses, TAVI and SAVR showed similar rates of clinically relevant SVD through 5 years. For midterm horizons (5–8 years), most contemporary studies report that clinically detected SVD and reintervention rates remain relatively low for both modalities, with cumulative reintervention rates typically in the single-digit percentages. Observational Lang-term reports more than 8–10 years after TAVI are still emerging, and interpretation is complicated by patient age and competing risks—older patients who received TAVI early in its adoption often had limited life expectancy, attenuating the observed incidence of late SVD.

Mechanistically, valve deterioration reflects bioprosthetic leaflet calcification and fatigue, cumulative mechanical stress (which depends on residual gradients and patient-prosthesis mismatch), leaflet thrombosis, and inflammatory or immune-mediated processes. Transcatheter valves differ from many surgical stented bioprostheses in leaflet mounting and crimping-related tissue injury; for balloon-expandable and self-expanding transcatheter valves there has been concern that crimping-induced structural alterations could accelerate degeneration, while in-vivo data to prove or disprove that effect are still accruing. Additionally, transcatheter valves often have supra-annular leaflet positions and larger effective orifice areas in small annuli compared with some surgical valves, which can produce lower gradients and may reduce long-term stress-related damage.

Quantitatively, reported SVD rates vary with definitions and follow-up duration. Using standardized definitions, severe SVD at 5 years after TAVI has commonly ranged from near zero to a few percent in trial cohorts, with clinical reintervention rates similarly low. Comparisons with surgical valves suggest comparable midterm durability, but head-to-head differences may emerge over longer horizons, especially in younger patients who survive long enough for degeneration to manifest. Importantly, subclinical leaflet thrombosis detected by imaging (reversible hypo-attenuated leaflet thickening on CT) appears relatively common after both TAVI and SAVR and may contribute to elevated gradients or SVD if persistent; its clinical significance and optimal management remain under investigation.

From a clinical decision perspective, anticipated durability intersects with patient age, comorbidity, and future access for coronary procedures. For a patient with life expectancy exceeding 10–15 years, the unknown longer-term durability of many transcatheter valves informs counseling about SAVR with mechanical or surgical bioprosthetic valves versus TAVI with a strategy that may entail subsequent valve-in-valve procedures.

# Incidence, risk factors and outcomes of prosthetic valve endocarditis

Prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) is an uncommon but severe complication of valve replacement. Incidence estimates depend on the population studied, valve type, and follow-up duration. For surgical prosthetic valves, annual incidence rates of PVE have been reported in the range of approximately 0.3% to 1.2% per patient-year. Reported incidence after TAVI has varied between studies; some series suggest similar or slightly higher early PVE rates compared with SAVR, particularly within the first year after implantation. Interpretation of these comparisons requires caution because TAVI recipients have typically been older and sicker, with different exposure risks (e.g., more frequent vascular access lines, shorter hospital stays), and detection methods and surveillance intensity differ across studies.

Risk factors for PVE include both patient-level and procedure-related elements. Patient comorbidities associated with higher PVE risk are older age, diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease (and hemodialysis), prior endocarditis, immunosuppression, and poor dentition. Procedural and device factors that increase risk encompass intraoperative contamination, prolonged or complicated vascular access, paravalvular leaks that create turbulent flow and tissue injury, early postoperative bacteremia from other sources, and the presence of permanent pacemaker leads crossing the valve. For TAVI specifically, transapical access historically had higher infectious complication rates than transfemoral approaches, and residual paravalvular leak has been implicated as a nidus for infection. Repeat invasive procedures after valve implantation, including catheterizations with vascular access breaches or lead placements, increase cumulative exposure to bacteremia and raise PVE risk.

Clinical manifestations of PVE can be protean. Fever and bacteremia may be less pronounced in elderly or immunocompromised patients; symptoms of heart failure, new or changing murmurs, conduction disturbances (suggesting perivalvular extension), embolic phenomena, and persistent bacteremia despite antibiotics should raise suspicion. Laboratory findings supporting infection include elevated white blood cell count (normal adult WBC range approximately 4.0–11.0 x 10^9/L), elevated inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein (CRP; typical reference <5 mg/L) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), and positive blood cultures. The modified Duke criteria are the traditional diagnostic framework for infective endocarditis, but their sensitivity is lower in prosthetic valve cases. Thus, imaging is critical: transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) is the preferred initial modality for detecting vegetations, abscesses, or prosthetic dehiscence. In prosthetic valves, acoustic shadowing limits sensitivity, and advanced imaging with cardiac computed tomography (CT) and 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography combined with CT (FDG PET/CT) has become valuable, particularly when TEE is non-diagnostic. FDG PET/CT can detect perivalvular metabolic activity indicative of infection and is incorporated in contemporary diagnostic algorithms.

Outcomes of PVE remain poor relative to native valve endocarditis. In-hospital and short-term mortality rates are substantial, with many series reporting in-hospital mortality in the range of 20–40% and 1-year mortality often exceeding 40% to 50%, depending on causative organism, presence of perivalvular extension, and need for urgent surgery. PVE after TAVI has been associated with similarly high mortality; some reports indicate particularly poor outcomes when PVE occurs early after TAVI or when caused by Staphylococcus aureus or fungal pathogens. Management requires prolonged targeted intravenous antibiotic therapy guided by culture results, and early involvement of an endocarditis heart team to determine candidacy for surgical intervention versus a medical strategy. Indications for surgery in PVE include heart failure due to valvular dysfunction, uncontrolled infection (abscess, persistent bacteremia), and prevention of embolic events in the setting of large vegetations. However, surgical decision-making is nuanced in prosthetic patients because of the higher operative risk and technical complexity of reoperation—these factors are especially salient in TAVI recipients who may be older and frailer.

# Reported outcomes of valve-in-valve and repeat transcatheter procedures

Valve-in-valve (ViV) TAVI denotes deployment of a transcatheter valve within a failing surgical bioprosthesis or within a prior transcatheter valve as a strategy to treat structural valve degeneration, paravalvular leak, or other forms of prosthetic dysfunction. ViV has expanded the therapeutic armamentarium for patients who are poor surgical candidates or wish to avoid repeat open surgery.

For failed surgical bioprostheses, ViV offers a less invasive alternative with generally favorable short-to-midterm outcomes: procedural success rates are high, and many patients experience immediate hemodynamic improvement and symptomatic relief. However, ViV procedures carry specific technical challenges that influence outcome. When a transcatheter valve is deployed within a surgical valve with a small labelled inner diameter, residual gradients can be higher than after initial valve implantation, producing patient–prosthesis mismatch and potentially accelerated hemodynamic deterioration. The risk of patient–prosthesis mismatch is greatest in small surgical prostheses and contributes to the selection of valve type and sizing strategy. Coronary obstruction is an infrequent but catastrophic complication of ViV, particularly when the surgical valve’s leaflets are externally mounted or when the virtual transcatheter valve-to-coronary distance is minimal; pre-procedural CT planning and techniques such as leaflet laceration (e.g., BASILICA) are used to mitigate that risk.

Outcomes of repeat transcatheter procedures—deploying a second transcatheter valve inside a degenerated transcatheter valve—are increasingly reported as initial TAVI cohorts age. Early reports indicate reasonable procedural success and symptomatic benefit, but cumulative valve-frame geometry changes can affect coronary access, create higher transvalvular gradients, and limit future reintervention options. Long-term durability of a valve-in-valve-in-valve strategy remains uncertain, and each successive layered valve potentially reduces effective orifice area.

Reintervention after TAVI for SVD, paravalvular leak, or PVE is technically feasible with acceptable short-term outcomes in many series, but the complexity increases with each subsequent intervention. For patients requiring valve explantation because of endocarditis or severe dysfunction, redo surgery carries higher operative morbidity and mortality than initial SAVR—decisions must balance the risks of open conversion versus percutaneous reintervention. Importantly, outcomes are influenced by the indication for reintervention: ViV for structural degeneration generally has better immediate outcomes than reintervention for PVE or for severe paravalvular complications.

Clinical decision-making for ViV includes assessment of the original valve type and size, the mechanism of failure, coronary height and ostial orientation, and patient-specific anatomic considerations. Use of pre-procedural CT imaging to simulate device implantation and predict residual gradients and coronary obstruction is standard practice. When residual gradient risk is high (e.g., small surgical prostheses), strategies such as bioprosthetic valve fracture—using high-pressure balloon dilation to fracture the surgical valve stent ring—have been adopted to enlarge the effective orifice and reduce gradients; this approach has particular anatomic prerequisites and carries its own risks.

# Knowledge gaps and areas of active research

Despite substantial advances, several important knowledge gaps remain and are the focus of active clinical and translational research.

First, definitive long-term durability beyond 10–15 years for contemporary transcatheter valves is not yet established in broad populations, especially in younger patients. Ongoing trials and registries are collecting extended follow-up to quantify late SVD rates, predictors of degeneration, and comparative durability against modern surgical bioprostheses. A central question is whether mechanisms unique to TAVI (crimping-related injury, supra-annular leaflet position, leaflet design) confer different long-term failure profiles.

Second, the natural history and clinical significance of subclinical leaflet thrombosis after TAVI and SAVR are incompletely resolved. Although routine CT imaging often detects hypo-attenuated leaflet thickening, the proportion that progresses to symptomatic dysfunction or predisposes to embolic events is not fully defined, and optimal antithrombotic management (antiplatelet vs anticoagulation, duration) remains an area of controversy. Randomized trials are exploring antithrombotic strategies tailored to patient thrombotic and bleeding risk.

Third, the epidemiology and optimal prevention of PVE after TAVI require more granular data. Outstanding questions include whether TAVI confers a different intrinsic infection risk than SAVR after accounting for confounding by indication, which procedural factors most increase risk, and what perioperative antimicrobial prophylaxis or procedural sterility measures most effectively reduce PVE incidence. Additionally, the role of device design (e.g., leaflet material, frame composition) in susceptibility to bacterial adherence and biofilm formation is under investigation.

Fourth, strategies for reintervention after TAVI—especially long-term planning for younger patients—are uncertain. The clinical consequences of sequential valve-in-valve procedures on hemodynamics, coronary access, and feasibility of future surgical explantation need more study. Comparative outcomes of initial SAVR (with the possibility of subsequent ViV) versus primary TAVI followed by ViV over a 20-year horizon are hypothetical at present and are being modeled and investigated through registry linkage and long-term trials.

Fifth, better diagnostic algorithms for PVE in prosthetic valves are needed. While FDG PET/CT has improved sensitivity for detecting perivalvular infection, standardization of imaging protocols, interpretation criteria to distinguish sterile post-implant inflammation from infection, and integration with microbiologic and echocardiographic data are ongoing research priorities.

Finally, health-systems research is exploring how to optimize allocation of TAVI versus SAVR across populations, taking into account not only clinical outcomes but also quality-of-life, costs, and access to reintervention. Comparative effectiveness studies that incorporate patient preferences, life expectancy modeling, and long-term device performance are crucial to developing evidence-based policies.

Conclusion

Population-level experience with TAVI has transformed the management of aortic stenosis by enabling less invasive therapy across a widening range of patient risk and age. Midterm durability of contemporary transcatheter valves appears comparable to surgical bioprostheses in many cohorts, but long-term data beyond a decade are still evolving and are especially relevant for younger patients. Prosthetic valve endocarditis remains an uncommon but high-mortality complication; its detection is more challenging in prosthetic valves and often requires multimodality imaging and multidisciplinary management. Valve-in-valve and repeat transcatheter procedures expand treatment options for degenerated prostheses but introduce unique hemodynamic and anatomic considerations that affect outcomes and future options. Finally, key knowledge gaps—especially concerning very long-term durability, subclinical leaflet thrombosis, PVE prevention and diagnosis, and life-course strategies for valve therapy—are the focus of ongoing research that will shape future practice.
