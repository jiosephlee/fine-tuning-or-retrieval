Introduction

Infective endocarditis (IE) and prosthetic valve interventions such as transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) intersect with multiple pharmacologic domains: targeted antimicrobial therapy for causative pathogens, peri- and post-procedural antithrombotic strategies, peri‑procedural management of anticoagulants and antiplatelets, and optimization of guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT) for coexistent heart failure. This chapter reviews the pharmacology most relevant to management of IE—particularly commonly encountered organisms like Enterococcus spp.—principles that determine intravenous (IV) versus oral antibiotic courses for prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE), antithrombotic approaches after TAVI including when to use single versus dual therapy and how atrial fibrillation (AF) and recent percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) change strategy, practical management of antithrombotics around procedures with bleeding-risk considerations, heart failure pharmacotherapy in patients with valvular disease, and the current recommendations for antibiotic prophylaxis for patients with prosthetic valves undergoing dental and other procedures.

# Antimicrobial regimens for common IE pathogens (eg, Enterococcus spp.): beta‑lactams, aminoglycosides, combination therapy alternatives

Enterococci, most commonly Enterococcus faecalis and E. faecium, are frequent causes of IE, especially PVE and healthcare‑associated native valve endocarditis. Their intrinsic and acquired resistance patterns and their relative tolerance to bactericidal antibiotics make therapy more complex than for streptococci or staphylococci.

Enterococcus faecalis
E. faecalis remains typically susceptible to beta‑lactams such as ampicillin and penicillin G. The classical bactericidal regimen relies on synergism between a cell‑wall active agent and an aminoglycoside: ampicillin (or penicillin) plus gentamicin. Ampicillin alone is bacteriostatic against enterococci at achievable concentrations in many cases; adding gentamicin achieves synergistic bactericidal activity through enhanced penetration and ribosomal inhibition. A commonly used regimen for susceptible E. faecalis IE is ampicillin 2 g IV every 4 hours (or continuous infusion equivalent) plus gentamicin 3 mg/kg/day in divided doses (commonly 1 mg/kg every 8 hours), with dose adjustment and therapeutic drug monitoring targeted to minimize nephrotoxicity. The duration for native valve enterococcal IE is typically 4–6 weeks; for PVE the duration is most often 6 weeks.

However, since the 2000s, combination therapy using double beta‑lactam therapy (ampicillin plus ceftriaxone) has become a preferred alternative for E. faecalis strains that are susceptible to ampicillin but show high-level aminoglycoside resistance or in patients at high risk for aminoglycoside toxicity (older age, baseline renal dysfunction). The mechanism of synergy is complementary inhibition of penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs). Typical dosing is ampicillin 2 g IV every 4 hours plus ceftriaxone 2 g IV every 12 hours, for a total duration similar to aminoglycoside-containing regimens (4–6 weeks for native, 6 weeks for prosthetic). Advantages include avoidance of aminoglycoside nephrotoxicity and ototoxicity while achieving bactericidal activity.

Enterococcus faecium
E. faecium often carries resistance to ampicillin and many beta‑lactams; vancomycin resistance (VRE) is also common in some centers. For ampicillin‑resistant but vancomycin‑susceptible isolates, vancomycin IV dosed to trough goals historically has been used, though contemporary practice prefers AUC‑guided dosing when available. Target vancomycin troughs of 15–20 mg/L (AUC24/MIC targets dependent) were used but AUC monitoring is superior. For VRE or when vancomycin fails or is not an option, daptomycin or linezolid are mainstays. Daptomycin is bactericidal and often used at higher doses for endovascular infections (e.g., 8–10 mg/kg IV once daily, sometimes higher), though combination therapy (daptomycin plus a beta‑lactam such as ampicillin or ceftaroline) may enhance activity and reduce resistance emergence. Linezolid is bacteriostatic and penetrates well but is generally reserved for selected situations or when daptomycin cannot be used, and prolonged therapy risks bone marrow suppression and neuropathies.

Key practical points
- Always interpret susceptibility testing in the context of MICs and the clinical situation. High‑level aminoglycoside resistance (HLAR) in enterococci (gentamicin MICs very high) negates synergism and mandates avoiding gentamicin-containing regimens.
- For E. faecalis that are ampicillin‑susceptible, consider ampicillin plus ceftriaxone as first-line for many patients (particularly older or renal impairment) to minimize aminoglycoside toxicity.
- For E. faecium and VRE, consider daptomycin (high dose) +/- a beta‑lactam, or linezolid when appropriate; consultation with infectious diseases is essential.
- Duration: native valve IE typically 4–6 weeks; prosthetic valve IE is usually treated for at least 6 weeks, often longer based on response.

# Indications and duration of intravenous versus oral therapy in PVE

Traditionally, IE and particularly prosthetic valve endocarditis have been treated with prolonged courses of parenteral bactericidal antibiotics because of concerns about poor oral bioavailability, tissue penetration to biofilms on prosthetic material, and the need for reliably high serum and tissue concentrations. However, recent evidence has supported the selective use of partial oral step‑down therapy in carefully selected patients with left‑sided IE, though PVE poses a higher risk and requires particular caution.

Indications for IV therapy
Initial therapy for PVE is almost always IV. Indications for initial and continued IV therapy include hemodynamic instability, persistently positive blood cultures, uncontrolled sepsis, complications requiring urgent surgery (e.g., heart failure from valve destruction, perivalvular abscess), organisms with limited or unreliable oral agents (e.g., staphylococci, enterococci with resistant patterns), presence of prosthetic material with biofilm formation risk, and inability to tolerate oral intake or absorb medications. IV therapy allows high and predictable drug concentrations and flexibility to adjust regimens based on susceptibilities.

Transition to oral therapy: evidence and selection criteria
The POET trial (Partial Oral versus Intravenous Antibiotic Treatment of Endocarditis) randomized stable patients with left‑sided IE (both native and prosthetic) to complete IV therapy versus a switch to selected oral regimens after an initial period (at least 10 days) of IV therapy. The trial showed noninferiority for composite outcomes in a highly selected population meeting stringent stability criteria: clinical stability for >10 days, afebrile, negative repeat blood cultures, no signs of uncontrolled infection or complications, and availability of oral agents with proven efficacy and bioavailability for the causative pathogen. Importantly, only certain pathogens and drug combinations were used in POET (e.g., streptococci, sensitive enterococci, staphylococci with available oral options), and many centers interpret POET as support for selective step‑down rather than routine oral therapy for PVE.

Specific considerations in PVE
PVE is inherently higher risk than native valve IE because of biofilm formation and prosthetic material. Many guidelines still recommend prolonged IV therapy for PVE. If considering oral step‑down in PVE, the patient should meet strict criteria: clinical stability for an extended initial IV period (often 2 weeks or more per some centers), no paravalvular complications, negative blood cultures, and assurance that the oral regimen attains adequate bactericidal concentrations against the isolate. Oral regimens must have proven bioavailability and activity; for enterococci this can be challenging. The decision usually involves infectious diseases consultation and close outpatient follow-up with the ability to readmit if relapse is suspected.

Duration of therapy
Typical durations vary by organism and valve status:
- Native valve IE: generally 4–6 weeks, depending on organism and regimen.
- Prosthetic valve IE: generally at least 6 weeks of targeted parenteral therapy from the date of the first negative blood culture; some recommendations extend therapy duration based on complications or persistent infection.
When oral step‑down is used, the total duration (IV + oral) should be the same as recommended total therapy for that pathogen and valve condition. For example, a PVE course totaling 6 weeks may include an initial IV phase followed by oral therapy only in exceptional, well‑selected cases.

# Antiplatelet and anticoagulant strategies after TAVI (single vs dual therapy, indications based on AF/PCI)

Antithrombotic management following transcatheter aortic valve implantation balances prevention of early thrombotic complications (valve thrombosis, early coronary or cerebrovascular events) against bleeding risk in an often elderly, comorbid population. Practices have evolved from routine dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) toward more conservative regimens informed by randomized trials.

Antiplatelet therapy for patients without another indication for anticoagulation
Early experience favored DAPT (aspirin plus clopidogrel for 1–3 months) after TAVI, extrapolating from coronary stent practice. Subsequent randomized trials demonstrated that single antiplatelet therapy (SAPT) with aspirin alone reduces bleeding without increasing ischemic events compared with DAPT for most patients undergoing TAVI who do not have an indication for systemic anticoagulation. Current common practice and guideline recommendations support aspirin monotherapy (e.g., aspirin 75–100 mg daily) in patients without other indications for anticoagulation. Clopidogrel monotherapy is an option in some contexts if aspirin is contraindicated.

Anticoagulation for patients with indications (e.g., AF)
Patients with established indications for long‑term anticoagulation, most commonly AF, should receive therapeutic oral anticoagulation (OAC) rather than antiplatelet therapy alone after TAVI. Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) are frequently used in those with nonvalvular AF unless there are contraindications; for mechanical valves DOACs are contraindicated (but mechanical valves are not placed in TAVI). The choice between a vitamin K antagonist (VKA) and DOAC depends on patient factors and local practice; for bioprosthetic valves and TAVI, DOACs are often reasonable after the immediate post‑prosthetic period, but evidence is still evolving.

Recent randomized data (e.g., POPular-TAVI antiplatelet cohort) support withholding routine clopidogrel and using SAPT in patients not anticoagulated. For patients on OAC for another indication, adding antiplatelet therapy increases bleeding without clear ischemic benefit in many cases and is generally avoided unless there is a compelling reason (e.g., recent coronary stent).

When recent PCI or coronary stenting complicates the picture
In patients undergoing TAVI who also have recent PCI requiring antiplatelet therapy, a balance is required. If PCI with stent implantation is recent, guidelines for coronary stents (e.g., duration of DAPT) apply, but clinicians should tailor therapy to bleeding risk. Typical pragmatic approaches:
- If PCI was remote and patient requires systemic OAC for AF, OAC alone may suffice after an initial short period.
- If PCI was recent (particularly within 1–6 months) and dual therapy is indicated for stent protection, consider OAC plus a single antiplatelet (usually clopidogrel) for the minimal necessary duration. Assess stent type, time since PCI, and bleeding risk.
- Triple therapy (OAC + aspirin + P2Y12 inhibitor) should be avoided if possible because of very high bleeding risk; if absolutely required for a very short period (days to weeks) use the shortest effective duration and prefer clopidogrel as the P2Y12 agent.

Valve thrombosis prophylaxis and empiric short-term regimens
Some centers have used short courses (e.g., 30 days) of anticoagulation post-TAVI to reduce subclinical leaflet thrombosis, especially in patients deemed at higher thrombotic risk, but randomized data are mixed. Routine OAC purely to prevent subclinical leaflet thrombosis in patients without another indication is not universally recommended.

Practical decision points
- No OAC indication: aspirin monotherapy is generally preferred after TAVI.
- Chronic OAC indication (eg, AF): continue OAC; do not add antiplatelet unless required for recent PCI.
- Recent PCI: tailor therapy to stent timing/type and ischemic vs bleeding risks; favor OAC + single antiplatelet for the shortest necessary duration rather than prolonged triple therapy.
- Always individualize based on bleeding risk (HAS-BLED), ischemic risk, and patient comorbidities.

# Management of antithrombotic therapy around procedures and bleeding risk considerations

Peri-procedural management of antiplatelet and anticoagulant medications requires balancing thrombotic risk from interruption versus bleeding risk if therapy is continued. Key elements include the indication for antithrombotic therapy, the thrombotic risk of interruption, the bleeding risk of the scheduled procedure, pharmacokinetics of the agent, and reversibility/bridging options.

Assessing thrombotic and bleeding risks
Thrombotic risk drivers include mechanical valves, recent venous thromboembolism, recent stent (especially within 1–6 months), and high‑risk AF (CHA2DS2-VASc score). Bleeding risk is driven by the procedure type (major surgery vs minor procedure) and patient factors (age, renal disease, prior bleeding, concurrent antiplatelet drugs).

Anticoagulant interruption and bridging
For vitamin K antagonists (warfarin), reversal is achieved with vitamin K and prothrombin complex concentrates when needed; cessation prior to surgery is typically 5 days to allow INR normalization, with checking INR preoperatively. Bridging with heparin (therapeutic LMWH or IV unfractionated heparin) historically was used for patients at very high thrombotic risk during warfarin interruption. However, bridging confers increased bleeding risk and should be reserved for the highest risk scenarios (e.g., mechanical mitral valve, recent thromboembolus). For many AF patients, temporary interruption of warfarin without bridging is safe.

For DOACs, because of shorter half‑lives, interruption timing is shorter and bridging is usually not needed. General guidance:
- Low bleeding-risk procedures: hold DOAC for at least 24 hours prior.
- High bleeding-risk procedures or reduced renal function: hold for 48–72 hours or longer depending on renal function and agent half‑life.
Restart DOACs post‑procedure once hemostasis is secured—often 24 hours after low risk and 48–72 hours after high‑risk procedures.

Antiplatelet therapy around procedures
Aspirin is often continued for many procedures unless bleeding risk is high and aspirin would materially increase bleeding (e.g., neuraxial anesthesia, certain surgeries). For dual antiplatelet therapy after recent PCI, if interruption is necessary, avoid stopping P2Y12 inhibitors within the early post‑stent period; if bleeding risk mandates interruption, plan timing carefully and resume as soon as hemostasis is achieved. For neuraxial anesthesia or spinal procedures, P2Y12 inhibitors have specific washout periods (e.g., clopidogrel 7 days).

Reversal agents and emergency management
Reversal agents are relevant for life‑threatening bleeding:
- Warfarin: vitamin K, prothrombin complex concentrate (PCC).
- Dabigatran: idarucizumab (specific reversal).
- Factor Xa inhibitors (apixaban, rivaroxaban, edoxaban): andexanet alfa (where available) or PCC may be used in severe bleeding; availability and cost limit universal use.
- Antiplatelet agents: no specific reversal; platelet transfusion may be considered in life‑threatening hemorrhage.

Risk scores and clinical tools
Bleeding risk can be estimated using scores such as HAS-BLED for AF patients, though these are imperfect. Decisions should integrate scores with clinical judgment, considering factors such as prior bleeding, frailty, renal function, and concurrent antiplatelet therapy.

Specific considerations in TAVI patients
TAVI recipients are often elderly with multiple comorbidities and elevated bleeding risk. For elective procedures after TAVI, consider continuing aspirin where safe, and treat OAC interruption in AF patients per DOAC/warfarin guidance. In patients with recent TAVI and concomitant coronary stenting, coordinate cardiology and surgical teams about timing to minimize interruption of crucial antithrombotic therapy.

# Heart failure pharmacotherapy relevant to valve disease (ACE inhibitors/ARBs, beta‑blockers, MRAs, SGLT2 inhibitors)

Heart failure pharmacotherapy remains central to managing patients with valvular heart disease who have left ventricular dysfunction or symptoms, and many heart failure drugs have important interactions with hemodynamics of valvular lesions.

ACE inhibitors / ARBs / ARNI
Angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEi) and angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs) reduce afterload, attenuate maladaptive remodeling, and improve morbidity and mortality in heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF). In valve disease, these agents can be beneficial when LV dysfunction or heart failure is present. However, in severe aortic stenosis (AS), marked afterload reduction with ACEi in the presence of fixed obstruction requires caution because of the risk of hypotension; gradual titration and close monitoring is advised. In aortic regurgitation (AR) and functional MR, vasodilators may reduce regurgitant volume and improve symptoms pending definitive valve therapy.

Angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitor (ARNI; sacubitril/valsartan) is superior to ACEi in appropriate HFrEF patients and should be used per guidelines in patients who remain symptomatic despite optimal ACEi/ARB therapy or substituted after appropriate washout from ACEi. Initiation in patients with valvular disease follows the same principles as in nonvalvular HFrEF, with attention to blood pressure and renal function.

Beta-blockers
Beta-blockers (e.g., metoprolol succinate, bisoprolol, carvedilol) improve survival in HFrEF. In patients with valvular disease, beta-blocker use is indicated for coexistent heart failure with reduced EF. In AS, beta-blockers must be used cautiously in decompensated states because heart rate control can compromise cardiac output when the stroke volume is fixed. In MR, rate control may be beneficial, especially in tachyarrhythmias. Beta-blockers also affect blood pressure and may complicate perioperative hemodynamics; start low and titrate.

Mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs)
Spironolactone or eplerenone reduce morbidity and mortality in moderate to severe HFrEF in appropriate patients already on ACEi/ARB and beta-blocker therapy. They are important in managing congestion and counteracting aldosterone-mediated fibrosis. Monitor potassium (normal range approximately 3.5–5.0 mmol/L) and renal function; discontinue or lower dose if eGFR declines or potassium rises above safe thresholds.

SGLT2 inhibitors
Sodium–glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors (e.g., dapagliflozin 10 mg daily, empagliflozin 10 mg daily) have demonstrated benefit in reducing heart failure hospitalizations and cardiovascular mortality in HFrEF and in some patients with preserved EF. These agents are now recommended as foundational therapy in HFrEF irrespective of diabetes status. In valvular patients with heart failure, SGLT2 inhibitors can be introduced to improve outcomes and reduce hospitalizations. Monitor for volume depletion, renal function deterioration, and genitourinary infections.

Diuretics and symptomatic management
Loop diuretics (e.g., furosemide) control congestion and are titrated to symptom relief. Diuretics do not alter long-term mortality but are essential for symptomatic management. Monitor renal function and electrolytes.

Integration with valve disease management
- Optimize GDMT in patients with valvular heart disease and HFrEF, tailoring to hemodynamics. For example, use vasodilators judiciously in severe AS; they can benefit afterload-sensitive regurgitant lesions but worsen hypotension in advanced fixed stenosis.
- Pre‑ and post‑valve intervention therapy: GDMT should be optimized prior to valve intervention where feasible; after valve correction, myocardial remodeling and symptomatology may change and medications may need modification.
- Monitor blood pressure, renal function, and electrolytes closely in older patients and those with concomitant infections or procedures.

# Antibiotic prophylaxis principles for prosthetic valves and dental procedures

Antibiotic prophylaxis aims to prevent infective endocarditis caused by bacteremia related to certain procedures. Guidelines (e.g., American Heart Association, European Society of Cardiology) have converged to limit prophylaxis to patients at highest risk of adverse outcomes from IE.

Who should receive prophylaxis?
Prophylaxis is recommended for patients at highest risk of adverse outcomes from IE, including:
- Patients with prosthetic heart valves, including transcatheter prostheses and homografts.
- Patients with prior infective endocarditis.
- Certain congenital heart disease categories (e.g., unrepaired cyanotic CHD, repaired prosthetic material during first 6 months after procedure).
- Cardiac transplant recipients who develop valvulopathy.

Patients with prosthetic valves (including valve repair with prosthetic material and TAVI prostheses) fall into this high‑risk category and should receive prophylaxis for procedures that are likely to produce bacteremia involving oral flora.

Procedures that warrant prophylaxis
- Dental procedures that involve manipulation of the gingival tissue, periapical region of teeth, or perforation of oral mucosa (e.g., tooth extraction, periodontal procedures, dental implants). Routine anesthetic injections in noninfected tissue and dental radiographs do not require prophylaxis.
- Prophylaxis is not recommended for routine gastrointestinal or genitourinary procedures except in rare circumstances where an active infection is present.

Regimen and dosing
The standard recommended oral prophylactic regimen for dental procedures in patients able to take oral medication is amoxicillin 2 g orally given 30–60 minutes before procedure (adult dosing). For those allergic to beta‑lactams, alternatives include:
- Clindamycin 600 mg orally 30–60 minutes before procedure.
- Azithromycin or clarithromycin 500 mg orally 30–60 minutes before procedure (where clindamycin is not appropriate).
Parenteral options (e.g., ampicillin 2 g IV/IM) are used if oral administration is not feasible.

Practical points
- Single preprocedural dose is generally recommended; prolonged postprocedural courses are not indicated.
- Emphasize good oral hygiene and regular dental care; the cumulative risk of bacteremia from daily oral activities is greater than single dental procedures, and optimization of oral health reduces IE risk.
- Inform patients with prosthetic valves, including those with TAVI, that they qualify for prophylaxis for qualifying dental procedures and that they should communicate this to dental providers.

Conclusion

Pharmacologic management at the interface of infective endocarditis, prosthetic valves/TAVI, and heart failure requires integration of antimicrobial stewardship, individualized antithrombotic strategy, peri‑procedural risk assessment, and evidence‑based heart failure therapies. Key principles include organism‑directed antibiotic selection with attention to synergistic combinations (e.g., ampicillin plus gentamicin or ampicillin plus ceftriaxone for E. faecalis), preference for IV therapy in PVE with selective step‑down to oral agents only in carefully selected stable patients, SAPT (typically aspirin) after TAVI for patients without other indications for anticoagulation, continuation of OAC for AF with minimization of combined antiplatelet therapy unless recent PCI mandates it, cautious interruption and reversal strategies tailored to bleeding and thrombotic risk, optimization of ACEi/ARB/ARNI, beta‑blockers, MRAs, and SGLT2 inhibitors for heart failure in the setting of valve disease, and adherence to guideline‑based antibiotic prophylaxis for dental procedures in patients with prosthetic valves. Coordination among cardiology, cardiac surgery/interventional teams, infectious diseases, and primary care/dental providers is essential to implement these pharmacologic strategies safely and effectively.
