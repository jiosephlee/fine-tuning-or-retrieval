Introduction

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI; also called transcatheter aortic valve replacement, TAVR) has shifted from an experimental therapy for patients deemed inoperable to a mainstream option for a broad spectrum of patients with severe aortic stenosis. Population-level experience now spans more than a decade and includes randomized trials, large registries, and administrative datasets. These data inform how TAVI is used across age and surgical-risk strata, the durability of transcatheter heart valves compared with surgical bioprostheses, the epidemiology and prognosis of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE), and outcomes when transcatheter valves undergo subsequent interventions such as valve-in-valve (ViV) procedures or repeat TAVI. Understanding these issues requires integrating device performance (hemodynamics and structural durability), infection risk and diagnostics, and the clinical decision-making framework that determines patient selection and long-term management. This chapter synthesizes current population-level knowledge to help the learner interpret clinical presentations and plan management in patients with prosthetic aortic valves.

# Trends in TAVI use across risk strata and age groups

The early era of TAVI was defined by use in patients at prohibitive or high surgical risk. Randomized trials comparing TAVI with surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR) initially enrolled predominantly older patients with multiple comorbidities. Over time, powered trials and registry analyses expanded enrollment to intermediate- and low-risk cohorts, and guidelines evolved to support TAVI in selected younger and lower-risk patients. At the population level, this progression has produced three major trends.

First, the absolute number of TAVI procedures has risen markedly in most regions. As device technology (lower-profile delivery sheaths, improved valve designs) and procedural techniques (percutaneous access, conscious sedation) matured, more centers adopted TAVI, and procedure volumes increased. Greater operator experience and lower periprocedural morbidity contributed to broader referral patterns, including patients who would previously have been directed to SAVR.

Second, the risk profile of patients undergoing TAVI has shifted downward. Contemporary registries show increasing proportions of intermediate- and low-surgical-risk patients receiving TAVI compared with earlier epochs. This shift partially reflects trial data demonstrating noninferiority or superiority of TAVI versus SAVR for certain endpoints in intermediate- and low-risk cohorts. However, the decision to offer TAVI is not based on surgical risk score alone. Age, life expectancy, anatomic suitability (aortic annulus size, valve morphology, coronary anatomy), frailty, comorbidities such as chronic kidney disease, and patient preferences all influence the heart team’s recommendation. As a result, many elderly patients still receive SAVR when additional indications (e.g., concomitant coronary bypass or complex aortic pathology) exist, while some younger patients with hostile chest surgery history or prohibitive surgical risk are preferentially offered TAVI.

Third, age has emerged as a central axis of decision-making. Because surgical bioprostheses and transcatheter valves undergo structural deterioration over time, life expectancy and the feasibility of future reinterventions guide device choice. In practice, many centers favor TAVI for patients older than the mid-70s, whereas SAVR remains commonly recommended for younger patients, particularly those likely to require durable valve function for decades. Nonetheless, the age cutoff is not fixed; it varies by country, institutional practice, and evolving evidence. The increasing use of TAVI in younger patients also raises questions about long-term valve durability and the practicality of future valve-in-valve or surgical re-replacement strategies.

In addition to age and risk, there is regional variability in TAVI adoption driven by reimbursement, local expertise, and institutional referral patterns. Finally, the expanding use of TAVI for aortic regurgitation, bicuspid aortic valve disease, and other nonstandard anatomies remains limited and is the subject of ongoing investigation; widespread adoption in these subgroups awaits more robust outcomes data.

# Durability data and rates of structural valve deterioration after TAVI versus surgical bioprostheses

Durability—often operationalized as freedom from structural valve deterioration (SVD) or the need for reintervention—is the principal long-term performance metric for bioprosthetic valves. Definitions are standardized by consensus documents from groups such as the Valve Academic Research Consortium (VARC) and the European Association of Percutaneous Cardiovascular Interventions (EAPCI). SVD broadly denotes intrinsic degeneration of the valve tissue or frame that results in stenosis and/or regurgitation, whereas non-structural dysfunction includes pannus, paravalvular leak, endocarditis, or thrombosis. Objective echocardiographic thresholds for SVD often include a new mean gradient exceeding a valve-specific increment or the emergence of new moderate or severe regurgitation attributable to leaflet or frame dysfunction.

Randomized trial data and long-term registry follow-up provide the principal durability evidence. For both transcatheter and surgical bioprosthetic valves, the risk of SVD is low in the first 5 years after implantation but rises with time thereafter. In several randomized trials and pooled analyses, TAVI and SAVR showed similar rates of clinically relevant SVD through 5 years. For midterm horizons (5–8 years), most contemporary studies report that clinically detected SVD and reintervention rates remain relatively low for both modalities, with cumulative reintervention rates typically in the single-digit percentages. Observational Lang-term reports more than 8–10 years after TAVI are still emerging, and interpretation is complicated by patient age and competing risks—older patients who received TAVI early in its adoption often had limited life expectancy, attenuating the observed incidence of late SVD.

Mechanistically, valve deterioration reflects bioprosthetic leaflet calcification and fatigue, cumulative mechanical stress (which depends on residual gradients and patient-prosthesis mismatch), leaflet thrombosis, and inflammatory or immune-mediated processes. Transcatheter valves differ from many surgical stented bioprostheses in leaflet mounting and crimping-related tissue injury; for balloon-expandable and self-expanding transcatheter valves there has been concern that crimping-induced structural alterations could accelerate degeneration, while in-vivo data to prove or disprove that effect are still accruing. Additionally, transcatheter valves often have supra-annular leaflet positions and larger effective orifice areas in small annuli compared with some surgical valves, which can produce lower gradients and may reduce long-term stress-related damage.

Quantitatively, reported SVD rates vary with definitions and follow-up duration. Using standardized definitions, severe SVD at 5 years after TAVI has commonly ranged from near zero to a few percent in trial cohorts, with clinical reintervention rates similarly low. Comparisons with surgical valves suggest comparable midterm durability, but head-to-head differences may emerge over longer horizons, especially in younger patients who survive long enough for degeneration to manifest. Importantly, subclinical leaflet thrombosis detected by imaging (reversible hypo-attenuated leaflet thickening on CT) appears relatively common after both TAVI and SAVR and may contribute to elevated gradients or SVD if persistent; its clinical significance and optimal management remain under investigation.

From a clinical decision perspective, anticipated durability intersects with patient age, comorbidity, and future access for coronary procedures. For a patient with life expectancy exceeding 10–15 years, the unknown longer-term durability of many transcatheter valves informs counseling about SAVR with mechanical or surgical bioprosthetic valves versus TAVI with a strategy that may entail subsequent valve-in-valve procedures.

# Incidence, risk factors and outcomes of prosthetic valve endocarditis

Prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) is an uncommon but severe complication of valve replacement. Incidence estimates depend on the population studied, valve type, and follow-up duration. For surgical prosthetic valves, annual incidence rates of PVE have been reported in the range of approximately 0.3% to 1.2% per patient-year. Reported incidence after TAVI has varied between studies; some series suggest similar or slightly higher early PVE rates compared with SAVR, particularly within the first year after implantation. Interpretation of these comparisons requires caution because TAVI recipients have typically been older and sicker, with different exposure risks (e.g., more frequent vascular access lines, shorter hospital stays), and detection methods and surveillance intensity differ across studies.

Risk factors for PVE include both patient-level and procedure-related elements. Patient comorbidities associated with higher PVE risk are older age, diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease (and hemodialysis), prior endocarditis, immunosuppression, and poor dentition. Procedural and device factors that increase risk encompass intraoperative contamination, prolonged or complicated vascular access, paravalvular leaks that create turbulent flow and tissue injury, early postoperative bacteremia from other sources, and the presence of permanent pacemaker leads crossing the valve. For TAVI specifically, transapical access historically had higher infectious complication rates than transfemoral approaches, and residual paravalvular leak has been implicated as a nidus for infection. Repeat invasive procedures after valve implantation, including catheterizations with vascular access breaches or lead placements, increase cumulative exposure to bacteremia and raise PVE risk.

Clinical manifestations of PVE can be protean. Fever and bacteremia may be less pronounced in elderly or immunocompromised patients; symptoms of heart failure, new or changing murmurs, conduction disturbances (suggesting perivalvular extension), embolic phenomena, and persistent bacteremia despite antibiotics should raise suspicion. Laboratory findings supporting infection include elevated white blood cell count (normal adult WBC range approximately 4.0–11.0 x 10^9/L), elevated inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein (CRP; typical reference <5 mg/L) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), and positive blood cultures. The modified Duke criteria are the traditional diagnostic framework for infective endocarditis, but their sensitivity is lower in prosthetic valve cases. Thus, imaging is critical: transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) is the preferred initial modality for detecting vegetations, abscesses, or prosthetic dehiscence. In prosthetic valves, acoustic shadowing limits sensitivity, and advanced imaging with cardiac computed tomography (CT) and 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography combined with CT (FDG PET/CT) has become valuable, particularly when TEE is non-diagnostic. FDG PET/CT can detect perivalvular metabolic activity indicative of infection and is incorporated in contemporary diagnostic algorithms.

Outcomes of PVE remain poor relative to native valve endocarditis. In-hospital and short-term mortality rates are substantial, with many series reporting in-hospital mortality in the range of 20–40% and 1-year mortality often exceeding 40% to 50%, depending on causative organism, presence of perivalvular extension, and need for urgent surgery. PVE after TAVI has been associated with similarly high mortality; some reports indicate particularly poor outcomes when PVE occurs early after TAVI or when caused by Staphylococcus aureus or fungal pathogens. Management requires prolonged targeted intravenous antibiotic therapy guided by culture results, and early involvement of an endocarditis heart team to determine candidacy for surgical intervention versus a medical strategy. Indications for surgery in PVE include heart failure due to valvular dysfunction, uncontrolled infection (abscess, persistent bacteremia), and prevention of embolic events in the setting of large vegetations. However, surgical decision-making is nuanced in prosthetic patients because of the higher operative risk and technical complexity of reoperation—these factors are especially salient in TAVI recipients who may be older and frailer.

# Reported outcomes of valve-in-valve and repeat transcatheter procedures

Valve-in-valve (ViV) TAVI denotes deployment of a transcatheter valve within a failing surgical bioprosthesis or within a prior transcatheter valve as a strategy to treat structural valve degeneration, paravalvular leak, or other forms of prosthetic dysfunction. ViV has expanded the therapeutic armamentarium for patients who are poor surgical candidates or wish to avoid repeat open surgery.

For failed surgical bioprostheses, ViV offers a less invasive alternative with generally favorable short-to-midterm outcomes: procedural success rates are high, and many patients experience immediate hemodynamic improvement and symptomatic relief. However, ViV procedures carry specific technical challenges that influence outcome. When a transcatheter valve is deployed within a surgical valve with a small labelled inner diameter, residual gradients can be higher than after initial valve implantation, producing patient–prosthesis mismatch and potentially accelerated hemodynamic deterioration. The risk of patient–prosthesis mismatch is greatest in small surgical prostheses and contributes to the selection of valve type and sizing strategy. Coronary obstruction is an infrequent but catastrophic complication of ViV, particularly when the surgical valve’s leaflets are externally mounted or when the virtual transcatheter valve-to-coronary distance is minimal; pre-procedural CT planning and techniques such as leaflet laceration (e.g., BASILICA) are used to mitigate that risk.

Outcomes of repeat transcatheter procedures—deploying a second transcatheter valve inside a degenerated transcatheter valve—are increasingly reported as initial TAVI cohorts age. Early reports indicate reasonable procedural success and symptomatic benefit, but cumulative valve-frame geometry changes can affect coronary access, create higher transvalvular gradients, and limit future reintervention options. Long-term durability of a valve-in-valve-in-valve strategy remains uncertain, and each successive layered valve potentially reduces effective orifice area.

Reintervention after TAVI for SVD, paravalvular leak, or PVE is technically feasible with acceptable short-term outcomes in many series, but the complexity increases with each subsequent intervention. For patients requiring valve explantation because of endocarditis or severe dysfunction, redo surgery carries higher operative morbidity and mortality than initial SAVR—decisions must balance the risks of open conversion versus percutaneous reintervention. Importantly, outcomes are influenced by the indication for reintervention: ViV for structural degeneration generally has better immediate outcomes than reintervention for PVE or for severe paravalvular complications.

Clinical decision-making for ViV includes assessment of the original valve type and size, the mechanism of failure, coronary height and ostial orientation, and patient-specific anatomic considerations. Use of pre-procedural CT imaging to simulate device implantation and predict residual gradients and coronary obstruction is standard practice. When residual gradient risk is high (e.g., small surgical prostheses), strategies such as bioprosthetic valve fracture—using high-pressure balloon dilation to fracture the surgical valve stent ring—have been adopted to enlarge the effective orifice and reduce gradients; this approach has particular anatomic prerequisites and carries its own risks.

# Knowledge gaps and areas of active research

Despite substantial advances, several important knowledge gaps remain and are the focus of active clinical and translational research.

First, definitive long-term durability beyond 10–15 years for contemporary transcatheter valves is not yet established in broad populations, especially in younger patients. Ongoing trials and registries are collecting extended follow-up to quantify late SVD rates, predictors of degeneration, and comparative durability against modern surgical bioprostheses. A central question is whether mechanisms unique to TAVI (crimping-related injury, supra-annular leaflet position, leaflet design) confer different long-term failure profiles.

Second, the natural history and clinical significance of subclinical leaflet thrombosis after TAVI and SAVR are incompletely resolved. Although routine CT imaging often detects hypo-attenuated leaflet thickening, the proportion that progresses to symptomatic dysfunction or predisposes to embolic events is not fully defined, and optimal antithrombotic management (antiplatelet vs anticoagulation, duration) remains an area of controversy. Randomized trials are exploring antithrombotic strategies tailored to patient thrombotic and bleeding risk.

Third, the epidemiology and optimal prevention of PVE after TAVI require more granular data. Outstanding questions include whether TAVI confers a different intrinsic infection risk than SAVR after accounting for confounding by indication, which procedural factors most increase risk, and what perioperative antimicrobial prophylaxis or procedural sterility measures most effectively reduce PVE incidence. Additionally, the role of device design (e.g., leaflet material, frame composition) in susceptibility to bacterial adherence and biofilm formation is under investigation.

Fourth, strategies for reintervention after TAVI—especially long-term planning for younger patients—are uncertain. The clinical consequences of sequential valve-in-valve procedures on hemodynamics, coronary access, and feasibility of future surgical explantation need more study. Comparative outcomes of initial SAVR (with the possibility of subsequent ViV) versus primary TAVI followed by ViV over a 20-year horizon are hypothetical at present and are being modeled and investigated through registry linkage and long-term trials.

Fifth, better diagnostic algorithms for PVE in prosthetic valves are needed. While FDG PET/CT has improved sensitivity for detecting perivalvular infection, standardization of imaging protocols, interpretation criteria to distinguish sterile post-implant inflammation from infection, and integration with microbiologic and echocardiographic data are ongoing research priorities.

Finally, health-systems research is exploring how to optimize allocation of TAVI versus SAVR across populations, taking into account not only clinical outcomes but also quality-of-life, costs, and access to reintervention. Comparative effectiveness studies that incorporate patient preferences, life expectancy modeling, and long-term device performance are crucial to developing evidence-based policies.

Conclusion

Population-level experience with TAVI has transformed the management of aortic stenosis by enabling less invasive therapy across a widening range of patient risk and age. Midterm durability of contemporary transcatheter valves appears comparable to surgical bioprostheses in many cohorts, but long-term data beyond a decade are still evolving and are especially relevant for younger patients. Prosthetic valve endocarditis remains an uncommon but high-mortality complication; its detection is more challenging in prosthetic valves and often requires multimodality imaging and multidisciplinary management. Valve-in-valve and repeat transcatheter procedures expand treatment options for degenerated prostheses but introduce unique hemodynamic and anatomic considerations that affect outcomes and future options. Finally, key knowledge gaps—especially concerning very long-term durability, subclinical leaflet thrombosis, PVE prevention and diagnosis, and life-course strategies for valve therapy—are the focus of ongoing research that will shape future practice.
