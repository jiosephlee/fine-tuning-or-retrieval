Introduction

Deciding between surgical and transcatheter approaches for valvular heart disease and infective endocarditis requires integration of patient-specific risk, anatomic suitability, prosthesis type, microbiology, and expected procedural outcomes. The choice is not binary; it involves estimating short- and long-term mortality and morbidity, predicting technical feasibility, and balancing the risks of reintervention and recurrent infection with the benefits of less invasive approaches. This chapter reviews commonly used risk stratification tools, formal indications for surgery in native and prosthetic valve endocarditis, technical and anatomic challenges encountered when removing transcatheter prostheses or performing aortic root replacement, comparative outcomes of surgical versus transcatheter reintervention in high-risk cohorts, and special perioperative considerations in frail and comorbid patients. Emphasis is placed on pathophysiologic rationales for operative intervention and the clinical reasoning that guides therapy selection.

# Risk scoring systems: EuroSCORE II, STS score and their applications

Risk scoring models attempt to provide an evidence-based estimate of perioperative mortality and major morbidity to inform shared decision-making and procedural planning. Two widely used tools are the European System for Cardiac Operative Risk Evaluation II (EuroSCORE II) and the Society of Thoracic Surgeons (STS) Adult Cardiac Surgery Risk Model. Both are derived from large surgical registries and use clinical variables to estimate operative mortality; the STS models also predict specific morbidities such as stroke, renal failure, prolonged ventilation, and need for reoperation.

EuroSCORE II is calculated from variables including age, sex, renal impairment, extracardiac arteriopathy, poor mobility, previous cardiac surgery, chronic lung disease, active endocarditis, critical preoperative state, left ventricular function, pulmonary hypertension, urgency of surgery, weight of the intervention, and other factors. It outputs an estimated percentage probability of in-hospital or 30-day mortality. EuroSCORE II is convenient and widely used in European centers and for international comparisons. Its calibration can drift over time as populations and techniques evolve; thus local validation is desirable.

The STS risk score family includes distinct models for isolated aortic or mitral valve procedures, combined procedures, and for specific morbidity endpoints. Key inputs include age, sex, body surface area, chronic lung disease, renal function (creatinine or dialysis), neurologic disease, cardiogenic shock, urgency, frailty markers, left ventricular ejection fraction, prior cardiac operations, and concomitant coronary artery bypass grafting. The STS models give separate probabilities for operative mortality and major complications and are commonly used in North America and in guideline recommendations for decision-making regarding transcatheter therapies. The STS-PROM (Predicted Risk of Mortality) is often used to define low-, intermediate-, and high-risk strata; for example, an STS-PROM >8% has been used historically to define high surgical risk, though thresholds vary by indication and era.

Applications and limitations: Risk scores are most helpful as one component of a multi-disciplinary assessment rather than as absolute determinants. They are useful for informing consent, benchmarking institutional performance, and triaging patients for heart-team discussions. Limitations include imperfect calibration in certain subgroups (for example, patients undergoing reoperation, endocarditis, or complex root reconstruction), inability to capture nuanced anatomic variables (such as extent of annular destruction, presence of fistula, or size and location of vegetations), and inadequate incorporation of frailty and cognitive function in older adults. Newer risk paradigms integrate objective frailty measures, nutritional status, and quality-of-life projections. Clinicians should apply risk scores in the context of individual patient goals, expected longer-term outcomes, and the team’s technical experience with either surgical or transcatheter approaches.

# Indications for surgical valve replacement in native and prosthetic valve endocarditis

Indications for surgical intervention in infective endocarditis are driven by complications that threaten survival or are unlikely to be controlled by antibiotics alone. Guidelines from major societies (e.g., European Society of Cardiology, American Heart Association) provide criteria to identify patients who should be considered for early surgery. The major indications include heart failure due to valvular dysfunction, uncontrolled infection, and prevention of embolic events.

Heart failure: Severe valvular regurgitation or obstruction causing hemodynamic instability, cardiogenic shock, or persistent pulmonary edema is a surgical emergency. For native aortic or mitral valve endocarditis, acute severe regurgitation with signs of pulmonary edema or low cardiac output typically mandates urgent valve repair or replacement. Left-sided prosthetic valve dysfunction causing obstruction or severe regurgitation with heart failure also commonly requires operation. Decision-making includes assessment of left ventricular ejection fraction and other measures of hemodynamic compromise.

Uncontrolled infection: Indications for surgery include abscess formation (perivalvular extension), fistulae, large or expanding vegetations despite appropriate antibiotic therapy, and persistent bacteremia lasting more than 5–7 days with organisms known to be difficult to eradicate (e.g., fungi, multidrug-resistant organisms) or with evidence of persistent septic emboli. Perivalvular abscess, especially in the aortic root or annular area, portends a high risk of conduction disturbances, pseudoaneurysm, and sepsis; surgery is generally required to debride infected tissue and reconstruct the annulus/root.

Prevention of embolism: Vegetations larger than approximately 10 mm, especially those that are mobile, are associated with increased risk of systemic embolization. The presence of recurrent emboli despite antibiotics, large vegetation size (>10 mm) with additional risk features (mobility, prior embolus), or vegetations >15 mm in some guidelines are considered thresholds for surgical consideration to prevent further embolic events. The timing of surgery must weigh embolic risk against procedural risk in the setting of recent cerebral embolism; in ischemic stroke without hemorrhage, early surgery is often safe and beneficial, while in intracranial hemorrhage surgery may be delayed.

Native versus prosthetic: Prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) carries higher morbidity and mortality and a higher incidence of perivalvular complications compared with native valve endocarditis. Indications for surgical intervention are similar but the threshold for operation is often lower because of the greater risk of annular involvement, dehiscence, and difficulty in achieving sterilization of infected prosthetic material. Early PVE (occurring within 60 days of valve implantation) and late PVE may differ in microbiology and pathogenesis, but both may require explantation of infected prostheses in the presence of structural dysfunction, abscess, or persistent infection.

Microbiology and timing: Certain pathogens drive the urgency of surgical management. Candida and other fungal endocarditis and infections due to multidrug-resistant organisms have poor response to medical therapy alone; early operation is recommended when feasible. Staphylococcus aureus, particularly methicillin-resistant strains, and enterococcal infections also have a higher propensity for complications; the presence of persistent bacteremia or the failure of high-quality antibiotics to sterilize the blood is an indication for surgery.

Valve repair versus replacement: When possible, valve repair is preferred for mitral valve endocarditis because it preserves the subvalvular apparatus and has better long-term outcomes in selected patients. However, the extent of destruction, presence of annular abscess, or prosthetic involvement often mandates replacement. Choice of prosthesis in endocarditis is individualized, with attention to the patient’s age, comorbidities, and the potential need for future interventions.

Decision-making framework: The heart team evaluates hemodynamics, structural destruction on echocardiography (transesophageal echocardiography is central), extracardiac complications (cerebral emboli, mycotic aneurysms), microbiology and antibiotic response, and patient comorbidities including frailty. Timing is categorized as emergent (within 24 hours), urgent (within days), or elective (after a period of stabilization) based on the clinical scenario.

# Technical challenges of explanting transcatheter valves and root replacement scenarios

The increasing adoption of transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) has created new technical challenges when these valves fail, become infected, or require removal. Explantation of transcatheter valves and reconstruction of the aortic root often present technical complexity beyond standard redo valve operations due to endothelialization, stent frame endothelial ingrowth, valve-in-valve constructs, and interaction with annular or root anatomy.

Biologic behavior of transcatheter valves: Transcatheter valves consist of a stent frame (often nitinol or stainless steel/cobalt-chromium), leaflets typically made from bioprosthetic tissue, and a fabric skirt. Over time the stent frame apposes and integrates with the aortic annulus and adjacent tissue. Endothelialization and fibrous ingrowth anchor the frame firmly, making complete extraction difficult and potentially injurious to the annulus, coronary ostia, and surrounding structures. When a valve-in-valve (ViV) is present, multiple layers of stent and leaflets can further complicate removal.

Explant techniques: Explant begins with careful exposure and identification of the frame edges. Strategies include sharp dissection to free fibrous tissue, cutting or fragmenting the frame, and in some instances leaving portions of the frame in place while reconstructing the annulus. Extraction risks include annular rupture, aortic root disruption, damage to the mitral-aortic curtain, injury to the conduction system, and coronary ostial injury. Surgeons must be prepared for extended cardiopulmonary bypass times, complex reconstruction, and the possibility of root replacement.

Aortic root replacement scenarios: When infection, annular destruction, or intraoperative injury compromises the native aortic root, replacement of the aortic root is often required. Options include composite valve grafts (Bentall procedure), root replacement with a biologic homograft, or use of xenograft roots. Homografts are attractive in endocarditis because of their resistance to infection and the absence of sewing ring prosthetic material, but availability is limited and long-term durability varies. Composite mechanical or bioprosthetic grafts provide excellent hemodynamics and durability but entail implanting prosthetic material in an infected field, which can be mitigated by aggressive debridement and prolonged antibiotics.

Conduction and coronary considerations: Explantation near the membranous septum risks conduction tissue injury with consequent atrioventricular block requiring permanent pacing. Proximity of the transcatheter frame to coronary ostia complicates safe removal; in some cases exteriorization of the left coronary artery or coronary reimplantation may be necessary during root replacement. The presence of a prior surgical graft or conduit also affects approach and exposure.

Planning and intraoperative decision-making: Preoperative imaging, including gated CT angiography, is essential to assess frame position, leaflet calcification, coronary heights, and annular dimensions. In endocarditis, CT can help identify abscesses, pseudoaneurysms, and extension to adjacent structures. The heart team should anticipate the need for alternative strategies—such as root replacement, patch reconstruction of annular defects, or adjunctive coronary bypass—and have appropriate grafts, homografts, and tissue available. Intraoperative flexibility is critical because the extent of debridement and the degree of tissue destruction may only be fully appreciated after opening the aorta.

# Comparative outcomes of surgical vs transcatheter reintervention in high-risk patients

Comparative outcomes between repeat surgery and transcatheter reinterventions (such as valve-in-valve procedures) reflect a balance of short-term procedural risk and long-term durability and complications. Randomized data comparing surgery to transcatheter reintervention for failed TAVR or for infected transcatheter valves are limited, so observational registries and cohort studies guide practice.

Short-term outcomes: In high-risk or frail patients, transcatheter reinterventions (for example, valve-in-valve TAVR in failed bioprostheses) are associated with lower immediate procedural morbidity and mortality compared with redo open surgery. Shorter operative times, avoidance of sternotomy and cardiopulmonary bypass, and more rapid recovery often favor transcatheter approaches in patients with prohibitive surgical risk. For primary TAVR failure unrelated to infection (structural valve degeneration or paravalvular leak), valve-in-valve TAVR can be an excellent option when anatomic factors permit.

Long-term outcomes and durability: Surgical reoperation provides the advantage of complete removal of dysfunctional or infected prosthetic tissue, comprehensive debridement, and the ability to reconstruct damaged annular and root structures. In cases of infection, surgical explantation with debridement and reconstruction generally has superior eradication rates compared with conservative or purely percutaneous approaches. For noninfectious valve degeneration, the long-term durability of valve-in-valve TAVR may be limited by patient–prosthesis mismatch due to decreased effective orifice area, residual gradients, coronary obstruction risk, and leaflets’ hemodynamics. Repeated transcatheter valves can compound these issues.

Endocarditis-specific outcomes: For infective endocarditis involving transcatheter valves or prosthetic material, early surgical removal plus reconstruction is associated with lower rates of persistent infection and recurrent sepsis in many series. Percutaneous interventions or conservative management may be considered in selected patients with prohibitive surgical risk, but the risk of uncontrolled infection and perivalvular complications must be carefully considered. Observational data suggest higher recurrence of infection and worse long-term survival when infected prostheses are not explanted when feasible.

Risk-adjusted decision-making: Comparative outcome data emphasize individualized decisions. For a frail, comorbid patient with a failed transcatheter leaflets causing high gradients but no infection, valve-in-valve TAVR may provide symptom relief with acceptable short-term risk. In contrast, a patient with prosthetic valve endocarditis and root abscess is generally best treated with surgical explantation and reconstruction despite elevated operative risk because the likelihood of cure with medical therapy alone is low. The heart team must incorporate risk scores, frailty assessment, expected quality-of-life improvement, and the patient’s priorities. In many centers, hybrid strategies (for example, percutaneous bridging to definitive surgery or staged procedures) are employed in selected high-risk patients.

Quality metrics and institutional experience: Outcomes of complex redo operations and explantation procedures correlate strongly with institutional and operator experience. Centers with multidisciplinary programs that include cardiology, cardiac surgery, infectious disease, imaging, and geriatric or palliative care achieve better outcomes through coordinated care and appropriate patient selection. Referral to high-volume centers should be considered for complex reinterventions.

# Operative perioperative considerations in frail and comorbid patients

Frail and comorbid patients represent a growing proportion of those referred for valve interventions. Frailty, multimorbidity, and cognitive impairment are independent predictors of adverse outcomes after cardiac surgery and transcatheter procedures. Effective perioperative management requires objective assessment of frailty, optimization of reversible conditions, perioperative planning to reduce complications, and shared decision-making that aligns interventions with the patient’s goals.

Frailty assessment and prognostication: Frailty captures physiologic reserve and vulnerability to stressors. Tools include gait speed (e.g., 5-meter walk time), grip strength, the Clinical Frailty Scale, Timed Up and Go, and composite indices such as the frailty phenotype or frailty index. Objective measures correlate with increased mortality, prolonged hospitalization, and lower quality-of-life gains after valve procedures. Incorporating frailty into risk assessment improves prediction over scores based solely on comorbidity. Cognitive screening and assessment of social support are also essential for postoperative recovery planning.

Preoperative optimization: Attention to nutrition, anemia, glycemic control, and pulmonary function can mitigate risks. Correcting reversible anemia and optimizing iron stores reduces transfusion requirements and may improve outcomes. Prehabilitation programs that include physical therapy and nutritional support can enhance functional reserve. In patients with chronic kidney disease, reviewing medication nephrotoxicity risks and optimizing volume status preoperatively reduces postoperative dialysis risk. Multidisciplinary involvement of geriatrics, nutritionists, and rehabilitation services should be initiated early.

Anesthetic and intraoperative strategies: Minimally invasive or transcatheter approaches may reduce physiologic stress and facilitate faster recovery in frail patients; however, they can be limited by anatomic factors or infection. When surgery is chosen, careful anesthetic planning to reduce hemodynamic instability, minimize cardiopulmonary bypass duration, and employ myocardial protection strategies is critical. Use of cerebral oximetry, tight glucose control, and avoidance of excessive transfusion are components of best practice. Consideration of regional anesthesia adjuncts and multimodal analgesia can reduce opioid-related delirium risk.

Perioperative infection control and antibiotics: In endocarditis or when infection is suspected, perioperative antimicrobial therapy must be coordinated with infectious disease specialists. Timing and duration of preoperative antibiotics should be individualized; in some urgent cases surgery proceeds after hours of therapy when indicated for hemodynamic collapse. Postoperative antibiotic plans depend on operative findings, tissue cultures, and whether prosthetic material was implanted.

Postoperative care and discharge planning: Frail patients have higher rates of delirium, functional decline, and prolonged rehabilitation needs. Early mobilization, delirium prevention protocols (sleep hygiene, minimizing deliriogenic medications, reorientation), and aggressive physiotherapy are essential. Discharge planning should involve assessment of the home environment, availability of caregivers, and need for inpatient rehabilitation. Advance care planning and alignment of postoperative goals with patient preferences are part of high-quality perioperative care.

Palliative considerations: For some frail patients with limited life expectancy or high procedural risk and low expected symptomatic benefit, nonoperative management or palliative approaches may better align with the patient’s goals. Discussions should frame the expected trajectory with and without intervention, including the likelihood of symptom relief, procedural risks, rehabilitation needs, and impact on independence. Multidisciplinary conversations including cardiology, cardiac surgery, geriatrics, and palliative care enhance patient-centered decisions.

Conclusion

Surgical and transcatheter valve interventions each have roles determined by anatomic suitability, infection status, patient risk, and goals of care. Risk scores such as EuroSCORE II and STS provide useful quantitative estimates but must be integrated with frailty assessment and detailed imaging. Endocarditis—particularly with annular destruction or root abscess—often compels surgical debridement and reconstruction, whereas noninfectious structural valve degeneration may be amenable to transcatheter solutions in the appropriate anatomic context. Explantation of transcatheter valves and aortic root replacement are technically demanding and require meticulous preoperative planning and institutional expertise. Comparative outcomes favor less invasive approaches for short-term recovery in high-risk patients but favor surgical explantation when infection control or durable reconstruction are priorities. In frail and comorbid patients, individualized assessment, preoperative optimization, and multidisciplinary planning are essential to achieve the best possible outcomes that align with patient values.
