Introduction

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) has become a cornerstone therapy for calcific aortic stenosis across surgical risk spectrums. As TAVI volumes rise and life expectancy after implantation increases, complications—both periprocedural and long-term—are increasingly encountered. Recognition of these complications, understanding their mechanisms and risk factors, and timely management are essential to optimize outcomes and to plan for possible repeat valve procedures, including valve-in-valve interventions. This chapter reviews the major complications associated with TAVI and redo valve procedures, emphasizing pathophysiology, clinical presentation, diagnostic strategies, prevention, and treatment options.

# Vascular complications: dissection, perforation, pseudoaneurysm and hematoma

Vascular complications remain among the most common early adverse events after TAVI because most procedures use percutaneous arterial access (typically transfemoral). The pathophysiology is related to large-bore sheath insertion, calcified or tortuous iliofemoral vessels, balloon valvuloplasty, and device passage. Vessel injury can range from intimal tear (dissection) to full-thickness perforation with extravasation, or focal arterial wall disruption producing pseudoaneurysm; soft tissue bleeding produces hematoma.

Clinical presentation varies with severity. Small dissections may be asymptomatic or manifest as limb ischemia from flow limitation. Perforation typically presents with hypotension, expanding groin hematoma, or signs of retroperitoneal hemorrhage (flank pain, abdominal distension, declining hemoglobin). Pseudoaneurysms often present with a pulsatile mass, bruit, or persistent groin pain days after access. Hematomas can be localized or cause compartment syndrome.

Diagnosis relies on a combination of clinical exam and imaging. Duplex ultrasound is first-line for suspected pseudoaneurysm or hematoma at the access site and can demonstrate flow within a pseudoaneurysm sac (yin-yang flow) and the characteristic "to-and-fro" waveform in the neck. Computed tomography angiography (CTA) of the pelvis and abdomen is the best test for complex injuries, retroperitoneal bleeding, vessel perforation, or dissections extending proximally; it also informs endovascular repair planning. Digital subtraction angiography during the procedure is essential when intraprocedural bleeding is suspected.

Management is graded to the lesion and hemodynamic status. Small pseudoaneurysms may thrombose spontaneously; ultrasound-guided thrombin injection is highly effective for pseudoaneurysms with a narrow neck. Covered stent graft placement (endovascular repair) is commonly used for perforation or extensive dissections when anatomy permits and is preferable in hemodynamically stable patients to avoid open surgery. Open surgical repair is reserved for cases not amenable to endovascular therapy or with ongoing hemorrhage and instability. Local hematomas without expanding or compressive features are managed conservatively with monitoring, analgesia, and transfusion if needed, but expanding hematomas or those causing neurovascular compromise require urgent evacuation and possible vascular repair.

Prevention is critical: preprocedural CT angiography to plan access route and gauge vessel diameter, calcification, and tortuosity; using the smallest feasible sheath and lower-profile devices; intraprocedural ultrasound-guided vascular puncture; percutaneous closure devices deployed by experienced operators; and bailout strategies (e.g., access-site covered stent availability). For patients with hostile femoral anatomy, alternative access routes (transaxillary, transcarotid, transcaval, or surgical transapical/transaortic approaches) should be considered.

# Bleeding risks and access-site management

Bleeding after TAVI encompasses access-site bleeding, intraprocedural anticoagulant-related hemorrhage, and delayed bleeding from antithrombotic therapy. Patient factors – advanced age, frailty, chronic kidney disease, anemia, and concomitant antiplatelet or anticoagulant medications – increase bleeding risk. Procedural factors include large-bore vascular access, multiple arterial punctures, and the need for surgical cutdown.

The Valve Academic Research Consortium (VARC) definitions are commonly used to classify bleeding severity into minor, major, and life-threatening/disabling bleeding. Major bleeding is associated with significant hemoglobin drop, transfusion, or procedural intervention; life-threatening bleeding includes fatal bleeding, hemodynamic compromise requiring surgery, or bleeding in critical sites. Clinical decision-making balances ischemic and bleeding risks when prescribing periprocedural antithrombotic therapy. Typical practice has been dual antiplatelet therapy briefly post-TAVI in patients without an indication for anticoagulation, but trials (e.g., POPular TAVI) showed that single antiplatelet therapy reduces bleeding without increasing ischemic events in many patients. For patients with atrial fibrillation requiring oral anticoagulation, guideline-endorsed strategies favor oral anticoagulant alone over combination therapy with antiplatelet agents except in specific high-thrombotic scenarios, limiting bleeding risk.

Access-site management aims to minimize bleeding through meticulous technique. Ultrasound-guided arterial access reduces inadvertent punctures. Preclosure with suture-based percutaneous closure devices (e.g., ProGlide) is routine for transfemoral TAVI; operators must be experienced and prepared for closure device failure, which is a known mechanism of groin bleeding. Postprocedural monitoring includes frequent inspection of puncture sites, hemodynamic surveillance, serial hemoglobin checks, and early imaging with duplex ultrasound if bleeding or pseudoaneurysm is suspected. In cases of major bleeding, transfusion thresholds should be individualized; however, restrictive strategies (e.g., transfuse for hemoglobin <7–8 g/dL in stable patients) are often applied, with higher thresholds for symptomatic or high-risk patients. Reversal agents for anticoagulants (e.g., protamine for unfractionated heparin) should be used judiciously.

When percutaneous closure fails or there is ongoing bleeding, options include manual compression, deployment of covered stents for arterial injury, surgical repair, and embolization techniques for smaller branches. Multidisciplinary team readiness (interventional cardiology, vascular surgery, and interventional radiology) improves outcomes.

# Paravalvular leak: mechanisms, assessment and percutaneous closure options

Paravalvular leak (PVL) refers to regurgitation around the implanted transcatheter valve due to incomplete sealing between the prosthesis and native annulus. PVL is more common after TAVI than surgical replacement because TAVI relies on radial force and stent frame apposition within a calcified, irregular annulus rather than sutured annular closure. Mechanisms include severe or asymmetric annular calcification preventing full valve expansion, undersized prosthesis relative to annular dimensions, irregular or elliptical annulus geometry, and malpositioning or under-expansion of the frame. Postdilatation can improve apposition but risks annular injury or conduction disturbances.

Assessment of PVL requires multimodality imaging. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) is the first-line tool for screening and grading regurgitation; transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) provides higher sensitivity and is valuable intra-procedurally. Criteria for quantification include jet width, circumferential extent of regurgitation around the prosthesis, regurgitant volume and effective regurgitant orifice area, and Doppler-based measures. Because prosthetic stents create acoustic shadowing and multiple eccentric jets may occur, integrative assessment combining color Doppler, spectral Doppler (e.g., continuous-wave Doppler of LV outflow tract), and quantitative methods is necessary. A widely used qualitative grading uses none/trace, mild, moderate, and severe categories; moderate or greater PVL is associated with worse outcomes and heart failure symptoms.

CT angiography plays a role in preprocedural sizing and in assessing the mechanism in symptomatic patients by delineating valve frame deformation and annular calcification. Aortography with selective contrast injection can visualize the regurgitant jets and is often performed at the time of implantation.

Management starts with prevention: careful annular sizing with CT, selection of appropriate valve type and size, and attention to positioning. Balloon-expandable valves may offer more predictable expansion in heavily calcified annuli, while self-expanding valves may be better tolerated in certain anatomies. Intraprocedural techniques include careful deployment depth control and, if appropriate, postdilatation to improve seal. When significant PVL is identified post-implant and persists despite ballooning or is associated with symptoms, percutaneous closure is the preferred first-line therapy in many centers.

Percutaneous PVL closure involves transcatheter delivery of occluder devices into the paravalvular channels to reduce regurgitation. Devices employed include dedicated paravalvular leak plugs and off-label use of vascular plugs such as the Amplatzer Vascular Plug II and III. Choice of device depends on the size, shape, and number of jets; multiple small devices or larger plugs may be required. Access can be transfemoral, transseptal (for mitral PVL), or transapical depending on valve location. Procedural success requires careful crossing of the paravalvular defect under fluoroscopic and echocardiographic guidance, accurate device sizing (often trialled under fluoroscopy), and monitoring for interference with valve leaflets or coronary flow. Complications of closure include device embolization, interference with valve function leading to stenosis or new regurgitation, and residual leak. In some patients with severe PVL not amenable to percutaneous repair, repeat valve implantation (valve-in-valve or valve-in-ring) or surgical intervention may be considered.

# Valve malposition, embolization and coronary obstruction

Valve malposition and embolization are uncommon but potentially catastrophic procedural complications. Malposition occurs when the transcatheter valve is deployed too high (aortic side) or too low (ventricular side) relative to the annulus. Both can result in paravalvular leak, coronary obstruction risk, conduction disturbances, or left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) obstruction. Embolization is complete displacement of the valve from the intended landing zone into the aorta or into the left ventricle; embolized valves may occlude branch vessels, impede flow, or cause arrhythmias.

Mechanisms include inaccurate annular sizing, insufficient anchoring due to undersizing or inadequate calcification, rapid pacing failure during deployment, operator error, or hostile anatomy (e.g., low coronary height, shallow sinuses of Valsalva, severe annular angulation). Emergent management depends on direction of embolization and hemodynamics. A valve embolized into the aorta can sometimes be secured proximally with a second valve deployed in the annulus (valve-in-valve) and the embolized valve left in the aorta if not causing obstruction, sometimes sandwiched against the aortic walls or anchored with a stent. An embolized valve into the left ventricle is more complex and often requires surgical retrieval. Prevention hinges on meticulous preprocedural CT planning, intra-procedural imaging, careful deployment technique, and readiness for bailout strategies.

Coronary obstruction is a life-threatening complication that results from occlusion of a coronary ostium by displaced native leaflet tissue or by the prosthetic valve frame or leaflets, leading to myocardial ischemia, cardiogenic shock, and malignant arrhythmias. Risk factors include low coronary ostial height (<10–12 mm to the annulus), small sinus of Valsalva diameter (<30–35 mm depending on prosthesis and operator assessment), bulky calcified leaflets, and valve-in-valve procedures where the surgical bioprosthetic leaflets may be displaced towards the coronary ostia. Presentation is acute ST-elevation or ischemia during or shortly after deployment, hypotension, and arrhythmia.

Prevention is critical and includes meticulous CT assessment of coronary heights and sinus anatomy, consideration of valve choice and implantation depth, and preventive techniques when high risk is identified. The BASILICA (Bioprosthetic or Native Aortic Scallop Intentional Laceration to prevent Iatrogenic Coronary Artery obstruction) technique intentionally lacerates the native or bioprosthetic leaflet(s) projecting toward a coronary ostium to create a controlled split that allows coronary flow post-implant. Intra-procedural prophylactic coronary protection (wiring the coronary artery and having a stent ready for immediate deployment) is another strategy. For acute coronary obstruction, immediate coronary angiography is required. If the coronary can be crossed, ballooning and urgent stent deployment (chimney stenting) can restore flow. In refractory cases, cardiopulmonary support and surgical intervention may be necessary.

# Conduction disturbances, new bundle branch block and need for permanent pacing

Conduction disturbances are a common and clinically significant complication after TAVI because of the close anatomical relationship between the aortic valve complex and the cardiac conduction system (notably the atrioventricular node and the left bundle branch system, which runs subendocardially near the membranous septum). Compression, inflammation, or ischemic injury of the conduction tissue by the valve frame or by annular calcium can produce new atrioventricular block (AVB), new left bundle branch block (LBBB), or wider QRS complexes.

New LBBB is common after TAVI, with rates varying by valve type and implantation depth—self-expanding valves and deeper (more ventricular) implants are associated with higher LBBB incidence. New LBBB may be transient or persistent and can be associated with adverse outcomes, including eventual progression to high-degree AV block and reduced left ventricular synchrony and function. The clinical consequences can include heart failure exacerbation and increased mortality in some studies.

Permanent pacemaker implantation (PPI) remains the primary therapy for high-degree (second-degree Mobitz type II or third-degree) AV block and for symptomatic bradycardia unresponsive to temporary pacing. Indications for PPI after TAVI follow standard pacing guidelines but must be applied with knowledge of TAVI-specific dynamics: some conduction disturbances are transient, so many centers observe patients with continuous telemetry for 48–72 hours before committing to PPI unless there is persistent high-grade AV block. Predictors of PPI include baseline right bundle branch block (higher risk), deep valve implantation, greater valve oversizing, and use of self-expanding devices. Preprocedural ECGs, baseline conduction abnormalities, and continuous postprocedural telemetry inform decision-making.

Strategies to reduce conduction complications include precise implantation technique to minimize ventricular extension of the frame (i.e., higher implantation), selecting valve type based on patient anatomy and conduction risk, and avoiding aggressive oversizing. When temporary pacing is used during the procedure, a temporary transvenous pacemaker or active fixation temporary leads provide short-term support; leadless pacing systems are emerging options for certain patients. Left bundle branch area pacing and His-bundle pacing are investigational approaches for preserving physiologic conduction in select patients.

# Acute kidney injury, stroke and infection risks

Acute kidney injury (AKI)

AKI after TAVI is multifactorial: contrast-induced nephropathy from intraprocedural iodinated contrast, hemodynamic instability, embolic renal ischemia from atheromatous debris mobilized during vascular manipulation, and pre-existing chronic kidney disease (CKD) contribute. AKI is commonly classified using KDIGO criteria: an increase in serum creatinine by ≥0.3 mg/dL within 48 hours, or a 1.5× increase within 7 days, or urine output <0.5 mL/kg/h for 6 hours. Baseline serum creatinine, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), and urine output should be closely monitored. Normal creatinine ranges depend on age, sex, and muscle mass; roughly 0.6–1.2 mg/dL for adult males and 0.5–1.1 mg/dL for adult females are common reference ranges, but eGFR provides a better assessment.

Prevention includes adequate hydration (isotonic saline), minimizing contrast volume using procedural techniques and alternative imaging, avoiding nephrotoxic drugs, and careful periprocedural hemodynamic management. For patients with established CKD, the threshold for alternative access, staged procedures, or modified contrast strategies should be considered. If AKI occurs, management is supportive with fluid and electrolyte optimization, avoidance of further nephrotoxins, and renal replacement therapy if indicated.

Stroke

Ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke are feared complications. The pathophysiology of periprocedural stroke is primarily embolic, from dislodged aortic arch plaque, native valve or annular calcium, or thrombus formed on devices or wires. Clinical presentation may be immediate or delayed within days after the procedure. Stroke assessment uses standard clinical scales (e.g., National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale, NIHSS) and brain imaging (non-contrast CT to rule out hemorrhage, CTA for large-vessel occlusion) to guide therapy. Mechanical thrombectomy is indicated for large-vessel occlusions in appropriate candidates.

Cerebral embolic protection devices (filters or deflectors deployed in the brachiocephalic and left common carotid arteries) have been studied to reduce embolic load to the brain, showing reduction in procedure-associated particulate matter but variable impact on clinical stroke rates. Their use is considered in selected high-risk patients but is not universally mandated. Strategies to reduce stroke risk include careful manipulation within the aorta, minimizing wire and catheter exchanges, maintaining adequate anticoagulation during the procedure, and prompt identification and management of arrhythmias such as atrial fibrillation.

Infection risks

Infection after TAVI includes access-site cellulitis, bloodstream infection, and prosthetic valve endocarditis. Perioperative prophylactic antibiotics are routine, but mucosal or device-related infections can still occur. Endocarditis after TAVI behaves similarly to prosthetic valve endocarditis after surgical replacement, with staphylococci, enterococci, and oral streptococci common culprits. Fever, bacteremia, new or changing murmur, heart failure, and septic emboli may be clues. Diagnosis uses modified Duke criteria adapted for prosthetic valves, supported by blood cultures and imaging (TEE is more sensitive than TTE for prosthetic valve infection). Positron emission tomography (FDG-PET/CT) may assist in difficult cases.

Management requires prolonged targeted intravenous antibiotics and often involves a multidisciplinary heart team including cardiology, infectious diseases, and cardiac surgery. Indications for surgical explantation include uncontrolled infection, abscess formation, persistent bacteremia, prosthetic dysfunction with heart failure, and end-organ septic embolization. Prevention centers on sterile technique, prophylactic antibiotics, and attention to dental and cutaneous sources of bacteremia.

# Long-term complications: structural deterioration, leaflet thrombosis, endocarditis

Structural valve deterioration (SVD)

SVD refers to intrinsic changes in the bioprosthetic valve leading to stenosis or regurgitation over time due to leaflet calcification, tearing, or degeneration. The rate and pattern of SVD depend on valve material, patient factors, and hemodynamic stresses. Surgical bioprostheses have known long-term failure curves; data for transcatheter prostheses are accumulating but suggest comparable durability in the medium term (5–8 years), with longer-term data still emerging. Clinical surveillance with periodic echocardiography is essential; increases in mean transvalvular gradient, new or worsening regurgitation, or reduced effective orifice area suggest SVD. Reference hemodynamic values post-TAVI vary by prosthesis size, but mean gradients >20 mmHg or a rapid rise in gradients warrant evaluation. Symptomatic SVD may be managed with redo-TAVI (valve-in-valve) in many patients, with attention to patient–prosthesis mismatch and coronary obstruction risk.

Leaflet thrombosis

Bioprosthetic leaflet thrombosis presents as hypoattenuated leaflet thickening (HALT) with reduced leaflet motion (RELM) on 4-dimensional CT imaging, and can manifest as increased transvalvular gradients or as subclinical findings. The pathophysiology includes thrombus formation on low-flow areas of the valve, influenced by patient prothrombotic states, atrial fibrillation, left ventricular dysfunction, and inadequate antithrombotic therapy. Subclinical leaflet thrombosis is common in early imaging studies but its clinical significance varies; however, it has been associated with increased gradients and possible increased risk of stroke in some analyses.

Diagnosis is best made by ECG-gated multidetector CT, which shows hypoattenuated masses on leaflet surfaces and reduced excursion. Echocardiography may detect increased gradients and abnormal leaflet motion but is less sensitive than CT for HALT. Management typically involves anticoagulation: oral anticoagulant therapy (often warfarin or direct oral anticoagulants) can resolve HALT and reduce gradients in many patients. There is ongoing debate about routine anticoagulation after TAVI; current practice is individualized, favoring anticoagulation when leaflet thrombosis is documented or when there is another indication (e.g., atrial fibrillation).

Endocarditis

Prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) after TAVI is a severe complication with substantial morbidity and mortality. Presentation may be subtle, particularly in elderly patients, and can include fever, embolic phenomena, heart failure, or persistent bacteremia. Risk factors include procedural contamination, poor dental hygiene, and repeated vascular access manipulations. Diagnosis uses modified Duke criteria supplemented by advanced imaging because echocardiography may be limited by prosthetic artifacts. TEE is more sensitive than TTE, and FDG-PET/CT and CT angiography can help detect perivalvular extension, abscesses, or prosthetic dehiscence.

Management includes prolonged intravenous antibiotics guided by culture and sensitivity results and individualized consideration of surgical explantation. Surgical indications are similar to other PVE contexts: refractory infection, abscess, prosthetic dysfunction with heart failure, and uncontrolled sepsis. However, many TAVI patients are older or have prohibitive surgical risk, complicating decisions; outcomes depend on timely diagnosis and multidisciplinary care.

Long-term surveillance and prevention

Given the spectrum of late complications, structured follow-up after TAVI is essential. Baseline TTE before discharge establishes a reference for future comparisons. Routine clinical review and imaging at 30 days, 6–12 months, and annually thereafter are common. Any new symptoms—heart failure, syncope, fever, embolic phenomena, or angina—should prompt early evaluation. Prevention of long-term complications includes patient selection, meticulous procedural technique, optimization of antithrombotic strategies tailored to each patient’s ischemic and bleeding risks, dental care and infection prophylaxis when indicated, and patient education regarding signs and symptoms that require urgent attention.

Conclusion

Complications of TAVI span vascular injury and bleeding, paravalvular regurgitation, valve malposition and coronary obstruction, conduction system disease, organ-specific insults such as AKI and stroke, and long-term prosthetic failures including thrombosis and endocarditis. Prevention begins with comprehensive preprocedural imaging and planning, careful intra-procedural technique, and multidisciplinary readiness for bailout therapies. Early recognition through vigilant monitoring and targeted imaging enables timely interventions that range from percutaneous repair to surgical management. As TAVI extends to younger and lower-risk populations, attention to durability, strategies to minimize late complications, and refined approaches to repeat interventions will be increasingly important.
