Introduction

Advances in cardiac computed tomography (CT) and three-dimensional (3D) image processing have transformed procedural planning for structural heart interventions. Where once operators relied primarily on two-dimensional imaging, angiography, and intra-procedural judgment, CT-based 3D modeling and virtual implantation now permit pre-procedural simulation of device–anatomy interaction. These tools can predict how prosthetic frames and skirts will sit relative to the aortic root, native leaflets, and coronary ostia; estimate the risk of coronary obstruction; and inform device selection and implant depth. A thorough appreciation of the steps in CT segmentation, the biophysical and geometric principles underlying virtual deployment, the metrics used to quantify valve-to-coronary relationships, and the limitations of current simulation approaches is essential for clinicians who interpret these models and translate them into procedural decisions.

# Workflow for CT segmentation and 3D model generation

CT-based 3D modeling begins with acquisition of high-quality cardiac CT angiography optimized for structural heart planning. This typically involves electrocardiographic (ECG)-gated contrast-enhanced imaging of the heart and aortic root, with isotropic or near-isotropic voxels and slice thickness usually in the range of 0.5–1.0 mm. Proper bolus timing to opacify the aortic root and coronary ostia is critical; inadequate contrast results in poor delineation of leaflet and leaflet/calcification borders. Multiphase acquisitions are often obtained to capture dynamic changes in annular size and leaflet position across the cardiac cycle; systolic or mid-diastolic phases may be selected depending on the structure of interest.

Segmentation is the process of delineating anatomic structures from the CT voxel data and converting them into 3D surface or volumetric meshes. Automated or semi-automated tools are used to segment the cardiac blood pool, aortic root, native leaflets, valve leaflets (in the case of bioprosthetic valves), coronary arteries to their ostia, and adjacent structures such as the mitral valve apparatus when relevant. Initial steps typically rely on Hounsfield unit (HU) thresholding to separate contrast-enhanced blood from soft tissue. Contrast-enhanced blood typically measures in the range of 150–400 HU depending on timing and scanner; thresholds are set to include the blood pool while excluding surrounding tissue. Thresholding is complemented by region-growing algorithms that expand from seed points within the aortic lumen and by edge-detection filters to refine the boundary.

Calcified leaflet nodules and degenerative bioprosthetic material present a segmentation challenge because calcifications may have much higher HU values and can produce blooming artifacts. Therefore, manual editing by an experienced operator is frequently required to ensure accurate leaflet geometry. The aortic annulus is defined by the nadirs of the three cusp insertions in the virtual ring plane; segmentation software often allows interactive annular plane fitting to extract annular perimeter, area, and derived diameters. Sinus of Valsalva contours, sinotubular junction, ascending aorta dimensions, and coronary ostial coordinates are derived from the segmented root.

Once segmentation is complete, surface meshes are generated to create watertight 3D models. The meshes may be smoothed and decimated to optimize file size while preserving essential geometric fidelity. For finite-element or computational fluid dynamics simulations, volumetric tetrahedral meshes may be created. Important quality-control steps include visual inspection in multiple planes to ensure anatomical landmarks are preserved, and comparison of derived measurements (annular area, perimeter, coronary heights) with standard 2D CT measurements for consistency.

# Virtual valve deployment simulation and prediction of neo-skirt and leaflet position

Virtual valve deployment reproduces how a specific prosthetic device would expand within a patient’s anatomy. There are two broad categories of virtual implantation: geometric placement and physics-based finite element simulation. Geometric approaches position a parametric device model into the 3D anatomy according to specified implantation depth and orientation and then compute intersections with surrounding tissues. Physics-based simulations model device expansion behavior (radial force, frame deformation), interaction with deformable host tissue, and contact forces—producing more physiologically realistic predictions, especially in heavily calcified anatomies.

A central output of virtual deployment is the neo-skirt geometry: the final configuration of native leaflets and prosthetic skirt material that forms the new effective barrier between the aortic root and the coronary ostia or distal aorta. In native aortic valve TAVR, native leaflets are displaced outward by the frame and become part of the neo-skirt; their bulk and residual position determine both paravalvular regurgitation risk and potential coronary obstruction. In valve-in-valve procedures, the degenerated surgical valve leaflets and stent frame also interact with the implanted transcatheter valve to produce a new skirt profile.

During simulation, the software computes the 3D position of leaflets after expansion, including their height above the annulus, radial position relative to the sinus walls, and whether portions of the leaflet are folded or pinned against the frame. The resulting neo-skirt silhouette in cross-section and in en face views is used to evaluate whether it overlaps the coronary ostia. Leaflet thickness, calcific nodules, and the presence of stent posts from prior prostheses influence how much leaflet tissue protrudes into the sinuses. For self-expanding frames that elongate or flare into the sinotubular junction, the neo-skirt may be pushed higher; balloon-expandable frames tend to have more fixed expansion geometry.

Predicting leaflet position also informs hemodynamic consequences. If leaflets are pinned close to the sinotubular junction without adequate space for coronary flow, there is potential for impaired coronary filling, particularly if the sinuses are shallow. Conversely, if the neo-skirt leaves a channel to the coronary ostium, coronary flow may be preserved even with low coronary heights. Advanced simulations may model the size and shape of residual flow channels and estimate cross-sectional area, which correlates with potential coronary perfusion; some groups further simulate contrast or flow to quantify obstruction risk.

# Quantification of valve-to-coronary distances and predicted obstruction

Objective, reproducible metrics are required to quantify the proximity of a planned prosthesis to coronary ostia and to predict coronary obstruction risk. Traditional CT measurements include coronary height (vertical distance from the annular plane to the coronary ostium) and sinus of Valsalva diameter. Coronary height is typically measured perpendicular to the fitted annular plane. General clinical thresholds used to stratify obstruction risk include coronary height less than approximately 10–12 mm and sinus of Valsalva diameter less than ~30–32 mm as markers of increased risk. These thresholds are imperfect and context-dependent because obstructive risk also depends on leaflet length, leaflet bulk, and the anticipated final position of displaced leaflets.

Virtual implantation enables calculation of the valve-to-coronary (VTC) distance, a metric that is often more predictive than coronary height alone. VTC is defined as the minimal distance between the projected outer surface of the implanted transcatheter valve (including displaced leaflets / neo-skirt) and the coronary ostium. Calculation involves performing the virtual deployment at the intended implant depth and then measuring the orthogonal distance from the ostium to the nearest point on the device or neo-skirt. A commonly cited threshold is VTC < 4 mm as high risk for coronary obstruction, with intermediate risk for VTC 4–6 mm and low risk for VTC > 6 mm. These numbers derive from cohort studies comparing predicted VTC with occurrence of coronary obstruction, but thresholds vary across centers and device types.

Another related metric is the valve-to-sinotubular junction (VTSTJ) distance and the predicted residual ostial orifice area when the neo-skirt overlaps the ostium. Simulation can compute the minimal cross-sectional area of the remaining coronary inlet after virtual implantation. In borderline cases, computed neo-skirt geometry may show a small residual slit-like flow channel even when coronary height is low — such information can influence decisions about prophylactic measures such as guide catheter placement or wire protection.

It is essential to recognize that these calculations take into account device-specific external dimensions and skirt heights. Different devices have different skirt lengths, frame heights, and configurations of cells or posts, all of which change the spatial relationship to the coronaries. Therefore, simulation should use manufacturer-provided 3D device models with accurate outer contours for the chosen device and size.

# Limitations of simulation and integration with fluoroscopic planning

Despite their power, CT-based simulations have limitations that influence their reliability. First, CT-derived anatomies represent a snapshot (or a set of snapshots) in time and may not capture dynamic motion or changes that occur during the cardiac cycle, with anesthesia, or under hemodynamic perturbations at the time of the procedure. Segmentation errors arising from limited image quality, motion artifacts, or heavy calcification can propagate through to inaccurate meshes. Calcific and fibrotic tissues do not deform uniformly; assumptions regarding tissue elasticity in physics-based models are approximations and may not reflect local heterogeneity. For example, asymmetric calcification can lead to eccentric expansion or frame deformation that deviates from simulation predictions.

Device behavior in vivo can differ from modeled behavior. Balloon-expandable and self-expanding frames exert distinct radial force patterns, and device foreshortening or frame flaring can be influenced by operator technique, deployment speed, and balloon inflation volumes. Models may not perfectly reproduce these operator-dependent factors. Moreover, the interaction between the prosthetic skirt, displaced leaflet tissue, and complex sinus geometry is influenced by friction, tissue tearing, and anchoring dynamics that are challenging to model precisely.

Integration with fluoroscopic planning helps bridge the gap between pre-procedural simulation and intra-procedural execution. CT-based planning can generate fluoroscopic projection angles (c-arm angulations) that produce a coplanar view of the aortic annulus and cusps for optimal visualization at implantation. Two commonly used projections are the three-cusp view and the "cusp-overlap" technique for left coronary cusp alignment; software can compute the optimal angulations that align anatomical landmarks in the fluoroscopic plane, reducing parallax and improving visualization of deployment depth relative to the annulus. During the procedure, fluoroscopy and transesophageal echocardiography provide real-time feedback about valve position, but they do not completely substitute for the anatomical insights offered by pre-procedural CT.

Because simulation cannot account for all variables, it should be used as an adjunct, not a substitute, for clinical judgment. When simulation predicts high-risk features—very low VTC, small sinuses, or a neo-skirt overlapping the coronary ostium—the operator can plan protective strategies (coronary wiring, positioning of undeployed stent for chimney technique, or consideration of leaflet laceration procedures). The decision to perform such adjunctive maneuvers will also depend on intra-procedural findings, fluoroscopic landmarks, and the patient’s clinical context.

# Examples of how modeling informs choice of valve type and implant depth

Modeling influences device selection and implantation strategy through specific, mechanistic insights. Consider a patient with low coronary heights but large sinuses: simulation may show that although coronary height is borderline, the neo-skirt avoids direct ostial coverage because the sinuses provide lateral space for leaflet displacement. In this scenario, a device with a shorter skirt and conservative implant depth may be favored. Conversely, if simulation shows that leaflet bulk will project directly over the coronary ostium, a device with a shorter skirt or a retrievable design that permits repositioning may be preferred.

Device type selection is also informed by predicted frame–anatomy interaction. For example, in a heavily calcified annulus with eccentric calcium, a balloon-expandable device may achieve more symmetric radial expansion and better sealing, but it may also press bulky native leaflets more directly toward the coronaries. A self-expanding device with a supra-annular leaflet position and taller frame may allow for higher implantation with a larger neo-sinotubular junction clearance, or it may increase the chance of coronary coverage depending on the specific anatomy. Virtual implantation allows the operator to test multiple device sizes and models digitally and observe how the neo-skirt and leaflet positions change with each choice.

Implant depth is another critical variable. A high implant (more atrial) tends to leave more of the prosthetic frame below the annulus and may reduce the risk that leaflets are displaced upward into the coronary ostia; however, it may increase the risk of paravalvular regurgitation or embolization in some anatomies. A deeper (ventricular) implant positions the skirt differently relative to the cusp nadirs and can reduce pacemaker risk by avoiding conduction tissue contact, but might increase the likelihood of leaflet tissue obstructing the coronary ostia. Virtual deployment can be performed iteratively at multiple depths to quantify VTC at each proposed depth, enabling a risk–benefit analysis: for instance, choosing an implant depth that provides adequate coronary clearance (VTC > 4–6 mm) while keeping device radial forces and conduction-system proximity within acceptable limits.

In valve-in-valve procedures, modeling is particularly informative because the prior surgical valve frame and leaflet mounting height govern the space available. Virtual implantation can predict whether the transcatheter valve expansion will trap bioprosthetic leaflets against the surgical frame and whether those trapped leaflets will obstruct coronaries. In such cases, simulations may guide preemptive use of the BASILICA (bioprosthetic or native aortic scallop intentional laceration to prevent iatrogenic coronary artery obstruction) procedure or planning for coronary protection with a guidewire and undeployed stent.

Conclusion

CT-based 3D modeling and virtual implantation are powerful tools that bring detailed anatomic and device-specific insights to procedural planning. A rigorous workflow—starting with high-quality ECG-gated CT, careful segmentation, and generation of accurate 3D meshes—and the use of both geometric and physics-based simulation approaches allow clinicians to predict neo-skirt formation, leaflet displacement, and valve-to-coronary relationships. Quantitative metrics such as coronary height, sinus dimensions, and especially valve-to-coronary distance (VTC) inform risk stratification for coronary obstruction and guide choices of valve type and implant depth. Awareness of limitations—image quality constraints, modeling assumptions, and intra-procedural variability—means these simulations should augment, not replace, clinical judgment and fluoroscopic planning. When integrated thoughtfully into pre-procedural strategy, 3D modeling improves the ability to anticipate complications and tailor the intervention to the patient’s unique anatomy.
