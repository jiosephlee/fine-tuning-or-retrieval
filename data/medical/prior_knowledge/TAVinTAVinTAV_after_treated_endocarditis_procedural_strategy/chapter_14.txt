Introduction

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) and valve-in-valve (ViV) procedures have transformed management of valvular heart disease. After implantation, accurate hemodynamic assessment and serial imaging are essential to determine prosthetic valve function, detect complications, and distinguish mechanical or biological failure modes that require different management strategies. This chapter reviews the principles of Doppler-based hemodynamic assessment, calculation and interpretation of effective orifice area (EOA), indexed EOA and Doppler velocity index (DVI) for prosthetic valves, criteria for patient–prosthesis mismatch (PPM) and its clinical significance, recommended echocardiographic surveillance, and the imaging features that distinguish structural valve deterioration, thrombosis, and paravalvular leak (PVL). The complementary roles of transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) and computed tomography (CT) in ambiguous cases are emphasized so that a clinician can integrate imaging findings with clinical context and decide on further management.

# Doppler assessment of transvalvular gradients and velocity profiles post-implantation

Doppler echocardiography is the cornerstone for noninvasive hemodynamic evaluation of prosthetic aortic valves. Continuous-wave Doppler (CWD) records the highest velocities through the valve and is used to derive peak velocities and transvalvular gradients. The maximal instantaneous gradient is calculated from the peak velocity (V) using the simplified Bernoulli equation: gradient = $4V^2$. The mean gradient is obtained by tracing the velocity curve over systole and integrating to provide the mean pressure drop across the valve. These measurements are flow-dependent, so interpretation must account for concomitant cardiac output, stroke volume, blood pressure, and heart rate.

Immediately after TAVI/ViV, Doppler velocities and gradients may differ from those expected for a given prosthesis because of altered flow state (e.g., anemia, sepsis, reduced left ventricular function), transient hyperdynamic circulation, or measurement errors (suboptimal acoustic windows, misalignment with jet). Accurate alignment of the CWD beam with the jet is essential; multiple acoustic windows (apical, right parasternal, suprasternal notch) should be interrogated to find the highest velocity. Spectral envelopes must be carefully analyzed: a smooth, well-defined envelope indicates laminar flow through an unobstructed orifice; a rounded, prolonged envelope may reflect impaired leaflet opening or elevated transvalvular resistance. Acceleration time (AT, time from onset of flow to peak velocity) can provide additional insight; an AT >100–120 ms suggests significant aortic stenosis physiology in native valves and, in prosthetic settings, prolonged AT should raise suspicion for obstruction.

Indexed interpretation is necessary because prosthetic valves generate higher baseline gradients than native valves of equivalent EOA due to prosthesis geometry and orifice position. Published valve-specific expected gradients and velocity ranges for each prosthesis model should be referenced. More important than an absolute gradient is change over time: a new increase in mean gradient >10–20 mmHg compared with a validated baseline should prompt investigation for thrombosis, pannus, degeneration, or PPM.

Key technical pitfalls include inaccurate measurement of the left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) diameter (which affects EOA calculation), failure to account for para-prosthetic or supra-annular flow convergence, and confounding by concomitant aortic regurgitation or subvalvular obstruction. Doppler recordings should be corroborated with 2D/3D morphology and TEE or CT as needed.

# Calculation and interpretation of EOA, indexed EOA and DVI in prosthetic valves

The effective orifice area (EOA) quantifies the functional opening through which blood flows and is calculated for prosthetic aortic valves using the continuity equation. The continuity equation for aortic prostheses is:

EOA = $\dfrac{CSA_{LVOT} \times VTI_{LVOT}}{VTI_{AV}}$,

where $CSA_{LVOT}$ is the cross-sectional area of the LVOT (calculated as $\pi \times \left(\dfrac{D_{LVOT}}{2}\right)^2$), $VTI_{LVOT}$ is the pulsed-wave Doppler velocity–time integral in the LVOT, and $VTI_{AV}$ is the CWD VTI through the prosthetic valve. Precision in each measurement is crucial. The LVOT diameter should be measured at the correct anatomic location, typically just below the prosthetic sewing ring in prosthetic valves; measurement error in diameter is squared in the CSA, so small errors can produce large EOA errors. For TAVI devices with supra-annular leaflets or stent frames, the LVOT measurement site and shape can complicate CSA estimation; in those situations, three-dimensional planimetry or CT measurements of LVOT area may improve accuracy.

Indexed EOA (iEOA) normalizes EOA to body surface area (BSA) to account for patient size:

iEOA = $\dfrac{EOA}{BSA}$.

This is essential for determining PPM and assessing whether a given EOA is adequate for the patient’s metabolic demands.

Doppler velocity index (DVI), also called the dimensionless index or velocity ratio, is an alternative when LVOT diameter measurement is unreliable. DVI is the ratio of velocity or VTI in the LVOT to the velocity or VTI through the valve:

DVI = $\dfrac{VTI_{LVOT}}{VTI_{AV}}$ (or equivalently peak velocity LVOT / peak velocity AV).

DVI is independent of LVOT area. For prosthetic aortic valves, a DVI less than approximately $0.25$–$0.35$ suggests significant obstruction; commonly used cutoffs are DVI < $0.25$ for severe stenosis and DVI between $0.25$ and $0.35$ for moderate disease, though prosthesis-specific norms should be consulted. DVI interpretation should be correlated with gradients and clinical picture.

When interpreting EOA and iEOA in prostheses, remember that normal EOA values differ by prosthesis type (supra-annular vs intra-annular) and size. Reference tables provided by manufacturers and published studies supply expected EOAs for model/size combinations. A substantially smaller measured EOA than expected for that prosthesis suggests either PPM (if valve leaflets are functioning normally), obstruction from thrombosis/pannus (if decreased leaflet mobility is seen), or measurement error.

# Criteria for patient–prosthesis mismatch and clinical significance

Patient–prosthesis mismatch (PPM) occurs when the prosthetic valve’s effective orifice area is too small relative to the patient’s body size, producing higher-than-expected transvalvular gradients despite normal prosthetic leaflet function. PPM is a hemodynamic diagnosis, distinct from structural obstruction. The widely accepted classification for aortic valve prostheses defines PPM using indexed EOA:

- No PPM: iEOA > $0.85\ \mathrm{cm}^2/\mathrm{m}^2$
- Moderate PPM: iEOA $0.65$–$0.85\ \mathrm{cm}^2/\mathrm{m}^2$
- Severe PPM: iEOA < $0.65\ \mathrm{cm}^2/\mathrm{m}^2$

For obese patients (BSA-derived indices may be misleading), adjusted thresholds are often applied; many experts suggest higher cutoffs (for example, defining severe PPM as iEOA < $0.6$–$0.7$ in patients with elevated BMI), and clinical judgment is required. Valve-specific expected EOAs must be referenced because supra-annular TAVI valves typically have larger EOAs for a given labeled size compared with intra-annular surgical valves.

The clinical significance of PPM is multifaceted. Moderate or severe PPM is associated with persistent elevation of transvalvular gradients, less regression of left ventricular hypertrophy, reduced improvement in symptoms and exercise capacity, and in some series worse long-term survival, particularly after surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR). After TAVI, the incidence and impact of PPM vary with valve type and implantation strategy; ViV procedures are particularly prone to PPM because the internal diameter of the failed surgical valve limits the new prosthesis’ EOA. Recognition of PPM has implications for procedural planning (e.g., choosing a valve with larger EOA, performing annular enlargement during SAVR, or considering bioprosthesis fracture techniques in ViV) and postoperative follow-up.

Clinically, distinguishing PPM from true obstruction is critical: PPM is a fixed mismatch in which valve leaflets open normally and no structural deterioration is present, whereas obstruction due to thrombosis, pannus, or structural degeneration will show abnormal leaflet motion and often a change from prior measurements. If elevated gradients are present but DVI and Doppler profiles suggest normal leaflet opening and imaging demonstrates normal mobility, calculate iEOA to evaluate for PPM. If iEOA indicates PPM, management is usually conservative and directed at symptoms, exercise tolerance, and consideration of re-intervention in severe, symptomatic cases.

# Echocardiographic surveillance schedule and key endpoints

Systematic surveillance after TAVI/ViV is necessary to detect early complications and to monitor long-term valve performance. A pragmatic schedule used in many centers and recommended by consensus documents includes a baseline transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) prior to discharge or within 30 days post-procedure, a follow-up at 30 days to 3 months if not already done, another at 1 year, and annually thereafter. Additional echocardiographic assessment is warranted whenever symptoms arise (dyspnea, heart failure signs, new murmur) or if there is a change in clinical status suggesting valve dysfunction.

Key echocardiographic endpoints to document at each visit include valve morphology (leaflet mobility, thickening or prolapse), Doppler hemodynamics (peak velocity, mean gradient), EOA and iEOA (or DVI), severity and mechanism of regurgitation (central vs paravalvular), left ventricular size and systolic function (including ejection fraction and diastolic parameters), right ventricular function and pulmonary pressures, and presence or progression of other valvular lesions. Record baseline values within the first 30 days to establish a reference; future changes are interpreted relative to this baseline.

Specific thresholds and trigger points for further investigation or intervention include a new mean gradient increase of more than $10$ mmHg from baseline, an absolute mean gradient above approximately $20$ mmHg for bioprosthetic valves (especially for valves implanted to treat aortic stenosis), or a DVI falling below expected values for the prosthesis. New or worsening regurgitation graded at least moderate (≥ grade 2–3/4) should prompt detailed evaluation for PVL or leaflet dysfunction. New reductions in leaflet excursion or evidence of thrombus on imaging, or detection of hypoattenuated leaflet thickening on CT in symptomatic patients or with gradient changes, should trigger consideration of anticoagulation or surgical/endovascular therapy. Serial progression of mean gradient or decreasing EOA over successive studies is suggestive of structural valve deterioration (SVD).

It is important to integrate clinical findings and laboratory data (e.g., biomarkers of hemolysis or inflammation) with imaging. Hemolysis may indicate significant regurgitation or PVL; elevated inflammatory markers are nonspecific but infections raise concern for endocarditis. Accurate documentation and standardized reporting (including regimen of image acquisition, measurement technique, and reference values) allow meaningful longitudinal comparison.

# Differentiating structural valve deterioration, thrombosis and paravalvular leak on imaging

Discerning the cause of prosthetic valve dysfunction requires synthesis of hemodynamics and imaging morphology. Structural valve deterioration (SVD), thrombosis, pannus formation, and paravalvular leaks each have characteristic features, although overlap occurs and multimodality imaging may be required.

Structural valve deterioration is a progressive, intrinsic change in the valve leaflets or supporting structure, most commonly in bioprosthetic valves. SVD manifests as leaflet calcification, fibrosis, tear, or disruption leading to stenosis, regurgitation, or a combination. Echocardiographic signs include increased leaflet echogenicity and thickness, restricted leaflet excursion on 2D imaging, increased transvalvular velocities and gradients, and reduced EOA. When regurgitation predominates, eccentric regurgitant jets or central regurgitation with abnormal coaptation can be seen. SVD typically evolves over months to years. In serial studies, SVD is suggested by a steady progression of gradients and worsening leaflet mobility over several follow-up exams.

Thrombosis of prosthetic leaflets can be subclinical or present with sudden hemodynamic changes. On echocardiography, thrombosis may appear as new leaflet thickening and reduced excursion, often more accentuated during systole than SVD depending on thrombus burden. Doppler shows an acute or subacute increase in transvalvular gradients. On CT, the hallmark is hypoattenuated leaflet thickening (HALT) and associated reduced leaflet motion (RELM); these findings are highly suggestive of thrombus when found in appropriate clinical context. Thrombus is often responsive to anticoagulation, so recognition is important: an increase in mean gradient of $>10$ mmHg with imaging evidence of reduced leaflet motion and HALT on CT favors thrombosis.

Pannus is organized fibrous ingrowth typically occurring on the sewing ring or around the valve frame and develops over months to years. Pannus produces fixed obstruction and may be difficult to distinguish from SVD on echocardiography. Pannus is usually denser and less mobile than thrombus and may have different CT attenuation characteristics; however, differentiation requires CT and occasionally fluoroscopic or intraoperative inspection.

Paravalvular leak (PVL) results from incomplete apposition of the prosthesis to the annulus or from annular/valve tissue defects. Echocardiographically, PVL is characterized by one or more eccentric jets originating outside the sewing ring rather than through the central flow of the valve. Color Doppler on TTE may underestimate PVL severity; TEE, particularly 3D TEE, improves localization and quantification. Key features include crescentic or focal jets adjacent to the valve frame, multiple jets around the circumference, and systolic/diastolic flow patterns depending on valve position and hemodynamics. Hemolysis and heart failure disproportionate to central regurgitation are clinical clues that suggest significant PVL. Quantification uses vena contracta width, regurgitant volume, and regurgitant fraction when feasible; regurgitant fraction >30%–40% typically indicates at least moderate-severe regurgitation.

Distinguishing features in summary: SVD is progressive structural change with calcification and restricted mobility developing over years; thrombosis tends to be subacute with HALT/RELM on CT and often reversible with anticoagulation; PVL is extracentral regurgitation with eccentric color Doppler jets and annular dehiscence or malapposition. Careful comparison with prior imaging is one of the most powerful tools for differentiation.

# Role of CT and TEE in ambiguous cases

When transthoracic echocardiography is inconclusive or when the mechanism of prosthetic dysfunction is uncertain, transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) and multi-detector computed tomography (CT) are the primary complementary modalities.

Transesophageal echocardiography, particularly 3D TEE, offers superior spatial resolution for valvular structures compared with TTE and is excellent for evaluating PVL. TEE allows precise localization of PVL jets relative to the sewing ring, characterization of the size and shape of defects, assessment of perivalvular abscesses or vegetations, and evaluation of leaflet motion. Intraoperative or procedural TEE is indispensable for guiding percutaneous PVL closure and for assessing valve seating and immediate complications. Limitations include semi-invasive nature, need for sedation or anesthesia in some cases, and artifact from prosthetic components, which may obscure visualization.

Cardiac CT provides excellent spatial resolution and is especially sensitive for leaflet morphology, stent-frame apposition, and tissue characterization. CT is the best modality to detect hypoattenuated leaflet thickening (HALT) and to assess reduced leaflet motion (quantitatively defined as RELM). CT can identify thrombus versus pannus based on attenuation values and enhancement patterns; thrombus commonly appears as low-attenuation material (<90–100 Hounsfield units) whereas pannus is denser and may have higher attenuation. CT also visualizes the prosthesis relative to the annulus and adjacent structures, detects prosthesis malapposition, and quantifies the circumferential extent of PVL by assessing gaps between prosthetic frame and annular tissue. CT is indispensable in TAVI planning and in evaluating suspected leaflet thrombosis, particularly when gradients rise or symptoms occur without clear TTE/TEE correlates.

The combined use of TEE and CT is often necessary. For example, when Doppler indicates increased gradients and TTE reveals reduced leaflet motion but cannot define the cause, CT demonstrating HALT strongly supports thrombosis and may prompt therapeutic anticoagulation. Conversely, a patient with significant regurgitation by TTE should undergo TEE to map PVL and CT to assess frame apposition and annular anatomy if percutaneous closure is contemplated.

In practice, the imaging pathway depends on the clinical scenario. For suspected thrombosis with hemodynamic compromise, CT (for HALT/RELM) plus TEE may be performed urgently. For suspected endocarditis or annular abscess, TEE is the first test. For pre-procedural planning of PVL closure, TEE and CT together provide complementary data on defect morphology, size, and spatial relationships.

Conclusion

Accurate hemodynamic assessment and structured echocardiographic surveillance after TAVI/ViV are essential to differentiate normal postoperative findings from PPM, thrombosis, SVD, or PVL. Doppler gradients, EOA, iEOA and DVI are central quantitative tools; their interpretation requires awareness of prosthesis-specific normal values and adjustment for flow state. PPM is a hemodynamic mismatch that must be distinguished from structural causes of obstruction, because management differs. A standardized imaging follow-up schedule with attention to changes from baseline permits early detection of clinically significant deterioration. When transthoracic echocardiography is ambiguous, TEE and CT provide complementary morphological and tissue-specific information indispensable for diagnosis and therapeutic planning. Integrating serial imaging with clinical and laboratory data allows rational, patient-centered decisions about medical therapy, anticoagulation, percutaneous interventions, or re-intervention.
