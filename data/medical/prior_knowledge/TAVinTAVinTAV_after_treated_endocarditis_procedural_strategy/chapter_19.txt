Introduction

Management of complex valvular heart disease increasingly relies on combined technical expertise, nuanced risk–benefit appraisal, and patient-centered decision-making. The advent of transcatheter therapies, valve-in-valve and valve-in-ring procedures, and novel devices has expanded options for patients who previously were considered inoperable or high risk. However, these advances also introduce new ethical, legal, and clinical challenges. Clinicians must integrate an understanding of pathophysiology, procedural risk, long-term outcomes, and patient goals into decisions that frequently occur under uncertainty. This chapter reviews how multidisciplinary heart teams are structured and operate, provides frameworks for weighing risks and benefits in high-risk reinterventions, outlines best practices for documentation and informed consent—particularly when devices are used off-label—discusses palliative approaches and goals-of-care conversations in structural heart disease, and details the regulatory and institutional governance that should guide novel or complex procedures.

# Structure and roles of the heart team in valvular disease management

The heart team is a formalized, multidisciplinary group established to synthesize expertise from different specialties and to provide a balanced assessment of therapeutic options for patients with valvular disease. The team’s composition typically includes interventional cardiologists with structural expertise, cardiothoracic surgeons, cardiac imaging specialists (echocardiography, CT, MRI), anesthesiologists (often cardiac anesthesiologists), heart failure specialists or general cardiologists, geriatricians or frailty experts when appropriate, and allied professionals such as nurse coordinators, clinical pharmacists, and sometimes ethicists or palliative care clinicians. Each member contributes a particular lens: surgeons assess operative candidacy and technical feasibility; interventionalists bring knowledge of device options and catheter-based approaches; imagers provide anatomic and functional data essential for procedural planning; anesthesiologists evaluate periprocedural risk and airway/cardiopulmonary management; heart failure specialists consider medical optimization and long-term prognosis; and geriatricians/frailty experts offer insights into physiologic reserves and vulnerability to complications.

Operationally, the heart team functions through regular meetings (virtual or in-person) where cases are presented in a structured fashion. A comprehensive presentation includes baseline clinical status, comorbidities, frailty and functional status, objective imaging (e.g., echocardiography with quantitative measures, CT annular sizing or CT angiography for access evaluation), prior surgical history, and patient preferences. The group evaluates: diagnosis and severity confirmation; candidacy for medical therapy alone versus intervention; choice among surgical, transcatheter, or hybrid approaches; timing of intervention; and postprocedural care plans. Decision-making should be evidence-informed and transparent, with documentation of dissenting opinions and the rationale for a given recommendation.

Communication with the patient and family is a critical role of the heart team. A designated clinician—often the structural interventionalist, surgeon, or heart failure physician—should lead discussion with the patient, presenting the team’s consensus recommendation, explicating alternatives, and ensuring comprehension. The team must assess the patient’s capacity and enlist surrogate decision-makers when appropriate. Importantly, the team should be attentive to the possibility of bias: enthusiasm for novel interventions should be tempered by objective appraisal of outcomes data, and conflicts of interest (financial or academic) must be declared and managed. The heart team model aims to improve consistency, reduce individual bias, and align care with the best available evidence and patient values.

# Frameworks for weighing risks and benefits in high-risk reinterventions

High-risk reinterventions in valvular disease—such as redo surgical valve replacement, transcatheter valve-in-valve procedures, or off-label device placements—require a structured framework to weigh potential benefits against procedural and long-term risks. This framework integrates probabilistic risk assessment tools, frailty and geriatric assessments, disease-specific prognostic metrics, and careful consideration of patient-centric outcomes such as symptom relief, functional status, and quality of life.

Risk stratification begins with validated surgical mortality models. Commonly used scores for cardiac surgery include the Society of Thoracic Surgeons Predicted Risk of Mortality (STS-PROM) and the EuroSCORE II. These scores quantify perioperative mortality risk and can guide comparison between surgical and transcatheter approaches, but they have limitations—particularly in reoperative and highly complex anatomies where the models may under- or overestimate risk. For transcatheter procedures, procedural risk models are less well standardized; institutional and operator experience significantly affects outcomes. Therefore, risk estimation must be individualized, integrating quantitative scores with qualitative clinical judgment.

Frailty is a crucial determinant of outcome beyond chronological age. Measures such as gait speed, the Fried phenotype, the Short Physical Performance Battery, or cognitive assessment tools (e.g., Mini-Cog) predict morbidity, prolonged hospitalization, and mortality after valvular interventions. For example, a gait speed slower than 0.8 m/s or inability to rise from a chair without using upper extremity support predicts poorer outcomes. Incorporating frailty into decision-making may shift recommendations from aggressive reintervention toward less invasive options or palliative approaches when the likelihood of meaningful recovery is low.

Disease-specific criteria refine decision-making. For aortic stenosis, objective thresholds such as valve area <1.0 cm^2, mean gradient >40 mmHg, or peak velocity >4 m/s define severe disease in symptomatic patients; biochemical markers like elevated B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) suggest hemodynamic stress and worse prognosis. For mitral valve disease, quantitative echocardiographic parameters—effective regurgitant orifice area, regurgitant volume, and pulmonary pressures—help to gauge the severity and consequence of regurgitation. For prosthetic valve dysfunction, imaging (transesophageal echocardiography, fluoroscopy, CT) can establish the mechanism—thrombosis, pannus, structural degeneration, or endocarditis—and this mechanistic understanding directly informs treatment risk and expected benefit.

Benefit analysis should address both short-term endpoints (survival, procedural success, complication avoidance) and patient-centered outcomes (symptom relief, functional improvement, reduced rehospitalizations, and quality of life). Decision thresholds are inherently individual: a 30% procedural mortality risk may be acceptable to a patient facing progressive NYHA class IV symptoms with poor life expectancy without intervention, while the same risk may be unacceptable to a patient prioritized for independence and minimal procedural burden. Explicitly articulating the magnitude and uncertainty of benefits and harms, and linking them to the patient’s goals, is essential.

When evidence is limited—common in reinterventions or novel techniques—the heart team should apply decision analysis principles: estimate probabilities of outcomes based on best available data (published series, registries, institutional outcomes), consider the quality-adjusted life expectancy implications, and discuss the range of plausible outcomes with the patient. Sensitivity analyses in one’s mind (e.g., “if the chance of major complication is 10% vs 30%, would you still choose this?”) help reveal patient preferences under uncertainty. Finally, the team should ensure realistic plans for contingency management—how complications would be handled, escalation pathways, and limits on escalation consistent with the patient’s wishes.

# Documentation and informed consent for off-label device use

Informed consent for any interventional procedure must include disclosure of diagnosis, recommended intervention, nature and purpose of the proposed procedure, reasonable alternatives (including nonintervention), the material risks and benefits, and an assessment of patient understanding and voluntary agreement. When devices are used outside their labeled indications (off-label use), additional documentation and discussion are ethically and legally necessary because the risk–benefit profile for that specific indication is often less established.

Key elements to address in the consent conversation for off-label device use include: the fact that the device is being used in a manner not approved by the regulatory authority for that specific indication; the rationale for using it given the patient’s individual anatomy and clinical circumstances; the evidence base supporting this use (e.g., series, registries, prior case reports), including the strength and limitations of that evidence; alternative, approved therapeutic options and their risks and benefits; and foreseeable additional risks stemming from the off-label use. The clinician should explain potential unknowns, such as durability, device–host interactions in the off-label setting, and the lack of long-term data. If data are limited, it is important to quantify uncertainty and to state what outcomes will be tracked post-procedure.

Documentation must go beyond a perfunctory signature. The informed consent form should explicitly state the off-label nature of the device use and the reasons it is considered appropriate for this patient. The narrative note in the medical record should summarize the discussion, the alternatives presented, the patient’s questions and responses, the patient’s stated goals and values, the patient’s consent (or refusal), and the names of team members present. If a surrogate decision-maker provides consent, the note should record the legal basis for surrogate authority and the assessment of the patient’s capacity that necessitated surrogate involvement.

When off-label use involves substantial uncertainty or increased risk, institutional oversight may require additional steps: review and approval by a device or procedural oversight committee, involvement of the hospital ethics team, or formal documentation of a heart team recommendation endorsed by multiple members. This oversight guards against inappropriate experimentation and helps ensure that the use is driven by patient benefit rather than novelty or financial incentives.

Consent should address logistical issues unique to complex interventions: potential need for conversion from transcatheter to open surgery, the likelihood of prolonged intensive care or rehabilitation, need for blood transfusion, and the plan if the device fails. Patients should be informed about postprocedural monitoring, imaging follow-up, and any requirements for antithrombotic therapy. If the off-label use will contribute data to a registry, quality-improvement program, or research study, this should be disclosed; research consent requirements are distinct from therapeutic consent and may necessitate separate informed consent processes approved by an institutional review board (IRB).

Finally, informed consent is a process, not a single event. Revisit consent with new information, changes in condition, or if there is a delay between consent and procedure. Ensure that translators or cultural mediators are used when language or cultural differences might impair understanding. Capacity assessment should be documented using standardized phrasing when there is any doubt about the patient’s ability to decide.

# Palliative considerations and patient goals of care in structural heart disease

Structural heart disease often affects older individuals with multiple comorbidities and frailty. Interventions can improve survival and symptoms, but may also prolong suffering or reduce quality of life if not aligned with patient goals. Incorporating palliative care principles early—coordinated with interventional and surgical options—ensures that decisions reflect what matters most to the patient.

Palliative care in the context of valvular disease emphasizes symptom management (dyspnea, pain, fatigue), psychosocial support, clarification of goals, advance care planning, and care coordination. It is not synonymous with choosing no intervention; rather, it complements disease-directed care by aligning treatment choices with patient values. For example, if a patient values maximal longevity and is willing to accept substantial perioperative risk for potential life-extension, an aggressive reintervention may be appropriate. Conversely, if a patient prioritizes maintaining independence and avoiding prolonged hospitalization, less invasive or noninterventional approaches—or a focus on symptom-directed management—may better reflect their goals.

Goals-of-care conversations should be structured and recurrent. They begin with exploring the patient’s understanding of their illness and prognosis, eliciting values and priorities, and framing choices in terms of likely outcomes that matter to the patient (survival with significant functional dependence, symptom relief without survival benefit, risk of prolonged rehabilitation). Clinicians should provide realistic estimates of prognosis both with and without intervention, using evidence when available and acknowledging uncertainty. Documentation of these discussions, including the patient’s stated preferences and any advance directives or durable power of attorney for health care, is essential to guide care should the patient lose decision-making capacity.

When procedural risk is high and chances of meaningful recovery are uncertain, time-limited trials of intervention can be considered: an intervention is pursued with predefined goals and a prearranged plan for reassessment and limitation if goals are not met. For instance, a time-limited trial might state that the team will pursue the intervention but will not continue aggressive life-sustaining therapies if the patient remains ventilator-dependent with no neurologic recovery after a specified period. Such agreements should be documented and communicated to all treating teams and the patient’s surrogates.

Integration of palliative care specialists into the heart team improves communication, symptom management, and decision-making. They can assist with complex discussions when there is discordance between patient/family expectations and clinical prognosis. Importantly, palliative care involvement reduces the likelihood that patients undergo burdensome interventions unlikely to achieve their stated goals.

# Regulatory and institutional governance for novel or complex procedures

Procedures that are novel, technically complex, or involve off-label device use require oversight beyond individual clinician judgment. Regulatory frameworks and institutional governance mechanisms exist to protect patient safety, ensure ethical standards, and maintain public trust. Clinicians must be familiar with national regulatory pathways as well as their institution’s policies.

At the national level, device use is regulated by agencies such as the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA). Approved indications reflect the evidence reviewed at the time of clearance or approval. Use outside those indications is permitted under “practice of medicine” but becomes ethically fraught when it departs substantially from the evidence base or when the use is effectively investigational. When a procedure is genuinely investigational (intended to generate generalizable knowledge about safety and efficacy), it typically requires an investigational device exemption (IDE) or research protocol approval by an IRB, and informed consent that complies with research regulations.

There are regulatory pathways that address access to investigational devices outside premarket approval, including compassionate use (expanded access) and emergency use provisions. These require specific applications and documentation, and often mandate data submission and monitoring. Device manufacturers may have responsibility under national regulations for reporting adverse events and for monitoring outcomes in expanded access programs.

Institutional governance typically involves committees such as a device oversight committee, procedural quality and safety committee, or a multidisciplinary peer-review panel (often termed a “valve review board” or “structural procedures committee”). These bodies review proposed novel practices, evaluate operator and team competency, set proctoring requirements, define credentialing criteria, and ensure that programs collect outcomes data for quality assurance. Approval of a new program or a departure from standard practice often requires evidence of the team’s training, procedural volume, and access to contingency resources (e.g., cardiac surgery backup).

Credentialing and privileging are central institutional mechanisms. Hospitals set criteria for granting privileges to perform specific structural heart procedures, often requiring documentation of training, case logs, proctorship, and outcomes monitoring. For complex procedures, an initial period of proctoring by an experienced operator may be required. Institutions should also define minimum volume thresholds to ensure maintenance of competency, recognizing that volume–outcome relationships exist for many complex cardiac procedures.

Quality governance requires systematic outcome measurement. Participation in national registries (e.g., transcatheter valve registries) allows benchmarking against broader datasets and supports surveillance for rare complications. Institutional morbidity and mortality review processes should include structured review of adverse events and near misses, with systems-based recommendations for improvement. Transparency with patients and families in the event of complications is ethically mandated—open disclosure policies should be in place.

Finally, conflict-of-interest policies must govern relationships between clinicians and device manufacturers. Financial relationships, research sponsorship, and speaking or advisory roles can introduce bias. Institutions should require disclosure of such relationships and manage them through recusal from decision-making when appropriate, independent review of proposed uses, and transparent reporting to patients when a clinician’s relationship could influence recommendations.

Conclusion

Complex valvular interventions require more than technical skill; they demand integrated multidisciplinary assessment, careful risk–benefit analysis, rigorous informed consent—especially when devices are used off-label—and alignment with the patient’s goals of care. Institutional and regulatory frameworks serve to ensure patient safety and ethical practice as technologies evolve. Effective heart team functioning, transparent documentation, and early incorporation of palliative principles promote decisions that are clinically sound and ethically defensible.
