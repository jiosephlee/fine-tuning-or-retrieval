Introduction

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) has evolved from an alternative for inoperable patients to a mainstream therapy for severe aortic stenosis across risk spectra. Safe, reproducible results hinge not only on device design but on precise procedural technique: selecting and obtaining vascular access, choosing appropriate sheaths and wires, managing rapid ventricular pacing, deciding when and how to perform balloon valvuloplasty, and deploying the chosen prosthesis with attention to depth and commissural orientation. This chapter synthesizes the procedural principles and tactics that underpin successful TAVI, linking anatomy and device mechanics to intra-procedural decision-making and complication avoidance. It is written for trainees who must understand the why and how of routinely encountered maneuvers so they can apply them adaptively in the cath lab.

# Vascular access options: transfemoral, transradial, alternative routes

Transfemoral access is the default access route for contemporary TAVI and is preferred whenever iliofemoral anatomy permits safe passage of the required sheath and delivery system. The transfemoral route benefits from a relatively straightforward trajectory to the aortic valve, ease of percutaneous closure, and minimal invasiveness compared with surgical approaches. Pre-procedural contrast-enhanced CT angiography is essential to evaluate vessel diameter, tortuosity, calcification, and the presence of iliofemoral disease or prior stents. A minimal luminal diameter requirement depends on the device and sheath; modern low-profile systems often require sheath outer diameters in the range of 14–16 Fr, though some devices and sheathless techniques reduce this requirement. Heavy circumferential calcification, a severe arterial tortuosity with a sharp bend (>60–90 degrees), or a vessel diameter smaller than the required sheath diameter are common reasons to consider alternative access.

Transradial access is used in TAVI centers primarily for ancillary arterial access — for example, contralateral femoral angiography or as the access site for a pigtail catheter in the aortic root. Transradial use for the main TAVI delivery is not standard because of vessel size limitations; a reach into the root for secondary angiography or a second arterial access site is however commonly performed via the radial approach. Radial access decreases the risk of access-site bleeding for adjunctive catheters and facilitates earlier mobilization.

Alternative non-transfemoral routes exist when femoral access is unfeasible. These include transaxillary/subclavian, transcarotid, transcaval, direct transaortic, and transapical approaches. Transaxillary/subclavian access uses a more favorable straight-line trajectory to the aortic valve and can be achieved percutaneously or via surgical cut-down. Vessels should be assessed for diameter (>6–7 mm desirable), calcification, and the presence of atherosclerotic plaque that could embolize. Transcarotid access is an option particularly when axillary anatomy is unsuitable; it requires careful neurologic assessment and often a surgical cut-down with cerebral protection strategy and neuro-monitoring. Transcaval access is a specialized technique in which the iliac vein is intentionally crossed into the adjacent abdominal aorta and a closure device is used to seal the aorto-caval tract at the end of the case. It is a valuable salvage or planned strategy when arterial access options are exhausted, but requires specific experience and appropriate CT anatomy (favorable aorto-caval apposition and absence of interposed tissue or large retroperitoneal structures). Transaortic and transapical access are more invasive surgical approaches, typically reserved for when no percutaneous option is viable; they require sternotomy or mini-thoracotomy and are used less frequently in contemporary practice.

Choosing the access route requires integration of CT angiography, consideration of comorbid conditions (vascular disease, prior CABG with internal mammary grafts), and the operator’s institutional experience. For any chosen route, detailed knowledge of complication profiles—vascular injury, bleeding, stroke risk (especially with carotid), and local infections—is essential to informed consent and procedural planning.

# Sheath selection and percutaneous closure devices

Sheath selection is determined by the device profile, the size and compliance of the access vessel, and whether the operator will use a dedicated introducer or a sheathless approach. Sheath outer diameter, rather than the nominal French size, defines the minimum arterial lumen required. Balloon-expandable devices traditionally required larger sheaths than newer generation devices; for example, older Sapien delivery systems required 18–24 Fr sheaths, whereas Sapien 3 Ultra transcatheter heart valves now commonly use 14–16 Fr expandable sheaths. Self-expanding devices such as Medtronic Evolut use an introducer system with an integrated sheath and may be deployed through 14–20 Fr equivalent systems depending on the valve size and whether a sheathless technique is used. Operators must also consider sheath flexibility and trackability through tortuous vessels and the presence of hydrophilic coatings to reduce friction and vascular trauma.

Percutaneous closure is standard for transfemoral access in most centers. Suture-mediated devices (e.g., Perclose ProGlide) are widely used. The “pre-close” technique involves placing two or more sutures before upsizing to the large-bore sheath; upon sheath removal these sutures are tightened to achieve hemostasis. Proper suture orientation, typically at the 10 o’clock and 2 o’clock positions when accessing the right common femoral artery (or mirrored for left), reduces the risk of residual bleeding. Collagen-based closures such as MANTA provide a single-device solution that deploys a biocompatible plug to seal the arterial puncture and are attractive for rapid hemostasis with immediate device removal. The MANTA device is available in different sizes for particular puncture diameters and is less dependent on operator suture technique but requires accurate measurement of the puncture depth and can leave an intraluminal or extraluminal component that may be associated with hematoma or pseudoaneurysm. Surgical cut-down remains a bail-out or primary strategy when percutaneous closure is contraindicated or artery quality is poor.

Common complications associated with percutaneous closure include bleeding, hematoma, pseudoaneurysm, arterial occlusion or dissection, and failure requiring conversion to open surgical repair. Failure rates for percutaneous closure vary with operator experience and patient factors; an experienced team using pre-close with Perclose devices typically achieves primary hemostasis rates >90%, but repeated attempts or heavy calcification increase failure risk. Meticulous ultrasound-guided puncture of the common femoral artery at the optimal anterior wall location (below the inguinal ligament and above the bifurcation) reduces complication rates. Intra-procedural imaging with fluoroscopic guidance using the femoral head as a landmark, and sometimes roadmapping with contrast, supports accurate arterial entry.

# Use of rapid ventricular pacing and its indications

Rapid ventricular pacing (RVP) transiently reduces cardiac output and systemic blood pressure by inducing ventricular tachycardia at high rates (commonly 160–220 beats per minute), thereby reducing cardiac motion and transvalvular flow. RVP is used principally to facilitate predictable balloon inflation during balloon aortic valvuloplasty and secure positioning and expansion of balloon-expandable transcatheter valves. By lowering ejection and stroke volume, RVP minimizes balloon displacement and reduces the force of blood flow against the balloon or valve during expansion, enabling controlled deployment.

RVP can be delivered via a temporary pacing lead positioned in the right ventricle through a venous access (typically femoral or jugular), or less commonly by rapid pacing through an LV support guidewire (if that wire can transmit sufficient current). The temporary transvenous RV lead remains the most reliable method, allowing stable capture and adjustable pacing output. Typical pacing rates range between 160 and 220 bpm; operators usually titrate the rate to achieve near–complete loss of arterial pressure pulsatility (systolic blood pressure typically falls below 50 mmHg), sufficient to immobilize the balloon/valve but minimize duration of profound hypotension. The typical RVP durations are brief—commonly 10–15 seconds per inflation—but multiple or prolonged episodes increase the risk of myocardial ischemia, arrhythmia, or hemodynamic collapse, especially in patients with limited left ventricular reserve.

Indications for RVP in TAVI include pre-dilation balloon aortic valvuloplasty to fracture calcific leaflets and facilitate valve passage, balloon inflation for balloon-expandable valve deployment, and post-dilation when further expansion is required to correct device under-expansion or paravalvular regurgitation. Some self-expanding valve deployments avoid RVP because self-expanding frames foreshorten and expand slowly; nonetheless, RVP can be used during critical phases such as rapid release of a recapturable frame in some cases or when controlled hemodynamics are desired. Contraindications to RVP include patients with unstable hemodynamics who cannot tolerate any drop in blood pressure, though in many such patients careful planning and short-duration RVP may still be possible with inotropic support and temporary pacing in place.

Complications of RVP include sustained ventricular arrhythmias, inability to achieve capture (sometimes due to poor lead contact), puncture-related venous complications, and ischemic myocardial injury if prolonged hypotension occurs. Operators should ensure immediate availability of defibrillation, consider backup ventricular pacing configurations, and limit the number and duration of RVP episodes. Hemodynamic monitoring of arterial pressure during pacing assures adequacy of suppression of pulsatility and guides timing of balloon inflation or valve release.

# Balloon valvuloplasty (pre- and post-dilation): indications and sizing

Balloon aortic valvuloplasty (BAV) has evolved from a stand-alone palliation to a strategic adjunct of TAVI. Two contexts are important: pre-dilation (balloon inflation before valve delivery) and post-dilation (balloon inflation after valve deployment).

Pre-dilation is performed to increase leaflet mobility, fracture or mobilize bulky calcific nodules, and facilitate passage and expansion of the transcatheter valve. Indications for routine pre-dilation have declined with improvements in delivery systems, but pre-dilation remains valuable in specific scenarios: severely calcified native valves, very tight stenosis with small valve area that may impede device crossing, heavily asymmetric calcification or bulky leaflet nodularity (e.g., in bicuspid valves), and when operator anticipates difficulty crossing or expanding the device. Pre-dilation can also serve as a hemodynamic test to assess the patient’s tolerance of temporary afterload reduction.

Post-dilation is used when the implanted valve exhibits under-expansion, high transvalvular gradients, or significant paravalvular regurgitation. Post-dilation can improve leaflet coaptation and reduce paravalvular leak by expanding the frame against the annulus and remodeling any incomplete apposition. However, post-dilation carries increased risk of annular injury or rupture, coronary obstruction (rare), cerebrovascular embolization, and device migration if the balloon forces shift the valve. The decision to post-dilate should weigh the severity of residual regurgitation or gradient against these risks.

Balloon sizing is based on accurate annular measurements obtained from ECG-gated CT. The procedural principle is that the balloon diameter should approximate the annular dimension but be selected conservatively relative to the prosthesis and annular calcification. For pre-dilation, operators often choose a balloon equal to or slightly smaller than the annulus diameter (e.g., 80–100% of the annular mean diameter) to fracture commissural calcification without excessive radial force. For example, with an annulus mean diameter of 23 mm, a pre-dilation balloon of 20–23 mm may be used. For post-dilation, balloon sizing often mirrors prosthesis size recommendations: operators may use the same nominal diameter as the target valve size or modestly oversize (but generally avoid aggressive oversizing) to optimize frame expansion. Precise recommendations vary by device; manufacturers’ instructions and institutional experience inform choices.

Careful preparation for valvuloplasty includes rapid pacing set-up, hemodynamic monitoring, and readiness to manage complications such as severe aortic regurgitation, ventricular arrhythmias, or annular rupture. Balloon inflation should be of short duration (typically 5–10 seconds), performed under rapid pacing as indicated, and with careful fluoroscopic and hemodynamic observation. Adjunctive cerebral protection strategies may be considered in patients at particularly high risk of embolic stroke from heavy calcific burden.

# Guidewire selection and positioning (eg, LV support wires)

Guidewire choice and positioning are critical to provide stable support for device delivery and to minimize the risk of ventricular injury. Guidewires used during TAVI differ by stiffness and tip shape. Commonly used LV support wires include the Safari, Safari2, Amplatz Super Stiff, Confida, and others specifically designed with a flexible curved tip and a stiff shaft. LV-support wires typically have a pre-shaped atraumatic curve at the tip to rest against the LV apex, providing coaxial alignment and stability during advancement and deployment of large devices.

The selection principle is to balance support and safety. A stiff shaft confers support for tracking the large-bore delivery system while a soft or pre-shaped atraumatic tip minimizes the risk of LV perforation. For example, the Safari wire has a nitinol curved atraumatic tip and a variable stiffness body to provide central support with less apex trauma. Amplatz Super Stiff wires have more stiffness and risk of perforation and are therefore used cautiously or with a floppy tip configuration. Some operators prefer placing an LV guidewire with a J-tip in the aortic valve cusp and positioning an extra-stiff shaft in the mid-LV cavity, while keeping a long soft polymer tip to reduce endocardial trauma.

Positioning the wire in the mid-to-apical LV cavity provides the best balance of support, but the wire should not abut the lateral LV wall or papillary muscles to avoid perforation. Continuous fluoroscopic guidance in orthogonal views helps confirm that the wire tip is in an acceptable midline apical location with gentle curves rather than a sharp, penetrating tip. When using an LV support wire as a rapid pacing conductor, operators must ensure the wire can reliably conduct pacing energy; otherwise, a separate RV pacing lead should be used.

Wire-related complications include LV perforation with tamponade, wire entrapment, and embolization. Careful manipulation, avoidance of forceful advancement, and using wires with appropriate tip softness reduce these risks. If using a stiff wire to support a self-expanding valve, consider using an extra-stiff wire only after careful crossing of the valve and confirmation of tip position, and ensure there is an immediate plan for pericardial drainage should perforation occur.

# Deployment techniques for self-expanding vs balloon-expandable valves

Deployment strategies differ between valve platforms because of differences in frame mechanics, foreshortening characteristics, and ability to recapture or reposition.

Balloon-expandable valves (e.g., Edwards Sapien family) are mounted on a balloon and require rapid inflation to expand the frame against the annulus. Deployment steps include precise positioning with fluoroscopic landmarks (cusp overlap and annular landing zone), securing rapid pacing to reduce ejection, and inflating the balloon to nominal volume for the selected valve size. Over- or underfilling the balloon can respectively oversize or underexpand the valve. Operators must measure the annulus via CT and choose the valve size accordingly; common implantation depths aim for 3–5 mm ventricular implantation for optimal conduction and paravalvular leak profile, but depth varies with anatomy and surgical preferences. Because balloon-expandable valves deploy rapidly and are not recapturable once fully expanded, accurate initial positioning is critical. Strategies to improve placement accuracy include using the cusp-overlap fluoroscopic view (to minimize parallax and better visualize the non-coronary cusp) and slow, controlled inflation during complete loss of pulsatility.

Self-expanding valves (e.g., Medtronic Evolut family) use a nitinol frame that expands gradually and often can be partially deployed, repositioned, or recaptured depending on the generation. Deployment typically begins with initial release of the frame to a point where the inflow portion is anchored, and then slow continued release allowing the valve to assume its final geometry. Implantation depth is targeted based on CT measurements and membranous septum height; for self-expanding valves, a slightly higher (aortic) implantation depth may reduce conduction disturbance risk but increases the risk of paravalvular regurgitation or valve embolization. Operators use controlled pull-back of the delivery catheter while maintaining tension to avoid inadvertent upward migration. For devices that allow repositioning, operators can recapture partially deployed frames and reposition prior to full release. Because self-expanding valves exert continuous outward radial force as they nitinol-heat-set to their full diameter, accurate initial centering and orientation are important.

Key deployment considerations common to both types include confirming coronary ostial distance to avoid obstruction, ensuring a coaxial trajectory to the annulus to avoid asymmetric expansion, monitoring for conduction disturbances, and having bailout strategies prepared (embolization management tools, valve-in-valve options, or emergency surgery availability). Imaging adjuncts — transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) or intracardiac echo in some centers — assist in fine-tuning positioning and assessing immediate valve function. Importantly, the choice between valve types often reflects patient anatomy (e.g., bicuspid valves, annular size), operator experience, and priorities such as the ability to recapture.

# Techniques for commissural alignment during deployment

Commissural alignment of the transcatheter valve seeks to align the prosthetic commissures with the native valve commissures to preserve coronary access, facilitate future coronary interventions, and potentially improve valve hemodynamics. Poor alignment can obscure coronary ostia behind frame struts and complicate future coronary cannulation.

Pre-procedural planning using ECG-gated CT is essential. The CT allows identification of the optimal fluoroscopic projection(s) (cusp-overlap view and a true en-face or short-axis view) and the native commissural locations relative to identifiable anatomical landmarks such as coronary ostia and the sinotubular junction. Different valve platforms have specific orientation markers on the delivery system or valve frame that can be used to achieve predictable commissural alignment. For example, some devices have an asymmetrical marker or radiopaque tab that corresponds to a commissural post. Operators can use the relationship between this marker in the catheter hub and a reference orientation during system insertion to rotate the valve to a pre-specified orientation.

Techniques used to achieve alignment include rotating the delivery system externally before advancing the valve across the iliofemoral system in a reproducible manner derived from CT-based simulation. Because the delivery catheter often rotates as it traverses tortuous vasculature, operators must anticipate and compensate for predictable rotations: e.g., when advancing through the iliofemoral axis, a known amount of torque may be transferred, so initial orientation is adjusted accordingly. Fluoroscopic checks in orthogonal views help refine the rotation as the valve approaches the annulus.

During deployment, maintaining slow, controlled release with continuous observation of frame rotation relative to native cusps facilitates final adjustments; for repositionable valves, partial recapture allows reorientation if alignment is suboptimal. Commissural alignment is intrinsically more straightforward with newer valve designs that incorporate alignment-friendly markers and delivery catheters engineered to preserve orientation. Nonetheless, in many cases perfect alignment is not achievable; the practical goal is to avoid placing commissural posts directly in front of coronary ostia and to orient frames to maximize future coronary access.

Clinical implications of commissural alignment extend beyond immediate procedure success. Patients with coronary artery disease or those likely to require future coronary access (younger patients, those with complex coronary disease) benefit most from deliberate alignment strategies. Finally, documenting orientation in the procedure note and implant imaging facilitates future coronary procedures by informing operators of frame orientation and expected strut locations.

Conclusion

Procedural success in TAVI depends on deliberate planning and meticulous execution of technical steps: selecting the optimal vascular access, matching sheath and closure strategies to vascular anatomy, using rapid pacing judiciously, deciding when to pre- or post-dilate, choosing and positioning supportive guidewires, and tailoring deployment techniques to the chosen prosthesis with attention to commissural alignment. Throughout, integration of high-quality pre-procedural CT, intra-procedural fluoroscopy and echocardiography, and a team-based approach to complications are essential. Mastery of these techniques, together with an understanding of the underlying device mechanics and patient-specific anatomy, enables safe expansion of TAVI indications and the maintenance of excellent short- and long-term outcomes.
