Introduction

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) has become a principal therapy for symptomatic severe aortic stenosis across a broad spectrum of surgical risk. Despite its less invasive nature, TAVI carries a nontrivial risk of injury to the cardiac conduction system because of the close anatomical relationship between the aortic valvar apparatus and the atrioventricular conduction pathways. Conduction disturbances after TAVI range from transient first-degree atrioventricular (AV) delay and new bundle branch block to persistent high-degree AV block requiring permanent pacemaker (PPM) implantation. Understanding the anatomic basis of these injuries, the clinical and procedural predictors for post‑TAVI conduction disease, device-related differences, strategies for intra-procedural monitoring and temporary pacing, and evidence-based indications and selection of pacing modes are essential for peri‑procedural management and for minimizing long‑term adverse consequences such as pacing‑induced cardiomyopathy.

# Anatomical basis for conduction disturbances after aortic valve interventions

The propensity for conduction injury during aortic valve interventions reflects close spatial relationships among the aortic annulus, the membranous septum, and components of the specialized conduction system — namely the AV node, the penetrating His bundle, and the proximal bundle branches. The AV node is located in the triangle of Koch at the base of the interatrial septum. The His bundle penetrates the central fibrous body and emerges at the crest of the interventricular septum near the base of the noncoronary cusp (NCC) and right coronary cusp (RCC). Thereafter, the His divides into the right bundle branch (RBB) and the left bundle branch (LBB); the LBB fan courses subendocardially along the left ventricular septum.

Key anatomic features that determine vulnerability include the length and position of the membranous septum and the distance from the annular plane to the His bundle. A short membranous septum or a His bundle that lies close to the inferior aspect of the aortic annulus increases the likelihood that valve frame expansion, prosthesis oversizing, or bulky calcification at the base of the leaflets will compress or stretch conduction tissue. The left bundle branch lies immediately beneath the noncoronary and right coronary cusps in many individuals, such that radial forces from a valve stent or adjacent hematoma can injure the LBB resulting in new left bundle branch block (LBBB).

Valve deployment depth and radial force are anatomically meaningful. A valve implanted deeper into the left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) approximates the stent frame to the His-Purkinje system; greater radial forces (as with some self-expanding devices) can produce sustained compression or ischemia of conduction tissue. In addition, extensive subannular or commissural calcification may directly impinge upon or transmit force to the conduction system during balloon dilatation or valve expansion. Understanding this three-dimensionally complex anatomy is central to procedural planning and to predicting which patients are at higher risk of conduction sequelae.

# Incidence and predictors of high-degree AV block and new pacemaker implantation

Incidence
Reported rates of new conduction abnormalities and PPM implantation after TAVI vary with patient population, device type, operator technique, and definitions used. Broadly, new-onset LBBB occurs in approximately 5% to 40% of patients depending on device and timing; high-degree AV block (second-degree type II AV block or complete AV block) leading to PPM implantation commonly occurs in about 5% to 30% of patients. Contemporary pooled analyses generally place overall permanent pacemaker rates in the range of roughly 8%–20%, with higher values associated with older self-expanding devices and lower values with balloon-expandable valves using modern techniques.

Predictors
Pre-procedural patient factors and baseline conduction abnormalities significantly influence the risk of post‑TAVI conduction disease:

- Baseline right bundle branch block (RBBB): This is consistently the strongest clinical predictor of subsequent high-degree AV block and PPM need. Because pre-existing RBB makes the conduction system dependent on the left-sided conduction fibers, any new injury to the LBB or distal His substantially raises the chance of complete AV block.

- Baseline first-degree AV block (PR interval >200 ms) and pre-existing LBBB: Both indicate existing conduction disease and increase risk for progression.

- Baseline QRS duration: Wider baseline QRS duration (e.g., >120 ms) is associated with higher risk.

- Advanced age and comorbidities: Older patients and those with greater burden of conduction system disease from ischemia, fibrosis, or prior surgery may be more susceptible.

Procedural and anatomic predictors include:

- Valve implantation depth: Deeper implantation into the LVOT — commonly quantified as distance below the noncoronary/right coronary cusp plane — correlates with increased PPM rates. Several series identify depths greater than approximately 6 mm below the annulus as higher risk; thresholds vary by device.

- Degree of valve oversizing: Excessive oversizing increases radial forces transmitted to the adjacent septum and conduction tissue.

- Pre-dilation and post-dilation: Balloon valvuloplasty or post‑dilatation can transiently or permanently injure conduction tissue, particularly with aggressive balloon sizing.

- Presence and distribution of calcification: Heavy and subannular calcification, especially when concentrated adjacent to the membranous septum, predisposes to conduction injury.

- Procedure-related factors: Valve type, operator technique, and need for multiple deployment attempts are relevant. Use of rapid ventricular pacing during deployment can transiently mask conduction abnormalities that manifest afterward.

Temporal course
Conduction disturbances may appear immediately during or after implantation, but some manifest hours to days later. Early transient conduction delays may recover within days to weeks; conversely, some patients experience delayed high-degree AV block beyond 24–48 hours, although most clinically significant events occur in the immediate post-procedural period.

# Device-specific rates and risk stratification

Device design and mechanical properties — particularly radial force, frame length, and interaction with the LVOT — influence conduction disturbance rates. Historically and in many series:

- Self-expanding nitinol frame valves (e.g., older-generation CoreValve/Evolut family) have been associated with higher rates of new LBBB and PPM requirement, ranging from roughly 10% to >30% in some registries. The long stent frame extending into the LVOT and continuous outward radial force contribute to this risk.

- Balloon-expandable valves (e.g., Sapien family) tend to have lower PPM rates, commonly reported in the range of about 3%–10%, although transcatheter valve iterations and implantation techniques affect these numbers.

- Mechanically expanding valves and newer-generation devices with repositionability or lower radial force aim to reduce conduction injury. Device-specific comparisons must account for patient selection and evolving operator experience.

Risk stratification combines baseline conduction status with procedural variables. Practical models used in clinical practice consider:

- Baseline ECG: presence of RBBB confers a high pre-test probability of post-TAVI high-degree AV block. Patients with RBBB plus first-degree AV block or prolonged HV intervals are at particularly high risk.

- Planned device and implantation strategy: If a device with historically higher conduction complication rates is selected, or if deep implantation is likely, the clinician anticipates increased risk.

- Imaging findings: Pre-procedural CT assessing membranous septum length, distribution of calcification, and annular anatomy helps predict the distance of the conduction system from the annulus and may guide valve choice and implantation depth to mitigate risk.

Risk stratification is dynamic and should inform intra‑procedural monitoring plans, decisions about pre-emptive temporary pacing, and post‑procedural observation.

# Intra-procedural monitoring and temporary pacing strategies

Continuous electrocardiographic monitoring during and immediately after TAVI is indispensable. Key elements include real-time 12‑lead ECG or multi‑lead monitoring, arterial pressure tracing, and, often, intracardiac electrograms when available. Rapid recognition of new conduction defects enables timely interventions.

Temporary pacing strategies
Temporary pacing serves two principal purposes: to support hemodynamics during deployment (rapid ventricular pacing for balloon-expandable valves) and to treat emergent bradyarrhythmias or high-degree AV block. Approaches include:

- Routine temporary transvenous pacing lead: Many centers place a temporary right ventricular pacing lead via the femoral or jugular vein before valve deployment. This provides immediate backup pacing for procedural asystole or post-deployment high‑degree AV block. The advantages are immediate availability and reliability; disadvantages include potential for vascular access complications and the need for careful lead stabilization during valve exchange.

- On-demand temporary pacing: Some operators avoid routine temporary transvenous leads, instead relying on external transcutaneous pacing and quick percutaneous venous access if pacing is required. This practice is riskier if high-degree AV block occurs unpredictably.

- Prophylactic temporary-permanent leads: An increasingly used strategy in high-risk patients is placement of a temporary-permanent active fixation lead connected to an externalized pacemaker generator. This provides more stable pacing over days while the conduction system may recover, reducing the need for urgent PPM implantation and allowing observation.

- Epicardial or coronary sinus approaches are not routinely used for acute backup.

Monitoring for conduction recovery and delayed block
Because conduction injury may evolve, continuous inpatient telemetry for at least 24–72 hours is recommended for most patients. Early recognition of progressive PR prolongation, new or worsening LBBB, or intermittent AV block allows timely PPM implantation if required. Continuous rhythm strips and frequent 12-lead ECGs (baseline, immediate post‑implant, daily until discharge, and with any symptoms) help detect deterioration.

Electrophysiological study (EPS)
Selected cases may benefit from EPS to localize the level of block (AV nodal vs infra-Hisian) and measure HV interval. Prolonged HV interval at baseline or after TAVI (e.g., HV >70 ms by some centers) suggests infra-Hisian disease and a higher likelihood of progression to complete heart block; such findings may sway the decision toward earlier PPM. EPS is most useful when the decision is equivocal — for example, persistent new LBBB with equivocal symptoms or intermittently prolonged PR but no sustained high-degree AV block.

Clinical decision points for temporary pacing:

- High-risk baseline (e.g., RBBB) undergoing implantation with a device/technique associated with higher conduction risk: strong consideration for pre-emptive temporary pacing with a stable lead.

- Emergent complete heart block or sustained symptomatic bradycardia: immediate temporary transvenous pacing and PPM implantation if block persists.

- New conduction abnormality without high-degree AV block: continue monitoring with low threshold for pacing if progression occurs.

# Indications for permanent pacemaker post-TAVI and pacing modes

Indications for PPM after TAVI are based on established pacing guidelines adapted to the TAVI context, clinical presentation, and the expected likelihood of conduction recovery. Key indications include:

Absolute indications
- Persistent high‑degree AV block: persistent second-degree Mobitz type II AV block or third-degree (complete) AV block causing symptoms or hemodynamic instability generally mandates PPM. If block persists beyond a brief period and is not expected to recover rapidly, implantation is indicated.

- Symptomatic bradycardia or pauses: recurrent symptomatic pauses, syncope attributable to bradyarrhythmia, or asystolic events.

- Alternating bundle branch block: alternating LBBB and RBBB indicates unstable infra‑Hisian conduction and is an accepted indication for PPM.

Relative/conditional indications
- Persistent new LBBB without high-degree AV block: isolated new LBBB per se is not an absolute indication. However, if accompanied by significant PR prolongation, syncope, or an abnormal invasive EPS (e.g., HV interval prolongation), PPM may be indicated.

- Intermittent high-degree AV block: episodes of transient complete AV block that recur or are prolonged suggest need for PPM.

Timing considerations
Because some conduction disturbances recover within days to weeks, many centers use an observation interval, typically 24–72 hours of continuous monitoring, before deciding on PPM for conduction changes that are not severe or symptomatic. In patients with persistent complete AV block immediately post-procedure, PPM implantation is not usually deferred. In borderline cases, a temporary-permanent lead allows safe observation while avoiding premature PPM placement.

Choice of pacing mode
Pacing mode selection should balance the immediate need for AV synchrony, minimization of right ventricular (RV) pacing burden, and long-term ventricular function preservation.

- Dual-chamber pacing (DDD) is commonly selected for patients with AV block when sinus node function is intact because it preserves AV synchrony and can reduce symptoms related to pacemaker syndrome. DDD pacing also allows algorithms to minimize unnecessary ventricular pacing (e.g., managed ventricular pacing).

- Single-chamber ventricular pacing (VVI) may be used when atrial lead implantation is contraindicated, in patients with permanent atrial fibrillation, or when quick implantation is required. VVI pacing is simple but can promote interventricular dyssynchrony and pacing-induced cardiomyopathy with high pacing burdens.

- His-bundle pacing (HBP) and left bundle branch area pacing (LBBAP): Physiologic conduction system pacing aims to maintain or restore synchronous ventricular activation by recruiting native His-Purkinje conduction. HBP or LBBAP may preserve ventricular function better than conventional RV apical pacing, particularly in patients expected to require high percentages of ventricular pacing or those with reduced LVEF. Technical considerations, operator expertise, and device compatibility influence feasibility. HBP may be limited by high pacing thresholds or lead instability, whereas LBBAP appears more stable and has emerged as an attractive alternative.

- Cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT): In patients with reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) (typically LVEF ≤ 35%) who develop high-degree AV block and are expected to be dependent on ventricular pacing, initial dual-site pacing with biventricular pacing (CRT) may be considered to reduce the risk of pacing-induced dyssynchrony. Decision to implant CRT rather than standard PPM should follow guideline-based criteria and individualized assessment.

Programming considerations
Programming to minimize ventricular pacing (e.g., adaptive AV delay algorithms) is especially important in patients with intact AV conduction who develop intermittent conduction abnormalities or new LBBB. When physiologic pacing is used, thresholds and sensing must be optimized to avoid unnecessary high output and premature battery depletion.

# Long-term implications of pacing on ventricular function and outcomes

While PPMs prevent bradycardia-related morbidity and sudden asystolic events, long-term chronic RV pacing may have adverse consequences, particularly when the percentage of ventricular pacing is high.

Pacing-induced cardiomyopathy
Chronic RV apical pacing produces ventricular activation patterns that mimic LBBB, causing electrical and mechanical dyssynchrony. Over time, this dyssynchrony can reduce LVEF and lead to heart failure in a subset of patients. Observational studies suggest that a high burden of RV pacing (commonly cited thresholds include >20%–40% ventricular pacing) is associated with an increased risk of developing pacing-induced cardiomyopathy, although thresholds vary among cohorts. Therefore, minimizing unnecessary RV pacing is a practical goal, particularly in patients with baseline reduced LVEF or those likely to be paced frequently (e.g., complete heart block and permanent dependence).

Impact on morbidity and mortality
PPM implantation after TAVI has been associated in some observational analyses with longer hospital stays and higher rates of rehospitalization for heart failure; long-term mortality data are mixed. The adverse impact is largely mediated by ventricular dyssynchrony and heart failure progression in those with high ventricular pacing burden. Conversely, appropriate pacing in patients who need it reduces syncope and sudden death risk. Net clinical impact depends on patient selection, pacing strategy, and device programming.

Strategies to mitigate long-term harm
- Minimize RV pacing: Use AV‑delay modulation and algorithms that favor intrinsic conduction when feasible.

- Physiologic pacing: HBP or LBBAP may preserve mechanical synchrony and reduce the risk of pacing-induced cardiomyopathy by using the native conduction system to achieve near-normal activation. Early data show promise, especially when high pacing burden is anticipated, but long-term outcome data are still evolving.

- Consider CRT in high-risk patients: In those with reduced LVEF who will be dependent on ventricular pacing, primary CRT may be preferable to conventional RV pacing.

- Surveillance: Routine follow-up with device interrogation and periodic echocardiography (e.g., at baseline, 3–6 months, and as clinically indicated) is recommended to monitor LVEF and detect early pacing-related decline. If LVEF falls significantly, consider upgrade to CRT or change of pacing strategy.

- Infection and lead complications: Long-term risks of PPM include lead dysfunction, pocket infection, and thromboembolic events. These risks should be weighed when deciding on PPM timing and the necessity of implantation, favoring observation when safe.

Recovery of conduction
A proportion of patients with early conduction disorders after TAVI experience recovery of AV conduction over days to weeks. If there is clinical equipoise and the patient is hemodynamically stable, temporary-permanent pacing and close monitoring allow time for recovery and may prevent unnecessary PPM implantation. Conversely, in patients with persistent or recurrent high-degree AV block, earlier definitive PPM avoids prolonged hospitalization and recurrent bradyarrhythmic events.

Conclusion

Managing conduction disturbances after TAVI requires a synthesis of anatomic knowledge, baseline conduction status, device- and procedure-related factors, and careful peri‑procedural monitoring. Risk stratification using ECG, imaging, and procedural planning informs the use of temporary pacing and the timing of PPM implantation. When a pacemaker is required, selection of pacing mode and strategies to minimize long-term ventricular dyssynchrony are critical to preserving cardiac function. An individualized approach that balances the risks of delayed PPM insertion against the potential harms of unnecessary chronic pacing optimizes patient outcomes.
