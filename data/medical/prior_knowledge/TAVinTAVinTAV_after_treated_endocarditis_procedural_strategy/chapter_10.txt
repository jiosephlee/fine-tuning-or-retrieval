Introduction

Valve-in-valve (ViV) transcatheter valve implantation has become an established option for patients with failing surgical bioprosthetic heart valves who are at elevated surgical risk. However, implanting a new transcatheter heart valve within a stenotic or small surgical bioprosthesis can result in patient–prosthesis mismatch, elevated transvalvular gradients, and suboptimal hemodynamics. Two interventional strategies have emerged to address these limitations: bioprosthetic valve fracture (BVF), which uses high-pressure balloon expansion to rupture a rigid sewing ring or stent frame of the surgical valve to enlarge the effective orifice area; and leaflet modification techniques such as intentional leaflet laceration (commonly referred to as BASILICA, or Bioprosthetic or Native Aortic Scallop Intentional Laceration to prevent Iatrogenic Coronary Artery obstruction), which split native or prosthetic leaflets to prevent coronary obstruction and to improve leaflet mobility. This chapter reviews the principles, mechanisms, procedural considerations, hemodynamic effects, and risks of BVF and leaflet modification, and summarizes the clinical evidence base, outcomes, and limitations for their use in repeat valve interventions.

# Principles of bioprosthetic valve fracture and applicable valve types

Bioprosthetic valve fracture is based on the mechanical principle that many surgical bioprosthetic valves have a rigid supporting structure—typically a polymeric or metallic stent frame, or a fibrous sewing ring—that constrains the internal diameter of the valve. By delivering a noncompliant high-pressure balloon within the surgical valve and inflating progressively to high pressures, the rigid frame or ring can be fractured, yielding an abrupt increase in internal diameter and effective orifice area. The desired outcome is to reduce transvalvular gradients and relieve patient–prosthesis mismatch when a transcatheter valve is implanted inside the surgical valve.

Not all surgical bioprostheses are amenable to BVF. The technique is most successful with valves that have readily fractureable components such as polyethylene terephthalate (Dacron)-reinforced sewing rings or certain polymer or thermoplastic stent structures. Examples of surgical valves that have been reported to fracture reliably include some models of the Carpentier-Edwards Perimount series and similar stented tissue valves with nonmetallic stent posts or sewing rings. Conversely, valves with fully metallic sewing rings or rigid metallic stent frames, such as certain older stented or stentless surgical valves with substantial metal reinforcement, often cannot be fractured. The material properties of the supporting frame (elastic modulus, yield strength, and fracture toughness) and its geometry dictate whether and how the structure will fail under radial load. When fracture occurs, it typically results in loss of structural integrity at one or more points around the ring, with an immediate and measurable increase in inner diameter.

Recognition of valve type prior to the procedure is critical. Pre-procedural multimodality imaging, including computed tomography and review of operative records or valve identification charts, is used to determine the valve model and the likelihood of successful BVF. The valve label, model number, and known manufacturer-specific behavior in response to high-pressure dilation should guide procedural planning. Operators must also consider the original labelled internal diameter of the surgical valve and the relation between that diameter and the intended transcatheter valve size, since BVF is most helpful when the predicted effective orifice without fracture would be insufficient or associated with high gradients.

# Balloon selection, inflation pressures and timing relative to valve deployment

Balloon selection and inflation strategy are central to BVF. The procedure employs noncompliant, high-pressure balloons engineered to resist longitudinal expansion while transmitting radial force to the target. Valves are sized using the labelled internal diameter of the surgical valve and measured internal diameters on computed tomography. Balloons are chosen to match or slightly exceed the true annular or valve internal diameter in order to apply sufficient radial stress to fracture the ring. Operators typically select balloon diameters that range from equal to the labelled internal diameter up to 1–2 mm larger, balancing the need for effective fracture against the risk of oversizing and annular injury.

Inflation pressures required to achieve fracture vary by valve type and balloon size but are substantially higher than pressures used for routine valvuloplasty. Reported fracture pressures commonly fall in the range of $12$–$24$ atmospheres (atm), with many fractures achieved between $12$ and $20$ atm. The choice of balloon brand and rated burst pressure is important: noncompliant balloons such as the Atlas Gold or True Dilatation balloons with rated burst pressures exceeding the target inflation pressure are typically used. The fracturing event is often accompanied by an audible “pop” and a sudden drop in inflation pressure or balloon waist release, and may be visible fluoroscopically as a change in the contour of the frame. Real-time hemodynamic monitoring will often demonstrate an immediate improvement in transvalvular gradient after fracture.

The timing of BVF relative to transcatheter valve deployment can follow one of two strategies: pre-deployment fracture (fracture before implanting the transcatheter valve) or post-deployment fracture (fracture after implanting the transcatheter valve within the surgical valve). Each approach has rationale and tradeoffs. Pre-deployment BVF allows the operator to maximally enlarge the surgical valve before transcatheter valve insertion, potentially enabling placement of a larger transcatheter valve and avoiding deformation of the transcatheter frame during fracture. However, fracturing an exposed ring may cause abrupt hemodynamic changes or embolization of material. Post-deployment BVF is frequently performed because the newly implanted transcatheter valve can contain displaced tissue and provide some protection against annular trauma and embolization; additionally, balloon fracture can further expand the transcatheter stent frame to optimize leaflet function and effective orifice area. In practice, many operators favor post-deployment BVF in ViV cases to directly expand the transcatheter frame and improve leaflet mobility, provided that the transcatheter valve is durable enough to withstand the high-pressure inflation and that the balloon position relative to the transcatheter valve commissures is carefully controlled.

Technical factors important for successful fracture include appropriate balloon centration within the valve, maintenance of coaxial alignment, and rapid ventricular pacing during inflation to minimize cardiac motion and reduce ejection. Rapid pacing at rates typically around $180$–$220$ beats per minute is used to reduce cardiac output transiently, thereby stabilizing balloon position and reducing the risk of balloon migration. The duration of inflation is brief—just long enough to confirm fracture and hemodynamic response. Contrast injection may be used to visualize the valve and surrounding structures during inflation, but excessive contrast should be avoided in patients with renal impairment.

# Hemodynamic effects and risks (annular injury, aortic root complications)

The primary hemodynamic effect of BVF is an increase in the internal diameter of the failing surgical prosthesis, which translates into greater effective orifice area (EOA) and lower transvalvular gradients. This improves forward flow and can reduce symptoms of heart failure attributable to prosthetic valve stenosis or patient–prosthesis mismatch. The magnitude of gradient reduction depends on the degree of enlargement achieved and on the interaction between the fractured ring and the transcatheter valve frame. Post-procedure echocardiography typically documents decreased peak and mean gradients, increased EOA, and improved transvalvular velocity profiles.

Despite these benefits, BVF introduces several potential risks related to disruption of prosthetic and native anatomical structures. The high radial forces required to fracture the prosthetic ring are transmitted to adjacent tissues. One major concern is annular injury, including dehiscence of the surgical annulus, disruption of sutures, or creation of perivalvular leaks. If the fracture propagates into adjacent tissues or if the balloon oversizes relative to the anatomical confines, tearing of the annulus or paravalvular tissue can occur, which may present acutely with significant regurgitation, hemodynamic instability, or requirement for urgent surgery.

Aortic root complications are another important consideration, particularly for aortic valve BVF. Fracture can produce jagged edges or outward deformation of the sewing ring that may impinge on the aortic root or coronary ostia. Although BVF is not typically associated with direct coronary obstruction, the risk increases if the fractured fragments or displaced leaflets obstruct coronary ostia. Aortic root injury, including dissection or rupture, is a catastrophic although uncommon complication. The risk is increased in patients with heavily calcified or fragile annular tissue, prior root enlargement procedures, or mixed prosthetic-native anatomy.

Embolic events are possible due to liberation of calcific or fibrous material when the valve structure fails. Thromboembolic protection devices have been considered in some centers, though their routine use in BVF has not been established by randomized data. Hemodynamic instability during the fracture event may occur, especially if rapid pacing is inadequate or if significant regurgitation is precipitated. There is also a theoretical risk that the transcatheter valve frame may be damaged by the high-pressure inflation; therefore, operators must ensure that the chosen transcatheter valve is compatible with post-deployment dilation.

Procedural planning should therefore include assessment of annular and root anatomy with CT, measurement of coronary heights, evaluation of calcification and tissue fragility, and contingency planning for possible surgical bailout. Intra-procedural transesophageal echocardiography and fluoroscopy are used to monitor for immediate complications. Recognizing early signs—new paravalvular leak, abrupt hemodynamic deterioration, or evidence of contained aortic root injury—allows for prompt management.

# Leaflet modification procedures (eg, intentional laceration) and indications

Leaflet modification refers to techniques intended to prevent or mitigate obstruction of coronary ostia and to improve leaflet opening after ViV procedures. The best-known method is intentional leaflet laceration of the native or bioprosthetic leaflets—BASILICA—which uses an electrified wire to split leaflets longitudinally before valve implantation. The rationale is that when a transcatheter valve is deployed inside a surgical bioprosthesis or native valve with bulky leaflets, the displaced leaflet tissue can be pushed outward and upward toward the coronary ostia, potentially occluding flow. By creating a controlled longitudinal split in the leaflet, the two resultant halves splay outward during transcatheter valve expansion, leaving a channel for coronary blood flow and reducing the risk of obstruction.

The BASILICA technique is performed under fluoroscopic and echocardiographic guidance. A guidewire is advanced and snared to create a rail across the aortic valve leaflet targeted for laceration. Electrified energy is applied to the guidewire to lacerate the leaflet from base to tip, creating a midline split. The procedure requires meticulous technique to ensure the laceration plane is centered and full-thickness, and to avoid unintended injury to adjacent structures such as the mitral valve or aortic wall. BASILICA can be applied to native valve leaflets, prosthetic surgical leaflets, and transcatheter leaflets in selected circumstances.

Indications for leaflet modification include high risk of coronary obstruction predicted by pre-procedural imaging—most commonly computed tomography measurements that show low coronary ostial height, small sino-tubular junction dimensions, narrow sinuses of Valsalva, or anticipated leaflet-to-coronary ostium distances below threshold values. Specific risk stratification often uses the virtual transcatheter valve-to-coronary (VTC) distance, where a VTC less than approximately $4$ mm is associated with high risk of obstruction. When the predicted risk is high, BASILICA or similar techniques may be indicated to prevent life-threatening coronary obstruction.

Leaflet modification may also improve hemodynamics by increasing leaflet mobility in ViV settings where immobile prosthetic leaflets could be pinned by the new valve, thereby reducing effective orifice area. In some ViV scenarios, combining BVF with leaflet laceration may be considered: BVF enlarges the ring, while leaflet laceration ensures that displaced leaflet tissue does not obstruct coronaries and allows better leaflet opening. The decision to perform leaflet modification requires assessment of the valve type, coronary anatomy, patient comorbidities, and operator expertise.

Risks of leaflet modification include perforation or laceration extending beyond the target leaflet, creation of severe regurgitation if leaflet competence is compromised unpredictably, embolization of leaflet material, and conduction disturbances. The BASILICA technique also adds procedural complexity, time, and radiation exposure, and may require transseptal or retrograde arterial access routes and specialized equipment.

# Clinical evidence, outcomes and limitations of BVF/BASILICA in repeat interventions

Clinical evidence for both BVF and BASILICA comes from registries, observational series, bench testing, and limited prospective studies rather than large randomized trials. For BVF, early multi-center registry data and single-center series have demonstrated substantial reductions in transvalvular gradients and improvements in effective orifice area in ViV procedures when fracture is successful. Patients with small surgical bioprostheses who underwent BVF showed lower residual gradients and less patient–prosthesis mismatch compared with historical controls undergoing ViV without BVF. Bench studies and imaging corroborate the mechanism by demonstrating abrupt increases in internal diameter after fracture.

Reported outcomes generally indicate high procedural success rates for valve models known to be fractureable, with acute gradient reduction and symptomatic improvement. However, the durability of the hemodynamic benefit over the long term is still under investigation, and some series report residual gradients in a subset of patients. Major complications such as annular rupture, coronary obstruction, and need for emergency surgery have been infrequent but not negligible, reflecting the importance of careful patient selection and operator experience.

For BASILICA, prospective registry data demonstrate feasibility in preventing coronary obstruction during high-risk transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) and ViV cases. Early multicenter experiences showed that BASILICA reduced the incidence of coronary obstruction compared with historical expectations based on pre-procedural CT risk stratification. Procedural success rates are high in experienced centers, and most reported complications are manageable. Follow-up data indicate acceptable short-term outcomes, but the impact of leaflet laceration on long-term valve function, thrombosis risk, and structural degeneration is not yet fully defined.

Limitations of the evidence include selection bias, institution-specific expertise, and relatively short follow-up durations. Many reports are from high-volume centers with considerable expertise in complex TAVR, which may not generalize to all settings. Device-specific factors further complicate evidence synthesis: fractureability is valve-model dependent, and BASILICA may be more challenging in certain anatomic configurations or with particular prosthetic leaflet materials. Comparative randomized data that quantify net clinical benefit—balancing improvements in gradients or prevention of coronary obstruction against the risk of procedural complications—are lacking.

Clinical decision-making should therefore be individualized, integrating detailed imaging-based risk assessment, knowledge of the surgical valve model, anticipation of hemodynamic benefit, and the institution and operator experience with these techniques. Key decision points include whether the surgical valve is fractureable, the predicted residual gradient without BVF, the coronary anatomy and calculated VTC or valve-to-sinotubular junction distances, the patient’s surgical risk, and the potential for adverse events. Multidisciplinary Heart Team discussion that includes cardiac surgeons, interventional cardiologists, imaging specialists, and anesthesiologists is recommended for complex ViV cases considering BVF and/or BASILICA.

Conclusion

Bioprosthetic valve fracture and intentional leaflet modification (BASILICA) have expanded the toolkit available for treating failed surgical bioprostheses and high-risk native valves in repeat transcatheter interventions. BVF can markedly improve valve hemodynamics by increasing internal diameter, while BASILICA reduces the risk of coronary obstruction and can enhance leaflet mobility. Both techniques require careful valve identification, detailed pre-procedural imaging, precise balloon and equipment selection, and skilled execution to balance benefits against risks such as annular injury, aortic root complications, embolization, and procedural complexity. Current clinical evidence supports their use in appropriately selected patients at experienced centers, but limitations in long-term data and randomized comparisons necessitate thoughtful, individualized application and ongoing study.
