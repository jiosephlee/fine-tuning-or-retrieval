Introduction

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) and valve‑in‑valve (ViV) procedures have transformed the management of aortic stenosis and failing surgical bioprostheses. Despite their overall safety, one of the most catastrophic and immediately life‑threatening complications is coronary obstruction, which can cause acute myocardial ischemia, hemodynamic collapse, and death if not anticipated and managed promptly. This chapter reviews the mechanisms by which coronary obstruction occurs in native and prosthetic valve settings, defines the anatomic predictors that increase risk, explains CT‑based preprocedural risk stratification metrics, and describes prophylactic and rescue strategies including coronary protection techniques, the chimney stent approach, leaflet laceration (BASILICA), and immediate management when coronary compromise occurs. The focus is on integrating pathophysiology, imaging interpretation, and procedural decision‑making to prevent and treat coronary obstruction during TAVI/ViV.

# Pathophysiology of coronary obstruction in native and prosthetic valve settings

Coronary obstruction during TAVI results when the ostium of a coronary artery is acutely occluded or its effective flow lumen is severely compromised at the time of valve deployment. Mechanisms differ between native valve TAVI and ViV procedures but share a common final pathway—mechanical impediment of coronary ostial flow producing acute ischemia.

In native aortic stenosis, obstruction typically arises when bulky native leaflets (often heavily calcified) are displaced after transcatheter heart valve (THV) deployment. A self‑ or balloon‑expanded THV displaces the diseased aortic leaflets outward toward the sinuses of Valsalva and the coronary ostia. If the displaced leaflet tissue, or a calcific nodule within it, lies close to or above the coronary ostium, it can cover or impinge on the ostium or create an external compression of the sinotubular junction (STJ) region that dynamically reduces the coronary inflow or causes ostial occlusion.

In ViV procedures, risk is often higher because the previously implanted surgical bioprosthesis provides a rigid frame and bioprosthetic leaflets that can be displaced in a predictable but potentially dangerous trajectory. Leaflets of surgical valves are typically mounted externally to the valve frame and, when a THV is implanted inside (ViV), the surgical leaflets are pushed outwards between the surgical frame and the aortic wall. This narrows the sinus and can position leaflet tissue directly across coronary ostia. Supra‑annular surgical valves (e.g., stented valves with tall leaflets) and stentless or stented valves with externally mounted leaflets confer increased risk because leaflet cusps are located higher relative to the coronary ostia.

Another mechanism is external compression of the coronary ostium by the THV frame itself, particularly with large‑diameter THVs or tall frames that extend above the STJ. In the presence of narrow sinuses or low ostial heights, the frame can physically contact or limit expansion of the sinus, compromising flow into the coronary sinus and ostium. Additionally, displaced leaflet tissue can be trapped between the THV frame and aortic wall, forming a flap that may impinge on the ostium intermittently or persistently, with dynamic effects during the cardiac cycle.

Hemodynamically, acute coronary occlusion abolishes antegrade flow into the downstream coronary circulation, producing myocardial ischemia according to the territory supplied and the degree/duration of occlusion. If the obstruction is complete and prolonged, it leads to arrhythmia, pump failure, and rapid hemodynamic collapse. Partial obstruction or severe reduction in coronary flow may manifest as ST changes, hemodynamic instability, or isolated biomarker elevation. Because TAVI patients often have limited coronary reserve and multiple comorbidities, even partial reductions in flow can have clinically significant consequences.

# Anatomic predictors: low coronary heights, small sinuses, narrow VTC

The risk of coronary obstruction is fundamentally anatomical. Key anatomic predictors include coronary ostial height above the annular plane, sinus of Valsalva (SOV) or sinus diameter, virtual transcatheter valve to coronary (VTC) distance, leaflet length and bulk, and the characteristics of prior surgical bioprostheses (in ViV).

Coronary height is measured from the basal annular plane to the coronary ostium. Lower coronary heights increase the chance that displaced native or prosthetic leaflets will reach the ostium. Historically, a coronary height ≤10–12 mm has been widely accepted as increased risk. Many centers use a cutoff of <10 mm as high risk and 10–12 mm as intermediate risk; risk increases as height decreases.

Sinus of Valsalva diameter: Small sinuses limit the space available for displaced leaflets and the THV frame. A small sinus (often cited as SOV diameter <30 mm) is associated with higher obstruction risk. If the sinus cannot accommodate the displaced tissue, leaflets are more likely to be forced directly toward the coronary ostia.

Virtual transcatheter valve to coronary (VTC) distance: This CT‑derived metric estimates the post‑implant distance between the anticipated outer surface of the THV skirt/leaflet complex and the coronary ostium. VTC integrates annular sizing, expected THV position, leaflet length, and coronary location to predict residual clearance. A small VTC indicates that the leaflets (or THV skirt) will be very close to or touching the coronary ostium after implantation. Cutoffs differ slightly in literature, but a VTC <4 mm is generally considered high risk in ViV procedures; some centers use <3–4 mm as the critical threshold. In native TAVI, a similar concept, the virtual transcatheter valve to coronary (virtual THV to coronaries) distance, is used with similar thresholds.

Leaflet length and bulk are particularly relevant in ViV. The length of the surgical bioprosthetic leaflets relative to coronary height may determine whether leaflet tissue will extend above the coronary ostium when displaced. Externally mounted leaflets and stented bioprostheses with tall leaflets (e.g., Mitroflow, Trifecta) are notorious for high ViV obstruction rates.

Sinotubular junction and ascending aortic dimensions: A narrow STJ or small ascending aorta restricts space for leaflet displacement and THV expansion, contributing to impingement at the coronary level. When STJ diameter is small (often <30–35 mm), the risk rises.

Other predictors: Dense leaflet calcification near the commissures or coronary cusps can create bulky nodules that, when displaced, are more likely to obstruct. Orientation of commissures and relation of surgical valve posts to coronary ostia can also affect risk.

Taken together, no single metric is perfectly predictive; risk is assessed by integrating coronary height, sinus dimensions, VTC, leaflet anatomy, and valve type.

# Preprocedural risk stratification using CT-based metrics

Computed tomography angiography (CTA) of the aortic root is the cornerstone of preprocedural risk assessment for coronary obstruction. Multidetector CT provides high‑resolution, gated images that permit precise measurement of annular anatomy, coronary heights, sinus dimensions, leaflet morphology, and the predicted interaction between an implanted THV and the coronary ostia.

Measurements performed on ECG‑gated contrast CT include:

- Annular plane delineation: define the basal annulus by the hinge points of the native leaflets or the sewing ring of the prosthetic valve.
- Coronary ostial height: perpendicular distance from the basal annular plane to the ostium of each coronary artery. Record left main and right coronary ostial heights separately. Standard thresholds: <10 mm high risk; 10–12 mm borderline.
- Sinus of Valsalva diameter: maximal orthogonal diameter measured at the mid‑sinus level. Diameter <30 mm often considered small.
- Sinotubular junction (STJ) diameter and height: small or low STJ increases risk.
- Leaflet length: measure the length of native or prosthetic leaflets from hinge to free edge; if leaflet length exceeds coronary height, the leaflet can reach the ostium.
- Coronary ostial orientation and takeoff angle: acute takeoff or ostia located near commissural posts elevate risk.
- Virtual transcatheter valve to coronary (VTC) distance: simulate the THV by reconstructing the intended THV frame and projecting its position relative to the coronary ostium. VTC may be calculated by creating a virtual cylinder representing the deployed THV and measuring the shortest distance from the outer aspect of this cylinder (and the expected displaced leaflet surface) to the coronary ostium. In ViV, use the inner diameter of the surgical valve and simulate THV expansion within that frame. A VTC <4 mm (or <3 mm in some series) carries high risk.

Software tools with dedicated TAVI planning modules facilitate these simulations and permit three‑dimensional virtual device implantation. The evaluation should be performed in both systolic and diastolic phases if possible, because leaflet position and coronary relations may shift dynamically. Some centers also use CT to simulate the potential position of displaced leaflets and to visualize whether the coronary ostium will be covered by such tissue.

Risk stratification is practical rather than binary. Patients are typically categorized as low, intermediate, or high risk of coronary obstruction based on integrated CT findings, valve type, and other clinical factors. For example, a patient with left main height 8 mm, SOV diameter 25 mm, and VTC 2–3 mm would be designated high risk and considered for preventative strategies. Conversely, a patient with 14 mm coronary height and broad sinuses would be low risk and likely proceed without prophylactic measures.

Importantly, CT assessment must be individualized for left and right coronaries since anatomy and risk often differ; left main obstruction carries greater hemodynamic consequences due to the larger myocardial territory supplied.

# Coronary protection techniques: guidewire protection, pre-positioned balloons/stents (chimney technique)

When preprocedural assessment identifies intermediate or high risk of obstruction, procedural coronary protection strategies can be employed. These can be prophylactic (intended to prevent obstruction during deployment) or elective (standby for immediate rescue). The principal approaches are (1) guidewire protection only, (2) protection with a pre‑positioned balloon, and (3) protection with a pre‑positioned stent (chimney technique) ready for immediate deployment.

Guidewire protection: This simplest form involves placing a coronary guidewire across the ostium into the distal coronary artery before THV deployment. The wire provides rapid access to the coronary artery if an obstruction occurs, facilitating immediate balloon angioplasty or stent delivery without the delay of wiring a collapsed ostium. Guidewire protection is low risk and is commonly used when CT indicates moderate risk. It does not itself prevent obstruction but expedites rescue.

Pre‑positioned balloon: An angioplasty balloon is advanced and positioned across the coronary ostium (sometimes partially in the proximal artery) over the guidewire and kept uninflated during THV deployment. If obstruction occurs, the balloon can be immediately inflated to attempt to restore lumen patency by displacing obstructing leaflet tissue outward. Balloon inflation can transiently open flow and facilitate subsequent stent delivery. Pre‑positioned ballooning can also be performed prophylactically during THV deployment in a protective “parking” position; however, balloon inflation at the time of THV deployment is usually a rescue maneuver.

Pre‑positioned stent (chimney technique): In this technique, a coronary guidewire is placed and a balloon‑mounted or balloon‑expandable coronary stent is advanced and positioned with its proximal portion protruding into the aortic root, just outside the coronary ostium. The stent is left undeployed while the THV is implanted. If obstruction occurs or is impending (visualized angiographically or by loss of flow), the stent is deployed and expanded, creating a channel between the coronary ostium and aortic lumen that runs alongside the THV frame—hence the “chimney” moniker. The chimney stent protrudes into the aorta beyond the THV skirt, preventing leaflet tissue from sealing the ostium. The technique can be employed prophylactically (deploy immediately after THV) or as salvage if obstruction appears.

Advantages of chimney stenting include immediate restoration of flow when obstruction occurs and potential avoidance of emergency surgery. Disadvantages include permanent placement of a stent that protrudes into the aorta (potential for thrombosis and challenges to future coronary access), risk of coronary dissection or malapposition, and potential for interaction between the stent and THV frame causing malexpansion or stent deformation. Long‑term outcomes of chimney stents after TAVI are not fully defined, and dual antiplatelet therapy is often employed postprocedure per operator preference and bleeding risk.

Choice of protection strategy depends on the perceived risk and the specific anatomy. For high‑risk ViV cases with very small VTC or extremely low coronary height, a chimney stent or BASILICA may be preferred. For moderate risk, wire or balloon protection with readiness to convert to chimney stenting is common.

# Leaflet laceration (BASILICA): principles, indications and technical steps

Principles: The Bioprosthetic or Native Aortic Scallop Intentional Laceration to prevent Iatrogenic Coronary Artery obstruction (BASILICA) is a preemptive transcatheter technique that lacerates the native or bioprosthetic leaflet that would otherwise be displaced over a coronary ostium during TAVI. By splitting the leaflet down its midline toward the surgical hinge, the two resulting flaps splay outward upon THV deployment and form a pair of halves that do not collectively obstruct the coronary ostium, thus preserving coronary flow. BASILICA converts a single large continuous leaflet flap that can seal the ostium into two smaller flaps that leave a channel for the coronary.

Indications: BASILICA is indicated for patients with high predicted risk of coronary obstruction where other strategies are insufficient or undesirable. Typical scenarios include ViV with externally mounted or tall leaflets and VTC predicted to be very small, native valve TAVI with low coronary height and bulky leaflets, or cases where chimney stenting is considered suboptimal due to concerns about future coronary access or stent thrombosis. BASILICA is most commonly applied prophylactically rather than as a rescue maneuver; performing it electively before THV deployment avoids hemodynamic deterioration.

Contraindications include inability to safely access the targeted leaflet, unfavorable leaflet anatomy (e.g., heavily scarred or calcified leaflet that resists laceration), severe coagulopathy, or lack of operator expertise/resources.

Technical steps:

1. Planning: Detailed CT planning identifies target leaflet(s) for laceration (usually the leaflet facing the threatened coronary ostium, often the left coronary cusp). Determine the optimal cusp, crossing and target points, and catheter/guidewire angles needed.

2. Vascular access: Obtain arterial and venous access per institutional practice. BASILICA is typically performed via transfemoral arterial access for delivery of THV components and transseptal or contralateral arterial access for snare retrieval, depending on operator technique. A coronary guiding catheter is positioned in the target coronary artery in many approaches to protect the coronary and to act as an external snare.

3. Leaflet traversal: Using a guiding catheter (e.g., an AL or multipurpose shape), an electrified guidewire assembly is directed to the target cusp and used to traverse the leaflet from the aortic side into the left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) or across the prosthetic leaflet. Traversal is performed under fluoroscopic and echocardiographic guidance. Electrified guidewires (e.g., Astato XS with insulation) are energized briefly to assist passage through leaflet tissue.

4. Creation of a snare loop: The traversing wire is captured in the LVOT or opposing arterial access with a snare or through a retrograde catheter, creating a rail across the leaflet. The wire is externalized so it forms a loop that passes through the leaflet.

5. Laceration: The midportion of the externalized wire loop is insulated except for a short exposed segment positioned along the leaflet midline. Controlled radiofrequency energy (or electrocautery) is delivered to the exposed segment while applying tension to the wire loop. The energized wire cuts the leaflet from the free edge toward the base, creating a longitudinal split. Continuous imaging confirms the laceration; echocardiography can visualize leaflet mobility.

6. Confirmation and THV deployment: After successful laceration, the THV is implanted. The lacerated leaflet halves splay apart during valve expansion, allowing coronary flow. Choice and timing of antithrombotic therapy and hemodynamic support follow institutional protocols.

Technical considerations: Accurate alignment of the traversal trajectory to the leaflet midline is critical to ensure an effective central laceration. Care must be taken to avoid cutting adjacent structures, such as the aortic wall, commissures, or conduction system. Adequate anticoagulation is maintained. Operators require training and experience; BASILICA is performed in centers with multidisciplinary TAVI teams.

Outcomes and limitations: Studies show that BASILICA reduces the incidence of coronary obstruction in high‑risk patients compared with historical controls and allows many patients to undergo TAVI who would otherwise be inoperable. However, it is not 100% protective—anatomic variation, incomplete laceration, or extensive calcification can limit effectiveness. Potential complications include leaflet avulsion, stroke from embolized tissue, perforation, or conduction disturbances. Long‑term data are still evolving.

# Rescue strategies and immediate management of coronary compromise

Despite careful planning, coronary obstruction may occur acutely during THV deployment. Rapid recognition and immediate action are essential to reduce myocardial injury and improve survival.

Recognition: Suspect coronary compromise if there is sudden hypotension, bradycardia or ventricular arrhythmia, ST‑segment elevation on ECG (especially in leads corresponding to involved territory), new regional wall motion abnormalities on echocardiography, or angiographic evidence of lost coronary flow. Intra‑procedural aortography and selective coronary angiography are definitive for diagnosis. Continuous hemodynamic and ECG monitoring during deployment facilitate early detection.

Initial immediate steps:

1. Maintain vascular access: Ensure guidewires and catheters are in place and ready. If a coronary guidewire or pre‑positioned balloon/stent is present, use it without delay. If not, rapid wiring of the coronary should be attempted immediately, but this can be difficult if the ostium is sealed.

2. Hemodynamic support: Begin or escalate hemodynamic support as needed. Options include volume resuscitation if appropriate, vasopressors, inotropes, and mechanical circulatory support. Rapid deployment of percutaneous devices such as intra‑aortic balloon pump (IABP), Impella, or venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA‑ECMO) may be lifesaving in profound cardiogenic shock or during prolonged resuscitation. Selection depends on institutional capability and patient condition.

3. Coronary reperfusion—preferred rescue strategies:

- If a pre‑positioned stent is available (chimney strategy), deploy the stent promptly to create a channel and restore flow. Post‑dilate as needed to optimize expansion.
- If a pre‑positioned balloon is in place, inflate the balloon to reestablish partial flow and facilitate stent delivery. Balloon inflation can temporarily open the ostium and may be lifesaving by restoring some perfusion.
- If only a guidewire is present, rapid balloon angioplasty followed by stent implantation is attempted. Use of aspiration thrombectomy is appropriate if thrombus is suspected but is rarely the primary maneuver in this setting.
- If no coronary access exists and ostial sealing prevents wiring, consider immediate snaring or externalization techniques to permit access. In extreme cases, emergency cardiac surgery (coronary bypass or surgical removal of the THV) may be required if percutaneous options fail and the patient remains salvageable.

4. Pharmacologic adjuncts: Administer antiplatelet and anticoagulant therapy as appropriate, balancing the need to prevent stent thrombosis with bleeding risk. Intracoronary vasodilators (nitroprusside, nitroglycerin) may be used if vasospasm contributes. Glycoprotein IIb/IIIa inhibitors can be considered if thrombotic occlusion is suspected, but caution is needed.

5. Imaging and confirmation: After reperfusion maneuvers, perform repeat angiography to confirm restoration of TIMI 3 flow when possible and echocardiography to assess ventricular function. Monitor biomarkers and ECG evolution.

Rescue when BASILICA was planned but fails: If BASILICA was attempted and incomplete laceration occurs with subsequent obstruction, proceed with chimney stenting or emergent wiring and stenting as above. Hemodynamic support is crucial because laceration attempts may prolong procedure time.

Postprocedural considerations: Patients who suffered coronary compromise require monitoring for myocardial injury (serial troponin, ECG, echo), management of arrhythmias, and optimization of heart failure therapy if myocardial dysfunction occurred. Antiplatelet therapy should be tailored to the stent strategy used and bleeding risk. Discuss long‑term implications: chimney stents may complicate future coronary access and influence antiplatelet duration decisions.

Prevention-first approach: Because rescue is complex and outcomes worsen with delay and greater myocardial injury, current best practice emphasizes prevention through CT‑guided risk stratification and the use of prophylactic strategies (BASILICA, pre‑positioned stent, or guidewire protection) in patients judged to be at elevated risk.

Conclusion

Coronary obstruction during TAVI and ViV is a rare but severe complication driven by the interaction of valve leaflets, THV frame, and patient anatomy. Preprocedural CT assessment—measuring coronary height, sinus dimensions, leaflet length, and calculating VTC—is essential to stratify risk. When significant risk is identified, prophylactic or standby coronary protection strategies (wire, balloon, chimney stent) and leaflet laceration (BASILICA) are effective tools to preserve coronary flow. Operators must be prepared with rapid rescue algorithms, including immediate coronary wiring, ballooning/stenting, and escalation to mechanical circulatory support or surgery when required. Multidisciplinary planning, meticulous imaging, and procedural readiness are key to preventing catastrophic outcomes.
