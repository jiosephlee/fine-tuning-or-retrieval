Introduction

The interpretation of genetic variation is a foundational skill in modern clinical medicine. Missense variants—single-nucleotide changes that result in amino acid substitutions—are among the most common types of coding variation encountered in clinical sequencing. Determining whether a given missense change is benign or alters protein function in a way that contributes to disease requires synthesis of molecular biology, population genetics, biochemistry and clinical context. In silico or computational predictors are tools that estimate the likely functional impact of amino acid substitutions using information such as evolutionary conservation, predicted or known protein structure, and patterns learned from labeled variant sets. This chapter explains the principles behind these predictors, surveys commonly used scores and newer model types, examines strengths and limitations of computational evidence, and provides practical guidance for integrating predictive data with other evidence and for responsibly reporting computational results in a clinical context.

# Principles of in silico predictors (conservation, structural modeling, machine learning)

In silico predictors use one or more sources of information to infer whether a missense substitution will disrupt protein function. Three broad conceptual categories underlie most methods: sequence conservation, structural modeling, and machine learning approaches that combine heterogeneous features.

Sequence conservation. Evolutionary conservation is a central concept. Amino acid positions that are highly conserved across orthologs and paralogs are likely to be functionally important; substitutions at these sites are more likely to be deleterious. Conservation-based predictors typically use multiple sequence alignments (MSAs) spanning diverse species to compute metrics such as the information content or conservation score per column, and then assess whether the specific amino acid change is tolerated in the alignment. Methods differ by the depth and taxonomic range of their alignments (e.g., vertebrate-only vs. broad eukaryotic or even prokaryotic sampling) and by the evolutionary models applied. Conservation captures selective constraint but has limits: a residue may be conserved for reasons unrelated to the disease mechanism of interest (e.g., structural stability vs. ligand binding), and lineage-specific functions may not be reflected in broad MSAs.

Structural modeling. Protein structure provides a mechanistic link between sequence and function. Structural predictors assess whether a substitution is likely to perturb local packing, disrupt secondary structure, alter active sites, change protein–protein interaction interfaces, or affect post-translational modification motifs. Historically, structure-based methods required experimentally solved structures from crystallography or NMR, or high-quality homology models. Advances in high-accuracy structure prediction (notably AlphaFold and related tools) have greatly expanded coverage of structural contexts for human proteins. Structural features commonly used include solvent accessibility (buried vs. exposed residues), hydrogen bonding patterns, presence in catalytic or binding pockets, proximity to metal ions, and predicted changes in protein stability (ΔΔG). Structural predictors can be especially informative when a variant is located in a well-characterized functional domain or interface, but their reliability depends on the accuracy of the model and on whether the disease mechanism involves structure perturbation versus subtle regulatory changes.

Machine learning and integrative models. Many modern predictors use machine learning (ML) to integrate multiple features—conservation metrics, structural annotations, biochemical properties of amino acids (e.g., charge, polarity, size), sequence context, splice impact predictions, and population frequency signals—into a single score that estimates deleteriousness. Supervised learning models are trained on labeled variant datasets such as curated pathogenic and benign variants from databases (ClinVar, HGMD) or deeply mutational scanning results. Common ML architectures include logistic regression, random forests, gradient boosting, and deep neural networks. The model learns patterns that separate known pathogenic from benign variants and then generalizes to new variants. Training set composition, class imbalance (pathogenic variants are rarer and often overrepresented in curated datasets), and feature selection strongly influence performance and biases.

Each of the three principles—conservation, structure, and ML—offers complementary insights. Conservation provides a broad evolutionary signal; structure offers mechanistic interpretation; ML integrates many weak signals to produce a calibrated probability-like score. Understanding these underlying principles helps clinicians and laboratorians evaluate the plausibility of computational predictions and their likely failure modes.

# Common tools and scores (PolyPhen, SIFT, CADD, REVEL, newer models like AlphaMissense-type predictors)

A wide range of computational tools is available, from single-feature methods to ensemble predictors. Familiarity with commonly used scores and their interpretation is essential for clinical genetics.

SIFT (Sorting Intolerant From Tolerant). SIFT is one of the early widely used conservation-based predictors. It uses an MSA to estimate whether the substituted amino acid is tolerated at a given position. SIFT reports a score from 0 to 1; traditionally, scores ≤ 0.05 are considered damaging, and scores > 0.05 tolerated. SIFT’s strengths include simplicity and interpretability; limitations include dependence on MSA quality and poor performance for proteins or regions with limited homologs.

PolyPhen-2 (Polymorphism Phenotyping v2). PolyPhen integrates sequence conservation with structural information when available. It computes a qualitative classification ("benign", "possibly damaging", or "probably damaging") and a numeric score between 0 and 1; higher scores indicate higher probability of damaging effect. PolyPhen uses multiple features including position-specific independent counts (PSIC) scores from MSAs and structural annotations such as solvent accessibility. PolyPhen performed well among early predictors but shares limitations of training bias and sensitivity to alignment errors.

CADD (Combined Annotation Dependent Depletion). CADD is an ensemble method that scores variants genome-wide by integrating diverse annotations including conservation, regulatory features, and coding impact metrics. CADD produces a raw score and a scaled PHRED-like score (CADD-Phred) that ranks a variant relative to simulated variants. A CADD-Phred score of 10 corresponds to the top 10% of deleterious variants, 20 to the top 1%, and 30 to the top 0.1%. CADD is widely used for prioritization in research and diagnostics, particularly because it covers both coding and noncoding variants; however, it is not specifically optimized for missense classification and can be prone to false positives when used in isolation.

REVEL (Rare Exome Variant Ensemble Learner). REVEL is an ensemble classifier specifically designed for missense variants. It integrates outputs from multiple individual tools (e.g., SIFT, PolyPhen, MutationAssessor) and other features to produce a score between 0 and 1. REVEL was trained on a set of rare pathogenic and rare putatively neutral missense variants and has been shown to outperform many individual predictors for missense classification. Suggested thresholds vary by application; some studies use REVEL ≥ 0.5 or ≥ 0.7 for suspect pathogenicity, but these are heuristics rather than formal clinical cutoffs.

Other ensemble and meta-predictors. Numerous other tools exist, such as MetaLR, MetaSVM, M-CAP, MPC (missense badness, PolyPhen-2, and constraint), and more recent deep learning-based methods. Many ensembles aim to leverage complementary strengths of component predictors and achieve better discrimination.

AlphaMissense-type and deep-learning structural models. The advent of highly accurate protein structure prediction (AlphaFold2) and methods that directly predict missense variant impact using large language models or structural embeddings has led to a new class of predictors. AlphaMissense (a conceptual term here referencing models that exploit AlphaFold-derived features) and related predictors use the structural models and deep-learning representations of sequence and structure to predict variant effects at scale. These methods can predict impacts on stability, folding, or function even for proteins previously lacking experimental structures. While promising, they are relatively new, and independent benchmarking, calibration and incorporation into clinical workflows are ongoing.

Interpretation and score thresholds. Because different predictors use different scales and training objectives, numerical thresholds vary. For example, SIFT ≤ 0.05 is considered deleterious, PolyPhen score > 0.9 often flagged as "probably damaging", REVEL > 0.7 is sometimes used for likely pathogenicity in research, and CADD-Phred ≥ 20 is considered high. These cutoffs are heuristics and should not be treated as definitive. Some tools provide probabilistic interpretations or rank-based scaling (e.g., CADD-Phred), which can be more interpretable across datasets.

# Strengths and limitations of computational evidence for pathogenicity

Computational predictions offer several advantages: they are fast, inexpensive, scalable to whole exomes and genomes, and can prioritize variants for further study. They are especially useful for triaging large variant lists, identifying candidates for functional testing, and providing preliminary evidence in resource-limited contexts. Predictors can also provide mechanistic hypotheses (e.g., destabilizing the fold, disrupting active site) that guide laboratory assays.

However, computational evidence has important limitations that constrain its weight in clinical decisions. First, predictors are probabilistic and imperfect; false positives and false negatives are common. For example, a benign variant at a conserved site may be tolerated due to compensation elsewhere, while a pathogenic variant might occur at a nonconserved site by altering a lineage-specific function. Second, many predictors suffer from biases in their training sets: pathogenic variants in databases are often ascertained from severe, early-onset diseases and may not represent the spectrum of clinical effect sizes, while benign variant sets may include mislabeled or uncharacterized variants. This can skew model performance and calibration.

Third, predictors generally assess a limited set of mechanisms—changes in protein stability, catalytic residues, or binding interfaces. They are less sensitive to variants that cause disease through gain-of-function mechanisms, dominant-negative effects, altered regulation, or tissue-specific isoform disruption. Predictors may also miss splice-disrupting exonic variants (exonic variants that affect splicing enhancers/silencers), unless splice-aware features are included. Fourth, structural predictors depend on the quality of the underlying structural model; even high-confidence AlphaFold predictions may lack dynamic, multi-protein complex, or ligand-bound conformations necessary to predict certain effects. Fifth, different predictors often disagree; concordance among multiple tools increases confidence, but discordance is common and requires clinical interpretation.

Finally, computational evidence alone lacks direct demonstration of pathogenic mechanism and cannot substitute for population genetics, segregation data, or experimental functional assays. Clinically accepted variant classification frameworks, such as ACMG-AMP, assign computational predictions only supporting or supporting-level evidence (e.g., PP3 or BP4), reflecting these limitations. Thus computational predictions should be considered supportive but not decisive except in exceptional, well-validated contexts.

# Integration of computational data with other evidence (functional assays, segregation, population frequency)

Robust variant interpretation synthesizes computational predictions with population frequency, segregation, functional data, clinical phenotype, and other orthogonal evidence streams. Each evidence type has specific strengths and clinical decision thresholds.

Population frequency. Population allele frequency is a powerful filter. Databases such as gnomAD provide allele counts and maximum population allele frequencies across ancestries. If a variant’s observed frequency exceeds what is compatible with disease prevalence and penetrance, it argues strongly against pathogenicity. For example, for an autosomal dominant, fully penetrant severe disorder with prevalence \( 1 \) in \(10,000\), a variant with population frequency 1% is implausible as the sole cause. Tools and calculators (e.g., allele frequency thresholds computed using maximum credible population allele frequency based on disease prevalence and penetrance) can formalize this reasoning. Clinically, ACMG guidelines use BS1 (allele frequency greater than expected) to designate benign evidence. Computational predictors should be weighed in light of population frequency—an in silico deleterious prediction does not override high population frequency.

Segregation and family data. Co-segregation of a variant with disease in multiple affected family members, or de novo occurrence in a proband with consistent phenotype, provides strong evidence. Calculating LOD scores or using Bayesian frameworks to quantify segregation evidence helps integrate with other data. Computational predictions can prioritize variants for segregation testing, but segregation evidence stands independently and often carries more weight than in silico scores.

Functional assays. Well-validated functional assays that demonstrate a variant alters protein function consistent with disease mechanism provide high-level evidence (PS3/BS3 in ACMG criteria). The design and validation of assays are crucial: the assay must reflect the relevant biological context, have appropriate controls, and be reproducible. In vitro assays measuring biochemical activity, cellular assays assessing localization or signaling, and in vivo models can all be informative. Computational predictions are particularly useful for generating mechanistic hypotheses to design such assays—e.g., predicting that a variant destabilizes the fold suggests testing protein stability or expression levels.

Phenotype and clinical correlation. The variant must be considered in the clinical context. Does the patient’s phenotype fit the known spectrum for variants in the gene? Is the inheritance consistent? A variant predicted damaging in a gene associated with a discordant phenotype is less likely to be causal. Computational evidence gains weight when the mechanistic prediction aligns with clinical features—e.g., a variant predicted to impair a cardiac ion channel’s pore correlating with an arrhythmia phenotype.

Aggregate and orthogonal evidence. Ensemble approaches like Bayesian aggregation or decision frameworks that combine independent lines of evidence reduce reliance on any single imperfect source. For example, a variant with a high REVEL score, absent from gnomAD, de novo occurrence in an affected child, and functional assay demonstrating loss-of-function would collectively support pathogenic classification. Conversely, a variant predicted deleterious by one tool but common in population databases and lacking segregation should be considered benign or uncertain.

Reporting conditional findings. When computational evidence is the only available support (e.g., for a rare variant in a gene plausibly linked to the phenotype but lacking segregation or functional data), the variant is often classified as "variant of uncertain significance" (VUS). The report should clearly state the nature and limitations of computational predictions and, where possible, recommend follow-up steps such as family testing or targeted functional assays.

# Guidelines for the responsible use of predictive scores in reporting

Clinical laboratories and clinicians must use predictive scores responsibly to avoid overinterpretation and harm from misclassification. The following principles guide responsible use and reporting.

Use predictors as supporting, not definitive, evidence. Clinical variant classification frameworks (ACMG-AMP) designate computational predictions as supporting evidence: PP3 (multiple lines of computational evidence support a deleterious effect) or BP4 (multiple lines support benign impact). Computational evidence alone is rarely sufficient to call a variant pathogenic or benign; it should be integrated with population frequency, segregation, phenotype match, and functional data.

Prefer ensemble and well-validated tools for missense variants. Ensemble predictors trained specifically for missense variants (e.g., REVEL, M-CAP) have generally better discrimination than single-feature methods. Use of multiple complementary tools and attention to calibration improves interpretation. When possible, consult benchmarks and independent evaluations to choose predictors with demonstrated performance for the gene or disease context.

Document tool versions, thresholds, and rationale. Reports should state which predictors were used, software versions, databases and date of access, and the numeric scores and thresholds applied. Because algorithm versions and training data change over time, precise documentation supports reproducibility and reinterpretation. If specific thresholds are used (e.g., REVEL ≥ 0.7), justify their selection—whether based on literature, internal validation, or consensus guidelines.

Emphasize uncertainty and recommend follow-up. For variants relying primarily on computational evidence, classify conservatively (often VUS) and recommend clinical or laboratory follow-up: family segregation studies, targeted functional assays, or periodic reanalysis as new data emerge. Communicate that in silico predictions are hypotheses about functional impact, not proof of disease causation.

Consider disease mechanism and variant context. Some genes have well-characterized mechanisms (loss-of-function vs. gain-of-function) that influence how computational predictions should be interpreted. For example, in genes where haploinsufficiency causes disease, predictors that estimate loss-of-function propensity may be more relevant. In genes with complex splicing or multiple isoforms, consider whether the variant affects relevant transcripts. If a predictor’s mechanistic assumptions mismatch the disease mechanism (e.g., predictor flags destabilization but disease is gain-of-function), down-weight the in silico evidence.

Avoid overreliance on default categorical outputs. Many tools provide qualitative labels (benign, possibly damaging) that may invite categorical interpretation. Instead, consider the numeric score, its calibration, and the broader evidence landscape. Beware of over-interpreting small differences in score or of treating all tools as independent; many ensemble methods incorporate overlapping features and thus are not statistically independent lines of evidence.

Engage multidisciplinary review. Variant interpretation benefits from input by clinical geneticists, molecular diagnosticians, bioinformaticians, and disease-specific experts. Multidisciplinary variant review boards can adjudicate conflicting evidence, recommend assays, and prioritize variants for reclassification. When reporting to clinicians and patients, use clear language about the strength and limits of computational evidence.

Update interpretations over time. As population databases grow, new functional data accumulate, and predictors improve, variant classifications may change. Laboratories should have policies for periodic reanalysis of unsolved cases or variants with substantial clinical implications. Reports should indicate that reinterpretation is possible and provide pathways for clinicians to request updates.

Ethical and communication considerations. Misclassification can lead to inappropriate treatment, anxiety, or missed diagnoses. Reports should avoid alarmist language when evidence is weak and should provide actionable recommendations only when evidence is robust. For variants reported in secondary findings or carrier screening, the same conservative principles apply.

Practical checklist for reporting computational evidence
- State which tools were used, versions, databases and date of access.
- Provide numeric scores and the numeric thresholds applied for interpretation.
- Describe whether multiple tools agreed and whether they are independent.
- Place computational results in context with population frequency, segregation, clinical phenotype, and any functional data.
- If reporting a VUS supported mainly by computational evidence, recommend specific follow-up (family testing, targeted functional assays) and state that the evidence is provisional.

Conclusion

Computational prediction of missense variant impact is a powerful component of modern genomic medicine. Conservation, structural modeling, and machine learning provide complementary insights into how amino acid substitutions may alter protein function. Common tools—SIFT, PolyPhen, CADD, REVEL, and newer deep-learning structural predictors—offer convenient scores but vary in scope, calibration, and performance. Computational evidence is most valuable when used to prioritize variants and generate testable hypotheses; it has intrinsic limitations and should generally be treated as supporting, not conclusive, evidence in clinical classification. Responsible use requires transparent documentation, conservative interpretation aligned with disease mechanism and population data, multidisciplinary review, and recommendations for follow-up testing when needed. As structural prediction and deep learning continue to evolve, their integration into clinical workflows should be guided by independent validation, calibration, and ongoing re-evaluation against experimental and clinical gold standards.
