Introduction

Sleep-related breathing disorders comprise a spectrum of conditions in which abnormal respiratory patterns during sleep lead to disturbed gas exchange, recurrent sleep fragmentation, and downstream multisystem consequences. Within this spectrum, sleep apnoea syndromes are broadly categorised as obstructive, central, or mixed. Central sleep apnoea (CSA) and sleep-related hypoventilation due to failure of automatic brainstem respiratory drive represent a distinct pathophysiologic and clinical group from the much more common obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA). Understanding the mechanistic differences, the characteristic polysomnographic patterns, and the systemic consequences of central disorders of breathing is essential for accurate diagnosis and appropriate management. This chapter will define and contrast obstructive and central events, delve into the pathophysiology of central apnoea (including ventilatory control instability and loss of automatic drive), describe Cheyne–Stokes respiration and related central patterns, examine the effects of central apnoea on gas exchange, sleep architecture and cardiovascular risk, review causes of acquired central apnoea—emphasising structural brainstem lesions—and outline clinical features that distinguish central from obstructive events.

# Distinction between obstructive, central and mixed sleep apnoea

Sleep apnoea describes repetitive episodes of cessation (apnoea) or reduction (hypopnoea) of airflow during sleep. The key categorisation is based on the presence or absence of ventilatory effort during these events. Obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA) is characterised by continued or increased respiratory effort against an occluded or markedly narrowed upper airway; central sleep apnoea (CSA) is defined by absent or near-absent respiratory effort, reflecting transient failure of central respiratory motor output; mixed apnoea begins as central (no effort) and transitions to obstructive (resumption of effort against a blocked airway) within the same event.

Clinically and diagnostically, the distinction is made via polysomnography (PSG) that records airflow, respiratory effort (thoracic and abdominal inductance plethysmography or esophageal pressure), oxygen saturation, and often capnography. In OSA, episodes present with reduced or absent airflow while thoracoabdominal effort persists or paradoxically increases—esophageal pressure swings and inspiratory effort-related arousals are common. In CSA, both airflow and effort cease concurrently; there is little to no diaphragmatic or accessory muscle contraction during the apnoeic interval. Mixed apnoeas show an initial absence of effort followed by the onset of effort without restoration of airflow until the obstructive component resolves, often with an arousal.

Diagnostic criteria rely on the apnoea–hypopnoea index (AHI): apnoea is typically defined as a ≥90% reduction in airflow lasting at least 10 seconds; hypopnoea definitions vary but often use a ≥30% airflow reduction with ≥3%–4% oxygen desaturation or an arousal. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) recommends scoring central apnoea when there is a clear absence of inspiratory effort throughout the event. Mixed apnoeas are scored when a central component is followed by resumption of effort without airflow. Clinically, mixed events suggest a combination of upper airway vulnerability and central instability; they commonly occur in advanced OSA or in settings of opioid use or heart failure.

Understanding the distinction affects management. OSA primarily responds to therapies that stabilise the upper airway (continuous positive airway pressure, mandibular advancement devices, weight loss, upper airway surgery), whereas CSA management targets ventilatory control mechanisms, addressing underlying causes (heart failure optimisation, withdrawal of offending medications) and, when indicated, noninvasive ventilation strategies such as adaptive servo-ventilation (with important contraindications), supplemental oxygen, or acetazolamide. Mixed apnoea may require combined approaches.

# Pathophysiology of central sleep apnoea: ventilatory control instability and failure of automatic drive

The pathophysiology of central sleep apnoea centres on dysfunction in the control of ventilation. Two broad mechanisms are important: instability of ventilatory control (high loop gain) and failure of the automatic respiratory drive (reduced central respiratory output), which may be manifest as defective brainstem respiratory rhythm generation or impaired chemoreception.

Ventilatory control can be conceptualised as a negative feedback loop where changes in arterial carbon dioxide tension (PaCO2) and oxygen tension (PaO2) alter chemoreceptor activity, which in turn modulates ventilatory drive to maintain homeostasis. Loop gain is a dimensionless quantity that describes the propensity of the system to oscillate: a high loop gain implies that a small perturbation in ventilation causes an exaggerated corrective response, leading to periodic overshoots and undershoots (hyperventilation followed by hypocapnia-induced central apnoea). Patients with heart failure, stroke, or advanced age often have increased chemosensitivity (high controller gain) and prolonged circulation time (increasing feedback delay), both of which raise loop gain and predispose to Cheyne–Stokes respiration (CSR) and other periodic breathing patterns.

Increases in sensitivity of central and peripheral chemoreceptors to PaCO2 and PaO2 enhance ventilatory responses to minor changes, causing greater alternation between hyperpnea and central apnoea. For example, pulmonary congestion and neurohumoral activation in heart failure augment chemoreflex sensitivity. Prolonged circulatory time (reduced cardiac output) increases the delay between a ventilatory change and the corresponding change in sensed arterial blood gases, promoting instability because corrective ventilatory response arrives out of phase with the oscillation.

Failure of automatic drive refers to diminished baseline respiratory output from the brainstem respiratory network. During wakefulness, voluntary cortical inputs and behavioural drive can sustain ventilation even when automatic drive is impaired. During sleep—particularly non-rapid eye movement (NREM) sleep when behavioural drive wanes—loss of automatic output leads to apnoea or hypoventilation. Causes include structural lesions of the medulla or pons (ischaemia, haemorrhage, demyelination, tumour), degenerative diseases (e.g., multiple system atrophy, Parkinson-plus syndromes), metabolic suppression (severe hypothyroidism, drugs such as opioids or sedatives), and failure of chemoreceptor afferents. In congenital central hypoventilation syndrome (Ondine’s curse), genetic defects in PHOX2B alter development of autonomic and chemoreceptive neurons, producing severe loss of automatic respiratory drive that worsens during sleep.

Central hypoventilation and apnoea occur when the PaCO2 falls below the apnoeic threshold during sleep—i.e., the PaCO2 at which respiratory motor output ceases. Healthy individuals maintain PaCO2 in a range that lies above this threshold; however, in the setting of unstable ventilatory control, hyperventilatory overshoots can reduce PaCO2 below threshold, precipitating central apnoea until CO2 accumulates and drive resumes. Conversely, in true failure of automatic drive, baseline ventilatory output may be insufficient to maintain adequate alveolar ventilation, producing sustained hypoventilation with hypercapnia and hypoxemia without periodic hyperpnea.

Clinically, these mechanisms explain why CSA may present as periodic breathing (oscillatory pattern due to high loop gain) or as sustained hypoventilation (reduced automatic drive). The therapeutic implications are different: if CSA arises from high loop gain, interventions that stabilise ventilatory control—such as supplemental oxygen to blunt peripheral chemoreceptor sensitivity, carbonic anhydrase inhibitors (acetazolamide) to induce metabolic acidosis and raise baseline ventilation, or adaptive servo-ventilation to smooth ventilation—are useful. If CSA arises from structural failure of the respiratory centres, results are less predictable and may require ventilatory support that ensures adequate minute ventilation.

# Cheyne–Stokes respiration and other central patterns

Cheyne–Stokes respiration (CSR) is a classic form of central periodic breathing characterised by cyclic crescendo–decrescendo changes in tidal volume separated by central apnoeas or hypopnoeas. The cycle consists of a gradual increase in ventilation (crescendo), a peak of hyperventilation, a gradual decrease (decrescendo), and a central apnoeic or hypopnoeic phase. Cycle length is typically 30 seconds to 2 minutes, often around 45–90 seconds. CSR is strongly associated with congestive heart failure (CHF) with reduced ejection fraction, although it can occur in stroke, brainstem lesions, and high-altitude periodic breathing.

Mechanistically, CSR reflects instability in ventilatory control. Heart failure increases chemoreceptor sensitivity and prolongs circulation time; together these increase loop gain and feedback delay, facilitating large amplitude oscillations. During the hyperventilation phase PaCO2 falls below the apnoeic threshold, leading to central cessation of effort until PaCO2 rises sufficiently. The phenomenon is self-perpetuating in the context of underlying cardiopulmonary and chemoreflex abnormalities.

Other central breathing patterns include:

- Periodic breathing without full apnoea: rhythmic oscillations in tidal volume during sleep that may not reach criteria for apnoea but still cause fragmentation and gas exchange abnormalities.

- Primary central apnoea: isolated central apnoeas occurring in the absence of heart failure, medication effects, or structural brain disease; seen transiently at high altitude or during opioid withdrawal.

- Opioid-induced central apnoea and hypoventilation: opioids depress central respiratory drive and alter chemoreflex sensitivity, causing irregular breathing, central apnoeas, and sustained hypoventilation. Patterns may lack the regular oscillation seen with CSR.

- Complex sleep apnoea: arises when obstructive events are present and treated with positive airway pressure, unmasking or inducing central apnoeas—thought to reflect reduction in PaCO2 below apnoeic threshold upon correction of obstructive hypoventilation and reduction of arousals.

- Ataxic or cluster breathing: irregular, unpredictable apnoeas and varying tidal volumes with increasing severity indicating more severe brainstem dysfunction, often seen with structural lesions of the medulla.

Polysomnography demonstrates these patterns with concurrent assessment of thoracoabdominal effort. CSR is identified by the regular crescendo–decrescendo tidal volume pattern and cyclic central apnoeas. Recognising the pattern is clinically important because CSR in heart failure carries prognostic significance and may alter treatment choices.

# Impact of central apnoea on gas exchange, sleep architecture and cardiovascular risk

Central apnoea and hypoventilation have immediate effects on gas exchange. Central apnoeas produce transient reductions in alveolar ventilation, causing drops in oxygen saturation (SpO2) and rises in PaCO2 during the apnoeic interval. In periodic breathing, the alternating hyperventilation produces large swings in PaCO2 and oxygenation, with cumulative hypoxemic burden over the sleep period. Sustained central hypoventilation produces persistent hypercapnia and chronic hypoxemia, which may lead to compensatory changes in hemoglobin and pulmonary vasculature over time.

Sleep architecture is disrupted by central events via arousals triggered by chemoreflexes, hypoxemia, or intrinsic respiratory pattern termination. Frequent apnoeas and hypopneas fragment sleep, reduce slow-wave and REM sleep, and result in daytime sleepiness, impaired cognition, and reduced quality of life. Because central events can be associated with fewer large inspiratory efforts than obstructive events, they may cause less snoring but still provoke arousals via other mechanisms. Hypoventilation that predominates during REM—or in severe central hypoventilation disorders—can disproportionately impact sleep-stage-specific gas exchange, leading to REM-related nocturnal hypoventilation and morning headaches, somnolence, and cognitive dysfunction.

Cardiovascular consequences of central apnoea are complex and may be as significant as those from obstructive events. Recurrent hypoxemia, cyclic sympathetic activation on termination of apnoeas, and oscillations in intrathoracic pressure all influence autonomic balance. In CSR associated with heart failure, cyclical ventilatory changes produce oscillating sympathetic drive and may worsen arrhythmogenic risk and myocardial ischemia. Chronic intermittent hypoxia and reoxygenation promote oxidative stress, systemic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and atherosclerosis. Observationally, CSR and CSA in heart failure are associated with worse prognosis, heart failure progression, and increased mortality. The degree of prognostic impact depends on the underlying aetiology and severity; in heart failure patients with left ventricular dysfunction, the presence of CSR is an adverse prognostic sign.

Interventions that reduce apnoeic burden can improve sleep quality and may ameliorate some cardiovascular parameters. However, therapeutic trials have shown mixed effects on hard cardiovascular outcomes, and in some settings (e.g., adaptive servo-ventilation in certain heart failure populations with reduced ejection fraction), caution is required due to potential harm. Therefore, understanding the underlying pathophysiology and comorbid conditions is essential before instituting therapies aimed at CSA.

# Causes of acquired central apnoea including structural brainstem lesions

Acquired central apnoea can result from a broad array of conditions that either destabilise ventilatory control or impair the brainstem respiratory network directly.

Cardiac causes: Heart failure, particularly with reduced ejection fraction and prolonged circulation time, is strongly associated with CSR and central apnoea. Myocardial ischaemia and arrhythmias that reduce cardiac output can similarly predispose to periodic breathing.

Cerebrovascular and structural brain lesions: Ischaemic stroke (particularly brainstem or bilateral cortical strokes), intracerebral haemorrhage, demyelinating diseases (multiple sclerosis), tumours, and compressive lesions can damage respiratory centres in the medulla and pons. Site-specific lesions may affect the dorsal respiratory group (nucleus tractus solitarius), ventral respiratory column, pre-Bötzinger complex, and other components of respiratory rhythmogenesis or the afferent chemoreceptor pathways. Such structural insults can cause hypoventilation, ataxic breathing, or central apnoea depending on extent and localization.

Degenerative neurological diseases: Multiple system atrophy (MSA), amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), and certain Parkinson-plus syndromes can cause progressive loss of automatic respiratory drive and upper airway control. In MSA, autonomic dysfunction and degeneration of pontomedullary respiratory centres lead to nocturnal stridor, central apnoea, and sleep-related hypoventilation.

Medication and substance effects: Opioids and benzodiazepines directly depress central respiratory drive and reduce chemosensitivity to CO2 and hypoxia. Chronic opioid therapy, especially in high doses or when combined with other sedatives, produces central apnoeas and sustained hypoventilation. Sedative-hypnotics, alcohol, and some anticonvulsants can exacerbate central hypoventilation.

Metabolic and endocrine disorders: Severe hypothyroidism can depress metabolic rate and ventilatory drive, producing hypoventilation. Central nervous system infections, sepsis with encephalopathy, and acute metabolic derangements can also transiently impair respiratory drive.

High-altitude periodic breathing: Reduced ambient oxygen at altitude increases chemoreflex sensitivity and causes periodic breathing with central apnoeas in healthy individuals; this is reversible with acclimatisation.

Postoperative and opioid-related conditions: After surgery, residual opioid analgesia, sedatives, and sleep deprivation predispose to central apnoea. Sleep-related hypoventilation may be unmasked in the postoperative period.

Congenital central hypoventilation syndrome: A rare genetic disorder (PHOX2B mutations) causes failure of automatic respiratory control, typically manifesting in infancy but with lifelong implications.

In the setting of structural brainstem lesions, the clinical presentation can include ataxic breathing (irregular amplitude and timing), cluster breathing, or persistent central apnoea. Lesions affecting the pre-Bötzinger complex tend to produce severe central hypoventilation; lesions in the nucleus tractus solitarius may disrupt chemoreceptor afferents. Identifying structural causes requires neuroimaging (MRI brainstem-focused) and correlation with other neurological signs.

# Clinical features that differentiate central from obstructive events

Differentiating central from obstructive events begins with clinical history and physical examination, but definitive differentiation requires polysomnography with respiratory effort monitoring. Clinically, OSA is strongly associated with loud snoring, witnessed apnoeas with gasping or choking, obesity, and anatomical risk factors for upper airway collapse (short neck, large tongue, retrognathia). CSA is more likely when there is minimal snoring, episodes occur in the context of heart failure, stroke, opioid use, or known brainstem disease, or when apnoeas are predominantly central on prior testing.

Key symptomatic distinctions: OSA often presents with excessive daytime sleepiness, morning headaches, and impaired concentration, though CSA can cause similar non-specific daytime symptoms. Orthopnea and paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea may point toward heart failure with possible CSR. A history of opioid use, neuromuscular disease, or prior brainstem insult raises suspicion for CSA or hypoventilation. In CSA due to hypoventilation, complaints of morning headache, daytime somnolence, and cognitive slowing are common; in CSR, breathlessness and symptoms of heart failure predominate.

On physical exam, features that support OSA include obesity (BMI > 30 kg/m^2), large neck circumference (> 40 cm in men is a commonly used threshold), crowded oropharynx (Mallampati score), and nasal obstruction. Neurological signs—cranial nerve deficits, ataxia, limb weakness, autonomic dysfunction—suggest central causes and warrant neurology referral and neuroimaging.

Polysomnographic and respiratory monitoring features: Central apnoeas are identified when airflow cessation is accompanied by absence of thoracoabdominal effort throughout the event. Obstructive apnoeas show continued or increased effort against absent airflow; often there are large negative intrathoracic pressure swings (esophageal pressure more negative) preceding arousal. The presence of a crescendo–decrescendo ventilatory pattern with cyclical central apnoeas is characteristic of CSR. In sustained hypoventilation, capnography shows elevated transcutaneous or end-tidal CO2 that remains high throughout sleep. Overnight oximetry alone cannot reliably distinguish central from obstructive events, but the pattern of oscillating desaturations without snoring may hint at central periodic breathing.

Ancillary tests and clinical decision points: When CSA is suspected, assess for contributing causes—obtain a focused cardiac evaluation (echocardiography to assess ejection fraction and exclude significant heart failure), review medications (opioids, sedatives), and consider neuroimaging if focal neurological signs or history suggests structural brainstem disease. Measure arterial blood gases if hypoventilation is suspected; chronic hypercapnia (PaCO2 > 45 mmHg) during wakefulness indicates chronic hypoventilation. Overnight monitoring of CO2 (transcutaneous or end-tidal) during PSG is increasingly recommended to detect hypoventilation; target normal awake PaCO2 is approximately 35–45 mmHg.

Clinical decision points include: if CSA occurs in heart failure with reduced ejection fraction, optimise guideline-directed medical therapy and consider nocturnal oxygen or positive airway pressure after evaluation; in opioid-induced CSA, reduce or eliminate opioid dose where feasible and consider respiratory stimulant therapies; in suspected brainstem lesion, urgent neuroimaging is indicated; and in sustained hypoventilation with daytime hypercapnia, noninvasive ventilation that guarantees adequate minute ventilation (e.g., bilevel ventilation or volume-assured modes) is generally required.

In summary, the differentiation hinges on the presence or absence of respiratory effort during events (best documented on PSG), clinical context (cardiac disease, neurological disease, opioids, obesity), and objective measurement of gas exchange including overnight CO2 monitoring. Accurate classification guides therapy and prognostication.

Conclusion

Central sleep apnoea and related hypoventilation syndromes represent a mechanistically distinct group of sleep-disordered breathing disorders driven by ventilatory control instability or failure of automatic brainstem respiratory drive. Recognising the characteristic patterns—Cheyne–Stokes respiration, periodic breathing, sustained hypoventilation—and linking them to underlying causes (heart failure, structural brainstem lesions, medications, degenerative disease) is essential for targeted management. Polysomnography with respiratory effort and CO2 monitoring, coupled with clinical evaluation for cardiac and neurological comorbidities, frames diagnostic and therapeutic decisions. Understanding the pathophysiologic basis informs appropriate interventions and clarifies why therapies effective for obstructive disease may be inadequate or contraindicated in central disorders.
