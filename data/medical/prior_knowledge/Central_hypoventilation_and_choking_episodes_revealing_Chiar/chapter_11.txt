Introduction

Pulmonary function testing (PFT) is an indispensable set of bedside and laboratory tools for evaluating dyspnea, ventilatory failure, and suspected respiratory muscle impairment. In patients with neuromuscular disease (NMD) or central causes of hypoventilation, the dominant problem is inadequate generation of ventilatory pressure and volumes rather than primary abnormalities of airways or alveolar gas-exchange. Correct interpretation of standard spirometry, static lung volumes, diffusion capacity, and specific measures of respiratory muscle strength is therefore essential to distinguish primary pulmonary disease from neuromuscular or central causes of dyspnea and to determine timing for interventions such as noninvasive ventilation (NIV). This chapter presents a clinically oriented synthesis of the physiological bases of common PFT measurements, practical performance points, normative values and diagnostic criteria, patterns seen in neuromuscular weakness versus primary lung disease, special tests of diaphragm and inspiratory function, and monitoring thresholds that influence clinical decision-making.

# Standard spirometry: FEV1, FVC, FEV1/FVC ratio and patterns (restrictive vs obstructive)

Spirometry is the foundational pulmonary function test and measures dynamic airflow during forced expiratory maneuvers. Key indices are forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1), forced vital capacity (FVC), and the ratio FEV1/FVC. FEV1 is the volume exhaled in the first second of a maximal forced expiration started from full inspiration; FVC is the total volume exhaled during that maneuver. The FEV1/FVC ratio reflects the proportion of lung volume that can be exhaled quickly and is central to distinguishing obstructive from non-obstructive defects.

Interpretation routinely uses absolute values, percent predicted (based on age, sex, height, ethnicity), and lower limits of normal (LLN). An FEV1/FVC below the LLN (or often simplified as <0.70 in adults) indicates airflow obstruction. Severity of obstruction is commonly staged by percent predicted FEV1 (e.g., mild ≥80%, moderate 50–79%, severe 30–49%, very severe <30%), although disease-specific guidelines differ.

Restrictive physiology on spirometry is suggested when FVC is reduced but the FEV1/FVC ratio is normal or increased (because both FEV1 and FVC are proportionately reduced). Spirometry alone, however, cannot confirm restriction because a reduced FVC may reflect poor effort, air-trapping with small airway obstruction, or technical factors. Confirmation requires measurement of static lung volumes (total lung capacity, TLC). Typical numerical cues: an isolated FVC reduction with normal/increased FEV1/FVC should prompt lung volume testing.

In neuromuscular weakness, the common spirometric pattern is a proportional reduction in FVC and FEV1 with preserved or increased FEV1/FVC ratio. For example, as inspiratory and expiratory muscle strength declines, FVC falls because maximal inflation is limited, and FEV1 falls in proportion because the lungs contain less volume to exhale. This pattern mimics restrictive disease on spirometry but differs mechanistically: parenchymal restriction reduces TLC and often produces reduced DLCO (diffusing capacity), whereas NMD primarily reduces respiratory muscle capacity with relatively preserved lung parenchyma and DLCO (unless coexisting pulmonary disease). Note that small airways disease may also produce a low FVC with normal ratio (pseudo-restriction) — hence the importance of full lung volumes.

Technical and clinical considerations: ensure maximal and reproducible effort, use seated and supine measurements when diaphragmatic weakness is suspected (a fall in FVC of >10–25% from seated to supine suggests diaphragmatic dysfunction), and interpret values relative to predicted using appropriate reference equations. In chronic neuromuscular disease, progressive declines in FVC over serial testing are often the most informative signal of disease progression.

# Maximal inspiratory and expiratory pressures (MIP/MEP) as markers of respiratory muscle strength

Maximal inspiratory pressure (MIP) and maximal expiratory pressure (MEP) are simple volitional measures of global inspiratory and expiratory muscle strength measured at the mouth. MIP (sometimes called PImax) is measured from residual volume with the subject performing a maximal inspiratory effort against an occluded airway; MEP (PEmax) is measured from total lung capacity with a maximal expiratory effort. Both are expressed in cmH2O and are highly reproducible when performed correctly.

Physiological significance: MIP primarily reflects the strength of the diaphragm and other inspiratory muscles (external intercostals, accessory muscles), while MEP predominantly reflects abdominal and internal intercostal muscle strength needed for cough and secretion clearance. Because the tests are effort-dependent, coaching and repeatable trials are required; the highest value from at least three acceptable efforts generally is recorded.

Reference values and interpretation: Normal ranges vary with age, sex, and methodology. Typical adult reference ranges are approximately:
- MIP: men about -80 to -120 cmH2O, women about -60 to -80 cmH2O (values are negative because they are measured relative to atmospheric pressure with inspiratory negative pressure).
- MEP: men about +120 to +200 cmH2O, women about +80 to +140 cmH2O.

Because of population variance and technique sensitivity, interpretation uses percent predicted and clinical thresholds. Common interpretive cut-points in neuromuscular practice:
- MIP (more positive/less negative) greater than about -60 to -80 cmH2O is generally considered acceptable; values less negative than -60 cmH2O suggest clinically significant inspiratory muscle weakness, and values less negative than -30 cmH2O indicate severe weakness associated with risk of hypoventilation and respiratory failure.
- MEP values less than +60–80 cmH2O indicate impaired expiratory muscle strength and an impaired ability to cough or clear secretions; MEP <40 cmH2O denotes high risk for ineffective cough.

Limitations and practical tips: Because MIP/MEP require patient cooperation and appropriate technique, low results must be interpreted in context. False low values can result from submaximal effort, poor mouth seal, or facial weakness; in bulbar dysfunction, oral pressures may be underestimated. Nonvolitional tests (phrenic nerve stimulation, transdiaphragmatic pressures) can be considered when volitional measures are unreliable.

Clinical utility: MIP is useful for screening and serial monitoring in NMD, for prognostication, and for timing of interventions (e.g., initiation of NIV, cough augmentation). MEP identifies patients at risk for ineffective cough and guides use of mechanical in-exsufflation or assisted cough devices.

# Indications for full lung volumes and diffusion capacity testing

Full pulmonary function testing beyond spirometry includes measurement of static lung volumes (total lung capacity, residual volume) and single-breath diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide (DLCO). These tests are indicated whenever spirometry suggests restriction, when there is discordance between clinical assessment and spirometry, or when assessment of diffusion or air-trapping is required.

Indications in the context of neuromuscular and central causes include:
- Confirmation of restriction suggested by reduced FVC and normal/increased FEV1/FVC on spirometry. Measurement of TLC is required to confirm true restrictive lung disease (TLC < LLN).
- Differentiation of parenchymal restrictive disease (e.g., pulmonary fibrosis) from extrapulmonary or neuromuscular causes. In parenchymal restriction, both TLC and DLCO are typically reduced (DLCO often reduced to a greater degree than expected), whereas isolated respiratory muscle weakness typically causes decreased TLC with relatively preserved DLCO.
- Quantification of air-trapping or hyperinflation when small airways disease or COPD is a consideration. Elevated residual volume or increased RV/TLC ratio suggests obstructive physiology with gas trapping.
- Assessment of diffusion capacity (DLCO) when gas-exchange impairment is suspected (e.g., interstitial lung disease, pulmonary vascular disease). DLCO is a sensitive indicator of alveolar-capillary membrane or pulmonary vascular pathology and is often preserved in pure neuromuscular disease unless there is concomitant lung disease.
- Preoperative assessment prior to major surgery in patients with NMD, when accurate evaluation of ventilatory reserve is necessary.

Interpretation essentials: TLC confirms restriction; TLC < LLN establishes restrictive physiology. Typical normal TLC values vary by sex, height, and age; percent predicted and LLN values from reference equations should be used. DLCO is usually reported as ml/min/mmHg and as percent predicted; normal DLCO typically is around 80–120% predicted, with LLN commonly at about 80% predicted depending on the equation. A reduced DLCO (<60–70% predicted or below LLN) suggests significant parenchymal or vascular disease. KCO (DLCO corrected for alveolar volume) can help distinguish reduced DLCO due to reduced alveolar volume vs impaired membrane conductance.

Testing modalities: Lung volumes can be measured by body plethysmography (preferred, especially when obstruction is present), helium dilution, or nitrogen washout. DLCO requires appropriate correction for hemoglobin and carboxyhemoglobin; anemia reduces DLCO and must be accounted for.

# Interpretation of tests in neuromuscular weakness versus primary pulmonary disease

Distinguishing neuromuscular respiratory impairment from primary pulmonary disease requires integrating spirometry, lung volumes, DLCO, respiratory muscle testing, clinical features, and imaging.

Pattern recognition:
- Neuromuscular disease: Characterized by reduced FVC and TLC reflecting inability to inflate the chest, with a preserved or increased FEV1/FVC ratio. DLCO is typically normal or near-normal when expressed per unit alveolar volume (KCO may be preserved), because the alveolar-capillary membrane is intact. Chest imaging (radiograph/CT) is often unremarkable for parenchymal disease, though chronic atelectasis from hypoventilation may be present. Supine FVC falls relative to upright FVC; a decline of >10–25% is strongly suggestive of diaphragmatic weakness. MIP and MEP are reduced. Patients may have hypoventilation (hypercapnia) especially during sleep, with nocturnal desaturation and morning headaches.
- Primary restrictive lung disease (interstitial lung disease): Presents with reduced TLC and FVC, often disproportionately reduced DLCO (reflecting decreased alveolar-capillary surface area), and radiographic/CT evidence of parenchymal fibrosis. FEV1/FVC ratio may be normal or increased. Gas-exchange impairment (hypoxemia) is often present at rest or with exertion. MIP/MEP may be preserved or only secondarily reduced due to deconditioning.
- Obstructive disease (COPD, asthma): Shows reduced FEV1, reduced FEV1/FVC ratio below LLN, and air-trapping with increased residual volume and possible hyperinflation (increased TLC in emphysema). DLCO is decreased in emphysema. Spirometric response to bronchodilator may help distinguish asthma from fixed obstruction.

Key clinical decision points:
- When spirometry shows reduced FVC with preserved ratio, confirm TLC by plethysmography to distinguish true restriction from pseudo-restriction due to airflow obstruction.
- If DLCO is normal and TLC is reduced with low MIP/MEP, neuromuscular causes are favored.
- Evaluate the seated-to-supine FVC change: a substantial fall supports diaphragmatic involvement.
- Consider arterial blood gas or capillary blood gas measurement to document hypercapnia (PaCO2 >45 mmHg) in ventilatory failure; nocturnal capnography or polysomnography may detect sleep hypoventilation earlier than daytime hypercapnia.

# Role of sniff nasal inspiratory pressure and diaphragmatic function tests

Sniff nasal inspiratory pressure (SNIP) and more direct measures of diaphragmatic function provide additional, sometimes more sensitive tests of inspiratory muscle and diaphragmatic performance, particularly when MIP is difficult to obtain (e.g., bulbar weakness, poor mouth seal, facial weakness).

SNIP: SNIP measures the negative pressure generated in the nasal cavity during a maximal sniff through an unobstructed nostril, using a small pressure probe. It is less dependent on lip/mouth seal and often better tolerated. Normal SNIP values again vary by sex and age; approximate thresholds used clinically:
- Normal SNIP: men often >60–80 cmH2O, women >40–60 cmH2O.
- SNIP <40 cmH2O is commonly regarded as indicative of inspiratory muscle weakness and correlates with risk of nocturnal hypoventilation in neuromuscular disease.

SNIP has particular value in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) and other NMDs where MIP may be underestimated. SNIP correlates with MIP and with transdiaphragmatic pressure in many studies.

Diaphragmatic function tests: Several techniques assess diaphragmatic strength and motion more directly:
- Maximal transdiaphragmatic pressure (Pdi) measured by placing pressure transducers in the esophagus and stomach and calculating the difference during maximal inspiratory effort or magnetic/phrenic nerve stimulation. Pdi is the gold-standard physiologic measure of diaphragmatic force but is invasive and mainly used in research or specialized centers.
- Twitch transdiaphragmatic pressure during phrenic nerve stimulation (electrical or magnetic) provides a nonvolitional assessment of diaphragmatic contractility and can confirm weakness when volitional tests are equivocal.
- Ultrasound assessment of diaphragmatic motion and thickening fraction provides noninvasive bedside assessment: reduced diaphragmatic excursion on sniff or quiet breathing, or a low inspiratory thickening fraction (percentage change in diaphragm thickness between end-expiration and end-inspiration), supports diaphragmatic dysfunction. Typical cutoffs vary; a thickening fraction <20–30% is often considered abnormal, though normative values depend on methodology and patient population.
- Fluoroscopic "sniff test" (radiographic observation of paradoxical diaphragmatic motion) historically used for unilateral paralysis, but less commonly performed now because of ultrasound availability.

Clinical utility: Use SNIP or diaphragmatic ultrasound when mouth pressures are unreliable, when you need to detect early inspiratory muscle involvement, or to evaluate asymptomatic patients with neuromuscular disorders. A falling SNIP or a declining diaphragmatic thickening fraction over serial measurements indicates progressive diaphragmatic dysfunction and should prompt consideration of sleep studies and ventilatory support.

# Serial monitoring and thresholds that prompt ventilatory support

Respiratory function in neuromuscular and central disorders often declines gradually, and serial testing provides the best insight into progression and timing for interventions. Regular monitoring intervals depend on disease trajectory: rapidly progressive conditions (e.g., ALS) may require assessments every 1–3 months, whereas more slowly progressive disorders might be monitored every 6–12 months.

Key measurements to follow serially:
- FVC in upright and supine positions. A decline in FVC over time is a robust marker of disease progression. Significant changes are often defined as a fall of ≥10% absolute or ≥20% relative decline in FVC over a period (use same technique and effort).
- MIP/MEP and SNIP—declining pressures indicate progressive respiratory muscle impairment.
- Nocturnal oxygen saturation and transcutaneous or end-tidal CO2 monitoring to detect nocturnal hypoventilation before daytime hypercapnia ensues.
- Cough peak flow and MEP to assess ability to clear secretions.

Common clinical thresholds that prompt consideration of NIV, cough-assist devices, or further evaluation:
- Vital capacity (VC or FVC) falling below 50% of predicted is a widely used threshold to evaluate for nocturnal hypoventilation and to consider baseline respiratory support and sleep studies. Some centers use absolute VC <1.0 L or <20 mL/kg as a practical indicator of risk for respiratory failure.
- A reduction in supine FVC by >25% relative to upright FVC strongly suggests diaphragmatic weakness and warrants prompt consideration of sleep-disordered breathing evaluation.
- MIP less negative than -60 cmH2O indicates significant inspiratory weakness; values less negative than -30 cmH2O are associated with high risk of ventilatory failure. MEP <60–80 cmH2O or cough peak flow <160 L/min (some use <270 L/min depending on source) indicates ineffective cough and the need for cough augmentation strategies; a cough peak flow <160 L/min is often considered the threshold below which secretion clearance is ineffective and mechanical cough assistance should be provided.
- Evidence of nocturnal hypoventilation—commonly defined as transcutaneous or end-tidal CO2 >55 mmHg for ≥10 minutes, or sustained increases from daytime baseline, or sustained oxygen desaturations during sleep—should prompt initiation of nocturnal NIV even in the absence of daytime hypercapnia.
- Daytime hypercapnia (PaCO2 >45–50 mmHg) or hypoxemia that is exertional or at rest indicates respiratory pump failure and requires urgent assessment for ventilatory support.
- Symptoms such as orthopnea, morning headache, unrefreshing sleep, daytime somnolence, and cognitive impairment alongside objective declines in FVC or MIP increase the urgency to consider NIV.

Clinical decision-making must be individualized. For example, progressively symptomatic patients with FVC in the range 50–80% predicted but with orthopnea, declining SNIP, or early nocturnal hypercapnia may need earlier initiation of nocturnal NIV. Conversely, asymptomatic patients with mildly reduced FVC may be observed more conservatively with frequent reassessment.

# Integration into clinical practice and practical considerations

Interpreting PFTs in patients with neuromuscular or central hypoventilation involves synthesizing test results with clinical presentation, imaging, and blood gas measurements. Important practical points include:
- Always consider effort and technique: poor effort, air leaks, or facial/bulbar weakness can invalidate mouth pressure measurements. Supplement volitional tests with SNIP, ultrasound, or phrenic nerve stimulation when appropriate.
- Measure seated and supine FVC to detect diaphragmatic weakness.
- Use body plethysmography to confirm restriction and quantify residual volume when obstruction or air-trapping is a possibility.
- Adjust DLCO for hemoglobin and smoking status when interpreting results.
- Monitor trends rather than isolated values; serial declines often matter more than single measurements.
- Combine objective thresholds (e.g., VC <50% predicted, MIP less negative than -60 cmH2O, SNIP <40 cmH2O, cough peak flow <160 L/min) with symptoms and nocturnal studies to time initiation of NIV and secretion management strategies.

Summary

Pulmonary function testing provides a spectrum of tools—from simple spirometry and mouth pressures to advanced lung volume, diffusion, and diaphragmatic assessments—that allow clinicians to distinguish between primary pulmonary disease and ventilatory pump failure due to neuromuscular or central causes, to detect early hypoventilation (especially during sleep), and to time interventions. Understanding the physiology behind FEV1, FVC, TLC, DLCO, MIP, MEP, SNIP, and more direct measures of diaphragmatic function is essential. Regular, standardized serial monitoring and attention to established thresholds (VC, MIP/MEP, SNIP, cough peak flow, nocturnal CO2) form the backbone of respiratory care in patients at risk of respiratory muscle failure.
