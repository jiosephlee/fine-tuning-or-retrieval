# Introduction

Monitoring respiratory status at the bedside and in the laboratory is a core skill for clinicians who care for acutely ill patients, those with chronic respiratory disease, and patients receiving sedation or respiratory support. Oxygenation and ventilation are distinct physiological processes: oxygenation refers to the loading of oxygen onto hemoglobin and the oxygen content of arterial blood, whereas ventilation refers to the movement of carbon dioxide out of the body and the control of arterial carbon dioxide tension. The clinician must be able to integrate noninvasive bedside monitors (pulse oximetry, capnography, transcutaneous CO2), nocturnal studies, and arterial blood gas (ABG) results to recognize hypoxemia, hypercapnia, ventilation–perfusion mismatch, and to guide therapy. This chapter explains the principles, strengths and limitations of common monitoring modalities and provides a framework for interpreting ABGs and the alveolar–arterial gradient, with a focus on the situations in which pulse oximetry may fail to detect important ventilatory problems.

# Pulse oximetry technology, accuracy, artefacts and limitations (motion, poor perfusion, sensor type)

Pulse oximetry is a noninvasive method to estimate arterial hemoglobin oxygen saturation (SpO2). The device uses two wavelengths of light—typically red (~660 nm) and infrared (~940 nm)—transmitted through a vascular bed (finger, earlobe, forehead). The technology relies on the different absorption spectra of oxyhemoglobin and deoxyhemoglobin and on the pulsatile nature of arterial flow to segregate arterial from venous and tissue absorption. The ratio of pulsatile changes at the two wavelengths is mapped to an SpO2 value by device-specific calibration curves derived from healthy volunteers.

Accuracy and performance
Most modern pulse oximeters have accuracy characterized by an root mean square (RMS) error or ARMS, typically ±2% for saturations between 90% and 100% under good conditions. Accuracy degrades below SpO2 of 80%, where error may be considerably larger. Important performance characteristics:

- Precision tends to be better in the 90–100% range than at lower saturations.
- Many devices report only functional oxygen saturation (percentage of hemoglobin bound to oxygen) and cannot distinguish dyshemoglobinemias (carboxyhemoglobin, methemoglobin) or significant anemia.
- SpO2 is an estimate of SaO2 (arterial oxygen saturation measured from arterial blood). Discrepancies can occur because SpO2 is influenced by probe placement, perfusion, and optical interference.

Common artefacts and limitations
- Motion artefact: patient movement causes fluctuating light absorption and can create false desaturation or spurious readings. Motion-resistant algorithms and signal processing reduce but do not eliminate this problem.
- Poor perfusion: vasoconstriction (cold extremities, shock, vasopressors), low cardiac output, or peripheral vascular disease reduces the pulsatile signal amplitude, decreasing accuracy and often causing dropouts or erroneous low SpO2 readings.
- Sensor type and site: disposable finger clips, reusable probes, forehead reflectance sensors, and ear probes differ in susceptibility to motion and perfusion. Forehead sensors (reflectance) may perform better during poor peripheral perfusion because forehead perfusion is preserved by central circulation; earlobe sensors may be preferable during vasoconstriction. Probe integrity (dirty or broken sensor, ambient light leakage) produces artefacts.
- Ambient light and electromagnetic interference: strong external light sources, infrared heaters, or electrocautery can cause erroneous readings unless probe shielding is adequate.
- Dyshemoglobinemias: carboxyhemoglobin (CO-Hb) absorbs light similarly to oxyhemoglobin at certain wavelengths, causing SpO2 to overestimate true SaO2 in carbon monoxide poisoning. Methemoglobinemia yields readings around 85% regardless of true oxygenation because absorption characteristics produce a fixed ratio at the two wavelengths used.
- Pigmentation and nail polish: heavy pigmentation or dark nail polish (blue/black) and some nail coatings can lower absorbance and produce false readings. Red and pink polishes less commonly interfere. Removing nail polish or using alternate sites can help.
- Low saturation range: at SpO2 below about 80%, the pulse oximeter calibration is less reliable and device-to-device variability increases.

Clinical implications
Clinicians must understand that SpO2 is a trend monitor rather than an absolute diagnostic test under many conditions. Rapid changes in SpO2 are valuable for detecting acute hypoxemia but should prompt consideration of artefact before escalating therapy. If poor perfusion, motion, suspected CO or methemoglobinemia, or clinical mismatch between SpO2 and patient appearance occur, arterial blood gas with co-oximetry is indicated.

# Arterial blood gas (ABG) interpretation: PaO2, PaCO2, pH, alveolar–arterial gradient

Arterial blood gas analysis provides direct measurement of PaO2, PaCO2, arterial pH, and calculated bicarbonate (HCO3−) and base excess. Integrating these values allows assessment of oxygenation, ventilation, acid–base disturbance, and efficiency of gas exchange.

Normal reference ranges
- pH: 7.35–7.45
- PaCO2: 35–45 mmHg
- PaO2: 80–100 mmHg (on room air at sea level)
- HCO3−: 22–26 mEq/L (mmol/L)
- SaO2: 95–100% (corresponding PaO2 varies with oxyhemoglobin dissociation curve)
These ranges vary with age, altitude, and comorbid conditions.

PaO2 and oxygenation
PaO2 quantifies arterial oxygen tension. Clinical interpretation requires knowledge of the fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2), altitude, and the alveolar gas equation to identify whether hypoxemia is due to hypoventilation, diffusion impairment, ventilation–perfusion (V/Q) mismatch, or shunt.

Alveolar gas equation and the A–a gradient
The alveolar gas equation expresses alveolar oxygen tension \(PAO_2\):

\(PAO_2 = F_{IO_2} \times (P_{b} - P_{H_2O}) - \dfrac{PaCO_2}{R}\)

where \(P_b\) is barometric pressure (760 mmHg at sea level), \(P_{H_2O}\) is water vapor pressure at body temperature (47 mmHg), and \(R\) is the respiratory quotient (commonly 0.8). For room air (\(F_{IO_2}=0.21\)) at sea level, a convenient form is:

\(PAO_2 \approx 0.21 \times 713 - \dfrac{PaCO_2}{0.8} \approx 150 - \dfrac{PaCO_2}{0.8}\)

The alveolar–arterial (A–a) gradient is:

\(A\text{-}a\ gradient = PAO_2 - PaO_2\)

Normal A–a gradient increases with age. A practical estimate is:

Expected A–a gradient ≈ \( \dfrac{age}{4} + 4 \) mmHg

An elevated A–a gradient implies impaired oxygen transfer due to V/Q mismatch, diffusion limitation, or intracardiac/intrapulmonary shunt. A normal A–a gradient with hypoxemia suggests hypoventilation or low inspired oxygen.

PaCO2 and ventilation
PaCO2 is a direct measure of alveolar ventilation in steady state. PaCO2 is inversely proportional to minute ventilation:

\(PaCO_2 \propto \dfrac{CO_2 \ production}{alveolar\ ventilation}\)

Acute changes in PaCO2 typically reflect changes in minute ventilation. Chronic respiratory disorders produce renal compensation with changes in bicarbonate.

pH and acid–base interpretation
Interpretation of acid–base status integrates pH, PaCO2, and HCO3−. The Henderson–Hasselbalch equation links pH to these variables. Clinical rules of thumb:

- Acute respiratory acidosis: every 10 mmHg rise in PaCO2 is associated with an acute fall in HCO3− of ~1 mEq/L; chronic (renal compensation) rise increases HCO3− by ~3–4 mEq/L per 10 mmHg.
- Acute respiratory alkalosis: every 10 mmHg fall in PaCO2 reduces HCO3− by ~2 mEq/L acutely; chronic compensation reduces HCO3− by ~4–5 mEq/L per 10 mmHg.
- Metabolic acidosis: expected respiratory compensation (Winter’s formula) PaCO2 ≈ \(1.5 \times [HCO_3^-] + 8 \pm 2\) mmHg.
- Metabolic alkalosis: expected PaCO2 increases by ~0.6–0.7 mmHg per 1 mEq/L rise in HCO3− (acute estimates vary).

Clinical use of ABG
Obtain ABG when there is unexplained hypoxemia, sudden deterioration, suspected acid–base disorder, or need to measure PaCO2 directly (suspected hypoventilation). Always interpret ABG in clinical context, considering oxygen therapy and timing relative to interventions.

# End-tidal and transcutaneous CO2 monitoring: principles and clinical uses

End-tidal CO2 (EtCO2) monitoring and transcutaneous CO2 (PtcCO2) are noninvasive methods to estimate ventilation and are used in different clinical contexts.

End-tidal CO2 (capnography)
Principles: Capnography measures CO2 in exhaled gas, presenting a continuous waveform and a numeric EtCO2 value—the maximum CO2 concentration at end-expiration. Main technologies are mainstream (sensor in airway circuit) and sidestream (small sample aspirated to analyzer). The EtCO2 approximates PaCO2 because CO2 diffuses from pulmonary capillaries into alveoli during expiration and reaches a peak at end-expiration.

Typical relationship and normal values: In healthy lungs, PaCO2 - EtCO2 (the arterial to end-tidal gradient) is usually 2–5 mmHg, with EtCO2 slightly lower than PaCO2. EtCO2 values of 35–45 mmHg are often considered normal in adult ventilated patients.

Clinical uses:
- Verification of endotracheal tube placement immediately after intubation (sustained EtCO2 indicates tracheal intubation).
- Continuous monitoring of ventilation in intubated patients and during procedural sedation or anesthesia.
- Monitoring quality of CPR: presence and waveform trends of EtCO2 during resuscitation correlate with cardiac output and return of spontaneous circulation.
- Detection of ventilatory obstruction, disconnection, rebreathing, or changes in dead space ventilation via changes in waveform shape and EtCO2.

Limitations and factors altering EtCO2–PaCO2 relationship:
- Increased physiologic dead space (pulmonary embolism, low cardiac output, severe V/Q mismatch) widens the PaCO2–EtCO2 gradient; EtCO2 underestimates PaCO2.
- Low pulmonary perfusion (hypotension, cardiac arrest) reduces EtCO2 despite rising PaCO2.
- Rapid changes in ventilation, airway occlusion, leaks, secretions, and faulty sampling can distort readings.
- In nonintubated patients, capnography via nasal cannulae can be influenced by mouth breathing and supplemental oxygen flow; values are less reliable quantitatively but can show trends and presence/absence of ventilation.

Transcutaneous CO2 monitoring
Principles: Transcutaneous monitors measure CO2 tension through the skin using a heated electrode that increases local capillary blood flow and gas diffusion. The device yields continuous PtcCO2 and is widely used in neonates and pediatrics where frequent ABGs are problematic.

Performance and caveats:
- PtcCO2 correlates well with PaCO2 when monitored and calibrated correctly, but there is a time lag (several minutes) to reflect rapid changes.
- Accuracy depends on electrode temperature (often 42–44°C), sensor site, skin thickness, perfusion, and calibration. Skin burns are a rare complication from prolonged heating.
- In low perfusion states, accuracy falls because local capillary blood flow is reduced.
- Regular site changes and calibration against ABG improve reliability.

Clinical considerations
- Choose EtCO2 monitoring for real-time ventilatory monitoring during anesthesia, intubation, and CPR. Use EtCO2 waveforms to detect airway and ventilatory problems.
- Use transcutaneous CO2 for continuous monitoring in neonates, children, and in certain critical care settings when frequent ABGs are impractical, remembering the lag and need for calibration.
- When precise PaCO2 is required (e.g., in unstable physiology, major acid–base disorders), obtain ABG with direct PaCO2 measurement.

# Nocturnal oximetry: indications, limitations and interpretation

Nocturnal oximetry is a simple, low-cost test that records SpO2 over sleep and provides indices of nocturnal hypoxemia. It is commonly used as a screening tool, not a definitive diagnostic test for sleep-disordered breathing.

Indications
- Screening for obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) when full polysomnography is not immediately available or when pretest probability is high.
- Monitoring oxygen desaturation in patients with chronic lung disease (COPD), neuromuscular disease, or obesity hypoventilation syndrome.
- Evaluating nocturnal oxygenation in infants, children, or high-risk postoperative patients.
- Assessing the need for nocturnal supplemental oxygen or monitoring response to therapy when pulmonary function tests and clinical context suggest risk.

Interpretation and indices
Commonly reported metrics include mean SpO2, lowest SpO2, time spent below specified thresholds (e.g., T90: percent of sleep with SpO2 <90%), and the oxygen desaturation index (ODI), which is the number of desaturation events per hour of recording—typically defined as drops in SpO2 of ≥3% or ≥4% from baseline depending on protocol. ODI thresholds vary by center; an ODI ≥5 events/hour can be considered abnormal; higher values suggest more severe disease.

Limitations
- Oximetry cannot differentiate central versus obstructive apneas, nor does it detect airflow or respiratory effort—so it cannot diagnose OSA definitively.
- Desaturation events may be missed if sampling frequency is low or if averaging times smooth brief dips.
- Motion artefacts during sleep, poor sensor placement, sensor detachment, and poor peripheral perfusion reduce data quality and can produce false positives or negatives.
- Overnight oximetry underestimates hypercapnia: patients may desaturate minimally but develop significant nocturnal hypoventilation with hypercapnia (e.g., obesity hypoventilation, neuromuscular weakness).
- Intermittent or transient arousals that cause brief hypoventilation may not produce prolonged desaturations but can be clinically relevant.

Clinical application
Nocturnal oximetry is most useful as a screening tool: a normal result does not exclude sleep-disordered breathing, particularly in patients suspected of primary hypoventilation or those on supplemental oxygen. Abnormal recordings guide the need for comprehensive testing with polysomnography or for measurement of nocturnal PaCO2 or transcutaneous CO2 in suspected hypoventilation.

# Relationship between oxygen saturation, PaO2 and hypoventilation

The oxyhemoglobin dissociation curve describes the nonlinear relationship between PaO2 and hemoglobin oxygen saturation (SaO2). Small changes in PaO2 produce large changes in SaO2 on the steep portion of the curve (PaO2 <60 mmHg), while on the flat portion (PaO2 >60 mmHg) large changes in PaO2 affect SaO2 minimally.

Key features
- At PaO2 ≈ 100 mmHg, SaO2 ≈ 98–100%.
- At PaO2 ≈ 60 mmHg, SaO2 ≈ 90%—this is an inflection point clinically considered the threshold for significant hypoxemia.
- The sigmoid shape means that above PaO2 ≈ 60 mmHg, SpO2 falls slowly with decreasing PaO2; below that, small falls in PaO2 lead to large SaO2 decreases.

Factors shifting the curve
Shifts to the right (facilitating oxygen unloading to tissues, i.e., lower affinity): increased temperature, increased PaCO2, decreased pH (acidosis), increased 2,3-DPG. Shifts to the left (increased affinity): hypothermia, decreased PaCO2, alkalosis, decreased 2,3-DPG, fetal hemoglobin.

Hypoventilation and its effect on PaO2 and SaO2
Hypoventilation causes an increase in PaCO2 (hypercapnia) and, by the alveolar gas equation, a decrease in PAO2 and therefore lower PaO2. The magnitude of hypoxemia depends on FiO2 and the baseline A–a gradient. In a patient breathing room air with normal A–a gradient, hypoventilation reduces PAO2 and thus PaO2 predictably. However, because of the flat portion of the dissociation curve, moderate reductions in PaO2 (e.g., from 100 to 80 mmHg) may produce little change in SaO2, and pulse oximetry may remain near-normal. Only when PaO2 falls below ~60 mmHg will SpO2 fall to <90% and be clearly abnormal.

Supplemental oxygen confounds this relationship: supplemental O2 raises PAO2 and PaO2 even in the presence of hypoventilation, so SpO2 may remain normal despite significant hypercapnia. This dissociation is a central cause of clinical scenarios where pulse oximetry appears reassuring while ventilation is inadequate.

Clinical takeaways
Pulse oximetry measures oxygenation (SaO2) but does not measure ventilation (PaCO2). Because of the shape of the oxyhemoglobin dissociation curve, hypoventilation may not cause a proportional drop in SpO2 until severe hypoxemia has developed, especially if supplemental oxygen is present. Detecting hypoventilation sometimes requires direct PaCO2 measurement via ABG, transcutaneous CO2 monitoring, or capnography.

# End-tidal and transcutaneous CO2 monitoring: principles and clinical uses (revisited with respect to hypoventilation)

(Combined exposition to emphasize ventilation monitoring.)

Monitoring carbon dioxide provides direct or surrogate measures of alveolar ventilation. The two main bedside surrogates are EtCO2 and PtcCO2. Both can detect hypoventilation earlier than pulse oximetry in many cases because rising PaCO2 often precedes significant hypoxemia.

EtCO2 in detecting hypoventilation
Continuous capnography provides immediate feedback about ventilation: progressive rise in EtCO2 or a decrease to zero (apnea) indicates hypoventilation or loss of airway patency. In patients breathing room air without major V/Q mismatch, EtCO2 trends mirror PaCO2 trends closely enough to detect acute hypoventilation. However, when physiologic dead space is increased (e.g., COPD, pulmonary embolism), EtCO2 may underestimate PaCO2 and fail to reveal the full magnitude of hypercapnia.

Transcutaneous CO2 in detecting hypoventilation
Transcutaneous CO2 measures rising CO2 in the skin capillaries and provides a continuous numeric estimate more directly related to PaCO2 than EtCO2 in nonintubated patients. It is particularly useful for overnight monitoring in patients at risk for hypoventilation (obesity hypoventilation syndrome, neuromuscular disorders) where intermittent ABGs are impractical. PtcCO2 lags behind rapid changes in PaCO2 but detects sustained hypoventilation earlier than nocturnal oximetry.

When to use which
- In intubated or mechanically ventilated patients, capnography is standard for confirmation of tube placement and continuous ventilatory monitoring.
- In sedated but spontaneously breathing patients, combined pulse oximetry and capnography is superior to pulse oximetry alone for early detection of hypoventilation and apnea.
- For nonintubated patients at risk for nocturnal hypoventilation, transcutaneous CO2 monitoring provides important information not available from oximetry alone.

# Nocturnal oximetry limitations and detection of hypoventilation

Reiterating the specific problem: nocturnal oximetry measures desaturation events but does not measure ventilation directly. Patients with nocturnal hypoventilation may maintain near-normal SpO2 for long periods—especially if they receive supplemental oxygen or have preserved oxygenation due to right-shifted oxyhemoglobin curve compensations—while developing significant hypercapnia. In obesity hypoventilation syndrome and neuromuscular disease, incremental increases in PaCO2 during sleep (particularly during REM when accessory muscle activity falls) may not produce large desaturations but will increase PaCO2 and cause daytime somnolence, morning headache, and cognitive changes. Therefore, in patients at risk for nocturnal hypercapnia, overnight monitoring should include transcutaneous CO2 or full polysomnography with capnometry rather than oximetry alone.

# Clinical scenarios where pulse oximetry may miss hypoventilation (normoxia with hypercapnia)

Understanding scenarios where SpO2 appears reassuring while PaCO2 is elevated is critical to avoid missed respiratory failure.

1. Opioid or sedative-induced hypoventilation
Opioids depress central respiratory drive, leading to alveolar hypoventilation and rising PaCO2. If the patient is receiving supplemental oxygen, SpO2 may remain within normal limits while CO2 accumulates. Clinical clues include somnolence, decreased respiratory rate, and shallow breathing. Reliance on SpO2 alone is dangerous in this context; capnography or ABG measurement should be used for monitoring.

2. Obesity hypoventilation syndrome (OHS)
Patients with OHS may have chronic hypercapnia with near-normal oxygenation when awake or on supplemental oxygen. Nocturnal hypoventilation, particularly during REM sleep, can lead to progressive hypercapnia without marked desaturation. Overnight PtcCO2 monitoring or polysomnography with CO2 measurements is required to detect hypoventilation.

3. Neuromuscular weakness (amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, myasthenia gravis, muscular dystrophies)
Weak inspiratory muscles reduce minute ventilation, causing hypercapnia that can be out of proportion to hypoxemia until late in the disease. Early hypoventilation symptoms are subtle; forced vital capacity measurements and serial ABGs or PtcCO2 measurements are important.

4. Advanced COPD with chronic CO2 retention
In some COPD patients, chronic hypercapnia coexists with relatively preserved SpO2, particularly if supplemental oxygen is given. Acute on chronic hypercapnia can occur without large changes in SpO2. Additionally, increased physiologic dead space in COPD widens the PaCO2–EtCO2 gradient, making EtCO2 less reliable unless calibrated.

5. Carbon monoxide poisoning and dyshemoglobinemias
CO poisoning produces falsely normal or high SpO2 because pulse oximetry cannot distinguish carboxyhemoglobin from oxyhemoglobin. Although this situation is not hypercapnia per se, it represents another important scenario where SpO2 is misleading.

6. Supplemental oxygen therapy
Any situation where FiO2 is raised (oxygen therapy, high-flow nasal cannula) can mask hypoventilation on pulse oximetry by increasing PaO2 and SaO2 while PaCO2 rises. This is particularly important in the emergency department and postoperative settings.

Clinical decision points
- Do not rely on SpO2 alone to assess ventilation. When clinical concern for hypoventilation exists—changes in mental status, over-sedation, morning headache, daytime somnolence, or risk factors as above—obtain ABG for PaCO2, use capnography for immediate monitoring in sedated or intubated patients, and consider PtcCO2 for continuous noninvasive monitoring.
- Remove supplemental oxygen briefly (with caution) to assess baseline oxygenation and ventilation when clinically appropriate and safe; this may unmask hypoventilation but must be done under controlled conditions.
- In the perioperative and procedural sedation setting, combined monitoring (pulse oximetry + capnography) is standard of care to detect apnea and hypoventilation earlier than oximetry alone.

# Summary and practical approach

- Pulse oximetry is an invaluable, continuous monitor of arterial hemoglobin oxygen saturation but is limited by motion, poor perfusion, dyshemoglobinemias, sensor issues, and the nonlinear relation between PaO2 and SaO2. It measures oxygenation, not ventilation.
- ABG provides definitive measurement of PaO2, PaCO2, pH, and HCO3−. Use the alveolar gas equation and the A–a gradient to distinguish hypoventilation from V/Q mismatch, diffusion impairment, and shunt.
- Capnography (EtCO2) is the standard real-time monitor of ventilation in intubated and sedated patients; it also helps confirm tube placement and guide resuscitation. EtCO2 correlates with PaCO2 under many conditions but underestimates PaCO2 when dead space is increased or pulmonary blood flow is low.
- Transcutaneous CO2 monitoring gives continuous, noninvasive estimates of PaCO2 useful in neonates, children, and patients at risk for nocturnal hypoventilation; it has a time lag and must be calibrated.
- Nocturnal oximetry screens for desaturation but cannot reliably detect nocturnal hypercapnia and cannot distinguish central from obstructive events. For suspected nocturnal hypoventilation, use polysomnography with CO2 monitoring or transcutaneous CO2.
- Be vigilant for clinical scenarios in which SpO2 can be normal despite dangerous hypercapnia: opioid/sedative depression, obesity hypoventilation, neuromuscular weakness, COPD with chronic CO2 retention, and whenever supplemental oxygen is used.

In clinical practice, choose monitoring modalities based on the physiologic question: oxygenation (ABG, pulse oximetry, A–a gradient assessment), ventilation (ABG PaCO2, capnography, transcutaneous CO2), and the need for trend versus absolute accuracy. Combine methods when necessary to avoid dangerous blind spots—particularly the common pitfall of normoxia with unrecognized hypoventilation.
