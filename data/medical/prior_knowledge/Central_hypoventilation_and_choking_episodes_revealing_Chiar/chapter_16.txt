# Introduction

Central hypoventilation and related sleep‑related respiratory disorders encompass a spectrum of conditions in which ventilatory drive and/or the mechanical ability to maintain adequate ventilation are impaired, most prominently during sleep. These disorders range from congenital central hypoventilation syndrome (CCHS) and opioid‑related respiratory depression to central sleep apnea (CSA) and hypoventilation associated with neuromuscular disease, obesity hypoventilation syndrome (OHS), or other central nervous system disorders. Non‑surgical and supportive management aims to maintain adequate gas exchange, prevent end‑organ injury from chronic hypoxaemia or hypercapnia, improve sleep quality, reduce daytime symptoms (sleepiness, cognitive impairment), and maintain or restore functional independence.

This chapter focuses on the practical principles and indications for nocturnal noninvasive ventilation (NIV) including bilevel positive airway pressure (BiPAP) and volume‑targeted ventilation; the role and limitations of continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) when obstructive elements coexist with central hypoventilation; pharmacologic respiratory stimulants (acetazolamide, theophylline, doxapram); strategies for monitoring at night and in the domiciliary setting; acute management of severe hypoxaemia and airway protection; and rehabilitative approaches such as respiratory muscle training within a multidisciplinary care framework. Emphasis is placed on pathophysiology, clinical decision points, objective thresholds used in practice, typical ventilator modes and settings, and the evidence base that guides therapy.

# Principles and indications for nocturnal noninvasive ventilation (BiPAP, volume‑targeted ventilation)

Noninvasive ventilation (NIV) supports alveolar ventilation without the need for an artificial airway and is delivered via interfaces such as nasal masks, oronasal masks, or mouthpieces. The main physiological aims of nocturnal NIV in central hypoventilation and related disorders are: (1) to augment minute ventilation during sleep to correct or prevent hypercapnia and hypoxaemia; (2) to stabilize upper‑airway patency where mixed central and obstructive events occur; and (3) to unload fatigued respiratory muscles, allowing restoration of daytime muscle function.

Two broad NIV strategies are commonly used at night: pressure‑targeted bilevel support (BiPAP) and volume‑targeted ventilation (including assisted pressure‑controlled modes with a target tidal volume). BiPAP provides two pressure levels: an inspiratory positive airway pressure (IPAP) to augment tidal volume and decrease PaCO2, and an expiratory positive airway pressure (EPAP) to maintain airway patency and functional residual capacity. Volume‑targeted ventilation (for example, average volume‑assured pressure support [AVAPS] or intelligent volume modes) aims to guarantee a preset tidal volume or minute ventilation by varying inspiratory pressure within predefined limits. Volume‑targeted modes can be particularly useful when tidal volume requirements vary across sleep stages or body position, as in central hypoventilation syndromes where ventilatory drive may be reduced disproportionately in rapid eye movement (REM) sleep.

Indications for nocturnal NIV are based on objective evidence of sleep‑associated hypoventilation or gas exchange abnormality, symptoms attributable to hypoventilation, and the risk of progression to daytime respiratory failure. Practical criteria used in clinical practice include:

- Nocturnal oxygen desaturation with SpO2 <88% for >5 minutes during sleep attributable to hypoventilation (occasionally using overnight oximetry as initial screening).
- Nocturnal transcutaneous or end‑tidal PCO2 elevation with values >55 mmHg during sleep, or a rise in PaCO2 by >10 mmHg from awake baseline during sleep.
- Daytime hypercapnia (arterial PaCO2 >45–50 mmHg) or evidence of chronic ventilatory failure (compensatory bicarbonate retention on serum chemistry).
- Symptoms such as excessive daytime sleepiness, morning headache, impaired cognition, or impaired quality of life where these are believed to result from nocturnal hypoventilation.
- Progressive respiratory muscle weakness (e.g., in neuromuscular disorders) with evidence on pulmonary function tests: forced vital capacity (FVC) <50% predicted in the supine position or a rapid decline in FVC over serial measurements.
- Failure of other measures (weight loss, treatment of reversible causes) to correct hypoventilation.

Initial NIV modality selection is guided by the underlying pathophysiology. For primary central hypoventilation (e.g., CCHS), modes that reliably guarantee minute ventilation (volume‑targeted or pressure support with mandatory backup rate—in BiPAP S/T mode) are preferred because patients have inadequate or absent spontaneous ventilatory drive, especially during REM sleep. BiPAP S/T (spontaneous/timed) provides support with a backup respiratory rate and is often the initial pressure‑targeted choice when some spontaneous effort persists. Volume‑targeted modes (AVAPS, volume control) are used when maintaining a specific tidal volume is necessary, when there is significant variability in respiratory mechanics across different sleep stages or positions, or when the aim is to limit nocturnal hypercapnia reliably.

Selection of initial settings usually follows physiological rationale: aim for an initial tidal volume of approximately 6–8 mL/kg predicted body weight for adults to achieve adequate alveolar ventilation while avoiding volutrauma and excessive hyperventilation. For pressure support modes, IPAP is titrated to achieve observed tidal volumes and reduction in end‑tidal or transcutaneous PCO2; EPAP is set to address upper airway patency (2–10 cm H2O depending on the presence of obstructive events). A backup rate is commonly set slightly below the spontaneous rate to permit patient‑triggered breaths but to ensure ventilation if central drive ceases. In volume‑assured modes, set a target tidal volume and define maximum and minimum pressure support limits to avoid excessively high pressures.

Monitoring during initiation (laboratory titration polysomnography or high‑resolution oximetry and transcutaneous CO2 monitoring) is crucial to titrate pressures, backup rates, and to assess for residual obstructive events or hypoventilation. Patient comfort, mask fit, leak compensation, and synchrony are determinant factors that influence efficacy and adherence. Long‑term follow‑up to reassess gas exchange, symptoms, and ventilator data is essential.

# Use of CPAP for obstructive components and limitations in central hypoventilation

Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) provides a single constant positive pressure throughout the respiratory cycle and is the cornerstone therapy for obstructive sleep apnea (OSA). CPAP splints the upper airway open, reduces pharyngeal collapsibility, and improves oxygenation and sleep architecture in obstructive events. In patients with mixed or complex sleep‑disordered breathing—where obstructive events coexist with central hypoventilation or CSA—CPAP may be effective for the obstructive component and can sometimes unmask or reduce central events by stabilizing ventilation. CPAP is also simpler, less expensive, and often better tolerated than bilevel or volume‑targeted modes.

However, CPAP has important limitations in primary central hypoventilation and in disorders where alveolar ventilation is inadequate because of impaired central drive or respiratory muscle weakness. CPAP does not augment tidal volume or provide ventilatory support; it does not provide inspiratory assistance and therefore cannot reliably reduce PaCO2 in hypoventilation syndromes. In patients whose nocturnal gas exchange abnormalities are driven predominantly by hypoventilation (elevated PaCO2), CPAP alone is often insufficient. This distinction can be approached clinically: patients whose nocturnal desaturation is mainly due to obstructive apneas/hypopneas will typically respond to CPAP with normalization of desaturation and improvement in sleep‑disordered breathing metrics; those with persistent hypercapnia or desaturation despite CPAP should be evaluated for hypoventilation and transitioned to ventilatory support that provides inspiratory assistance or volume targeting.

Another limitation is that CPAP may worsen central apneas in some patients by increasing ventilation during non‑REM sleep and producing hypocapnia that promotes central apneas—this is particularly relevant in heart failure‑related Cheyne‑Stokes respiration. For mixed disorders, an incremental approach is reasonable: if obstructive events predominate on diagnostic polysomnography, trial CPAP with close monitoring (including transcutaneous PCO2 if hypoventilation is a concern). If nocturnal hypercapnia persists (e.g., transcutaneous PCO2 >50–55 mmHg or arterial PaCO2 elevated on daytime sampling) or symptoms continue, switch to bi‑level or volume‑targeted NIV with a backup rate to guarantee ventilation.

Clinical decision points include reassessing daytime arterial blood gas (ABG) values (normal PaO2 80–100 mmHg, PaCO2 35–45 mmHg), evaluating nocturnal CO2 monitoring, and reviewing residual sleep studies or ventilator data showing persistent hypoventilation or significant leak. CPAP remains valuable as part of combined management in patients with a significant obstructive component, but clinicians must be vigilant for its inability to correct primary hypoventilation.

# Pharmacologic respiratory stimulants: acetazolamide, theophylline, doxapram—mechanisms and evidence

Pharmacologic respiratory stimulants have historically been used to increase ventilatory drive in central hypoventilation and certain cases of sleep‑disordered breathing. Their role is typically adjunctive to mechanical ventilation rather than primary therapy in severe hypoventilation, and evidence varies across agents.

Acetazolamide

Mechanism: Acetazolamide is a carbonic anhydrase inhibitor that induces a mild metabolic acidosis by promoting renal bicarbonate (HCO3−) excretion. The resulting decrease in serum bicarbonate lowers the arterial pH and stimulates ventilation via central chemoreceptors, thereby increasing minute ventilation and reducing PaCO2. In sleep disorders, acetazolamide is most effective in conditions where periodic breathing or high loop gain contributes to central apneas—by lowering the apneic threshold for CO2 and stabilizing breathing.

Clinical use and evidence: Acetazolamide has shown benefit in high‑altitude periodic breathing and in some forms of central sleep apnea, including opioid‑induced central apnea and idiopathic CSA, with reductions in central apnea index and improvements in oxygenation and sleep quality in short‑term trials. Typical adult doses range from 250 mg once or twice daily to 500 mg daily; higher doses increase side effects including paresthesias, polyuria, and electrolyte disturbances. Contraindications include sulfonamide allergy (relative), severe hepatic disease, and significant renal impairment. Monitor serum electrolytes and bicarbonate; therapeutic effect may be transient as compensatory renal mechanisms evolve.

Theophylline

Mechanism: Theophylline is a methylxanthine that acts as a nonselective phosphodiesterase inhibitor and adenosine receptor antagonist. It increases ventilatory drive, bronchodilation, and diaphragmatic contractility, and can improve respiratory muscle endurance. Its stimulatory effects on the central nervous system and respiratory centers can decrease the apneic threshold and increase minute ventilation.

Clinical use and evidence: Theophylline has been used in central apnea related to heart failure and in neonatal apnea; however, evidence in adult central hypoventilation is limited and inconsistent. The drug has a narrow therapeutic index; target plasma concentrations for bronchodilation and respiratory stimulation are commonly in the range of 5–15 mg/L (μg/mL), but toxicity can occur even within this range. Side effects include tachyarrhythmias, seizures, gastrointestinal upset, and drug interactions via cytochrome P450 metabolism. Given modest and variable efficacy and safety concerns, theophylline is rarely first‑line in adults with significant hypoventilation and is typically reserved for selected patients who cannot tolerate NIV and where benefit outweighs risk.

Doxapram

Mechanism: Doxapram is a potent respiratory stimulant that acts centrally (brainstem chemoreceptor stimulation) and peripherally (carotid body stimulation), increasing tidal volume and respiratory rate. It produces a rapid, short‑lived increase in ventilation.

Clinical use and evidence: Historically used in acute settings for postoperative respiratory depression and COPD exacerbations, doxapram has largely fallen out of routine use in many centers due to side effects (agitation, hypertension, tachycardia, arrhythmias, and the need for careful monitoring) and the availability of safer alternatives (e.g., naloxone for opioid overdose, NIV, mechanical ventilation). It may be considered as a temporizing agent in acute respiratory depression when other measures are unavailable or contraindicated. Doses are typically given intravenously as a bolus followed by an infusion; close cardiovascular and neurological monitoring is required.

General considerations

- These stimulants can provide partial benefit in selected patients, particularly when ventilatory instability rather than fixed alveolar hypoventilation is the primary mechanism. They are less likely to correct severe hypoventilation caused by absent central drive or profound respiratory muscle weakness.
- Observe for compensatory mechanisms. For example, acetazolamide’s effect on ventilation may be attenuated over days to weeks as renal compensation occurs.
- Pharmacologic stimulants are most often adjuncts—used when NIV is contraindicated, poorly tolerated, unavailable, or as part of a combined strategy (e.g., acetazolamide used with adaptive servo‑ventilation for Cheyne‑Stokes respiration in specific contexts).
- Always evaluate for and treat reversible causes of hypoventilation (e.g., medication effects such as opioids or benzodiazepines, hypothyroidism, metabolic disturbances).

# Nocturnal monitoring, domiciliary oximetry and ventilator follow‑up

Effective management of central hypoventilation and sleep‑related respiratory disorders requires systematic nocturnal and longitudinal monitoring to ensure adequate gas exchange, optimize ventilator settings, assess adherence and efficacy, and identify complications.

Monitoring modalities

- Polysomnography (PSG): Full in‑lab polysomnography with continuous oximetry and transcutaneous CO2 (TcCO2) or end‑tidal CO2 (ETCO2) monitoring is the gold standard for initial diagnosis and for NIV titration in complex cases. PSG allows correlation of sleep stages, respiratory events, and arousals with gas‑exchange abnormalities, and is particularly helpful to titrate backup rates, pressure support, and EPAP to correct both obstructive and central events.
- Transcutaneous CO2 monitoring: TcCO2 provides continuous, noninvasive estimation of arterial PaCO2 during sleep and is essential for detecting hypoventilation that may not be accompanied by marked desaturation. Typical clinical thresholds include TcCO2 >55 mmHg during sleep or a rise of >10 mmHg from awake baseline as diagnostic of nocturnal hypoventilation.
- Oximetry: Overnight pulse oximetry (dwell‑time logging) is widely used for screening and domiciliary monitoring. It detects desaturation events but cannot detect isolated hypercapnia. Clinical thresholds used include SpO2 <88% for >5 minutes as clinically significant nocturnal desaturation. Average nocturnal SpO2 <90% or significant time spent below 90% are often used as signals for escalation of therapy.
- Capnography: ETCO2 can be helpful during NIV titration but may be less reliable than TcCO2 in the presence of significant mask leak or noninvasive interfaces. When accurate, trends in ETCO2 can help titrate ventilator support.
- Arterial blood gas (ABG): Daytime ABG remains important to confirm daytime hypercapnia or hypoxaemia (normal PaO2 80–100 mmHg, normal PaCO2 35–45 mmHg). Baseline bicarbonate level can indicate chronicity of hypercapnia. ABG is often repeated after initiating NIV to document correction of daytime hypercapnia.

Domiciliary monitoring and ventilator follow‑up

- Home oximetry and TcCO2: Many centers provide home overnight oximetry to assess response to therapy; increasingly, transcutaneous home monitors are used where persistent hypercapnia is expected. Devices should be reviewed for data quality; clinicians must be aware of artifact and probe displacement.
- Ventilator data download: Modern home ventilators record usage hours, leak, tidal volumes, respiratory rate, spontaneous vs. machine breaths, and events per hour. Regular download and review (monthly early after initiation, then every 3–6 months when stable) are recommended. Key decision thresholds include insufficient usage (<4 hours/night as an initial marker), persistent large uncorrected leaks, inadequate tidal volumes or sustained TcCO2 elevation, and recurrent obstructive events despite therapy.
- Clinical follow‑up schedule: Early follow‑up within 1–4 weeks after initiation is important to address mask fit, comfort, and initial ventilator issues. Subsequent follow‑up at 3 months and then every 6–12 months is typical, with more frequent review for progressive neuromuscular disease or newly symptomatic patients. Repeat PSG or cardiorespiratory polygraphy with CO2 monitoring is indicated when symptoms persist despite reported adherence or when ventilator downloads suggest inadequate ventilation.
- Outcome measures: Evaluate daytime symptoms, morning headaches, cognitive function, blood gas parameters, sleep quality, and objective device data. In neuromuscular disease, serial pulmonary function tests (FVC, maximal inspiratory and expiratory pressures) and cough efficacy tests help guide the need for escalation.

Quality control and safety

- Education and training of patients and caregivers on mask fitting, cleaning, battery backup, and troubleshooting are essential to ensure adherence and safety.
- For patients dependent on nocturnal ventilatory support, plans for electricity failure (battery backups), alarm settings on ventilators, and emergency contact pathways are integral.
- Recognize alarm signals of hypoventilation such as persistent daytime somnolence, morning headaches, or progressive encephalopathy—prompt re‑evaluation including blood gases is warranted.

# Acute management of severe hypoxaemia and airway protection

Severe hypoxaemia in central hypoventilation can be life‑threatening and requires prompt, systematic management focusing on airway, breathing, and circulation (the ABCs), while addressing the underlying cause and preventing further respiratory decompensation.

Initial assessment and oxygenation

- Rapidly assess airway patency and breathing. Check responsiveness, breathing pattern, accessory muscle use, and cyanosis.
- Provide supplemental oxygen to correct hypoxaemia, aiming for SpO2 in a target range appropriate to the condition (usually 92–96% in most acute settings). In hypercapnic respiratory failure, avoid excessive oxygenation that may worsen hypercapnia in COPD; target SpO2 88–92% when concomitant COPD or risk of CO2 retention exists. Continuous monitoring of SpO2 and, ideally, serial ABG or TcCO2 are required since oxygen alone can mask worsening hypercapnia.
- If hypoxaemia is due to hypoventilation and ventilation is insufficient, oxygen alone may not correct underlying hypercapnia and can precipitate CO2 narcosis; therefore simultaneous assessment and support of ventilation are critical.

Support of ventilation

- Noninvasive ventilatory support (NIV): If the patient is conscious, protecting the airway, hemodynamically stable, and has no contraindications to NIV (e.g., uncooperative, high aspiration risk, facial trauma, ineffective cough), trial NIV with close monitoring. Initiate bilevel ventilatory support with appropriate settings to rapidly support minute ventilation and reduce PaCO2. In the acute setting, use an oronasal mask for better seal and consider ICU or high‑acuity setting for close monitoring.
- Endotracheal intubation and invasive ventilation: Indications for intubation include loss of airway protective reflexes, severe hypoxaemia refractory to NIV, hemodynamic instability, inability to clear secretions (ineffective cough), severe acidosis, or failure of NIV. Early intubation may be lifesaving in patients with profound hypercapnic encephalopathy or respiratory arrest. Intubated patients require strategy to prevent ventilator‑associated complications and should have goals of weaning and plans for transition back to NIV or tracheostomy when appropriate.
- Airway protection: Assess the risk of aspiration. For patients with impaired consciousness or bulbar dysfunction, secure airway rather than attempting NIV. Suctioning and chest physiotherapy may be needed to clear secretions; cough assist devices (mechanical insufflation–exsufflation) can be adjuncts in neuromuscular weakness.

Address reversible causes and medications

- Review and reverse precipitants such as sedatives, benzodiazepines, or opioids. For opioid‑induced respiratory depression, naloxone is the specific antidote and should be administered per protocol; titrate to restore adequate ventilation while avoiding acute withdrawal.
- Correct metabolic disturbances (hypoglycemia, severe electrolyte abnormalities), treat infections, and manage heart failure or pulmonary edema as indicated.
- Consider short‑term use of doxapram only in selected acute cases where other measures fail or as bridge to definitive ventilation; close monitoring for cardiovascular side effects is essential.

Post‑acute care and disposition

- Once stabilized, determine the need for ongoing ventilatory support, possible tracheostomy in cases where prolonged invasive ventilation is anticipated, and whether the patient is a candidate for transition to home NIV.
- Arrange multidisciplinary follow‑up including respiratory medicine, sleep medicine, and, if applicable, neurology or rehabilitation services for ongoing assessment and long‑term management planning.

# Rehabilitative approaches: respiratory muscle training and multidisciplinary care

Rehabilitation in central hypoventilation and neuromuscular‑related hypoventilation focuses on maintaining or improving respiratory muscle function, optimizing cough efficacy, and integrating respiratory support within a broader multidisciplinary care plan to preserve function and quality of life.

Respiratory muscle training

- Principles: Respiratory muscle training (RMT) applies targeted loading to inspiratory and/or expiratory muscles to increase strength and endurance. Two main approaches are used: inspiratory muscle training (IMT) using threshold loading devices and expiratory muscle training (EMT) aimed at improving cough strength and expiratory flow.
- Indications and evidence: IMT is useful in patients with demonstrable inspiratory weakness (reduced maximal inspiratory pressure [MIP]) who retain some volitional control of the diaphragm and accessory muscles. MIP normal values vary by age and sex; values less than 60 cm H2O in adult men or less than 50 cm H2O in adult women suggest weakness, but interpretation must be individualized and compared to predicted values. Studies in COPD, heart failure, and some neuromuscular conditions show that IMT can improve inspiratory strength, exercise tolerance, and dyspnea. In progressive neuromuscular disease, benefits may be limited and are patient‑specific; early implementation before advanced weakness yields better results.
- Protocols: Typical IMT programs use threshold devices set at a percentage of baseline MIP (e.g., 30–60% of MIP), performed twice daily for 15–30 minutes per session, with progressive increases as tolerated. Monitoring includes reassessment of MIP, symptoms, and fatigue. EMT can be similarly structured, aiming to increase maximal expiratory pressure (MEP) and peak cough flow.
- Safety and limitations: Monitor for fatigue, desaturation, and hemodynamic effects; ensure RMT is not applied in the immediate post‑exacerbation period or in those with unstable cardiopulmonary status. In patients with severely reduced ventilation or absent central drive, RMT will have limited efficacy.

Cough augmentation and airway clearance

- Effective secretion clearance is critical in neuromuscular weakness. Techniques include assisted cough maneuvers, mechanical insufflation–exsufflation (cough assist), manual chest physiotherapy, and lung volume recruitment (air stacking) to prevent atelectasis and pneumonia.
- Peak cough flow (PCF) is a practical measurement: values >270 L/min are generally adequate for airway clearance, while PCF <160 L/min signals a high risk of ineffective cough and need for assisted cough techniques.

Multidisciplinary care

- A coordinated multidisciplinary team improves outcomes by addressing the complex needs of patients with hypoventilation. Core team members include pulmonologists or sleep physicians, respiratory therapists, physiotherapists, speech and language therapists (for bulbar dysfunction), neurologists (for neuromuscular disease), cardiologists where relevant, specialized nurses, social workers, and palliative care when appropriate.
- Roles: Respiratory therapists manage NIV initiation, interface fitting, ventilator troubleshooting, and domiciliary support. Physiotherapists lead RMT and airway clearance interventions. Speech therapists assess swallowing and aspiration risk. Nursing and social work coordinate home care supports, equipment procurement, and advanced care planning. Regular multidisciplinary meetings facilitate care plans for progressive disease, decisions about tracheostomy, and goals of care discussions.
- Pulmonary rehabilitation: When feasible, comprehensive pulmonary rehabilitation programs that incorporate exercise training, education, and psychosocial support can benefit functional capacity and quality of life, though programs must be tailored to limitations imposed by ventilatory support and potential desaturation.

Long‑term planning and end‑of‑life considerations

- For progressive disorders, early and open conversations about prognosis, long‑term ventilatory dependence, options for tracheostomy ventilation, and advanced care preferences are essential. Palliative care involvement should be considered early to assist with symptom management, complex decision‑making, and support for patients and families.
- Transition to home care requires robust education of caregivers, contingency planning for equipment failure, and linkage with home ventilation services to ensure continuity and safety.

# Conclusion

Management of central hypoventilation and sleep‑related respiratory disorders without surgery centers on individualized application of nocturnal ventilatory support (pressure‑ or volume‑targeted NIV), careful use of CPAP when obstructive components exist, judicious adjunctive use of respiratory stimulants, structured nocturnal and domiciliary monitoring, acute resuscitative strategies for severe hypoxaemia and airway compromise, and rehabilitation embedded in multidisciplinary care. Clinical decisions are guided by objective measures—daytime and nocturnal PaCO2/TcCO2, SpO2 thresholds, pulmonary function tests, and ventilator data—and by the patient’s symptoms, disease trajectory, and goals of care. Optimal outcomes require attention to technical details of ventilator settings and interfaces, close follow‑up, patient and caregiver education, and coordination across specialties.
