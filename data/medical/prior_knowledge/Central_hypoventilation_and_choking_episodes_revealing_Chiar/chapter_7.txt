Introduction

Central alveolar hypoventilation syndromes are a group of disorders characterized by impaired automatic control of ventilation such that alveolar ventilation is insufficient to maintain normal arterial carbon dioxide tension (PaCO2) and oxygenation, particularly during sleep. These syndromes may be congenital, with a genetic basis and lifelong implications, or acquired as a consequence of focal or diffuse injury to brainstem respiratory centers. Clinically they present a distinct pattern: hypoventilation that is most conspicuous during sleep, an attenuated ventilatory response to hypercapnia and hypoxia, and often a striking absence of dyspnea despite significant hypoxemia or hypercapnia. Understanding the underlying developmental and physiologic mechanisms, as well as the diagnostic approach and implications for long-term care, is essential for clinicians who encounter affected patients.

# Congenital central hypoventilation syndrome (CCHS): PHOX2B genetics and phenotype

Congenital central hypoventilation syndrome (CCHS) is the prototype congenital disorder of automatic ventilation. It is a rare neurocristopathy that manifests as impaired autonomic control of breathing and a spectrum of other autonomic nervous system abnormalities. The majority of CCHS cases are due to mutations in the PHOX2B gene, which encodes a transcription factor critical for the development of autonomic neurons derived from neural crest cells and certain brainstem nuclei involved in respiratory control.

Genetics. PHOX2B mutations fall into two broad categories. The most common are polyalanine repeat expansion mutations (PARMs), in which an in-frame expansion of a normally 20-alanine repeat tract occurs. Normal PHOX2B alleles contain 20 alanines (designated 20/20). Pathogenic PARMs most often expand this tract to 24–33 alanines (e.g., 20/25 through 20/33). Larger expansions tend to be correlated with more severe phenotypes. The less common class of mutations are non-polyalanine repeat mutations (NPARMs), including frameshift, missense, nonsense, and other in-frame deletions or insertions. NPARMs are disproportionately associated with severe phenotypes and with associated enteric and neural-crest tumor manifestations.

Phenotype and genotype-phenotype correlations. CCHS classically presents in the neonatal period with hypoventilation, usually most prominent during sleep and often necessitating ventilatory support from birth. The breathing abnormality arises from defective automatic (brainstem-mediated) control of respiration, while voluntary control may be relatively preserved. Associations include Hirschsprung disease (aganglionic megacolon) and tumors of neural crest origin, most notably neuroblastoma, ganglioneuroma, and ganglioneuroblastoma. The risk of these associated conditions is higher with certain genotypes, especially NPARMs and larger PARM expansions.

Genotype correlates with clinical severity: individuals with short PARM expansions (e.g., 20/25-20/26) may have milder phenotypes that require nocturnal ventilatory support but allow daytime spontaneous breathing, whereas those with larger expansions or NPARMs frequently require continuous ventilatory assistance and have a higher likelihood of associated Hirschsprung disease and tumor risk. Autonomic dysfunction beyond respiratory control is common and may include impaired heart rate variability, resting or exercise-related bradycardia or asystole, diminished pupillary responses, temperature dysregulation, impaired gag and swallow, and gastrointestinal dysmotility.

Pathobiology. PHOX2B plays a crucial role in the embryologic development of autonomic neurons, including those forming brainstem respiratory networks such as chemosensitive neurons in the retrotrapezoid nucleus (RTN), which are involved in central chemoreception for CO2/pH. Disruption of PHOX2B-dependent pathways results in abnormal development and function of the circuitry that generates automatic breathing and integrates chemosensory input—hence the characteristic blunted ventilatory responses and sleep-predominant hypoventilation.

# Clinical presentation across the lifespan: sleep-predominant hypoventilation, blunted chemosensitivity

Pattern of presentation. The unifying clinical feature across congenital and many acquired central hypoventilation syndromes is that impaired ventilation is most evident when wake-related voluntary control is reduced—during non-rapid eye movement (NREM) sleep and even more so during rapid eye movement (REM) sleep. Newborns with CCHS typically present with apneas, cyanosis, and hypercapnia observed on blood gases, often with minimal respiratory distress or apparent dyspnea. Older children and adults may present later with daytime somnolence, morning headaches, poor school or work performance, or newly noted sleep-disordered breathing. Some individuals with milder genotypes are diagnosed only when stressors (anesthesia, sedatives, or intercurrent illness) unmask hypoventilation.

Blunted chemosensitivity. A hallmark physiologic abnormality is reduced responsiveness of ventilatory drive to rises in arterial carbon dioxide (hypercapnia) and to hypoxemia. Chemoreception is mediated centrally by pH-sensitive neurons (central chemoreceptors) in the ventrolateral medulla (including RTN) and peripherally by carotid bodies responding to hypoxia. In CCHS, central chemoreflexes are especially impaired, leading to a markedly attenuated increase in minute ventilation for a given rise in PaCO2. Peripheral chemoreceptor responses may be variably affected, contributing to poor ventilatory response to hypoxia as well. Clinically, this means patients may tolerate very high PaCO2 (e.g., >60 mmHg) and low PaO2 without dyspnea or compensatory increases in ventilation; conversely they may be prone to dangerous hypercapnia during sleep, sedation, or when respiratory load increases.

Life-span considerations. The expression and consequences of hypoventilation evolve across the lifespan. In neonates, primary concerns are survival through the immediate perinatal period and establishing reliable ventilatory support. During infancy and childhood, growth, neurodevelopment, and risk of associated autonomic abnormalities are central issues. Adolescents and adults face transitions in care, evolving autonomy, potential refusal of therapy, and special considerations for pregnancy (in women), anesthesia, and risk during intercurrent illnesses. In acquired forms, presentation depends on lesion location and acuity: abrupt brainstem insults may cause acute, severe hypoventilation requiring urgent support; subacute or progressive lesions may produce gradual onset sleep-predominant hypoventilation and more limited daytime involvement.

# Acquired central hypoventilation: mechanisms related to brainstem compression, infarct, demyelination

Anatomical and physiological substrates. Automatic ventilation is generated by interconnected networks in the brainstem, including the pre-Bötzinger complex (rhythm generation), the ventral respiratory column, the nucleus tractus solitarius (integration of sensory input), and the retrotrapezoid nucleus/parafacial region (central chemoreception). Injury to these structures or to pathways that carry chemosensory, autonomic, or cortical modulatory inputs can produce hypoventilation that mimics congenital forms.

Mechanisms of acquired central hypoventilation. Several pathophysiologic mechanisms result in acquired central hypoventilation:

- Focal brainstem infarction or hemorrhage: Ischemic injury in the territory of the vertebrobasilar system—particularly the ventrolateral medulla, pontine tegmentum, or other structures housing respiratory rhythm or chemosensitive neurons—can acutely abolish automatic breathing. Clinical severity depends on lesion size and laterality; bilateral lesions of key regions are most likely to produce severe, persistent hypoventilation.

- Mass effect/compression: Tumors (primary brainstem gliomas, posterior fossa metastases), Chiari I malformation with tonsillar herniation, or expanding posterior fossa lesions can compress medullary respiratory centers. Compression may impair neuronal function before frank destruction, producing potentially reversible hypoventilation if decompression is performed timely.

- Demyelinating disease and inflammatory disorders: Multiple sclerosis, acute disseminated encephalomyelitis (ADEM), brainstem encephalitis, and other inflammatory processes can damage brainstem circuits intermittently or progressively. Lesions that interrupt central chemoreceptor regions or their afferent/efferent connections produce impaired automatic ventilation. The clinical course may be relapsing-remitting or progressive, with variable recovery dependent on remyelination and inflammation control.

- Traumatic brain injury: Shearing or focal injury to lower brainstem structures may interrupt respiratory networks or autonomic control. In prolonged coma, loss of automatic breath can occur, while focal brainstem injury in an otherwise conscious patient may present with sleep-predominant hypoventilation.

- Neurodegenerative disorders: Certain neurodegenerative diseases that affect brainstem autonomic nuclei (e.g., multiple system atrophy, some forms of synucleinopathies) can cause progressive impairment of automatic ventilation.

Pathophysiological consequences. Regardless of cause, the net effect is reduced automatic drive, blunted CO2 and O2 chemoreceptor responses, and impaired reflexive adjustments of ventilation to metabolic demands. The severity and reversibility vary: in compressive lesions there is potential for recovery after relief; in infarction or destructive processes recovery is limited and longer-term ventilatory support may be necessary.

# Diagnostic approach: polysomnography with CO2 monitoring, daytime ABGs and hypercapnic challenge

Clinical suspicion and initial evaluation. Diagnosis begins with high clinical suspicion in any patient with disproportionate sleep-related hypoventilation, unexplained hypercapnia, diminished respiratory symptoms despite hypoxemia, or relevant neurologic disease. A careful history should document timing (sleep vs awake), nocturnal symptoms (snoring, witnessed apneas, cyanosis), daytime sequelae (headache, somnolence), family history of CCHS, and exposure to sedatives. Physical exam should focus on cranial nerve/brainstem signs, autonomic features, and evidence of associated conditions (e.g., bowel dysfunction that might suggest Hirschsprung disease).

Polysomnography with CO2 monitoring. Overnight polysomnography (PSG) with simultaneous CO2 monitoring is central to diagnosis. Standard PSG records EEG, electro-oculography, electromyography, respiratory effort, airflow, oximetry, and can be supplemented by sensor(s) for end-tidal CO2 (PetCO2) and/or transcutaneous CO2 (PtcCO2). In central hypoventilation syndromes, PSG typically demonstrates sustained hypoventilation with reduced tidal volumes and minute ventilation during sleep, intermittent or sustained elevations in CO2, and oxygen desaturation without obstructive respiratory events (sleep apnea from central rather than obstructive origin). REM sleep may be disproportionately affected. Continuous transcutaneous CO2 monitoring is often preferred because PetCO2 can be unreliable when tidal volumes are low or there is dead space ventilation; PtcCO2 correlates reasonably with PaCO2 when calibrated and interpreted correctly.

Diagnostic thresholds. Published definitions vary by age, but useful practical thresholds include:
- Normal arterial PaCO2: 35–45 mmHg.
- Abnormal sleep hypoventilation (adult criteria commonly used): a rise in PaCO2 (or surrogate) during sleep to >45 mmHg or an increase of ≥10 mmHg above awake supine baseline; some criteria use a cutoff of PaCO2 >50 mmHg for ≥10 minutes or representing sustained hypercapnia. In pediatrics, the American Academy of Sleep Medicine suggests that an increase in PtcCO2 to >50 mmHg for >25% of total sleep time is diagnostic of sleep-related hypoventilation (criteria vary with age and must be interpreted in context).
- Daytime hypercapnia: PaCO2 >45–50 mmHg while awake and unstressed indicates hypoventilation that extends into wakefulness and often signifies more severe disease.

Daytime arterial blood gases and biochemical assessment. A baseline arterial blood gas (ABG) obtained while the patient is awake and breathing room air is important. Persistent daytime hypercapnia (PaCO2 >45–50 mmHg) indicates severe or unremitting hypoventilation and may mandate continuous ventilatory support. Measurement of bicarbonate and base excess helps identify chronicity; chronic hypercapnia often produces metabolic compensation with elevated serum bicarbonate. Normal arterial oxygen tension (PaO2) is roughly 75–100 mmHg at sea level; lower values in the setting of hypercapnia indicate alveolar hypoventilation contributing to hypoxemia.

Chemoreflex testing and hypercapnic challenge. Formal assessment of ventilatory responsiveness can be performed using controlled hypercapnic or hypoxic challenges. Examples include rebreathing tests (Read rebreathing) or controlled CO2 inhalation (e.g., 5% CO2) while measuring ventilation. The hypercapnic ventilatory response is defined as the slope of minute ventilation versus PaCO2 (or end-tidal CO2) and is markedly reduced in central hypoventilation syndromes. These studies must be done in monitored settings because patients with severe blunting of response may develop profound hypercapnia; they are most useful to characterize physiology, guide prognosis, and distinguish primary central hypoventilation from other causes of hypoventilation such as neuromuscular weakness or severe chest wall disease.

Neuroimaging and genetic testing. For suspected acquired causes, high-resolution MRI of the brainstem (brainstem and posterior fossa) is indicated to identify ischemia, demyelination, tumor, Chiari malformation, or compression. For suspected congenital cases or when family history/clinical features suggest CCHS, genetic testing for PHOX2B mutations is essential. Genotyping clarifies prognosis and guides screening for associated conditions (Hirschsprung disease, neural crest tumors). Additional evaluations include autonomic testing (heart rate variability, tilt-table testing), gastrointestinal evaluation for Hirschsprung disease (contrast studies, anorectal manometry), and tumor surveillance imaging and catecholamine metabolite assessment when indicated.

Differential diagnosis. It is critical to exclude other causes of hypoventilation such as obstructive sleep apnea, primary neuromuscular disorders (e.g., myasthenia gravis, muscular dystrophies), severe chest wall deformities, obesity hypoventilation syndrome, sedative or opioid intoxication, and chronic pulmonary disease. PSG with CO2 monitoring helps distinguish central hypoventilation from obstructive sleep apnea and quantifies the severity of hypoventilation.

# Prognosis and long-term ventilatory needs

Natural history and prognostic determinants. Prognosis depends on etiology, lesion extent, and in congenital cases, PHOX2B genotype. In CCHS, many patients require life-long ventilatory support, often starting with positive-pressure mechanical ventilation via tracheostomy in infancy. With age, some patients transition to noninvasive ventilation (bi-level positive airway pressure) for nocturnal use if their bulbar function is adequate and risk of aspiration is low. Diaphragm pacing is an option for selected patients with intact phrenic nerves and appropriate surgical candidacy; it can reduce dependence on ventilators and tracheostomy in some. The presence of daytime hypercapnia generally indicates a need for continuous (24-hour) ventilatory support. Individuals with milder genotypes may only require nocturnal ventilatory support and can maintain spontaneous daytime ventilation.

In acquired forms, prognosis is highly variable. Hypoventilation due to compressive lesions may improve after surgical decompression. Ischemic or destructive lesions often result in permanent deficits, though partial recovery can occur over months depending on neuronal plasticity and rehabilitation. The potential for recovery should be assessed over time, with serial respiratory and neurologic evaluation. In demyelinating and inflammatory disorders, disease-modifying therapy and immunosuppression can lead to improvement in some cases.

Long-term management considerations. Long-term ventilatory needs require individualized planning. For pediatric patients, growth, speech, swallowing, and neurodevelopmental support are priorities. Regular follow-up should include polysomnography with CO2 monitoring after changes in therapy, assessment of growth and development, and surveillance for associated conditions in CCHS (enteric dysfunction, tumors). For adults, monitoring includes ongoing evaluation of nocturnal ventilation adequacy, daytime gas exchange, and management of comorbidities. Transition planning from pediatric to adult care is important in congenital cases.

Device selection and monitoring. Choice of ventilatory support (invasive via tracheostomy, noninvasive positive-pressure ventilation, or diaphragm pacing) depends on severity, bulbar function, airway protection, and lifestyle goals. Home monitoring with oximetry and CO2 sensors, caregiver training, and emergency planning are essential. Regular reassessment of ventilator settings is required because growth, illness, or changes in sleep architecture can alter ventilatory requirements.

# Complications: hypoxaemia without dyspnoea, neurocognitive sequelae

Silent hypoxemia and lack of dyspnea. A striking clinical feature in central hypoventilation syndromes is the weak or absent subjective sense of breathlessness despite substantial hypoxemia and hypercapnia. This arises from impairment of the afferent and central integrative pathways that generate the experience of dyspnea. Clinically this is hazardous: patients (and caregivers) may not notice progressive respiratory compromise until hypoventilation is severe enough to cause daytime somnolence, cyanosis, or cardiopulmonary decompensation. Therefore, objective monitoring rather than symptom report is paramount in surveillance and management.

Neurocognitive sequelae. Recurrent or chronic nocturnal hypoxemia and hypercapnia are associated with adverse neurocognitive outcomes. In infants and children, disrupted oxygenation and sleep architecture during critical periods of brain development increase the risk of global developmental delay, deficits in attention and executive function, learning disability, and behavioral problems. In adults, chronic hypercapnic hypoventilation is associated with daytime somnolence, impaired attention, mood changes, and reduced quality of life. The magnitude and reversibility of these effects depend on severity and duration of gas exchange abnormalities, the presence of comorbid neurologic disease, and the timeliness and adequacy of ventilatory support.

Cardiopulmonary complications. Chronic hypoventilation can contribute to pulmonary hypertension through chronic hypoxemia-driven vasoconstriction and vascular remodeling, which may progress to right ventricular dysfunction and cor pulmonale if unrecognized. Arrhythmias including bradycardia and pauses are also a concern, especially in CCHS, where autonomic dysfunction may manifest as life-threatening bradyarrhythmias or asystole. Regular cardiac monitoring (ECG, Holter monitoring) is appropriate when indicated by symptoms or genotype.

Infectious and mechanical complications. Long-term mechanical ventilation, especially via tracheostomy, carries risks including airway infection, tracheal stenosis, and device-related complications. Noninvasive ventilation carries risks of mask-related skin breakdown, aerophagia, and inadequate ventilation if interface fit or adherence is poor. Diaphragm pacing has surgical risks and potential for device failure.

Multisystem autonomic dysfunction. In congenital forms, the widespread autonomic dysfunction can result in gastrointestinal dysmotility, temperature regulation problems, abnormal pupillary responses, and impaired baroreflexes, each of which contributes to morbidity and requires multidisciplinary management.

Summary and clinical pearls

Central alveolar hypoventilation syndromes unite a pathophysiologic theme: loss of automatic ventilatory drive with relative preservation of voluntary control. CCHS is most often due to PHOX2B mutations with genotype-phenotype correlations that inform prognosis and risk of associated neural crest disorders. Acquired forms arise from focal or diffuse injury to brainstem respiratory networks and are potentially reversible depending on cause. Diagnosis relies on polysomnography with CO2 monitoring, daytime ABGs, and assessments of chemosensitivity, combined with neuroimaging and genetic testing as indicated. Long-term care focuses on appropriate ventilatory support, monitoring for complications (including silent hypoxemia and neurocognitive sequelae), and multidisciplinary management of autonomic and associated systemic disorders. Early recognition and systematic follow-up are key to reducing morbidity and optimizing neurodevelopmental and cardiopulmonary outcomes.
