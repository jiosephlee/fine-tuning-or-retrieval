# Introduction

Disorders of the craniocervical junction (CCJ) encompass a spectrum of developmental, degenerative, and acquired conditions that alter the relationship between the hindbrain and the foramen magnum. Chiari malformations — most commonly Chiari I malformation — and related CCJ abnormalities are primarily anatomic and hydrodynamic disorders: changes in bony anatomy and cerebellar position can disturb cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) pathways and lead to symptomatic brainstem and spinal cord dysfunction, most notably syringomyelia. Radiologic imaging is central to recognizing the anatomic features, quantifying severity, and guiding management decisions. This chapter reviews the neuroimaging principles and practical interpretation of CT and MRI for evaluation of Chiari and craniocervical junction disorders. Emphasis is placed on which MRI sequences are essential, how to perform and read cine phase-contrast CSF flow studies, the role and limits of CT at the foramen magnum, accepted measurement techniques for tonsillar descent and posterior fossa metrics, how to identify crowding and obliteration of subarachnoid spaces, the imaging appearance and measurement of syringomyelia and syringobulbia, and common sources of misinterpretation and inter-reader variability.

# MRI sequences critical for Chiari assessment: sagittal T1/T2, FLAIR, cine phase-contrast CSF flow studies

Magnetic resonance imaging provides the soft-tissue contrast and multiplanar capability essential for evaluating Chiari malformations and associated sequelae. A standard MRI protocol for suspected Chiari/CCJ pathology should include high-resolution sagittal T1- and T2-weighted sequences, axial T2 sequences through the brainstem and upper cervical cord, coronal images when bony or posterior fossa asymmetry is suspected, and fluid-sensitive sequences such as FLAIR when assessing concomitant supratentorial pathology. Each of these sequences has a specific utility.

Sagittal T1-weighted images give excellent anatomic delineation of the skull base, clival and basiocciput relationships, cerebellar tonsils, and brainstem orientation. T1 is useful for assessing contour and position of anatomical landmarks (clivus, dorsum sellae, foramen magnum) and for identifying hemorrhage or fat-containing lesions. T2-weighted sagittal images afford high sensitivity for CSF spaces and are the preferred sequence to visualize the relationship between the tonsils and subarachnoid CSF at the foramen magnum. T2 sagittal images also depict syrinx cavities as centrally located hyperintense signal within the cord and delineate the extent of syringomyelia.

Axial T2 sequences through the cervicomedullary junction and upper cervical cord are essential to assess cross-sectional cord morphology, to detect syrinx diameter and eccentricity, and to evaluate perimedullary CSF spaces ventrally and dorsally. Thin-section axial imaging (slice thickness \( \le 3 \) mm) through the foramen magnum improves detection of focal CSF flow restriction and visualization of tonsillar morphology (peg-like descent versus rounded tonsils).

FLAIR sequences are most helpful when there is concern for associated supratentorial white matter disease or to exclude other causes of symptoms, such as demyelination or small vessel ischemia, which may coexist or mimic Chiari-related symptoms. Post-contrast T1-weighted imaging is not routinely required for primary Chiari evaluation but should be obtained when alternative diagnoses (neoplasm, inflammation) are being considered.

Cine phase-contrast (PC) MRI — often referred to simply as “cine CSF flow” — assesses CSF dynamics across the foramen magnum. This technique encodes velocity into phase-contrast images and visualizes the pulsatile, biphasic CSF motion that normally occurs with the cardiac cycle: caudal flow during systole and rostral flow during diastole. Cine PC studies may be performed as qualitative cine images or as quantitative assessments yielding peak velocities and stroke volumes. Proper technique typically acquires through-plane velocity-encoded data perpendicular to the plane of CSF flow at the level of the foramen magnum or C1. Typical velocity-encoding settings (VENC) for foramen magnum studies are low, often in the range of 5–10 cm/s, to maximize sensitivity to slow CSF motion; selecting too high a VENC will decrease sensitivity, while too low a VENC risks aliasing. Careful cardiac gating (pulse oximeter or ECG) and acquisition over multiple cardiac phases (e.g., 16–32 frames per cardiac cycle) produce cine loops that show dynamic obstruction, jetting, or absence of flow. Cine PC is most informative when correlated with anatomic images: absence or marked attenuation of caudal and rostral flow at the foramen magnum supports a hydrodynamic obstruction that may contribute to syrinx formation, even when the degree of tonsillar descent is modest.

# CT imaging roles and limitations at the foramen magnum

Computed tomography (CT) is complementary to MRI in the evaluation of craniocervical junction pathology because of its superior resolution of osseous anatomy. High-resolution CT with bone algorithm reconstructions is the modality of choice for assessing congenital bony anomalies (occipitalization of C1, basilar invagination, assimilation of the atlas), traumatic fractures, atlanto-occipital instability, abnormal condylar morphology, and the size and orientation of the foramen magnum. CT can detect osteophytes, calcified ligaments, and lytic lesions that may narrow the foramen magnum or otherwise alter the osseous boundaries of the posterior fossa. Thin-slice CT with multiplanar reformats (sagittal and coronal) provides precise landmarks for surgical planning, particularly in posterior fossa decompression or posterior fixation procedures.

However, CT has significant limitations in Chiari assessment. CT provides poor soft-tissue contrast for cerebellar tonsils, brainstem parenchyma, and intramedullary cavities, making it unsuitable as a primary modality for assessing tonsillar morphology or syringomyelia. CT cannot evaluate CSF flow dynamics, which are crucial to understanding pathophysiology in many Chiari cases. Radiation exposure is another drawback, particularly in children and young adults, where serial imaging may be required. Even with thin slices, CT may miss subtle soft-tissue crowding at the ventral subarachnoid space. Therefore, CT should be used as an adjunct to MRI when bony detail is required or when MRI is contraindicated.

# Measurement techniques for tonsillar descent and posterior fossa metrics

Accurate and reproducible measurement of cerebellar tonsillar position and posterior fossa dimensions is fundamental to the radiologic diagnosis and grading of Chiari I malformation and related CCJ disorders. A number of anatomic reference lines and metrics are in common use; the interpreter must explicitly state the line or method used, because different lines yield different numeric values.

The most widely used measurement for tonsillar descent is the perpendicular distance from the inferior margin of the cerebellar tonsils to the foramen magnum, most commonly referenced to the McRae line. The McRae line is drawn between the basion and opisthion (the anterior and posterior margins of the foramen magnum); tonsillar descent is measured as the vertical distance below this line in millimeters on the midline sagittal image. Historically, cerebellar tonsils extending more than 5 mm below the foramen magnum by this measurement have been labeled Chiari I malformation in adults; however, the 5 mm threshold is arbitrary and should not be used alone to direct management — clinical correlation is essential. Tonsillar position from 0 to 5 mm below the foramen magnum may be a normal variant, particularly in adolescents and in patients with small posterior fossae. Pediatric definitions are more complex: in children, tonsils can be lower without pathology, and age-based thresholds or percentiles are sometimes applied.

Alternative reference lines include Chamberlain’s line (posterior edge of hard palate to opisthion) and McGregor’s line (posterior hard palate to caudal occiput), which are primarily used to assess basilar invagination rather than routine tonsillar descent. The tonsillar descent distance should always be reported with the reference line used and the imaging plane (midline sagittal) and should note whether the tonsillar tip is rounded, peg-shaped, or flattened, as morphology can have diagnostic and prognostic significance.

Posterior fossa morphometrics aim to quantify whether the posterior fossa is “small” or hypoplastic, which is one hypothesized cause of tonsillar herniation. Common measures include posterior fossa height (distance from the dorsum sellae to the internal occipital protuberance or opisthion), clival length (from the dorsum sellae to the foramen magnum), and posterior cranial fossa area or volume. Volumetric assessment — typically performed with dedicated post-processing and segmentation of the posterior cranial fossa — provides the most physiologically relevant measure but is more resource intensive. A simple index used in several studies is the posterior fossa crowdedness index, which is the ratio of hindbrain tissue (cerebellum and brainstem) area to posterior fossa area on midsagittal sections; increased ratios suggest crowding. Tentorial angle and basal angle may also be reported; a more acute tentorial angle can exacerbate crowding. No single posterior fossa metric has universal acceptance; rather, combined morphometrics interpreted in the clinical and radiographic context provide the most value.

When giving reported numbers, radiologists should include the measurement technique, the exact numeric result in millimeters, and any relevant normative range or institutional cutoff used. For example: “Tonsillar descent 7 mm below the McRae line on midsagittal T2-weighted image.”

# Identifying crowded foramen magnum and obliteration of subarachnoid spaces

Anatomic crowding at the foramen magnum is central to Chiari pathophysiology: reduced space around the cerebellar tonsils and at the CCJ can obstruct CSF flow between cranial and spinal compartments. Imaging evidence of crowding includes obliteration or narrowing of the dorsal and ventral subarachnoid cisterns, effacement of the prepontine or premedullary cisterns, and compression of the cisterna magna.

On sagittal and axial T2-weighted images, the subarachnoid space should appear as a continuous hyperintense rim of CSF around the brainstem and upper cervical cord. Obliteration is visualized as the loss of that hyperintense layer adjacent to the tonsils or brainstem due to soft tissue approximation to the bone or spinal cord. The ventral subarachnoid space (ventral to the brainstem) and dorsal subarachnoid space (dorsal to the tonsils) should be assessed separately; isolated ventral compression (e.g., from basilar invagination causing anterior brainstem impingement) can produce symptoms and CSF disturbance even when dorsal CSF spaces appear relatively preserved.

Axial imaging at the level of the foramen magnum is particularly useful to describe circumferential crowding and to identify focal jetting or flow channels between tonsil and bone. The presence of arachnoid adhesions (when visible) or scarring in postoperative patients can be inferred from fixed focal obliteration. While MRI delineates soft tissue and CSF, cine PC studies add dynamic information: markedly reduced or absent bidirectional pulsatile CSF flow at the foramen magnum indicates functional obstruction even when residual static CSF is visible on T2 images.

Interpretation must account for physiologic variables: head position and neck flexion/extension alter tonsillar position and CSF space geometry; therefore, if clinical suspicion is high but imaging looks equivocal, consider repeat imaging with neutral head position or dynamic maneuvers in specialized centers. Also, artifacts from flow voids or partial-volume averaging can mimic or obscure small CSF spaces, reinforcing the need for thin-section imaging and consistent protocoling.

# Detection and assessment of syringomyelia and syringobulbia

Syringomyelia — a fluid-filled cavity within the spinal cord — is one of the most important complications associated with Chiari malformations. Syringobulbia is the extension of such cavities into the brainstem. MRI is the gold standard for detection, characterization, and longitudinal monitoring of syrinx cavities.

On T2-weighted images, syrinx cavities appear as well-defined, centrally located hyperintense regions within the spinal cord parenchyma. They may be simple tubular cavities or more complex, septated, or eccentric shapes. T1-weighted images usually show corresponding hypointensity. Contrast enhancement of the cavity walls is not typical and, if present, should prompt consideration of alternative diagnoses such as neoplasm, inflammatory conditions, or post-surgical change.

When reporting a syrinx, the following descriptive elements are clinically important: location (vertebral level span), maximal diameter, relationship to the cord (central versus eccentric), and associated T2 or T1 signal changes in the adjacent cord that may reflect myelomalacia. Quantitative assessment should include the maximal transverse diameter in millimeters and the syrinx-to-cord ratio (maximum syrinx diameter divided by the maximal cord diameter at the same level), because this ratio has prognostic relevance: a syrinx occupying a larger proportion of the cord cross-section is more likely to be symptomatic or to progress. Length is usually reported in vertebral segments (e.g., extending from C2 to T4) rather than centimeters. Longitudinal extension into the medulla indicates syringobulbia and requires careful assessment of lower cranial nerve nuclei and the floor of the fourth ventricle.

Differential diagnoses include hydromyelia (dilatation of the central canal continuous with the fourth ventricle, often more common in infants), intrinsic intramedullary neoplasm, post-traumatic cysts, and focal gliosis. In cases where the syrinx is small or equivocal, high-resolution axial sequences are helpful to distinguish an eccentrically located syrinx from an extrinsic compressive lesion. Additionally, if there is suspected communication between ventricular CSF and the syrinx, sequences that visualize the fourth ventricle and foramen of Magendie (sagittal T2) and cine flow imaging can be instructive.

Monitoring strategy is determined by symptoms and imaging: asymptomatic small syrinxes may be observed with interval MRI, while enlarging syringes, progressive neurological signs, or persistent CSF flow obstruction on cine PC studies often prompt surgical consideration. When serial imaging is performed, consistent technique (slice thickness, plane, and sequence type) is essential to allow meaningful comparison of syrinx size over time.

# Common radiologic pitfalls and inter-reader variability

Interpreting neuroimaging for Chiari and CCJ disorders is subject to multiple pitfalls that can lead to overdiagnosis or underestimation of disease. Awareness of these limitations is essential for clinicians relying on imaging to make management decisions.

One major pitfall is rigid reliance on a numerical tonsillar descent threshold (commonly >5 mm) without clinical correlation. Tonsillar position varies with age, body habitus, and head position, and mild descent (1–5 mm) may be a normal variant. Conversely, symptomatic patients occasionally have minimal tonsillar descent but pronounced CSF flow obstruction due to crowding or arachnoid adhesions. Therefore, imaging reports should integrate anatomy, CSF flow studies, posterior fossa metrics, and clinical context.

Technical factors introduce variability. Slice thickness and plane positioning affect measured tonsillar descent: thicker slices and off-midline sagittal cuts can underestimate the true extent. Head flexion or extension during the examination can increase or decrease observed tonsillar excursion. Partial-volume effects may create the appearance of a peg-like tonsil when in fact the tonsil is rounded. Motion artifact, particularly in uncooperative patients or in long cine sequences, degrades image quality and complicates flow measurements.

Cine phase-contrast imaging is susceptible to specific technical pitfalls: inappropriate velocity encoding (VENC) leads to underestimation of flow (too high) or aliasing (too low). Cardiac gating errors or irregular heart rhythms produce inconsistent phase images. Interpretation errors occur when relying solely on static measurements rather than viewing the dynamic cine loop; focal jets may be brief and localized, requiring cine playback to appreciate. Postoperative patients present another challenge: persistent flow on cine does not always correlate with symptomatic improvement, and scar tissue may produce focal flow abnormalities that are hard to distinguish from residual or recurrent obstruction.

Inter-reader variability is well documented in interpretation of Chiari-related imaging. Studies have shown significant variability in measurement of tonsillar descent, grading of crowding, and interpretation of cine flow. Sources of variability include choice of reference line (McRae vs Chamberlain), measurement method (perpendicular distance vs vertical projection), the observer’s experience, and institutional protocol differences. Standardization mitigates variability: always measure on the midline sagittal image, explicitly state the reference line used, use thin-section imaging, and include cine PC studies with standardized VENC and gating parameters when possible. When a second opinion will affect management, involvement of neuroradiologists with specific experience in CCJ imaging is advisable.

Finally, clinical overinterpretation of incidental findings is a hazard. Incidental low-lying tonsils discovered on imaging done for unrelated reasons should be interpreted conservatively: in asymptomatic patients, imaging findings alone rarely mandate intervention. Management decisions should be driven by symptomatology, neurological examination, and longitudinal imaging trends rather than a single arbitrary measurement.

# Conclusion

Accurate and clinically meaningful neuroimaging for Chiari malformations and craniocervical junction disorders requires attention to both high-quality anatomic sequences and dynamic assessment of CSF flow. Sagittal T1 and T2 sequences, thin-section axial imaging, fluid-sensitive sequences, and cine phase-contrast MRI together provide the necessary information to evaluate tonsillar position, posterior fossa crowding, CSF dynamics, and syringomyelia. CT is a valuable adjunct for osseous anatomy but does not replace MRI for soft-tissue and CSF evaluation. Standardized measurement techniques, clear reporting of reference lines and parameters, and awareness of technical and physiologic sources of variability reduce interpretive errors. Ultimately, integration of imaging findings with the clinical presentation and longitudinal assessment guides appropriate management and avoids both under‑ and over-treatment.
