Introduction

The act of breathing is governed by an integrated network of sensors, central pattern generators, motor pathways and modulatory systems that together maintain blood gas homeostasis and meet metabolic demands. Respiratory control balances automatic brainstem-driven ventilation with voluntary and behavioral influences from the cortex, and is modulated across sleep–wake states, exertion and disease. Understanding how chemical and mechanical inputs are sensed, how central circuits generate rhythm and adjust ventilatory output, and how perception of breathlessness arises, is essential for interpreting clinical presentations such as hypoventilation, sleep-disordered breathing, respiratory failure and unexplained dyspnoea. This chapter reviews the physiology of breathing and respiratory control, emphasizing mechanisms of chemoreception for CO2 and O2, mechanoreceptor input and reflexes, the distinction between automatic and voluntary pathways, sleep-related changes, arousal and gas-exchange responses, and the neural basis of ventilatory drive and dyspnoea.

# Central and peripheral chemoreception: CO2 and O2 sensing mechanisms

Chemoreception provides the dominant tonic and phasic drive to ventilation. Two anatomically and physiologically distinct chemoreceptor systems contribute: central chemoreceptors located within the brainstem that primarily sense changes related to arterial CO2 (via changes in pH of the brain extracellular fluid), and peripheral chemoreceptors—chiefly the carotid bodies—that sense arterial oxygen tension and also respond to CO2 and pH.

Central chemoreception. Central chemoreceptors are classically located near the ventrolateral medullary surface (retrotrapezoid nucleus (RTN), nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS) adjacent regions, and medullary raphe). These neurons respond to changes in the pH of the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and extracellular brain interstitium, which reflect arterial PaCO2 because CO2 readily diffuses across the blood–brain barrier (BBB). CO2 combines with water to form carbonic acid; hydration to bicarbonate and protons is catalyzed by carbonic anhydrase. Most central chemosensitivity arises from proton sensing rather than from bicarbonate per se. Changes in CSF pH alter the firing of chemosensitive neurons, which modulate the respiratory rhythm generator (pre-Bötzinger complex, adjacent networks) to change ventilatory output.

The central chemoreflex has a characteristic sensitivity and time course. The ventilatory response to incremental increases in PaCO2 is approximately linear over the physiological range; typical awake ventilatory response slopes are on the order of 2–4 L·min^-1 per mmHg PaCO2 (values vary with subject, age, metabolic state and sleep). Central chemoreceptor responses are relatively slow compared with peripheral chemoreceptors because of the time required for CO2 equilibration across the BBB and CSF buffering. Moreover, central chemoreception is modulated by sleep state, anesthesia, and neuromodulators (serotoninergic, noradrenergic inputs), and is diminished in certain disease states (congenital central hypoventilation syndrome, opioid-induced respiratory depression).

Peripheral chemoreception. The carotid bodies at the carotid bifurcation are the principal peripheral chemoreceptors in humans, with a smaller contribution from aortic bodies. Carotid body glomus (type I) cells are exquisitely sensitive to arterial oxygen tension (PaO2), as well as to arterial pH and PaCO2. The carotid body response is steep at low PaO2: ventilatory stimulation becomes pronounced when PaO2 falls below approximately 60 mmHg (approximately the inflection point on the O2–response curve). At normal PaO2 (>80 mmHg) carotid body contribution is small. Peripheral chemoreceptors are fast-acting, providing immediate increases in ventilation in response to hypoxia or hypercapnia; they also sensitize central chemoreceptors through afferent signaling.

Molecular mechanisms. In the carotid body, chemosensing involves inhibition of oxygen-sensitive potassium channels in glomus cells when PaO2 falls, leading to depolarization, opening of voltage-gated calcium channels, neurotransmitter release (e.g., ATP, acetylcholine, dopamine) and activation of sensory afferents to the petrosal ganglion and NTS. For CO2/H+ the mechanisms are more complex: CO2 can act directly or via intracellular acidification to alter channel conductances and neurotransmitter release. In the central chemoreceptive regions, proton-sensitive ion channels (TASK channels, ASICs, and other pH-sensitive conductances) and chemosensitive neurons expressing specific transcription factors (e.g., Phox2b in RTN) mediate CO2/H+ detection.

Clinical note: The integrated ventilatory response to hypercapnia and hypoxia depends on both systems. For example, in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) with chronic hypercapnia the central chemoreceptor sensitivity may be blunted, making peripheral chemoreceptor-mediated hypoxic drive more important; this is the physiologic rationale for careful oxygen titration. Normal reference ranges pertinent to chemoreception are arterial PaCO2 35–45 mmHg and PaO2 75–100 mmHg at sea level; ventilatory responses are often described relative to PaCO2 in mmHg.

# Mechanoreceptors, pulmonary stretch receptors and reflexes

Mechanical sensors in the airways, lungs, chest wall and respiratory muscles continually inform the central nervous system about lung volume, airflow, airway irritation and the mechanical load of breathing. These afferents modulate respiratory rhythm, patterning and reflex responses that protect the lung and optimize ventilation.

Pulmonary stretch receptors. Slowly adapting stretch receptors (SARs) are mechanosensitive afferents located mainly in the smooth muscle of small bronchi and bronchioles. SARs respond to lung inflation and mediate the Hering–Breuer inflation reflex: sustained lung inflation increases SAR firing, which through vagal afferents inhibits inspiratory drive and terminates inspiration. In adults at normal tidal volumes the Hering–Breuer reflex plays only a modest role in rhythm termination, but it becomes important in infants and at large tidal volumes or when mechanical ventilation settings change. Rapidly adapting receptors (RARs), in contrast, are sensitive to dynamic mechanical changes, airway collapse, mucus, and chemical irritants; they trigger cough, bronchoconstriction, and rapid, shallow breathing.

Juxtacapillary (J) receptors and C-fibre afferents. J receptors lie in the alveolar interstitium and are activated by pulmonary interstitial edema, congestion, and certain chemical stimuli. Stimulation produces rapid shallow breathing, expiratory braking, and sensations of dyspnoea. Small unmyelinated C-fibres in the airway mucosa transmit information about noxious stimuli and contribute to reflex bronchoconstriction, cough, mucus secretion and autonomic responses.

Chest wall and muscle receptors. Muscle spindles and Golgi tendon organ–like receptors in respiratory muscles sense contraction and load; joint and proprioceptive receptors report thoracic cage position. These inputs are important for fine-tuning motor output during exercise and in the perception of effort: when inspiratory muscle load increases, increased efferent motor command is paired with afferent feedback to cortical centers that contributes to dyspnoea.

Reflexes and clinical implications. Numerous reflexes arise from mechanoreceptor activation. The Hering–Breuer reflex helps prevent overinflation. The irritant receptor and cough reflex protect against aspiration and inhaled irritants. The negative-pressure reflex, triggered by airway obstruction and large inspiratory efforts, can cause sudden increases in sympathetic outflow and cardiovascular effects (important in obstructive sleep apnea and acute upper-airway obstruction). Clinically, mechanoreceptor dysfunction or altered reflexes can contribute to disorders such as chronic cough, ventilatory pattern abnormalities and altered breathlessness perception.

# Automatic (brainstem) versus voluntary (cortical) respiratory pathways

Respiration is unique among autonomic processes for being both automatic and under voluntary control. Automatic breathing is generated and regulated by brainstem networks that produce rhythmic motor output, whereas voluntary breathing originates from cortical and subcortical structures that can override or modulate automatic rhythms.

Brainstem automatic pathways. The core automatic respiratory rhythm generator lies in the medulla and pons. The pre-Bötzinger complex in the ventrolateral medulla is considered a primary expiratory–inspiratory rhythmogenic kernel; it interacts with adjacent groups (e.g., Bötzinger complex, ventral and dorsal respiratory groups) to shape the basic respiratory cycle. The dorsal respiratory group (within the nucleus tractus solitarius) processes afferent sensory input—particularly from the vagus and carotid bodies—and integrates chemoreceptive and mechanoreceptive signals. The ventral respiratory group contains premotor neurons that project to spinal respiratory motoneurons controlling the diaphragm (via the phrenic nerve) and other respiratory muscles. Pontine centers (e.g., the pneumotaxic and apneustic centers) modulate the pattern and transition between inspiration and expiration.

Neurotransmitters and modulatory influences. Automatic rhythmicity and chemosensitivity are strongly influenced by monoaminergic neuromodulators (serotonin, norepinephrine), glutamate, GABA and peptides. These modulatory inputs alter chemoreceptor gain, neuronal excitability and the stability of the rhythm generator. This is why sedative drugs, opioids and certain neurologic disorders can profoundly depress automatic respiratory drive.

Voluntary and behavioral pathways. Volitional breathing arises from the motor cortex and supplementary motor area, enabling voluntary breath-holding, speech, singing, swallowing coordination, and complex behaviors. Cortical neurons project to respiratory motoneurons via corticospinal tracts and can bypass or transiently override brainstem pattern generators. For example, during breath-holding, cortical inhibition of automatic inspiratory drive prevents immediate inspiration despite rising CO2. Volitional control also uses cortico–bulbar pathways to coordinate upper airway and laryngeal muscles for airway protection and phonation.

Interaction and hierarchy. The automatic system is dominant for homeostatic control but is highly susceptible to suprapontine modulation. During wakefulness, cortical inputs and behavioral states reset the set-point and responsiveness of brainstem circuits, while during sleep the loss of cortical facilitation unmasks the intrinsic properties of the automatic network (with important clinical consequences). The ability of cortical commands to transiently override chemoreceptor-driven automatic breathing explains phenomena such as voluntary hyperventilation and breath-holding, and underlies therapeutic strategies like respiratory muscle training and cortical retraining for functional dyspnoea.

# Sleep-state changes in ventilatory control and upper airway tone

Sleep imposes profound changes on ventilatory control, chemoreflex function and upper airway mechanics, predisposing to hypoventilation and obstructive events. Differences between non-REM (NREM) and REM sleep further influence the pattern and stability of breathing.

Reduced wakefulness drive and altered chemoreflexes. Wakefulness provides a tonic excitatory input to brainstem respiratory networks (the "wakefulness stimulus"). Upon transition to sleep, particularly to NREM sleep, this tonic drive decreases. Consequently, ventilation falls by roughly 1–2 L·min^-1 in normal adults during stable NREM sleep compared with quiet wakefulness, leading to a slight rise in PaCO2 (typically ~2–8 mmHg) and a mild fall in PaO2. During sleep there is also a reduction in the slope of the chemoreflex responses: both central and peripheral chemoreceptor sensitivities to CO2 and hypoxia are blunted, particularly during REM sleep. This reduced responsiveness increases the likelihood that perturbations in gas exchange (e.g., due to airway obstruction or hypoventilation) will persist and lead to hypercapnia and hypoxia before corrective ventilatory responses occur.

Changes in respiratory pattern. Sleep is associated with more regular breathing in stable NREM but can show periodic breathing (cyclical fluctuations in tidal volume and PaCO2) in certain pathologic states (e.g., congestive heart failure). REM sleep produces an irregular breathing pattern with variable tidal volumes and respiratory rate, and transient suppression of intercostal and accessory muscle activity.

Upper airway tone. Upper airway dilator muscle tone, particularly of the genioglossus, decreases in sleep. Loss of tonic pharyngeal dilator muscle activity narrows the pharyngeal lumen and increases airway collapsibility. These changes are most pronounced in REM sleep when muscle atonia develops in many skeletal muscles (except the diaphragm), further elevating the risk of upper airway collapse. Anatomical predisposition (obesity, craniofacial features) combined with sleep-related reductions in neuromuscular compensation underlies obstructive sleep apnea (OSA).

Clinical consequences. The combined effects—increased upper airway collapsibility, diminished ventilatory drive and blunted chemoreflexes—mean that hypoventilation syndromes and sleep-disordered breathing are highly influenced by sleep. Therapeutic strategies (CPAP for OSA, noninvasive ventilation for sleep hypoventilation) target either airway patency or augment ventilatory drive/support. In addition, sedatives and alcohol exacerbate sleep-related reductions in airway tone and chemoreceptor responsiveness, increasing risk.

# Control of breathing during arousal and response to hypoxia/hypercapnia

Arousal from sleep is a critical defense against prolonged hypoxemia and hypercapnia. The integration of chemoreceptive and mechanoreceptive inputs triggers arousal and an augmented ventilatory response, but the pathways are complex, and the outcome depends on the severity and duration of the insult and the sleep state.

Arousal physiology. Arousal mechanisms involve ascending reticular activating system pathways and limbic structures. Chemoreceptor input—particularly from the carotid bodies—ascends to the NTS and then to arousal-promoting nuclei in the brainstem and forebrain. Mechanical perturbations such as large negative intrathoracic pressures (from inspiratory efforts against an occluded airway) also trigger arousal via vagal and spinal afferents. Arousal restores cortical input to respiratory centers, increases sympathetic outflow, enhances upper airway dilator muscle activity and leads to a surge in ventilation. This rapid restoration of ventilatory drive and muscle tone often terminates obstructive events, but repeated arousals fragment sleep and may perpetuate a cycle of instability in sleep-disordered breathing.

Responses to hypoxia and hypercapnia. Peripheral chemoreceptors produce rapid ventilatory increases in response to hypoxia, with maximal sensitivity when PaO2 falls below ~60 mmHg. The magnitude of the ventilatory response is modulated by concurrent PaCO2: the hypoxic ventilatory response is potentiated by hypercapnia and attenuated by hypocapnia. Central chemoreceptors drive ventilation in response to rising PaCO2; this response is graded and somewhat slower. Together, hypoxia and hypercapnia produce synergistic stimulation of ventilation. Clinically, the speed and magnitude of these responses determine whether gas exchange perturbations are corrected by increased minute ventilation or whether arousal is required.

Pathophysiologic considerations. In heart failure and central sleep apnea (Cheyne–Stokes respiration), an exaggerated ventilatory chemoreflex gain and long circulatory delay can lead to overshoot–undershoot cycles in ventilation (high loop gain) and recurrent central apneas. Conversely, opioid overdose, intoxication, or neurologic injury can blunt chemoreflexes and raise the threshold for arousal, causing dangerous hypoventilation without timely arousal. In OSA, arousal is often necessary to restore airway patency, but repeated arousals degrade sleep quality and may worsen daytime symptoms.

Clinical thresholds and decision points. When treating patients with sleep-disordered breathing or suspected hypoventilation, it is important to recognize the interplay between chemical stimuli and arousal thresholds. For example, therapies that raise arousal threshold (e.g., sedatives) may reduce sleep fragmentation but risk allowing more prolonged hypoxemia; conversely, increasing ventilatory support or improving airway patency can reduce the need for arousal and improve sleep continuity.

# Physiology of ventilatory drive and perception of dyspnoea

Ventilatory drive encompasses the central motor output generated by respiratory centers in response to chemoreceptive, mechanoreceptive and behavioral inputs, while dyspnoea (subjective breathlessness) reflects conscious perception of breathing effort, air hunger, chest tightness, or suffocation. Understanding the physiology and neural basis of drive and dyspnoea clarifies why patients with similar gas exchange can have very different symptom burdens.

Components of ventilatory drive. The primary determinant of automatic ventilatory drive is the central integration of chemoreceptor signals (PaCO2/H+ and PaO2), mechanoreceptor feedback, and higher-order behavioral commands. The central chemoreceptor–mediated component provides a tonic drive proportional to arterial CO2. Superimposed on that are phasic inputs from peripheral chemoreceptors and mechanoreceptors that adjust the timing and amplitude of each breath. Motor output is translated into muscle contraction by respiratory motoneurons; the effectiveness of this drive depends on respiratory muscle strength and mechanical properties of the lung–chest wall.

Neural correlates of dyspnoea. Dyspnoea arises when there is a mismatch between the ventilatory demand (set by chemoreceptors or behavioral intention) and achieved ventilation (as sensed by afferent feedback). There are three common mechanisms: (1) increased drive relative to ventilation (e.g., acute hypercapnia or hypoxia where the brain sends a strong drive but ventilation is insufficient), (2) increased work or load on the respiratory muscles (e.g., COPD, obesity) such that normal drive produces insufficient ventilatory output, and (3) direct activation of chemoreceptors or sensory afferents that produce unpleasant sensations (e.g., pulmonary embolism stimulating J receptors causing air hunger).

Neuroanatomically, perception of respiratory sensations involves insular cortex, anterior cingulate, somatosensory cortices, and limbic structures (amygdala). Functional imaging studies show that air hunger and inspiratory load evoke consistent activation patterns in these regions, which underlie the affective and cognitive components of dyspnoea. Cognitive appraisal, anxiety, prior experience and attention modulate the intensity of perceived breathlessness.

Quantification and clinical thresholds. Ventilatory drive is often assessed indirectly by measuring mouth occlusion pressure at 0.1 s (P0.1) as a simple index of central respiratory motor output; normal P0.1 is typically <0.6 kPa (≈6 cm H2O) at rest, with larger values indicating increased central drive. Maximal inspiratory pressure (MIP; normal roughly −75 to −120 cm H2O in healthy young adults) and negative inspiratory force provide measures of muscle strength; a low MIP indicates potential failure to generate adequate ventilation despite normal drive. Arterial blood gases remain central to assessment: rising PaCO2 reflects inadequate ventilation for metabolic CO2 load, while hypoxemia may increase drive via peripheral chemoreceptors.

Clinical implications and management. Effective clinical assessment of dyspnoea requires integrating objective measures (blood gases, spirometry, P0.1, MIP, polysomnography) with subjective report and examination. Treatments may target the underlying cause—reducing ventilatory load (bronchodilators, diuresis), supporting ventilation (oxygen, noninvasive ventilation), or modulating perception (anxiolytics, pulmonary rehabilitation, cognitive-behavioral therapy). Recognize that in some conditions (e.g., neuromuscular weakness) ventilatory drive may be high but ineffective, producing severe dyspnoea with minimal change in PaCO2 early on; in others (e.g., central hypoventilation) drive may be low, producing blunted dyspnoea despite dangerous hypercapnia.

Summary

Respiratory control is a dynamic interplay between chemical sensing (central and peripheral chemoreceptors), mechanical inputs (pulmonary and chest wall receptors), intrinsic brainstem rhythm generators and modulatory suprapontine influences that allow voluntary control. Sleep and arousal state profoundly modify chemoreflex sensitivity and upper airway mechanics, with important clinical consequences for hypoventilation and airway collapse. Ventilatory drive reflects the integrated motor output required to maintain gas exchange, while perception of dyspnoea arises from cerebral processing of motor commands and sensory feedback. Mastery of these basic physiological principles enables clinicians to interpret respiratory symptoms, tailor ventilatory support, and understand the pathophysiology of a wide range of respiratory disorders.
