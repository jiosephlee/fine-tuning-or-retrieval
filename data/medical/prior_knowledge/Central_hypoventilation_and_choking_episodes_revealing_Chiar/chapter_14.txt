Chapter 14: Neuropathology of Brainstem Compression and Syrinx Formation

Introduction

Chronic compression of the brainstem and the formation of intramedullary cavities (syringomyelia) are important causes of progressive neurological dysfunction. The neuropathology of these processes reflects a spectrum of mechanical, vascular, and secondary degenerative changes that evolve over time and determine clinical presentation and outcome. Understanding the cascade from initial mechanical deformation through ischemia, ependymal breach, gliosis, demyelination, and neuronal loss is essential for interpreting clinical signs, guiding imaging interpretation, anticipating complications, and recognizing factors that influence recovery after decompression. This chapter reviews the cellular and structural changes that occur with protracted brainstem compression and syrinx formation, addresses current models of syringogenesis emphasizing fluid dynamics and perivascular flow, describes the characteristic microscopic pathology, links anatomical injury to respiratory, cranial nerve, and spinal cord deficits, discusses the extent to which changes are reversible after relief of compression, and summarizes insights gained from animal models.

# Ischemic and degenerative changes from chronic compression

Chronic mechanical compression subjects the brainstem to sustained deformation and altered microvascular perfusion. The brainstem's dense concentration of vital nuclei and long tracts means that even modest, persistent pressure can produce significant dysfunction. Pathophysiologically, compression raises interstitial and intravascular pressures locally and disrupts autoregulatory mechanisms, leading to a combination of reduced capillary perfusion, venous congestion, and ischemia-reperfusion injury over time.

Early stages are often dominated by focal ischemia driven by arteriolar narrowing and compromised capillary flow. Unlike large-artery occlusion, compression produces a gradient of ischemia: subependymal and perivascular regions, watershed regions between vascular territories, and regions with relatively tenuous collateralization are most vulnerable. Neurons are highly susceptible to hypoxia; within minutes they manifest reversible changes such as dendritic beading and cytoplasmic eosinophilia, whereas irreversible neuronal loss develops over hours to days if perfusion is not restored. In the chronically compressed brainstem, repeated episodes of low-grade ischemia and intermittent hypoperfusion promote cumulative neuronal and axonal injury.

Venous outflow obstruction is a key contributor. Compression impairs small venous drainage and leads to increased interstitial fluid, tissue edema, and elevated tissue pressure that further diminishes capillary perfusion. Chronic venous hypertension also predisposes to blood–brain barrier disruption and capillary leakage. These processes encourage a progressive cycle: edema increases tissue pressure, reduces perfusion, worsens hypoxia, and incites further edema.

Degenerative changes in white matter are pronounced. Axonal transport is mechanically and metabolically compromised, leading to axonal swelling, fragmentation, and eventual Wallerian-type degeneration both anterograde and retrograde relative to the site of compression. Myelin sheaths disintegrate secondarily to axonal loss and to direct oligodendrocyte injury from ischemia. Chronic compression also induces microglial and astrocytic activation, driving phagocytosis of myelin and cellular debris and establishing a gliotic scar that replaces lost parenchyma.

Chronic deformation also stimulates extracellular matrix remodeling and fibrosis, particularly in subpial and subependymal zones where meningeal and ependymal interfaces experience shear stress. This remodeling can stiffen the region and perpetuate focal compression even after partial surgical decompression, contributing to persistent deficits.

Clinically, the interplay of ischemia and degeneration explains progressive deficits that may worsen insidiously. Signs reflecting neurons with high metabolic demand or long axons (e.g., bulbar nuclei, corticospinal tracts) often manifest early. The timescale of degeneration is variable and influenced by degree and intermittency of compression, vascular reserve, and individual tissue susceptibility.

# Pathophysiology of syrinx formation: fluid dynamics, perivascular flow and ependymal injury

Syringomyelia—formation of an intramedullary fluid-filled cavity (syrinx)—has complex and evolving pathogenetic hypotheses. Contemporary understanding integrates altered cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) dynamics, transmission of pulsatile pressures into the cord, disruption of ependymal integrity, and interstitial and perivascular fluid flow.

A cardinal initiating factor is abnormal CSF flow in the subarachnoid space around the foramen magnum or spinal canal. Chiari malformations, arachnoid scarring, tumors, and post-inflammatory adhesions can produce focal obstruction or impedance to normal craniospinal CSF oscillations. With each cardiac cycle, arterial expansion transmits pulsations to the CSF; when outflow is impeded, pressure waves are amplified or redistributed. Two broad mechanisms are emphasized:

1) Pulsation-driven transmission into the cord: Elevated subarachnoid pressure waves may be transmitted into the spinal cord parenchyma via perivascular (Virchow-Robin) spaces surrounding penetrating arteries. These spaces serve as conduits for fluid oscillations; forced transmission of augmented pulses can drive interstitial fluid movement centrally, progressively distending the parenchyma and coalescing into a cavity.

2) One-way valve or piston mechanism: Dynamic crowding at the foramen magnum or focal arachnoid narrowings may produce a piston-like effect, where downward movement of the cerebellar tonsils during systole forces CSF into spinal subarachnoid spaces but prevents its return during diastole. This cyclic pressure differential can drive fluid into the cord. Similarly, spinal canal crowding during trauma or degenerative disease can cause segmental piston effects.

Ependymal injury is central to cavitation. The ependyma provides a barrier between CSF and the spinal cord parenchyma; mechanical stress, ischemia, inflammation, or direct shear forces can denude or disrupt the ependymal lining. Once breached, CSF or interstitial fluid gains access to the central canal or parenchymal extracellular space, allowing small foci of edema to expand. Progressive dissolution or coalescence of microcystic spaces forming around necrotic or degenerated tissue creates a macroscopic syrinx.

Perivascular flow plays a key role in maintenance and expansion of a syrinx. Experimental and imaging studies suggest that fluid can track along perivascular channels from the subarachnoid space into the cord. Aquaporin channels (notably aquaporin-4 on astrocytic endfeet) modulate water movement in the interstitial space; altered expression or dysfunction of these channels in response to chronic stress may change water handling and favor cavity formation.

Hydrodynamic considerations also explain directional growth of a syrinx. Pressure gradients, tissue compliance, and tethering determine whether a cavity expands longitudinally (along the cord), centrally (toward the central canal), or laterally (toward dorsal or ventral horns). Tethering at the foramen magnum or adhesions can focus distention in particular segments. Additionally, repetitive micro-injuries from pulsatile flow and shear stress can cause progressive tissue breakdown at the cavity margins.

Inflammation contributes as well. Chemical mediators released from injured tissue and infiltrating inflammatory cells can increase vascular permeability and promote degeneration. However, syrinx fluid is usually largely acellular and similar to CSF chemistry, which suggests that pure inflammatory exudation is not the sole mechanism.

In summary, syrinx formation is best conceptualized as a dynamic process in which abnormal CSF pulsations and impeded subarachnoid flow transmit forces into the cord via perivascular routes and through damaged ependyma, leading to interstitial fluid accumulation, tissue necrosis, and cavity coalescence.

# Microscopic features: gliosis, demyelination and neuronal loss in brainstem nuclei

Microscopically, chronic brainstem compression and syringomyelia share several pathological hallmarks: reactive gliosis, variable demyelination, axonal pathology, neuronal loss in selective nuclei, and changes in the ependyma and microvasculature.

Gliosis is the most consistent and prominent feature. Astrocytes proliferate and hypertrophy, expressing increased glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), and form dense astrocytic scars that replace lost neuropil. In chronic cavities such as syringes, the cavity walls are often lined by reactive astrocytes and connective tissue rather than intact ependyma. This gliotic border can contain processes that bridge across the cavity and is associated with deposition of extracellular matrix proteins, including collagen and laminin, particularly when meningeal fibrosis or chronic inflammation is present.

Demyelination is prominent in regions subject to axonal degeneration or ischemia. Oligodendrocytes are susceptible to hypoxic injury; in chronic compression zones there is a loss of mature oligodendrocytes and marked reduction of myelin basic protein (MBP) staining. Myelin loss may be diffuse in white matter tracts near a syrinx or focal around areas of necrosis. Secondary demyelination often follows axonal transection, seen as Wallerian degeneration with accumulation of myelin debris and subsequent phagocytosis by microglia and macrophages.

Neuronal loss in brainstem nuclei is selective but clinically consequential. Motor nuclei (e.g., hypoglossal, nucleus ambiguus), respiratory centers in the ventrolateral medulla, and cranial nerve nuclei (III–XII depending on level of compression) can display shrunken, hypereosinophilic neurons or eventually be absent in regions of advanced atrophy. Neuronal loss is accompanied by microglial activation and often by myelin pallor in associated tracts.

Axonal pathology can be identified as spheroids—focal axonal swellings containing neurofilaments and organelles—on microscopy. Over time, these progress to fragmentation and clearance. The corticospinal tracts and long ascending sensory tracts show both primary axonal injury within compressed segments and trans-synaptic degeneration in connected regions.

Ependymal changes are central to syringomyelia pathology. The ependymal lining can display denudation, hypertrophy of residual cells, and focal accumulation of lysosomal material. In syrinx cavities, the ependymal lining is often discontinuous; where present it can show cuboidal to columnar cell morphology with variable ciliation, or a complete loss leading to astrogliotic cavity walls.

Microvascular changes include capillary rarefaction, basement membrane thickening, and perivascular fibrosis. Red blood cell extravasation or hemosiderin deposition may be present where repeated microhemorrhages have occurred. Chronic venous congestion can be evident as dilation of small veins and venules, and may be associated with hemosiderin-laden macrophages.

Immunohistochemical studies often reveal upregulation of markers for reactive astrocytes (GFAP), activated microglia (Iba1, CD68), and changes in water channel expression (aquaporin-4) at cavity margins. The pattern of these changes informs the chronicity: dense gliosis and extensive matrix deposition indicate a longstanding lesion, whereas more macrophage-rich histology with fresh axonal spheroids suggests an active, evolving process.

# Correlation of neuropathology with respiratory, cranial nerve and spinal cord signs

Clinical deficits map closely to the anatomic distribution of neuropathologic changes. Understanding the relationship between histopathology and functional anatomy enables clinicians to interpret presenting syndromes and predict progression.

Respiratory dysfunction arises from involvement of medullary respiratory centers and their afferent and efferent pathways. The pre-Bötzinger complex, dorsal respiratory group, and ventral respiratory group reside in the ventrolateral medulla and are metabolically active and highly interconnected. Neuronal loss or gliosis in these regions—through direct compression, ischemia, or syrinx extension—can impair rhythmogenesis and the integration of chemosensory inputs. Additionally, involvement of cranial nerve nuclei (e.g., nucleus ambiguus) compromises glossopharyngeal and vagal control of upper airway patency and laryngeal function, predisposing to aspiration and sleep-disordered breathing. Pathologically, even partial loss of these neuronal populations can generate clinically significant hypoventilation, abnormal respiratory patterning (e.g., ataxic breathing), or impaired cough reflex.

Cranial nerve signs reflect focal injury to the respective nuclei and exiting nerve roots within the brainstem. For instance, compression at the pontomedullary junction can produce facial weakness from facial nucleus involvement, abducens palsy from nucleus/ fascicle damage, and trigeminal sensory loss from trigeminal nucleus compromise. Pathological correlates include neuronal shrinkage or loss in the targeted nuclei, demyelination of cranial nerve fascicles, and gliotic scarring replacing damaged tissue. Lower cranial nerve dysfunction (IX–XII) often signals medullary involvement and may manifest as dysphagia, dysphonia, and impaired gag reflex, corresponding to neuronal loss in nucleus ambiguus and hypoglossal nucleus. Since the nuclei are small and functionally specialized, even localized microscopic damage produces distinct clinical syndromes.

Spinal cord signs in syringomyelia depend on both the cavity’s location and the degree of surrounding tract involvement. Central cavitation classically produces a "cape-like" distribution of dissociated sensory loss (pain and temperature) in the upper extremities because the crossing fibers of the spinothalamic tract in the anterior commissure are interrupted early. Microscopically, this corresponds to central cavitation with adjacent gliosis and degeneration of decussating spinothalamic fibers. As the syrinx expands dorsally or laterally, dorsal horn neurons and dorsal columns become affected, producing impairment of proprioception and vibration. Ventral expansion can damage corticospinal tracts, producing weakness and upper motor neuron signs below the lesion; pathological correlates are demyelination and axonal loss in the lateral corticospinal tracts. Motor neuron pools in the anterior horn may undergo degeneration if the cavity reaches the ventral horn, resulting in lower motor neuron signs at the level of the lesion.

Autonomic dysfunction—e.g., Horner’s syndrome or autonomic dysreflexia—arises from involvement of sympathetic pathways or interconnections in the intermediolateral cell columns (thoracic cord) and descending autonomic fibers. Microscopically, this corresponds to disruption of white matter tracts and neuronal loss in relevant autonomic centers.

Importantly, symptom patterns may evolve as syrinxes enlarge or as compressive lesions progress. Focal ischemic damage may initially cause transient dysfunction, whereas permanent neurological deficits are more likely when neuronal loss and gliotic scarring are established.

# Reversibility of changes after decompression and factors predicting recovery

The potential for neurological recovery after surgical decompression depends on the nature and chronicity of the underlying pathology. Reversible elements include edema, transient conduction block, and early demyelination where axons remain intact. Irreversible elements include frank neuronal loss, extensive axonal transection, and dense gliosis with matrix deposition.

At the cellular level, relief of compression often leads to reduction in interstitial edema and restoration of microvascular perfusion, which can rescue neurons and oligodendrocytes that have suffered sublethal injury. Early remyelination may occur if oligodendrocyte progenitor cells survive and axons remain structurally intact; this process can improve conduction over weeks to months. In syringomyelia, decompression that restores normal CSF flow may halt syrinx expansion and allow gradual collapse of the cavity. Following collapse, surrounding tissue can show partial reconstitution with decreased edema and reduced gliosis, and some degree of functional improvement is often seen.

However, established gliotic scars and loss of neurons limit recovery. Astrocytic scars replace lost neural elements and form physical and chemical barriers to axonal regeneration. Where corticospinal axons have been transected, functional recovery is typically poor because central axons regenerate very poorly in the adult CNS. Additionally, changes such as perivascular fibrosis and microvascular basement membrane thickening may persist, limiting full restoration of tissue homeostasis.

Predictors of recovery identified in clinical and pathological studies include:

- Duration of symptoms: Shorter intervals between symptom onset and decompression are strongly associated with greater recovery. Chronic deficits (months to years) are less likely to improve substantially.
- Severity and progression: Rapidly progressive or severe deficits—especially those associated with radiographic evidence of extensive cavitation or marked atrophy—carry a poorer prognosis.
- Imaging correlates: Smaller syrinx size, less spinal cord atrophy, and absence of extensive T2 hyperintense signal in adjacent cord tissue predict better outcomes. Signal changes that normalize after decompression correlate with reversible edema rather than permanent tissue loss.
- Age and comorbidities: Younger patients often have more plasticity and potential for remyelination; vascular comorbidities (e.g., diabetes, atherosclerosis) impair microvascular recovery and are associated with worse outcomes.
- Presence of residual mechanical factors: Persistent CSF flow obstruction, arachnoid scarring, or bony abnormalities that are not fully corrected increase the risk of recurrence and limit recovery.
- Electrophysiological findings: Preserved evoked potentials and motor-evoked potentials preoperatively predict better functional recovery—these suggest intact axonal conduction despite clinical deficits.

Timing of decompression is crucial. Because early ischemic and demyelinating changes are amenable to reversal, prompt restoration of normal pressure and flow dynamics yields the best chance of meaningful neurological improvement. Conversely, once neuronal loss and dense gliosis are established, expect limited restitution of function.

# Animal models and experimental insights into syringogenesis

Experimental animal models have been indispensable for dissecting the mechanisms of syrinx formation and testing interventions. Models have used mechanical obstruction, syrinx induction by fluid injection, chronic compression, and genetic or developmental manipulation to reproduce features of human syringomyelia.

Classic models include:

- Craniospinal CSF obstruction models: Insertion of a small mass or adhesive at the foramen magnum or creation of arachnoid scarring in cats, dogs, and rodents produces altered CSF flow and, in many cases, syrinx formation downstream in the spinal cord. These models support the role of impaired subarachnoid CSF dynamics and transmit the piston mechanism of tonsillar descent in syrinx genesis.

- Pressure-pulse models: Controlled application of pulsatile pressure to subarachnoid spaces has induced syrinxes, indicating that transmission of high-amplitude pulsations into the cord is sufficient to drive cavity formation, particularly when accompanied by perivascular pathway access.

- Intramedullary injection models: Injection of small volumes of fluid or blood products directly into the cord parenchyma can create cystic cavities that evolve into syrinx-like structures, highlighting the role of ependymal disruption and local tissue necrosis. These models also reproduce gliotic margins and demyelination seen in human syringes.

- Tethering and traction models: Spinal cord tethering models show that mechanical tension can alter CSF flow and promote cavitation, suggesting that mechanical stretch and altered tissue mechanics contribute to syringogenesis.

Key insights from animal studies:

- Perivascular routes are primary conduits: Tracer studies demonstrate that fluid introduced into the subarachnoid space can enter the spinal cord parenchyma via perivascular spaces, lending strong evidence to models positing that perivascular flow is central to syrinx development.

- Ependymal disruption is critical: Animals with preserved ependyma and normal subarachnoid flow rarely form syrinxes, even when subjected to elevated pulsatile pressure. Conversely, deliberate ependymal injury greatly increases syrinx risk.

- Role of aquaporin-4: Experimental modulation of aquaporin-4 expression alters interstitial fluid movement and can modify syrinx progression. Knockout or pharmacologic modulation in rodents indicates that aquaporin-mediated water transport contributes to cavity dynamics.

- Reversibility depends on timing: Early decompression in animal models often leads to syrinx regression and functional improvement, mirroring clinical observations. Delayed intervention yields persistent cavities lined by gliosis and limited recovery.

- Inflammation and scarring modulate outcomes: Models that introduce inflammatory components (e.g., blood products into the subarachnoid space) demonstrate increased arachnoid scarring, disrupted CSF flow, and a higher propensity for syringogenesis, reinforcing the clinical linkage between meningitis/hemorrhage and syrinx formation.

Limitations of animal models include species differences in CSF dynamics, cord size and compliance, and relative paucity of congenital Chiari analogs. Nevertheless, these models clarify mechanisms—particularly the interplay of pulsatile forces, perivascular pathways, ependymal integrity, and astrocytic responses—and they provide preclinical platforms for testing interventions such as syrinx shunting, arachnoid dissection techniques, and molecular modulators of fluid transport.

Conclusion

Neuropathology of chronic brainstem compression and syrinx formation reflects an integrated process of mechanical stress, vascular compromise, fluid dynamics, and secondary degenerative change. Ischemia, venous congestion, gliosis, demyelination, neuronal loss, and ependymal disruption compose the histopathologic spectrum that determines clinical manifestations affecting respiratory control, cranial nerve function, and spinal cord systems. Clinical recovery after decompression is best when intervention occurs before irreversible neuronal loss and dense gliosis have developed; animal models affirm the central roles of perivascular flow and ependymal integrity in syringogenesis and offer avenues for therapeutic innovation. A mechanistic appreciation of these processes enhances clinical reasoning about the natural history, imaging findings, and prognosis of patients with brainstem compression and syringomyelia.
