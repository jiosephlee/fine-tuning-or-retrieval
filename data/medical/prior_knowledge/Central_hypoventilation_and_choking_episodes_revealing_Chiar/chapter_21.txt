Introduction

Patients who present with choking, unexplained hypoxaemia, or bulbar symptoms often sit at the intersection of multiple organ systems and specialties. The immediate clinical imperative is to rapidly identify treatable, time-sensitive causes (airway obstruction, aspiration, myasthenic crisis, pulmonary embolism, acute brainstem pathology) while organizing a coordinated, efficient diagnostic pathway that minimizes redundant testing and delays. This chapter reviews the roles of the core specialties involved in these presentations, presents practical stepwise diagnostic algorithms for dysphagia with normal endoscopy, describes indications and timing for neuroimaging in bulbar or respiratory presentations, explains how to integrate sleep, swallowing and pulmonary studies into a coherent workup, details preoperative assessment and optimization before posterior fossa surgery, and outlines strategies for interdisciplinary communication and shared decision-making about conservative versus surgical management. The approach is clinically oriented and emphasizes pathophysiology and decision points that guide rational test selection and specialist involvement.

# Roles of primary care, neurology, neurosurgery, ENT, gastroenterology, pulmonology and sleep medicine

Effective care begins with clear role delineation and early communication among disciplines. Primary care or the emergency clinician is usually the first contact and is responsible for initial stabilization, triage, and early recognition of red flags (airway compromise, hypoxaemia refractory to supplemental oxygen, bulbar weakness with poor airway protection, stridor, rapidly progressive neurologic deficit). Primary clinicians must perform a focused history and exam to categorize symptoms as oropharyngeal versus esophageal dysphagia, isolate respiratory versus neurological causes of hypoxaemia, and initiate immediate management such as airway protection, supplemental oxygen, empiric antibiotics if aspiration pneumonia is suspected, or urgent neuroimaging when indicated.

Neurology is central when bulbar signs (dysarthria, dysphagia, nasal regurgitation, pharyngeal weakness), fluctuating fatigability, cranial nerve findings, or other focal brainstem signs are present. Neurologists lead the evaluation for neuromuscular causes (myasthenia gravis, motor neuron disease, neuropathies, myopathies), central nervous system causes (ischemic or hemorrhagic brainstem stroke, demyelinating disease), and coordinate electrophysiologic testing (repetitive nerve stimulation, single-fiber EMG), neuroimmunologic testing (serologies for acetylcholine receptor and MuSK antibodies), and directed imaging.

Neurosurgery must be involved when structural central nervous system pathology is suspected or confirmed that may require surgical decompression—posterior fossa lesions, Chiari malformation with brainstem compression, tumors compressing lower cranial nerves, or unstable cervical spine pathology. Neurosurgeons evaluate surgical candidacy, plan approaches that consider cranial nerve and airway risk, and coordinate perioperative planning with anesthesiology and critical care.

Ear, Nose and Throat (ENT) specialists have primary responsibility for direct visualization of the upper aerodigestive tract, airway evaluation, and procedural interventions to protect the airway. ENT performs flexible nasendoscopy, transnasal fiberoptic laryngoscopy, and in-office or operative microlaryngoscopy with diagnostic or therapeutic interventions (biopsy, dilation). ENT is essential for evaluation of mechanical obstructions (tumors, vocal cord paralysis, laryngeal inflammation), decision-making about temporary airway procedures (nasopharyngeal airway, tracheostomy), and coordinating laryngeal electromyography (laryngeal EMG) when vocal cord paresis of uncertain etiology is present.

Gastroenterology (GI) focuses on distal swallowing mechanisms and esophageal causes of dysphagia and reflux-related respiratory disease. GI performs upper endoscopy (esophagogastroduodenoscopy, EGD), assesses for peptic strictures, rings, webs, eosinophilic esophagitis, and may perform dilation or therapeutic interventions. When upper endoscopy is normal, GI often coordinates esophageal physiologic testing such as high-resolution manometry and ambulatory pH or pH-impedance monitoring to detect motility disorders, achalasia variants, and pathological reflux that can cause cough or aspiration.

Pulmonology evaluates respiratory causes of hypoxaemia, aspiration pneumonitis/pneumonia, chronic aspiration leading to bronchiectasis, and primary lung disease that may account for dyspnea or hypoxaemia. Pulmonologists perform bronchoscopy for airway visualization and lavage, order pulmonary function testing (spirometry, lung volumes, diffusion capacity), assess gas exchange with arterial blood gases, and recommend interventions such as airway clearance techniques, noninvasive ventilation, and tracheostomy management. In suspected aspiration, bronchoscopy can obtain samples for culture and cytology.

Sleep medicine contributes when symptoms suggest sleep-disordered breathing (snoring, witnessed apneas, daytime somnolence) or when nocturnal hypoxaemia might explain unexplained hypoxaemic episodes. Sleep specialists order polysomnography or home sleep apnea testing, guide positive airway pressure therapy, and assess interplay between bulbar dysfunction and nocturnal hypoventilation in neuromuscular disease.

Across all these roles, multidisciplinary coordination—often via an airway/dysphagia clinic or tumor board-style meetings—reduces diagnostic delays, streamlines care, and aligns diagnostic testing with likely etiologies suggested by the initial clinical assessment.

# Stepwise diagnostic algorithms for dysphagia with normal endoscopy

Dysphagia must first be categorized as oropharyngeal (transfer or pharyngeal phase) versus esophageal (transport or obstructive), because the diagnostic sequence differs. A normal upper endoscopy (EGD) rules out mucosal and many structural esophageal lesions but leaves open motility disorders, oropharyngeal causes, and extrinsic compression.

When EGD is normal, begin with careful re-evaluation of the history to determine whether symptoms are consistent with oropharyngeal dysphagia—immediate cough or choking on swallowing, nasal regurgitation, multiple swallows needed to clear, and change in voice quality—or with esophageal dysphagia—sensation of food sticking in chest, delayed onset after swallowing, and relation to solids versus liquids. Physical examination should include cranial nerve testing (especially IX, X, XI, XII), evaluation of cough and gag, assessment for sarcopenia, and observation of a bedside water swallow with pulse oximetry when aspiration risk is uncertain.

If oropharyngeal dysphagia is suspected despite normal EGD, the next diagnostic step is an instrumental swallowing assessment. Videofluoroscopic Swallow Study (VFSS), also known as a modified barium swallow, allows dynamic visualization of oral and pharyngeal phases, documentation of aspiration (silent or overt), quantification of penetration-aspiration, and assessment of compensatory maneuvers and diet modification. Fiberoptic Endoscopic Evaluation of Swallowing (FEES) complements VFSS by providing endoscopic views of pharyngeal anatomy and pooling, and is better for repeated bedside assessments and for assessing vocal fold mobility. The choice between FEES and VFSS depends on available resources and the specific clinical question; both are complementary and often used sequentially.

If instrumental studies show pharyngeal weakness or neuropathic features, neurology referral for electrophysiologic testing (repetitive nerve stimulation, single-fiber EMG) and serologic testing (acetylcholine receptor antibodies, MuSK, titin in appropriate contexts) is indicated to evaluate for neuromuscular junction disorders. Laryngeal EMG may help distinguish vocal fold paresis from neuromuscular disease and guide prognosis.

If instrumental studies are normal or suggest esophageal dysphagia despite prior normal EGD, esophageal physiologic testing is the next step. High-resolution esophageal manometry (HRM) assesses peristalsis and esophagogastric junction function and can diagnose achalasia subtypes, esophagogastric junction outflow obstruction, distal esophageal spasm, ineffective motility, or absent peristalsis. When HRM is abnormal, targeted interventions follow; when HRM is normal but symptoms persist, ambulatory pH-impedance monitoring helps ascertain pathologic acid or non-acid reflux contributing to symptoms or reflux-related aspiration risk. Timed barium esophagram may detect subtle structural abnormalities missed by EGD such as pseudo-diverticula or intermittent obstruction.

If upper swallowing studies and esophageal physiology are inconclusive, consider cross-sectional imaging of the neck and chest (contrast CT or MRI) to identify extrinsic compression from thyroid disease, mediastinal masses, vascular rings, or cervical osteophytes. Cricopharyngeal dysfunction or Zenker diverticulum may be identified by contrast esophagram even when EGD is normal. Psychiatric and behavioral contributors to globus or functional dysphagia should be considered only after structural, neurologic, and motility causes are excluded.

Throughout this pathway, integrate nutritional assessment and aspiration risk into decision-making. If aspiration is documented or there is significant malnutrition or dehydration, involve nutrition services early and consider temporary enteral feeding strategies while diagnostic evaluation proceeds.

# When to order neuroimaging for bulbar or respiratory presentations

Neuroimaging is indicated whenever central nervous system pathology could explain bulbar dysfunction, rapidly progressive symptoms, focal neurologic deficits, or when clinical presentation raises concern for acute vascular or structural lesions. Indications include acute or subacute onset of dysarthria, dysphagia, or cranial nerve palsies suggestive of brainstem ischemia or hemorrhage; rapidly progressive bulbar weakness suggesting compressive mass or inflammatory process; new-onset stridor with neurologic signs; unexplained respiratory failure with neurologic features (altered level of consciousness, hemiparesis, ataxia); and suspected Chiari malformation or posterior fossa lesion in the context of raised intracranial pressure, occipital headaches, or cerebellar signs.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain with and without contrast is the modality of choice for posterior fossa and brainstem assessment because of superior soft tissue contrast and absence of beam-hardening artifact that degrades CT in the posterior fossa. Specific sequences should include T1-weighted, T2-weighted, fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR), diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) with apparent diffusion coefficient maps to detect acute ischemia, susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI) or gradient echo to detect hemorrhage or microbleeds, and contrast-enhanced sequences when neoplasm, demyelination, or infectious/inflammatory processes are suspected. For suspected lower cranial nerve or skull-base pathology, thin-slice MRI with focused sequences (constructive interference in steady state, CISS) and cranial nerve protocol imaging improves detection.

Computed tomography (CT) is useful in the acute setting for detecting hemorrhage, bone lesions, and for rapid assessment when MRI is unavailable or contraindicated. CT angiography (CTA) or MR angiography (MRA) is indicated when vascular etiologies such as basilar artery thrombosis, vertebral artery dissection, or vascular malformations are in the differential. Cervical spine imaging (CT or MRI) is appropriate when high cervical spondylosis, osteophyte formation, or vertebral instability is suspected to cause compressive myelopathy or lower cranial nerve dysfunction.

Timing of imaging depends on clinical acuity. In any patient with acute focal neurologic deficits or signs of brainstem involvement, early brain imaging (within hours) is required to identify ischemic stroke for reperfusion consideration or to detect hemorrhage. For chronic, progressive bulbar symptoms without red flags, outpatient MRI can be arranged, but neuroimaging should not be delayed when the cause may alter immediate management.

# Integration of sleep studies, swallowing studies and pulmonary testing into the workup

Patients with bulbar dysfunction may have overlapping problems: nocturnal hypoventilation, aspiration during sleep, obstructive or central sleep apnea, and progressive respiratory muscle weakness. Integrating polysomnography, instrumental swallowing assessment, and pulmonary function testing provides a comprehensive view of airway protection and ventilatory capacity.

Polysomnography (in-lab attended study) is indicated when there are symptoms or signs of sleep-disordered breathing, unexplained nocturnal hypoxaemia, or suspected nocturnal hypoventilation in neuromuscular disease. The study records electroencephalography, airflow, respiratory effort, pulse oximetry, and often transcutaneous or end-tidal CO2 to detect hypoventilation. Home sleep apnea testing (HSAT) may be used for straightforward suspected obstructive sleep apnea in low-comorbidity patients, but it is not adequate for diagnosing central events or hypoventilation—scenarios common in bulbar and neuromuscular disease—therefore in these patients in-lab polysomnography with CO2 monitoring is preferred.

Instrumental swallowing studies (VFSS and FEES) identify aspiration risk and guide diet modification and rehabilitation strategies. Repeated FEES can monitor progress or response to interventions (postural adjustments, thickened liquids, targeted exercises). When nocturnal aspiration is suspected, swallow studies can indicate pooling and residue that may predispose to aspiration in recumbency; combined with clinical context, this may prompt consideration of nocturnal airway protection strategies or early enteral feeding.

Pulmonary function testing (PFT) quantifies ventilatory reserve and respiratory muscle strength. Standard spirometry (forced vital capacity, FVC), lung volumes, and single-breath diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide (DLCO) provide baseline lung function. In neuromuscular or bulbar disorders, supine FVC is particularly informative because diaphragmatic weakness produces a significant drop when supine (a fall in FVC of more than 20% from upright to supine suggests diaphragmatic weakness). Normal reference ranges vary by age, sex, height and ethnicity; FVC and FEV1 are expressed as percent predicted, with values below 80% of predicted often considered abnormal. Maximal inspiratory pressure (MIP) and maximal expiratory pressure (MEP) quantify respiratory muscle strength; MIP less negative than −60 cmH2O or a maximal expiratory pressure less than 40 cmH2O suggests clinically significant respiratory muscle impairment. Arterial blood gas (ABG) analysis demonstrates gas exchange: normal PaO2 is approximately 75–100 mmHg and PaCO2 35–45 mmHg; hypercapnia indicates hypoventilation and may be present before marked oxygen desaturation.

Integration of these tests means sequencing them to answer specific clinical questions: if the chief complaint is choking with suspected aspiration, perform FEES or VFSS first to document swallowing safety. If nocturnal symptoms or unexplained daytime hypercapnia are present, perform polysomnography with CO2 and PFTs including supine FVC and MIP/MEP to assess ventilatory reserve. Findings of nocturnal hypoventilation or significant respiratory muscle weakness warrant early involvement of pulmonology and sleep medicine to consider noninvasive ventilation (NIV) and to coordinate timing of invasive airway procedures. Finally, the combined data should inform risk stratification for aspiration pneumonia, candidacy for surgical interventions, timing of enteral feeding, and perioperative respiratory planning.

# Preoperative assessment and optimization prior to posterior fossa surgery

Posterior fossa surgery carries unique perioperative risks: proximity to lower cranial nerves and brainstem respiratory centers predisposes to postoperative bulbar dysfunction, aspiration, and respiratory failure; cerebellar swelling and obstructive hydrocephalus can rapidly alter consciousness and airway protection. Preoperative assessment aims to identify modifiable risks, establish baseline neurologic and respiratory function, and plan postoperative monitoring and airway management.

Assessment should include a thorough neurologic examination focusing on cranial nerves IX–XII, speech and swallowing function, and presence of preoperative ataxia, dysmetria, or brainstem signs. Baseline instrumental swallowing assessment (FEES or VFSS) is recommended if there is any history of dysphagia, aspiration, recurrent pneumonia, or abnormal cough. Documenting baseline swallowing physiology informs intraoperative consent and postoperative expectations; if preoperative aspiration is significant, discuss the likelihood of prolonged enteral feeding or tracheostomy postoperatively.

Respiratory assessment includes PFTs with upright and supine FVC, MIP and MEP, and ABG if clinically indicated. The anesthesiology team should be engaged early to evaluate airway anatomy (Mallampati score, neck mobility), consider difficult airway risk, and plan for potential need for prolonged ventilation. In patients with neuromuscular weakness or marginal respiratory reserve (for example, FVC < 50% predicted or rapid decline), preoperative respiratory optimization may include inspiratory muscle training, chest physiotherapy, and consideration of preoperative noninvasive ventilation trials to establish tolerance.

Cardiopulmonary comorbidities should be optimized: smoking cessation, treatment of active infections, and control of heart failure and arrhythmias. Nutritional status must be assessed; malnutrition increases infection risk and delays wound healing. Correct anemia and coagulation abnormalities; reversal of anticoagulation should be planned with neurosurgery regarding timing to avoid surgical bleeding risks.

Medication review is crucial. Patients on antiplatelet or anticoagulant agents require perioperative planning for withdrawal versus bridging. Agents that exacerbate neuromuscular weakness (e.g., certain antibiotics, aminoglycosides, magnesium, or neuromuscular blockers) should be identified and managed. For patients with myasthenia gravis or other neuromuscular disorders, coordination of immunomodulatory therapy and perioperative steroid or plasmapheresis plans is essential.

Surgical planning must factor airway contingencies. The team should decide whether elective tracheostomy is indicated preoperatively (rare) or whether planned postoperative ICU admission with delayed extubation is appropriate. Criteria for extubation should be clearly defined preoperatively: adequate oxygenation on minimal ventilatory support, hemodynamic stability, intact protective airway reflexes, ability to clear secretions, and acceptable cough strength. In settings of high risk for postoperative bulbar dysfunction, plan for early bedside FEES to assess swallowing before oral intake. Postoperative arrangements should include access to speech and language therapy, physiotherapy, and nutrition support, and close ICU-level monitoring for at least 24–48 hours in high-risk cases.

# Communication strategies and shared decision-making regarding conservative versus surgical management

Management decisions frequently involve trade-offs between risks of conservative measures (ongoing aspiration, recurrent pneumonia, malnutrition) and the risks of surgery (nerve injury, postoperative respiratory failure, need for tracheostomy, neurologic deterioration). Effective communication and shared decision-making ensure patients and families understand options, likely outcomes, and uncertainties.

Begin with clear, jargon-free explanations of the problem: describe the suspected or confirmed diagnosis, the physiologic basis of symptoms (e.g., why brainstem compression causes dysphagia), and the likely natural history without intervention. Use diagrams or imaging when helpful to demonstrate anatomical relationships. Present both conservative and surgical options along with expected benefits, probabilities of improvement, and potential adverse events. For example, explain that conservative management (diet modification, swallow rehabilitation, enteral feeding) may reduce aspiration risk but may not address an underlying compressive lesion that could progress, whereas surgery may alleviate compression but carries specific risks of cranial nerve dysfunction or need for prolonged mechanical ventilation.

Use decision aids when available to quantify risks and visualize outcomes. Elicit patient values and preferences early: prioritize what matters most to the patient (preservation of speech, ability to swallow safely, avoidance of prolonged hospitalization, maximal survival). Discuss the patient’s goals of care, advance directives, and realistic postoperative recovery timelines. For patients with progressive neuromuscular disease, talk about longer-term trajectories and how an intervention aligns with overall prognosis and quality-of-life goals.

When uncertainties exist (for example, imaging shows a lesion that may or may not be the cause of symptoms), consider staged approaches and conditional plans: trial conservative therapy with defined reassessment points, or obtain additional diagnostic testing before committing to surgery. Ensure that contingency plans are explicit: if conservative measures fail within a specified period or if certain clinical thresholds are crossed (recurrent aspiration pneumonia, worsening nutritional status, progressive neurologic deficit), specify when surgical options will be reconsidered.

Interdisciplinary case conferences with the patient and family, including surgeons, neurologists, ENT, pulmonology, speech-language pathology, and nutritionists, consolidate messaging and reduce conflicting recommendations. Document the plan and ensure timely follow-up. When patients are incapacitated or decision-making capacity is impaired by neurologic disease, involve surrogates early and document the known wishes and best interests consistently with ethical and legal standards.

Conclusion

Care of patients presenting with choking, unexplained hypoxaemia, or bulbar symptoms demands a structured multidisciplinary approach that aligns initial stabilization with targeted diagnostics and thoughtful coordination of specialty care. Clear role delineation among primary care, neurology, neurosurgery, ENT, gastroenterology, pulmonology and sleep medicine streamlines evaluation. Stepwise algorithms tailored to whether dysphagia is oropharyngeal or esophageal, judicious use of neuroimaging for suspected central causes, and practical integration of sleep, swallowing and pulmonary testing enable precise diagnoses while minimizing unnecessary tests. Thorough preoperative assessment and perioperative optimization are essential prior to posterior fossa surgery. Finally, robust communication and shared decision-making centered on patient goals optimizes selection between conservative and surgical options and facilitates informed, patient-centered care.
