Introduction

Anatomic knowledge of the posterior cranial fossa and the craniocervical junction (C0–C2) is essential for understanding disorders that affect cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) flow, the position of cerebellar tonsils, and potential sites of neural compression at the foramen magnum. The region is compact: bony elements, dural reflections, cerebellar structures, cranial nerves, vertebral arteries, and CSF cisterns all lie within millimeters of one another. Small differences in bone morphology, cerebellar volume, or CSF pathways can produce large clinical effects, as typified by conditions such as Chiari malformations, basilar invagination, and atlantoaxial instability. This chapter reviews the gross and radiologic anatomy relevant to these processes, emphasizing spatial relationships and measurements that guide clinical reasoning.

# Bony landmarks: occiput, foramen magnum, opisthion, clivus, atlas and axis relationships

The occiput forms the posterior and inferior part of the skull base. Its central feature for posterior fossa anatomy is the foramen magnum, a large opening that transmits the lower brainstem (medulla), meningeal coverings, vertebral arteries, spinal accessory nerve roots, and CSF between intracranial and spinal subarachnoid spaces. The bony margin of the foramen magnum is conventionally described by the anterior margin (basion) and posterior margin (opisthion). The basion-opisthion distance approximates the anteroposterior diameter of the foramen magnum and is used in morphometric assessments.

Anterior to the foramen magnum, the clivus is a sloping bony surface formed by the basiocciput and sphenoid body. It extends from the dorsum sellae superiorly down to the foramen magnum. The clivus forms a bed for the pons and the basilar artery and is an important landmark for assessing basilar invagination or platybasia. The clival length and the angle it makes with the cranial base influence the course of the brainstem and the space available in the posterior fossa.

The first cervical vertebra (atlas, C1) is a ring-shaped bone without a vertebral body. It articulates superiorly with the occipital condyles at the atlanto-occipital joints and inferiorly with the axis (C2) at the lateral masses and atlantodental joint. The anterior arch of C1 and the posterior arch form a ring that surrounds the spinal cord at the level of the foramen magnum. The posterior arch often forms part of the dorsal margin of the foramen magnum region, and variations (such as hypoplasia not uncommon congenitally) can affect local anatomy.

The axis (C2) is characterized by the odontoid process (dens), which projects superiorly from the C2 body and articulates with the anterior arch of the atlas, forming the pivot that enables rotation of C1 on C2. The odontoid is a key structure in craniocervical relationships: its position relative to the foramen magnum and clivus is central in assessing basilar invagination, which occurs when the odontoid migrates upward into the foramen magnum.

Important linear landmarks used clinically include Chamberlain’s line (from the posterior margin of the hard palate to the opisthion), McGregor’s line (from the posterior hard palate to the lowest point of the occipital squama), and McRae’s line (basion to opisthion — the foramen magnum line). These lines are used to determine whether the odontoid projects above the normal cranial base, suggestive of basilar invagination. For example, projection of the odontoid more than 3 mm above Chamberlain’s line suggests basilar invagination, though specific thresholds vary by modality and population.

# Dural reflections, tentorium and foramen magnum dural attachments

The intracranial dura mater is a bilaminar structure; its periosteal layer adheres to the inner skull table while the meningeal layer folds inward to produce dural reflections and septations that compartmentalize the cranial vault. The two dural folds most relevant for posterior fossa anatomy are the tentorium cerebelli and the falx cerebelli, along with dural attachments at the foramen magnum.

The tentorium cerebelli is a crescentic horizontal fold separating the occipital lobes and temporal lobes superiorly from the cerebellum inferiorly. Its free edge, the tentorial notch (incisura), provides a passage for the brainstem. The tentorium attaches anteriorly to the clinoid processes and tensorially along the superior petrosal sinuses laterally; posteriorly it fuses with the occipital bone near the internal occipital protuberance. The tentorial slope and the size of the tentorial incisura influence the degree of crowding at the posterior fossa and the potential for transtentorial herniation.

Dural attachments at the foramen magnum include the marginal dura that blends with the periosteum of the occiput, the tectorial membrane (an extension of the posterior longitudinal ligament that becomes continuous with the dura above the foramen magnum), and the posterior atlanto-occipital membrane which attaches the posterior arch of the atlas to the margin of the foramen magnum. The falx cerebelli is a small vertical dural fold in the midline attached to the internal occipital crest and extending inferiorly toward the foramen magnum; its size is variable. These attachments help define the configuration of the subarachnoid spaces and can act as fixed points around which mass lesions or herniation exert traction on neural tissues.

Clinically, dural reflections can restrict CSF flow locally and transmit forces that contribute to compression syndromes. For example, an intrinsically tight tentorial notch or low-lying tentorial insertion may exacerbate compression of the cerebellar tonsils at the foramen magnum, whereas thickened or adherent dura at the foramen magnum can affect the dynamics of CSF pulsations and surgical access.

# Cerebellar anatomy with emphasis on vermis and tonsils

The cerebellum is organized into a medial vermis and paired lateral cerebellar hemispheres. The vermis lies in the midline over the fourth ventricle and contributes to axial and proximal motor control, balance, and coordination. The cerebellar tonsils are rounded lobules of cerebellar cortex located at the inferior aspect of the cerebellar hemispheres, flanking the vermis and lying above the foramen magnum in a normal configuration. The tonsils are contiguous with the inferior vermis and are separated from the medulla by the cerebellomedullary (cisterna magna) subarachnoid space.

Anatomically, the cerebellar tonsils correspond to lobule IX (and sometimes parts of VIII) of the cerebellar cortex. Their position is functionally and developmentally variable; in many adults the tonsils lie at or slightly below the level of the foramen magnum. Tonsillar ectopia—downward displacement of the tonsils into the foramen magnum—is central to the definition of Chiari I malformation, though the pathophysiologic significance depends on degree of descent, CSF flow obstruction, and posterior fossa morphology.

The cerebellar vermis is subdivided into superior and inferior portions. The inferior vermis (nodulus and uvula) is located adjacent to the tonsils and has close anatomic relations to the fourth ventricle and dorsal brainstem. Pathology that alters vermian size or position (congenital hypoplasia, tumors, ischemia) can impinge on the foraminal region, change CSF dynamics in the fourth ventricle, and alter tonsillar position by mass effect or developmental constraints.

Understanding the microanatomy and folding of the tonsils is important surgically because their resection or decompression has implications for cerebellar function and for the restoration of CSF pathways. The tonsils have a dense cortical mantle and underlying white matter that connect to deep cerebellar nuclei; manipulation risks ataxia or dysmetria if extensive tissue is violated.

# Craniovertebral junction spatial relationships

The craniovertebral junction (CVJ) comprises the occiput, atlas, and axis and the soft tissues that bind them, including ligaments, dura, and neurovascular structures. It is the transition zone between the rigid skull base and the mobile cervical spine and must simultaneously provide stability and mobility. The occipitoatlantal (C0–C1) joints allow flexion-extension and limited lateral bending, while the atlantoaxial (C1–C2) joints permit the majority of cervical rotation.

Key spatial relationships include the close proximity of the odontoid to the anterior foramen magnum and the brainstem; even small upward or posterior migration of the odontoid can compress the lower medulla or rostral spinal cord. The atlantodental interval (ADI), measured between the posterior border of the anterior arch of C1 and the anterior aspect of the odontoid, is a commonly used measure of atlantoaxial stability. On lateral radiographs or flexion-extension films, the normal adult ADI is generally considered less than $3\ \text{mm}$, and in children less than $5\ \text{mm}$. An increased ADI suggests ligamentous injury or laxity that may permit translation and spinal cord compression.

Ligamentous structures central to CVJ stability include the tectorial membrane, alar ligaments, and transverse atlantal ligament. The transverse ligament holds the odontoid against the anterior arch of C1; its rupture allows anterior subluxation of C1 on C2. Injury, congenital dysplasia, or inflammatory erosion of these ligaments (as in rheumatoid arthritis) can lead to atlantoaxial instability with dynamic compression at the level of the cervicomedullary junction.

Vascular anatomy at the CVJ is also crucial. The vertebral arteries ascend through the transverse foramina of C6–C1, take a tortuous course over the posterior arch of C1, and then pass medially through the foramen magnum to join and form the basilar artery. Anatomical variants in their loopiness, or compression by anomalous osseous processes or hypertrophic facets, can become clinically relevant in CVJ pathology and during surgical exposure.

Clinically, the CVJ spatial relationships determine whether a lesion will produce primarily brainstem/cranial nerve signs or cervical myelopathy. For example, an upwardly displaced odontoid produces ventral brainstem compression with early bulbar signs, whereas posterior elements like an enlarged posterior arch of C1 or an osteophyte may compress the dorsal cord.

# Normal CSF cisterns and subarachnoid space at the craniocervical junction

The subarachnoid space around the craniocervical junction contains several named cisterns that act as reservoirs for CSF and conduits for CSF pulsations. The largest is the cerebellomedullary cistern (cisterna magna) which lies posterior to the medulla and inferior to the cerebellar tonsils, between the cerebellum and the posterior occiput. The cisterna magna communicates with the fourth ventricle via the median aperture (foramen of Magendie) and laterally with the pontine cisterns via the lateral apertures (foramina of Luschka).

The cisterna magna is a critical space: its patency permits normal egress of CSF from the ventricular system into the subarachnoid space. Obstruction at the outlets of the fourth ventricle or impingement of the cisterna magna by downward herniation of the tonsils can lead to impaired CSF flow and syringomyelia due to altered pressure gradients and abnormal transmission of CSF pulsations into the spinal subarachnoid space.

Other relevant cisterns include the prepontine cistern, ambient cisterns (lateral to the midbrain), and the superior cerebellar cisterns. At the foramen magnum, the subarachnoid space is relatively narrow compared to the supratentorial subarachnoid compartments; the thecal sac is enveloped by dura and restricted by bony margins and ligaments. The dynamic component of CSF flow — driven by cardiac pulsations and respiration — is particularly sensitive to constrictions at the foramen magnum, which can create a one-way valve effect if tonsils seal against the dural rim or if arachnoid adhesions form.

In imaging, flow-sensitive MRI sequences (phase-contrast cine MRI) can demonstrate CSF flow across the foramen magnum and detect jet-like flows, blockages, and turbulence associated with Chiari I and other CVJ pathologies. Clinically, restoration of cisternal patency is a principal aim when operative decompression is undertaken to improve CSF dynamics and reduce syrinx formation.

# Radiologic planes and standard measurements used at C0–C2

Radiologic evaluation of the posterior fossa and CVJ relies on standard imaging planes — sagittal, axial, and coronal — each offering unique information about bone, brain, and CSF spaces.

The midsagittal plane is indispensable for assessing tonsillar position, clivus length and angle, odontoid position, and lines used to assess basilar invagination. Important lines and measurements include:

- McRae line: the line connecting basion to opisthion (the anteroposterior dimension of the foramen magnum). The tip of the odontoid projects above this line in basilar invagination.
- Chamberlain line: from the posterior edge of the hard palate to the opisthion. Protrusion of the odontoid more than approximately 3 mm above Chamberlain’s line is suggestive of basilar invagination; thresholds vary by series.
- McGregor line: from the posterior superior margin of the hard palate to the most caudal point of the occipital squama. An odontoid tip more than 4.5 mm above McGregor’s line is considered abnormal.
- Wackenheim’s clival canal line: a line along the clivus extended caudally; its relationship to the odontoid indicates clival-canal alignment and may suggest basilar invagination when the odontoid projects abnormally above this line.

Tonsillar position is commonly measured on midline sagittal MRI as the vertical distance of the lowest point of the tonsils below the foramen magnum line (often approximated by McRae’s line). A descent greater than $5\ \text{mm}$ has classically been used to define Chiari I malformation in adults, but this threshold is not absolute: some symptomatic patients have less descent and some asymptomatic individuals have greater descent. Thus, tonsillar position must be interpreted alongside posterior fossa size, CSF flow studies, and clinical findings.

Posterior fossa dimensions can be quantified by measuring the anteroposterior diameter of the posterior fossa (distance from the dorsal clivus to the internal occipital protuberance or to the torcular) and the height of the posterior fossa. Posterior fossa hypoplasia (small volume) is an important etiologic factor in tonsillar herniation; more recently, volumetric MRI studies have been used to measure posterior fossa volume, but normative thresholds vary by age and sex.

At the C0–C2 level, axial and coronal images evaluate the lateral masses, facets, and the course of the vertebral arteries. On lateral radiographs and sagittal CT/MR, the atlantodental interval (ADI) should be measured; the normal adult value is less than $3\ \text{mm}$. The posterior atlantodental interval (retro-odontoid space) — the distance between the posterior aspect of the odontoid and the anterior margin of the posterior arch of C1 or the spinal canal — is also clinically relevant; values less than approximately $14\ \text{mm}$ (on CT/MR) may indicate stenosis at the cervicomedullary junction, but norms vary with modality and technique.

Other dynamic assessments include flexion-extension radiographs for detecting atlantoaxial instability and rotational CT or MRI when rotational subluxation is suspected. Foramen magnum dimensions can be quantified by measuring the basion-opisthion distance and cross-sectional area on axial imaging; marked narrowing or asymmetry may predispose to compression.

Clinical decision points tied to these measurements:

- Atlantoaxial instability is suggested when $\text{ADI} > 3\ \text{mm}$ in adults or $>5\ \text{mm}$ in children, or when dynamic imaging shows translation producing cord compression or symptoms.
- Basilar invagination is suspected when the odontoid projects above Chamberlain’s or McGregor’s lines beyond accepted thresholds (e.g., $>3\ \text{mm}$ for Chamberlain; $>4.5\ \text{mm}$ for McGregor), or when McRae’s line is violated.
- Tonsillar descent exceeding $5\ \text{mm}$ is a commonly used criterion for Chiari I, but clinical correlation, CSF flow studies, and posterior fossa dimensions are required before labeling pathology.

In practice, measurements should be interpreted in the context of symptoms, neurologic findings, and dynamic imaging. Small measurement errors or variant anatomy can alter interpretation; therefore, comparison with prior imaging and use of multiple planes and modalities (CT for bone detail, MRI for soft tissue and CSF flow) is standard.

Summary

The posterior cranial fossa and craniocervical junction are anatomically compact regions where bone, dura, cerebellum, brainstem, and CSF pathways converge. Precise knowledge of the occipital and cervical bony landmarks, dural reflections, cerebellar anatomy (particularly the vermis and tonsils), and the cisternal CSF spaces provides the framework for understanding how congenital variants, developmental anomalies, trauma, or degenerative disease produce symptoms by displacing neural tissue or obstructing CSF flow. Radiologic planes and standard measurements at C0–C2—applied judiciously and in clinical context—guide diagnosis and management, from identifying basilar invagination and atlantoaxial instability to assessing the significance of tonsillar descent.
