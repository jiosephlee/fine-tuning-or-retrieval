Introduction

Polysomnography (PSG) is the integrated recording of multiple physiologic signals during sleep and is the gold standard for diagnosing sleep-disordered breathing, parasomnias, movement disorders, and disorders of sleep architecture. A clinical understanding of the component signals, how they are recorded and interpreted, and the strengths and limitations of different types of sleep studies is essential for diagnosing and managing patients with suspected obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA), central sleep apnoea (CSA), hypoventilation syndromes, and complex sleep pathophysiology. This chapter reviews the physiologic basis and technical aspects of the core PSG channels, contrasts full laboratory (type I) testing with the limited device classifications (type II–III), explains contemporary scoring criteria for respiratory events including the separation of central and obstructive events, defines the apnoea–hypopnoea index (AHI) and its clinical thresholds, and describes the role of carbon dioxide monitoring and common sources of error that lead to over- or under-estimation of disease severity.

# Polysomnography components: EEG, EOG, EMG, airflow, respiratory effort, oximetry, ECG

The standard polysomnogram integrates neurophysiologic and cardiorespiratory signals to permit simultaneous assessment of sleep stages, arousals, respiratory events, oxygenation, cardiac rhythm, and limb movements. Each channel serves a specific purpose and has characteristic placement and signals that must be understood to interpret PSG correctly.

Electroencephalography (EEG)
EEG is the foundation for sleep staging. Typical clinical PSG uses multiple scalp electrodes referenced and placed according to a simplified montage (for example, C3-A2, C4-A1, O1-A2, O2-A1) to capture cortical activity. EEG allows recognition of wakefulness (low-amplitude mixed-frequency activity, eye-blink artifacts), NREM sleep stages N1–N3 (transitioning from theta to high-amplitude slow-wave delta), and REM sleep (low-voltage mixed-frequency with sawtooth waves). Sleep staging follows standardized criteria (commonly AASM) using 30-second epochs. EEG also detects cortical arousals—abrupt shifts in frequency lasting ≥3 seconds (with separate criteria in children)—which are important in scoring respiratory-related arousals and determining sleep fragmentation.

Electro-oculography (EOG)
EOG channels record horizontal and sometimes vertical eye movements. EOG distinguishes N1 (slow-rolling eye movements), REM (rapid eye movements), and wakefulness. Eye movement patterns, together with EEG and EMG, are essential to identify REM sleep and detect REM-related respiratory events (e.g., REM-predominant OSA).

Electromyography (EMG)
Chin EMG is used to identify muscle tone and to differentiate REM (muscle atonia) from NREM; reduction in chin EMG is part of REM definition. Limb EMG (usually anterior tibialis) records periodic limb movements during sleep (PLMS), which can cause arousals and influence the respiratory event assessment. EMG signals are also used to detect bruxism and note movement artifact that may simulate respiratory events.

Airflow
Airflow measurement is critical to detect apnoea and hypopnea. Nasal pressure transducers provide a sensitive surrogate for airflow and are especially better at detecting partial airflow reductions (hypopnea). Thermistors or thermocouples detect thermal fluctuations and are good at discriminating flow cessation (apnea) but less sensitive for partial flow reduction. Current practice often records both: nasal pressure for quantitative amplitude changes and a thermistor for confirmation of apneic cessation. Signal quality, sensor placement, and nasal obstruction influence the fidelity of these channels.

Respiratory effort
Respiratory effort belts around the chest and abdomen (inductance plethysmography or respiratory inductance plethysmography) record thoracoabdominal motion. The presence versus absence of respiratory effort during a cessation of airflow is the key distinction between obstructive and central events. In obstructive apnoea the belts show continued or paradoxical effort despite absent airflow; in central apnoea there is an absence of thoracoabdominal movement. Careful attention to belt placement, calibration (if used for quantitative measurement), and recognition of belt signal artifact (e.g., movement, poor contact) is necessary.

Oximetry
Pulse oximetry measures arterial oxygen saturation (SpO2). Pulse oximetry provides critical objective evidence of impairment in gas exchange associated with respiratory events. Standard oximeters sample at rates of 1 Hz or greater; averaging times and signal filtering affect the depth and timing of desaturation reported. Typical normal awake SpO2 is 95–100% at sea level; nighttime desaturation episodes tied to apnoea/hypopnea are used in scoring hypopneas (see later). Frequent technical issues—motion artifact, poor perfusion, nail polish—must be considered.

Electrocardiography (ECG)
A single-lead ECG is routinely recorded during PSG to monitor heart rhythm and to identify arrhythmias or cardiac events that may be caused or unmasked by sleep-disordered breathing. ECG also assists in identifying arousals associated with heart rate accelerations and in research contexts for heart rate variability analysis. Typical placement mirrors lead II or a modified lead to optimize R-wave detection.

Other sensors
Many PSGs include additional channels such as position sensors, snore microphones, video monitoring, and end-tidal or transcutaneous CO2 monitoring. Each can augment interpretation: body position is relevant for positional OSA, snoring helps confirm flow limitation, and video documents parasomnias.

# Differences between type I (full) and limited (type II–III) studies and their diagnostic limitations

Sleep studies are classified by the number and types of channels recorded and by whether they are performed attended in the laboratory. Understanding these classifications is important when selecting the appropriate test and when interpreting outcomes.

Type I — Full attended polysomnography
Type I studies are comprehensive attended overnight PSGs performed in a sleep laboratory with simultaneous neurophysiologic, respiratory, cardiac, and oxygenation monitoring (including EEG, EOG, EMG, airflow, respiratory effort, oximetry, and ECG). The presence of EEG allows accurate sleep staging and assessment of total sleep time (TST), arousals, and sleep architecture. This is the gold standard for diagnosing a broad range of sleep disorders, particularly when differential diagnosis requires sleep stage information (e.g., REM-related OSA, REM behavior disorder, narcolepsy suspicion, parasomnias, complex cases, or suspected hypoventilation).

Type II — Unattended full PSG (portable, comprehensive)
Type II devices record the same physiologic signals as type I but are typically unattended and performed in the home environment. They usually include EEG channels, airflow, effort, oximetry, and often ECG. Type II provides the advantage of recording sleep and wake, but because they are unattended, signal loss and artifact are more common; equipment setup errors may degrade EEG or other channels.

Type III — Limited channel sleep apnea testing
Type III devices record a minimum of four channels, typically including airflow (often nasal pressure), respiratory effort (thoracic and/or abdominal belt), oximetry, and ECG or heart rate. They do not include EEG/EOG/EMG; therefore they cannot determine sleep stages or total sleep time. They are often used for home sleep apnea testing (HSAT) when OSA is strongly suspected and comorbidities are absent. Advantages are convenience, lower cost, and greater accessibility.

Type IV — Very limited devices
Type IV devices record 1–3 channels, often just oximetry and/or airflow. Their diagnostic accuracy is limited and they are generally only used for screening or in situations with resource constraints.

Diagnostic limitations and implications
The absence of EEG in type III/IV devices has several important consequences. Without EEG you cannot reliably determine total sleep time, so event rates must be normalized to recording time (see later). This commonly results in underestimation of severity when sleep efficiency is low (i.e., wake time during recording dilutes event frequency). Conversely, devices that infer sleep from absence of movement or changes in actigraphy may misclassify quiet wakefulness as sleep and thus overestimate or underestimate events. Type III devices are accurate in patients with high pretest probability of moderate-to-severe OSA and without significant cardiopulmonary or neuromuscular disease. They are inappropriate when central sleep apnea, hypoventilation, neuromuscular weakness, or other sleep disorders are suspected—conditions that require EEG and often CO2 monitoring. Unattended studies also have higher rates of technical failure; careful quality control and patient selection are essential.

# Scoring criteria for apnoea, hypopnoea and central vs obstructive events

Scoring respiratory events uses standardized rules (commonly AASM 2012/2017 updates) that define the magnitude and duration of airflow reduction and associated physiologic consequences. While small differences exist among guideline versions, the core definitions remain: apnoea is near-complete airflow cessation; hypopnea is a partial reduction with physiologic consequence; central vs obstructive apnoea is distinguished by respiratory effort.

Apnoea
An apnoea is typically defined as a drop in airflow amplitude by at least 90% of baseline for at least $10$ seconds. The airflow source can be thermistor, nasal pressure, or a combination; most scorers require confirmation across devices and consider sensor limitations. Events are labeled as obstructive, central, or mixed depending on respiratory effort.

- Obstructive apnoea: Airflow is absent or nearly absent while thoracoabdominal effort persists and may be paradoxical. The presence of continued respiratory effort on belts distinguishes obstructive apnoea.
- Central apnoea: Airflow cessation is accompanied by a cessation of respiratory effort (flat or near-flat effort signals) for the duration of the event. Central apnoeas may be idiopathic, heart-failure-related, opioid-induced, or related to instability in ventilatory control.
- Mixed apnoea: Begins as central (no effort) and ends with renewed respiratory effort; often scored as mixed but classified by the predominant component (practice varies).

Hypopnoea
Hypopnoea definitions have evolved and commonly require both a reduction in airflow amplitude and an associated oxygen desaturation or EEG arousal. A frequently used definition is a reduction in nasal pressure or airflow amplitude by ≥30% of baseline for ≥10 s associated with either ≥3% oxygen desaturation or an arousal (this is known as the AASM "recommended" rule). Some scoring systems use a ≥4% desaturation criterion in lieu of arousal documentation. The choice of 3% versus 4% thresholds affects prevalence estimates—using a 3%/arousal rule identifies more events than a 4% desaturation-only rule.

Central vs obstructive hypopnoea
Distinguishing central from obstructive hypopnoeas is more nuanced. If respiratory effort decreases substantially or is absent during the reduced airflow, the event is considered central; if respiratory effort persists, it is considered obstructive. Criteria often require analysis of thoracoabdominal signals: a paradoxical pattern or continued effort indicates flow limitation from upper-airway obstruction. Flow-limited waveforms on nasal pressure (flattening of the inspiratory contour) can support obstructive physiology even when effort belts show preserved motion.

Associated events and rules
Events are typically scored only during sleep epochs; events during wake are excluded. Events separated by less than $2$ breaths are often considered part of a single prolonged event. Periodic breathing and Cheyne–Stokes respiration are scored when cyclic crescendo–decrescendo ventilation with central apnoeas/hypopnoeas is present over a sustained period and meet numerical thresholds.

# Apnoea–hypopnoea index (AHI) calculation and interpretation

Definition and calculation
The apnoea–hypopnoea index (AHI) quantifies sleep-disordered breathing burden by dividing the total number of apnoeas plus hypopnoeas during a sleep study by the sleep time in hours:

AHI = (Number of apnoeas + Number of hypopnoeas) / Total sleep time in hours

For type I and type II studies that include EEG, total sleep time should be used. For type III devices lacking EEG, some reporting uses total recording time (TRT) rather than TST—this must be explicitly stated because it affects interpretation (see the section on sources of error).

Clinical thresholds
Although thresholds are somewhat arbitrary and outcomes are continuous, commonly used AHI categorical cutoffs are:

- Normal: AHI < 5 events/hour
- Mild OSA: AHI 5–14.9 events/hour
- Moderate OSA: AHI 15–29.9 events/hour
- Severe OSA: AHI ≥ 30 events/hour

Clinical interpretation
The AHI guides diagnosis and management but must be interpreted in clinical context. Symptoms (excessive daytime sleepiness, impaired cognition, cardiovascular comorbidity), observed apnoea, and comorbid conditions influence management decisions. Two patients with identical AHIs may have very different clinical risks depending on oxygen desaturation severity, sleep fragmentation, REM-related predominance, positional effects, and underlying cardiopulmonary disease.

Alternative indices
Other indices include the respiratory disturbance index (RDI), which may include respiratory-effort-related arousals (RERAs) in addition to apnoeas/hypopneas, and the oxygen desaturation index (ODI), which counts desaturation events per hour (commonly ≥3% or ≥4%). RDI is useful when RERAs contribute to sleep-disordered breathing but requires EEG/arousal detection.

Limitations of AHI
AHI treats all events equivalently regardless of associated desaturation or arousal; it does not capture event duration, depth of desaturation, CO2 retention, or clinical consequences. A patient with a modest AHI but prolonged desaturations or significant hypercapnia may have greater physiologic impairment than AHI alone suggests. Thus, AHI should be integrated with oximetry, CO2 monitoring when indicated, and clinical features.

# Utility of end-tidal and transcutaneous CO2 monitoring for hypoventilation

Rationale for CO2 monitoring
Pulse oximetry reflects oxygenation but is an insensitive marker for hypoventilation in many settings: supplemental oxygen can mask hypoventilation, and oxygen desaturation can lag behind rising CO2. Monitoring of carbon dioxide partial pressure provides direct physiological assessment of alveolar ventilation and is essential for diagnosing hypoventilation syndromes (e.g., obesity hypoventilation syndrome (OHS), neuromuscular weakness, chest wall deformity, opioid-induced respiratory depression, and central hypoventilation syndromes).

Modality options: end-tidal CO2 (P_ETCO2) and transcutaneous CO2 (P_tcCO2)
End-tidal CO2 (P_ETCO2) measures the CO2 concentration at the end of exhalation, typically using mainstream or sidestream capnography. It is widely used because of its immediacy and noninvasiveness. However, P_ETCO2 reflects alveolar gas and can underestimate arterial CO2 (PaCO2) due to ventilation–perfusion mismatch, airway obstruction, or sampling issues. The typical gradient (PaCO2 – P_ETCO2) in healthy subjects is small (1–5 mmHg) but can be larger in disease.

Transcutaneous CO2 (P_tcCO2) measures CO2 diffusing through heated skin using an electrochemical sensor; it approximates PaCO2 after a calibration and equilibration period and is less influenced by breath-by-breath variation or entrainment problems. P_tcCO2 is commonly used during sleep studies when continuous CO2 monitoring is required, particularly in patients with suspected hypoventilation, neuromuscular disease, or opioid therapy.

Diagnostic thresholds for hypoventilation
Consensus and guideline statements suggest specific criteria for defining sleep-associated hypoventilation:

- Sustained hypercapnia: P_tcCO2 (or PaCO2) > 55 mmHg for ≥10 minutes during sleep is considered diagnostic of significant hypoventilation.
- Relative rise: A rise in P_tcCO2 of ≥10 mmHg above awake supine baseline to a value >50 mmHg for ≥10 minutes is also used as a criterion.

Different societies may apply slightly different numerical cutoffs; clinicians should apply the definitions used by their local laboratory and consider the clinical context. Important practical thresholds include awake PaCO2 > 45 mmHg often indicating daytime hypoventilation, whereas nocturnal elevations that meet the above criteria indicate sleep hypoventilation.

Advantages and limitations
P_ETCO2 advantages: immediate response to ventilatory changes, simple setup for many patients, good for detecting events with preserved tidal volumes. Limitations: underestimation of PaCO2 with COPD, airway obstruction, or mask leaks; sidestream delays; and dependence on nasal breathing for sampling accuracy. P_tcCO2 advantages: better correlation with PaCO2 over prolonged monitoring, less sensitive to mouth breathing or minor leaks. Limitations: site heating can cause skin irritation or burns if improperly used, sensor drift over prolonged recordings, requirement for calibration and equilibration (several minutes), and potential lag in tracking rapid changes.

Clinical applications
CO2 monitoring is essential when hypoventilation is suspected: evaluation of obesity hypoventilation syndrome (OHS), neuromuscular disease, advanced COPD with suspected hypercapnic respiratory failure, or opioid-induced respiratory depression. It should be used in the lab when treating with or titrating noninvasive ventilation and when oxygen therapy may mask hypercapnia. Interpretation requires understanding of sensor-specific biases and correlation with arterial blood gas when necessary.

# Sources of under- and over-estimation (e.g., total recording time vs total sleep time)

Estimating event frequency and severity is vulnerable to several sources of error that can lead to under- or over-estimation of disease. Recognizing these sources is essential for accurate reporting and clinical decision-making.

Total recording time (TRT) versus total sleep time (TST)
The most common and important source of discrepancy is whether the denominator used to calculate an event index is total recording time (minutes from lights-off to lights-on or from device start to stop) or total sleep time (epochs scored as sleep). Type I/II PSGs with EEG use TST, which is the physiologic correct denominator. Type III/IV studies typically lack EEG and therefore often normalize events to TRT. If a patient has poor sleep efficiency (many wake epochs) the use of TRT will dilute event frequency and underestimate AHI relative to a PSG-derived AHI using TST. Conversely, if devices or algorithms misclassify wake as sleep (e.g., based on actigraphy or low movement), event rates may be overestimated.

Sensor failure and signal artifact
Loss of key channels (e.g., nasal pressure, thermistor, effort belts, oximetry) leads to missed events or misclassification. For example, if the nasal cannula is dislodged, hypopneas that depend on nasal pressure amplitude reduction may not be detected, causing underestimation. Conversely, motion artifact on nasal pressure or belts can create spurious fluctuations scored as events, leading to overestimation. EEG lead loss may prevent scoring of arousals; hypopneas defined by arousal may therefore be missed.

Use of different airflow sensors and scoring rules
Thermistors detect flow cessation well but are less sensitive for hypopneas; nasal pressure is more sensitive for partial reductions. Studies that use only thermistors will tend to under-detect hypopneas relative to those using nasal pressure. Similarly, the choice of hypopnea scoring rule (≥3% desaturation or arousal versus ≥4% desaturation-only) has a direct impact on event counts—3%/arousal rules produce higher AHI values. Therefore, reports must document the sensor types and scoring criteria used.

Averaging time and oximeter response
Pulse oximeters have adjustable averaging times and filtering. Longer averaging blunts rapid desaturations and may underestimate nadir SpO2 and the ODI. Irregular heart rates, low perfusion, or motion can produce erroneous desaturations (overestimation) or loss of signal (underestimation). When oxygen is administered, SpO2 may remain normal despite hypoventilation; reliance on SpO2 alone in these settings is dangerous.

Body position and sleep stage distribution
Patients may spend disproportionate time in supine position or in REM sleep during a single-night study, and both positional and REM-related OSA can produce night-to-night variability. A single-night PSG may under- or over-represent habitual sleeping conditions. Home studies without positional sensors may miss positional dependency leading to underestimation of disease amenable to positional therapy.

Event duration weighting and desaturation severity
AHI counts events equally regardless of duration. A few prolonged apnoeas producing deep desaturation may be more physiologically impactful than many brief events. Exclusive reliance on AHI may therefore misrepresent risk. Measures such as T90 (percentage of sleep time with SpO2 < 90%), mean and nadir SpO2, ODI, and event duration distributions provide additional clinically relevant information.

Algorithms and auto-scoring
Automated scoring algorithms can process large datasets rapidly but vary in sensitivity and specificity. Many automated systems require expert review; overreliance on unattended auto-scoring may yield both false positives (artifacts scored as events) and false negatives (missed subtle events, RERAs). Best practice is human review and correction, especially for borderline or complex studies.

Patient factors and co-morbid conditions
Nasal obstruction, mouth breathing, obesity, COPD, heart failure, and neuromuscular disease all influence signal quality and physiology. For example, COPD causes V/Q mismatch that widens the PaCO2–P_ETCO2 gradient, reducing the accuracy of end-tidal CO2 as a surrogate for arterial hypercapnia and potentially masking hypoventilation on capnography. Supplemental oxygen can mask desaturation but not hypercapnia. Opioids can produce periodic respiration and hypoventilation that may be under-recognized on limited-channel studies.

Practical recommendations to mitigate errors
To minimize under- and over-estimation: choose the appropriate study type for the clinical question (use full PSG with CO2 monitoring when hypoventilation or complex sleep disorders are suspected); ensure careful sensor placement and technician oversight; document the sensors and scoring rules used; review for and correct artifacts; and interpret AHI in conjunction with oxygenation metrics, CO2 data when available, sleep architecture, and clinical presentation. Explicitly report whether indices are normalized to TST or TRT and include TST, TRT, sleep efficiency, ODI, mean and nadir SpO2, and total time with elevated CO2 when present.

Conclusion

An integrated understanding of PSG components, their physiologic basis, the classifications of sleep study types, standardized scoring criteria, and the role of CO2 monitoring is essential for accurate diagnosis and management of sleep-disordered breathing and hypoventilation. Recognizing the limitations and potential sources of error—particularly the difference between total recording time and total sleep time, sensor-specific biases, and patient-specific factors—permits appropriate selection of testing modalities and cautious interpretation of results in clinical practice.
