Introduction

Bulbar dysfunction and choking are clinical syndromes encountered across multiple specialties, including neurology, otolaryngology (ENT), gastroenterology, and primary care. “Bulbar” refers to involvement of cranial nerve nuclei in the medulla (the bulbar region), their lower motor neuron axons, or the muscles they innervate, producing dysarthria, dysphonia, dysphagia, chewing difficulty, and impaired cough or airway protection. Choking episodes may reflect transient aspiration, airway obstruction, impaired swallowing coordination, or heightened sensation/behavioral phenomena. The differential diagnosis is broad and spans neuromuscular junction disorders, motor neuron and other neurodegenerative diseases, vascular and inflammatory lesions of the brainstem, structural and mucosal diseases of the oropharynx and larynx, gastroesophageal reflux-related laryngopharyngeal disease, and psychogenic/functional disorders. A systematic approach integrating history, examination, bedside tests, directed ancillary testing, and multidisciplinary evaluation maximizes diagnostic yield while avoiding unnecessary studies or delays in treatment.

This chapter organizes the clinical reasoning necessary to sort these causes, emphasizing pathophysiology, key features that distinguish entities, high-yield investigations, and red flags that prompt urgent intervention.

# Neuromuscular junction disorders: myasthenia gravis evaluation and red flags

Neuromuscular junction (NMJ) disorders produce fatigable weakness of voluntary muscles. Myasthenia gravis (MG) is the prototypical autoimmune NMJ disorder, caused by antibodies targeting postsynaptic acetylcholine receptors (AChR) or associated proteins such as muscle-specific kinase (MuSK) and low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein 4 (LRP4). The pathophysiology involves complement-mediated receptor loss, impaired neuromuscular transmission, and reduced safety factor for neuromuscular transmission. Bulbar muscles are commonly involved because their high-frequency discharge and fine control make them susceptible to impaired transmission; hence MG commonly causes diplopia, ptosis, dysarthria, dysphagia, and fatigable chewing.

Clinical evaluation emphasizes fatigability and fluctuation: symptoms worsen with exertion and improve with rest. In bulbar MG, the hallmark is progressive dysarthria or dysphagia that fluctuates through the day and responds to rest or neostigmine/edrophonium challenge. Examination may reveal fatigable ptosis, gaze-limited ophthalmoparesis, nasal dysarthria, reduced palatal elevation, and weakness of neck extensors. Respiratory muscle weakness manifests as reduced vital capacity and may progress to myasthenic crisis.

Diagnostic testing proceeds in stages. Serum antibodies are first-line: AChR binding antibodies are positive in approximately 85–90% of generalized MG and in a lower proportion of purely ocular MG; MuSK antibodies are more common in seronegative generalized MG and often associate with prominent bulbar and respiratory involvement. LRP4 antibodies are less frequent but present in some seronegative patients. Reference interpretation: a negative antibody panel does not exclude MG.

Electrophysiology is essential when antibody testing is negative or to confirm diagnosis. Repetitive nerve stimulation (RNS) at 2–3 Hz shows a decremental response in postsynaptic disorders. Single-fiber electromyography (SFEMG) is the most sensitive test for impaired neuromuscular transmission, detecting increased jitter and blocking even when RNS is normal. Pharmacologic testing historically used edrophonium (Tensilon) for transient improvement; edrophonium is less commonly used now because of false positives and cholinergic risks, but neostigmine or an oral pyridostigmine trial may also support the diagnosis clinically.

Red flags in the setting of suspected MG include rapidly progressive bulbar symptoms, hypoxia, stridor, ineffective cough, inability to handle secretions, and vital capacity falling below 20 mL/kg or dropping rapidly—these require urgent monitoring and consideration for respiratory support. Bulbar-dominant MG with severe dysphagia can lead to aspiration pneumonia and malnutrition, mandating multidisciplinary management with speech therapy and nutritional assessment.

Laboratory and ancillary studies supportive of evaluation include routine tests to exclude mimics: creatine kinase (CK) is typically normal or only mildly elevated in MG; markedly elevated CK suggests myopathy rather than NMJ disorder (normal CK varies by lab, commonly approximately 30–200 U/L). Chest imaging (contrast-enhanced CT or MRI) is indicated to evaluate for thymoma in AChR antibody–positive disease because thymoma has implications for oncologic resection and immunotherapy.

Treatment decisions hinge on confirming the diagnosis and assessing severity. Rapidly progressive bulbar weakness or respiratory compromise should prompt hospital admission, pulmonary function monitoring, and early involvement of neurology and critical care. Immunotherapies, cholinesterase inhibitors, thymectomy for thymoma, and supportive measures are guided by disease classification and severity.

# Motor neuron diseases (bulbar-onset ALS) and other neurodegenerative causes

Motor neuron disease (MND), particularly amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), is a key neurodegenerative cause of progressive bulbar dysfunction. In ALS, there is degeneration of both upper motor neurons (UMN) in the cortex and lower motor neurons (LMN) in the brainstem and spinal cord. Bulbar-onset ALS presents with progressive dysarthria, dysphagia, tongue wasting and fasciculations, and sialorrhea. Distinguishing features include a combination of LMN signs in bulbar-innervated muscles (atrophy, fasciculations, reduced reflexes) together with UMN signs (spasticity, hyperreflexia, pathological reflexes) either locally or in other body regions. Bulbar-onset disease tends to have faster progression and worse prognosis compared with limb-onset ALS; aspiration and respiratory failure are common causes of morbidity.

Diagnostic criteria for ALS have been formalized (revised El Escorial and Awaji criteria), emphasizing the combination of LMN signs in multiple regions and UMN signs, progression over time, and exclusion of mimics. Electromyography (EMG) demonstrates widespread denervation and reinnervation in multiple segments, including evidence of active denervation (fibrillation potentials, positive sharp waves) and chronic neurogenic changes such as large motor unit potentials. For bulbar-onset presentations, EMG of tongue and limb muscles is helpful. Laboratory tests and imaging are required to exclude treatable mimics (metabolic, structural, inflammatory, infectious) that can produce UMN/LMN features.

Other neurodegenerative disorders can produce bulbar symptoms. Progressive supranuclear palsy and multiple system atrophy can have dysphagia and dysarthria as part of a broader syndrome with ocular or autonomic features. Spinocerebellar ataxias, late-onset Tay–Sachs variants, and inclusion-body myositis may mimic bulbar weakness. In inclusion-body myositis, dysphagia occurs due to pharyngeal muscle involvement; CK may be mildly to moderately elevated and EMG/myopathology will show myopathic changes with rimmed vacuoles. Differentiation relies on pattern recognition, labs (e.g., CK), EMG, muscle biopsy when indicated, and MRI of brain and spinal cord.

Prognostication and urgent decision points: progressive weight loss, frequent aspiration pneumonia, and shortening of intervals between choking/aspiration events signal need for nutritional and respiratory interventions. Measurement of forced vital capacity (FVC) and sniff nasal inspiratory pressure are key for monitoring respiratory involvement; FVC < 50% predicted or rapid decline are indications for noninvasive ventilation referral and advanced care planning discussions. Referral to multidisciplinary ALS clinics improves outcomes.

# Brainstem stroke, demyelinating disease and compressive lesions

Focal lesions of the brainstem can produce acute or subacute bulbar dysfunction. Vascular events such as lateral medullary (Wallenberg) infarcts classically cause bulbar dysfunction with dysphagia, hoarseness (nucleus ambiguus involvement), ipsilateral facial sensory loss, and contralateral body pain/temp sensory loss. Paramedian pontine lesions can affect corticobulbar fibers producing dysarthria and impairment of bulbar motor control. Strokes are usually abrupt in onset and accompanied by other focal signs—vertigo, ataxia, hemiparesis, sensory changes, ocular motor palsies—depending on vascular territory.

Demyelinating disease such as multiple sclerosis (MS) can involve the brainstem producing internuclear ophthalmoplegia, facial numbness or weakness, and acute or subacute bulbar signs. In MS, lesions often disseminate in time and space and are accompanied by typical MRI white-matter lesions and oligoclonal bands in cerebrospinal fluid. Brainstem demyelination may present with diplopia and dysarthria in young adults, raising suspicion for MS when clinical context and MRI findings fit.

Compressive lesions of the posterior fossa and foramen magnum—tumors (e.g., metastases, primary gliomas, meningiomas), Chiari malformation, vertebral artery dissection with mass effect, and infectious/inflammatory processes—can produce progressive bulbar signs. Compression may result in isolated lower cranial nerve palsies, progression over weeks to months, and signs of increased intracranial pressure. Skull base lesions (jugular foramen syndromes) produce specific patterns involving CNs IX–XI.

Diagnostic approach to suspected central causes prioritizes neuroimaging. MRI of the brain with diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) is essential to detect acute ischemia; MRI brainstem sequences and high-resolution T2/FLAIR sequences increase sensitivity for demyelinating plaques and small structural lesions. When vascular dissection or posterior circulation pathology is suspected, MR or CT angiography is indicated. Lumbar puncture is considered when inflammatory or infectious processes are in the differential. Urgent neurovascular coordination is necessary for acute ischemic stroke; swallowing assessment and aspiration precautions are critical because acute bulbar dysfunction markedly increases aspiration risk.

Key clinical decision points: an abrupt onset of bulbar symptoms with focal neurological deficits mandates emergent MRI/DWI and neurovascular evaluation; subacute or progressive focal deficits require MRI with contrast to evaluate for neoplasm or demyelination, and contrast-enhanced imaging of the skull base for suspected compressive lesions. Early ENT involvement for airway assessment is indicated when laryngeal dysfunction or airway compromise is suspected.

# Structural lesions of the oropharynx and larynx, and reflux-related laryngopharyngeal disease

Anatomic and mucosal pathology of the oropharynx and larynx is a common non-neurologic cause of choking, cough, hoarseness, and dysphagia. Structural lesions include tumors (squamous cell carcinoma of the oropharynx/larynx, hypopharyngeal carcinoma), infectious processes (abscesses, epiglottitis), iatrogenic or traumatic injuries, foreign bodies, vocal cord paralysis, and age-related changes (presbyphagia). Pathophysiology ranges from mechanical obstruction of the airway or bolus pathway, impaired epiglottic or laryngeal elevation, to vocal fold immobility causing ineffective airway protection and aspiration.

Endoscopic evaluation is key: flexible nasoendoscopy with stroboscopy performed by ENT can visualize vocal fold mobility, structural lesions, mucosal abnormalities, and secretion pooling. Vocal fold paralysis is often unilateral and causes breathy dysphonia and aspiration with liquids; bilateral vocal fold paralysis can present with stridor and life-threatening airway compromise. The presence of a mass, mucosal irregularity, asymmetric movement, or cricopharyngeal dysfunction on endoscopy necessitates imaging (contrast CT or MRI of the neck) and tissue diagnosis via biopsy for suspected malignancy.

Laryngopharyngeal reflux (LPR) produces chronic laryngeal symptoms without classic heartburn and is implicated in chronic cough, throat clearing, hoarseness, globus sensation, and recurrent aspiration-like episodes. Pathophysiology involves episodic reflux of gastric contents (acid and non-acid) that cause inflammation of the laryngeal and pharyngeal mucosa, impairing laryngeal sensation and coordination of swallowing. Diagnosis is clinical and supported by ENT laryngoscopic findings (erythema, posterior laryngeal edema, interarytenoid hypertrophy), but these signs are nonspecific. Objective testing using 24-hour pH impedance monitoring can confirm pathological acid or non-acid reflux episodes reaching the hypopharynx. Empiric trials of proton pump inhibitors (PPI) are frequently used, but randomized data are mixed and PPIs may not alter non-acid reflux. Lifestyle modification and speech–language therapy for voice and reflux behavioral modification are also therapeutic pillars.

Barium swallow or videofluoroscopic swallowing study (VFSS) assesses oropharyngeal and esophageal phases, revealing aspiration patterns, pooling, penetration, cricopharyngeal dysfunction, and structural obstruction. Esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) evaluates for esophageal strictures, rings, webs, or malignancy that may produce choking or regurgitation. Decision-making about surgical intervention (tumor resection, laryngeal framework surgery, cricopharyngeal myotomy) requires correlation between symptoms, endoscopy, imaging, and swallow study findings.

Key clinical thresholds include urgent ENT evaluation for stridor, drooling, impending airway obstruction, or signs of upper airway infection/abscess. Any unexplained persistent hoarseness > 2–4 weeks, especially with risk factors for malignancy (smoking, alcohol), warrants laryngoscopic evaluation and possible biopsy.

# Psychogenic and functional causes of choking and cough

Psychogenic (functional) causes can mimic bulbar dysfunction or produce paroxysmal choking and cough in the absence of structural or neurophysiologic disease. Functional dysphagia and functional cough (formerly psychogenic cough or somatic cough disorder) are manifestations of altered sensorimotor control of the larynx and pharynx, often with heightened perception, maladaptive behaviors, and central processing differences. Conversion disorder may present with dysphagia, globus sensation, or episodic choking that is inconsistent with organic disease.

Clinical features suggesting a functional disorder include abrupt onset temporally related to stressors, variability and distractibility of symptoms, inconsistency between reported severity and objective findings, normal structural and electrophysiologic testing, and symptom improvement with suggestion or distraction. In functional cough, cough may be absent during sleep and triggered by attention or suggestion; in functional dysphagia, instrumental swallowing studies may show inconsistent bolus flow, normal anatomy, and preserved coordination during nonprovoked tasks.

Diagnosis is one of positive identification rather than exclusion alone: recognition of typical features, normal investigations that reliably exclude structural and neurologic causes, and the presence of psychological comorbidity support a functional diagnosis. However, because organic causes can coexist or be intermittent, careful and methodical evaluation is essential before assigning a psychogenic label. Multidisciplinary management includes speech–language therapy with behavioral cough suppression and retraining, cognitive behavioral therapy, treatment of coexisting anxiety/depression, and reassurance. Importantly, avoid dismissive attitudes; patients benefit from a clear explanation that their symptoms are real and potentially reversible with targeted therapies.

# Diagnostic algorithms and prioritized testing strategy (ENT, GI, neurology, imaging)

A pragmatic diagnostic algorithm begins with a focused history and targeted physical examination that clarifies three key questions: (1) Is there evidence of acute neurological injury requiring emergent management? (2) Are signs of airway compromise or aspiration present requiring immediate protective measures? (3) Is there a likely structural or mucosal ENT/GI cause versus a neuromuscular or central neurological cause?

Initial triage: any patient with stridor, hypoxia, inability to handle secretions, progressive dysphagia with weight loss, or signs of acute focal neurologic deficit should receive immediate airway stabilization and urgent imaging (MRI brain with DWI for suspected stroke, CT neck for airway compromise) and specialty consultation (ENT, neurology, critical care).

Baseline studies appropriate for most patients include pulse oximetry, bedside swallowing assessment (e.g., water swallow test), speech–language pathology evaluation, and nasoendoscopy if ENT symptoms predominate. Bedside tests that suggest neuromuscular transmission failure include fatigability maneuvers (sustained upgaze, repetitive speech) and assessment for ptosis or ocular motility abnormalities.

When ENT structural disease is suspected due to hoarseness, stridor, visible mass, or focal laryngeal symptoms, flexible laryngoscopy is first-line. Findings of vocal fold immobility, mucosal lesions, or pooling of secretions prompt cross-sectional imaging of the neck (contrast CT or MRI) and biopsy for suspected neoplasm. If oropharyngeal or esophageal obstruction is suspected, VFSS (barium swallow) and/or EGD are indicated. If reflux is suspected, a trial of behavioral therapy and medical treatment may be attempted—but in refractory or atypical cases, 24-hour pH impedance monitoring is the definitive test for objective reflux reaching the hypopharynx.

When a neuromuscular junction disorder is suspected (fluctuating fatigable weakness, ocular involvement, preserved reflexes early), obtain serologic testing for AChR and MuSK antibodies and refer for EMG/SFEMG. Chest CT to assess for thymoma should accompany positive AChR antibody results. In suspected generalized MG with respiratory compromise, hospital admission and pulmonary function testing are urgent.

When motor neuron disease or other central neurodegenerative process is suspected (progressive course, LMN/UMN signs, tongue fasciculations), perform EMG demonstrating denervation in multiple segments and MRI to exclude structural lesions. Routine labs to exclude metabolic mimics (thyroid function tests, B12, infectious serologies) should be included. Muscle enzyme testing (CK) and, when appropriate, muscle biopsy can distinguish myopathies such as inclusion-body myositis.

If acute onset with focal brainstem signs, MRI brain with DWI is the priority to detect ischemia; vascular imaging (CTA or MRA) follows for posterior circulation evaluation. When demyelinating disease is suspected, MRI brain and spine with contrast and lumbar puncture for CSF analysis including oligoclonal bands are appropriate.

A prioritized, time-sensitive testing flow might be conceptualized as follows: immediate bedside airway and swallowing assessment; if alarm features present, urgent imaging and specialty consultation; if not, use history/exam to steer early testing: ENT (flexible laryngoscopy, VFSS) for suspected structural/laryngeal disease; neurology (antibody testing, EMG, MRI brainstem) for suspected NMJ, MND, or central causes; GI (EGD, pH impedance) for suspected reflux/esophageal causes. Throughout, coordinate multidisciplinary input—ENT for laryngeal visualization and airway management, speech-language pathologists for swallowing assessment and therapy, neurologists for neuromuscular evaluation, gastroenterologists for esophageal and reflux assessment, and radiologists for directed imaging.

Key diagnostic thresholds and normal ranges to remember: serum AChR and MuSK antibody assays are qualitative but positive results are diagnostic when clinical context aligns; normal CK is commonly approximately 30–200 U/L (lab dependent), and markedly elevated CK suggests primary myopathy rather than NMJ or ALS. Pulmonary function thresholds guiding urgency include a decline in forced vital capacity to <50% predicted or absolute values that fall rapidly or approach 20 mL/kg for consideration of ventilatory support. VFSS findings of aspiration on thin liquids require urgent dietary modification and swallowing therapy. Positive pH impedance monitoring demonstrating increased acid exposure time or proximal reflux episodes supports LPR diagnosis when correlated with symptoms.

Conclusions

Bulbar dysfunction and choking demand a broad, integrated approach. Recognizing patterns—fluctuating fatigability for NMJ disorders, progressive combined UMN/LMN signs for motor neuron disease, abrupt focal deficits for vascular lesions, structural or mucosal laryngeal findings for ENT pathology, and characteristic features of functional disorders—guides efficient investigation. Multidisciplinary assessment with timely use of endoscopy, imaging, electrophysiology, swallow studies, and reflux testing yields accurate diagnoses and expedites appropriate therapies. Urgent referral and airway protection remain paramount when red flags are present.
