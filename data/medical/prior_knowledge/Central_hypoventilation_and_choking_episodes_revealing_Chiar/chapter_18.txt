Introduction

Headache syndromes that arise from posterior fossa pathology and occipital neuralgia occupy an important intersection of neuroanatomy, pain physiology, and clinical neurology. Posterior fossa structures—brainstem, cerebellum, 4th ventricle, cranial nerve roots and the upper cervical spinal cord—are intimately related to pathways that mediate head and scalp sensation and cranial autonomic features. Focal pathology in this region can therefore produce headaches that mimic primary headache disorders such as migraine or tension-type headache, but that differ in etiology, clinical features and management. Occipital neuralgia is a prototypical peripheral neuropathic scalp pain syndrome produced by irritation of the occipital nerves. Other localized scalp pain disorders such as nummular headache and burning vertex syndrome are uncommon but distinctive clinical entities. This chapter reviews the clinical features that distinguish occipital neuralgia from other common headache types, presents the diagnostic criteria and relevant peripheral nerve anatomy, discusses localized scalp pain disorders, outlines the role of peripheral nerve blocks and modulation and neurosurgical options, reviews sensory testing and patterns of allodynia on the scalp, and provides an approach to evaluation when posterior fossa abnormalities are present on imaging.

# Clinical features distinguishing occipital neuralgia from tension-type and migraine headaches

Occipital neuralgia is a neuropathic pain syndrome characterized by paroxysmal, stabbing, electric-shock–like pain in the distribution of the occipital nerves. Clinically, its features contrast in important ways with tension-type headache and migraine, entities that may also cause posterior head pain.

In occipital neuralgia the pain is typically unilateral but may be bilateral. It is described as sharp, lancinating, shooting or electric and often begins in the upper neck or suboccipital region and radiates forward over the posterior scalp to the vertex or even peri-orbital regions. Attacks are usually brief (seconds to minutes) but can cluster or become continuous with superimposed paroxysms. Pain is frequently precipitated by neck movement, pressure over the occipital region (e.g., combing hair, wearing a hat), or palpation of the occipital nerve. There may be associated dysesthesia, numbness or a burning quality in the same distribution. Tenderness over the occipital nerve(s) or reproducibility with direct pressure or with stretching of the neck is a common finding. Photophobia or nausea may be present but when present they are less prominent than in migraine and often accompany the paroxysmal attacks rather than preceding or following an aura-like progression.

Tension-type headache typically produces a bilateral, pressing or tightening quality, described as a constant, nonpulsatile pressure or band-like sensation. Intensity ranges from mild to moderate and is rarely incapacitating; it is not characteristically lancinating or electric. Associated symptoms such as photophobia or phonophobia can occur but are usually mild and not the dominant features. Tenderness in pericranial muscles (frontal, temporal, occipital, neck) may be present, but the pain character, duration (hours to days), and absence of paroxysmal electric-shock pain distinguish tension-type headache from occipital neuralgia. Tension headache is not typically provoked by focal cutaneous pressure in the occipital region producing the type of paroxysmal firing seen in occipital neuralgia.

Migraine commonly causes unilateral, throbbing or pulsatile head pain, often moderate to severe, and is associated with nausea, vomiting, photophobia, phonophobia, and aggravation by routine physical activity. Migrainous pain may begin in the occipital region in some patients, but the longer duration (4–72 hours for typical attacks), progressive buildup, and throbbing character differ from the brief lancinating paroxysms of occipital neuralgia. Migraines may produce cutaneous allodynia during attacks—discomfort to normally nonpainful stimulation such as brushing hair—but this is part of widespread central sensitization and occurs during sustained migrainous pain rather than as transient shock-like events originating from a peripheral nerve. Aura phenomena (visual, sensory, speech) preceding the headache favor migraine and not occipital neuralgia.

Key clinical distinctions, therefore, rest on pain quality (electric/shooting versus pressing or throbbing), duration and temporal pattern (brief paroxysms versus sustained hours to days), focal reproduction with occipital nerve palpation or neck movement, and presence of neuropathic features (numbness, dysesthesia) or a clear peripheral trigger in occipital neuralgia. When attacks include autonomic features (lacrimation, conjunctival injection) in a strictly posterior location, secondary causes should be considered because cranial autonomic symptoms more typically accompany trigeminal autonomic cephalalgias.

# Diagnostic criteria for occipital neuralgia and relevant peripheral nerve anatomy

Diagnostic criteria

The International Classification of Headache Disorders, 3rd edition (ICHD-3) defines occipital neuralgia with criteria that emphasize the clinical phenotype of paroxysmal neuropathic pain in the distribution of the greater, lesser or third occipital nerves. The essential features are:

1) Paroxysmal stabbing pain, with or without persistent aching between attacks, in the distribution(s) of the greater, lesser and/or third occipital nerves.

2) Tenderness over the affected nerve(s) and reproduction of pain by direct pressure over the nerve and/or by percussion at the superior nuchal ridge.

3) Optional associated symptoms such as decreased sensation or dysesthesia in the affected skin territory; remissions and exacerbations may occur.

4) Pain not better accounted for by another ICHD-3 diagnosis.

For a diagnosis of occipital neuralgia one seeks the classic distribution, paroxysmal quality, reproducibility with nerve palpation or block, and exclusion of secondary causes such as trauma, compression by local masses, infection, inflammatory conditions, or upper cervical radiculopathy. It is important to document whether the occipital neuralgia is idiopathic (no structural lesion identified) or secondary to an identifiable cause.

Peripheral nerve anatomy

Appreciation of occipital nerve anatomy is central to diagnosis, examination and invasive interventions. The principal nerves involved are the greater occipital nerve (GON), the lesser occipital nerve (LON), and the third occipital nerve (TON).

The greater occipital nerve arises from the dorsal ramus of the C2 spinal nerve. It courses between the posterior arch of C1 and the superior articular process of C2, then ascends deep to the obliquus capitis inferior muscle, pierces the semispinalis capitis and trapezius aponeurosis, and emerges approximately 2–3 cm lateral to the external occipital protuberance. It supplies sensation to the scalp region from the upper neck and occiput to approximately the vertex and posterior parietal area. Because of its deep-to-superficial course and its relation to musculature and fascial planes, the GON is susceptible to entrapment or irritation at multiple sites: at its exit through the semispinalis capitis, beneath the trapezius aponeurosis, or by muscular spasm and myofascial structures.

The lesser occipital nerve originates from the ventral rami of C2 and C3 (sometimes described as the cervical plexus). It ascends posterior to the sternocleidomastoid muscle along the posterior border and supplies the lateral occiput behind the auricle. Its more superficial and lateral course makes it vulnerable to compression from cervical lymphadenopathy, surgical scars, or external pressure from headgear.

The third occipital nerve is the superficial medial branch of the dorsal ramus of C3 and supplies a small region in the lower occipital area and upper neck. Clinically, TON-related pain is often localized lower and medial in the neck and occiput and can be confused with cervicogenic headache originating at the C2–3 zygapophyseal joint.

Because of shared innervation and convergence of cervical and trigeminal afferents in the trigeminocervical complex (nucleus caudalis and upper cervical dorsal horns), pain originating from occipital nerves or upper cervical roots can be perceived in frontotemporal or peri-orbital distributions and can trigger migrainous or cranial autonomic phenomena. Distinguishing cervicogenic headache (referred pain from cervical structures) from occipital neuralgia (peripheral neuropathy of occipital nerves) requires careful history and examination, and occasionally diagnostic blocks to isolate the pain generator.

# Nummular headache, burning vertex syndrome and other localized scalp pain disorders

Nummular headache and burning vertex syndrome illustrate the concept that the scalp itself can be the primary site of pain generation. These localized scalp pain disorders are uncommon but should be recognized because they have characteristic patterns and treatment implications.

Nummular headache is characterized by a well-circumscribed, coin-shaped (hence “nummular”) area of scalp pain, typically 1–6 cm in diameter. The pain may be continuous or intermittent and is often described as pressure-like, stabbing, or burning. The lesion is fixed in location and does not migrate. On examination there may be focal tenderness, allodynia, or sensory changes within the circumscribed area. Nummular headache is a diagnosis of exclusion: secondary scalp pathology (e.g., localized infection, neoplasm, arteriovenous malformation, cranial skin disease) must be ruled out. The ICHD-3 notes that investigations are usually normal; however targeted scalp imaging or dermatologic evaluation is warranted if clinical suspicion is raised. The pathophysiology is incompletely understood but is thought to involve terminal branches of cutaneous nerves or small peripheral nerve entrapment/inflammation leading to localized neuropathic pain.

Burning vertex syndrome (also termed “ice-pick” variant in some contexts when attacks are very brief) presents with burning or lancinating pain localized to the vertex or midline scalp. Patients report focal pain at the crown of the head that may be continuous or paroxysmal. Clinical examination may reveal hyperesthesia, allodynia, or a small focal tender point at the vertex. The entity sometimes overlaps with nummular headache when the area is small, but burning vertex syndrome typically involves the vertex rather than a lateralized coin-shaped patch. Differential diagnoses include migrainous cutaneous allodynia, occipital neuralgia with radiation to the vertex, and central pain syndromes. As with nummular headache, evaluation should exclude secondary causes.

Other localized scalp pain disorders include post-traumatic scalp neuropathies (scar neuromas producing focal tender points), post-craniotomy scalp neuralgia, herpes zoster involving scalp branches, and localized cutaneous allodynia secondary to chronic migraine. Each disorder can be distinguished by history (preceding trauma, surgery, rash), examination (distribution of sensory deficits, Tinel-like sign over scar), and, where necessary, imaging or dermatologic assessment.

Management of these disorders often begins with conservative neuropathic pain strategies: topical agents (lidocaine 5% patches, capsaicin cream or high-concentration capsaicin under specialist guidance), oral neuropathic agents (gabapentin, pregabalin, tricyclic antidepressants), and local nerve blocks. For nummular headache, small series and case reports support benefit from local anesthetic injections, onabotulinumtoxinA in recalcitrant cases, or peripheral neuromodulation for refractory pain. The rarity of these disorders means that high-quality randomized trials are limited; treatment decisions should be individualized with attention to side-effect profiles.

# Role of nerve blocks, peripheral nerve modulation and neurosurgical considerations

Nerve blocks: diagnostic and therapeutic roles

Peripheral occipital nerve blocks (greater and lesser occipital nerves) serve both diagnostic and therapeutic purposes. A diagnostic block that transiently abolishes the pain supports a peripheral generator (occipital nerve or upper cervical root). Therapeutically, local anesthetic (commonly 1–3 mL of 0.25–0.5% bupivacaine or 1–3 mL of 1%–2% lidocaine, sometimes combined with a corticosteroid such as 20–40 mg of triamcinolone) can relieve pain for days to weeks; steroid may prolong effect by reducing local inflammation. The blockade technique involves injecting at the anatomical location where the nerve emerges—approximately 2–3 cm lateral to the external occipital protuberance for the GON—or using ultrasound guidance for more precise placement, especially in patients with variant anatomy or prior surgery. Complications are uncommon but include bleeding, infection, local anesthetic systemic toxicity (rare with appropriate doses), numbness at the scalp, and transient motor weakness if deeper structures are affected.

Peripheral nerve modulation: neuromodulation and radiofrequency techniques

For patients with medically refractory occipital neuralgia, several less invasive interventional options exist. Pulsed radiofrequency (PRF) applied to the occipital nerve(s) has an emerging evidence base. PRF delivers brief bursts of high-frequency current that modulate nociceptive signaling without producing frank neurodestructive heat lesions. Clinical series show variable but often meaningful reductions in pain duration and intensity; PRF is attractive because it avoids permanent sensory loss. Conventional continuous thermal radiofrequency ablation is less favored due to risk of dysesthesia and anesthesia dolorosa.

Occipital nerve stimulation (ONS), a form of peripheral nerve stimulation (PNS), involves implantation of subcutaneous electrodes over the occipital nerve(s) connected to a pulse generator. ONS modulates pain via gate-control and descending inhibitory pathways and has demonstrated efficacy in open-label and randomized controlled trials for refractory chronic migraine and occipital neuralgia. Patient selection is crucial: best candidates have reproducible pain in the GON/LON distribution, prior positive response to occipital nerve block, and failure of conservative and pharmacologic therapy. Complications include lead migration, infection, hardware malfunction, and need for battery replacement; careful programming and lead anchoring mitigate some risks.

Neurosurgical considerations: decompression and neurectomy

Surgical options are considered for severe, intractable occipital neuralgia that does not respond to conservative, pharmacologic and interventional therapies. Two principal approaches exist: neurolysis/decompression and neurectomy/transection.

Decompression involves surgically releasing compression points along the occipital nerve course—releasing muscular bands (e.g., semispinalis capitis, obliquus capitis inferior), dividing fascial tunnels, and excising osteophytes or vascular loops compressing the nerve. The goal is to preserve nerve function while removing mechanical irritants. Success rates in observational studies are variable; careful patient selection and surgical expertise are determinants of outcome.

Neurectomy entails partial or complete transection of the affected occipital nerve. It can provide durable pain relief but carries the tradeoff of permanent numbness, potential stump neuroma formation, and risk of deafferentation pain (anesthesia dolorosa), which can be difficult to treat. Neurectomy is usually reserved for highly selected patients after exhaustive nonablative measures have failed and when pain is clearly localized to a single nerve territory.

Other neurosurgical considerations include addressing secondary causes of occipital pain—posterior fossa tumors, Chiari I malformation with syringomyelia, cervical spine instability or inflammatory radiculopathy—where treating the underlying lesion can alleviate the headache. Consultation with neurosurgery is warranted when imaging suggests structural lesions or when considering invasive interventions such as ONS implantation which requires surgical expertise.

# Patterns of allodynia and sensory testing of scalp/vertex pain

Allodynia and central sensitization play important roles in headache disorders, and mapping sensory abnormalities on the scalp can help differentiate peripheral from central pain generators and guide therapy.

Patterns of allodynia

Allodynia refers to pain evoked by normally non-painful stimuli. On the scalp, allodynia can be mechanical (hair brushing, wearing a hat), thermal (cold wind), or dynamic (light stroking). In migraine, allodynia commonly develops as part of central sensitization during prolonged attacks and tends to be widespread—affecting frontal, temporal and occipital scalp as well as periorbital regions. In contrast, occipital neuralgia and localized scalp neuropathies produce regionally circumscribed allodynia confined to the affected nerve distribution or the nummular patch.

Patterns that suggest peripheral nerve involvement include: sharply demarcated territories of hyperalgesia or hypoesthesia corresponding to the expected cutaneous territory of the GON, LON or TON; a Tinel-like sign (percussive provocation of shooting pain) over a localized point; and provocation by focal pressure or neck movement. Central sensitization is suggested by widespread allodynia, secondary hyperalgesia beyond the nerve territory, or development of allodynia in body sites remote from the head.

Sensory testing methods

Bedside sensory testing of the scalp complements the history and includes assessment of light touch, pinprick, temperature and dynamic mechanical allodynia.

Light-touch testing is performed with a cotton ball or gauze pad to map areas of decreased sensation or increased sensitivity. Pinprick testing with a disposable neurological pin or Neurotip assesses nociceptive small-fiber function; identification of hyperalgesia or hypoalgesia within a defined scalp region can indicate neuropathic involvement. Temperature sensation can be approximated using test tubes filled with warm and cold water or commercially available thermal probes; localized thermal hypoesthesia supports peripheral nerve dysfunction. Dynamic mechanical allodynia is assessed by lightly stroking the skin with a brush and asking whether the stimulus elicits pain.

Quantitative sensory testing (QST), when available in specialized centers, provides standardized measures of mechanical detection thresholds, pain thresholds, thermal thresholds and conditioned pain modulation. Tools such as Von Frey filaments for mechanical detection and pressure algometers for pressure pain thresholds provide reproducible metrics. Mapping with these modalities can aid research, document baseline deficits before interventions, and monitor response.

Interpreting sensory testing

Interpretation should integrate topography (does the abnormality match known nerve territories?), temporal features (static vs. paroxysmal), and associated clinical signs (tenderness, trigger points). For example, a patient with lancinating occipital pain, a sharply demarcated patch of decreased pinprick sensitivity following the GON distribution, and reproduction of pain with percussion strongly supports peripheral occipital nerve pathology amenable to nerve blocks or neuromodulation. Conversely, diffuse scalp allodynia without focal deficits or reproducible trigger points suggests central sensitization as in chronic migraine and directs therapy toward systemic migraine treatments and neuromodulatory approaches.

# Approach to headache evaluation when imaging shows posterior fossa abnormalities

When neuroimaging reveals posterior fossa abnormalities in a patient presenting with headache, a structured approach is needed to determine the relationship between imaging findings and the clinical syndrome. Posterior fossa lesions that can be implicated in headache include posterior fossa tumors, Chiari I malformation, arachnoid cysts, vascular compressive phenomena (e.g., vertebral artery loop impinging on nerves), cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) flow abnormalities, cerebellar ischemia, and demyelinating plaques affecting the dorsal brainstem or upper cervical cord. Determining whether the lesion is incidental or causally related to the headache is a key clinical decision point.

Correlate lesion location with pain topography and phenotype

Assess whether the lesion’s anatomical location plausibly explains the patient’s pain pattern. For example, Chiari I malformation can cause occipital or suboccipital pain that worsens with Valsalva maneuvers and neck flexion, and may produce lower cranial nerve and cerebellar signs; a midline posterior fossa tumor with mass effect at the foramen magnum may cause neck pain, occipital headache and signs of cranial neuropathy or hydrocephalus. A vascular loop compressing the dorsal root entry zone of C2 could produce occipital neuralgia–like symptoms. Conversely, small asymptomatic arachnoid cysts or incidental posterior fossa white matter lesions may be unrelated to the headache picture.

Evaluate for red flags and signs of secondary headache

Any of the following increase the likelihood that imaging abnormalities are clinically significant and warrant urgent intervention: progressive neurological deficit (motor weakness, cranial nerve palsies), new-onset severe headache with features of raised intracranial pressure (worse in the morning, vomiting, papilledema), positional headache suggesting CSF hypovolemia or hydrocephalus, new seizures, systemic symptoms (fever, weight loss), or signs of infection/inflammation. In the absence of red flags, an isolated small posterior fossa lesion in a patient with a typical primary headache phenotype (e.g., classic migraine) may be incidental.

Use targeted clinical tests to refine causality

Perform a detailed neurological examination focusing on cranial nerves, cerebellar function, and upper cervical sensory and motor findings. Provocative testing for occipital neuralgia—tenderness over the GON/LON, reproduction of pain with direct pressure, relief with local anesthetic injection—helps demonstrate a peripheral cause. If a lesion is suspected to compress or irritate the C2 root or occipital nerves (e.g., foramen magnum crowding), then a diagnostic occipital nerve block that temporarily relieves the pain supports peripheral attribution and may obviate neurosurgical intervention. Alternatively, lack of response to peripheral blocks in presence of mass effect or hydrocephalus increases the likelihood that the intracranial lesion is the pain generator.

Match imaging characteristics with clinical urgency

Certain imaging features—mass effect, hydrocephalus, contrast enhancement suggesting neoplasm or infection, expansile lesions at foramen magnum, or migratory lesions consistent with inflammatory demyelination—warrant expedited neurosurgical or medical consultation. Chiari I malformation accompanied by syringomyelia or progressive neurological symptoms is a surgical disease that can benefit from posterior fossa decompression. Small, stable lesions without mass effect may be managed conservatively with serial imaging and symptomatic headache therapy unless the clinical course changes.

Integrate multidisciplinary care and plan interventions

When imaging suggests a potentially causal posterior fossa abnormality, multidisciplinary evaluation with neuroradiology and neurosurgery is often necessary. If the lesion is judged likely causal and treatable (e.g., compressive mass, symptomatic Chiari I), definitive treatment may resolve the headache. If a peripheral nerve generator is suspected in the setting of posterior fossa pathology (for example, a vascular loop causing focal C2 irritation), targeted peripheral interventions—blocks, PRF, or focal decompression—can be considered. For patients with refractory occipital neuralgia without surgically treatable posterior fossa disease, neuromodulation (ONS/PNS) offers an effective long-term option, but device planning should consider the presence of intracranial lesions and potential future need for neurosurgical procedures.

Imaging modalities and interpretation

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain and high cervical spine with contrast is the modality of choice for posterior fossa assessment. Thin-slice sequences through the foramen magnum, constructive interference in steady state (CISS) or FIESTA sequences to assess cranial nerve root entry zones, and MR angiography for vascular pathology are valuable. When suspecting skeletal or foraminal bony abnormalities, CT or CT myelography can be informative. When a vascular loop is suspected compressing a nerve root, high-resolution MRI or MR angiography combined with dedicated neurovascular sequences is appropriate. Always interpret imaging in the clinical context—radiologic abnormalities are common and must be correlated with history and examination findings.

Conclusion

Headaches arising from posterior fossa pathology and occipital neuralgia require careful clinical discernment, knowledge of occipital nerve anatomy and pain mechanisms, and an integrated diagnostic approach. Distinguishing peripheral neuropathic occipital neuralgia from tension-type headache, migraine and cervicogenic pain relies on pain quality, temporal pattern, focal reproducibility and sensory findings. Nummular headache and burning vertex syndrome are examples of localized scalp pain disorders that demand exclusion of secondary causes. Peripheral nerve blocks have diagnostic and therapeutic utility; PRF, nerve stimulation and surgical options are available for refractory disease but require careful selection. Sensory testing and mapping of allodynia help to localize the source of pain and decide between peripheral and central mechanisms. Finally, when posterior fossa abnormalities appear on imaging, clinical correlation, assessment for red flags, and multidisciplinary planning are essential to decide whether imaging findings are causal and whether neurosurgical or interventional therapies are indicated.
